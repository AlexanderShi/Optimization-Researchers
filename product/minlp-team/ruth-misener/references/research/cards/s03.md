### S076 · Detecting symmetry in designing heat exchanger networks (FOCAPO/CPC 2017, arXiv:1711.02564)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-6 read, including Appendices A-B, p. 6; reference list skimmed). Version read: arXiv v1 of the FOCAPO/CPC 2017 paper (footnote p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: G. Kouyialis (first author, EPSRC DTP student, p. 6) · **Position in trajectory**: first step of the symmetry line that SKILL.md Method 1 cites through the later Processes paper (doi:10.3390/pr7110838); here only characterisation, no exploitation.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Enters from an application gap: symmetry is well studied in MILP but "has not been characterised" for heat exchanger network synthesis, and current MILP solvers do not detect or use it there (abstract, p. 1). Chooses the sequential (LP → MILP → NLP) route because each subproblem "nicely isolates computational difficulties" of the simultaneous MINLP (p. 1). Posits that the many equal-objective MILP transshipment solutions reported by Gundersen and Grossmann are due to symmetry (p. 2), and checks that switching CPLEX 12.6's symmetry feature on and off does not alter the search tree (p. 2).
  - Transferable: before claiming a structure is unexploited, toggle the solver's own symmetry/structure feature and report whether the tree changes (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Fix one temperature interval t' of the Papoulias-Grossmann MILP transshipment model (p. 3) so that the continuous heat-splitting no longer hides the structure, then use group theory: streams with equal flow-rate heat capacity F(Cp) leave the residual change ΔR unchanged (Eq. 4) and can be permuted (Lemmas 2-3, p. 4); classes of equal streams give symmetric groups S_nw (Props. 4-5), direct products over classes (Props. 6-7) and the interval group G(HP) × G(CP) (Theorem 8, p. 4). Keeps the simpler unit-weight objective because reweighting (Chen et al. 2015) gave no significant computational advantage (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a continuous variable masks combinatorial symmetry, fix the continuous "slice" (here one temperature interval) and characterise the symmetry group slice by slice (pp. 2-3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Constant, equal temperature change δT_t for all streams within an interval (p. 2); only hot-to-cold matches, utilities treated as streams in intermediate intervals, no HU-CU matches (pp. 2-3). Results said to extend "trivially" to equal heat loads Q (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: Counting argument for n^m configurations (Lemma 1, Appendix B, p. 6); invariance of ΔR under swapping equal-F(Cp) streams (Lemma 2, p. 4); n! permutation count for Prop. 4 (Appendix B, p. 6); direct-product composition cited from Liberti (2012) and Costa et al. (2013) for Prop. 6 (p. 4). Observation (mine): Prop. 4's statement uses S_nw but its proof counts n! permutations, mixing class size and total count (pp. 4, 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: One minlp.org instance (Transshipment V1_5: 5 hot, 5 cold, 2 HU, 1 CU), GAMS 24.7.1 model of Chen et al., CPLEX 12.6 solution pool; ten optimal solutions of objective 24 inspected by hand to show swapped streams H1/H3 and C1/CU (pp. 4-5, Figs. 3-4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution is a characterisation ("for the first time", p. 5), not an algorithm; exploitation via symmetry-breaking or weighted objectives is left as future work (p. 5); the test case is "relatively small" (p. 5); the investment-cost NLP stage is "not examined in this work" (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: steps 1-3 present (read how the transshipment model is written, name the structure, state it algebraically, pp. 2-4), but no detector is implemented, validated against a library, or exploited inside B&B (steps 5-6 absent, p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: "check the solver's own structure feature first": CPLEX's symmetry switch does not change the tree, which motivates the paper (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Toggle the commercial solver's symmetry handling and compare search trees as the empirical motivation for a structure paper (p. 2).
- Use the solver's solution pool to enumerate equal-objective optima and display them as a match table, so symmetric optima are visible (pp. 4-5, Fig. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "State-of-the-art solver software CPLEX does not seem to use problem symmetry in the MILP transshipment model;" (p. 2)
- "This paper has explored, for the first time, the symmetric structure of the HENS problem." (p. 5)

### S095 · Bayesian multi-objective optimisation of neotissue growth in a perfusion bioreactor set-up (Computer Aided Chemical Engineering 40 / ESCAPE-27 2017, doi:10.1016/b978-0-444-63965-3.50361-5)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-6 read, including Table 1, p. 5; references skimmed).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Olofsson (first), M. Mehrian, L. Geris (Liège), R. Calandra (Berkeley), M. P. Deisenroth, R. Misener (corresponding) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: early Bayesian-optimisation application paper; companion to S030 (same bioreactor model, Mehrian et al. 2017, p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A bioreactor design with two conflicting objectives: neotissue filling (an expensive black-box simulator) and operating cost (analytical, cheap) (pp. 1-2). The entry is that standard Bayesian MOO treats every objective as a black box; here one objective is known exactly (abstract, p. 1). Weighted-sum scalarisation is rejected because weights must be chosen a priori and only convex parts of the Pareto front are reachable (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Extend expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI, Emmerich et al. 2008) to one deterministic and one GP-modelled objective: p(y|x) = δ(y1 − f1(x)) p(y2|x), so the integral collapses to one dimension over the rectangles below the current front, giving a closed-form, differentiable EHI(x) (Eqs. 1-3, pp. 3-4).
  - Transferable: when only part of a multi-objective problem is uncertain, model only that part and put a Dirac delta on the known objective; the acquisition often becomes closed form (pp. 3-4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Two design variables (refreshment period r ∈ [12h, 96h], ratio a ∈ [0%, 100%]); cost c(r, a) = 1 + ⌊504/r⌋·a with ∂c/∂r approximated by ignoring the floor (p. 2); front sorted and bounded by surrogate points r0, r_{np+1} (p. 3); query-selection time ignored because evaluations dominate (p. 5).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a (a derivation of Eq. 3, no theorems).
- **D5 Experiments**: EHVI vs NSGA-III on Fonseca-Fleming (concave), Schaffer (convex), Kursawe (discontinuous) and the TE problem; 10 initial points, 50 further iterations, mean of 25 runs, metrics GD, MPFE, volume ratio (pp. 4-5, Table 1); one baseline only, no variance reported.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic (closed-form EHVI variant) plus application. Scope sentence: GAs "can be preferable" for cheap evaluations and more design variables (p. 5); the win is called "unsurprising" given few variables and iterations (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (scope of benchmarking): a single GA baseline at fixed settings, averages only, query time excluded (pp. 5-6), far from the auditor protocol; consistent with a 6-page conference application paper.
- New-pattern candidate: "model only the unknown part": known analytical objectives kept exact inside the acquisition function (pp. 3-4); compare S033, where the cheap MILP and the expensive simulation are also separated.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive the acquisition in closed form by integrating out only the GP-modelled objective, and report that it is differentiable in (f1, μ2, σ2) so a gradient optimiser can maximise it (p. 4).
- State the do-not-use regime next to the win (cheap evaluations, many variables → GA) (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "The computational time required to choose the next query point has not been taken into account since our focus is on applications where evaluating the black-box function is significantly more expensive than optimising for the next query point." (p. 5)
- "Whereas genetic algorithms can be preferable for applications with cheap function evaluations and higher number of design variables" (p. 5)

### S155 · Integrating Mixed-Integer Optimisation and Satisfiability Modulo Theories: Application to Scheduling (FOCAPO/CPC 2017, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-6 read, including Tables 2-3 and both performance profiles; references skimmed).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Mistry (first author, HiPEDS CDT student, p. 6) · **Position in trajectory**: first MILP/SMT paper; its LBBD introduction reappears almost verbatim in the journal paper S058 §3.5 (S058 p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Hybrid MILP/CP logic-based Benders decomposition (LBBD) works for scheduling because CP has bespoke scheduling constraints, but "if the application does not have a suitable CP constraint, a hybrid method may be poor" (p. 1). Question: replace CP by satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) in the same hybrid (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep Hooker's (2007) LBBD formulations, cuts and subproblem relaxations unchanged and swap only the subproblem solver to Z3 (p. 3). The SMT unsatisfiable core directly supplies the conflicting task set J̄_hi for the no-good cut (Eq. 20) and the makespan cuts (Eqs. 25-29), which in the CP version needs a separate greedy filtering algorithm (p. 4). The pure-SMT model is reformulated as generalised 2-D bin packing so the exponential resource constraints (Eq. 12) become quadratic pairwise disjunctions (Eq. 17) whose cores are interpretable (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when testing a new component inside a published decomposition, hold the published formulation, cuts and relaxations fixed and change only that component (p. 3).
  - Transferable: rewrite a family of subset constraints as pairwise disjunctions so that infeasibility certificates (cores) name small item sets (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Hooker's discrete-time MILP planning and scheduling model with release/due times and a cumulative resource per facility (Eqs. 1-7, pp. 2-3); SMT used as a feasibility oracle with an iteratively tightened objective bound (p. 4); discrete-time chosen because the continuous-time MILP (Türkay-Grossmann) is harder on these instances (p. 2).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: none new; correctness rests on Hooker (2007) LBBD and on the core being mutually unsatisfiable (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: Hooker's 335 instances (4 classes), instances with fewer than 18 tasks discarded as toys (230 remain); Gurobi 6.0.3 vs Z3 vs hybrid, 3600 s, performance profiles plus average times on 'c' instances with 2-4 facilities (pp. 4-6, Figs. 1-2, Tables 2-3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Hybrid MILP/SMT solves close to 90% of min-cost instances and is fastest on more than half (p. 5), but is weaker than either MILP or SMT for makespan (abstract p. 1; pp. 5-6); explained by where learning happens: the MILP master learns the feasible space only through Benders cuts, and per-facility subproblems stop SMT from learning across facilities (pp. 5-6).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (step 8, losses written in): the negative makespan result is in the abstract and conclusion (pp. 1, 6); a written exclusion rule for toy instances with its reason (p. 5).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the verdict is split by objective class (min cost vs makespan, pp. 5-6), as in Method 4 step 3, but the "component" is a whole subproblem solver, not a relaxation inside a B&B.
- New-pattern candidate: "swap one component of a published hybrid for a neighbour-community tool, keep the rest fixed" (p. 3); recurs in S058 (pp. 4-7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Discard instances below a size threshold from profiles because toy results "may be biased towards implementation", and say so (p. 5).
- Explain a negative result by locating where each method learns (master via cuts vs SAT conflict learning across iterations) (pp. 5-6).

**Quotes**:
- "But the hybrid MILP/SMT method is weaker than either MILP or SMT on a minimum makespan model." (p. 1)
- "Results for these toy test cases may be biased towards implementation opposed to algorithm performance." (p. 5)

### S030 · Maximizing neotissue growth kinetics in a perfusion bioreactor: an in silico strategy using model reduction and Bayesian optimization (Biotechnology and Bioengineering 2018, doi:10.1002/bit.26500)

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Mehrian (first), Y. Guyot, I. Papantoniou, S. Olofsson, M. Sonnaert, R. Misener, L. - **D1 Problem and entry point**: Bioreactor medium refreshment is set by "historically determined time points" (p. 3); the Guyot 3D mechanistic model (level-set neotissue growth, CFD, oxygen, glucose, lactate, pH) takes about 3 days per run, which makes optimisation "almost intractable" (p. 5); evolutionary algorithms need too many evaluations (p. 3).

Reduced model runs in about 2 s, a 10^5-fold speed-up (p. 12).
  - Transferable: validate a reduced model three ways (against the full model, against dedicated experiments, and by re-running the same optimisation on both models and comparing optima) (pp. 14-19).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Spatial heterogeneity irrelevant for the culture-strategy decision (p. 5); Michaelis-Menten uptake, linear pH-lactate relation (Eqs. 9-12, p. 9); refreshment modelled as instantaneous re-initialisation (p. 9); a single averaged parameter set for all triply periodic scaffolds (pp. 21-22).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: Reduced vs 3D on five scaffold geometries (Fig. 3, Table 2); comparison to hPDC culture data on a titanium Gyroid scaffold (Fig. 4, pp. 13-17); BO at day 7 and day 21 on both models; 3D BO stopped after about 30 evaluations (about 2 months) and augmented with manually chosen points (pp. 18-19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Claims the first full reduction-reparametrisation-sensitivity-optimisation pipeline on one TE bioreactor model (pp. 4-5). Limits stated: homogenisation loses spatial mechanisms such as local-pH-triggered refreshment (p. 20); the optimum maximises growth with no cost term, so a multi-objective follow-up is announced (pp. 19-20; S095 is that direction); averaged parameters lower the predicted maximum filling from 88% to 83% (p. 22). Observation (mine): Fig. 4's caption says G10 while the text says G7 (pp. 16-17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: optima checked across two models and against literature (p. 19), but BO on the 3D model is a single truncated run with hand-added samples (pp. 18-19), not an audited comparison.
- New-pattern candidate: "reduce the simulator until rigorous optimisation fits, then cross-check the optimum on the full model" (pp. 4-5, 12, 17-19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use cheap early-time outputs (day 7) as a proxy screen before expensive full-horizon runs, after checking the proxy correlates (p. 17).
- Report how much compute the full-model optimisation actually consumed and where it was stopped (p. 19).

**Quotes**:

- "Homogenizing the spatial variables reduces the mechanistic character of the model and does not address a number issues for which the spatial character is important." (p. 20)

### S033 · Data-driven optimization of processes with degrading equipment (Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research 2018, doi:10.1021/acs.iecr.8b03292; read as arXiv:1810.09289v2)
**Read level**: partial (main text pp. 1-30 read, with the STN model equations pp. 17-19 skimmed; Appendix B proof read in part, pp. 32-33; Appendices A, C, D and the reference list not read). Version read: arXiv v2, 24 Jan 2019 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Wiebe (first, EPSRC/Schlumberger CASE student, p. 30), I. Cecílio (Schlumberger Cambridge Research) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: first of the Wiebe robust-optimisation-with-degradation papers; SKILL.md Heuristic 10 cites the later doi:10.1021/acs.iecr.9b01772 from this line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Integrated maintenance/production scheduling papers assume known, deterministic decay, while the condition-based maintenance (CBM) literature has rich stochastic degradation models but optimises one unit at a time (pp. 2-3); the closest prior work computes maintenance cost before optimisation and so cannot let operating strategy affect degradation (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Model each unit's degradation signal as a Lévy process whose increments depend on discrete operating modes (Eq. 5, p. 6); write the health model as constraints (Eq. 6) and robustify with a box uncertainty set and a linear decision rule for the signal (Eqs. 7-9, pp. 7-8); prove a cheap deterministic surrogate with worst-case increments is robust-feasible (Theorem 1, p. 9); estimate failure probability by Monte Carlo over operating-mode sequences generated by a frequency or Markov-chain model fitted by logistic regression on short-horizon solutions (Algorithms 1-2, Eqs. 14-15, pp. 12-15); choose the uncertainty-set size α by Bayesian optimisation of cost plus expected failure cost (Eq. 16, pp. 15-16). Contributions listed as five bullets (p. 4).
  - Transferable: turn the robustness parameter into a one-dimensional outer black-box problem (expected total cost vs α) and solve it with BO instead of asking the user for a violation probability (pp. 15-16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: SMAX (failure at a threshold) and AGAN (as-good-as-new after maintenance) (pp. 5-6); piecewise-constant operating modes (p. 6); uncertainty revealed once (simplifying assumption, p. 8); independent increments implied by the box set (p. 9); each unit fails at most once in the horizon (p. 16).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: Theorem 1 by explicit construction of the decision-rule coefficients (Eq. 11: [s_t]_0 reset at maintenance, [s_t]_k = count of mode-k periods since last maintenance) and a constraint-by-constraint check using d̃ ≥ 0 (p. 9; Appendix B, pp. 32-33). Stated limit: "only guarantees solution feasibility, not optimality" (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: Five STN instances (toy, Kondili P1 and three literature instances; Table 1, p. 20), Pyomo + CPLEX 12.7.1, 1-5 min limits, 2% gap; robust vs deterministic surrogate 25 runs each (Fig. 7, pp. 22-23); estimator quality by three metrics against rolling-horizon solutions (Table 2, p. 26) and against an a-priori bound (pp. 24-25); BO vs random search, five runs each (pp. 27-29); code public under MIT licence (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Layers: modelling, a feasibility theorem, data-driven estimators, BO for the set size. Limits stated: box sets ignore correlation (p. 9); the surrogate may be conservative for more complex sets, which "require solving the robust problem" (p. 23); the bound estimates miss up to 48% of points but by little (pp. 26-27); costs are noisy because solutions are not optimal (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: α parameterises a family from the deterministic model (α = 0.5) to the fully robust one (α = 0) (pp. 9-10), and the parameter is then removed from the user by an outer BO on expected cost (pp. 15-16, 27-29). This is Method 2 step 7 (delete the parameter), done by optimisation rather than by a structural result.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (partial): estimators compared against independent rolling-horizon simulations and against a published a-priori bound (pp. 24-26), with repeated runs, a random-search control for BO (pp. 27-29) and public code (p. 20).
- New-pattern candidate: "prove the cheap surrogate feasible, measure the rest": theorem for feasibility only, optimality gap of the surrogate measured empirically (pp. 9, 22-23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove that replacing uncertain nonnegative increments by their maximum yields a robust-feasible solution, by constructing the decision-rule coefficients explicitly (p. 9; pp. 32-33).
- Grade a probability estimator as an upper bound with separate metrics: overall RMS, fraction of points above the bound, RMS of only those points (Eqs. 21a-c, p. 26).
- Generate training data for a cheap surrogate of the long-horizon schedule by solving only the short scheduling horizon on Latin-hypercube demands (pp. 19-20).

**Quotes**:
- "Theorem 1 only guarantees solution feasibility, not optimality." (p. 9)
- "Noticeably, none of these authors make use of the wealth of knowledge regarding degradation modeling and inference from data available from the CBM literature." (p. 3)

### S058 · Satisfiability modulo theories for process systems engineering (Computers & Chemical Engineering 113, 2018, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2018.03.004)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-13 read, §§1-5 including Lemmas 1-2 and their proofs, pp. 7-8; Appendix A table and the reference list, pp. 13-17, not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Mistry (first), A. Callia D'Iddio, M. Huth (Imperial Computing, verification), R. Misener (corresponding) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: position-plus-testbed paper that generalises S155; ManyOpt is Callia D'Iddio and Huth's solver, presented elsewhere (pp. 1, 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: PSE and computer science both built logic/optimisation hybrids, but different applications "led to a divergence in mathematical developments" (p. 1). Positioned through a strengths table: SMT strong in deductive reasoning, propositional satisfiability and warm starting; MINLP strong in nonlinear functions and optimising an objective (Table 1, p. 2).
  - Transferable: open a cross-community paper with a two-column strengths/weaknesses table whose rows say why each side needs the other (Table 1, p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three testbeds (p. 1): (i) 2-D bin packing solved by SMT used as a feasibility oracle: descending and ascending strategies and an SMT-based branch-and-bound that branches on the unfixed items of an unsatisfiable core, with dominance filtering of item subsets and push-back cuts (pp. 5-8); (ii) unsatisfiable cores for LBBD cut generation and as "model explainers" that localise implementation bugs (pp. 9-10, Fig. 7); (iii) SMT-based MINLP solvers, mainly ManyOpt (parallel feature vectors over pre-processing, integrality management, continuous-relaxation search and feasibility checking, pp. 11-12).
  - Transferable: debug a B&B implementation by asking an SMT solver for the unsatisfiable core of a node that is known to be feasible, after filtering trusted constraints such as bounds (pp. 9-10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: MINLP with inferable finite bounds and finite function images (p. 2); 2BP oriented, free cutting (p. 5); ManyOpt needs polynomial nonlinearities only and uses exact rational arithmetic (pp. 11, 13).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: Lemma 1 (push-back cuts preserve the best objective) by contradiction with an explicit bin-permutation map f2 (pp. 7-8); Lemma 2 (identical items can also be pushed back) by a bin-swap map (p. 8). Observation (mine): in Example 1 (p. 3) the stated termination point x1 = 5 violates the conjunct x1 ≤ 1, and with x1 ≤ 1, x2 ≤ 2, x3 ≤ 3 the sum cannot reach 10, so formula (1) as printed is unsatisfiable.
- **D5 Experiments**: 500 Berkey-Wang 2BP instances (10 classes), CPLEX 12.7, Gurobi 6.0.3, Z3 4.5.1, one thread, 1 h; time and heuristic performance profiles plus solved counts by class, with Pisinger-Sigurd's specialised method as a "can solve" line (pp. 8-10, Table 3); ManyOpt warm-start demonstrations on prob03 and haverly from MINLPLib2 (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Position and demonstration ("The purpose and novelty of this paper is to show the broad applicability of SMT to PSE", p. 1). Limits stated: about half the 2BP instances solved in the hour; classes with many small items are hard for all methods (pp. 8-9); SMT handles polynomials only (p. 13); ManyOpt "frequently slower" than state-of-the-art MINLP solvers (p. 13); warm starting carries no guarantee (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (step 7): the bespoke Pisinger-Sigurd method is plotted as a "can solve" line to show what specialised methods achieve (p. 8), and ManyOpt's slowness is conceded (p. 13).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (step 8 and scope): per-class solved counts reveal where SMT-BB loses (classes 2, 4, 6) and why (large cores) (p. 9); one-thread limits for all solvers (p. 9).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: structure is exploited (bin-permutation symmetry, dominance of unsatisfiable item sets, pp. 6-8), but through the logic solver's cores rather than a detector inside a global MINLP solver.
- New-pattern candidate: "unsatisfiable core as a debugging instrument" for optimisation implementations (pp. 9-10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Promote local cuts to global ones by adding the fixing item as an antecedent when moving to sibling branches (p. 8).
- Show the value of a solver feature (warm start) with a concrete what-if: add one constraint after a solve and count oracle calls warm vs cold (37 + 19 vs 39 Z3 calls, p. 13).
- Distinguish "rigorous" (exact arithmetic) from "complete" (floating-point) global methods in Neumaier's sense when comparing a new solver to ANTIGONE (p. 13).

**Read level**: full (pp. 1-11 read; Literature Cited pp. 11-12 not read; Supporting Information not available, not read). Version read: early-view PDF dated 2017 with pages 1-12.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A nominal global optimum ($277 per RBC unit) existed but "did not incorporate uncertainty and therefore we had no way of evaluating if our design decisions would stand up to scrutiny" (Discussion, p. 10). Cell therapy needs four things petrochemical optimisation does not: cellular kinetics, spatial scales from cell to layout, temporal scales from metabolism to differentiation, and uncertainty/heterogeneity (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A six-component toolkit (Fig. 1, p. 2) applied in a fixed recipe (Fig. 2, p. 3): high-fidelity models, one- and two-parameter sensitivity of the optimisation output (not of a simulation), global superstructure optimisation, robust optimisation, and NPV market analysis. Model uncertainty is converted into parameter uncertainty or a bounded cost change: CFD stress-tests the Krogh approximation (p. 8, Fig. 9), and swapping in a competing hematopoiesis model moves the price from $277 to $306 with the same design decisions (p. 8).
  - Transferable: bound model-form error by re-solving with a plausibly pessimistic alternative model and reporting the change in optimum and in design decisions (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Strict uncertainty (ranges, no distributions) except material properties, so worst-case robust optimisation (p. 5); 30 uncertain parameters at 50/90/110/150% of nominal where no error bars exist (p. 7); model kept to about 200 variables so one global solve takes about 300 s (p. 5); NPV assumptions (i)-(v), e.g. a two-year horizon to qualify as disruptive (p. 9).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a (the robust counterpart is in the Supporting Information, p. 6, not read).
- **D5 Experiments**: Computational study: 9 of 30 parameters move the global optimum by at least 10% (p. 7); bivariate sensitivities show the strongest nonlinear interactions at the smallest (cellular) scales (p. 8); robust optimum $383 per unit with more reactors and growth factors and a different ceramic/polymeric fibre mix (p. 8); NPV scenarios at $800, $500 and $225 per unit (pp. 9-10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Claims the first use of superstructure and robust optimisation in stem-cell tissue engineering (p. 10). Limits stated: worst-case estimate is pessimistic, and more bioreactor data would allow "more reasonable uncertainty rules" (p. 8). Observation (mine): the abstract puts the price of robustness at "approximately 15%" over the nominal solution (p. 1), while the Results give $277 → $383, about 38% (p. 8).
