### S066 · Probabilistic predictions for partial least squares using bootstrap (AIChE Journal 2023, doi:10.1002/aic.18071)

**Read level**: full main text (pp. 1-13: abstract, §1-§5, Algorithms 1-2, all figure captions, CRediT and acknowledgments; Notation p. 12 skimmed; appendices A-D and references pp. 13-16 not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: James Odgers, Chrysoula Kappatou, Salvador García Muñoz (Eli Lilly), Sarah Filippi (corresponding author) (p. 1); Misener is 3rd of 5. CRediT gives her "equal" roles in conceptualisation, methodology, supervision and writing (p. 12) · **Position in trajectory**: an Eli Lilly / EPSRC Prosperity Partnership project (p. 13). It sits next to her surrogate and uncertainty work in pharmaceutical process design, with no global optimisation in the paper.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entered from a tool practitioners already use: PLS is how chemical manufacturing handles high-dimensional correlated data (p. 1), but its prediction uncertainty is hard to model "because of the nonlinear effect of the observed data on the latent space" (abstract, p. 1). The standard linearised (zeroth-order, student-t) estimators date from the 1980s-90s and were approximations chosen for "limited computational power" (p. 2). Probabilistic PLS gives up NIPALS's "speed, familiarity and memory efficiency" (p. 3). Bootstrap by residuals ignores input variation and so misses the uncertainty in which part of the input the latent space selects (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when practitioners trust an algorithm (NIPALS), keep it unchanged and wrap the uncertainty method around it, instead of replacing it with a reformulation that "does not provide the same solution" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Bootstrap by pairs, following Fushiki: resample (x, y) pairs B times, rerun the standard NIPALS on each resample, and fit a Gaussian with that resample's MLE noise variance. The predictive density is the average of the B Gaussians, eqs. (15)-(21), Algorithm 1 (pp. 4-6). Input-dependent uncertainty comes from the spread of the bootstrap means, not from per-model variances (p. 5). Design Space probability uses a difference of normal c.d.f.s for 1-D outputs, eq. (23), and Monte Carlo sampling for multi-D outputs, Algorithm 2 (p. 6). Changed relative to Denham: pairs instead of residuals, so latent-space uncertainty is captured (p. 4). Unchanged: NIPALS itself (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Three stated assumptions: normally distributed prediction error, PLS being a reasonable model for the data, and training data drawn from a distribution similar to future data (p. 2). The authors note they know of no result that PLS parameters are a joint MLE (p. 4). They concede that Design Space problems "often do not follow the latent structure implicitly assumed by PLS" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis devices**: n/a for proofs. Derivation device: the bootstrap predictive distribution as an expectation over bootstrap data sets drawn from the empirical distribution, eqs. (17)-(19), approximated by Monte Carlo (pp. 4-5). The student-t baseline is derived in Appendix C (not read).
- **D5 Experiments**: Five case studies against the widely used zeroth-order method (p. 5): a synthetic 2-D example with a known true Design Space (N = 40, B = 1000; pp. 7-8), Michael addition (simulated, 25 grid points; pp. 7-9), an industrial reductive amination with 15 inputs and MCMC sampling inside a Knowledge Space (p. 9), high-shear wet granulation with leave-one-out (pp. 9-11), and confidential spectroscopy with N = 50 and N = 1000 (p. 11). Comparisons are visual (contours, violin plots); no coverage statistic is reported. The spectroscopy case is a tie, reported as such (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds explicitly on Denham and Fushiki (p. 4). Older methods are judged as products of their compute era (p. 2), not as wrong.
- **D7 Writing**: The claim is scoped in the setup: "especially strong ... in low-data settings that do not follow a standard linear model" (p. 5). For reductive amination, the authors admit "it is not possible to comment on the accuracy of this Design Space" (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Methodological and application contribution, with code released except for the confidential cases (p. 6); the data are restricted to Eli Lilly (p. 13). Stated limit: the method assumes a linear model plus noise and "is unable to distinguish" noise uncertainty from model-mismatch uncertainty (p. 12). Future work: model-mismatch uncertainty, experimental-space identification and Real Time Release Testing (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (the partner's trained model is the problem) ⚠ variant: step 1, "start from the model the partner trusts", is kept. The pharma partner's PLS/NIPALS model is preserved for "speed, familiarity" (p. 3), but it is wrapped with uncertainty, not encoded as optimisation constraints (pp. 4-6).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor) ⚠ variant: one widely used baseline (p. 5), a ground-truth check only where the truth is known (synthetic and simulated cases, pp. 7-9), and the tie written in (p. 11). There is no quantitative coverage metric and no second competitor (Denham's bootstrap by residuals is discussed but not run, p. 4).
- Heuristic 10 (make uncertainty explicit) ✅ evidence: the whole paper; the decision use is accepting or rejecting product "without making (invalid) assumptions regarding the trustworthiness of the predictions" (p. 12).
- Heuristic 2 (partner data stays inside) ✅ evidence: the reductive amination and spectroscopy data and code are withheld "for confidentiality reasons" (p. 6, p. 11). A public synthetic example and Michael addition stand in.
- new-pattern candidate: keep the practitioner's standard estimator and wrap uncertainty around it, rather than replacing it with a Bayesian reformulation (pp. 3-4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Validate an uncertainty method in two regimes: first where the true answer is computable (a synthetic generator, giving a "true Design Space", p. 7), then on real data, where only coverage of held-out observations can be checked (leave-one-out, pp. 9-11).
- Include a case where the incumbent is known to work well (spectroscopy, p. 11) and report the tie: it shows that the new method does not lose where the old one is trusted.

**Quotes**:
- "The novelty of our method is in how to weight different sets of PLS parameters according to how well they describe the data." (p. 2)
- "One point which we would like to acknowledge is that Design Space identification problems often do not follow the latent structure implicitly assumed by PLS." (p. 7)

### S071 · Data augmentation driven by optimization for membrane separation process synthesis (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2023, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2023.108342)

**Read level**: full main text (pp. 1-13: abstract, §1-§5, Tables 1-9, figure captions, CRediT; appendices A (membrane model) and B (optimisation model) pp. 14-17 and references not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Bernardetta Addis (corresponding author), Christophe Castel, Amalia Macali, Veronica Piccialli (p. 1); Misener is 4th of 5. CRediT: she "Wrote the introduction, Literature review, Supervised the development of the ADORO data-augmentation strategy" (p. 13) · **Position in trajectory**: a LORIA/Lorraine–Sapienza collaboration in the optimisation-over-trained-models line, here a shallow ANN embedded in an NLP solved by multistart local search (Knitro), not by a deterministic global solver.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** (her section, per CRediT): An ANN surrogate replaces the discretised ODEs of a membrane module to avoid numerical trouble (p. 2). The catch is that "ANNs may have difficulty extrapolating to new regimes", and optimal decision-making "may select regions where the trained ANN is inaccurate" (p. 1). Accuracy matters because every optimised design is re-simulated in MEMSIC: overestimated purity gives a discarded design, underestimated purity an overly expensive one (p. 3). A large 343k-point data set gives an accurate model, but cross-validation and training take more than 5 days (p. 5, Table 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: ADORO densifies the data only where the optimiser goes. Run multistart over several purity and recovery scenarios, simulate every stationary point, keep the points whose surrogate–simulator discrepancy d_q exceeds ε (eq. 2), add random perturbations around them, and retrain with a warm start. Steps S1-S4 are on pp. 9-10. "The core idea is that the model accuracy must be high not everywhere" (p. 3). The baseline is literature-standard adversarial augmentation (AAD), which perturbs every point along the loss gradient and doubles the data set each iteration (eq. 1, p. 7). Unchanged: the one-hidden-layer sigmoid network, chosen because it enters the NLP as equality constraints and "the single layer keeps these constraints simple" (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Three-input, two-output single-membrane surrogate trained on MEMSIC simulations (pp. 3-4). The design superstructure follows Ramírez-Santos et al., and the full NLP is in Appendix B (not read; p. 4). Points where the simulator fails are learned by a linear SVM (F1 92.4%), and its hyperplane, with a tolerance, is imposed as a constraint so that the optimiser stays in the reliable region (pp. 3-4). The case study is two-stage CO2/H2 separation at 90/95/99% purity (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis devices**: n/a (no proofs). Algorithmic devices: the SVM feasibility-region constraint (p. 4) and a discrepancy-filtered perturbation set around stationary points (eq. 2, p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: Every best design is re-simulated in MEMSIC, and simulated purity, H2 errors and flow errors are reported for grid / Latin / random sampling (Table 1, p. 5), for 10k-125k data sets (Table 4, p. 7), AAD (Table 5, p. 9) and ADORO (Table 7, p. 10). Cost is normalised to the 343k reference (p. 11). Total time includes model building, compared with the discretisation baseline (Table 9, p. 13). The ADORO runs starting from 50k gave "disappointing results" and were omitted (p. 10). No repetitions over random seeds are reported.
- **D6 Positioning**: Placed against adversarial augmentation from image and NLP ML (p. 3) and process-systems sampling work (Cremaschi, Ierapetritou; p. 2). The conclusion relates it to batch and multi-fidelity BO, including Folch et al. 2022 (SnAKe) (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing**: The introduction opens with the decision problem, not the ML method (p. 1). A counter-intuitive result is explained, not hidden: better box-plot errors do not give feasible designs, because "Small errors in the prediction of two output variables can impact strongly the shape of the feasible region" (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and application contribution. ADORO reaches the 343k model's cost with at most 50k points and about 7 h of model building, against more than 50 h for pure sampling (Table 9, p. 13). Data cannot be shared (p. 13). Limits shown in the tables: a single case study, a single augmentation iteration (p. 10), 10 + 5k failing at 99% purity (p. 10), and no future-work list beyond reusing the augmented data across purity levels (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (the trained model is the problem: constraints) ✅ evidence: the trained ANN, "shape and weights", is inserted into the NLP in place of the discretised ODEs (abstract, p. 1; p. 3). The architecture is chosen for the optimisation model, not for prediction (p. 3). The SVM hyperplane keeping the optimiser in the valid region (p. 4) is a variant of step 4, "keep the optimiser near the data" (a learned failure boundary instead of a PCA subspace).
- Method 4 (judge a component inside the whole) ✅ evidence (analogue): data sets and augmentation schemes are graded by the simulated feasibility and cost of the optimiser's final design, not by training error (pp. 5, 7). The authors show the two disagree ("These results seem in contradiction with the quality presented in the box plots, but it is not the case", p. 7).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor) ✅ evidence: every returned design is checked against the simulator, with over- and under-estimated purity separated (pp. 4-5). Timings include building the model (Table 9, p. 13), matching her stated "timings where we are including our building". The 50k ADORO failure is reported as omitted (p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: optimisation-driven data augmentation. Densify the surrogate's training data at multistart stationary points where surrogate and simulator disagree, rather than everywhere (pp. 3, 9-10, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Grade any surrogate by re-simulating the optimiser's answer, and report over- and under-estimation separately, since they cost differently: one gives an infeasible design, the other an expensive one (pp. 3, 5).
- Learn the simulator's failure region with a simple classifier and impose its boundary as a constraint, so that the optimiser cannot exploit regions where the training data are meaningless (pp. 3-4).
- Augment around all multistart stationary points across several requirement scenarios, not only around the global optimum, which "leads to generating too few additional data points" (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "The core idea is that the model accuracy must be high not everywhere, but only around some regions of interest, namely, the regions around the local optimum points." (p. 3)
- "focusing only on the global minimum point over the entire decision-making problem is therefore not enough to us" (p. 13)

### S083 · Practical path-based Bayesian optimization (NeurIPS 2023 Workshop RealML, arXiv:2312.00622)

**Read level**: full (pp. 1-12: all text, Algorithms 1-3, Tables 1-2, figure captions, appendices A-B; references pp. 7-9 skimmed).
**Role**: supplement (short workshop paper: 6 pages of main text plus appendix) · **Coauthors**: Jose Pablo Folch (first), James Odgers, Shiqiang Zhang, Robert M Lee, Behrang Shafei, David Walz (BASF SE), Calvin Tsay, Mark van der Wilk (p. 1); Misener is last of 9 · **Position in trajectory**: a direct follow-up to SnAKe (Folch et al., NeurIPS 2022; p. 2), the method she later described as having turned a global method into a local one (SKILL.md Taste, warning sign 3). Funded by EPSRC StatML CDT and BASF (p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entered from the practical limits of the group's own method. "SnAKe is limited in some practical aspects" (p. 2), listed as three numbered extensions: (I) the experiment budget must be fixed a priori; (II) single input changes are unbounded, which may be unsafe (e.g. temperature rate limits); (III) a single objective only (p. 2). Further limits are stated and left to others: poor performance in high dimensions and with budgets below 100, addressed by MONGOOSE (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three minimal modifications of SnAKe (pp. 3-4). Self-stopping (ssSnAKe): point deletion uses EI per unit cost below δ and, as a misspecification guard, keeps points whose posterior variance is still at least ν. The batch empties and the run stops (Algorithm 1, p. 3). Truncated SnAKe: a step longer than δMAX is cut to δMAX in the same direction (Algorithm 2, p. 4). MO-SnAKe: the Thompson-sampling batch is replaced by random-scalarisation Thompson sampling (linear or Tchebyshev; Algorithm 3, p. 4), chosen "for its computational simplicity" (p. 4). Unchanged: the TSP ordering of the batch and the point-deletion structure (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Cost = fixed experiment cost plus input-change cost, eq. (2) (p. 3). The rate-of-change constraint is eq. (3) (p. 3), emulated by a cost with a jump of 1 beyond δMAX (p. 5). The K objectives are modelled by independent GPs (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis devices**: n/a (no theory; no regret or stopping guarantee stated).
- **D5 Experiments**: Synthetic Branin2D, Hartmann3D and Hartmann6D, plus Shekel and SnAr (Summit) for multi-objective runs (p. 5). Each run is repeated 25 times from random initial conditions, plotted as ±0.5 standard deviations, with the average budget used printed in the legends (pp. 4-5). The authors also built a strengthened baseline, EIpu + Std (p. 3). A "true" Pareto front comes from NSGA-II (p. 6). Losses are reported: TrEI wins at small δMAX (p. 12, Fig. 4) and EIpu + Std is best on Hartmann3D (p. 10). The case where Random beats BO on IGD and MPFE for SnAr is explained with a relaxed Pareto front (p. 10, Table 2).
- **D6 Positioning**: Situated in path-based BO (Ramesh et al., Mutny et al., MONGOOSE, Paulson et al.; p. 2). SnAr is moved back to the multi-objective form that SnAKe had reduced to a single objective (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing**: A short paper, organised as one subsection per numbered limitation. The conclusion makes no claim beyond "empirically show the advantages" (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic extensions with an empirical check. Code is promised "after peer-review" in the SnAKe repository (p. 10). Limits are not discussed in the conclusion. Thresholds δ and ν have no selection rule in the text, and the ±0.5 std bands are narrow error bars (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (re-grade your own earlier work, step 8) ✅ evidence: the paper opens by listing its own method's practical limits (p. 2) and states where SnAKe "expends the entire budget and therefore incurs high costs for very small gains" (p. 5).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor) ✅ evidence for step 3 (strengthen the baseline): the authors improve the competitor EIpu with their own variance criterion, "we were able to significantly boost EIpu's performance, achieving comparable results to SnAKe" (p. 3). Also step 8, losses written in (pp. 10-12). ⚠ variant on scale: synthetic benchmarks, ±0.5 std bands, no statistical test (pp. 4-5).
- Method 2 (parameters) ⚠ variant: two new thresholds (δ, ν) and δMAX are introduced, with no robustness sweep for δ and ν reported. The only robustness shown is to the maximum budget (Fig. 1, p. 5; Fig. 3, p. 11).
- Heuristic 1 (choose by broken assumption) ✅ evidence: a fixed budget, unbounded moves and a single objective each collide with lab practice (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When adding a stopping rule to BO, add a model-uncertainty guard (do not stop while posterior variance at a candidate is high). Test the vanilla rule as a baseline and show that it stops too early (Algorithm 1, p. 3; Fig. 1, p. 5).
- Report "average budget used" next to regret, and rerun with a larger maximum budget to show that the self-stopping rule's spend does not scale with the cap (Fig. 1, p. 5).
- If a baseline wins on a metric for a structural reason (a near-horizontal Pareto front), show the metric's sensitivity with a relaxed definition instead of dropping the metric (p. 10, Table 2).

**Quotes**:
- "SnAKe is limited in some practical aspects." (p. 2)
- "Vanilla EIpu stops too early due to model misspecification; our modification that includes uncertainty criteria greatly improves performance." (p. 5)

### S098 · Gaussian processes for monitoring air-quality in Kampala (NeurIPS 2023 Workshop Tackling Climate Change with ML, arXiv:2311.16625)

**Read level**: full (pp. 1-8: all text, Tables 1-2, appendices A-E; references pp. 5-6 skimmed).
**Role**: supplement (workshop paper) · **Coauthors**: Clara Stoddart, Lauren Shrack, Richard Sserunjogi, Usman Abdul-Ganiy, Engineer Bainomugisha, Deo Okure (Makerere / AirQo), Jose Pablo Folch and Ruby Sedgwick (equal contribution) (p. 1); Misener is 7th of 9 · **Position in trajectory**: a collaboration that began at the Dagstuhl seminar on AI for Social Good, seminar 22091 (p. 7). It sits outside optimisation; GPs are the modelling layer of her group's BO work.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: PM2.5 is a major health threat. Sensors are expensive, so cities in low- and middle-income countries rely on sparse networks (abstract, p. 1). The model must be data-efficient and give uncertainty for decisions (p. 2). Prior GP air-quality studies used other pollutants as inputs, which AirQo's low-cost sensors do not measure, and only forecast (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A model-building study, not a new method: four choices are varied one at a time on top of each other. These are a periodic temporal kernel (daily × weekly) added to a spatial SE kernel, removal of training outliers beyond 1.5 IQR, weather inputs, and sparse approximations (SVGP with 100 inducing points; ST-SVGP) (p. 3; pp. 7-8). Two tasks: nowcasting by leave-one-site-out, and forecasting by holding out the last day (pp. 3-4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Gaussian noise GP regression (p. 2; Appendix C, p. 7). One month of calibrated hourly data from 66 sites (2 sites with fewer than 100 readings dropped) (pp. 3, 7). Weather comes from one station and is assumed valid citywide, which the authors flag (p. 8). Non-sparse models are trained on 1000 random points, averaged over 4 repeats (pp. 3, 8).
- **D4 Analysis devices**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: Cumulative tables (Tables 1-2, p. 4) with min, average and max RMSE across sites. Outliers are kept in the test set "for fair comparison" (p. 4). Negative and mixed results are reported: weather inputs give no clear gain, and ST-SVGP improves nowcasting but worsens forecasting (p. 4). No uncertainty calibration metric is reported, although uncertainty was the stated reason for choosing GPs (p. 2).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against Reggente, Jang and Cheng (p. 2); differentiated by the inputs available and the nowcasting task.
- **D7 Writing**: A short empirical note. The conclusion names the open item: a non-stationary kernel and sensor placement (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An application study with code and data promised at a public GitHub repository (p. 7). Limits: one month of data, a single weather station (p. 8), and similar max RMSE across nowcasting models, suggesting more sensors are needed (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (cumulative ablation, step 2) ⚠ variant: modelling choices are stacked one row at a time in Tables 1-2 (p. 4), the same design as her solver ablations but on a prediction pipeline rather than inside a B&B.
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor) ✅ evidence (light): test outliers are kept "for fair comparison" (p. 4), min/average/max across sites are reported rather than a mean only (p. 4), and negative results are written in (p. 4).
- Heuristic 9 (release per paper) ✅ evidence: "The full code and dataset will be available" (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Clean only the training set and leave test outliers in place, and say so. Otherwise the reported error flatters the cleaning step (p. 4; p. 8).
- Report the worst site (max RMSE) next to the average. It shows where the model fails (nowcasting max RMSE stays between 85.86 and 87.26 across all models, Table 1, p. 4), which motivates more sensors or non-stationary kernels (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "We do not remove outliers from the test set for fair comparison." (p. 4)
- "The additional variables don’t seem to lead to any clear improvement in model performance." (p. 4)
