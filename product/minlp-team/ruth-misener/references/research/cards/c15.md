### S090 · Limeade: Let integer molecular encoding aid (Computers & Chemical Engineering 199 2025, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2025.109115; read as arXiv:2411.16623v1)

**Read level**: full (all 32 file pages: main text pp. 1-19, acknowledgements p. 20, Appendix A with all constraints C1-C25 pp. 21-23; reference list pp. 24-32 skimmed). The text read is the arXiv v1 preprint, not the journal version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shiqiang Zhang, Christian W. Feldmann, Frederik Sandfort, Miriam Mathea (BASF SE), Juan S. Campos; Misener is the corresponding author (∗, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a spin-off of her group's GNN-in-MIP molecular design line (refs 42-43 = Zhang et al. NeurIPS 2023 and Comput. Chem. Eng. 2024, p. 28), turned into a lightweight generator for the BASF partner (funded by BASF and the BASF/RAEng chair, p. 19).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: MIP-based computer-aided molecular design (CAMD) encodes the molecule space plus a score function such as a GNN, but "both the extremely large search space and complicated scoring process limit the use of MIP-based CAMD to specific and tiny problems", and an optimal molecule "may not be meaningful in practice if scores are imperfect" (abstract, p. 1). The two named limitations of MIP+ML hybrids are weak scalability from nonconvex property models and molecular feasibility (p. 4). Entry point: "we take a step back and think how to exploit the applicability and compatibility of MIP framework" (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when your own optimise-over-the-model line hits a scaling wall, ask whether the user needs the optimum at all; if the score is imperfect, the feasible set may be the product (pp. 1, 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Drop the objective. Use the MIP feasible domain to approximate the desired molecular space ("more constraints result in a smaller feasible domain and a larger probability of generating desired molecules", p. 4) and let Gurobi's solution pool (PoolSearchMode=2, PoolSolutions = number of molecules) enumerate feasible molecules, decoded to SMILES (pp. 11-12). Kept from earlier work: the atom/bond feature matrix and structural constraints C1-C19 of Zhang et al. (p. 5; App. A pp. 21-23). Changed: (i) automatic translation of SMARTS patterns into linear constraints for substructure inclusion and exclusion, the "key contributions" (pp. 12-13); (ii) exclusion as a count of matched components S(Vn) < M over all ordered n-subsets (eq. 1, p. 9; Table 2 components, p. 9); (iii) inclusion with a big-M indicator σ(Vn) (eqs. 3-5, p. 10), restricted to windows of consecutive atom indices so that only O(N - n) constraints and binaries are added (eq. 6, p. 11); (iv) symmetry breaking switched off because inclusion assigns consecutive indices, with a post-hoc validation step that removes symmetric duplicates and checks deferred substructures ("check later", p. 16); (v) IIS-based infeasibility diagnosis when the model has no more than 10^5 constraints (p. 16); (vi) batch-based generation with different random seeds (default batch 100, 600 s per batch) because generation time grew slowly with pool size and pools were full of symmetric solutions (p. 17). A Pyomo version is released so open-source solvers can be used (p. 11).
  - Transferable: when a constraint family is exponential (P(N, n) subsets), restrict it to a structured sub-family that keeps feasibility of what the user asked for (consecutive-index windows) and say in words that it is a restriction (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Heavy atoms only, hydrogens implicit (p. 5); fixed molecule size, the diagonal of A is not a variable (p. 6); atom features: type, neighbour count, hydrogen count, double/triple-bond flags, F = 15 for {C, N, O, S} (Table 1, p. 6); aromatic bonds are not supported (p. 13). Feasibility is mathematical only: "By feasibility, we mean the mathematical feasibility of our optimization formulation, which is not saying that Limeade could generate practically desirable molecules." (p. 4). The case study's chemical-feasibility proxy: a molecule is infeasible if it contains a radius-1 Morgan fingerprint occurring fewer than 5 times in ChEMBL (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis and devices**: No theorems. Named devices: (a) substructure matching linearised as a count of matched linear "components" (bond present/absent, bond order, atom-type set, neighbour count, hydrogen count; Table 2, p. 9) so a pattern match becomes S(Vn) = M (eqs. 1-2, p. 9); (b) big-M existence indicator plus a covering constraint Σσ ≥ 1 (eqs. 3-5, p. 10); (c) a back-of-envelope probability argument to justify omitting large exclusions: about 5×10^4 six-atom molecules after structural, bound and symmetry constraints, so a given substructure appears with probability about 2×10^-5 (p. 10); (d) the asymmetry argument for why inclusion cannot be deferred like exclusion: "generating several unwanted molecules is much less critical than when most generated molecules are unwanted" (p. 10); (e) lexicographic symmetry-breaking constraints C24-C25 with 2^(F-f-1) and 2^(N-u-1) weights (p. 8; App. A.6, p. 23). The relaxed inclusion (6) is argued, not proved, to preserve what users need (p. 11).
  - Transferable: before encoding an expensive constraint, estimate how often a feasible solution violates it; if rare, filter after generation instead (p. 10, p. 16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Two demonstrations, no baselines. (1) Aspirin retrieval (C9H8O4) in four constraint-adding steps, 1000 molecules generated per step, 10 plotted; after step 4 only 6 feasible molecules remain and aspirin is among them (pp. 13-15, Fig. 2). (2) Case study, N = 20 atoms from {C, N, O, S} with composition bounds: iteration 1 gives 957 unique molecules of which 2 are feasible; excluding [CH0] gives 970 unique, 62 feasible; excluding heteroatom-heteroatom and heteroatom-C-heteroatom patterns gives 855 unique, 787 feasible; at 10^5 requested molecules, 63,973 unique and 55,498 feasible (about 86%) (pp. 18-19). Each iteration lists the top-10 uncommon fingerprints with counts and uses them to pick the next exclusion (pp. 18-19). Not reported: generation times (only the 600 s per-batch default, pp. 12, 17), seed-to-seed variation, any comparison with a generative model or another CAMD tool.
  - Transferable: an iterative constraint-refinement protocol: generate a batch, score against a reference database, list the most frequent failure patterns with counts, exclude them, repeat, and report the feasible fraction per iteration (pp. 17-19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Uses Alshehri et al.'s knowledge-driven vs data-driven split (pp. 2-3) and says the boundary is unclear, highlighting hybrids that link MIP-based CAMD with optimisation over trained models, including her group's trees, ReLU-network, OMLT and GNN work (p. 3; refs 47-57, pp. 29-30). Concedes priority twice: the constraint-adding principle "is classic in MIP-based CAMD literature" (p. 4) and the basic functionalities "are already in the literature, but we aggregate and release these functionalities in open-source software" (p. 12). Claimed novelty is the bridge: requirements and molecular space "automatically processed into a MIP" so users need no optimisation expertise (p. 5).
  - Transferable: state what is classic and what is yours in two separate sentences; it makes a tool paper's contribution reviewable (pp. 4, 12).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Tool-paper style: a three-line Python demo (p. 12), code listings for every function (pp. 12-14, 16-19), error messages printed verbatim (pp. 16-17). Claims are hedged and scoped (the aspirin constraints "do not have practical meanings", p. 14). The conclusion asserts that examples "show the efficiency" of Limeade (p. 19) without any timing data in the paper. Copy-editing slips in v1: "non-trivility" (p. 4), "bounds" for bonds (p. 12), "only appears at a few molecules" (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: software (Limeade v1.0, MIT licence, GitHub cog-imperial, p. 4) plus the inclusion/exclusion formulations (pp. 9-11). Limits written in the text: fixed molecule size (p. 6), no aromatic bonds (p. 13), large exclusions only checked afterwards (pp. 9-10, 16), symmetric duplicates possible (p. 16), so the number of molecules returned is usually below NumSolutions (p. 16); IIS diagnosis only in the Gurobi version, the Pyomo version prints "Infeasible model" only (p. 16). No future-work paragraph in the conclusion (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (the partner's trained model is the problem; then a toolkit): variant ⚠. Same pipeline (BASF partner, MIP encoding of molecules, release as a pip-style tool with an open-source solver path, pp. 4, 11, 19) but the trained score model is removed: "Instead of pursuing optimality" the MIP becomes a generator (p. 1). Method 3's step "solve exactly where possible" is given up on purpose because scores may be imperfect (p. 1).
- Method 4 (the default follows the evidence): evidence ✅. Symmetry breaking, a contribution of her own group (ref 42), is switched off by default in this tool because it conflicts with substructure inclusion and matters less for generation than for optimisation; a cheap post-hoc dedupe replaces it (pp. 11, 16).
- Heuristic 7 (write do-not-use conditions next to the method): evidence ✅. "By feasibility, we mean the mathematical feasibility ... not saying that Limeade could generate practically desirable molecules" (p. 4).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): contradiction ✗ (scope-limited). A tool paper with no baseline, no timings and a single case study (pp. 13-19), while the conclusion claims efficiency (p. 19).
- new-pattern candidate: "generate, don't optimise": use the MIP solution pool as a constrained sampler when the objective model is untrustworthy (pp. 1, 4, 11).
- new-pattern candidate: "encode cheap, check later": constraints whose encoding cost is exponential are deferred to a validation filter, justified by an estimated violation probability (pp. 9-10, 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Convert a pattern-match requirement into "number of matched linear components equals M", which yields linear exclusion (S < M) and big-M inclusion constraints (pp. 8-10).
- Restrict an existence disjunction over all subsets to consecutive-index windows to cut binaries from P(N, n) to O(N - n) (p. 11).
- Return an IIS of user-named constraints ("upper bound of rings", "include C1=CC=CC=C1") so non-expert users can see which requirements contradict (pp. 16-17).
- Generate in seeded batches rather than one large solution pool when pools fill with symmetric solutions (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "Instead of pursuing optimality, this paper exploits the ability of MIP in molecular generation and proposes Limeade as an end-to-end tool from real-world needs to feasible molecules." (p. 1)
- "Such negligible possibility does not merit more efforts on the generation process: if the MIP generation procedure generates a molecule with an unwanted substructure, we just remove it from the candidates." (p. 10)

### S093 · Global optimization of graph acquisition functions for neural architecture search (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2505.23640)

**Read level**: full (all 19 file pages: main text pp. 1-9, Appendix A encoding and full proof of Theorem 1 pp. 13-16, Appendix B kernel encoding pp. 16-17, Appendix C settings, baselines and extra results pp. 17-19; references pp. 10-12 skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yilin Xie, Shiqiang Zhang, Jixiang Qing, Calvin Tsay (corresponding author, p. 1); Misener 4th of 5; acknowledgements note her concurrent Amazon Scholar appointment and that the work was done at Imperial (p. 10) · **Position in trajectory**: extends BoGrape (Xie et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.05642) from strongly connected graphs to NAS DAGs (pp. 2, 4); the graph branch of "optimise the acquisition function globally as a MIP" (Method 3 step 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Graph BO is strong for NAS, but most work develops surrogates and kernels while "the acquisition optimization, as a discrete optimization task over graph structures, is not well studied" (abstract, p. 1). Sample-based and evolutionary acquisition optimisers must hard-code validity into sampling/mutation and give "no theoretical guarantee about the optimality of solutions" (pp. 1-2). BoGrape globally optimises graph acquisition functions but requires strong connectivity, while neural architectures are weakly connected DAGs (p. 2).
  - Transferable: find the step in a popular ML pipeline that everyone solves by sampling (here acquisition optimisation) and ask whether a MIP can solve it exactly; that is where an optimisation group has an edge (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Encode the whole graph space, not just adjacency, as MIP variables: node existence and edges A, reachability r, shortest distance d (with n meaning infinity), and shortest-path membership δ (Table 1, p. 5), tied by the linear system (Graph-Encoding) (p. 5). Then (i) drop BoGrape's connectivity requirement and show that adding symmetry constraints and r_uv ≥ A_uu + A_vv - 1 recovers BoGrape's encoding (p. 4); (ii) restrict to NAS: DAG via r_uv + r_vu ≤ 1 (p. 5), node-labelled one-source/one-sink DAGs with at most E edges (eq. 1, NAS-Bench-101 = n 7, E 9, Ln 5) and edge-labelled DAGs (eq. 2, NAS-Bench-201 = n 4, Le 4) (pp. 5-6); (iii) a general kernel as a weighted sum of structure (shortest-path), node-label and edge-label kernels, with a simplified edge kernel that uses fixed node indexing (pp. 6-7) and an exponential variant (p. 7); (iv) LCB minimised by Gurobi with the best 5 pool solutions as the batch (pp. 9, 17). Unchanged from BoGrape: the shortest-path kernel and node-label encodings (App. B, pp. 16-17).
  - Transferable: build the most general encoding first, prove it exact, then obtain the previous special case and the target domain by adding constraints; one theorem then covers all of them (pp. 4-6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: All nodes labelled differently to avoid isomorphism from indexing (p. 4); node counts in [n0, n] (p. 4); DAGs with one source and one sink, the "most classic setting in cell-based NAS" (p. 5); GP with shortest-path kernels and LCB (p. 2); kernel hyperparameters initialised at 1 within [0.01, 100], β^(1/2) = 3, Gurobi TimeLimit 1800 s per MIP (p. 17). Variable graph size breaks the constant kernel normalisation, so for NAS-Bench-101 two MIPs with N = 6 and 7 are solved, justified by "architectures with more nodes usually have better performance" (p. 17). Main results are in the deterministic setting (accuracies averaged over seeds), noisy setting in App. C (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: Theorem 1: a bijection between the feasible set of (Graph-Encoding) with sizes [n0, n] and all graphs with node counts in [n0, n] (p. 4; proof pp. 14-16). Proof skeleton: (a) list necessary logical conditions C1-C8 from the definitions (existence, initialisation, edges, distance-reachability compatibility, path-reachability compatibility, path construction, triangle inequality) and rewrite each implication as linear big-M constraints (pp. 13-14); (b) Lemma 1: the true values of any graph are feasible (surjection, p. 14); (c) reduce smaller graphs to the n-node case using C1, C2, C7 (p. 15); (d) injection by induction on min(d_uv(G), d_uv), with Case I (the graph's distance is the minimum) and Case II (the variable's distance is the minimum), each split on δ = 1 / δ = 0 with contradiction arguments (pp. 15-16). Kernel linearisation devices: indicator variables d^s_uv and P^c to turn squared path counts into linear sums (pp. 16-17); x² = x for binaries (pp. 6-7).
  - Transferable: to prove a MIP encoding of a combinatorial object exact, prove necessity by direct check and sufficiency by induction on a natural integer quantity of the object (here the shortest distance) with a two-sided case split (pp. 14-16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: NAS-Bench-101 (about 423k architectures) and NAS-Bench-201 (15,625 architectures, CIFAR-10/100, ImageNet-16-120) (p. 7). Kernel study: 50 training and 400 test architectures, 20 replications, RMSE, MNLL and Spearman for RW, WL, WL-edge, SP and ESP kernels (Table 3, p. 8); WL and ESP best, SP worse (p. 8). BO loop: 10 initial samples, 30 iterations, batch of 5, median ± one standard deviation over 20 replications, validation error minimised and test error reported (p. 9). Baselines from published implementations (Naszilla; NAS-BOWL's own code), 11 in total, 6 shown in the main text "since they usually achieve better results" (pp. 7, 9, 17-18). Results: both variants "find (near-)optimal architectures in all scenarios", NAS-GOAT-L slightly better (p. 9); against the remaining baselines "NAS-GOAT still outperforms others in all cases" (p. 18); in the noisy setting "comparable performance" to NAS-BOWL, NASBOT, BONAS (p. 18). Hardware: 4.2 GHz i7-7700K, 16 GB (p. 7). Not reported: MIP solve times, optimality gaps at the 1800 s limit, or how often the limit was hit; wall-clock comparison with the sampling-based baselines.
- **D6 Positioning**: Places the work in the "mathematical programming over trained ML models" line (NNs, trees, GPs, including her group's Tsay et al. 2021, Mistry et al. 2021, Thebelt et al. 2021-22, Zhang et al. 2023) and its uses in BO, verification and molecular design (p. 2). Table 2 classifies 12 NAS algorithms by surrogate and acquisition optimiser (mutation / sampling / MIP), making NAS-GOAT the only MIP entry (p. 7). The direct predecessor (BoGrape) is described as the first discrete-optimisation treatment of graph acquisition functions (p. 2).
  - Transferable: a comparison table whose columns are pipeline components (surrogate, acquisition optimiser) shows in one glance which component your paper changes (p. 7).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Four bulleted contributions ending with the algorithm name (p. 2); "Firstly and most importantly" for the search-space definition (p. 4); the constraint-design rationale stated as a principle (p. 4); a caveat that kernel quality and optimisation results trade off (pp. 8-9); hedged explanation "perhaps owing to its simpler form" (p. 9). The abstract claims the method "efficiently finds the optimal architecture for most cases" (p. 1) without efficiency (time) data. A typo duplicates "Eq. (2c)" for two different equations (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: exact general graph encoding with reachability and shortest paths, a general labelled-graph kernel, NAS restrictions, and a full BO loop (p. 2). Future work: kernels beyond shortest-path, other graph decision problems (p. 9). The variable-size normalisation issue is acknowledged and handled by enumerating sizes (p. 17). No separate limitations paragraph; the cost of the MIP is not discussed.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (partner's model as constraints; reuse in BO): evidence ✅. Step 8 exactly: the GP posterior with a graph kernel is written as MIP constraints and the acquisition function optimised globally (Acq-Opt, p. 3; pp. 6-7, 9).
- Method 1 (structure as insider information): evidence ✅. The graph properties the kernel needs (reachability, shortest paths) are made explicit variables, and NAS structure (single source/sink, index ordering u < v) is added as constraints that also cut isomorphic copies (eq. 1a, pp. 5-6).
- Method 4 (judge a component inside the whole algorithm): evidence ✅. Kernels are compared in isolation (Table 3, p. 8) and then inside the full BO loop, with the explicit warning that "a better kernel may not necessarily translate to better optimization results" (pp. 8-9); the simpler linear kernel wins in the loop (p. 9).
- Heuristic 4 (prove the hierarchy, experiment for the rest): evidence ✅. Exactness of the encoding is proved (Theorem 1, pp. 4, 14-16); performance is shown only experimentally (p. 9).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): variant ⚠. Uses the NAS community protocol (published baseline code, 20 replications, median curves, noisy-setting appendix, pp. 7, 9, 17-19) but drops the solver-paper items: no MIP times, gaps or time-limit hits are reported.
- new-pattern candidate: "generalise, prove, then restrict": a single exactness theorem for the most general encoding, with the earlier encoding and each target domain obtained by adding constraints (pp. 4-6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode shortest distances and path membership in a MIP with distance n as "infinity" and big-M triangle-inequality constraints switched by path indicators (Table 1 and Graph-Encoding, p. 5; C8, p. 14).
- Prove exactness of a combinatorial MIP encoding by surjection (true values feasible) plus injection by induction on shortest distance (pp. 14-16).
- Linearise squared counts in a kernel with one-hot "count equals c" indicators so that k(x, x) stays linear (pp. 16-17).
- Use the solver's solution pool (PoolSearchMode=2) to return the best k acquisition values as a BO batch (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "There are often multiple ways to encode a combinatorial problem, but insufficient constraints result in an unnecessarily large search space with symmetric solutions, while excessive constraints may cutoff feasible solutions from the search space." (p. 4)
- "Note that a better kernel may not necessarily translate to better optimization results" (p. 8)

### S097 · The catechol benchmark: Time-series solvent selection data for few-shot machine learning (Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 38 2025, doi:10.52202/085713-3994; read as arXiv:2506.07619v2)

**Read level**: full (all 22 file pages: main text pp. 1-10, acknowledgements p. 11, Appendix A benchmarking and model details pp. 17-19, Appendix B reactor details, calibration and descriptor imputation pp. 19-22; references pp. 11-16 skimmed). The text is the arXiv v2 preprint (27 Nov 2025, "Preprint.", p. 1), not the NeurIPS proceedings version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Toby Boyne (first author), Juan S. Campos, Becky D. Langdon, Jixiang Qing, Yilin Xie, Shiqiang Zhang, Calvin Tsay, Ruth Misener (Imperial Computing), Daniel W. Davies, Kim E. Jelfs (Imperial Chemistry), Sarah Boyall, Thomas M. Dixon, Linden Schrecker, Jose Pablo Folch (SOLVE Chemistry; Folch corresponding) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an AIchemy-hub dataset paper (p. 1, p. 11) in her group's BO-for-chemistry line; the experimental partner is a start-up (SOLVE Chemistry) founded around a solvent-ramping patent (ref 29, p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Chemical datasets are inaccessible to ML because they "require cleaning, thorough understanding of the chemistry, or are simply not available" (abstract, p. 1); existing yield datasets (Suzuki-Miyaura, Buchwald-Hartwig, Ni-catalysed additives) focus on discrete variables at fixed temperature and residence time, and Nguyen et al. discretise process conditions (pp. 2-3). Solvent selection is chosen because solvents dominate manufacturing waste and solvent effects are "very challenging to model theoretically" (pp. 1-3). The broken assumption is named via Schwaller et al.: assuming every reported reaction is already optimised "proved too loose", hence the need for full, possibly sub-optimal, conditions (p. 2).
  - Transferable: pitch a dataset by the variable type the field's benchmarks lack (continuous process conditions, time series) rather than by size (pp. 2-3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Release the first ML-ready transient-flow dataset: 1227 points over 24 solvents, solvent mixtures as pseudo-continuous inputs, temperature and residence time as continuous inputs, yields of starting material and two products as outputs, plus a 656-point single-solvent subset and a 283-point allyl phenyl ether dataset for transfer (Table 1, p. 3); data on Kaggle and code on GitHub (p. 3). Transient flow (step changes in flow rate, temperature ramps, solvent ramps) produces a continuous series of conditions per run (pp. 4-5). The solvent pairs to ramp were chosen by active learning: a GP trained on the earlier dataset and the entropy criterion over solvent pairs (eq. 1, p. 5). The benchmark tasks are regression on unseen solvents, GP extensions, transfer learning, active learning, single-objective and multi-objective BO (§3, pp. 6-10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model (experimental system)**: Uni-molecular rearrangement of allyl-substituted catechol at high temperature (Fig. 1, p. 2); online HPLC every two minutes (p. 5); conditions of each sample reconstructed from reactor volume and flow-rate history (eqs. 2-3, pp. 5-6); mixture featurisation by weighted mean of solvent descriptors, flagged as possibly an oversimplification (p. 6); outliers removed only for slugs that experienced a flow-rate step-up (p. 20); reactions with acetic acid omitted because of an unintended side reaction (p. 6, footnote 3). Appendix A.1.1 says acetonitrile and acetic acid are both excluded (p. 17), which does not match the main text (p. 6). Appendix B.1.1 describes the reactant solution as allyl phenyl ether (p. 20), the reactant of the earlier dataset, not the catechol of Fig. 1; whether this is a carry-over is not stated.
- **D4 Analysis and devices**: No theorems. Named devices: (a) residence time from solving V = ∫ Fc(t) dt backwards from the measurement time, with solvent fraction and average temperature from the same entry time (eqs. 2-3, pp. 5-6); (b) HPLC calibration constants fitted by a constrained least-squares problem that penalises deviation from initial estimates and from mass balance at full-conversion measurements, with yields ≤ 100% and concentration bounds, solved by SLSQP from a 100,000-point grid (pp. 21-22); (c) missing Spange descriptors imputed by a multitask GP with a Tanimoto kernel (p. 22); (d) a BaselineGP that ignores the solvent entirely, as the floor every featurisation must beat (p. 7).
  - Transferable: when a calibration cannot be measured directly, write it as a small constrained regression on physical invariants (mass balance, yields ≤ 100%) and report the fitted constants (pp. 21-22).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Leave-one-out CV, one solvent out (single solvent) or one ramp out (full data), MSE and NLPD (p. 6; p. 17). Regression (Table 3, p. 7): Spange descriptors strongest; MLP slightly better MSE than GP; LLM embeddings (RXNFP, ChemBERTa, frozen backbones) poor; latent/neural ODEs only on single solvents. Key negative result: "many of these models fail to outperform the baseline model, which does not use any solvent information" (p. 7). GP extensions (decomposed kernel, multitask, input warping) help single solvents but "struggle to beat the simpler GP" on ramps (Table 4, p. 8). Transfer learning: GP MSE 0.030 → 0.020 with the extra dataset, MLP 0.027 → 0.031 (Table 5, p. 9), while the text concludes "the utility of learning across multiple datasets" (p. 9). Active learning: entropy better early, mutual information better long-term (Fig. 5a, p. 9). BO: EI and UCB find the optimum "usually after 20 iterations" vs random, 30 initialisations, median with 10th/90th quantiles (Fig. 5b, p. 9); acquisition functions optimised by exhaustive search over the dataset (p. 17). MOBO with Thompson sampling and random scalarisations vs random on GD, IGD, MPFE (Table 6, p. 10). Appendix: neural processes and the group's own shortest-path graph-GP kernels, where ESP has MSE 1.068 (Table 7, p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against ORD/ORDerly and USPTO-derived yield data (inconsistent, not ML-ready, pp. 2-3) and against curated discrete-variable datasets (pp. 2-3); answers "the call for more flow chemistry reaction data" (p. 2). Cites the group's BO line: the multi-fidelity BO paper as the "battery design" example of ML in science (ref 4, pp. 1, 11), SnAKe and transition-constrained BO as transient-flow design of experiments (refs 68, 70, p. 9, p. 15), BARK among the non-stationary kernels (ref 67, p. 8, p. 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Strong priority claims ("first-ever transient flow dataset", abstract p. 1; "first machine-learning-ready", p. 2; again p. 10); a direct appeal ("We call on the machine learning community", p. 10); a separate "Limitations of this paper" paragraph (p. 10); negative results stated in the main text, not hidden (p. 7, p. 8).
  - Transferable: in a benchmark paper, put the "most models do not beat the no-information baseline" finding in the main text; it is the reason the dataset is needed (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: dataset, collection technique description, featurisation and model benchmarks (pp. 3-10). ### S115 · Weighted-Sum of Gaussian Process Latent Variable Models (AISTATS 2025, PMLR 258, arXiv:2402.09122)

**Read level**: full (main text pp. 1-9, checklist p. 12, notation tables pp. 13-14, Appendices D-H pp. 21-25 read in full; the ELBO derivations of Appendix B.1-B.2 and the kernel-expectation expressions of Appendix C, pp. 14-20, skimmed for structure; references pp. 9-12 skimmed). The text is arXiv v4 (19 Mar 2025) with the AISTATS 2025 header (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: James Odgers, Ruby Sedgwick, Chrysoula Kappatou, Sarah Filippi (all Imperial; Misener 4th of 5, Filippi last; no corresponding author marked, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the chemometrics strand with Eli Lilly funding (EPSRC Prosperity grant, p. 9), continuing the group's PLS-bootstrap and preprocessing-optimisation papers (Odgers et al. 2023; Kappatou et al. 2023; cited pp. 6-7, 10-11); a statistics paper, not an optimisation-algorithm paper.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Spectroscopy signal separation: each spectrum is a weighted sum of pure-component signals (Beer-Lambert), but the pure signals vary with unobserved conditions such as temperature (p. 1). Entry is by three settings "no existing methods can solve": no parametric signal model, few and mostly unlabelled observations (rules out deep learning), and possibly no pure-component data (p. 2).
  - Transferable: write the problem setting as a short list of simultaneous constraints, each ruling out one existing family of methods by name (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Put a latent variable h_i inside each pure-component signal: y_ij = Σ_c r_ic f_c(h_i, λ_j) + ε_ij (eq. 1, p. 1), with GP priors over each f_c(h, λ) (eqs. 3-4, p. 3), a unit normal prior on h, and a Dirichlet prior on the weights (eq. 6, p. 4). Inference follows the Bayesian GPLVM of Titsias and Lawrence: inducing points, mean-field q(H) and Dirichlet q(R*), and an ELBO whose ξ0, ξ1, ξ2 statistics are weighted analogues of the GPLVM ψ statistics (eqs. 7-17, pp. 4-5). Extensions: categorical weights for classification and an independent-wavelength variant WS-GPLVM-ind (pp. 5-6). Kept from GPLVM: the variational scheme and ARD kernel for latent-dimension selection (pp. 3, 6); changed: the likelihood mixes C GPs with unknown test weights, and test spectra enter the variational posterior (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linear mixing plus iid Gaussian noise (eq. 1, p. 1); weights on the simplex (eq. 2, p. 2); training and test spectra available at fitting time (transductive, p. 3); single shared ARD squared-exponential kernel across components, chosen for automatic latent-dimension selection and analytic ξ expressions (p. 6; p. 22); SNV preprocessing (p. 6). Non-identifiability of eq. (1) without a prior coupling data points is argued in the text (p. 3). Cost O(L³C³), "designed for smaller data sets" (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: No theorems ("We do not make theoretical claims about our models performance", checklist p. 12). Devices: (a) ELBO with an analytically optimal q(U) following King and Lawrence (2006) (p. 5; App. B.1, p. 17); (b) a degenerate-case check: with one component and weight 1 the ξ statistics "collapse exactly" to the Bayesian GPLVM ψ statistics (p. 5); (c) moments of the Dirichlet variational distribution are all that the bound needs, which is why other weight distributions can be swapped in (p. 5); (d) initialisation by PCA on the residuals of a static least-squares fit F̂ = Yᵀ R (Rᵀ R)⁻¹ (eqs. 18-19, p. 6); (e) a three-stage optimisation: fit hyperparameters on training data, inflate the noise variance and anneal it back over 20 steps while fitting the test variational parameters, then optimise jointly (p. 6; Adam then L-BFGS, p. 23).
  - Transferable: when a flexible component (the latent GPLVM) absorbs everything and leaves the interpretable one (the weights) unused, stage the optimisation and inflate-then-anneal the noise so the interpretable part is fitted first (p. 6, p. 23).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Toy example (100 train, 100 test, 5 latent dimensions of which ARD keeps 1, p. 6). Near-infrared spectroscopy with varying temperature (Wülfert et al. 1998; 3 components, 22 ratios, 5 temperatures, 10 random 50/50 splits, temperature withheld; pp. 7-8): WS-GPLVM MSE 2.67×10⁻⁴ vs PLS 3.04×10⁻⁴, but NLPD worse than CLS-GP, read as variational overconfidence (p. 8; Table 2, p. 24). Oil flow (simulated, 12 measurements, WS-GPLVM-ind): the inverse models PLS and ILMC are best and WS-GPLVM-ind "very nearly matches" them while beating CLS by the largest margin (p. 8; Table 2: ILMC 2.82, PLS 3.81, WS-GPLVM-ind 5.97, CLS 81.5, all ×10⁻⁴, p. 24). Rock classification (70 points, 10-fold CV): WS-GPLVM 81.4% vs PLS 82.9% accuracy (p. 9). Baselines (ILMC, CLS/CLS-GP as the model without latent variables, PLS) all run with five restarts, dimensions selected by ELBO or CV (pp. 7, 21-24). Restart sufficiency checked with 20 and 32 restarts (p. 23). Inconsistency: the text gives standard deviations of 21.3% and 17.9% (p. 9), while Table 3 gives 21.3 and 16.2 and states the brackets are "two times the standard error of the mean" (p. 25).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues the GPLVM line (Lawrence 2005; Titsias and Lawrence 2010) and differs from multi-output GPs (no per-observation variation of latent functions), GPFA (no h, all weights unobserved) and Altmann et al. 2013 (latent variables tied to weights) (pp. 2-3). Treats PLS as the chemometrics standard and compares to it on its home ground (p. 7; p. 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Key idea stated as "Our key idea, and the starting point for this paper" (p. 1); a surprise reported and explained (a latent dimension tracks water fraction because SNV rescales spectra differently, p. 8); hedges ("may be overconfident", p. 8; "could be a compelling reason", p. 9). Leftover earlier model name: "MO-GPLVM" (p. 6, p. 20) and a supplement titled "Mixed-Output Gaussian Process Latent Variable Models" (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a model, its ELBO, a training procedure, and three applications (p. 3, p. 9). Limits stated: small data because of the O(L³C³) inverse, with stochastic variational inference as the scaling route (p. 5); overconfidence (p. 8); losses on oil flow and parity with PLS on rock classification (pp. 8-9); other kernels would need numerical expectations (p. 20). Code "will be open-sourced after publication" (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 1 (choose by broken assumption): evidence ✅. Three simultaneous complications of the application each break one family of methods (p. 2).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor), step 8 (write losses and scope in): evidence ✅. The oil-flow loss to the inverse models and the NLPD loss to CLS-GP are stated in the main text (p. 8); every baseline gets the same five restarts (p. 7); the restart count is itself tested (p. 23). Against step 7, the text/table mismatch on the dispersion statistic (p. 9 vs p. 25) is a reporting slip.
- Method 4 (judge a component inside the whole): variant ⚠. The latent variable is ablated by a nested baseline (CLS-GP = WS-GPLVM without h; WS-GPLVM-ind vs WS-GPLVM) (pp. 7, 22, 24), a statistical-model analogue of the cumulative solver ablation.
- Method 1 (structure as insider information): variant ⚠. Domain structure (Beer-Lambert additivity, weights on the simplex, smoothness across wavelengths) is built into the probabilistic model rather than detected in an algebraic model (pp. 1-3, 6).
- new-pattern candidate: "staged fitting against a degenerate optimum": when joint optimisation lets a flexible part explain everything, fit in stages with noise annealing and check restart sufficiency (pp. 6, 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Check a new variational bound by its degenerate case (one component, weight 1) collapsing exactly to the known bound (p. 5).
- Initialise latent variables by PCA on the residuals of the simplest static model, so the latent space only has to explain what the static model misses (eqs. 18-19, p. 6).
- Test how many random restarts are needed by running far more on two datasets and reporting where the best objective plateaus (p. 23).
- Include the model-minus-your-component as a baseline (CLS-GP) so the contribution of the new component is isolated (p. 7, p. 22).
