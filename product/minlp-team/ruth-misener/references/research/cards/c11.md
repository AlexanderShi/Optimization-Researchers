### S072 · Solving the pooling problem at scale with extensible solver GALINI (Computers & Chemical Engineering 159, 2022, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2022.107660; read as arXiv:2105.01687v1)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v1, all 42 pages: main text pp. 1-23, references pp. 23-27, tables pp. 28-35, appendices A-D pp. 35-42; the code listings of Appendix D, pp. 39-41, were skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: F. Ceccon (first author, GALINI's developer; Department of Computing, Imperial, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: after the 2016 pooling-structure detector (Ceccon, Kouyialis & Misener, AIChE J., cited p. 2) and the GALINI solver (Ceccon et al. 2020, cited p. 24); the last pooling paper of the EPSRC fellowship (acknowledgement, p. 23); the Method 4 "plug-in, not default" case named in SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: MINLP solvers see "flat" problems "with little to no information about what the optimization problem represents" (pp. 1-2). Pooling is chosen for its many industrial applications (crude oil, water networks, natural gas, mining, p. 2) and its NP-hardness, including her own complexity results (p. 2); the stated goal is "practically solving larger problem sizes" (p. 2). The entry is a weakness of her own earlier route: detection in a flat problem "is a heuristic approach that may miss special structure developed by the user"; the 2016 detector found pooling in 6% of MINLPLib (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a detector for a structure already exists, ask whether the structure could instead be carried from the modelling layer, so nothing has to be re-discovered (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep the network with the model: a Python `Network` object (nodes in layers, edge attributes; pp. 7, 37-38) generates a Pyomo `PoolingPQFormulationBlock` that keeps a reference to the network (pp. 7-9). From the stored network the library builds (i) the Luedtke et al. (2020) convex relaxation: linear inequalities (11)-(12) added once, convex nonlinear inequalities (13)-(14) separated as gradient cuts when violated by more than ε (pp. 8-13), and (ii) the Dey-Gupte (2015) MIP restriction that splits each pool into τ auxiliary pools, each sending flow to one output (pp. 13-14). Both enter GALINI as runtime plug-ins (a `CutsGenerator` and an `InitialPrimalSearchStrategy`) through Python entry points; "GALINI itself does not require any change" (pp. 2, 6, 14-16). Changed relative to her earlier work: the source of structural knowledge (stored, not detected). Unchanged: the relaxation and heuristic are taken from the literature; the contribution is software architecture and a large computational comparison.
  - Transferable: implement a class-specific relaxation and heuristic as plug-ins behind a generic solver's callback interface (six cut-loop hooks, one primal-search hook, p. 6), so the general solver stays class-agnostic and the user opts in by configuration (pp. 15-16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: PQ-formulation (1)-(2) with three node layers and arcs I×L, I×J, L×J (pp. 3-4). The library expects a pooling-like three-layer network, does not validate it, ignores extra edges and layers beyond the third (pp. 7-8). The primal heuristic succeeds only if the user's additional constraints are linear (pp. 15-16). The relaxation is built at the root and not recomputed in the tree because "preliminary results suggest" bound changes do not significantly affect it (p. 16). Heuristic settings: τ = 1, MIP time limit 60 s, gap 1% (p. 15). Adding all Luedtke inequalities rather than separating them was a design choice justified by tests showing "the change in performance is not significant" (p. 12); those tests are not shown.
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: No theorems. Validity of (11)-(14) is cited from Luedtke et al. (pp. 10-11). Devices: outer-approximation (gradient) cuts for the convex quadratic inequalities, generated only on ε-violation (p. 12); McCormick envelope of the scaled product r = s·p (eqs. (3)-(6), p. 10); recovery of fractional flows q_il = v_ilj / y_lj from the MIP restriction solution (p. 14). A correctness argument by design: nonlinear side constraints are not dropped from the restriction because "the new model would not be a restriction of the original model" (p. 16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: One laptop-class machine (Intel i7-6700HQ, 32 GB), pooling-network 1.0.0, GALINI 1.0.1 with CPLEX 12.10 and Ipopt 3.13.2, against Gurobi 9.1 with NonConvex = 2; relative gap 1e-6, absolute 1e-8, 600 s, other options default (pp. 16-17). Instances: 360 sparse Haverly-based instances (18 classes × 20; Luedtke et al.) and 50 dense randstd instances (Dey & Gupte), all shipped in the library (p. 17; Table 2, p. 28). Gurobi is run in six configurations, including "oracle" variants that exclude the time to generate cuts or the warm start (pp. 17-18); GALINI in four (p. 18). Gurobi's own cut statistics are tabulated to understand the default (Table 3, p. 29). Dolan-Moré performance profiles on time and on relative gap (p. 18; Figs. 6-10, pp. 20-22), shifted geometric means with feasible-solution counts (Tables 4-8, pp. 30-34), a "difficult" subset (Gurobi default > 10 s, 30 instances; p. 19, Fig. 8 p. 21). Findings, including negatives: Gurobi gains nothing from its own cuts on dense instances; the Luedtke cuts hurt Gurobi on dense instances even as an oracle; the MIP restriction helps Gurobi on larger dense instances (p. 19); on sparse instances Gurobi defaults are best and "the GALINI performance is not competitive with Gurobi" (p. 21; GALINI hundreds of seconds vs Gurobi under 6 s, Table 8, p. 34). On the 20 largest dense instances GALINI with the heuristic wins the relative gap 9 of 10 times per group (Table 7, p. 33); the gain is attributed by a bound breakdown: lower bounds similar, GALINI upper bound on average 28% better (p. 21; Table 9, p. 35). Configurations that found no feasible solution are reported as such (Fig. 9 caption, p. 22; Table 6 counts, p. 32). Not done: no other global solver (BARON, ANTIGONE, SCIP) and no independent re-check of returned solutions is mentioned.
  - Transferable: give the commercial rival your own components twice, once with their generation time counted and once as an oracle with it excluded, so the reader can separate the value of the idea from the cost of your implementation (pp. 17-18).
  - Transferable: decompose a gap win into lower-bound and upper-bound contributions per size class before claiming why you win (Table 9, p. 35; p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues Misener & Floudas (2009) on pooling and her 2016 detector (p. 2); places network-structure detection in a MIP lineage (Brown & Wright 1984, Bixby & Fourer 1988, Achterberg & Raack 2010, Salvagnin 2016, p. 2). Reuses rival groups' results as components (Luedtke-D'Ambrosio-Linderoth-Schweiger; Dey-Gupte) and checks them: "Our experiments confirm the Dey and Gupte (2015) results" (p. 19); the Luedtke cut benefit is weaker "because Gurobi is more performant than the ones used in the original paper" (p. 19), a dated-comparison remark rather than a criticism.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Software-paper register: interface method lists (p. 6), variable/constraint tables (Table 1, p. 8), notation tables and full code listings in appendices (pp. 35-42). Claims scoped in the abstract ("competitive with Gurobi 9.1 on the largest problem instances", p. 1); the conclusion's stronger "smaller gap than the best available commercial solver" is tied to "large scale dense pooling problems" in the same sentence and followed by the sparse-instance negative (p. 23). Hedges on internal design choices ("preliminary results suggest", p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution is software (library + two plug-ins + instance generators, Apache-2 licensed, published with the paper, p. 3) plus a computational study. Limits are specific: GALINI's B&B "is not as fast as Gurobi's in visiting the hundreds or thousands of nodes" (p. 21); heuristic fails with nonlinear side constraints (pp. 15-16); relaxation not updated in the tree (p. 16). Directions: other network problems such as optimal power flow (p. 2); combining with automatic detection (p. 7). No future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (structure as insider information): variant ⚠. Same goal (let the solver "behave as if it has perfect knowledge" of the structure, p. 2), but the structure is stored by the modeller rather than detected, explicitly because detection "may miss special structure developed by the user" (p. 2). This is the "hybrid between automatic detection and disciplined programming" of Method 1 step 4 realised in code.
- Method 4 (judge inside the whole solver; class-specific gains as opt-in): evidence ✅. Both plug-ins are off by default and enabled by a configuration entry (pp. 15-16); the verdict is split by class: dense large instances benefit, sparse do not (pp. 19, 21, 23).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): evidence ✅ with gaps. Strengthened rival and oracle runs (pp. 17-18), harmonised tolerances (pp. 16-17), profiles on time and gap at limit (p. 18), loss written into the paper (p. 21). Gaps: a single competitor, one laptop, no independent feasibility re-check stated.
- new-pattern candidate: "attribute the win to the primal or dual side" (Table 9, p. 35; p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: "structure carried by the modelling object (smart block) instead of detected" (pp. 2, 5-9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ship class-specific cuts and heuristics as plug-ins of a class-agnostic solver, enabled by configuration (pp. 2, 6, 15-16).
- Run the rival with and without your components, each as time-counted and oracle variants (pp. 17-18).
- Explain a gap win by a lower-bound/upper-bound breakdown per size class (p. 21; Table 9, p. 35).
- Refuse a convenience that would silently turn a restriction into a non-restriction; fail loudly instead (pp. 15-16).

**Quotes**:
- "The advantage of this approach is that, although GALINI itself is unaware of the pooling problem, GALINI can, through this library, behave as if it has perfect knowledge of its structure." (p. 2)
- "We decided not to disable the non-linear constraints since by doing that the new model would not be a restriction of the original model, creating a subtle bug that is difficult to debug for users." (p. 16)

### S015 · Formulating data-driven surrogate models for process optimization (Computers & Chemical Engineering, 2023, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2023.108411)

**Read level**: full (all 6 pages of the conference-format text, including references pp. 4-6; see page mapping for the version caveat).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Lorenz T. Biegler (CMU; joint corresponding authors, p. 1). Voice: joint; the text does not say which sections are whose. The trust-region filter material (p. 4) is Biegler's line of work by citation (Eason & Biegler 2016, 2018), the ReLU/tree/OMLT material (p. 3) Misener's by citation; this attribution is inferred from the cited works, not stated. · **Position in trajectory**: a 2023 perspective written alongside OMLT and the partition-based ReLU work, which it cites (pp. 3-4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: "a new wave of research into data-driven techniques for mathematical optimization" (p. 1). The paper enters by asking what must hold for a surrogate to be usable inside process optimisation: two "essential conditions", two perspectives on building surrogates, and the "verification problem, i.e., checking that the optimum of the surrogate corresponds to the optimum of the truth model" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: An organising frame rather than a method. (a) Condition 1: constrain surrogates to where they do not extrapolate with large errors; Condition 2: avoid overfitting; both tied to stability of optimal solutions and Lipschitz continuity (p. 1). (b) Three "levels of surrogacy" (plant, unit, physical property), each written as a system of equations (1)-(3) with explicit trade-offs: plant-level surrogates allow DFO but violate conservation laws when extrapolated and are not reusable; unit-level keep plant balances; property-level keep unit and plant balances but need large-scale solvers (pp. 1-2). (c) Surrogate-led vs mathematical-programming-led perspectives (p. 1, p. 3). (d) A survey of formulations per surrogate class: polynomial (ALAMO, ARGONAUT), ReLU networks (big-M, convex-hull cuts, partition-based, SDP, S-lemma; the three Yang et al. formulations: smoothing, binary, complementarity), gradient-boosted trees, Gaussian processes (pp. 2-3). (e) Verification through the trust-region filter (TRF) method, which samples the truth model and converges to its local optimum independent of the surrogate choice (p. 4).
  - Transferable: before choosing a surrogate, decide the level of surrogacy (plant / unit / property) by writing which balance equations remain exact at each level and what each choice costs in solver class and reusability (pp. 1-2).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: TRF guarantees need truth models "which have smooth input-output properties" (p. 4); DFO methods considered are for unconstrained problems min f(x) (p. 3). Robustness of the surrogate optimum depends on Lipschitz continuity and parameter confidence regions (p. 1).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: n/a (no proofs). Convergence of TRF is cited, "based on DFO properties in Conn et al. (2009)" (p. 4).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (none). Results of others are reported, e.g. Ma et al. (2022): ALAMO for less complex systems, ReLU networks for complex ones, DFO with smooth penalties most effective (p. 3).
- **D6 Positioning**: Justifies the mathematical-programming view by the gap between SGD training practice and convergence theory (Zhang et al. 2022: weights often do not converge to stationary points, pp. 2-3). Places global DFO methods with "in the limit" guarantees below spatial B&B because they "do not provide lower bounds and certificates for global solutions" (p. 4). Self-citation is moderate and integrated (OMLT, ENTMOOT, Mistry et al., Tsay et al., Wiebe & Misener; pp. 3-4); rival tools are listed alongside (reluMIP, ALAMO, ARGONAUT, p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Two-sided bullet framing of the perspectives (p. 1), numbered conditions (p. 1), aphorism ("all models are imperfect, but some are useful", p. 3). Very short conclusion: "There is still much work to be done in this area." (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution is a framing/perspective. Open problems named: whether DNNs trained by SGD compete with large-scale optimisation strategies for process models (p. 2); smoothing ReLU via complementarity constraints, "there is lots more to do in this research area" (p. 3); extending TRF to model mismatch and noise via ε-exact models (p. 4). No limitation of the framing itself is discussed.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (the partner's trained model is the problem: constraints, then a toolkit): evidence ✅. The mathematical-programming-led view writes trained surrogates as MIP/NLP formulations and points users to OMLT and reluMIP for adding them to Pyomo (pp. 3-4); Condition 1 (constrain the surrogate against extrapolation, p. 1) is the stated form of Method 3 step 4 (keep the optimiser near the data).
- Method 1 (structure): weak evidence ✅. The "mathematical programming-led" perspective chooses a surrogate for its optimisation properties (e.g. linearity) when problems are so hard the surrogate must conform (p. 1), i.e. structure is designed in rather than detected.
- new-pattern candidate: "levels of surrogacy" decomposition (plant / unit / property) with exactness of conservation laws as the criterion (pp. 1-2). One source; probably Biegler's framing, not hers (inferred).
- new-pattern candidate: "two directions" framing, surrogate-led vs mathematical-programming-led (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before embedding a surrogate, write down which conservation laws stay exact at the chosen surrogacy level and what solver class it forces (pp. 1-2).
- Verify a surrogate optimum against the truth model with a trust-region scheme that re-samples the truth model, rather than trusting the surrogate's optimum (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "The second, mathematical programming-led, perspective is important for PSE domains where the optimization problems being solved are so difficult that a surrogate model must conform to specific properties." (p. 1)
- "For optimization, all models are imperfect, but some are useful." (p. 3)

### S031 · Combining multi-fidelity modelling and asynchronous batch Bayesian Optimization (Computers & Chemical Engineering, 2023, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2023.108194; read as arXiv:2211.06149v2)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v2, all 27 pages: main text pp. 1-19, references pp. 20-23, appendices A-B pp. 24-27; the notation table, pp. 24-25, skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. P. Folch (first author, PhD student, StatML CDT funded with BASF, p. 19), R. M. Lee, B. Shafei, D. Walz (BASF SE), C. Tsay, M. van der Wilk (Imperial) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: same student and BASF team as SnAKe (cited p. 2); the BO strand of the BASF data-driven-optimisation programme, and a case for Heuristic 1 in SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Broken assumptions of classical BO in a battery laboratory: measurements "may come from different sources" and "may require significant waiting times" (abstract, p. 1). The application (coin cells approximating pouch cells, months-long tests, batches; pp. 1-2) generates the research question: "How can we automatically and continuously design new experiments when the prior experimental data returns on different (and uncertain) time-frames and the type of measurements may vary?" (p. 2). The structural insight: multi-fidelity and batching cannot be combined "without asynchronicity arising", because lower fidelities finish earlier (p. 9).
  - Transferable: show that two separately studied extensions force a third property when combined (here: multi-fidelity + batch ⇒ asynchronous) and make that third property the problem setting (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea**: A modular algorithm: (a) a multi-task GP (linear model of coregionalization) to transfer information across fidelities (pp. 4-5, 12); (b) any acquisition function on the target fidelity, made batch-aware by local penalization, fantasies or Thompson sampling (pp. 8-9, 12); (c) fidelity chosen afterwards, either by the MF-GP-UCB variance threshold (eq. (21)) or by cost-weighted MF-MES information gain evaluated only M times (eq. (22)) (p. 12); (d) a batch-space budget Λ with fidelity-dependent λ^(m) (eq. (19), p. 10); Algorithm 1 (p. 13). Changed relative to Takeno et al. MF-MES: the information-based criterion is used only to pick the fidelity, not to optimise the input, avoiding its approximation cost and single-acquisition lock-in (pp. 3, 12). Unchanged: the component methods themselves.
  - Transferable: decouple "where to sample" (any acquisition function) from "at which fidelity" (a separate cheap selector), so an expensive criterion is evaluated M times instead of being optimised over the input space (p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Discrete, fixed fidelities ("in practice will be two or three", p. 4); known scalar costs, cost read as time (p. 6); random or deterministic delays τ (p. 10); Gaussian noise possibly fidelity-dependent (p. 10); batch space freed only on observation (p. 10). MF-GP-UCB needs known non-increasing maximum biases ζ^(m) (eq. (8), p. 6); LMC drops it (p. 7). Local penalization assumes Lipschitz continuity with an estimated L and maximum (pp. 9, 13-14). Benchmarks "quasi-normalized" so outputs are near 1, and γ = 0.1 works under that scaling (p. 13). Hyper-parameter heuristics: train only on the lowest fidelity for independent GPs (p. 12); smoothed box priors on all hyper-parameters (p. 25).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: No theorems. Devices used and explained: tightest-of-M UCBs (eq. (9), p. 6, Fig. 2 p. 7); Lipschitz-ball penalizers and the Alvi et al. hard penalizer (eq. (17), p. 9); a Lipschitz estimate from the GP posterior gradient mean, locally per region (p. 14); the observation that fantasies need only re-train the GP mean because the posterior variance depends only on input locations (p. 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Metric: log-regret against time-step (p. 14). Synthetic: Currin2D, BadCurrin2D (low fidelity = negative of target), Hartmann3D/6D, Park4D, Borehole8D, budget for 200 high-fidelity observations, batch size 4 (pp. 14-16); Ackley 40D with batch 20 (p. 17); battery: an anonymised BASF data set, a GP fitted and two posterior samples used as high/low fidelity, batch budget 20, pouch cell costs 2 units and takes 10× longer (pp. 17-18). Ten methods (Table 2, p. 14). Fairness device: single-query baselines are turned into batch methods by filling the batch with random queries "In the interest of giving a fairer comparison" (p. 15). Negatives reported: TuRBO "performs very poorly in Park4D and Borehole8D" (p. 19; also p. 15); independent GPs "works surprisingly well" on the battery problem (p. 19); MF-MES omitted on Hartmann for compute cost (p. 16) and MF-MES and TuRBO omitted on the battery problem because of input constraints (pp. 19, 27); Bayesian neural networks dropped after failing to give good inference (p. 5). A fidelity-query histogram explains why the information-based selector does marginally better (Fig. 7, p. 18). The number of repetitions behind the uncertainty bands is not stated in the text read.
  - Transferable: when the real experiment is proprietary or slow, fit a GP to the partner's anonymised data and use posterior samples as the benchmark functions for each fidelity; publish data and code (pp. 17-18).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Kandasamy et al. (2016a) and Takeno et al. (2020), and generalises the latter "to help avoid the computational complexities of the method, and possible pitfalls from being constrained to a single acquisition function" (p. 3); contrasts with Li et al. (2021) BNN batch multi-fidelity by aiming at "simpler, more robust and computationally feasible methods" (p. 3). A strengths/weaknesses table of all component methods (Table 1, p. 11) serves as a mini-review (contribution I, p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Numbered contributions I-III (p. 2); about half the paper is tutorial review (§§3-4, pp. 3-10); everyday analogies (cakes vs muffins for fidelity-dependent batch size, p. 9). The closing argument is for simplicity: UCB with local penalization "always achieves a top four performance", so "following Occam's Razor, we may choose the simplest model that fits the problem parameters" (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: a review, a general algorithm for any acquisition function, an empirical study (p. 2). Limits and future work: multiple cost types left to future work (p. 6); multi-objective acquisition functions (p. 12); how to choose the acquisition function "remains an open problem" (p. 19); fixed thresholds "should be carefully scaled with the outputs" (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (generalise behind one interface with switchable components): variant ⚠. Not a trained model as constraints, but the same design move in BO: the acquisition function becomes a user-switchable slot of a general algorithm (pp. 2, 12, 19).
- Method 3 step 6 (public stand-in for a confidential instance): evidence ✅. Anonymised BASF data plus GP posterior samples as the benchmark, with data and code at a public repository (pp. 17-18).
- Method 2 (parameter scepticism; try to delete the parameter): evidence ✅. The information-based fidelity selector is preferred because it is "parameter-free", while the variance-based one "requires better tuning" (p. 19); TuRBO's many "unintuitive choices" are held against it (p. 19).
- Method 4 (default follows robustness across classes, not peak results): evidence ✅ (BO variant). The recommended default is the method that is always top four, not TuRBO, which wins on some benchmarks and fails on others (p. 19).
- Heuristic 1 in SKILL.md (choose by broken assumption): evidence (this is the case cited there): sequential sampling and single-source data both fail in the battery lab (pp. 1-2).
- new-pattern candidate: "combination forces a new property": combining two extensions creates asynchronicity, which becomes the setting (p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Separate the input choice (any acquisition function) from the fidelity choice (a cheap selector) and budget batch space per fidelity (pp. 10, 12-13).
- Turn non-batch baselines into batch ones by random filling, so comparisons do not leave baseline resources idle (p. 15).
- Build a benchmark from posterior samples of a GP fitted to the partner's data when the real experiment cannot be run (pp. 17-18).
- Recommend the method that is robustly near the top over the one that is best somewhere and fails elsewhere, and say so in the conclusion (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "However, we emphasise that we cannot fully use both tools simultaneously without asynchronicity arising." (p. 9)
- "We were interested in applying Bayesian Neural Networks models, but, we were unable to consistently obtain good inference so we discarded them from our analysis." (p. 5)

### S051 · Optimizing over trained GNNs via symmetry breaking (Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 36, 2023, doi:10.52202/075280-1946; read as arXiv:2305.09420v2)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v2, all 27 pages: main text pp. 1-10, references pp. 11-16, appendices A-C pp. 17-27 including all proofs and the worked example).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Zhang (first and corresponding author, PhD student), J. S. Campos, C. Tsay (Imperial), C. Feldmann, D. Walz, F. Sandfort, M. Mathea (BASF SE) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: extends the trained-model line (trees → ENTMOOT → OMLT → partition-based ReLU, cited p. 2) to graph inputs; the GNN symmetry-breaking practice cited in Method 1 of SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: GNNs are used "forward" for property prediction, but the "backward" problem of finding inputs with optimal predicted properties (computer-aided molecular design) is little studied; existing GNN-based CAMD ignores both the input graph structure and the trained network's inner structure (pp. 1-2; Fig. 1, p. 2). Two challenges are named: symmetry from permutation invariance, which enlarges the feasible domain and impedes the B&B search tree, and implementation across many GNN architectures (p. 2).
  - Transferable: name the property that is a virtue in the forward (learning) problem and a vice in the inverse (optimisation) problem, here permutation invariance (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Break the N! indexing symmetry of the abstract graph rather than the symmetry of the MIP: (S1) connectivity to a lower index (from Mc Donald), (S2) node 0 has the minimal feature hierarchy h, (S3) lexicographic order of neighbour sets is non-decreasing in the index (pp. 3-4). Prove that the constraints never remove every representative of a graph via a constructive indexing algorithm (Algorithm 1, p. 4; Theorem 1, p. 4). Encode general GNN layers of form (⋆) with linear aggregation (pp. 7-8) for non-fixed graphs by two MIP formulations: bilinear (edge binaries times features; an MIQCP) and big-M (auxiliary contribution variables), implemented in OMLT (pp. 7-8). With a fixed graph the GNN reduces to a dense network (p. 7). Changed: symmetry handled at the level of the abstract nodes, a group of size N! instead of the much larger group the solver would see on 616 + 411 variables (p. 6).
  - Transferable: when a solver's automatic symmetry detection only sees the variable-constraint graph, state symmetry-breaking constraints on the modelling-level objects whose permutations generate the symmetry (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Undirected graphs with node features; directed graphs and edge features said not to change the analysis (p. 3). GNN layers of form (⋆) with constant weights and biases (p. 7); bounded features for big-M (p. 8). CAMD instance: atoms C, N, O, S (QM7) or C, N, O, F (QM9), aromatic and radical molecules removed, one covalence per atom type (pp. 22, 25); fixed exact N in experiments (p. 22). Atom-type, bond and ring bounds (C22)-(C25) computed from the data set so every training molecule satisfies them (pp. 8, 25). Optimality is "defined on the trained GNN instead of true properties" (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: Lexicographic order on multisets via padding to fixed-length non-decreasing sequences (pp. 3-4). Algorithm 1 builds the indexing with temporary indexes and temporary neighbour sets at each step (p. 4); the worked example shows why the temporary indexes are needed: without them a naive choice violates (S3) (Appendix A.2, pp. 19-21, note p. 20). Properties 1-3 (index bounds, nesting of indexed-neighbour sets, monotonicity of LO under truncation) feed Lemma 1 (final order implies temporary order at every iteration) (pp. 4-5; proofs pp. 17-18). Theorem 1 by contradiction at the minimal violating index s, with two cases (u has k−1 neighbours; u has a k-th neighbour) and Lemma 1 applied at iterations s and s+1 (pp. 5-6). Lemma 2: for connected graphs (S3) implies (S1) (p. 6; induction plus contradiction, p. 18). (S2)/(S3) are linearised by binary-weight "universal ordering vector" coefficients 2^(F−f−1) and 2^(N−u−1) (C26)-(C27) (pp. 8-9). A counting illustration: 720 → 636 (S1) → 120 (S2) → 4 (S3) indexings of a 6-node graph (Fig. 2, p. 5).
  - Transferable: prove that symmetry-breaking constraints are valid by giving a constructive algorithm whose output always satisfies them, plus a step-by-step worked example that shows why each algorithmic detail is needed (pp. 4-6, 19-21).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Two measurements. (1) Symmetry removal measured directly by counting feasible structures with Gurobi's solution pool (PoolSearchMode = 2, up to 10^9 solutions, 48 h limit): (S1)-(S3) remove 40-41% (N = 2), 86-87% (N = 4) and ≥ 98% (N = 6) of solutions relative to (S1) on both data sets (Table 1, p. 9). (2) Optimisation over trained GNNs (two GraphSAGE layers, add pooling, three dense layers; Tables 4-5, pp. 25-26): 10 models trained, the 5 lowest-loss kept, each solved with 10 Gurobi seeds, 50 runs per N ∈ {4,...,8} and formulation, Gurobi 10.0.1, 10 h limit, default settings (pp. 9-10, 26). Reported: number of successful runs, mean, Q1 and Q3 of time to tolerance and of time to first optimal solution, and presolved model sizes (Tables 6-7, pp. 26-27); bars omitted below 20 successful runs (Fig. 3 caption, p. 10). Examples: QM7, N = 7, big-M solves 0 of 50 runs, big-M + symmetry breaking 49 (Table 6, p. 26); big-M beats bilinear in 341 of 500 runs, and finds the optimum earlier in 292 (p. 10); the presolve tables are used to explain why (fewer binaries after presolve, p. 9). Accuracy of the trained GNNs is compared to the state of the art with an explicit caveat that "this comparison is unfair" because of preprocessing (p. 26).
  - Transferable: measure a symmetry-breaking scheme by counting feasible solutions with the solver's solution pool, separately from solve-time results (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: Names Mc Donald's master thesis as the first direct MIP on GNNs and generalises its GraphSAGE big-M formulation to all GNNs of form (⋆) (pp. 2, 7). Contrasts with solver-side symmetry handling (SCIP with BLISS, orbital fixing, orbital pruning), which sees only the MIP graph (p. 6). Presents (S3) as "a non-trivial generalization" of lexicographic column-ordering constraints for job-to-machine orbitopes, from bipartite to arbitrary undirected graphs, recovering the same indexing on those cases (p. 7). Notes alternatives in constraint programming and SAT/SMT (Reluplex) (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions announced in the abstract and a "Paper structure" paragraph (pp. 1-2); theorem statements in the body with proofs deferred to the appendix (pp. 4-6, 17-18); engineering choices labelled as such: "These are all engineering choices and we could have, for example, extended software other than OMLT" (p. 8). Explicit Limitations and Future-work paragraphs (p. 10); the acknowledgement thanks reviewers and meta-reviewers for helping "clarify and improve this paper" (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: symmetry-breaking constraints with a feasibility guarantee, a general MIP framework for GNNs in OMLT, a CAMD formulation (pp. 2, 10). Limits stated specifically: constant weights and biases exclude some GNN types, "an artifact of our framework"; MIP may limit GNN size, edge features enlarge the problem (p. 10); N = 8 on QM9 still solves only 29 of 50 runs with symmetry breaking (Table 7, p. 27). Future: edge features, more architectures, more efficient formulations, symmetry breaking for other isomorphism-invariant functions, aromaticity and formal charge, lead-structure optimisation for larger molecules (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (structure as insider information): evidence ✅. The structure is the graph-isomorphism symmetry; the solver cannot see it because it "does not have access to the input graph structure" (p. 6); the symmetry is stated so a program can use it ((S1)-(S3), (C26)-(C27), pp. 3-4, 8-9), and the detector question is answered by construction (Theorem 1). Matches Method 1 step 3 ("symmetry-breaking constraints over GNN inputs").
- Method 3 (trained model as constraints, then a toolkit): evidence ✅. The trained GNN is written as constraints and added to OMLT as a general framework with two switchable formulations (bilinear, big-M) compared head to head (pp. 2, 7-10); data-derived composition bounds (C22)-(C25) keep designs near the training set (pp. 8, 25), the Method 3 step 4 idea in discrete form.
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): evidence ✅. 50 runs per cell over model and solver seeds, success counts, quartiles, time to optimum separated from time to tolerance, presolve sizes (pp. 9-10, 26-27); a contrast with the single-run NeurIPS 2021 lapse recorded in SKILL.md. Only one solver (Gurobi) is used.
- Method 2 (between formulations): not applicable; bilinear and big-M are compared as two alternatives, not as a family.
- new-pattern candidate: "measure symmetry removal by exhaustive solution counting" (Table 1, p. 9).
- new-pattern candidate: "validity of symmetry-breaking constraints proved by a constructive indexing algorithm" (pp. 4-6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Break symmetry on the abstract objects (nodes) with lexicographic neighbour-set constraints, linearised by powers-of-two coefficients (pp. 3-4, 8-9).
- Prove that symmetry-breaking constraints keep at least one representative per orbit by constructing one (Algorithm 1, Theorem 1, pp. 4-6), and add a worked example (pp. 19-21).
- Count feasible solutions with the solver's solution pool to quantify how much symmetry is removed (p. 9).
- Report successful runs, quartiles, time to optimum and time to tolerance over many seeds, and leave out averages built from too few successful runs (pp. 10, 26-27).
- Derive side-constraint bounds from the training set so every training example remains feasible (pp. 8, 25).
