### S052 · Exact lexicographic scheduling and approximate rescheduling (European Journal of Operational Research 2021, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2020.08.032; arXiv:1805.03437)

**Read level**: partial (arXiv v3: abstract and §1 pp. 1-6, §2-§3 with Algorithms 1-2 and Lemmas 1-3 pp. 6-12, §4 statements of Theorems 2-5 with the Theorem 3 proof and the Type-1 part of the Theorem 5 proof pp. 13-19, §5 pp. 19-23, §6 p. 24, supplementary Appendix F on degenerate instances pp. 44-46; Appendices A-E proofs not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Dimitrios Letsios (King's College London, first author), Miten Mistry (Imperial), Ruth Misener (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the EPSRC EP/M028240/1 scheduling line (p. 24), which S079 later extends to Royal Mail.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two-stage robust P||Cmax: plan on a nominal instance, then repair after machine failures, job arrivals/cancellations and processing-time changes, with only a bounded number of changes to the plan (pp. 1-2). The entry is an open question from robust optimisation: "no prior work analyzes the price of robustness for P||Cmax" (p. 4), and determining it "has been repeatedly posed as an open question" (p. 4).
  - Transferable: start from a known open question in a neighbouring theory (price of robustness) and answer it for the simplest canonical problem before any application.
- **D2 Core idea**: Plan with a lexicographically optimal (LexOpt) schedule, i.e. lex-min of the sorted machine completion times (p. 7). LexOpt imposes an optimal substructure: the sub-schedule on any m-1 machines is optimal for its jobs (Lemma 4, pp. 14-15). Recovery keeps all "binding" decisions and places the "free" jobs by LPT (Algorithm 3, Definition 2, p. 13). For stage 1, a new LexOpt branch-and-bound with vectorial lower/upper bounds from fractional pseudo-schedules (Definition 1, Algorithms 1-2, pp. 9-12) replaces the sequential, weighting and highest-rank-objective methods.
  - Transferable: when a downstream repair step needs structure, choose among the many optimal first-stage solutions with a secondary lexicographic criterion that creates that structure.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Γ-type uncertainty set U(f, k, δ): at most k "unstable" jobs beyond factor f, plus δ surplus machines (pp. 3, 9). Recovery may not modify binding decisions (p. 13); a flexible variant that changes ≤ g of them is in the supplement (pp. 13, 23).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Strong NP-hardness of the recovery problem (Theorem 2, p. 13). Lower-bound construction: an arbitrary optimal plan gives an Ω(m) price of robustness even for one perturbation (Theorem 3, p. 14, Fig. 3). With LexOpt the ratio is tightly 2 for a single perturbation (Theorem 4, p. 15), and O(f(1+k/(m-k))(f+k)(1+δ/m)) for multiple perturbations (Theorem 5, p. 17), proved by composing per-perturbation-type guarantees in a fixed order (Table 1, p. 17); asymptotic tightness is in the supplement (p. 18, not read).
  - Transferable: pair every positive guarantee with a degenerate instance showing what happens without your structural choice (Theorem 3 versus Theorem 4); the contrast is the argument for the choice.
- **D5 Experiments**: Python/Pyomo with CPLEX 12.6.3 and Gurobi 6.5.2, code on GitHub (p. 19). Well-formed instances in three sizes and three processing-time distributions (Table 2, p. 20), plus degenerate instances placed at the P||Cmax phase transition κ* = log2 m/(m-1) (pp. 44-45). Performance profiles of time and solution quality (Fig. 4, p. 22). B&B converges on >60% of moderate and >30% of intermediate/hard instances (p. 21). Recovered makespan improves as the plan approaches LexOpt (Fig. 5, p. 23).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions are analytical (price of robustness) and algorithmic (vectorial-bound B&B) (pp. 5, 24). The B&B's limits are reported plainly: it times out on most intermediate/hard instances, and the highest-rank-objective heuristic "produces the best heuristic results for most test cases" (p. 21). Future work: min-max partitioning problems such as facility location and network design (p. 24).

**Method links**: Method 1 variant ⚠ (the exploited structure is the optimal substructure that LexOpt imposes, pp. 14-17, not structure detected in a user's model); Method 5 evidence ✅ (three rival exact methods adapted and run under one tolerance and time limit, profiles, degenerate instances calibrated by phase transition, own-method timeouts reported, pp. 20-21, 44-45); new-pattern candidate: lexicographic first stage as a robustness device (also S079).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Generate hard test instances from a known phase-transition threshold (κ* for P||Cmax) instead of uniform random draws, and report them apart from the easy "well-formed" ones (pp. 19, 44-45).
- To test "closer to the structured optimum ⇒ better recovery", draw a pool of 50 diverse optimal-ish plans from the solver's solution pool and scatter-plot normalised plan quality against normalised recovered quality (pp. 21-23).
**Quotes**:
- "Faced with the lack of strong lower bounding techniques for LexOpt scheduling, we develop a new branch-and-bound algorithm, based on vectorial bounds." (p. 24)
- "Numerical results with randomly generated instances demonstrate that the closest to LexOpt the initial solution is, the better the recovered solution quality we get." (p. 24)

### S063 · A disjunctive cut strengthening technique for convex MINLP (Optimization and Engineering 2021, doi:10.1007/s11081-020-09551-6)

**Read level**: partial (preprint 8 Aug 2020: abstract and §1 pp. 1-3, §2.1 assumptions p. 3-4, §3 theory pp. 5-9, §4 pp. 10-12, §5-§7 pp. 12-19, appendix instance tables pp. 26-27; §2.2 ESH description pp. 4-5 skimmed, appendix model pp. 25-26 skimmed).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jan Kronqvist (first author; Royal Society Newton International Fellow at Imperial, p. 19), Ruth Misener (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: start of the Kronqvist-Misener line on disjunctive structure in convex MINLP that leads to P-split (SKILL.md Method 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The efficiency of outer-approximation-type methods (OA, ECP, ESH) depends on how tight the polyhedral outer approximation is (abstract, pp. 1-2). ESH cuts are as tight as possible for the nonlinear constraints but "do not in general form supporting hyperplanes to the convex hull of all integer feasible solutions" (p. 2). The gap is largest for big-M formulations of disjunctions (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Take one ESH cut αᵀx ≤ β and one exclusive-selection constraint Σ x_i = 1. Solve one convex NLP per disjunct (max αᵀx with x_i = 1) instead of the lifted hull formulation (p. 6). Single tightening (ST) lowers the right-hand side to the largest b_i (eq. 12, p. 7); multi tightening (MT) uses Σ b_i x_i, one right-hand side per disjunct (eq. 13, p. 7). An infeasible disjunct NLP fixes x_i = 0 (Proposition 2, p. 8). Rule 1 picks which selection constraint to use by counting direct and step-one variable connections (p. 10); an objective cutoff and earlier cuts are added to the NLPs (p. 10).
  - Transferable: rather than solve the lifted convex-hull (perspective) problem, solve |D| small independent subproblems in the original space; they parallelise, avoid division by zero, and expose infeasible disjuncts as a side product (pp. 6, 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Convex, continuously differentiable g_j; compact linear constraints; Slater (Assumptions 1-2, p. 3); at least one exclusive-selection constraint (Assumption 3, p. 3). It is also assumed that the integer restrictions are the hard part, so continuous NLPs are cheap relative to the MILP relaxations (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Validity of ST by optimality of the disjunctive program (Proposition 1, p. 7). Validity of MT by contradiction using exclusivity (Theorem 1, p. 7). Dominance MT ⪰ ST by the decomposition b_i = z* − Δ_i, Δ_i ≥ 0 (Theorem 2, pp. 7-8). Solver inexactness is handled by adding ε to the right-hand side, which works because only the right-hand side comes from an NLP (p. 11).
  - Transferable: design the cut so that numerical error enters only the right-hand side; then a tolerance shift restores validity, unlike separation cuts whose angle also depends on the NLP solution (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: Basic ESH in MATLAB/OPTI with IPOPT and Gurobi, deliberately without LP preprocessing or other enhancements (pp. 12, 14). Test set: 43 big-M MINLPLib instances (clay, flay, slay, sssd, tls, stockcycle) plus self-designed p_ball instances (pp. 14-15, 26-27). Geometric-mean iterations 94 → 63 (ST) → 39 (MT) (Table 1, p. 16). The no-gain classes rsyn/syn are reported (Table 2, p. 17). A three-way comparison of big-M, hull and big-M + MT gives MT a 7.2× iteration cut versus big-M and 1.5× versus hull (Table 3, pp. 18-19).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The cut framework is presented as applicable to OA, ECP and GBD too (p. 19). Limits are stated precisely: separation-problem cuts can be stronger (pp. 11-12); the NLPs were not parallelised (p. 14); the results are "preliminary" (p. 12); and the method is expensive, so it suits big-M models rather than hull models (p. 14).

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅ (big-M "weak" vs hull "heavy" framed as the two extremes; the MT cut on big-M is tested against both on the same instances, Table 3, pp. 17-19); Method 1 evidence ✅ (exclusive-selection constraints detected as the disjunctive structure; Rule 1 picks one by variable connections, pp. 3-4, 10); Method 4 variant ⚠ (judged in a deliberately stripped-down ESH "as a proof of concept", not inside a full solver, pp. 12, 14); Method 5 evidence ✅ (failure class rsyn/syn reported, p. 17).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a parametrisable synthetic family (p_ball: balls × points × dimension) that is "challenging due to both the nonlinearity and the combinatorial aspects", and publish it next to the library instances (pp. 14, 27).
- Report your cut on the weak formulation against both the weak and the strong formulation, so readers see whether it approaches the hull and at what subproblem size (Table 3, p. 18).
- Count side effects such as variables fixed by the cut machinery (53 binaries in slay10m, 299 of 432 in stockcycle) and check that simpler means (LP-based bounds tightening) cannot fix them (p. 15).
**Quotes**:
- "These are preliminary results and are mainly intended as a proof of concept." (p. 12)
- "Therefore, the trade-off of our cut strengthening approach is a reduced computational complexity at the expense of a possibly weaker cut." (p. 12)

### S079 · Approximate and robust bounded job start scheduling for Royal Mail delivery offices (Journal of Scheduling 2021, doi:10.1007/s10951-021-00678-7; arXiv:1912.06862)

**Read level**: partial (arXiv v2: abstract and §1 pp. 1-6, §2 Theorems 1-2 pp. 6-8, theorem statements of §3-§5 pp. 8, 12, 14, 15-17, §6 pp. 19-23, §7 setup and result text pp. 23-31, §8 pp. 32-33; the approximation proofs of §3-§5 not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Dimitrios Letsios (King's College London, first author), Jeremy T. Bradley (Royal Mail GBI/Data Science Group), Suraj G, Ruth Misener, Natasha Page (Imperial) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: application sequel of S052; the conference version is COCOA 2019 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A Royal Mail delivery office has a van exit gate that bounds how many vans can leave per time unit. This gives the bounded job start scheduling problem (BJSP): P||Cmax plus at most g job starts per time unit (p. 2). In the application the makespan (working day) is fixed, so the usual price-of-robustness measure from S052 fits poorly. Robustness is instead measured by machine augmentation, i.e. short-term van rentals (pp. 4-5).
  - Transferable: when the partner's objective is fixed by the business (the working day), move the robustness measure to the resource the partner can actually buy (vans) (pp. 4-5, 20-21).
- **D2 Core idea**: Approximation side: LPT is tightly 2-approximate (Theorem 3, p. 8). Splitting instances into "long" (p_j ≥ m) and "short" (p_j < m) gives LPT 5/3 on long and optimal on short (Theorems 4-5, pp. 12, 14). Long-Short Mixing (LSM) runs the two classes in parallel to get 1.985 on a broad family, or with 1.2-machine augmentation in general (Theorem 7, p. 17). Robust side: stage 1 lexicographically minimises job completion times via weights 2^{C_j}, plus θ times the number of vans (characteristic value F(S), IP (8), pp. 21-22). Stage 2 keeps start times and assigns vans online (Definition 1, pp. 22-23).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Integer processing times, non-preemptive, identical machines. The analysis takes g = 1, justified because "any Royal Mail instance can be converted to g = 1 using small discretization" (p. 5); LSM needs m ≥ 7 (p. 15). Uncertainty is a perturbation factor F with 1/F ≤ f_j ≤ F (p. 20). B = 2 and θ are "empirically" chosen (pp. 21-22), and completion times are rounded into periods to avoid numerical issues from large exponents (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Strong NP-hardness from 3-Partition for g = 1 (Theorem 1, p. 6). An Ω(log n) integrality gap for the natural IP shows LP rounding needs a stronger relaxation (Theorem 2, p. 8). A concave relaxation bounds idle machine time before the last job start (pp. 3, 5). Proofs not read.
- **D5 Experiments**: 300 real instances from three delivery offices with partly encrypted data, a 15-minute discretisation and g taken from the data (pp. 23-24). Heuristics are compared for m ∈ [5, 50] (Fig. 9, p. 27). The MILP (8) with θ = 0 saves at least 10, 25 and 10 vans per day against historical schedules (p. 28). Robustness test: ±50% processing-time noise; a lower initial characteristic value gives fewer final vans (Fig. 14, pp. 30-32).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Better-than-2 algorithms for several cases, and a robust two-stage approach "whose performance is substantiated empirically" (p. 32), i.e. without a price-of-robustness theorem, which is left open (p. 33). A ratio below 2 without augmentation is also open; pointers to vehicle sharing across offices and to vehicle routing (pp. 32-33).

**Method links**: Method 5 variant ⚠ (the baseline is the partner's own historical practice, not rival solvers; the gap to practice is the headline, p. 28); new-pattern candidate: lexicographic first stage as a robustness device (with S052); new-pattern candidate: approximation algorithms for process systems engineering ("our larger aims", p. 32).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Split instances by a size threshold tied to the machine count (long p_j ≥ m vs short p_j < m) because idle time has a different cause in each class; prove a ratio per class, then design an algorithm that mixes them (pp. 5, 15).
- Check the real data for how many instances fall in each theoretical class before choosing which case to optimise (§7.2, pp. 24-25).
**Quotes**:
- "Furthermore, any Royal Mail instance can be converted to g = 1 using small discretization." (p. 5)
- "This finding is a strong indication that more efficient fleet management might be possible in Royal Mail delivery offices." (p. 28)

### S080 · Design of dynamic experiments for black-box model discrimination (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv:2102.03782)

**Read level**: partial (arXiv v2, titled "Using Gaussian Processes to Design Dynamic Experiments for Black-Box Model Discrimination under Uncertainty": abstract and §1 pp. 1-3, §3 opening and §3.1-§3.1.1 pp. 7-10, §3.2 opening p. 10, §4 pp. 12-17, §5-§6 pp. 17-19, author contributions p. 19; §2.1 GP regression, moment derivations and Appendices A-F not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Simon Olofsson (first author, Imperial), Eduardo S. Schultz, Adel Mhamdi, Alexander Mitsos (RWTH Aachen), Marc Peter Deisenroth (UCL), Ruth Misener (p. 1); author contributions list "Conceptualisation, S.O. and R.M." and supervision by Mitsos, Mhamdi, Deisenroth and Misener (p. 19) · **Position in trajectory**: journal-length sequel to Olofsson et al. ICML 2018 (abstract).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Choose dynamic experiments (initial state, piecewise-constant controls, measurement times) that maximise divergence between rival mechanistic models (eq. 5, p. 7). Industrial models in legacy code are black boxes without gradients (p. 3). The literature splits into analytical (linearised, Gaussian) and data-driven (Monte Carlo) design; the paper extends the GP-surrogate hybrid of Olofsson et al. (2018) to dynamic models and to more uncertainty sources (abstract, p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Put the GP prior on the one-step state transition, (x_k, u_k, θ) → x_{k+1}, not on the whole input-output map, so the input dimension stays D_x + D_u + D_θ regardless of the horizon (p. 18). Uncertainty is propagated by Taylor/moment approximations using GP gradients (pp. 8-9). A "third approach" uses the true black box for the mean and the GP only for gradients, so models are not discarded because the surrogate is inaccurate (Table 2, pp. 9-10).
  - Transferable: when a surrogate must stand in for a model over long horizons, learn the transition (step) map, not the trajectory map, to keep the surrogate's input dimension fixed (p. 18).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Gaussian process noise, measurement noise, control-input noise and parameter uncertainty (Laplace approximation) (p. 7). Noise covariances are known (p. 18), first-order state-space form, observations linear in states (pp. 17-18), open-loop design (p. 18). The authors admit that linear and Gaussian approximations are made "for analytical (and hence computational) tractability" and that errors accumulate over the horizon (p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a (no theorems; derivations of predictive moments in §3.1 and the appendices, not read).
- **D5 Experiments**: Yeast-fermentation case study (Espie and Macchietto 1989; constants from Chen and Asprey 2003). First the published optima are reproduced and slightly improved (7.8% and 8% larger divergence) (pp. 13-14). Mean vs cone chance constraints use 25 simulations each and 100-sample Monte Carlo violation levels (Table 3, Fig. 4, pp. 14-15). Under- and over-estimated control covariance are tested (Table 4, p. 16). GP surrogates do marginally worse than the analytical method (p. 17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The methodological claim is that the method "reduces to previously acquired results" (p. 18). The GP prior covariance had to be shrunk to 1e-4 I to find a feasible first experiment, "overly confident" by the authors' admission (p. 17). Closed-loop design would likely be too costly (p. 18). Future: advanced GPs needing sampling, hybrid mechanistic/data-driven models (p. 19).

**Method links**: Method 3 variant ⚠ (a learned surrogate is embedded inside the optimisation problem, but as a smooth GP whose gradients feed a local NLP solver, not as piecewise structure solved globally, pp. 8-10); Method 5 evidence ✅ (published optima re-evaluated with the original criterion before extending; Monte Carlo checks of constraint violation rather than trusting the chance-constraint model, pp. 13-15).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Before claiming a new design method, re-solve the classic published instance under the original formulation, report the objective of the published solution and yours, and attribute the gap honestly (p. 13).
- Test misspecified uncertainty by crossing modelled vs true covariance (small/large × small/large) and report success, failure and inconclusive rates separately (Table 4, p. 16).
**Quotes**:
- "Since the problem formulation is identical, we assume this discrepancy is due to the improvement in optimisation solvers after 20 years." (p. 13)
- "Hence, we are punished less for conservative estimates of the control covariance than for overly optimistic estimates." (p. 16)

### S035 · Multi-objective constrained optimization for energy applications via tree ensembles (Applied Energy 2022, doi:10.1016/j.apenergy.2021.118061; arXiv:2111.03140)

**Read level**: partial (arXiv v1: abstract and §1 pp. 2-4, §2.3 p. 8, §3.1 pp. 9-10, §3.2 opening p. 10, §3.3 opening p. 13, §3.4 pp. 14-15, §4 setup and metrics pp. 15-17, synthetic results p. 18, windfarm benchmark pp. 21-25, battery benchmark pp. 28-29, §5 p. 30; tree encoding equations §3.2 and similarity measures §3.3 not read in detail).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Alexander Thebelt (first author), Calvin Tsay (Imperial), Robert M. Lee and David Walz (BASF SE), Nathan Sudermann-Merx (DHBW Mannheim), Tom Tranter (UCL), Ruth Misener corresponding (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the multi-objective, constrained extension of ENTMOOT in the trees → ENTMOOT → OMLT line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Energy-system design problems are black boxes with several objectives, mixed continuous/categorical inputs and explicit safety or physical input constraints (abstract, p. 3). Evolutionary methods need many evaluations and handle constraints poorly. GP-based BO needs one-hot encodings for categories (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep ENTMOOT's tree-ensemble surrogate encoded as a MIP (Mišić formulation) and its distance-based exploration term (pp. 4, 10, 13). Add a weighted Chebyshev scalarisation with random weights, made single-level by an epigraph variable (eqs. 4-5, pp. 9-10). Categorical uncertainty uses similarity-based measures (p. 4). The acquisition problem is a nonconvex MIQP solved by Gurobi 9 (p. 14), so input constraints are satisfied by construction.
  - Transferable: when the surrogate is optimised by a MIP solver anyway, write the problem's known input constraints (including symmetry-breaking "supporting" constraints) directly into the acquisition problem, so every proposal is feasible (pp. 21, 25).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Black-box evaluations dominate the cost (p. 15). Objective normalisation uses observed min/max unless the user supplies bounds (p. 10). 400 trees of depth 3, κ = 1.96 fixed across all runs "to allow for a fair comparison" (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a (no theorems; the epigraph reformulation is argued equivalent, p. 10). Scaling is discussed qualitatively: the encoding grows with ensemble size, the exploration term with data size and dimension (pp. 14-15).
- **D5 Experiments**: Synthetic bi-objective benchmarks with GD, IGD, MPFE and VR metrics, where the "true" front comes from long NSGA-II runs (pp. 16-18). Wind-farm layout with 16 turbines (32 continuous + binary variables) and battery design in PyBaMM (1 categorical + 9 continuous, 7 input constraints) use bounded hypervolume (pp. 21-29). Every comparison uses 25 seeds (101-125), the same initial points for all methods, and medians with quartiles (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Presented as an "all-in-one" method for expensive constrained multi-objective black boxes (abstract, p. 30). The authors concede that these formulations "do not scale well" (p. 14) and that a 100 s Gurobi limit applied only to their method (p. 16). Only bi-objective cases were tested (p. 30).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅ (the trained tree ensemble is written as MIP constraints and optimised with input constraints; BASF co-authors; code released, pp. 10, 14, 15); Method 5 variant ⚠ (fairness protocol of same initial points, 25 seeds and default competitor settings, and the time-limit asymmetry disclosed, pp. 15-16; but the only rival is NSGA-II plus random/feasible sampling, with no GP-based MOBO such as TSEMO or ParEGO run, although both are named on p. 4).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Give every method the same seeded initial design and report medians with first/third quartiles over 25 fixed seeds, because "performance of different algorithms is highly dependent on the quality of the initial population" (pp. 15, 18).
- Include a pure feasible-sampling baseline: in the wind-farm case it alone beat NSGA-II, which separates the value of feasibility from the value of the surrogate (p. 24).
- Compare against the source benchmark's published best (here up to 10^6 iterations on a discrete grid) to show what the continuous formulation buys (p. 25).
**Quotes**:
- "We note that this procedure only affects the method presented here, and not other tools that we compare against." (p. 16)
- "Based on preliminary studies we found that the performance of different algorithms is highly dependent on the quality of the initial population." (p. 18)

### S057 · ROmodel: modeling robust optimization problems in Pyomo (Optimization and Engineering 2022, doi:10.1007/s11081-021-09703-2; arXiv:2105.08598)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v1 pp. 1-17: all sections, code listings, Table 1 and Fig. 3; references pp. 17-19 not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Johannes Wiebe (first author, EPSRC/Schlumberger CASE student), Ruth Misener (pp. 1, 17) · **Position in trajectory**: software paper of the robust-optimisation line (GP-based uncertainty sets, drill scheduling); a sibling of OMLT's "one interface, switchable formulations" design.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Many robust-optimisation variants and the domain knowledge they need "discourage practitioners" from leaving deterministic models, and the lack of a common platform makes approaches hard to compare (p. 2). Existing tools (ROME, RSOME, ROC++, PyROS) are solvers rather than modelling layers, and JuMPeR is tied to Julia (pp. 2-3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three Pyomo objects: UncParam, UncSet (generic Pyomo constraints or library polyhedral/ellipsoidal sets) and AdjustableVar (linear decision rules) (pp. 4-8). Three solvers: duality-based reformulation, cutting planes and nominal (pp. 8-11). The reformulation solver detects each uncertain constraint's set geometry and applies the matching transformation, or raises an error (Fig. 1, p. 9). Extension needs only a library-set class plus _check_applicability/_reformulate functions; the demonstration is GP and warped-GP sets for black-box chance constraints (pp. 11-14).
  - Transferable: design the extension interface as "detect applicability + reformulate", so a new reformulation plugs in without touching models (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Uncertain constraints must match a known geometry for reformulation. Cutting planes need convex sets and appropriate master/sub-solvers (p. 10). GP sets are decision-dependent, so cutting planes do not apply (p. 13), and they need constraints linear in the uncertain parameter (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a (software paper; reformulations cited from [3, 5, 26], p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: Six case studies (portfolio, knapsack, pooling, facility location with adjustable variables, GP-priced production planning, drill scheduling), 30 uncertainty-set sizes each (pp. 14-15). Median times: reformulation 74 ms vs cuts 330 ms. Cuts win on nonconvex pooling with ellipsoidal sets and on facility location, where reformulation does not finish in 10 minutes (Table 1, p. 15). Transformation overhead has median 3.2 ms (p. 16). Conservatism is checked as ellipsoidal ⊇ polyhedral, with standard GP sets less conservative than warped ones (Fig. 3, pp. 16-17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Accessibility and a platform "for prototyping novel robust optimization algorithms and comparing them" (p. 17). Limits are stated as scope: only linear decision rules (p. 8), and not every set that can be modelled can be solved (p. 6).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅ for its toolkit half (rival reformulations and uncertainty sets become switchable options behind one modelling interface, pp. 2, 6, 15; GP models trained in GPy enter the optimisation model as uncertainty sets, pp. 11-12); Method 1 evidence ✅ (automatic recognition of set geometry before transformation, p. 3, Fig. 1 p. 9); Method 4 evidence ✅ (per-class verdict: reformulation faster overall, cuts better on pooling-ellipsoidal and facility, pp. 15-16).
**Transferable techniques**:
- For a modelling-layer paper, report solver-time and transformation-time separately, so readers see that the layer's own overhead is negligible (median 3.2 ms, p. 16).
- Ship every case study as an importable example (`romodel.example`) so the paper's results are one function call away (p. 14).
**Quotes**:
- "However, not every set that can be modeled with Pyomo constraints can necessarily also be solved." (p. 6)

### S056 · An optimization approach coupling preprocessing with model regression for enhanced chemometrics (Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research 2023, doi:10.1021/acs.iecr.2c04583)

**Read level**: partial (publisher version: abstract and §1 pp. 1-2, §4 formulation pp. 4-6 including the robustness-metric text, §5 setup p. 7, §5.1 pp. 8-9, §5.2 pp. 9-12, §5.3 pp. 13-15, §6 pp. 15-16; §2-§3 on preprocessing and PLS background and the Supporting Information not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chrysoula D. Kappatou (first author), James Odgers (Imperial), Salvador García-Muñoz (Eli Lilly), Ruth Misener corresponding (p. 16) · **Position in trajectory**: industrial chemometrics application that uses the group's ENTMOOT as the optimiser.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In multivariate calibration, preprocessing (SNV, Savitzky-Golay filter) is chosen by trial and error before PLS regression. This sequential practice "fails to capture their synergistic effect" (p. 2), and model selection uses accuracy (RMSEP) alone (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Treat preprocessing parameters and the number of latent variables as one decision vector u = (window, polynomial order, derivative order, α) (p. 6). Optimise it with ENTMOOT for either accuracy (RMSEP) or a new robustness metric (p. 7). The metric matches first and second moments of model predictions across the realisations of a known variability source, such as plant scale (eq. 3, pp. 4-5).
  - Transferable: turn "robust to a known nuisance factor" into a loss by grouping validation predictions by the nuisance level and penalising pairwise distances of their means and variances (pp. 4-5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Unimodal output distributions, so two moments suffice. Realisations are uncorrelated with other variation sources; similar concentration levels across classes; categorical, not continuous, nuisance (pp. 4-5). The weight β between moments is set "by trial and error" (p. 10). The domain is a small integer grid with step 5 in window size so that complete enumeration is possible (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a (no theorems).
- **D5 Experiments**: One simulated example and two NIR/Raman literature datasets with 70/20/10 or 70/30 splits (pp. 7-13). Accuracy-optimised models choose more latent variables (up to seven), while robustness-optimised ones choose one or two (pp. 9, 11). Complete enumeration confirms the ENTMOOT optimum in all but one case, which is within 1% (pp. 12, 15). Random-split sensitivity (Table 6) and several further test sets are reported (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The method automates building and updating chemometric models (p. 15). Robust models have lower complexity "together with an inferior performance in terms of model prediction" in some cases (p. 15). Only the two extremes (accuracy-only, robustness-only) are optimised. Combining them is left open, and "deciding on the trade-off between the two objectives is identified as a challenging task" (p. 16). Code is public, except the third example "for confidentiality reasons" (p. 7).

**Method links**: Method 5 evidence ✅ (the black-box optimiser's answer is independently checked by complete enumeration on a deliberately coarsened domain, pp. 7, 12, 15); Method 3 evidence ✅ (in-house tree-ensemble optimiser reused on a partner's problem, p. 7); Method 2 contradiction ✗ (two textbook extremes, accuracy and robustness, are each optimised but the family between them is not built: "we show only the extreme cases", p. 16).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When you use Bayesian/black-box optimisation, choose the decision grid so that complete enumeration is still feasible for the paper, and report where BO and enumeration disagree (pp. 7, 15).
- Test the nuisance-robustness claim by holding out an entire nuisance level (the middle one kept in training to avoid extrapolation) and checking which model complexity still predicts it (p. 9).
