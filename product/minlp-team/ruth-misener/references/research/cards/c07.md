### S017 · Argumentation for explainable scheduling (AAAI 2019, DOI 10.1609/aaai.v33i01.33012752; arXiv:1811.05437)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v2 "Full Paper with Proofs", pp. 1–9: all text, proofs, examples, references).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Kristijonas Čyras (first author), Dimitrios Letsios, Francesca Toni, all at Imperial (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: written under the EPSRC ROAD2H project, with Letsios on the "Uncertainty-Aware Planning and Scheduling" grant (acknowledgements, p. 8). The scheduling side continues Letsios–Misener lexicographic scheduling (cited p. 8; refs p. 9). The text does not say what Misener herself contributed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters through a user-trust gap: solvers state optimality conditions "with formal mathematics, so users often consider the optimization solver as an unexplainable black box" (p. 1). The application pull is nurse rostering, where staff and managers ask about fairness, optimality and recovery after illness (p. 1). The problem is stated as three questions: why a schedule is or is not feasible, efficient, or consistent with fixed user decisions (§3, p. 2). Three desiderata frame the answer: soundness and completeness, computational tractability, and cognitive tractability (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: turn a vague user need ("explain this") into named formal desiderata, then prove one theorem that meets all of them together (Theorem 5.1, p. 8, discharges the p. 3 list).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Each assignment variable x_ij becomes an argument a_ij. Feasibility becomes mutual attacks between machines competing for the same job, so stable extensions correspond one-to-one to feasible schedules (Def. 4.1, Thm 4.1, p. 4). Optimality is replaced by two necessary exchange conditions, SEP and PEP (Def. 3.2, Lemma 3.2, p. 3). They are encoded by removing or adding attacks for a specific schedule (Def. 4.4, p. 5). A negative user decision becomes a self-attack, and a positive one becomes an unattacked argument (Def. 4.6, p. 6). An explanation is a single attack inside the extension or a single non-attack (Defs 5.1, 5.5, p. 7), filled into natural-language templates (p. 7). Stable semantics was chosen because it is global and polynomially verifiable (p. 4). The change from the argumentation literature: explanations are paths of length 1 instead of reasoning chains (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: when certifying optimality is NP-hard, explain against a polynomially checkable set of necessary optimality conditions (here, single and pairwise exchanges), and say plainly that you have done so (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Makespan scheduling on identical parallel machines, with m, n > 1 (p. 2). "Efficient" means feasible plus SEP plus PEP, explicitly not optimal (pp. 3, 5). Fixed decisions must satisfy D⁻ ∩ D⁺ = ∅, with no job positively fixed to two machines (Def. 3.3, p. 3). The authors admit the restriction to efficiency is forced by NP-hardness (p. 3). Contractual constraints and robustness are left out (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: Each AF gets an iff theorem. Thm 4.1 argues by contradiction on Σ_i x_ij > 1 (a conflict) or = 0 (an unattacked argument) (p. 4). Lemma 3.2 proves SEP and PEP necessary by exchange: move or swap a critical job and compare makespans (p. 3). Thm 4.3 checks that the SEP/PEP attack edits preserve conflict-freeness and stability in the underlying feasibility AF (p. 5), and Thm 4.5 does the same for fixed decisions (p. 6). Every construction has a complexity lemma using adjacency lists: O(nm²) to build and O(n²m²) to verify stability (Lemmas 4.2, 4.4, 4.6, pp. 5–6). Thm 5.1 assembles them (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: pair each correspondence theorem with a lemma bounding construction and verification time, so that "tractable" is a proved statement rather than an adjective (pp. 5–6, 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a. There is no computational study and no user study. Everything is illustrated on 2-machine examples with 2 or 3 jobs (Examples 4.3–5.8, pp. 5–7; Fig. 3, p. 6). The paper states "No new data was collected" (p. 8).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: It claims to have found no prior work on explainable scheduling, or on argumentation used to explain optimization (p. 8). It separates itself from explainable planning, argumentation for multicriteria decisions, abduction for scheduling, and optimization used to implement argumentation solvers ("opposite to using argumentation to supplement optimization", p. 8). Comparison with ML-explanation formalisms (decision diagrams, counterfactuals) is deferred (p. 8). The OR anchors are Graham 1969 and Williamson–Shmoys (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paradigm gets a name (ArgOpt) and a layer diagram on page 1 (Fig. 1, p. 1). Contributions are introduced by a question: "What makes an optimization solution good?" (p. 1). The text follows a definition–lemma–theorem–example cadence with one running nurse example, and prints the natural-language templates verbatim (p. 7). Claims are modest ("We take the first steps", p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a conceptual paradigm with correspondence and complexity theorems; there is no software and no user evaluation. "Cognitive tractability" is defined as polynomial explanation size (p. 3) and proved in that form only (p. 8). Future work is concrete: contractual constraints, robustness to uncertainty, explanations for "fractional relaxations or cutting planes" (p. 8), and a formal comparison with ML explanation methods (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the MILP's structure is re-represented as a graph object. Feasibility reduces to constraint (1d) alone (Lemma 3.1, pp. 2–3), and the assignment structure becomes attacks (p. 4). But the target is a human explanation, not a stronger relaxation, and no detector is validated.
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): this paper is outside the solver-paper genre. There is no benchmarking and only toy instances (pp. 5–8), so her audit standard does not travel to this co-authored AI-venue theory paper.
- New-pattern candidate: **necessary conditions as tractable certificates** (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: **optimization brought into a neighbouring AI formalism** (pp. 1, 4, 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace an NP-hard certificate (optimality) by polynomially checkable necessary conditions, and explain violations of those (pp. 3, 5).
- State the desiderata for an explanation formally (sound and complete, polynomial time, polynomial size) and prove them in a single theorem (pp. 3, 8).
- Give every encoding theorem a companion lemma on construction and verification cost (pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "We have restricted attention to feasible and efficient (rather than optimal) solutions because answering and explaining why an arbitrary schedule is optimal in polynomial time is ruled out due to NP-hardness of makespan scheduling, unless P=NP." (p. 3)
- "SEP and PEP are necessary optimality conditions (but not sufficient; see e.g. the list-scheduling algorithm tightness analysis in (Williamson and Shmoys 2011))." (p. 3)

### S021 · Scoring positive semidefinite cutting planes for quadratic optimization via trained neural networks (preprint 2019, no DOI/arXiv in list)
**Read level**: full (preprint dated November 19, 2019, pp. 1–41). Main text pp. 1–35 read closely. Appendices A.1–A.4 (pp. 35–37) read. Reference list (pp. 37–41) skimmed. The cell-by-cell timings of Table 6(b) (p. 28) were skimmed for pattern only.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Radu Baltean-Lugojan (first; EPSRC DTP and IBM PhD Fellowship), Pierre Bonami and Andrea Tramontani (CPLEX Optimization, IBM) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: written during her EPSRC Research Fellowship (p. 1). It follows Baltean-Lugojan & Misener on pooling structure (ref. 8, p. 37). The code is released at cog-imperial/SDPCutSel-via-NN (ref. 9, p. 38). This is her first paper that puts machine learning *inside* the solver rather than optimizing *over* a trained model.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: SDP relaxations complement polyhedral ones, but "global optimization solvers built on polyhedral relaxations cannot fully exploit this advantage" (abstract, p. 1). The entry is the host solver's needs, set out as four desiderata: easy integration, computationally light, few cuts, convergence (p. 2). The gap is that MIP cut selection relies on feasibility (violation) and ignores the objective (pp. 2–3). Selecting by objective improvement would mean solving many small SDPs, which the authors call "a prohibitive proposition" (p. 3). BoxQP is the test bed and QCQP the extension (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: write the host solver's requirements as numbered desiderata on page 2, and close the paper by ticking each one with a section pointer (pp. 2, 34–35).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The PSD constraint is relaxed into low-dimensional principal-submatrix PSD constraints over a cover (S(V), p. 5; S^{E*}_n, p. 6). The classic eigenvalue cuts are kept (EigCut, p. 11), but candidate submatrices ρ are ranked by expected objective improvement I_X(ρ). That score comes from a ρ-subproblem SDP at the current LP point x̃ (pp. 11–12). The SDP is replaced by a neural-network estimator f̂*_n(Q_ρ, x̃_ρ) for n ≤ 5, trained offline and independent of any instance (pp. 12, 15). Optimality and feasibility measures are combined in C(ρ), with a switch to pure feasibility once optimality selection does worse than random (Def. 5.2.1, pp. 24–25). What stays the same: eigenvalue cuts in the extended (x, X) space with a McCormick base, chosen for integration over a projection (p. 4). What changes: small cut dimension, selection by objective, and selection cost moved offline.
  - Transferable technique: if a good selection score requires solving a small parametric convex problem thousands of times, sample that problem offline over a normalised input domain, train a regression surrogate, and keep a cheap exact check as a fallback (pp. 12–18, 24–26).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The model is a QP over [0,1]^N (any bounded box rescales) with linear constraints and an indefinite symmetric Q (p. 3). The sparsity graph has no disconnected vertex (p. 4). X_ii ≤ x_i is required to bound the SDP (p. 4). Fixing x at the LP solution "guarantees an unconditional matrix PSD completion", so every cut is valid (Remark 3.2.5, p. 10). Network inputs are rescaled so that eigenvalues lie in [−1, 1]; this relies on f being linear in Q_ρ (p. 15). Training data are sampled uniformly over eigenvalues and orthonormal bases, not over matrix entries (Table 1, p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: Lemma 3.1.2 adds a universal vertex v to the chordal extension so that the x variables enter every clique, then applies the Grone/Fukuda chordal decomposition (pp. 6–7). Remark 3.1.4 gives the hierarchy S₂ ⊇ S₃ ⊇ … ⊇ S (p. 7). Lemma 3.2.3 proves optimality-based outer-approximation via the separation theorem. Part (ii), "becomes more restrictive" with more constraints on B or a larger fixing (p. 9; proof App. A.1, p. 35), is later cited to *explain* observations (o4)–(o6) and (o9) (pp. 26, 29). Corollary 3.2.4 gives z_qp ≥ z^L_qp (p. 10; App. A.2, pp. 35–36), which explains why optimality selection ends with weaker bounds (o3, p. 24). Lemma 4.1.1 is a short eigenvalue-to-entry bound (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: prove one small monotonicity lemma that predicts the direction of every empirical trade-off, then cite it beside each numbered observation (Lemma 3.2.3(ii) → (o4)–(o6), (o9), pp. 26, 29).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The network setup: 1M samples split 75/15/10, tanh with scaled conjugate gradient, 3–4 hidden layers of about 50–64 neurons, 5–20 h of training (App. A.3, p. 36). Fit and residual plots use a 0.5M test set (Fig. 7, p. 17). Speed is compared with Mosek, SeDuMi and the eigendecomposition: the network is 1–2 orders of magnitude faster than the interior-point solvers (Table 2, p. 18). The cut loop (Algorithm 1, p. 19) runs 20 rounds, selects a fraction of |V| capped at 5000, and stops when a round improves by less than 0.1%. It is tested on all 99 BoxQP instances grouped by size and density (Table 4, p. 20), with random selection as a control (Table 3, p. 19; Figs 9–10, pp. 22–23). Further studies: sparse vs. dense eigen-cuts (Table 6, p. 28); n = 3, 4, 5 (Table 7, p. 30); chordal extensions (Table 8, p. 30). The method is also mixed with triangle inequalities and compared with BGL, described as a "very good proxy of the current CPLEX linear relaxation" (Remark 5.4.1, p. 31; Table 9, p. 32; Figs 13–14, pp. 32–33). QCQP instances come from Bao et al., with all 320 results on GitHub (p. 33). Runs use a single thread on AWS, and per-instance results are on GitHub (p. 18). The metric is % gap closed between M and M+S, plus time. **Not run**: full branch-and-cut, or time to global optimality.
  - Transferable technique: report gap closed *per added cut* (Fig. 11, p. 25) and the share of selected cuts that are actually violated, a false-positive rate (Fig. 12, p. 25). This separates cut quality from cut quantity, against a random-selection control.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper builds on Qualizza–Belotti–Margot and Sherali-style eigenvalue cuts (pp. 8, 11). It answers Dey–Molinaro's "hint to enlist machine learning in cut selection" (p. 3) and takes deep objective cuts from Boyd–Vandenberghe (pp. 11, 23). It agrees with predecessors openly ("Table 6 largely echoes the results of Qualizza et al.", p. 26). It disagrees openly too: its bottom-up choice of cut dimension is "contrary to [94, 105]" (p. 29). Multiterm relaxations, her own edge-concave cuts among them, are placed as complements in a comparison table (Remark 3.1.7, pp. 8–9). It extends the MIP intuition that sparse cuts are strong to the continuous setting (p. 29).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Numbered desiderata (p. 2) are closed one by one in the conclusion (pp. 34–35). Numbered observations (o1)–(o10) are each tied to a lemma (pp. 23–29), and Table 3 summarises the strategy trade-offs (p. 19). The central claim is hedged: "Our hypothesis ... We claim, and attempt to show in the sequel" (p. 20). The take-away ends with an exclamation mark (p. 27). The two-part contribution sentence ("(i) sparser ... (ii) explicitly selected to improve the objective") appears in both the abstract and the conclusion (pp. 1, 35).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and engineering, with open code (p. 3). The limits are concrete:
  - no advantage on fully dense instances (p. 27);
  - the estimator becomes inaccurate as n grows (p. 29);
  - estimator error flips close rankings in late rounds (pp. 20–22);
  - jumbo high-density instances with n = 5 run out of memory (Table 7, p. 30);
  - QCQPs with little objective structure "require further research" (p. 33);
  - the Lagrangian extension may not converge (App. A.4, p. 37).

  Future directions: copositive 3D relaxations, pooling cuts, and orthogonality-based selection (p. 35). The effect inside a full branch-and-cut is not shown.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (evidence ✅): the n-dimensional family S^{E*}_n runs from an SOCP-type relaxation at n = 2 to the full SDP. It reaches the full SDP once n reaches the clique number of the chordal extension (Lemma 3.1.2, p. 6; Remarks 3.1.4 and 3.1.6, pp. 7–8). The paper sweeps n ∈ {3, 4, 5} (Table 7, p. 30) and labels its choice of n as "heuristic guidelines" (Remark 5.3.1, p. 29).
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): it exploits the sparsity-pattern graph and the objective structure (pp. 3, 7, 11), and prunes zero-coefficient subproblems (P*₃, p. 30).
- Method 4 (variant ⚠): the design targets the whole solver (desideratum 2, p. 2; "easy integration" over projection, p. 4). The method is also judged with other cut classes present, where the optimality-vs-feasibility gap vanishes ((o5), p. 26; §5.4, pp. 31–32). But the evidence stops at the root cut loop, with no branch-and-cut runs.
- Method 5 (evidence ✅, partial): the whole BoxQP set, a random control, a proxy for the commercial relaxation, losses written in (dense instances, p. 27) and public per-instance results (pp. 18, 33).
- New-pattern candidate: **a learned surrogate for an inner solve inside the solver**, i.e. ML for optimization rather than optimization over ML (pp. 3, 12–18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Train an instance-independent surrogate offline on a scaled canonical domain, and rescale each instance's inputs to it using a linearity property (pp. 14–15).
- Combine a learned score with an exact feasibility check, and fall back to the exact rule once the learned rule does worse than random (Def. 5.2.1, pp. 24–25).
- Sample matrix inputs by spectrum (random eigenvalues times a Haar-random orthonormal basis), not by entries, so that training covers curvature uniformly (Table 1, p. 14; Figs 4–5, p. 13).
- Put a "Diff." column between competing strategies and show that the gap disappears once standard cut classes are added (Table 5, p. 27).

**Quotes**:
- "An expensive approximation may be useful for a specific application, but general-purpose solvers cannot invest too much time in possibly unproductive cutting surfaces." (p. 2)
- "There is no advantage for using our proposed sparse cuts for fully-dense instances." (p. 27)

### S048 · Robust optimization for the pooling problem (Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research 2019, DOI 10.1021/acs.iecr.9b01772; arXiv:1906.07612)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v1, pp. 1–30: text, all figures, Tables 1–2, references, nomenclature).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Johannes Wiebe (first; EPSRC/Schlumberger CASE studentship), Inês Cecílio (Schlumberger Cambridge Research) (p. 1; acknowledgements, p. 21) · **Place in trajectory**: an invited contribution to the I&EC Research "2019 Class of Influential Researchers" issue (p. 21). It extends Wiebe–Cecílio–Misener's ESCAPE-29 conference paper (p. 3) and sits in her pooling line (refs 22, 23, 30–33, pp. 24–25). All NLPs are solved with her own ANTIGONE 1.1 (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The pooling problem "has largely been treated deterministically" (abstract, p. 1), and robust optimization has mostly been applied to convex or MILP problems (abstract; p. 2). The paper sorts robust problems into four categories by convexity in x and concavity in ξ (pp. 4–5, Fig. 1). Pooling falls into Category 2, which has "received limited attention" (p. 5). The application case: uncertainty that stays unresolved until the final blend, and safety-critical quality limits (p. 3). The paper argues against multistage approaches because they "assume perfect knowledge of the uncertain parameters after the uncertainty has been revealed" (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: place your problem in a 2×2 grid, outer convex or not × inner concave or not, to show which known method families still apply and where the gap lies (Fig. 1, p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Two standard robust approaches are applied to uncertain inlet concentrations in the quality constraints. One is the duality reformulation (Table 1 and eq. (14), p. 11). The other is robust cutting planes (pp. 6–7, 13), linked to a global solver that has no callbacks by adaptively tightening the master problem's tolerance (Algorithm 1, p. 7). A safety-factor model serves as a practitioner baseline, and Algorithm 2 (bisection plus secant) finds the smallest safe factor (pp. 14–15). Ellipsoids correlated by geography are built with a squared-exponential kernel (p. 10). The q-formulation is chosen because "there are no potentially uncertain equality constraints" (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: when the global solver has no lazy-constraint callback, solve the master to a loose tolerance until a robustly feasible point appears, then shrink the tolerance geometrically (Algorithm 1, p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Uncertain constraints are treated one at a time and the objective is certain, both without loss of generality via an epigraph (p. 4). Only C_ik is uncertain, inside p-norm sets of size r (p. 9). Experiments set Ĉ = C (p. 15). The model is single-stage (p. 3). Squaring the ellipsoidal constraint is valid only if the nominal constraint is also added (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: The robust counterpart follows by strong duality (pp. 6, 11). For a box set it reduces to a worst-case coefficient shift, so complexity does not change (p. 12). Model sizes are counted: an ellipsoid adds O(|i||j|) constraints and introduces higher-order terms, while a polyhedron adds O(|i||j||k|) but stays bilinear (p. 12). Cutting-plane convergence is argued from the finitely many vertices for box and polyhedral sets, and from Mínguez–Casero-Alonso's outer-approximation equivalence for ellipsoids (pp. 13–14). The safety factor is shown equivalent to box uncertainty on the bounds P^U, P^L (p. 14). No new theorem is stated; the arguments are in prose.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Setup: GAMS 26.1.0 on an i7-6700, ANTIGONE 1.1 for NLPs and CPLEX 12.8 for MILPs. 14 literature instances × 30 values of r × 3 geometries, with a 1 h limit, 10⁻⁶ tolerance and at most 200 cuts (p. 15). Results:
  - flow diagrams for Adhya 1 (Fig. 2, p. 16), with the profit drop from 446.2 to 65.9 once Product 2 stops beyond r = 0.14 (p. 17);
  - objective-vs-r curves (Figs 3–4, pp. 16–18);
  - reformulation vs. cutting planes (Fig. 5, p. 19);
  - % solved and median time, computed over solved instances only (Table 2, p. 20);
  - an average of 13.3 iterations and 335 constraints, against 328/166/154 for the reformulations (p. 18);
  - the safety-factor comparison (Fig. 6, p. 21) and correlation scenarios (Fig. 7, p. 22).

  Unsolved runs are marked in black (pp. 16, 18). Only one global solver is used.
  - Transferable technique: plot the price of robustness (objective against uncertainty-set size) and mark where the optimal flow structure changes, so readers see the decision consequence and not just the cost (Figs 2–3, pp. 16–17).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper extends the authors' own ESCAPE paper (p. 3). It sets itself next to Li–Misener–Floudas demand-uncertainty robust scheduling and Barton/Li two-stage stochastic pooling (p. 2), and takes the reformulation-vs-cutting-planes question from Bertsimas–Dunning–Lubin for MILP (p. 7). It relates cutting planes to "backoff" in control (pp. 7–8). Robust optimization is reviewed first (§2), as a tutorial for the I&EC audience (pp. 4–8).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Four contribution bullets (p. 3). The verdict is balanced: cutting planes solve more, "the reformulation approach has one important advantage" (p. 19). Causal claims are hedged ("most likely due to", "may, however, be at least partially because", p. 18). There is a graphical abstract (p. 1). The conclusion is short and has no future-work paragraph (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a methodological transfer and a computational comparison. The limits are named concretely:
  - the polyhedral set "may also be less robust" (p. 17);
  - the reformulation does poorly on ellipsoids (pp. 17–18);
  - intermediate cutting-plane solutions are not robustly feasible (p. 19);
  - the safety factor is "generally a conservative approximation" (p. 20);
  - temporal and multistage effects are excluded (p. 2);
  - lazy-constraint integration is left as an idea (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): a structural property selects the method. The problem is linear, hence concave, in every uncertain parameter, so Category 2 applies, and the q-formulation has no uncertain equalities (p. 9).
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): the verdict is split by property, not given to one winner. Cutting planes solve more; reformulation gives feasible intermediate solutions (p. 19).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): the instances are a standard literature set, and unsolved runs and medians are disclosed (pp. 15–20). But the paper uses one global solver, her own; computes median times over solved instances only (explained, p. 18); and describes no independent check of the returned points.
- New-pattern candidate: **calibrate a practitioner baseline to the rigorous answer** (minimal safety factor, Algorithm 2, pp. 14–15, 19–20).
- Also evidence for SKILL Heuristic 10 (uncertainty made explicit), which cites this DOI.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Adapt the master problem's tolerance to couple cutting planes with a black-box global solver (p. 7).
- Find the smallest safety factor that makes a conservative heuristic robustly feasible, and use it as the baseline (Algorithm 2, p. 15).
- Build correlated uncertainty sets from a spatial kernel on source locations (eqs (10)–(11), p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Unlike mature MILP solver software, however, global optimization solvers do not routinely offer callbacks for adding lazy or incumbent constraints." (p. 7)
- "In contrast, intermediate solutions in the cutting plane algorithm are often not robustly feasible." (p. 19)

### S091 · Symmetry detection for quadratic optimization using binary layered graphs (Processes 7(11) 2019, DOI 10.3390/pr7110838)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–12: text, algorithms, figures, author contributions, references).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Georgia Kouyialis (first; later Schlumberger), Xiaoyu Wang (Imperial) (p. 1). Author contributions: "Conceptualization, G.K. and R.M.; methodology, G.K.; validation, X.W.; writing, G.K., X.W., and R.M.; supervision, R.M." (p. 10) · **Place in trajectory**: Kouyialis's DTP project, funded under Misener's EPSRC fellowship (p. 10). It follows their FOCAPO 2017 heat-exchanger symmetry paper and the Letsios–Kouyialis–Misener heuristics (refs 27–28, p. 11). SKILL.md cites this paper under Method 1.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Symmetry creates equivalent solutions, so branch-and-bound may "explore many unnecessary subtrees" (p. 1; abstract). Detection methods already exist for CP, IP and MINLP via Liberti's expression graphs (p. 1). The motivating examples are circle packing and two-machine scheduling (pp. 1–2, Figs 1–2). The problem: compute the formulation group of a QCQP (Def. 1, pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The QCQP is written as a sparse tensor of size (n+1)×(n+1)×(m+2n) (pp. 3–4, Figs 4–5), turned into an edge-labelled, vertex-coloured graph (p. 4), then into a vertex-coloured "binary layered graph" that nauty accepts (Def. 2, p. 5; Algorithms 1–2, p. 6). Coefficient labels are written in binary across ⌈log₂(ℓ+1)⌉ layers, avoiding one layer per distinct coefficient (p. 5). This is offered as an alternative to Liberti's expression DAG (pp. 3, 7). The symmetry is then exploited in a point-packing grid approximation whose search drops reflected arrangements (Algorithms 3–4, pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable technique: when an automorphism tool accepts only vertex colours, encode ℓ edge labels in binary across ⌈log₂(ℓ+1)⌉ graph layers instead of ℓ layers (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Since dom(f) may be nonconvex, symmetry is required on the whole box, which gives a restriction of the formulation group (p. 3). The bounds are finite (p. 2). The grid approximation can miss the optimum: for 6 points the grid gives θ = 0.5 against the optimal 0.6009 (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: No new proofs. Correctness rests on two cited results: the conversion from edge-labelled to vertex-coloured graphs is an isomorphism (McKay–Piperno, p. 5), and Liberti's map from graph automorphisms to the formulation group (p. 3). A 4-variable worked example yields the generators and G = (x₁x₄)(x₂x₃) (p. 7). The pruning bound 1/(k*−1) gives a minimum distance between points (pp. 8–9).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Thin. There is one worked example through dreadnaut (p. 7) and a qualitative comparison with the DAG representation: "the directed acyclic graph representation uses a smaller number of vertices and edges" on that example (p. 7). For point packing, one figure shows the runtime of 2D symmetry removal for 6 points against grid size and the number of occupied grids m (Fig. 11, p. 10). The exhaustive-search results were "eliminated" from the figure (p. 9). Hardware is stated (p. 9). There is no instance library, no detection timing and no solver comparison.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper builds on Liberti's expression-graph detection, Margot, and Pfetsch–Rehn's computational comparison (pp. 1, 3). The tensor representation is "new to this paper" (p. 3). It cites the group's own heat-exchanger symmetry work and SUSPECT as related structure detectors (refs 27, 28, 42, pp. 11–12).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A short applied paper whose abstract is repeated nearly verbatim at the end of the introduction (pp. 1–2). The conclusion claims "we can design significantly better methods" (p. 10), which is stronger than the single-instance evidence. A CRediT-style contribution statement records roles (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a representation and a small demonstration of exploiting it. The limits are admitted: the grid can miss the optimum (p. 8); on the example the DAG is smaller (p. 7); and a symmetry-aware branch-and-bound was not built (p. 9). The claimed advantage is for many distinct coefficients (p. 7), but it is not measured. Future relevance: industrial packing and nesting problems (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅ for steps 3 and 6): symmetry is stated as a graph so that a program (nauty) can match it, and the symmetry is then exploited (pp. 5–9).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠ for step 5): the detector is not validated against a labelled library. That contrasts with SUSPECT's 1527-problem validation cited in SKILL.md.
- Method 5 (contradiction ✗): no benchmark set, no competitor timings, no detection-cost measurement and a single 6-point experiment (pp. 7–10). The auditor standard of her solver papers is absent here.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Binary layering of edge labels to feed coefficient-labelled graphs to vertex-colour-only automorphism tools (Def. 2 and eq. (2), p. 5).
- A sparse four-array tensor (M, I, J, K) as an intermediate representation of a QCQP for structure detection (p. 4).

**Quotes**:

### S005 · Efficient verification of ReLU-based neural networks via dependency analysis (AAAI 2020, DOI 10.1609/aaai.v34i04.5729)
**Read level**: full (published AAAI-20 version, pp. 1–9 = printed pp. 3291–3299: text, tables, figures, references). The paper contains no proofs or appendix; the lemmas and Theorem 1 are stated without proof.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Elena Botoeva, Panagiotis Kouvaros, Jan Kronqvist, Alessio Lomuscio, all Imperial Computing (p. 1); the author order is alphabetical · **Place in trajectory**: a collaboration with Lomuscio's verification group, funded in part by DARPA Assured Autonomy and her EPSRC fellowship (pp. 8–9). This is her entry into NN verification (Workflow A step 2: a neighbouring community with the question). Kronqvist is the later fellow named in SKILL Heuristic 8.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Safety-critical AI needs certification, and "no method scales to the analysis of the neural networks presently used in industrial strength applications" (p. 1). The gap in prior work: methods cut the number of variables or tighten bounds. This one instead cuts the branch-and-bound search space by exploiting dependencies (p. 1). The problem is stated formally: decide ∀x₀ ∈ X₀ : x_k ∈ X_k, which covers local robustness and reachability (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A dependency relation links unstable ReLU nodes whose stable states imply each other (Def. 1, p. 3). Intra-layer dependencies come from bounds on one node's pre-activation along the hyperplane where the other's is zero (Lemma 1, p. 4). Consecutive-layer dependencies are enough to give the minimal configuration space (Lemmas 2–3, p. 4). Dependency cuts are generated at runtime for the current partial configuration and added through solver callbacks (Def. 2, p. 4; Algorithm 1, p. 5). Around this the tool adds input-domain splitting chosen by stability ratio, a depth-discounted stopping score (Algorithm 3, pp. 5–6), symbolic interval propagation, and the "ideal cuts" of Anderson et al. (p. 5). Venus is written in Python 3.7 with Gurobi 8.1 and parallel splitters and workers (p. 6, Fig. 3).
  - Transferable technique: compute implications between binary states *online*, relative to the solver's current partial assignment. Offline most nodes are unstable, so dependencies are rare (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Feed-forward ReLU networks (p. 2), with X₀ an intersection of polyhedra so that a MILP applies (p. 2). The big-M encoding uses precomputed pre-activation bounds (pp. 2–3). Only complete methods are compared (p. 6). The tunable parameters are the splitting discount m, the stability-ratio cut-off and the callback frequency (pp. 6, 8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: A counting argument: each dependency rules out 2^{n−2} configurations, "a factor of 1/4" of the space (p. 3). Theorem 1 shows a dependency cut is equivalent to the dependency for every extension of h and trivially satisfied otherwise, so it is valid globally (p. 5). The procedure runs in O(k·s²) (p. 5). Lemmas 1–3 and Theorem 1 are stated without proofs in this version (pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Benchmarks (p. 6):
  - ACAS Xu: 45 networks, 10 specifications, 172 queries;
  - MNIST: 2×512 network, ε = 0.05, 100 images;
  - CIFAR-10: 1024-512-512 network, ε = 0.01, 100 images.

  The competitors are Marabou, Neurify and the plain-MILP NSVerify (p. 6). Each query has a 1 h limit and each benchmark a 24 h global limit, on an i7-7700K (p. 7). Table 1 reports queries solved, total time, time and average time over queries the three best tools solved, and the ratio to Venus (p. 7). Further results: a cactus plot (Fig. 4, p. 7), an MNIST ablation over eight component combinations (Table 2, p. 7), a sweep of callback frequency (Fig. 5, p. 8) and a sweep of perturbation radius from 0.01 to 0.2 (Fig. 6, p. 8). Venus's parameters differ by benchmark; the dependency analyser is **off** for ACAS (p. 6). Losses are reported: Neurify is faster on commonly solved ACAS queries and in finding counterexamples (p. 7). Single runs, no variance.
  - Transferable technique: report queries solved, total time, and time over the commonly solved core side by side, with a ratio column. Both "solves more" and "faster where all solve" are then visible, as are losses on the common core (Table 1, p. 7).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The literature is sorted into complete vs. incomplete and MILP / SMT / refinement (pp. 1–2). Incomplete methods are excluded from the comparison because they "may not answer the verification problem due to false negatives" (p. 2). The paper builds on Anderson et al.'s ideal formulations and Wang et al.'s symbolic intervals (pp. 2, 5). NSVerify, from Lomuscio's group, is the plain-MILP baseline that isolates the gain (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution is put strongly in the introduction: "speed-ups of at least one order of magnitude" (p. 1); "Crucially, and differently from the related state-of-the-art" (p. 1). The mechanisms are hedged ("suggest", pp. 7–8). The conclusion is sober about scope: "It is unlikely that this scalability issue will be solved in the immediate future" (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An algorithmic idea, a tool (Venus) and a benchmark comparison. The limits are concrete:
  - dependency analysis has a cost, and its call frequency must be tuned (pp. 5, 8);
  - counterexample generation is slower than Neurify's (p. 7);
  - domain splitting is ineffective in high input dimension (p. 7);
  - performance drops for radii 0.05–0.07 (p. 8).

  Future work: transformational robustness (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): structure is the lever. Dependencies between ReLU states are detected and exploited, and the paper's own conclusion is that MILP solvers should not be used as black boxes (p. 7).
- Method 3 (evidence ✅): the trained network is written as MILP constraints (big-M plus ideal cuts), and its piecewise-linear structure is exploited inside branch-and-bound (pp. 2–5). This is an early instance of the NN-as-constraints line that leads to OMLT.
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): components are judged inside the complete tool. The ablation (Table 2, p. 7) finds splitting not effective in high dimension. The dependency analyser is switched off where it does not pay (ACAS, p. 6), and cut callbacks are rationed (pp. 5, 8).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): strong current competitors, a plain-MILP control, a cactus plot and written losses (pp. 6–7). But the tool is tuned per benchmark (p. 6), runs are single, and returned counterexamples are not independently checked as far as the text says. This matches the lapse already logged for NeurIPS 2021 in SKILL.md.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive pairwise implications between binary variables from interval bounds under a hyperplane restriction, then add them as no-good-style cuts valid for all extensions of the current node (Lemma 1, Def. 2, Thm 1, pp. 4–5).
- Choose the split dimension by maximising the average stability ratio of the two children, and stop splitting with a depth-discounted score (pp. 5–6).
- Sweep the frequency of an expensive callback and report the U-shaped cost curve (Fig. 5, p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "This suggests that MILP solvers are not necessarily best used as black boxes, but application-specific considerations can help to improve their effectiveness." (p. 7)
- "The only aspect we found Venus to be less performing was counterexample generation where Neurify was the fastest tool." (p. 7)
