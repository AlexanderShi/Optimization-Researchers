### S049 · Piecewise parametric structure in the pooling problem: from sparse strongly-polynomial solutions to NP-hardness (Journal of Global Optimization 2018, doi:10.1007/s10898-017-0577-y)
Read level: full (pp. 1–36: body pp. 1–32, Appendix A omitted proofs pp. 32–34, references pp. 34–36 skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Radu Baltean-Lugojan (PhD student; first author) · **Place in trajectory**: the 2018 "closing" paper of the pooling line that began with APOGEE; dedicated to Floudas (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a gap between practice and theory. Deterministic global algorithms have "significant optimality gaps and impractical or unknown convergence times" on large pooling instances, yet Beale et al.'s simple piecewise-linear program works as a heuristic, and Meyer–Floudas, Karuppiah–Grossmann and others approximate pooling well by MILP (p. 2). The paper says it "validates and substantiates Professor Floudas’ intuition" that pooling is rooted in piecewise-defined functions (abstract p. 1; p. 2). It is also framed as filling a gap on the P/NP boundary left between strongly NP-hard single-quality I+H−L−J and polynomial I−1−J (Remark 4.6, p. 27). The problem is the single-quality standard pooling p-formulation, now with direct (bypass) feeds, "a somewhat more complex pooling problem than previous work analyzing the P/NP boundary" (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when a heuristic works suspiciously well on an NP-hard class, make "why" the research question and answer it by finding the subclass where it is provably exact.
- **D2 Core idea**: Treat the pool concentration p as a parameter. For fixed p, eliminating p turns the one-pool, one-output problem into an LP with at most three tight constraints, so the optimal basis is tiny. The parametric form then shows which feeds are active on each p-interval (p. 7). Active sets, dominance relations and breakpoints (Defs 3.1–3.4, pp. 7–8) give a piecewise-linear f*(p) for inputs only (Thm 3.1.6, p. 11), constant pieces for directs only (Thm 3.2.7, p. 15), and monotone convex/concave pieces for mixed triples (Prop 3.3.7, Thm 3.3.8, pp. 20–21). That yields an O(I³+H³) algorithm (Thm 3.3.9, p. 21). Multiple outputs decompose additively over outputs sharing p (Thm 4.1, p. 23). Any non-monotone pieces are handled by solving a univariate rational polynomial (Thm 4.4, p. 25). Multiple pools reduce to at most two active pools (Thm 5.2, p. 29). What stays the same: the classical p-formulation (p. 3). What changes: the solve is by analysis, not by spatial B&B or a black-box LP (p. 32).
  - Transferable: in a bilinear model, pick the variable that makes the rest linear, keep it as a parameter instead of eliminating it, and characterise the optimal basis as a function of that parameter.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.2 restricts to one quality, assumes feasibility, drops feed-availability and pool-capacity constraints and fixes product demands D_j > 0 (p. 5). Remark 2.1 drops redundant hard bounds (p. 5). The authors say the assumption is "tightly conditioned": Remarks 3.3.10, 4.6 and 5.6 show that relaxing it erodes sparsity or gives NP-hardness (pp. 5–6, 22, 27–28, 31). Feasibility is assumed only to skip a trivial check (p. 6). Equal-concentration ties are broken by a pre-filtering rule to avoid zero denominators (Remark 3.5, p. 9). Thm 4.4 and Cor 4.5 need rational parameters (pp. 25–26).
- **D4 Proofs and analysis**: (i) A contradiction by geometric construction: a quadrilateral i–l–j–k whose diagonals meet at the supposed breakpoint b, so the side i–l beats it. Hence breakpoints occur only at input concentrations (Thm 3.1.6 proof, Fig. 3, pp. 11–12). (ii) "Joint node" aggregation: an active pair is treated as one node with averaged cost γ_ij(p), linear in p (Def 3.1.3, p. 10). (iii) Reduction by analogy: the directs-only problem mirrors the inputs-only one, with p replaced by the active quality bound P(i,j) (Prop 3.2.4, Cors 3.2.5–3.2.6, pp. 14–15). (iv) Pre-solve then augment: the inputs-only optimum becomes an extra "direct" node in a directs-only problem (Thm 3.3.1, pp. 16–17). (v) Closed-form n-th derivatives give monotone convexity/concavity (Prop 3.3.7, pp. 20, 34). (vi) Additive decomposition of the master problem over outputs linked by p (Thm 4.1, pp. 23–25). (vii) A two-output counterexample shows non-monotonicity, and a four-output one shows multimodality (Prop 4.3, p. 25). (viii) Complexity comes from LLL factorisation of rational univariate polynomials (Thm 4.4, Eq. 37, p. 26). (ix) Hardness boundary: every relaxation of Assumption 2.2 maps to a bivariate or multivariate polynomial system that is NP-hard (Remark 4.6, pp. 27–28).
  - Transferable: prove a complexity frontier by listing each assumption and showing what NP-hard object its removal produces (a bivariate polynomial, a coupled multivariate system). That justifies the assumptions and maps the boundary at once.
- **D5 Experiments**: No computational benchmark. There are four constructed numerical illustrations: five inputs (Fig. 4, p. 12), five inputs plus three directs (Fig. 6, p. 21), two outputs with a non-convex interval (Fig. 8, p. 27) and two pools (Fig. 10, p. 31). The code is released on GitHub (ref. [8], p. 34; mentioned p. 3). No timing study and no comparison with B&B solvers.
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends and unifies the |K|=1 complexity results of Boland et al. "in the semantics of" their Fig. 1 (p. 2). Fills the gap in Alfaki–Haugland's Corollary 2 (p. 27). Credits Beale et al., Meyer–Floudas, Karuppiah–Grossmann and the Dey–Gupte MILP discretisation heuristic (p. 2), and closes by pointing to Dey–Gupte's heuristic as the direction for disjunctive cuts (p. 32). Earlier work is restated, never criticised. Links pooling to queueing via Woodside–Tripathi (Remark 4.8, p. 28). Cites her own group's pooling recognition paper to argue that pooling recurs inside general MINLP (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: Uses rare exclamation marks for the surprising result (Haverly, p. 2; Remark 4.7, p. 28). Each section opens with a roadmap paragraph listing the definitions, lemmas and theorems to come (pp. 9, 13, 15–16, 22–23, 28). Proofs come with worked numerical figures. Hedging is sparse: claims are stated as theorems, and the limits sit in the "tightly conditioned" remarks. Intuition is credited to named people (Floudas, Beale et al.) (pp. 1–2, 32).
  - Transferable: after each structural theorem, add a small worked example figure with parameters printed on the network diagram, so the reader sees the breakpoints and active sets.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory and algorithms: strongly-polynomial algorithms for I+H−1−1, I+H−1−J and I+H−L−1 under Assumption 2.2, and a new point on the P/NP boundary (pp. 31–32). The limits are specific: sparsity vanishes and analytic solution fails once flow bounds return (Remark 3.3.10, p. 22), and multiple qualities or variable demands give NP-hardness (pp. 27–28). Future work: approximation algorithms that start from the boundary subclass, and disjunctive cuts built on the identified structures for the NP-hard subclasses (p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): structure as "insider information". The paper finds sparsity, meaning dominant active topologies with at most 2–4 active feeds, and the piecewise objective structure, then exploits them for exact solution (pp. 2–3, 7, 12, 29, 31–32). The detection here is analytical, not a software detector.
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): auditing test instances by theory, not computation. It shows the Haverly instances, "used as test cases for exponential algorithms for more than 35 years", lie in a strongly-polynomial subclass (pp. 2, 28).
- Also bears on Heuristic 6 (keep the "why does it work" question and answer it structurally). The paper states that goal (abstract p. 1; pp. 2, 32). It also fits the SKILL's inferred reading of the "proofs boring" tension: here the theory is structural, not a routine convergence proof.
- New-pattern candidate: parametrise-and-track-breakpoints (pp. 7–12).
- New-pattern candidate: assumption-tightness as hardness frontier (pp. 22, 27–28, 31).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Keep the complicating variable of a bilinear model as a parameter, bound the LP basis cardinality for fixed parameter, and enumerate dominance breakpoints. This gives a full parametric description of the optimum (pp. 7–12).
- Climb a ladder of subclasses (one pool/one output → many outputs → many pools) and reuse each result as a building block for the next (pp. 3, 6, 22–23, 28–29).
- Justify a restrictive assumption by showing that each way of relaxing it is NP-hard, which turns "a convenient assumption" into "a boundary" (Remarks 3.3.10, 4.6, 5.6, pp. 22, 27–28, 31).
- Check whether a classic benchmark family actually lies in an easy subclass before using it to evaluate an exponential algorithm (Remark 4.7, p. 28).

**Quotes**:
- "The proofs lead to the unexpected outcome that the famous Haverly [33] pooling instances, i.e. the first-recorded pooling instances, belong to a strongly-polynomial subclass!" (p. 2)
- "The position on the P/NP boundary of the multiple outputs and one pool subclass is thus an ideal starting point for approximating algorithms that cross into NP-hardness." (p. 32)

### S119 · Designing and implementing algorithms for mixed-integer nonlinear optimization (Report from Dagstuhl Seminar 18081, Dagstuhl Reports 8(2) 2018, doi:10.4230/DagRep.8.2.64)
Read level: full (all 24 file pages, printed pp. 64–87). Voice: the executive summary (pp. 1–2), working groups (pp. 21–23) and open problems (p. 23) are the joint voice of the four editors. §3.3 (pp. 6–7) and §3.26 (p. 17) are abstracts from her group. All other §3 abstracts are other speakers' words and are not used as evidence about Misener.
**Role**: core (edited report) · **Coauthors**: co-editors Pierre Bonami, Ambros M. Gleixner, Jeff Linderoth; "Edited in cooperation with Radu Baltean-Lugojan" (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: 2018, fellowship era; she co-organises the field's agenda alongside MIP and NLP solver developers.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Framed as a mismatch: "natural optimization models for important scientific problems and practical optimization solvers" (p. 1). Algorithms and software "are often unable to solve practically-sized instances". The named obstacles are the computability boundary, exploiting known technologies for specialised MINLP classes, and using logical formulas holistically (abstract p. 1). Historical framing: the first MINLP algorithms and software "were designed by application engineers", and a "transformational shift" is now needed (abstract p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: The seminar mixes theory and implementation experts, MIP and NLP communities, application experts and ML. It has four highlighted themes: solver software as a needs analysis, the MIP/NLP intersection, driving applications, and MINLP–ML connections (p. 2). Her group's two contributions: (a) §3.3: an offline-trained neural-network estimator selects a few strong low-dimensional linear cuts that keep most of the tightness of an SDP relaxation inside branch-and-cut, which avoids the lack of warm-starting and the poor interplay with RLT cuts (pp. 6–7). (b) §3.26: a MINLP over gradient-boosted trees modelling catalyst behaviour, with risk-mitigating and composition penalties. It uses heuristics for feasible solutions plus an exact B&B that "leverages structural properties" of trees and penalties, tested on a BASF industrial instance (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: n/a (seminar report). §3.3 assumes nonconvex continuous QP with SDP relaxations available (p. 6). §3.26 assumes trained gradient-boosted trees as the objective model (p. 17).
- **D4 Proofs and analysis**: n/a (no proofs by the editors or by her group in this report).
- **D5 Experiments**: No experiments reported. However, the "Sound experimentation with MINLP software" working group sets an evaluation protocol with developers of ANTIGONE, BARON, Couenne, Gurobi, Minotaur, SCIP and FICO Xpress present (pp. 21–22). Its conclusions: permuted-model experiments "as has become standard in the MIP community", more informative solver output including sensitivity, a MINLP analogue of "MIP kappa" for a-posteriori numerical safety, and no reliance on "simplistic comparisons" (p. 22). The executive summary reports that "a new set of metrics for comparing MINLP solvers were developed at this meeting" (p. 2). The metrics themselves are not given in the report.
- **D6 Positioning**: MINLP is placed as a superset of MIP and NLP that should become "as ubiquitous a paradigm" as they are (pp. 1–2). The energy working group, led by Martin and Liers, records that the mathematical community "potentially overemphasizes research on proving optimality" while engineering needs near-optimal answers quickly and robustly, and recommends adapting benchmarks to those needs (p. 21). The ML working group (Lodi) records that MINLP is unlikely to suit neural-network training but can encode ReLU networks (pp. 22–23).
- **D7 Writing**: Agenda prose with bullet lists of conclusions and open questions (pp. 2, 22–23). It promises follow-ups: an *Optimization & Engineering* special issue and a larger open-problem document "that will start a conversation with the entire mathematical optimisation community" (p. 2; p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is community-level: an agenda, protocol recommendations, open problems (column generation with convex objective, SOC-representability of convex hulls of semi-algebraic sets; reliability of SDP/NLP sub-solvers; borrowing HPC, multigrid and logic tools; why extended formulations are hard to solve) and growth applications (ML, optimal power flow, pooling) (p. 23). Limits: the report gives no data, and the promised metrics and open-problem paper are only announced (p. 2, p. 23); whether they appeared is not in this text.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (evidence ✅, joint voice): the working-group conclusions on permuted models, informative output, numerical-safety measures and distrust of simplistic comparisons (p. 22). The SKILL already quotes the last item. The SKILL's U7 notes that permuted-model runs were not followed in her NeurIPS 2021 paper.
- Method 3 (evidence ✅): §3.26 is an early (2018) form of the trees-as-constraints line. It starts from a partner's trained model for "picking the best catalyst", uses heuristics plus a structure-exploiting exact B&B, and tests on a BASF industrial instance. The "risk control" wording is still in the title (p. 17).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): the executive summary celebrates "a new set of metrics" developed at the seminar (p. 2). That contrasts with Warning sign 2 (winning on a metric you invented); the difference here is that the metrics are community-made and not paired with a winner.
- New-pattern candidate: offline-learned selection of expensive cuts (pp. 6–7). This is the NN-scored SDP-cut line the SKILL lists as U5 (never published as a paper). This report confirms it was presented in 2018 but adds no results.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When benchmarking MINLP solvers, run permuted copies of each model (variable and constraint order), check tolerance sensitivity, and report variability, not single runs (p. 22; Vigerske–Gleixner abstract p. 20).
- Move an expensive per-node selection step (here, choosing which SDP-derived linear cuts to add) offline into an estimator trained before deployment, so the solver pays only an inference cost at run time (pp. 6–7).

**Quotes**:
- "the need to incorporate experimentation on permuted models as has become standard in the MIP community;" (p. 22)
- "Finally, a new set of metrics for comparing MINLP solvers were developed at this meeting and will greatly aid future solver testing." (p. 2)

### S010 · QPLIB: a library of quadratic programming instances (Mathematical Programming Computation 2019, doi:10.1007/s12532-018-0147-4)
Read level: full (preprint file pp. 1–42: body pp. 1–21 read closely; references pp. 22–33 skimmed; Appendix A instance table pp. 33–38 skimmed; Appendix B file format pp. 38–42 read). The text is a preprint layout, not the typeset journal version.
**Role**: core (13-author community paper) · **Coauthors**: F. Furini, E. Traversi, P. Belotti, A. Frangioni, A. Gleixner, N. Gould, L. Liberti, A. Lodi, H. Mittelmann, N. Sahinidis, S. Vigerske, A. Wiegele (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Imperial era; ANTIGONE is one of the complete solvers used to filter instances (Table 2, p. 11; refs [104, 105]). Her own contribution is not stated in the text, so nothing below is attributed to her personally.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Unlike MILP, QP (in general MIQCQP) has had "no single library devoted to all different kinds of instances" (p. 2). Existing libraries are application-specific or structure-specific (p. 2). The central difficulty is choosing instances that are "not overwhelmingly onerous but sufficiently challenging" for very different communities (abstract p. 1; p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Define "interesting" operationally as "challenging for a significant set of solution methods". An instance is dropped if a significant fraction of solvers solve it quickly, and kept if only one or a few solve it efficiently by exploiting structure (p. 2). A three-field OVC taxonomy (objective, variables, constraints), with fields ordered by containment, drives the selection (pp. 6–7). Instances are kept unreformulated, in the contributor's "natural" form (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Convexity is classified only by the sufficient condition (definiteness, eigenvalue thresholds ±10⁻¹²). Two-sided quadratic constraints count as nonconvex, and SOC constraints written as quadratics count as "potentially nonconvex". This is justified because "not all QP solvers are capable of detecting and exploiting these structures" (pp. 6, 8, 15). The solver taxonomy follows Neumaier: incomplete, asymptotically complete, complete, rigorous (pp. 10–11). There is a Turing-machine vs Real-RAM caveat on calling floating-point solvers "exact" (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Proofs and analysis**: n/a (no theorems). The analytic content is reformulation reasoning that shows class boundaries blur: binaries ⇔ nonconvex quadratic equalities; a quadratic objective → linear objective plus quadratic constraint; PSD Hessian → diagonal via Cholesky with extra linear constraints, which explains why D*B differs from C*B (pp. 6–8). Complexity landmarks: LIQ undecidable (Jeroslow), and QCB NP-hard even with one negative eigenvalue (pp. 7, 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: The library-construction protocol is the experiment. An online call (Jan 2014, open ten months) plus seven named existing libraries gave >8000 instances, all translated to GAMS (pp. 13–14). The first filter drops instances solved by ≥30% of the applicable complete GAMS solvers within 30 s (p. 15). The second filter removes near-duplicates by donor and size, keeping "the larger and computationally harder ones", then drops anything a complete QGQ solver solves in 120 s (p. 16). Result: 319 discrete + 134 continuous instances (Tables 3–6, pp. 16–18), with distribution plots of size, discreteness, quadratic and nonconvex constraints, problematic eigenvalues and density (Figs 1–4, pp. 19–20). No solver performance data, by design (pp. 2, 4, 21). Performance is delegated to Mittelmann's benchmark pages (p. 4).
- **D6 Positioning**: Modelled on MIPLIB and the MINLPLib 2 tooling (pp. 2, 20). Lists source libraries: BARON, CUTEst, GAMS Performance, MacMINLP, Maros–Mészáros, MINLPLib, POLIP (p. 14). Places QP as the "sweet spot" between MILP and MINLP in expressiveness versus cost (p. 3), and as a meeting ground for the combinatorial and nonlinear communities (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing**: States explicitly what the paper does not do. The no-solver-comparison disclaimer is given three times (pp. 2, 4, 21). Design choices are justified as bias avoidance (p. 8). The closing calls the library "only a snapshot", to be extended dynamically (p. 21).
  - Transferable: when a design choice could favour one solver family (reformulating instances, classifying convexity), say which bias you refuse to add and keep the neutral version.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are infrastructure: the library, the taxonomy, a website at qplib.zib.de with metadata, sparsity plots, feasible points and CSV export, and a new .qplib file format (pp. 20–21, 38–42). Stated limits: a snapshot of past problem types; the coarse taxonomy does not record finer structures such as network flow, knapsack, semi-continuous or SOC-representable (p. 10); convexity is under-detected (p. 8). Future work: literature references on the website and dynamic extension (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (evidence ✅, joint voice): large documented collection with provenance (call dates, source libraries, counts per class, pp. 13–14); difficulty calibrated by a solver portfolio with explicit thresholds (pp. 15–16); public metadata and formats (pp. 20–21). Variant: the calibration aims at hard instances (drop anything ≥30% of complete solvers solve in 30 s), not Heuristic 3's "baseline wins some" (pp. 15–16).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the library refuses to detect hidden convex structure (SOC as quadratic) and leaves detection to solvers, explicitly because only some solvers detect it (pp. 8, 15). This is the opposite design choice to a structure-detection layer. For a neutral library it is a deliberate non-detection, which matches Method 1's concern that what a solver detects is itself a variable.
- Method 4 (no link): the paper declines any solver judgement (p. 21).
- New-pattern candidate: define "interesting" by solver-portfolio performance (pp. 2, 15–16).
- New-pattern candidate: keep instances in the contributor's natural form to avoid solver bias (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a benchmark set with a two-stage cascade: (1) drop instances that ≥X% of applicable complete solvers solve in T seconds; (2) remove near-duplicates by donor and size, keep the larger and harder representatives, and drop anything a strong complete solver solves within a longer limit. Report the counts at each stage (pp. 15–16, Table 3).
- Classify each instance with a short containment-ordered code (here OVC) computed from data (eigenvalue signs with a stated threshold, density, variable and constraint types), and publish the code and raw features as downloadable metadata (pp. 6, 14–15, 20–21).
- State the computational-model caveat when calling floating-point global solvers "exact" (complete vs rigorous, Turing machine vs Real RAM) (pp. 10, 12–13).

**Quotes**:
- "Thanks to these extensive preliminary tests, we discarded all instances that are solved by at least 30% of the complete solvers within a time limit of 30 seconds." (p. 15)
- "This being a bias that we do not want to add we have chosen to keep the instances in their “natural” form, this being the one in which the original contributor initially wrote them." (p. 8)
