# Batch c08 · Ruth Misener · core

Batch intent: see how Misener's group, in 2019-2021, turns a structure that is already in the model (convexity hidden in Pyomo expression trees, the two-branch disjunction inside each ReLU node, the piecewise-constant leaves of a gradient-boosted tree ensemble) into an exploitable object: a validated detector (SUSPECT), a hierarchy of formulations between big-M and the convex hull (partition-based ReLU), a decomposition bound and branching scheme (GBT branch-and-bound); and how each is evaluated against an oracle, weak controls, or black-box solvers.

### S068 · SUSPECT: MINLP special structure detector for Pyomo (Optimization Letters 2020, doi:10.1007/s11590-019-01396-y)

**Read level**: full (main text pp. 1-11 read in full, references pp. 12-15 scanned; supplementary pp. 16-31 read: interval rules and rule tables App. A-C pp. 16-23 skimmed as tables, Bound interface App. D pp. 18, 24, RecLMTD detector code App. E pp. 25-28 and result tables App. F pp. 28-31 read). The extracted text is the author manuscript (SAND2019-1376J, p. 1); formulas are partly garbled by the extraction (e.g. the MINLP statement p. 1, rule tables pp. 17-18), the prose is clean.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Francesco Ceccon (Imperial, PhD student), John D. Siirola (Sandia National Laboratories) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the stand-alone, open-source form of the structure-detection line that runs from GloMIQO/ANTIGONE through the pooling pattern-matcher (cited as [11], p. 12) to GALINI; funded by her EPSRC Research Fellowship (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Global MINLP solvers exploit special structure (convexity to generate polyhedral cuts or to relax only the nonconvexities, p. 1), but each solver keeps its detection inside; the paper offers a separate, extensible detector for models written in Pyomo (abstract, p. 1; contributions p. 2). The claim of novelty: "SUSPECT is the first open source code to explicitly detect convexity as a stand-alone utility" (p. 2). The intended uses are solver-side: identify which variables appear in nonconvex expressions to shrink the spatial-branching space, and solver selection (pp. 2, 10).
  - Transferable: when every solver hides a capability internally, extract it into a stand-alone library with a stated consumer (here: B&B variable selection, solver choice) and claim novelty on the separation itself (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Convert Pyomo's per-constraint expression trees into one DAG that merges common subexpressions (p. 3, §5.2 pp. 6-8), then run forward/backward tree-walking passes for feasibility-based bound tightening (§3, pp. 4-5), monotonicity and convexity propagation by composition rules (§4, pp. 5-6). Tree walking is chosen deliberately over expensive alternatives (interval Hessians, quantifier elimination, sampling, p. 3) because it is cheap enough for preprocessing (p. 3); the abstract states the balance between "lightweight computation" and "effective special structure detection" (p. 1). Extensibility is the declared primary contribution: third-party convexity detectors register through setuptools entry points without changing SUSPECT (§6, pp. 8-10); four built-in extensions (PSD quadratics, second-order cone, perspective functions, linear-fractional expressions, p. 9) and a worked plug-in for the reciprocal LMTD from her heat-exchanger work (Example 3, p. 9; code pp. 25-28).
  - Transferable: ship the detector with a plug-in mechanism and demonstrate it with one detector taken from your own application paper (RecLMTD, pp. 9-10, 25-28); this turns domain knowledge into reusable structure detection.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Pyomo "Pyomo4" expression trees (p. 3 fn. 1); supported operators are those of Fig. 4 (p. 7), others (e.g. the error function) make an instance unreadable (p. 10). Rules assume unary/binary vertices; n-ary vertices are folded left (p. 5). Non-differentiable compositions are "unknown" except |x| on sign-definite domains (pp. 5-6). No disjoint intervals in bound tightening "because their support is uncommon in most solvers" (p. 5). Only linear expressions get child-bound tightening, not products or divisions (p. 5). FBBT capped at 10 iterations because convergence time can be infinite (p. 8). Hashing is heuristic and the DAG "depends on the model building style since no simplifications are performed" (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis and devices**: No theorems. Devices: (i) monotonicity and convexity rules derived from the chain rule for gradient and Hessian of k = g∘h (p. 5; full rule tables Tables 6-11, pp. 19-23); (ii) recursion without recursion: vertices stored sorted by depth so a forward (backward) list traversal visits each vertex after its children (parents) and at most once, avoiding stack overflow (§5.1 p. 6, §5.3 p. 8); (iii) change-driven revisiting: a visitor returns a Boolean so only parents/children of changed vertices are revisited (p. 8); (iv) a recursive hash (Eq. (3)) plus bucket comparison that ignores coefficients when bucketing (pp. 6-7, Fig. 5); (v) a Bound interface that isolates all floating-point-sensitive operations so users can supply arbitrary-precision or safe interval arithmetic (p. 8; App. D pp. 18, 24).
  - Transferable: when a rule system is naturally recursive but must run on large models, store the DAG topologically sorted and drive rules by forward/backward list scans with change flags (pp. 6, 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: MINLPLib 2 as accessed on 13 Dec 2017 (1527 problems), one AWS C5 instance per problem (2 cores, 4 GB), SUSPECT packaged as a Docker image, results from Git tag v7 dated 18 May 2018, Pyomo 5.2, OSiL input (p. 10). MINLPLib's own structure labels are the oracle (p. 10). Results: 135 instances (9%) unreadable (unsupported functions or >5 min conversion); 1003 (72%) of the remaining 1392 processed in under 2 s; 1275 of 1392 (91.6%) matched the oracle; 35 (2%) time out or raise an exception; no false positives (p. 10). The 82 remaining misses are explained by cause: 53 from eigenvalue numerical errors on quadratics, trim-loss problems (tls*) from the square root of a product of nonnegative variables, st_e17 from a reciprocal constraint (p. 10); each miss is listed (Table 15, pp. 29-31), as are the >10 s instances (Table 13, p. 29) and the 13 time-outs (Table 14, p. 29), 12 of which are pooling_spp* instances. No comparison with other detectors (e.g., Dr.AMPL or solver-internal detection, cited p. 3) is reported. Small inconsistency: the text says 72% in under 2 s (p. 10), the Fig. 6 caption says 70% (p. 11).
  - Transferable: validate a classifier-like tool against a labelled community library used as an oracle, and report three numbers separately: unreadable inputs, time-outs/exceptions, and wrong classifications, each with the instance list and a cause (pp. 10, 29-31).
  - Transferable: freeze reproducibility metadata in the text: library access date, tool Git tag and date, container image, interpreter and modelling-library versions (p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on tree-walking convexity detection by Fourer et al. and Dr.AMPL [17-19] and on DAG interval analysis (Schichl-Neumaier, Vu et al.) (pp. 3, 8); positions SUSPECT next to disciplined convex programming tools (CVX, CVXPY, Convex.jl), which impose conventions rather than detect (p. 3). No direct criticism of predecessors; the differentiator is "stand-alone", "open source" and "easy extensibility" (p. 2). Self-citation to her solvers and her LMTD and signomial papers [38-42] (p. 14). Note: reference [48] and [49] are the same Schichl-Neumaier paper (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Short software-paper format: a one-paragraph contributions statement (p. 2), a roadmap paragraph (p. 2), an illustrative toy problem (1) carried through Figs. 2-3 (pp. 4-7), worked examples for bound rules (Examples 1-2, pp. 4-5). Limitations are written into the method sections, not saved for the end: hashing may not give the minimal DAG (p. 6), double-precision eigenvalues "may obtain incorrect results because of numerical issues" with a named alternative (pivoted Cholesky) not implemented because numpy lacks it (p. 9), polynomial matching missing (p. 7).
  - Transferable: state each implementation shortcut where it is made, with the better alternative and why it is not implemented yet (pp. 6-7, 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: software and engineering (data structures, algorithms, plug-in architecture), published with the source code under Apache 2.0 (p. 2). Future work: convexity disproving when detection is inconclusive, detecting difference-of-convex structure, recognising pooling structure, OBBT for bounds, other modelling languages, integration with solvers for automatic algorithm choice (p. 11). Limits: 9% unreadable, numerical eigenvalue misses, and the 5-minute conversion ceiling on large pooling instances (pp. 10, 29). The exploitation step (using the detected structure inside a solver) is not evaluated in this paper (p. 11 lists it as future work).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (structure detection as "insider information"): evidence ✅. A detector built on the term structure of factorable models, validated like an instrument (SKILL.md wording) against MINLPLib labels with every miss listed and no false positives (pp. 10, 29-31); domain experts add detectors (pp. 2, 9). This is the primary text behind Method 1 step 5, and the numbers in SKILL.md (1527; 1275 of 1392) are confirmed (p. 10).
- Method 1 (step 6, exploit and measure inside the full B&B): variant ⚠. SUSPECT stops at detection; the gain inside a solver is not measured here and is deferred to future integration (p. 11).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): partial evidence ✅ for steps 1 and 7: dated test-set access, frozen tool version, container, exact library versions, full failure lists (pp. 10, 29-31). No competitor comparison, so steps 2-4 do not apply.
- Heuristic 9 (release per paper): evidence ✅. "This paper is published alongside the source code [10]" with a Zenodo DOI and GitHub link (pp. 2, 6, 12).
- New-pattern candidate: "isolate numerical risk behind a swappable interface". All floating-point-sensitive bound operations go through a Bound interface with an arbitrary-precision default and room for safe interval arithmetic (p. 8, App. D pp. 18, 24), and the remaining numerical failure (double-precision eigenvalues) is named as the largest miss cause (pp. 9-10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Validate a structure detector against a labelled public library as oracle; report unreadable, timed-out and misclassified instances separately with full instance lists and per-cause explanations (pp. 10, 29-31).
- Make extensibility a measured feature: provide a plug-in API and include one non-trivial detector from your own application work as the worked example (pp. 9-10, 25-28).
- Recursion-free rule propagation on a depth-sorted DAG with change flags (pp. 6, 8).

**Quotes**:
- "SUSPECT does not have false positives on the test set, this gives confidence in the implementation and makes it possible to use it for solver selection." (p. 10)
- "The heuristic approach of hashing Pyomo expressions may not converge to the DAG with the fewest vertices, but it should obtain an acceptable DAG." (p. 6)

### S013 · Partition-based formulations for mixed-integer optimization of trained ReLU neural networks (NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2102.04373)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v2 pp. 1-19 read: main text pp. 1-10, references pp. 11-13 scanned, appendix A.1-A.5 pp. 14-19 read including the Fourier-Motzkin derivation and Tables 4-8).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Calvin Tsay (Imperial, research fellow), Jan Kronqvist (KTH), Alexander Thebelt (Imperial, BASF-funded PhD student) (p. 1; funding p. 10) · **Position in trajectory**: the ReLU-specific application of the "between big-M and convex hull" hierarchy of Kronqvist, Misener and Tsay (CPAIOR 2021, cited as [21], p. 12); the formulation later became a switchable option in OMLT (per SKILL.md, not in this text).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: MILP encodings of trained ReLU networks are used in verification, adversarial examples, compression and acquisition-function optimisation, and nearly all use big-M (p. 1). Entry point is a known tension: big-M is not tight, while Anderson et al.'s convex-hull formulations need exponentially many constraints or many auxiliary variables, so "despite its weaker continuous relaxation, the big-M formulation can be computationally advantageous owing to its smaller size" (p. 1). The paper asks for a family that sits in between (p. 1).
  - Transferable: open with the two textbook extremes and the folklore that the tighter one often loses in practice; the contribution is then the interpolation (pp. 1, 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: View each ReLU node as a two-term disjunction (5) and, instead of disaggregating every input, partition the node's inputs into N groups, introduce one auxiliary sum z_n per group, and form the extended (hull) formulation over the groups (12)-(17) (p. 3). Compared with Kronqvist et al.'s general disjunctive hierarchy, both sides of the ReLU disjunction can share partitions, which saves auxiliary variables (p. 3). Partition choice is then a design question: theory (pp. 6-7) motivates partitions with weights of similar magnitude; two proposed strategies (equal size via sorted weights, equal range with 5%/95% quantile thresholds) and two deliberately weak controls (random, "uneven magnitudes" snake-draft) (p. 7). The formulation also makes OBBT per partition meaningful: partitioned OBBT LPs capture dependencies within each partition (p. 4, p. 9).
  - Transferable: when introducing a knob, also introduce the strategies that set it and at least one control you expect to be worse (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Trained feed-forward ReLU networks with fixed weights (pp. 2, 16); bounded inputs and bounded partition sums z_n (pp. 3, 5); bounds from interval arithmetic (18)-(19) or OBBT on the LP relaxation (p. 4). Proposition 3 (convex hull at N = η) assumes bounded inputs and gives the hull of a single node over a box; the paper notes "the overall model tightness still hinges on the bounds used" (p. 16). Proposition 4's strictness relies on the cited fact that each constraint of (20) is facet-defining [3] (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Proposition 1: project out the N auxiliary variables by Fourier-Motzkin elimination to get a non-lifted form with 2^N constraints (p. 5; derivation A.2 pp. 14-16, with the lower inequalities shown redundant by checking σ = 0, 1, p. 15). Proposition 2: N = 1 equals big-M, read as the convex hull over a single aggregated variable z = w^T x (p. 5). Proposition 3: N = η recovers the convex hull via a change of variables z_n = w_n x_n (p. 5; A.3 p. 16). Proposition 4: merging two partitions strictly loosens the formulation, shown by comparing which subsets I_j are representable (p. 5). Remark: the non-lifted form of a partition formulation equals pre-selecting a subset of Anderson et al.'s hull inequalities (p. 5). Section 3.3 uses a 4-input toy (w = [1, 1, 100, 100]) to compare partitions through both bound strength and the non-lifted constraints (pp. 6-7).
  - Transferable: prove a formulation hierarchy with three statements: the endpoints (equals big-M; equals hull) and a monotonicity-under-refinement result; then characterise intermediate members by projecting to the original space and identifying which hull inequalities they keep (p. 5, A.2).
  - Transferable: test a design rule on a tiny hand-computable instance before any experiment, and say when the toy is misleading: the 4-input case seems to favour unbalanced partitions, but the experiments confirm that balanced partitions perform better (Remark, p. 7).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Gurobi 9.1 on one i7-8700 (12 threads, 16 GB), dual simplex, cuts = 1, MIPFocus = 3 "to ensure consistent solution approaches across formulations", 3600 s limit (p. 16). Three tasks: optimal adversary (ℓ1), verification (ℓ∞, early termination), ℓ1-minimally distorted adversary (pp. 7-10). Instances: first 100 test images, random adversarial labels, same 100 per dataset (p. 7); perturbation sizes chosen "such that some big-M problems were solvable within 3600s" (p. 7). Networks: MNIST and CIFAR-10 dense and CNN models, standard training (p. 16). Metrics: number solved and average time over instances solved by all formulations (p. 8, p. 16). Ablations: N = 1..10 (Fig. 2 right, Fig. 3), four partitioning strategies with and without OBBT (Fig. 3, p. 9), convex-hull cuts of Anderson et al. at a range of cut frequencies (Fig. 2 left, p. 8) and as root cuts (Tables 5-8, pp. 18-19), lifted vs non-lifted forms (Table 4, p. 17). Headline: 25% more problems solved, average 2.2X speed-up on solved problems (p. 2). Honest negatives: best N is problem-dependent, N ≥ 7 degrades (p. 8); 4-partition loses to big-M on some verification instances (Table 2, p. 9); with OBBT and poor partitions big-M wins (p. 8). Not reported: repeated runs or seeds (one configuration per formulation, p. 16); whether reported times include OBBT time is not stated (pp. 9, 16; OBBT LPs capped at 5 s each, p. 9). Settings differ between arms in verification: cuts turned off for partition formulations but kept for big-M, with the stated reason that removing cuts helped some big-M problems and hurt others (p. 9).
  - Transferable: calibrate instance difficulty so the baseline solves some but not all instances, and compute average times only over instances solved by every method (pp. 7, 16).
  - Transferable: give the strongest rival technique its best setting by sweeping its parameter (cut frequency over a log-scale range) before comparing (Fig. 2 left, p. 8).
- **D6 Positioning**: Distinguishes exact MILP encodings from relaxation-based verifiers (LP, SDP, Lagrangian, multi-neuron relaxations) that are faster but may fail to verify (p. 2). Directly builds on Anderson et al. (convex hull of a single node) and Tjandraatmadja et al., and differentiates: "Our approach is fundamentally different, as it constructs computationally cheaper formulations that approximate the convex hull, and we start by deriving a stronger relaxation instead of strengthening the relaxation via cutting planes" (p. 2). Integrates competitors' devices rather than opposing them: Anderson et al.'s linear-time separation is "naturally compatible" with the partition formulation (p. 7). Cites her own verification collaboration with Lomuscio's group ([5], p. 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Numbered contributions paragraph that maps to sections (p. 2); the headline number appears in the introduction (p. 2) and again in the conclusion (p. 10). Propositions are short, each followed by a one-paragraph proof and a Remark that gives the practical reading (p. 5). Hedging is explicit where evidence is experimental: "We hypothesize these bounds are most effective when ..." (p. 6); the best choice of N is called problem-dependent (pp. 7-8). Figure 1 shows the hierarchy visually on a 2-input network for N = 1, 2, 5, 20, with interval vs OBBT bounds (p. 4). Small inconsistency: the appendix disjunction (28) writes "y ≤ 0" where (12) has "y ≥ 0" (pp. 3, 14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution levels: formulation theory (hierarchy), algorithmic guidance (partitioning strategies, partitioned OBBT), computation, code release on GitHub (p. 10). Limits stated: no rule for N ("problem-dependent", p. 8); hull can be worse than intermediate N (p. 8); minimally distorted problems only manageable for small MNIST nets (p. 10); partitioned OBBT is cheap only if parallelised (p. 9). Future direction named: parallel OBBT "represents an avenue to significantly improve MILP optimization of NNs via parallelization" (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (between formulations): evidence ✅, the primary text for this method's ReLU case. Endpoint and order theorems (Props. 2-4, p. 5), the parameter admitted and swept (N = 1..10, Figs. 2-3, pp. 8-9), random and "uneven" controls (p. 7, Fig. 3 p. 9), and no single best N ("problem-dependent", p. 8). This also confirms the limitation recorded in SKILL.md: no rule for choosing N is given.
- Method 3 (the partner's trained model is the problem): evidence ✅. A trained network is encoded as exact MILP constraints and its piecewise-linear structure exploited (pp. 2-3); code released (p. 10). The application here is verification, not a partner's process model.
- Method 4 (judge inside the whole solver): evidence ✅. Formulations are judged inside Gurobi's B&B with cuts, OBBT and cut callbacks interacting (pp. 8-9, 17-19), not by root relaxation alone.
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): variant ⚠. Steps followed: calibrated difficulty (p. 7), a tuned rival (cut frequency sweep, p. 8), averages over commonly solved instances (p. 16), losses reported (pp. 8-9). Steps missing: repeated runs or seeds (p. 16), an explicit OBBT-time accounting (pp. 9, 16), and identical solver settings across arms in verification (p. 9). This agrees with the documented lapse in SKILL.md.
- Heuristic 3 (calibrate so the baseline wins some instances; weak controls): evidence ✅ (p. 7; Fig. 3 p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Interpolate two formulations by aggregating variables into N groups and building the hull over the groups; prove both endpoints and monotonicity under merging (pp. 3, 5).
- Project a lifted formulation back to the original space (Fourier-Motzkin) to explain which hull inequalities it keeps, and show the lifted form is the one to implement (p. 5, Table 4 p. 17).
- Sweep the knob across 1..10 with proposed strategies and deliberately weak controls on the same instances, with and without the companion preprocessing (OBBT) (Fig. 3, p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "The formulations are hierarchical: their relaxations start at a big-M equivalent and converge to the convex hull." (p. 1)
- "While these formulations have tighter relaxations, they can perform worse than big-M due to having more difficult branch-and-bound subproblems." (p. 5)

### S020 · Mixed-integer convex nonlinear optimization with gradient-boosted trees embedded (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2021, doi:10.1287/ijoc.2020.0993; read as arXiv:1803.00952 v3)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v3, 25 Sep 2019, pp. 1-40: main text pp. 1-27 read, references pp. 28-32 scanned, electronic companion pp. 33-40 read: full formulation, notation, heuristics C.1-C.3, heuristic results D.1-D.2). Not the IJOC typeset version; page numbers are arXiv v3 pages.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Miten Mistry, Dimitrios Letsios (Imperial), Gerhard Krennrich, Robert M. Lee (BASF SE) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the first paper of the trees → ENTMOOT → OMLT line (SKILL.md, Signature Work Anatomy); funded by BASF and her EPSRC fellowship (p. 28).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: An industrial question: having learned a function from sparse, noisy data with GBTs, optimise over it inside a larger decision, "e.g., for picking the best chemical process catalyst" (abstract, p. 1). Two motives are stated: repurpose legacy predictive models, and optimise a discrete model that represents the data well (p. 1). The added twist relative to Mišić (2017) is a convex penalty term in the objective that controls risk where the model is poorly trained (pp. 2-3, 5). Global optimality is motivated by engineering cases where local minima mislead (p. 2).
  - Transferable: enter a field adjacent to a recent paper by adding one realistic ingredient (here a convex risk penalty) that changes the algorithmic picture, and state the difference in two numbered points (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Formulate min cvx(x) + GBT(x) over a box as a convex MINLP: Mišić's MILP for the tree ensemble (3a)-(3g) plus linking constraints (4a)-(4b) to the continuous x (pp. 6-7). Then a custom spatial B&B that branches only on GBT split points (p. 8) and exploits the separation of the two objective parts: Lemma 1 bounds the convex and GBT parts independently (p. 10); Lemma 2 bounds the GBT part by partitioning the ensemble into subsets solved as smaller MILPs, relaxing leaf consistency across subsets (p. 11); Algorithm 2 refines the partition at child nodes by merging pairs under a time limit (pp. 12-13); split weights based on leaf cover initialise pseudocosts (Eq. (6), p. 14); strong branching uses only the cheap convex bound to find a child that can be pruned at once (Algorithm 3, pp. 15-16). Heuristics (augmenting convex-MINLP over growing tree subsets, PSO, SA) supply incumbents (p. 16; pp. 34-37).
  - Transferable: when an objective is a sum of a cheap convex part and an expensive combinatorial part, bound them separately and use the cheap part alone for strong branching; reserve the expensive bound update for nodes where no cheap prune is found (pp. 10, 15-16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Box-constrained problem (p. 4); regression GBTs without categorical inputs (p. 3); several independently trained GBTs merged into one ensemble without loss of generality (p. 6); non-strict inequalities in the linking constraints "to avoid computational issues" (p. 7). The penalty in the case studies is a PCA-based convex quadratic (Eq. (7), p. 17) because CPLEX and Gurobi "cannot solve general convex MINLP", so a more general solver would be needed for non-quadratic penalties (pp. 18-19). The authors concede the PCA penalty can fail for clustered data (Example 4, p. 17; p. 26).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Worst-case count: at most 2^{d|T|} leaf combinations and a bound on feasibility checks, used to motivate branching on splits (p. 8). Lemma 1: separable lower bound, one-line proof by evaluating each part at the joint minimiser (p. 10). Lemma 2: relax the consistency requirement between trees by partitioning the ensemble; any partition gives a valid bound (p. 11). Definition 2 and Lemma 3: partition refinement gives a partial order and coarser partitions give bounds at least as strong, so merging parent subsets at a child cannot weaken the bound (p. 12). The paper names an alternative it declined (augmented-Lagrangian-style integration of penalty and trees) because it "would eliminate the possibility of the strong branching method" (p. 10).
  - Transferable: give a family of relaxations by partitioning a coupled set and dropping the coupling across parts; prove validity for any partition and monotonicity under coarsening, then refine the partition lazily down the B&B tree (pp. 11-13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Two instances: a public concrete-mixture instance from the UCI repository (8 variables, 7,750 trees, depth 16) and the BASF catalysis instance (42 variables, 8,800 trees) (Table 2, p. 18; pp. 19, 22-23). Python 3.5.3, Pyomo 5.2, CPLEX 12.7 and Gurobi 7.5.2 with default tolerances listed, wall-clock times, one stated desktop (pp. 18-19). Protocol: GBT lower bound vs partition subset size (Figs. 5-6, 9-10); B&B with lookahead l = 1 vs 100 and pseudocost ordering vs "10 independent tests of random branch orderings" (p. 20, p. 24); λ = 1 and 1000 (Figs. 7-8, 11-12); 24-hour runs vs Gurobi for λ = 1, 10, 100, 1000 (Tables 3-4, pp. 22, 25). Budget: B&B gets 1 h root bounding plus 1 h search vs 3-hour black-box runs (p. 21, p. 24). Results reported with losses: Gurobi's heuristics give better incumbents, the B&B gives better lower bounds (pp. 22, 25-26); ≥ 22% gap remains on concrete (p. 22); on catalysis the B&B proves optimality only at λ = 1000 (Table 4, p. 25). Root partition size is chosen per instance (70 trees vs 150 trees, pp. 20, 24) from the bound-vs-time curves. Solver outputs are checked by hand: Gurobi reports −174.1 at λ = 0 "however a manual evaluation results in −158.5" (p. 39).
  - Transferable: re-evaluate every solver's returned objective with your own code; report the discrepancy as a finding about solver tolerances (p. 39).
  - Transferable: test a branching-order heuristic against several random orderings on the same instance and budget, and test the strong-branching lookahead at two extremes (pp. 20, 26).
- **D6 Positioning**: Closest work is Mišić (2017), named in the introduction with a two-point difference: a more basic problem (no convex penalty) vs this paper's specialised large-scale B&B (p. 3); the GBT MILP is Mišić's (p. 6), with alternatives (Verwer et al., CP, SMT) acknowledged (p. 6). Places the encoding in Lombardi-Milano's "native language" category (p. 2). Separates itself from predict-then-optimise training approaches (Donti et al., Elmachtoub-Grigas, Wilder et al.): "we are more focused on the optimization side" (p. 27). Cites her own piecewise-linear modelling work as the state of the art behind the MILP (p. 6) and her solvers for strong branching and convex MINLP (pp. 15-16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Abstract opens with a data property ("Decision trees usefully represent sparse, high dimensional and noisy data", p. 1) and an industrial example; the "risk" framing is still in the v3 body ("penalty functions mitigating risk", p. 1; "risk control term", p. 5) though not in the title. The abstract calls the problem "nonconvex" (p. 1) while the title says "convex" MINLP; the discrete GBT part makes the problem nonconvex in x while its MINLP formulation has only convex nonlinearities (pp. 1, 6). Worked toy examples precede each device (Example 1 tree evaluation p. 4; Example 2 refinement p. 12; Example 3 weights p. 14; Example 4 PCA failure p. 17). **Transferable techniques**:
- Separable bounding of a mixed objective plus strong branching on the cheap part only (Lemma 1, Algorithm 3, pp. 10, 15-16).
- Partition-based relaxation of a coupled combinatorial object, valid for any partition and monotone under coarsening, refined pair-wise under a time limit at child nodes (Lemmas 2-3, Algorithm 2, pp. 11-13).
- Initialise pseudocosts from model structure (fraction of leaves covered by each split) and validate the ordering against random orderings (Eq. (6) p. 14; p. 20).
- Seed a growing-subset heuristic with trees in training order, since early boosting trees carry the coarse shape (training-aware selection, p. 35; on the concrete instance it beats best-improvement and random, p. 38, while on catalysis at λ = 1000 best-improvement finds the best solution, pp. 39-40).
