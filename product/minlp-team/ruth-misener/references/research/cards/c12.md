### S078 · Partial Lasserre relaxation for sparse Max-Cut (Optimization and Engineering 2023, DOI 10.1007/s11081-022-09763-y)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-22, including references; the paper has no appendices).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Juan S. Campos, Panos Parpas (all three at Imperial College Computing, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: follows Campos-Misener-Parpas 2019, a multilevel analysis of the Lasserre hierarchy (cited p. 5), and the Baltean-Lugojan et al. 2018 work on selecting SDP submatrices (p. 3). SKILL.md already lists it as Method 2 practice.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In practice the first and second Lasserre levels "often provide a good solution", but even level 2 cannot be solved by interior-point methods for medium or large problems (p. 2). The authors' own attempt at the sparse level-2 relaxation (Waki et al.) failed for many 300-node graphs at 2% sparsity (Mosek 8.1, 16 GB, p. 6). Above 30 nodes, typically only level 1 is solvable (p. 5). The entry point is the gap between levels: add some of the level-2 PSD constraints, chosen by the cliques of a chordal extension (abstract, p. 1; p. 3).
  - Transferable: before proposing an intermediate relaxation, show on your own hardware that the next level of the standard hierarchy fails at the target size. The failed run is the motivation (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Partial Relaxation (6) uses level 2 on the maximal cliques with at most r nodes and level 1 on the larger ones (p. 6). Augmented Partial Relaxation (7) also adds level-2 constraints on p subsets of size r of each large clique, chosen by a heuristic H (pp. 6-7). There are five selection heuristics: H1 random; H2 largest Laplacian-submatrix norm; H3 subsets in many cliques; H4 subsets in few cliques; H5 combines H2 and H4 (p. 7). What is kept: the Waki et al. sparse hierarchy and its chordal-extension cliques. What changes: the selection of which clique moment matrices get level 2. The selection uses graph clique patterns "rather than data-driven methods", unlike Baltean-Lugojan et al. (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a hierarchy level is too big, index the family by a size threshold r on the blocks you lift. The endpoints are then the two textbook levels, and r is a single dial (pp. 6, 12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Max-Cut as a ±1 POP with Laplacian objective (pp. 2, 4). Integrality does not change the sparsity pattern, so the sparse hierarchy depends only on the graph (p. 2). Convergence of the sparse hierarchy needs the running-intersection property plus redundant constraints, both cited from Lasserre 2006 (pp. 5-6). Instances are generated with SparsePOP's randomUnconst.m, so the maximal cliques of the chordal extension are not bounded by the generator's u (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof devices**: No theorem of its own. (i) Containment by citation: the dense level 2 lies inside the metric polytope (Anjos-Wolkowicz, with a footnote transferring their proof from another hierarchy, p. 10) and hence inside MET1st+ (p. 11). (ii) Comparison argument: for r ≤ 4, MET1st+ is at least as tight as (6), because metric-polytope vertices are cuts for graphs of size 3-4 (Laurent 1996, p. 12). (iii) A numerical counterexample: a specific point y on a 5-clique satisfies MET1st+, while the feasibility SDP for level-2 PSD with y fixed has no solution. So the two feasible sets differ for r ≥ 5 (footnote 2, p. 12).
  - Transferable: when two relaxations cannot be ordered by proof, show non-containment with one explicit point and a numerically solved feasibility problem, and print the point's coordinates (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: 240 random graphs (n = 300/500, u = 4-10, three weight sets, ten repeats; Table 1, p. 8); toroidal Ising grids from Biq Mac lib (Table 8, p. 15); the ten large instances (up to 5005 nodes) of the CS-TSSOS paper (Table 10, p. 17). Reference values f* are the best BiqCrunch feasible values, with the caveat "we did not obtain a certificate of optimality for all problems" (p. 9). BiqCrunch is run in four configurations (default B&B, plus three root-only tolerance settings), and the fastest that reaches the partial bound is kept (p. 11). For the Ising grids there are eight settings, including the Ising defaults; one instance needed hand-tuned settings, given in footnote 3 (pp. 15, 17). Two time tables: with and without the authors' MATLAB clique and subset construction (Tables 6-7, pp. 13-14). For CS-TSSOS, "Total time" is reported next to interior-point time, because the authors' code is Max-Cut-specific and CS-TSSOS is general (p. 20). Heuristics are ranked per (r, p), with a random control H1 that beats H3 (Table 5, pp. 12-14). Losses are printed: BiqCrunch is always faster for u = 8, 10 (p. 12); the partial relaxation cannot solve all t3 instances and is slower there (p. 17); the augmented relaxation is "often" more efficient, "but there is not a clear pattern" (p. 14).
  - Transferable: give the specialised competitor several parameter settings and keep its best time per instance, and say so (p. 11).
  - Transferable: when your preprocessing code is a prototype, report every time table twice, with and without it (Tables 6 vs 7, pp. 13-15).
  - Transferable: include a random selection rule as a control whenever you propose selection heuristics, and report how often it ranks first (Table 5, p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper calls itself "the first to computationally study sparse versions of the Lasserre hierarchy to form intermediate relaxations between levels" (p. 3), and credits Josz-Molzahn's multi-order relaxation as the same basic idea (p. 2). It reports that after the preprint, Chen et al. 2022 generalised this paper's ideas into a sublevel hierarchy and used some of its heuristics (p. 2). It concedes that linear branch-and-cut (Liers et al.) beats Biq Mac on sparse toroidal grids (p. 3). It relates the work to cutting-plane approximations of SDP relaxations, including Misener's own 2018 work (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**: Short and table-driven: 11 tables carry the results (pp. 8-19). Claims are scoped by graph density each time ("particularly useful if the maximal cliques ... are small", p. 3; conclusion, p. 20). Hedging is specific ("there is not a clear pattern", p. 14). It openly calls its own MATLAB code improvable: "our intention was not at this point to generate a very efficient code" (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is computational: a parameterised family plus selection heuristics, evaluated on three instance families. The limit is stated precisely: the partial relaxations "lose power as the density and/or the size of the maximal cliques increases" (p. 20). The open question is how to choose r and p. The proposed route is multilevel techniques (pp. 20-21).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the family between Lasserre level 1 and level 2, indexed by r (and p). The endpoints are the textbook levels (pp. 6, 12). A random control is included (p. 14). The parameter-choice problem is printed as open (pp. 20-21), which matches SKILL.md's "no rule for choosing the parameter published".
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: four BiqCrunch configurations with the fastest kept (p. 11), which matches the T3 [24:29] talk quote in SKILL.md; timings with and without the authors' own construction code (pp. 13-14), which matches T3 [29:04]; uncertified reference values flagged (p. 9); losses by density class printed (pp. 12, 17).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the relaxation is not embedded in a branch-and-bound. It is judged as a stand-alone bound against the time BiqCrunch's full B&B needs to reach the same value (pp. 11-12). The full solver serves as the yardstick, not as the host.
- new-pattern candidate: **numerical counterexample for relaxation non-containment** (footnote 2, p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Time-to-match comparison: measure how long the exact solver needs to reach your relaxation's bound, using integrality (BC* < ⌈Q⌉) to make "as good as" exact (pp. 11, 15).
- Report the size statistics of the structure that drives tractability (max/median/mean clique size of the chordal extension) for every instance family. Results can then be read against that statistic rather than against n (Tables 1, 8, 10).

**Quotes**:
- "For each Max-Cut instance, our comparisons are with respect to the fastest time of these four implementations that reached the solution" (p. 11)
- "Although the partial relaxation showed potential, there is still the question of how to select the parameters r and p in the partial relaxation." (p. 20)

### S038 · Augmenting optimization-based molecular design with graph neural networks (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2024, DOI 10.1016/j.compchemeng.2024.108684; read as arXiv:2312.03613v1)
**Read level**: full, arXiv v1 preprint (file pp. 1-28 of text and figures, pp. 28-35 references skimmed). The published CACE version was not read. Pages are those of the arXiv preprint ("S038 arXiv").
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shiqiang Zhang, Juan S. Campos (Imperial); Christian Feldmann, Frederik Sandfort, Miriam Mathea (BASF SE, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the journal extension of the NeurIPS 2023 paper "Optimizing over trained GNNs via symmetry breaking" (p. 3). It feeds S050, which "inherit[s]" its formulation (S050 p. 3). It is the GNN branch of Method 3's trees → ENTMOOT → OMLT line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: CAMD has well-established MIP formulations of molecules, but GNN-based design mostly uses GAs or BO that only evaluate the GNN "rather than directly handling the inverse problems defined on GNNs" (p. 2). The exception is McDonald et al. 2023 (p. 2). The authors "advocate using machine learning models, including GNNs, as score functions", which "brings new challenges to optimization" (p. 2). The conference version had two limits: at most N = 8 atoms, and no aromaticity (p. 17).
  - Transferable: open a journal extension by listing the conference version's limitations as bullets, then map each new section onto one of them (pp. 3, 17).
- **D2 Core idea**: (i) A big-M encoding of a GNN layer with a variable adjacency matrix: auxiliary z = A·x with four big-M inequalities. The bounds are tighter than in the conference version, and the bilinear alternative is dropped because the big-M version won on time-to-optimum and time-to-proof (p. 5). (ii) A GNNLayer class in OMLT with a PyG import path, so a sequential PyG model becomes a Pyomo block (pp. 6-9; Table 1, p. 10). (iii) The atom-based CAMD MIP is generalised to fragments: atom types are replaced by fragment types and covalence by attachment positions, which admits aromatic rings and larger molecules (pp. 16-17, Table 4). (iv) Symmetry-breaking constraints (S1)-(S3) for graph isomorphism, realised as (C5), (C26) and (C27) with "universal ordering vector" coefficients (pp. 15-16).
  - Transferable: when an existing formulation is too small or misses a feature (aromaticity), change the unit of the decision graph (atoms → fragments) so that every structural and symmetry constraint carries over unchanged (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Weights and biases are fixed. Aggregation must be linear (sum, mean), in the form (lth layer) (p. 4). With a non-fixed graph, GCNConv and mean aggregation are excluded because they give "difficult mixed-integer nonlinear" problems (p. 9). Nonlinear activations are allowed but "inadvisable" (p. 10). Bounds on input features propagate by interval arithmetic (p. 5). For the experiments Av,v = 1, so molecules have exactly N fragments (p. 11).
- **D4 Proof devices**: No new theorem. The key device is the existence of a feasible indexing under (S1)+(S3) for any graph with a chosen node 0, cited from Zhang et al. 2023 and illustrated by an indexing algorithm (pp. 15-16, Figs. 3-4). (S2) is then imposed by an application-specific hierarchy function h, built as a bijection from feature vectors to integers through powers of 2 (p. 16). Rearranging these coefficients encodes chemistry requirements: for banana odour, ordering h so that an aromatic fragment or a double-bonded oxygen must take index 0 turns an existential requirement into one constraint (B1)-(B2) (pp. 18-20).
  - Transferable: to impose "there exists a node with property P" under symmetry breaking, give P the highest lexicographic weight in the symmetry-breaking order. The existential condition then becomes a bound on node 0 (pp. 18-20).
- **D5 Experiments**: Odour data from Sharma et al. 2021 (4682 molecules), with banana and garlic chosen for diversity and compatibility (p. 17). Positive and negative classes are built by fragment-set filtering: 300 and 262 molecules (pp. 22-23). Ten GNNs per odour (two SAGEConv layers, mean pooling, dense layer). Train/test accuracy is 0.905/0.703 (banana) and 0.997/0.846 (garlic) (p. 23). Optimality runs: N = 4-12, 5 Gurobi seeds × 10 models = 50 runs per N, 10 h limit. The tables report variables and constraints after presolve, the number of successful runs, and Q1/mean/Q3 of time-to-optimum and total time (Tables 6-7, p. 24; Fig. 5, p. 25). Banana reaches 9/50 successful runs at N = 12; garlic reaches 5/50 at N = 12. A separate feasibility mode covers N = 15-50 (5 h, MIPFocus=1, one run each; p. 23). Positive objectives come for most runs with N ≤ 25; solutions with N = 30-40 are mostly negative; for N > 40 only 1 of 20 runs is feasible (p. 25). There is no competing method (no GA/BO or McDonald et al. baseline). The experiments measure scaling, not a comparison.
  - Transferable: run each instance size with several trained models and several solver seeds, and report quartiles and success counts out of all runs, not a single time (Tables 6-7, p. 24).
  - Transferable: once proving optimality stops scaling, switch to a declared feasibility protocol (feasibility-focused solver setting, shorter limit). Report objective sign or quality per run, and the N at which even feasibility fails (pp. 23, 25).
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits McDonald et al. as first and generalises their big-M from GraphSAGE to all GNNs of the (lth layer) form (pp. 2, 5). Inherits the "well-established" CAMD MIP literature from Odele-Macchietto onward (p. 2). Places itself in the optimisation-over-trained-models literature, citing her own tree and ReLU work (p. 3). The new contributions over the conference paper are four bullets (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**: Software sections teach by worked examples (Linear, GCNConv, SAGEConv, pooling as GNNLayer parameters, pp. 7-8). Each chemistry requirement is stated in words, given a reason ("unstable or reactive", "low vapor pressure"), and then written as a linear constraint (pp. 20-21). Admissions are candid: requirements were added because "the optimization results showed a tendency" toward heteroatom chains (p. 20), and "we still find several impractical molecular structures" (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution combines a formulation (tighter big-M), software (OMLT GNN support) and an application (fragment-based odorant design with chemistry constraints). Stated limits: optimality is reachable only up to 12 fragments, and the reason given for the failure at large N is "possible", not established (p. 25). Unsupported layers are listed (Table 1, p. 10). The conclusion has no explicit future-work list (p. 25). I note, without the paper discussing it, that the test accuracy of 0.703 for banana is modest, and that no chemist evaluation of the designed molecules is reported (Figs. 6-7 only display them, pp. 26-27).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the partner's model class (GNN) is written as constraints, solved exactly, and generalised into OMLT behind a PyG interface (pp. 5-10). BASF coauthors supply the chemistry. The data set is public (Sharma et al.), so no stand-in is needed (p. 17).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (step 4, "keep the optimiser near the data"): bounds taken from the data set on atom counts, double/triple bonds and rings (C22)-(C25), "to avoid spurious extrapolation" (p. 14), plus filters on the negative class (p. 22). A PCA distance penalty is not used.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: graph-isomorphism symmetry (N! indexings, Fig. 2) as structure. It is removed by constraints that keep at least one feasible indexing (pp. 15-16).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: rigorous on run statistics (50 runs, quartiles, success counts), but there is no competitor and the scaling study is the only evaluation (pp. 23-25).
- new-pattern candidate: **domain-expert constraint iteration** (requirements added after inspecting the optimiser's molecules, with stated chemical reasons; pp. 17-18, 20-21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode "at least one of these groups exists" by ordering the symmetry-breaking coefficients so that the group must sit at index 0 (pp. 18-20).
- Implement a new layer type in a toolkit through a general layer class plus worked conversions for each supported operation. Publish a supported/unsupported table separately for fixed and variable graph structure (pp. 7-10).

**Quotes**:
- "These symmetric solutions significantly enlarge the search space and slow down the solving process." (p. 15)
- "This requirement is included as the optimization results showed a tendency to include a huge amount of connected heteroatoms in suggested structures." (p. 20)

### S050 · Verifying message-passing neural networks via topology-based bounds tightening (ICML 2024, PMLR 235; arXiv:2402.13937)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-14: main text, Appendix A proofs, Appendix B implementation notes, the Appendix C narrative and Tables 3-5). Tables 6-11 (pp. 21-26) were skimmed for the per-budget numbers I cite. The figures on pp. 17-20 have only captions in the text file.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Christopher Hojny (TU Eindhoven, equal contribution), Shiqiang Zhang (equal contribution), Juan S. Campos (Imperial) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: carries the S038 GNN encoding over to verification of trained models. The collaboration was started at the Mittag-Leffler seminar "Learning from Both Sides: Linear and Nonlinear Mixed-Integer Optimization" (p. 10). It is the GNN counterpart of her ReLU verification work (Botoeva et al. 2020, cited p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: GNNs are vulnerable, and certificates in the literature each restrict the perturbation type (features only, structure only, removal only) or the architecture (mostly GCNs) (pp. 1-2). Because the MIP route encodes perturbations as constraints, it "admits (i) both adding and removing edges, (ii) both global and local budgets, and (iii) both topological perturbations and feature modifications" (abstract, p. 1). The problems are too large for convex-hull-type ReLU formulations, so the lever is variable bounds in the big-M model (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when the best-known formulation (here the convex hull) is too large for the instances, improve the big-M coefficients instead, and say in the introduction why the stronger formulation was not used (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Topology-based bounds tightening. Static (sbt): the pre-activation lower bound is the contribution of the original neighbours plus the most negative changes that fit in the local budget qv. It is found by sorting Δu→v, with complexity O(N²d_{l−1}d_l + N log N) (Property 3.2, Eqs. 17-19, pp. 4-5). Aggressive (abt): in every B&B node the same computation runs on the graph modified by the A-variables fixed so far, with the remaining local and global budget q′v (Eq. 23). It is implemented as local cutting planes through a SCIP separator callback (pp. 5-7; App. B.1, pp. 13-14). Neither basic interval arithmetic nor sbt uses budgets or branching decisions; Figure 1 shows the bounds [−9, 9] → [−4, 9] → [−1, 4] on one toy node (p. 2). The verification MIP is stopped as soon as robustness or non-robustness is decided (p. 7).
  - Transferable: if a bound only needs a "best k changes" combinatorial step, prove a closed form (sort and take the k most negative). This makes the tightening cheap enough to run in every B&B node (pp. 5, 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: ReLU MPNNs (GraphSAGE-type) with fixed weights. Perturbation sets: P1 is symmetric, with global budget 2Q and local budgets, for graph classification; P2 allows removal only, on the 2-hop neighbourhood, for node classification (pp. 4, 8). The paper notes that 2-hop suffices for MPNN but not for GCN (p. 8). The experiments perturb only the adjacency matrix. Feature perturbations are "well-studied", so they are left out (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Property 3.2 splits the neighbour sum into original, added, removed and inactive nodes, which gives Σ lb_{u→v} + Σ Δ_{u→v} over the modified set (App. A, p. 13). Property 3.4 is proved by reduction: apply Property 3.2 to the modified target graph with the reduced budget (Remark 3.5, pp. 6, 13). Validity is handled by node-local cuts: an inequality based on fixed variables is valid only in the subtree (pp. 7, 13-14). The paper also argues why FastLin, CROWN and DeepPoly do not carry over: a binary adjacency matrix breaks the linearity between layers that they rely on. OBBT is the only existing method that does carry over (p. 6).
  - Transferable: prove the node-level version of a bound by reduction to the root version on a modified instance (fixed decisions folded into the data, budget reduced). One proof then covers both (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: SCIP 8.0.4 with SoPlex, one thread, on a shared cluster ("the jobs have not been run exclusively", p. 8). Benchmarks: ENZYMES and MUTAG for graph classification; Cora and CiteSeer for node classification (Table 1, p. 6). Local attack strength s ∈ {2,3,4} and global budget δ = 1-10% of the edges. Limits are 2 h per graph and 30 min per node (pp. 8-9). Methods: SCIPbasic, SCIPsbt, SCIPabt, GRBbasic and GRBsbt. abt is not run in Gurobi, which has no local cuts (p. 8). Model creation and the negligible basic/sbt bound computation are excluded from the times, while abt's in-tree bounding time is included (pp. 8-9). Metrics: average time, shifted geometric mean (shift 10) and number solved, each on all instances and on robust instances. Robust instances are the headline set, because non-robust ones "may frequently be found by more heuristic approaches" (Table 2 caption, p. 7). Ground truth is by consensus: an instance counts as robust if all methods say so, and inconsistent instances are excluded (p. 9; App. B.2, p. 13). The authors found and reported a Gurobi bug. They kept the buggy version, with a 1e-11 right-hand-side shift, because "there was insufficient time to redo all the experiments" after the rebuttal (p. 13). There is an OBBT control on the 44 robust MUTAG instances: 6045 s of bounding for a 331.60 s solve, against 336.41 s for SCIPsbt (p. 14). Results: SCIPsbt is about 3× faster than SCIPbasic (p. 9). abt is roughly 10% faster than sbt in shifted geometric mean on robust MUTAG (p. 9), but not overall (Table 2, p. 7). GRBsbt is slower than GRBbasic on all MUTAG instances, which the paper explains by MUTAG instances being mostly non-robust (p. 9). Node classification is trivial for all methods, and there extra cuts only cost time (p. 9; Table 3, p. 14).
  - Transferable: when ground truth is unknowable (robust = a dual bound), label an instance only when all solvers agree, and drop the inconsistent ones rather than trusting either solver's status (pp. 9, 13).
  - Transferable: split results by answer type (robust vs non-robust). Bound-tightening helps proofs and can hurt the search for a counterexample, and a pooled average hides that (pp. 7, 9).
  - Transferable: test against OBBT as a "tightest affordable bounds" control, and report both its bounding cost and a per-layer relative bound tightness against your cheap bounds (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: Divides prior work into adversarial attacks (mostly first-order, flipping binaries) and certifiable robustness (GCN-specific, perturbation-specific, or randomized smoothing) (pp. 1-2). Builds on the group's GNN encoding (McDonald et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2023, 2024) and on Anderson et al.'s big-M for ReLU (pp. 2-3). Adopts "aggressive bounds tightening" from Belotti et al. 2016 (p. 5). Notes that PySCIPOpt-ML could have been extended instead (p. 6). Cites Badilla et al. 2023 for the finding that tighter bounds can slow solving (p. 9), and Carvajal et al. 2014 for running strategies in parallel (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing**: ICML format, with three numbered "key outcomes" in the introduction (p. 2). The intro claim "from a factor of 10 to a factor of 3" (p. 2) is roughly consistent with my own ratios from Table 2 for robust ENZYMES average times (278.58/32.80 ≈ 8.5 → 82.89/22.90 ≈ 3.6, p. 7), but the paper does not give the calculation. The "extra 266" instances match s = 2 (ENZYMES 5831−5579 = 252, plus MUTAG 1589−1575 = 14; Table 2, p. 7; my arithmetic). One labelling slip: p. 6 names static tightening "SCIPabt" and aggressive "SCIPsbt", the reverse of every table. The negative results are given in full. abt does not dominate sbt, and the paper explains why harder problems need not be robust (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic (two tightening routines with proofs) plus software (a SCIP extension, with code on Zenodo, p. 6). Limits: abt does not beat sbt on average (Table 2, p. 7). Node-classification instances are too easy to tell the methods apart (p. 9). Feature perturbations are not tested (p. 4). The conclusion gives no future directions (p. 9). Instead of choosing a default, the paper recommends "running SCIPsbt and SCIPabt in parallel" (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: graph topology and perturbation budgets are the "insider information" for bounds. A generic solver's interval arithmetic ignores them (basic, Remark 3.3, p. 5), and exploiting them gives a 3× speed-up (p. 9).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the trained MPNN is the problem. Verification becomes a MIP over the trained model, reusing the group's GNN encoding. OMLT's GNN pull request is used to debug the C/C++ SCIP implementation (p. 6), so the toolkit doubles as a test oracle.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the components are judged inside full SCIP and Gurobi B&B, split by answer type (pp. 7-9). But no default is chosen between sbt and abt. The verdict is "run both in parallel" (p. 9), a portfolio, not the "robust variant becomes the default" rule.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence, with a documented lapse: consensus ground truth, inconsistent instances excluded, a solver bug found and reported, three metrics on two instance subsets, and an OBBT control (pp. 9, 13-14). The lapse: the buggy Gurobi version was kept for lack of time and patched by an RHS perturbation, which made Gurobi return infeasible solutions on some instances, and those were then excluded (p. 13). This echoes SKILL.md's "infeasible answer is a failure" rule, but here the instances are dropped rather than counted as Gurobi failures.
- new-pattern candidate: **consensus ground truth across solvers** (label only where all solvers agree; pp. 9, 13).
- new-pattern candidate: **portfolio instead of default** (when neither variant dominates, recommend parallel execution; p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Implement bound improvements that depend on branching decisions as local cutting planes in a separator callback, so the solver adds only the violated ones (pp. 7, 13-14).
- Stop the MIP as soon as the sign question is answered (negative primal = attack; positive dual = certificate), instead of solving to optimality (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "We cannot know the ground truth of a robust instance without complete enumeration or relying on a solver’s numerical tolerances" (p. 9)
- "we found and reported a small bug in Gurobi wherein several instances were declared infeasible despite having a feasible solution found by SCIP." (p. 13)
