### S117 · A practical analysis of optimisation and recovery under uncertainty (MEng thesis, Imperial College London 2019, no DOI)

**Read level**: partial. Read: title page, abstract, acknowledgements, contents (pp. 1-6); Chapter 1 in full (pp. 7-9); §3.7 opening (pp. 36-37); §4.3-4.5 (pp. 49-54); §5.5 summary (p. 72); §6.5 light recoverable robustness in full (pp. 83-87); §7.4-7.6 opening (pp. 96-98); Chapter 8 in full (pp. 105-108). Not read: Chapter 2 background, most of Chapter 3 models, §4.2 statistics, §5.1-5.4 recovery experiments, §6.1-6.4 buffering and recoverable robustness, §7.1-7.3, appendices.
**Role**: supplement (student's voice, supervised by Misener) · **Coauthors**: Suraj G (author), Ruth Misener (supervisor), Aldo Faisal (second marker) (p. 1); "This project is run in conjunction with the Royal Mail Data Science Group." (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: the thesis feeds S135 ("A recent MEng thesis considers several of these contexts in greater detail [7]", S135 arXiv p. 20) and builds on Letsios and Misener's lexicographic-optimisation recovery result for makespan scheduling (cited as [29], p. 72).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Royal Mail's delivery offices must minimise vans per delivery office with same-day delivery while jobs expand, shrink or vans fail during the day (abstract, p. 2; §1.1, p. 7). Entry is a critique of a prior model (Page [36]) that "does not model which job a van executes" (p. 8). Problem statement: "To what extent can we measure the efficacy of, and then design schedules which are both robust to and recoverable from uncertainty?" (p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea**: (i) a unified van-itinerary-timeslot model, judged intractable (cubic-order constraints), and an approximate model shown to be a Variable Size Bin Packing Problem (pp. 8, 36); (ii) a "3-step process" (initial schedule → uncertainty realisation → recovery) used as the common frame for comparing strategies (§4.3, p. 49); (iii) a weighted "disturbance" score (affected itineraries, spilling vans, extra vans; weights a < b < c, a + b + c = 100, set to 10/40/50) normalised by the optimal number of vans (§4.5, pp. 52-54); (iv) recovery as an integer program, compared against LPT recovery (pp. 72, 96-97); (v) "light recoverable robustness" (LRRP): recoverable robustness with per-row slack minimised and the objective capped by a price factor Pr times a nominal optimum (§6.5.2, p. 84).
- **D3 Assumptions**: uncertainty realised once, at one arbitrary point in time (p. 49); no van starts an itinerary earlier than scheduled (timetabling condition after Liebchen et al., p. 49); perturbation types 4-6 (new van, new itinerary, lost itinerary) ignored (pp. 49-50); price factor Pr and recovery budget D are fixed constants that must be chosen by hand (pp. 86-87, 107).
- **D4 Analysis**: one proof: LRRP generalises recoverable robustness, argued by contradiction after fixing all slacks to 0 and Pr = 1 (§6.5.3, pp. 85-86). The disturbance score's limitation is stated: it "had no regard for the optimal number of vans" (p. 54).
- **D5 Experiments**: Royal Mail data from three delivery offices; integer recovery programs against LPT recovery as "ground truth" baseline; only model #5 beats LPT on the thesis's α and γ measures (p. 96); parameters varied one at a time, not jointly (p. 98); the Chapter 6 models were not evaluated computationally (p. 107).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: four contributions are listed (pp. 105-106): remodelling with the VSBPP link, IP recovery, the first stochastic delay-resistant formulation for the VSBPP (by the student's claim), and LRRP. Limits are specific: the Chapter 6 models were not evaluated because they are too large; LRRP needs Pr and D chosen by hand, and setting Pr = 1 likely makes the model infeasible (pp. 107-108). Future work: symmetry-breaking cuts and bounds for the unified model, multistage settings, multi-objective scalarisation "such as those proposed in LexOpt" (pp. 107-108).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (variant ⚠, student's voice): losses and evaluation limits are written in, with a section titled "Criticisms Of Analysis and Evaluation Techniques" (p. 98) and an admission that only one IP beats the LPT baseline (p. 96). But the protocol is weaker than Method 5: parameters are changed one at a time and the final models are not run (pp. 98, 107).
- Heuristic 10 (evidence; not a Method): uncertainty made explicit through a two-stage frame, with a stakeholder-facing cost function (spill over union hours, backup vans) (pp. 49, 52-53).
- new-pattern candidate: "Industrial line through a student project": a partner's operational problem is first explored in a supervised MEng thesis with partner data, then turned into a group paper with theory (S135 arXiv p. 20 cites the thesis).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before comparing scheduling strategies under uncertainty, fix one life-cycle frame (plan → perturb → recover) and one normalised score, so every strategy is judged on the same three stages (pp. 49, 52-54).
- When a full model is too large, write an approximate model, name what it drops (here the time relation and the gate constraint), and identify the classical problem it becomes (VSBPP) to reuse that literature (pp. 8, 97, 105).

**Quotes**:
- "To what extent can we measure the efficacy of, and then design schedules which are both robust to and recoverable from uncertainty?" (p. 8)
- "Due to time constraints, the models in the very last chapter could not successfully evaluated with the framework we set up (perturbation generation, disturbance scoring, etc.)." (p. 107)

### S135 · Approximating Bounded Job Start Scheduling with Application in Royal Mail Deliveries Under Uncertainty (COCOA 2019, doi:10.1007/978-3-030-36412-0_6; read as arXiv:1912.06862v1)

**Read level**: partial, on the extended arXiv version (the COCOA proceedings text is not the file). Read: abstract and §1 with contributions (pp. 1-4); §2 with Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 proofs (pp. 4-6); §3 Theorem 3 statement and proof opening (pp. 6-7); §4 opening, Lemma 1 proof and Lemma 2 statement (pp. 7-9); §5 algorithm LSM and Theorem 7 statement (pp. 12-13); §6-§8 in full (pp. 16-24). Not read: proofs of Lemmas 2-3 and Theorems 3-7 in detail (pp. 7-16), references.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Dimitrios Letsios (first author in the arXiv version), Jeremy T. Bradley (Royal Mail GBI/Data Science Group), Suraj G, Natasha Page (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: a physical constraint at Royal Mail delivery offices, a van exit gate that bounds how many vehicles can depart per unit time, generalises P||Cmax into the Bounded Job Start Scheduling Problem (BJSP): at most g ≤ m jobs may start per time unit (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). The authors find no approximation algorithms for bounded job starts in the literature (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: split instances by where idle time comes from, long (pj ≥ m) versus short (pj < m) (p. 3), then (i) LPT is tightly 2-approximate in general, 5/3-approximate on long and optimal on short instances (pp. 3, 6, 10-11); (ii) Long-Short Mixing (LSM) reserves mL = ⌈5m/6⌉ machines for long jobs and runs short jobs on the rest in parallel, giving 1.985 on a broad family, or with 1.2-machine augmentation in general (Algorithm 1, Theorem 7, p. 13); (iii) for uncertainty, a two-stage robust approach: stage 1 minimises a characteristic value V(S) + θ·W(S) with exponential completion-time weights (lexicographic optimisation), stage 2 keeps start times fixed and reassigns machines online (§6.3, pp. 18-19).
- **D3 Assumptions**: integer processing times, non-preemptive jobs, identical machines (p. 4); g = 1 in §4-§5, justified because "any Royal Mail instance can be converted to g = 1 using small discretization" (p. 3); m ≥ 7 and m = ω(1) for LSM (p. 12); processing times perturbed by factors in [1/F, F] (p. 17); the θ weight is chosen by experiment: "Section 7 selects the θ value empirically" (p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: strong NP-hardness for g = 1 by reduction from 3-Partition with processing times n²aj (pp. 4-5); an O(log n) integrality gap for the natural time-indexed IP, so LP rounding needs a stronger relaxation (p. 6); an exchange argument giving an optimal "compact" schedule plus an idle-time lower bound m(m-1)/2 (Lemma 1, p. 8); a concave relaxation for bounding idle machine time is named as the key ingredient for the 5/3 ratio (p. 3); an Ω(m) gap between the P||Cmax relaxation and the BJSP optimum shown by adding unit jobs (p. 2).
- **D5 Experiments**: historical data from three delivery offices (78, 111, 111 working days, 300 instances, 15-minute discretisation, part of the data encrypted), Pyomo + CPLEX 12.8: nominal optimal schedules use at least 10, 25 and 10 fewer vehicles per day than the historical schedules (pp. 20-21); perturbation and g-sensitivity plots (Figs. 3-4); robustness: lower characteristic value in a CPLEX solution pool goes with fewer final vehicles (Fig. 5, p. 22). The text says processing times are "decreased by a factor fj = 0.5" and then speaks of "doubling itinerary durations" (p. 21), an unresolved inconsistency in the arXiv version.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: contributions restated as better-than-2 approximations for cases of the problem and a two-stage robust approach "substantiated empirically" (p. 23). Open questions are specific: a ratio below 2 without resource augmentation; the price of robustness of the two-stage method; multi-office vehicle sharing (pp. 23-24).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): structure detection appears in a theory form. The long/short split is read off the mechanism that creates idle time, and the proofs and the LSM design exploit it (p. 3). No solver-side detector is involved.
- Method 2 (variant ⚠): LSM sits between "long first" and "short first" greedy orders, which "cannot achieve an approximation ratio better than 2" (p. 3), and fixes its knob at mL = ⌈5m/6⌉ by analysis. The θ weight of the robust model is chosen by experiment only ("Section 7 selects the θ value empirically", p. 18); no θ sweep is reported in the §7 text read (pp. 20-22).
- Heuristic 2 (evidence; not a Method): industrial instance first (Royal Mail data, partly encrypted, p. 20), general problem class second (BJSP generalises P||Cmax, p. 2).
- new-pattern candidate: "Industrial constraint → named problem class → approximation theory": an operational detail (the van exit gate, p. 2) becomes a new named scheduling class with NP-hardness, integrality-gap and approximation results.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To motivate a new problem class, show that the obvious relaxation (drop the new constraint) can be a factor Ω(m) off with a two-line instance construction, and that any ρ-approximation for the relaxed class gives a 2ρ-approximation for the new class (pp. 2-3).
- Before trying LP rounding, compute the integrality gap of the natural time-indexed formulation on a trivial instance; a non-constant gap (O(log n) here) tells you to look for a stronger relaxation first (p. 6).
- Split instances by the mechanism that causes waste (here: idle time from simultaneous long-job completions versus from start-rate limits on short jobs) and prove a ratio per class before mixing them (pp. 3, 12-13).

**Quotes**:
- "distinction comes from the observation that idle time occurs mainly because of (i) simultaneous job completions for long jobs and (ii) limited allowable parallel job executions for short jobs." (p. 3)
- "This finding is a strong indication that more efficient fleet management might be possible in Royal Mail delivery offices." (p. 21)

### S069 · Approximation algorithms for process systems engineering (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2020, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2019.106599; arXiv:1909.12328)

**Read level**: partial (arXiv v1 preprint). Read: abstract and §1 (pp. 1-3); §2 definitions (pp. 3-4); §3 applications with Examples 1-4 (pp. 4-6); §4 opening and §4.1 (pp. 6-7); §4.4 with Table 1 (pp. 10-11); §5 opening, §5.1, §5.4 (pp. 12-16); §6 opening, §6.4 (pp. 17, 21-22); §7 in full (pp. 22-23). Not read: problem definitions and formulations §4.2-4.3, §5.2-5.3, §6.1-6.3 (pp. 7-10, 13-16, 17-21), references.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Dimitrios Letsios, Radu Baltean-Lugojan, Francesco Ceccon, Miten Mistry, Johannes Wiebe (p. 1); Misener is the corresponding author (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: a bridge argument between two communities. Five contrasts between theoretical computer science and process systems engineering (worst case vs practical instances, analytical guarantees vs computational global optimality, bottom-up special cases vs top-down decompositions, near-optimal vs global, polynomial tractability vs industrial scalability) (pp. 1-2), then the claim that approximation algorithms "are deeply applicable to PSE" despite those surface distinctions (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: no new algorithm. The paper offers four uses of approximation theory in PSE: evaluating relaxation quality by worst-case analysis (modelling), classifying problems and heuristics (classification), designing fast certified methods for huge instances (solution methods), and characterising robust and recoverable solutions (uncertainty) (§3, pp. 4-6). It then surveys three NP-hard PSE problems (pooling, process scheduling, heat exchanger network synthesis), each with a complexity and approximation section (§4.4, §5.4, §6.4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: standard approximation definitions (ρ-approximation, PTAS/FPTAS) under P ≠ NP (pp. 2-4) (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: a survey; the proof template is explained as matching an analytic lower bound C_LB with an algorithm's value C_A ≤ ρ·C_LB (Figure 1, pp. 3-4). Table 1 maps pooling subcases to P or NP-hard with the reduction used (Independent Set, Exact Cover by 3-Sets, Partition, Max/Min Satisfiability) (p. 11), including the group's own strongly polynomial single-pool single-attribute case (Baltean-Lugojan and Misener 2018, p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a field-building survey with six numbered open questions, each ending in a question (pooling as multicommodity flow; sparse and discrete relaxations for classification; controllable processing times and speed scaling; relaxations from precedence-constrained and shop scheduling; complexity of the multistage minimum utility cost problem; packing view of minimum number of matches) (pp. 22-23). It concedes that "heuristics with performance guarantees cannot fully address the very complex, highly inapproximable, industrially-relevant optimization problems in PSE" (p. 2) and that PTAS are often not competitive in practice (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): structure is used to delimit tractability: "Approximation algorithms exploit special structure and expose tractable optimization problem subcases." (p. 5); Table 1's P/NP-hard boundary for pooling is a structure map (p. 11). The variant is that the output is a complexity classification, not a detector inside a solver.
- Workflow B step 4 and Heuristic 6 (evidence; not Methods): borrowing from a neighbouring community (TCS), and the "why does it work?" question answered by complexity results (p. 11).
- new-pattern candidate: "Numbered open-question list as the paper's deliverable": the conclusion turns each surveyed gap into a question to the reader (pp. 22-23); S135 arXiv (pp. 23-24) and S099 (p. 4) also end with specific next steps.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write a cross-community position paper as: numbered list of intellectual differences → definitions the other side needs → four concrete uses, each with one published example → per-problem complexity tables → numbered open questions (pp. 1-6, 11, 22-23).
- Summarise a problem's complexity landscape as one table: subcase, P or NP-hard, and the source problem of the reduction (Table 1, p. 11). Gaps in the table then become research questions.

**Quotes**:
- "This paper argues that, contrary to the aforementioned, surface-level distinctions, approximation algorithms are deeply applicable to PSE." (p. 2)
- "Approximation algorithms exploit special structure and expose tractable optimization problem subcases." (p. 5)

### S073 · AI-assisted schedule explainer for nurse rostering (AAMAS 2020 demonstration, doi:10.65109/djgo9548)

**Read level**: full (2 pages of text and the reference list, pp. 1-3).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Kristijonas Čyras, Amin Karamlou, Myles Lee, Dimitrios Letsios, Francesca Toni (p. 1); the first four did the work while at Imperial (p. 1 footnote).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: solvers are "black-boxes" whose outcomes "are hardly explainable even to experts, let alone lay users" (p. 1); the earlier ArgOpt paradigm (Čyras, Letsios, Misener, Toni, AAAI 2019) was "described only in principle", so this paper builds the system (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: a three-part system: an optimisation solver (GLPK) for makespan scheduling, an abstract-argumentation layer that encodes feasibility, single- and pairwise-exchange efficiency and fixed decisions, and an interface that turns argument paths into ranked, actionable natural-language explanations with suggested job moves; after each user action the schedule is re-checked (pp. 1-2). Explanations are filtered by importance: feasibility and fixed decisions first, then moves "in decreasing order of time saved" (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions**: P||Cmax makespan scheduling (jobs = patient procedures, machines = nurses) (p. 2); "good" means efficient, i.e. feasible and not improvable by exchanging one or two jobs, a provably approximate optimality notion used because optimal schedules are NP-hard to find (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no proofs; soundness and completeness are cited from the AAAI 2019 paper, p. 1). Complexity of the check: at most quadratic in mn time and memory (p. 2).
- **D5 Experiments**: over 30 informal usability surveys, "not statistically significant"; one urgent care centre manager's qualitative feedback; runtime under a second for mn ≤ 1000 on a 2-core VM (p. 2).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: claims "a first-of-a-kind, argumentation-supported explainable scheduling system" (p. 2). Limits are stated: the nurse GUI "is not intended to handle real scenaria", and the surveys lack statistical significance (p. 2). Future work: scheduling variants and evaluation with domain experts (p. 2). Code and video are public (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- new-pattern candidate: "Explainability layer between solver and lay user": the optimiser becomes one component in a human-facing system whose goodness criterion (single and pairwise exchanges) is chosen to be checkable and explainable, not optimal (p. 2).
- Workflow A step 2 (evidence; not a Method): the problem source is a user facing a decision (a shift-lead nurse), with NHS feedback acknowledged (p. 2).
- No link to Methods 1-5: this is a demonstration paper with no solver comparison or formulation family.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the full optimality certificate is not explainable, define "good" through a local property that a user can act on (no improving single or pairwise exchange) and generate explanations as violations of that property, ranked by gain (p. 2).
- State a demo's evaluation limits in the paper itself (informal surveys, GUI not for real scenarios) next to the positive feedback (p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "While the survey results do not present statistically significant results, we found that Schedule Explainer’s backend was largely usable and the explanations comprehensible and manageable." (p. 2)
- "GUI is not intended to handle real scenaria or depict how the system would appear to the user, e.g. the shift-lead." (p. 2)

### S087 · GALINI: An extensible mixed-integer quadratically-constrained optimization solver (Optimization Online 2020/2021, no DOI)

**Read level**: partial. Read: §1-§8 in full (pp. 1-17), Figure captions on p. 18. Not read: references (pp. 19-24) and Appendices A-B on Coramin relaxations and plug-in interfaces (pp. 25-26).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Francesco Ceccon, Radu Baltean-Lugojan, Michael L. Bynum (Sandia), Can Li (p. 1); Misener is the contact author (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: many MIQCQP solvers exist (about 30 listed, with a release timeline, pp. 2-3), but only SCIP and MINOTAUR target developers; GALINI is a standalone open-source Python/Pyomo solver that algorithm developers extend at runtime through plug-ins "without modifying the underlying solver" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: a generic BranchAndBoundAlgorithm class with seven abstract methods (Listing 1, pp. 7-9) and a branch-and-cut built on it with dynamic extension points (initial solution search, cutting planes, primal heuristic, node selection, branching, relaxation) registered via Python entry points (§5.1, p. 11). It reuses the ecosystem: Pyomo for models, SUSPECT for FBBT and convexity/monotonicity detection, Coramin for OBBT and relaxations (§2, pp. 3-5). Structure use: convex quadratic parts are found as connected components of the variable graph and disaggregated so outer-approximation cuts can be added (p. 13). Included cuts: outer approximation, triangle, and SDP cuts selected by neural networks (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions**: all variables bounded; without deducible bounds "GALINI may not converge", and the base code imposes no artificial bounds (pp. 1-2, 4); maximisation handled by negation (p. 2); OBBT only at the root, FBBT at each node and skipped on expressions with more than 100 terms (p. 4); branching on the variable with highest nonlinear infeasibility, at λ = 0.25 between midpoint and relaxation solution (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a for proofs. Design arguments only: FBBT capped at 10 iterations "since FBBT converges only in the limit" (p. 12); one convergence tolerance used across B&B, LP, MIP and NLP (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: 761 MIQCQP instances from MINLPLib 2 and 453 from QPLIB, 300 s limit, gap 10⁻⁶, runs over the limit by more than 5% counted as unsolved; GALINI 1.1.0 against Gurobi 9.1 beta and ANTIGONE, BARON, Couenne, SCIP from GAMS 31.1; ablation over every single cut class, every pair, and all three; time and remaining-gap profiles (§7, pp. 16-18). No independent check of returned solutions is described in §7.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a solver toolkit, not a fast solver: GALINI "spends more time solving problems" while its 5-minute gap is comparable, which is expected "since GALINI is built to be easy to extend and does not focus on performance" (pp. 16-17). Future work: specialised cuts for separable nonconvex QPs and pooling (pp. 15, 17). Writing slip: the text refers to Figures 8 and 9 for MINLPLib results, while the captions number them Figures 6 and 7 (pp. 16-18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): SUSPECT special-structure detection (convexity, monotonicity) runs at every node and feeds the relaxation; its cost is measured as negligible against FBBT (p. 4); convex quadratic components are detected by graph connectivity and exploited for OA cuts (p. 13).
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): cut classes are judged inside the full branch-and-cut on the whole MINLPLib 2 set with every single class, every pair and all three (p. 16), matching Method 4 step 2; the recommendation to activate all cut generators follows that evidence (p. 16).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): competitors at stated versions including a prerelease Gurobi, two public libraries, a time-overshoot rule, time and remaining-gap profiles, and the loss written in (pp. 16-17). Missing against Method 5: no feasibility re-check of returned points is described (§7 read in full, pp. 16-17).
- Method 3 (variant ⚠): the "one interface, switchable components" design of Method 3 step 7 appears here for solver components (cuts, heuristics, branching) rather than for trained models (pp. 3, 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ablate solver components combinatorially: each class alone, every pair, all together, on the full library, reported as time and remaining-gap profiles (p. 16).
- Write the bound-honesty rule into the solver paper: say what happens without variable bounds and that a plug-in imposing artificial bounds may return a feasible but not globally optimal point (pp. 1-2).
- Debugging affordances for an extensible solver: a "paranoid mode" that checks every step and drops into a console at the failing function, tensor logging to HDF5, and a dashboard of bound evolution and the B&B tree (§6, pp. 15-16).

**Quotes**:
- "Time spent computing special structure is negligible compared to FBBT, so GALINI recomputes special structure at every node without any code optimization." (p. 4)
- "We can see that activating all cuts generators is the best choice for perforamnce since it has no performance impact." (p. 16; "perforamnce" [sic])

### S099 · Design of Experiments for Verifying Biomolecular Networks (NeurIPS 2020 Machine Learning for Molecules workshop, arXiv:2011.10575)

**Read level**: full (pp. 1-8, including Appendices A-B captions).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ruby Sedgwick (first author), John Goertz, Molly Stevens, Mark van der Wilk (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: designed biomolecular networks (logic-gate responses) must be checked against experiments, which are costly; previous validation used a fixed, predetermined grid of inputs (p. 1). The question shifts from optimising a response to deciding, with few experiments, whether to accept or reject a design (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: model the discrepancy between experimental data and the mechanistic model with a GP (Kennedy-O'Hagan decomposition, p. 2), pick the next experiment with a standard BO criterion, and stop with a new criterion: draw GP posterior samples, compute the RMSE of each over a uniform grid, and accept or reject when p(M_true < T_avg) crosses p_accept or p_reject (§2.2, pp. 2-3). The stopping rule is stated as the main contribution (p. 1).
- **D3 Assumptions**: simulated, noiseless data (noise variance fixed at 1e-4) "to better understand how the algorithm is working" (p. 2); squared exponential kernel with gamma priors and MAP hyperparameters; MAP's over-confidence on small samples handled by a minimum number of experiments n_min (pp. 2-3); T_avg = 0.2 set by a domain expert (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no proofs).
- **D5 Experiments**: four BO criteria against grid, random and Sobol on 8 test surfaces (none dominates; Appendix A, pp. 3, 7); 12 simulated networks, 10 runs each, sensitivity/specificity over n_min and certainty levels; n_min = 8 chosen as the knee; one of 120 runs ends inconclusive because its true RMSE is near the threshold, and this is shown in a figure (pp. 3-4).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a workshop-level method with specific next steps: Bayesian-quadrature acquisition functions, and removing n_min by integrating over GP hyperparameters (p. 4). The design criterion is conceded to be standard and improvable (p. 1).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (evidence ✅): a parameter (n_min) is introduced, swept on a grid with sensitivity/specificity plots, set at a knee value, and a plan to delete it is written into the conclusion (pp. 3-4), matching Method 2 steps 4, 5 and 7.
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): the inconclusive case is reported and explained rather than dropped (p. 4), as in Method 5 step 8; but the test bed is simulated and noiseless, and the BO criteria were run 20 times against 100 for the baselines "Due to time constraints" (p. 7).
- Heuristic 7 (evidence; not a Method): the claimed scope is stated as any setting where "a designed response surface matches experimental reality" must be tested (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn "how many experiments are enough?" into a probabilistic stopping test: sample the surrogate posterior, compute the decision metric per sample, and stop when the probability of meeting the expert threshold crosses accept or reject levels (pp. 2-3).
- Apply the stopping rule retrospectively to recorded runs so every stopping-parameter setting is compared on the same data (p. 3).
- Report the run that never terminated and show why (true value near the threshold) (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "We use standard Bayesian optimization design criteria to select the next experimental point, although this element of our method could be improved." (p. 1)
- "We also aim to eliminate the need for the minimum number of experiments parameter by integrating over the Gaussian process hyperparameters." (p. 4)
