### S042 · A systematic framework for the design, simulation and optimization of personalized healthcare: making and healing blood (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2015, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2015.03.008)

**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-14 main text read in full; Appendix 1 on robust optimisation of the bioreactor, pp. 28-31, read; reference list pp. 14-18, figure pages pp. 19-27 and Appendices 2-3 (PK/PD equations, GSA), pp. 33-38, not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Fuentes-Garí, E. Velliou, E. Pefani, M. Rende, N. Panoskaltsis, A. Mantalaris, E. N. Pistikopoulos (corresponding) (p. 1); Misener is 3rd of 8, Department of Computing (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a consortium review that cites her own bioreactor MINLP work (Misener et al. 2014a, 2014b) as the in silico optimisation component (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Personalised medicine needs in vitro systems that reproduce in vivo behaviour, validated predictive models, and models that identify targets (p. 3); ex vivo blood costs $8330 per unit against $225.42 for a typical transfused unit (p. 5), so the entry point for optimisation is cost.
- **D2 Core idea**: A framework of three environments (in vitro, in silico, in vivo) and two system types: type 1 = in vitro/in silico laboratory systems, type 2 = in vivo/in silico patient systems (p. 4). Misener's part is the type 1 example: a superstructure model of the hollow-fibre bioreactor that chooses design (aspect ratio, number of hollow fibres) and operation (flow rate, inlet composition, oxygen) to minimise the cost of one unit of red blood cells, subject to a minimum yield and a residual stem-cell constraint, "implemented in GAMS 24.1 and solved using the MINLP solver ANTIGONE 1.1" (p. 7). Appendix 1 gives the formulation: integer numbers of polymeric and ceramic hollow fibres in {1,...,20}, continuous length and radius, a five-period cell model (pp. 28-31).
- **D3 Model and assumptions**: Uncertain parameters listed explicitly (amplification parameters, diffusivities, porosities, death rates, cellular flux across ceramic fibres with ± range, p. 30); sensitivity analysis selects which ones matter, the most influential being the cellular flux across ceramic hollow fibres (p. 28); robust optimisation takes the worst-case realisation (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis device**: Sensitivity analysis → robust optimisation → "multiple runs of the same optimization model to explore alternative scenarios" (p. 7). The result is split into design decisions that hold across parameter and model changes (long, thin, packed with fibres, high flow rate, each with a physical reason, p. 8) and decisions that are sensitive (the minimum number of polymeric fibres, a trade-off between reliability and harvest, p. 8).
  - Transferable: after solving a design MINLP under uncertainty, report two lists: decisions that remain the same across parameter and model changes (with the physical reason for each) and decisions that move, with the trade-off that moves them (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**: none of its own for the optimisation part (cites Misener et al. 2014b for cost competitiveness with rare-blood transfusion, p. 7); in vitro K-562 culture results are summarised for the leukaemia part (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution is integrative (a framework and two worked domains, pp. 13-14). Future work is stated generically: more detailed grey- or white-box models from biomarker data (p. 14); for anaesthesia, the on-line optimisation and control problem "remains to be addressed" (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (the partner's model is the problem): variant ⚠. The partner's model here is a mechanistic bioreactor model, not a trained ML model; Misener's role is to write it as a MINLP and solve it with her own global solver (ANTIGONE 1.1, p. 7), with design rules extracted for the experimentalists (p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate: "robust vs sensitive decisions": after robust optimisation, separate design decisions that are invariant to parameter/model changes from those that are not, and explain each (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split the output of a design optimisation under uncertainty into invariant and sensitive decisions, and give the physical cause of each invariant (p. 8).
- In a framework paper, put the full optimisation model (objective, constraints, parameter table with uncertain ranges, decision-variable bounds) in an appendix so the main text can stay qualitative (pp. 28-31).

**Quotes**:
- "The model is implemented in GAMS 24.1 and solved using the MINLP solver ANTIGONE 1.1 (Misener and Floudas, 2013; 2014)." (p. 7)
- "Robust optimization helps quantify these tradeoffs (Appendix 1)." (p. 8)

### S043 · A mathematical model of subpopulation kinetics for the deconvolution of leukaemia heterogeneity (Journal of the Royal Society Interface 2015, doi:10.1098/rsif.2015.0276)

**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-7 and 9-13 read: abstract, introduction, model, experimental validation, blinded co-culture tests, discussion, computational tools; p. 8 conservation/initialisation equations skimmed; p. 15 acknowledgements and author contributions read; remaining methods pp. 13-14, references pp. 15-19 and figures pp. 20-25 not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Fuentes-Garí (first), D. García-Münzer, E. Velliou, M. C. Georgiadis, M. Kostoglou, E. N. Pistikopoulos, N. Panoskaltsis, A. Mantalaris (corresponding) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Misener 2nd of 9; per the contributions statement she "carried out the experiments and participated in the model development" (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: AML heterogeneity drives treatment response; cell-cycle-specific chemotherapy needs cell-cycle signatures (p. 3). Existing population balance models (PBMs) use state variables that cannot be measured (age) or are too noisy (size, volume), so they "cannot inform experiments" (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: A three-compartment PBM whose state variables are measurable: cyclin E for G0/G1, DNA for S, cyclin B for G2/M (p. 4), discretised by an upwind scheme into ODEs (p. 6), so that model bins can be compared with flow-cytometry geometric means (pp. 6-7).
- **D3 Model and assumptions**: Aggregated G0/G1 (leukaemic lines are highly proliferative) and aggregated G2/M (M is short) (p. 5); normal-CDF transition probabilities, justified by a cited study showing the transition function matters little (p. 5); maximum cyclin set where 99.99% of cells have transitioned (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis device**: Numerical conservation check (duplication factor set to one, maximum cell loss 1.2·10^-5 %, within solver tolerance, p. 7); Sobol GSA on 24 parameters decides which parameters must be measured and which can stay nominal (pp. 8-9, 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: EdU-labelled chase experiments on K-562, MEC-1, MOLT-4 (p. 9); co-culture fit on 50% mix, validation on 10% and 90% (p. 9); nine blinded mixtures (operator 1 prepares, operator 2 blinded), forward/backward prediction and scenario ranking; failures reported (T4, T5 not identified, earlier composition found in 2 of 3 cases) (pp. 9-10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Limits named concretely: no nutrient depletion in the model, cell-line density limits explain over-prediction at late points (p. 10); the three cell lines have close cycle times, primary cells may differ more (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- new-pattern candidate: "blinded-operator validation": one person prepares unknown mixtures, a blinded second person runs the model, then the panel is revealed and the model's ranking scored, with misses listed (pp. 9-10). The contributions statement credits the idea of a co-culture blinded experiment to A. Mantalaris (p. 15), so this is consortium practice, not evidence of Misener's own method.
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): weak variant ⚠. The misses are written in (T4, T5; 2 of 3 backward predictions, p. 10), which matches step 8 (losses written in), but the comparison is model vs experiment, not solver vs solver.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use GSA before experiment design to decide which parameters must be measured accurately and which can be fixed at nominal values (pp. 8-9).
- Validate a predictive model with a blinded protocol: unknown mixtures prepared by one operator, model run by a blinded second operator, results scored by rank, failures counted (pp. 9-10).
- Choose state variables that are measurable so the model can be validated directly against the instrument's aggregate statistic (pp. 4, 7).

**Quotes**:
- "Operator 2, blinded to the nature of the samples, performed the analysis at time 0 and determined the initial percent of each cell type in order to run the model." (p. 9)
- "RM carried out the experiments and participated in the model development, as well as providing feedback throughout." (p. 15)

### S070 · Selecting a differential equation cell cycle model for simulating leukemia treatment (Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research 2015, doi:10.1021/acs.iecr.5b01150)

**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-7 and 11-30 read: abstract, introduction, ODE and DDE model definitions, PK/PD embedding, results and discussion, acknowledgement; pp. 8-10 (PBM definition and steady-state equations) and Supporting Information pp. 31-37 not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Fuentes-Garí (first), M. C. Georgiadis, M. Kostoglou, N. Panoskaltsis, A. Mantalaris and E. N. Pistikopoulos (both corresponding) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Misener 2nd of 7; companion to S043.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A PK/PD chemotherapy model (Pefani et al.) used a three-ODE cell cycle; GSA showed phase durations dominate outcomes, and the authors hypothesise "that the cell cycle model itself, in addition to its parameters, would have an impact on simulated chemotherapy response" (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Compare three model classes of increasing complexity (CCP-ODE, a new CCP-DDE with phase-duration delays, CCP-PBM) on (i) fit to chemotherapy-free data and (ii) treatment outcome when embedded in the same PK/PD model (pp. 4-5). The question is stated as "are the models under study suitable for the application envisioned?" (p. 14).
- **D3 Model and assumptions**: The DDE is not closed, transient total-cell variations below 3% (p. 7); oscillation tolerance ε = 1% because experimental error exceeds 1% (p. 18); oscillatory behaviour needs three degrees of freedom per phase, which only the PBM has (pp. 18-19, 23).
- **D4 Analysis device**: Degree-of-freedom counting (ODE/DDE parameters already used up by transient fit, p. 19); linear stability analysis of the ODE (p. 15); Sobol GSA short-term and over 500 h (pp. 14, 19-20); a parameter-conversion procedure that equalises growth rate and steady-state phase fractions across models so the comparison is fair (pp. 23-24, Table 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: Fits of three cell lines (RSS Table 3, phase times vs experiment Table 2, pp. 16-17); one example patient (80-year-old male, standard Ara-C/DNR protocol) simulated under all three models (p. 25); ODE kills least, PBM most, with 2-3 orders of magnitude between DDE and PBM totals (pp. 25-26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: "Cell cycle model selection has a critical impact on predicted chemotherapy response." (p. 29); selection criteria: measurable parameters, accuracy in both transient and steady state, complexity vs degrees of freedom (p. 29). Concedes the DDE "could benefit from adding phase duration variability" at the cost of an extra parameter per phase (pp. 26, 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (judge a component inside the whole): variant ⚠. The component (cell-cycle model) is judged by what it does inside the full PK/PD treatment model, not only by its isolated fit (abstract p. 2; pp. 24-26); the isolated-fit ranking (DDE fits as well as PBM, Table 3, p. 17) differs from the in-system verdict.
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): variant ⚠. Before comparing, the models are put on equal footing (same growth rate and steady-state distribution, pp. 23-24), the analogue of harmonised settings in step 2; a rival's possible improvement is conceded (pp. 26, 28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When comparing model classes inside a larger system, first convert parameters so all candidates share the same steady state (growth rate and distribution), then compare dynamics under the intervention (pp. 23-24).
- Count the degrees of freedom each model has against the number of observable features to be matched (time to steady state, steady-state value, amplitude per phase) before fitting (pp. 18-19).
- Set numerical tolerances by the experimental error (ε = 1% because the measurement cannot see below it, p. 18).

**Quotes**:
- "Based on this choice, and for CCP-PBM/CCP-DDE/CCP-ODE comparisons to be fair, the total kinetics (so that the cell number is equal) and the cell cycle distribution (so that the percentage of cells likely to be affected when chemotherapy starts) will have to be equal at steady state." (p. 24)
- "Cell cycle model selection has a critical impact on predicted chemotherapy response." (p. 29)

### S029 · Bayesian optimization with dimension scheduling: Application to biological systems (Computer Aided Chemical Engineering 2016, doi:10.1016/b978-0-444-63428-3.50180-6; read as arXiv:1511.05385 v1)

**Read level**: full (all 7 pages of the arXiv v1 preprint, dated 18 November 2015, arXiv p. 1; the published chapter's pagination not seen).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: D. Ulmasov (first), C. Baroukh, B. Chachuat, M. P. Deisenroth; Deisenroth and Misener co-corresponding (arXiv p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: her first BO paper in this corpus, before the tree-ensemble BO line of Method 3.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A broken assumption: BO "assumes that computation cost of BO is cheap, but experiments are time consuming" (abstract), whereas the microalgae simulation runs in a fraction of a second, so thousands of evaluations make GP cost dominant (arXiv pp. 1-2). Deterministic global optimisation is ruled out by size (arXiv p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Dimension Scheduling Algorithm: at each iteration sample a small subset Z of coordinates from a probability vector P (PCA eigenvalues recomputed every 50 iterations), optimise the acquisition function of a GP that sees only those coordinates, clamp the others at the incumbent, and add the new point only to that subset's GP (arXiv pp. 3-4, Alg. 2). Relative to additive BO (Kandasamy et al.) it drops the disjoint-decomposition assumption (arXiv p. 3).
- **D3 Model and assumptions**: GP with squared-exponential kernel, EI acquisition optimised with DIRECT (arXiv pp. 3-4); weighted least squares between model and data with weights equal to the mean of each measurement type (arXiv p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis device**: n/a (no proofs). The authors state the missing guarantee instead: no global convergence because DSA never uses all data (arXiv p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: 10 model variants (d = 10-12) × 4 runs × 500 iterations, same random initial data for BO and DSA; subset size 2 chosen from five averaged runs on M19 and then fixed for all; DSA lower in most cases but not uniformly (Table 1, arXiv pp. 4-5); about a fifth of BO's computation time (arXiv p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Limits stated concretely: no convergence guarantee; accuracy falls as d grows because data spread thinly over many GPs; number of GPs bounded only by the number of subsets, pruning by marginal likelihood suggested; parallelisation sketched as future work (arXiv pp. 6-7).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (parameterised family, one setting across a class): variant ⚠. The subset-size parameter is chosen on one model (M19) and held fixed across all ten variants (arXiv p. 5), consistent with "never re-tune per comparison" (step 5), but robustness across the class is not shown (one model's sweep only).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): partial ⚠. Losses are kept in the table (M19o, M29G and others where BO wins, Table 1, arXiv p. 4) and "not uniformly superior" is written (arXiv p. 5); but the additive-BO rival is dismissed with "we do not get good results using ABO" and no numbers (arXiv p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: "scope statement against her own core tool": the paper says outright that deterministic global optimisation is out of range for this problem size and picks BO for sensitivity information (arXiv p. 2); matches Heuristic 7 (do-not-use conditions) and the Workflow A checkpoint.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Enter a method paper through the method's cost assumption: show the application where the assumption is inverted (cheap evaluations, expensive optimiser) and redesign for that regime (arXiv pp. 1-2).
- Keep losses in the results table and say in words that the method is not uniformly better (Table 1, arXiv pp. 4-5).
- Write the guarantee you lose next to the speed-up you gain ("we cannot assert global algorithm convergence", arXiv p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Problems of this size are, practically speaking, out of the range of current deterministic global optimization technology, so we are left to consider heuristics and gradient-based approaches." (arXiv p. 2)
- "Since the DSA algorithm never uses all of the data, we cannot assert global algorithm convergence." (arXiv p. 6)

Note (D7, not a method link): the text refers to "Figure 4" three times (arXiv p. 5) while the preprint has Figures 1-3 (arXiv pp. 5-6); the subset-size evidence is in Figure 1.

### S065 · A personalized framework for dynamic modeling of disease trajectories in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (IEEE Transactions on Biomedical Engineering 2016, doi:10.1109/tbme.2016.2533658)

**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-7 main text read in full; Appendix D end (feasibility problem and adjustment loop), p. 12, and Appendix H (multistart), pp. 16-17, read; Appendices A-C, E-G detail pp. 9-15 and references pp. 7-9 not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. Savvopoulos (first), N. Panoskaltsis, E. N. Pistikopoulos, A. Mantalaris (p. 1); Misener 2nd of 5, contact author with Panoskaltsis (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: same consortium; the parameter algorithm is written in GAMS (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: CLL accumulates in four compartments (bone marrow, blood, lymph nodes, spleen) with heterogeneous presentation; earlier models are one- or two-compartment (pp. 1, 5-7). The paper claims the first physiologically-relevant multi-compartment, migration-linked model (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three Mackey-type G0 delay-differential cell-cycle models (BM, PB, LN with spleen aggregated) linked by migration rates for proliferating and non-proliferating cells, with a carrying capacity in bone marrow (pp. 2-3). Parameters are split into assumed, measured (from routine clinical tests, Table 3) and calculated; the calculated ones come from an algorithm (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and assumptions**: Index-1 system, 6 differential and 23 algebraic equations, 76 parameters (22 assumed, 38 calculated, 10 measured, 6 initial conditions) (p. 3); explicit assumptions (a)-(f) on stable normal lymphocyte counts, blood volume, maximum recruitment rate, stage durations (p. 4); 90% of lymphocytes in affected tissue taken as CLL cells (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis device**: Parameter calculation as a GAMS feasibility problem seeded with initial and final cell numbers, then MATLAB DDE simulation and a rule-based adjustment loop with 15%/20% (then 10%) error tolerances (p. 12); multistart (50 initialisations, 4 and 3 distinct feasible points for P1, P2) showing trajectories within 10% (pp. 16-17); RS-HDMR global sensitivity analysis, 5000 samples (pp. 3, 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: Five hypothetical patients P1-P5 spanning Binet stage transitions (pp. 4-7, Tables 4-6); no real patient data; 0.1-0.2 CPU s per simulation (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Limits stated: hypothetical test cases only, validation with primary patient data needed (pp. 1, 7); Binet C approximated by bone-marrow burden instead of anaemia/platelets (p. 5); convergence of the algorithm to one point not proved (p. 16). Future direction: migration rate and proliferation induction as new clinical parameters (pp. 5, 7).

**Method links**:
- new-pattern candidate: "reasonable, not optimal": when the goal is to capture dynamics, pose parameter calculation as a feasibility problem, say so, and check non-uniqueness with a multistart rather than claim an estimate (pp. 3, 12, 16). Related to Heuristic 4 (prove what you can, report the rest as computational experience).
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): weak variant ⚠. The non-uniqueness of the feasibility problem is tested and reported (Appendix H, pp. 16-17) rather than hidden.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When data cannot identify parameters, solve for any parameters satisfying the observed constraints, state that they are "reasonable rather than optimal", and multistart the feasibility problem to show how much the conclusions move (pp. 3, 12, 16).
- Classify every model parameter as assumed, measured (with the clinical test that gives it) or calculated, in a table (p. 3, Table 3).
- Use global sensitivity analysis to propose which unmeasured quantities should become clinical measurements (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "We seek reasonable parameters rather than guaranteed parameter estimates of the best possible values; our purpose is to capture disease dynamics." (p. 3)
- "We cannot prove that the algorithm will always converge to the same point, but this section demonstrates our computational experience that results of the Appendix D algorithm give answers within 10% of one another." (p. 16)

### S053 · A quantitative three-dimensional image analysis tool for maximal acquisition of spatial heterogeneity data (Tissue Engineering Part C: Methods 2017, doi:10.1089/ten.tec.2016.0413)

**Read level**: full (main text pp. 1-22 read in full; references pp. 23-25 and the start of the Supplementary Information p. 26 glanced at; SI pp. 27-30 not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. C. Allenby (first), N. Panoskaltsis, A. Mantalaris (corresponding) (pp. 1-2); Misener 2nd of 4, Department of Computing (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the most computational of her biomedical co-authorships; Monte Carlo geometry, not optimisation.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: 3D tissue imaging is expensive and destructive, yet current analyses only use cells far from the image boundary, discarding nearly half of 3D data (pp. 4, 18).
- **D2 Core idea**: Compute cell density in concentric shells around a point or cell even where shells leave the image: analytic area corrections in 2D (subtract and add back extraneous segments, p. 9) and Monte Carlo volume estimation in 3D (p. 13), with sampling restricted to a small cuboid around each cell, which cut computation from 9.1 minutes to 22 seconds (pp. 13-14).
- **D3 Model and assumptions**: Estimates depend on user-defined inputs (bin width, number of sample points), stated up front (p. 5); 10x lens at about 3 µm per pixel as the minimum magnification for single-cell recognition (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis device**: Validate the estimator against known answers: Monte Carlo error measured against the analytic 2D solution over 4 geometries × 40 intervals, choosing a 10^-2 % convergence criterion with error below 5% (pp. 9-11); synthetic uniform cell sets of known density (2000 cells) recovered (p. 16); with/without-void comparison on the same image (pp. 16-17).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two stained bioreactor sections; 1116 cells used instead of 524 in the "conservative comparison" with current methods, 26% and 23% changes in density estimates (p. 15); manual scoring comparison (pp. 15-16, SI).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Claims 210% more cells and 23-fold longer distances (p. 3); trade-off between partition fineness and boundary error stated with numbers (4% vs 0.015% standard error, p. 18); future use on MRI/CT and complex regions of interest (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 step 5 (validate a detector like an instrument against an oracle): variant ⚠. The estimator is validated against analytic 2D solutions and synthetic data of known density before use on real images (pp. 9-11, 16), the same move as validating SUSPECT against a labelled library, here for a geometric estimator.
- Method 5 (benchmark like an auditor): variant ⚠. The comparison with current methods is labelled "conservative" (p. 15) and accuracy targets are set against controls (pp. 5, 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Validate a Monte Carlo estimator first on the cases with a closed-form answer (2D), pick the convergence criterion from the error-vs-time curve, then use it on the cases without one (3D) (pp. 9-11).
- Check an analysis pipeline on synthetic data of known density shaped like the real data (same cell count and image geometry) (p. 16).
- Restrict sampling to a bounding box of known volume around each query to cut Monte Carlo cost (pp. 13-14).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, results must be validated against controls, such as" (p. 5)
- "utilizing, for the first time, the entire 3D image captured." (p. 3)
