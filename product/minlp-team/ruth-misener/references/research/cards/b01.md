- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The pooling problem is entered from applications (petroleum refining, wastewater treatment, supply chains, communications; abstract, p. 97) and from regulation: the extended pooling problem appends the EPA Title 40 CFR Part 80.45 Complex Emissions Model, a model of VOC, NOX and toxic emissions as a function of eleven fuel qualities, to the standard pooling constraints; the RFG programme "impacts roughly 75 million people" (pp. 97-98). The generalized pooling problem makes the network topology itself a decision variable, which turns a nonconvex QCQP into a nonconvex MINLP with quadratic equalities (p. 98). The framing sentence is that resource allocation in process synthesis "is best approached using recent theoretical advances in global optimization" (p. 97). Stated challenge: rigorous global methods for problems at large scale (p. 98).
  - Transferable: take a well-studied benchmark class (standard pooling) and make it harder along two orthogonal real-world axes (a codified regulatory model in the constraints; topology as a decision), giving two papers from one backbone (pp. 97-98).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: No new relaxation is introduced here; the contribution is assembling known components into one branch and bound and pushing it to large instances. For the extended problem: a linear relaxation of the MINLP built from piecewise-linear relaxations of bilinear terms and edge-concave relaxations, integrated into B&B (p. 98). For the generalized problem: an alternative formulation (not stated in the abstract, deferred to ref. [15]) plus piecewise-linear underestimators inside B&B, after exploring McCormick envelopes, RLT, piecewise-linear underestimators, branching strategies and bounds tightening (p. 98). What is kept: the Meyer-Floudas (2006) B&B with piecewise underestimators as the base algorithm (p. 98, refs. [12], [7]). Setting the piecewise partitioning level was instance-informed: the 15-plant case was solved "after some experience with the particular topology by predicting a good partitioning level" (p. 99).
  - Transferable: when the paper's lever is a combination of known relaxations, list every component explored (McCormick, RLT, piecewise, branching, bounds tightening) so the reader sees the design space, even if the final choice is reported elsewhere (p. 98).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Bilinear terms arise from tracking qualities "under the assumption of linear blending"; the standard pooling problem is called a "non-convexifiable" QCQP (p. 97). The EPA model brings multilinear, exponential and power-law terms on top of bilinear ones (p. 98); its MINLP representation follows Furman & Androulakis (ref. [6], p. 98). Generalized problem: binary activation of each pipe or intermediate node, linear mixing at pools, quadratic equalities and inequalities, multiple local optima (p. 98). Variable bounds and relaxation details are not stated in the abstract (not read in this text; deferred to refs. [14], [15]).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: n/a (no proofs or theoretical statements).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Extended problem: three case studies, sizes given as continuous / binary variables and bilinear-only / all nonlinear terms (Case 1: 214/30, 62/108; Case 2: 331/45, 111/180; Case 3: 1104/150, 410/640; Table 1, p. 98), all "solved ... to global optimality" (p. 98); times not reported in the abstract. Generalized problem: an instance ladder built on one wastewater superstructure (Meyer-Floudas test cases, up to twenty treatment options with all interconnections, p. 98; Figure 1, 10-plant superstructure with sources S1-S7 and plants T1-T10, p. 99): 4-plant (150 eqs, 63 cont., 55 bin., 48 bilinear) to 0.1% gap in 38.25 s; 10-plant (516, 207, 187, 300) in 680.66 s; 15-plant (986, 382, 352, 675) in 2489.76 s on a desktop or 784.81 s on an eight-threaded parallel processor; two 20-plant instances (1663, 634, 594, 1260) to within 0.9% and 2.3% of optimality (Table 2, pp. 98-99). Comparison to prior work: the best previous result reaches a 1.2% gap in 1561.6 s "when run on a modern hardware and software", against 0.1% in as few as 38.2 s, called a forty-fold time improvement with a gap more than an order of magnitude tighter (p. 98). The unsolved 20-plant instances are reported with their remaining gap rather than omitted (p. 99). No competitor solvers, no independent feasibility check, no repeated runs are mentioned in the abstract.
  - Transferable: grow one superstructure into an instance ladder (4/10/15/20 units) and tabulate equations, continuous, binary and bilinear counts per rung (Table 2, p. 99), so scale-up is read off one table.
  - Transferable: re-run the best previous method on current hardware and software before quoting its time, and compare on time and final gap jointly (p. 98).
  - Transferable: report serial and parallel wall-clock times side by side for the largest solved instance (p. 99).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds explicitly on the Floudas group line: Meyer & Floudas 2006 generalized pooling (B&B and test cases) and Meyer & Floudas 2005 edge-concave envelopes, and her own Gounaris-Misener-Floudas 2009 piecewise-linear comparison (pp. 98, 100). Credits Audet et al. (2004) for introducing the generalized pooling problem and their 2000 branch-and-cut (p. 98). Cites a broad set of contemporary piecewise-linear work by other groups: Bergamini-Grossmann et al. 2008, Hasan-Karimi 2010, Karuppiah-Grossmann 2006, Pham-Laird-El-Halwagi 2009, Saif et al. 2008, Wicaksono-Karimi 2008 (refs. [4, 7-9, 12, 16, 17, 24], pp. 98, 100). The prior-work comparison is direct and quantitative (1.2% in 1561.6 s vs 0.1% in 38.2 s, p. 98). The two other pooling papers in the same volume (Alfaki-Haugland, Frimannslund et al.; contents pp. 5-6) are not cited, nor is the Tawarmalani-Sahinidis pooling work beyond their book (ref. [22], p. 100).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Very compact: the abstract and §1 repeat the same two-item list ("(i) an extended pooling problem ... and (ii) a generalized pooling problem ...", p. 97), and §4 repeats it again (p. 99). Claims are carried by numbers (sizes, seconds, gaps) rather than by method description. One hedge on generality: experience with small instantiations "may lead to insight" for industrially-sized cases (p. 99). Minor inconsistencies: the abstract says "maximizing profit" while §1 says "minimize cost" (p. 97); duplicated citation markers ("[6] [6]", "[2] [2]", "[12] [12, 15]", p. 98) look like editing leftovers. Application stakes stated in societal terms (75 million people, Clean Air Act; p. 97).
  - Transferable: give hardness in a size table with term counts by type (bilinear only vs all nonlinear terms, Table 1, p. 98) instead of describing instances in words.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: application modelling plus computational (solving the EPA-extended pooling MINLP to global optimality, Table 1, p. 98; solving generalized pooling instances up to 15 plants to 0.1% gap, p. 99), resting on an algorithm assembled from existing relaxations (p. 98). Limits are given concretely: the two 20-plant instances end at 0.9% and 2.3% gaps (p. 99); the 15-plant solve needed topology-specific experience to choose the partitioning level (p. 99). Future direction is offered as a practitioner suggestion, not as an open research question: use small instantiations of a topology to gain insight for industrial sizes (p. 99). Closing claim: large-scale pooling problems "are best approached using rigorous global optimization methods" (p. 99).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build an instance ladder from one superstructure and tabulate equations, continuous, binary and bilinear counts per rung (Table 2, p. 99).
- Before claiming a speed-up, re-run the best previous method on current hardware and software and compare time and final gap together (p. 98).

- Tune a partitioning parameter on small instantiations of the same topology before attacking the industrial size, and say that you did (p. 99).
