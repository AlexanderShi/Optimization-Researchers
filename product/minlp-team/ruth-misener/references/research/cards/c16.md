### S046 · P-split formulations: a class of intermediate formulations between big-M and convex hull for disjunctive constraints (Mathematical Programming 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10107-025-02232-1; read as arXiv:2202.05198v3)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v3, file pp. 1-33: body pp. 1-26, references pp. 27-30, Appendix A pp. 31-33. Appendix A's full 1-, 2- and 4-split formulations, pp. 32-33, were skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jan Kronqvist (KTH, corresponding author, p. 1), Calvin Tsay (Imperial) · **Place in trajectory**: the journal extension of the CPAIOR 2021 paper [41] and companion to the NeurIPS 2021 ReLU partition paper [69]. It generalises from "a two-term disjunction with a single constraint per disjunct" to multiple constraints and nonconvex disjuncts and grows the test set "from 12 to 344 instances" (pp. 2-3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a known trade-off. Big-M is small but weak, and the convex hull is sharp but large and uses the perspective function, which "can result in numerically more challenging constraints" (p. 2). A folklore observation from Anderson et al. adds that "extended convex hull formulations tend to perform worse than expected" (p. 2). The paper says the best MIP encoding of a disjunction "is an open question" (p. 2). The aim is stated as different from exact-hull work: "the goal is not to obtain a formulation whose continuous relaxation forms the convex hull" but compact approximations of it (p. 2). The target structure is named from the start: "many variables in the constraints of each disjunct, but with only a few constraints per disjunct", as in clustering and trained NNs (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open by stating the two textbook extremes, a folklore observation that the "strong" one underperforms in practice, and the problem structure where the gap between them matters most.
- **D2 Core idea**: Split each additively separable constraint into P partial sums with auxiliary variables α (Eq. 2, p. 4). Relax the disjunction by making the x-constraints global, which linearises it (Eq. 5, p. 4; "effectively linearizes the disjunction", p. 5). Then take the extended convex hull of the linear disjunction over α (formulation (P-split), p. 5). What changes: the hull is taken in a P-dimensional projected space, so the number of auxiliary variables is independent of n (Remark 2: P·(|D|²+|D|) continuous and |D| binary, p. 5). What stays: the binary λ per disjunct and the extended-formulation machinery of Balas [8]. For multiple constraints per disjunct each constraint gets its own α block (P-split*, p. 10). Optional linking constraints over pairs of α's strengthen it (Eq. 33-34, p. 13). Nonconvex separable disjuncts are handled by taking convex envelopes of the partial sums (Eq. 37, p. 15).
  - Transferable: to approximate a convex hull cheaply, project the disjunct constraints onto a few aggregated "split" variables, linearise, and take the exact hull in that small space.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1 is convex additively separable g_k with nonempty disjuncts. Assumption 2 is boundedness over X. Assumption 3 is a minimal number of α variables. Assumption 4 is |C_l| << n (p. 3). They are "sufficient to apply a P-split formulation but not necessary". Assumption 4 "characterizes favorable problem structures", and Assumption 1 can be relaxed to partial separability (Definition 1, p. 3). The strict hierarchy needs additive bounds (Definition 3, p. 7), which is "not required by the P-split formulations" and holds for boxes or interval arithmetic (p. 7). Only globally valid bounds (Eq. 4) are used, "as these are in general computationally more tractable" (p. 4). Section 5 restricts X to a box B for nonconvex disjuncts (p. 14).
  - Transferable: separate the assumptions needed for correctness from those needed for an ordering theorem, and say which one is only a description of the good use case (as Assumption 4 is here).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Fourier-Motzkin elimination projects out ν and α to show that the 1-split equals big-M with the smallest valid M_l = ᾱ^l − b_l (Theorem 1, pp. 6-7; Theorem 4, p. 11). (ii) Hierarchy by constraint aggregation: relaxing a (P+1)-split by summing the two split blocks recovers the P-split, provided the bounds are additive (Theorem 2, pp. 7-8). Strictness comes from an example point (Corollary 1, p. 8). (iii) For two-term disjunctions, a non-extended realisation with one inequality per subset S_p ⊂ S, so exponentially many constraints in P, is obtained by repeated FM elimination ("Each elimination step doubles the number of constraints", Theorem 3, pp. 9-10). The paper notes it could serve as a cut family but cut selection is "not considered in this paper" (p. 10). (iv) An upper endpoint: for affine h and a box, the n-split gives the convex hull, via an invertible linear map α = Γx (Theorem 5, pp. 11-12). Corollary 3 then gives the full big-M → hull hierarchy (p. 12). (v) A ceiling theorem: for fully disjoint disjunctions with strictly convex functions, no P-split gives the hull. The proof takes the midpoint of two extreme points on a hull facet and uses Jensen's inequality to find a feasible ball around it (Theorem 6, pp. 12-13). (vi) A redundancy criterion for linking constraints via bound additivity (Proposition 2, p. 13), giving two selection rules. (vii) Lifting to nonconvex disjuncts by the separability of convex envelopes over a box (Horst-Tuy, Proposition 4, p. 15; Corollaries 4-5, p. 16). Size: the number of potential linking constraints "is independent of the number of variables" (p. 14).
  - Transferable: prove an interpolating family with three results, not one: the bottom endpoint by projection (FM elimination), monotonicity by aggregation (the coarser member is a relaxation of the finer), and both an attainment theorem (linear case) and an impossibility theorem (strictly convex case) for the top endpoint.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There are 344 instances. Table 1 lists 320: clustering (G2 and MNIST data, 6 classes × 15), P_ball (2 × 15), and optimal sparse input features of MNIST ReLU nets (4 classes × 50) (p. 20). The other 24 are semi-supervised clustering instances (8 types × 3, Table 2, p. 21). All formulations are written in gurobipy and solved by Gurobi 9 with MIPFocus=3, Cuts=1, MIQCPMethod=1 "To minimize the variations caused by heuristics" (p. 21). PreMIQCPForm=2 was given only to the convex hull because it "drastically improves" the hull but hurt the others (pp. 21-22). The time limit is 1800 s. Hardware is split: an i9 laptop for clustering and P_ball, i7 desktops for NN (p. 22). Metrics are average time and nodes over solved instances, or the average remaining gap if more than half time out (Table 3, p. 24). For NN, averages are over "problems solved by all formulations", with interval-arithmetic bounds and OBBT bounds compared and "The time to compute bounds by OBBT is not included" (Table 4, p. 25). Convex hull and n-split were dropped for NN as intractable (">5 minutes to get past the root node", p. 22). Big-M was dropped from semi-supervised clustering because "it was clear that it would time out" (p. 23). Results: big-M times out with 99-100% gap on every clustering and P_ball class, and intermediate splits beat both extremes (e.g. Cluster_g1 3.6 s at |I_s|=2 vs 61 s for the hull, p. 24). For NN, P = 2 or 4 is fastest and P = 50 slowest (p. 25). The hull times out from 4 constraints per disjunct (p. 26). On P: "For the different classes of problems, the optimal value of P varies" (p. 22), and "overall we obtain a good performance with both 2, 3, and 4 variables per partition" (p. 23). Locally valid α-bounds were deliberately left out of the comparison (p. 17).
  - Transferable: give the strong-but-heavy baseline its best solver setting even when that setting hurts your own method, write the asymmetry down, and leave out any strengthening (here local bounds) that only your method can use.
  - Transferable: when more than half of a class times out, report the average remaining gap instead of time or nodes, in the same table cell.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper places itself in the Balas → Grossmann disjunctive-programming lineage (p. 2). It separates itself from "Efficient convex hull formulations" work with "The motivation behind the P-split formulations is different" (p. 2). Related non-extended strengthening is acknowledged (improved big-M, hybrid formulations, Cayley embedding; Vielma's review [70], p. 2). Remark 1 ties the minimal-α rule to "the interval arithmetic and factorable programming literature [50, 74]", that is, her own ANTIGONE and SCIP (p. 4). The linking constraints are flagged as similar to RLT [51, 63] and to Belotti (2013) (p. 13). The NN results are aligned with "prior observations [2]" on hull intractability (p. 22). Prior work is restated, not attacked.
  - Transferable: position a formulation paper by its goal ("approximate the hull compactly"), not by beating a rival, so that exact-hull papers become neighbours rather than competitors.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is "The main idea is to capture the best of both ..." (abstract, p. 1), restated as "the best of both worlds" (p. 2). Each theory section opens with a picture: ex-1 with 1-, 2- and 4-split relaxations, where the figure itself shows the hierarchy failing under non-additive bounds (Fig. 1, pp. 6, 8), and ex-2 for linking constraints (Fig. 2, p. 14). Results that do not help are said plainly: the 1-split has "no advantage" (p. 7), and Theorem 5 is "merely of theoretical interest" (Remark 3, p. 12). Claims are hedged in the abstract and conclusion ("promising potential", p. 1; "great potential", p. 26). Minor slips: "Corallary" (p. 8), "disjoint" for "disjunct" (pp. 23, 26), and a Table 4 caption that mentions shading against "convex hull formulations" although Table 4 has no hull column (p. 25).
  - Transferable: when your ordering theorem needs an assumption, show a figure where that assumption fails and the ordering visibly breaks, then show the same example with the assumption restored (Appendix A, Fig. 6, p. 31).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: It contributes a formulation framework with theory (endpoints, hierarchy, ceiling) and a 344-instance computation, implemented as a Pyomo.GDP plugin (p. 3) and, for NNs, as a partitioning strategy in OMLT (p. 16). The limits are specific. P is "a parameter that can be selected to balance the tradeoff" with no selection rule (p. 26). Without Assumptions 1-4 there is no advantage (p. 26). Without Assumption 4 the formulations "are inadvisable" (p. 10). Cut selection for the non-extended form (p. 10), selection of linking constraints (p. 14) and practical local bounds via OBBT (p. 17) are left open.
  - Transferable: write the do-not-use regime as a structural condition tied to a numbered assumption (|C_l| << n), not as a vague caveat.

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ evidence, the full text it lacked. Endpoints are proven (Theorems 1, 5, pp. 6-7, 11-12) and the hierarchy is proven (Theorem 2, Corollary 3, pp. 7-8, 12). The "order of magnitude" claim of step 6 is confirmed (abstract p. 1; conclusion p. 26). The family ships as switches (Pyomo.GDP p. 3; OMLT p. 16). Method 2 ⚠ variant on step 5: this paper has no random-partition or deliberately weak control. Partitioning was by ordered indices, with "initial tests indeed showed little variation" (p. 22) but no table, and the best P varies by class (p. 22). The "partly kept" verdict on the parameter rule stands. Method 5 ✅ on strengthening the baseline (PreMIQCPForm=2 for the hull only, pp. 21-22) and on refusing an unfair advantage (no local bounds, p. 17). Method 5 ⚠ variant: one solver (Gurobi 9), two different machines (p. 22), and OBBT time excluded (p. 25), the same lapse SKILL.md records for NeurIPS 2021. Heuristic 3 ✅: the ℓ1 budget was set "mainly to keep the problems solvable within a reasonable time limit" (p. 19). Heuristic 7 ✅: do-not-use conditions are stated (pp. 10, 26). New-pattern candidate: "ceiling theorem for one's own family" (Theorem 6, pp. 12-13).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove the bottom endpoint of a formulation family by Fourier-Motzkin projection onto the original space (Theorem 1, pp. 6-7).
- Prove monotonicity in the family parameter by showing that the coarser member is the finer one with two constraint blocks aggregated (Theorem 2, pp. 7-8).
- State and prove where the family cannot reach the ideal (Theorem 6: fully disjoint + strictly convex ⇒ never the hull, pp. 12-13), so readers do not expect P = n to equal the convex hull.
- Use a bound-additivity test to discard redundant pairwise linking constraints before adding them (Proposition 2, p. 13).
- Give the competitor's formulation its own best solver flag and document it (pp. 21-22).
**Quotes**:
- "Our numerical comparisons do not include locally valid bounds on the α-variables, as such bounds are typically not easily obtainable and could give the P-split formulations an unfair advantage." (p. 17)
- "Without this structure, the P-split formulations do not necessarily have an advantage over either the big-M or convex hull formulation." (p. 26)

### S128 · Beyond adjacency: Graph encoding with reachability and shortest paths (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2509.20247)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-24: body pp. 1-17, references pp. 17-19, Appendices A-B worked indexing examples pp. 20-24. The appendices were read; the Appendix B figures are not in the text layer).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shiqiang Zhang (Imperial, BASF-funded PhD, p. 17); Misener is corresponding author (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a two-author paper that says it aims to "distill the fundamental contributions of our recent works [45, 46, 47, 48]" (BoGrape S067, its workshop version, NAS graph BO, and NeurIPS 2023 GNN symmetry breaking) "and extend them from specific applications to general settings" (p. 2). The acknowledgements add that she is an Amazon Scholar and that the work predates that role (p. 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Graph optimisation (molecular design, NAS, topology) is mostly done by sample-then-evaluate heuristics or by generative models over a latent space. The paper names three limitations: feasibility "learned from data and checked later", optimality "not guaranteed", and methods "highly problem-specific and mostly data-driven" (p. 1). The response is to "take a step back and rethink how to optimize over graph spaces using discrete optimization" (p. 1). The technical gap: the adjacency matrix alone "has no extra graph information, e.g., connectivity, reachability, and shortest distance", and encoding these for any graph in the space is "significantly more challenging" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when ML methods learn feasibility from data, ask whether the feasible set can be written exactly as constraints, so that feasibility is guaranteed rather than learned.
- **D2 Core idea**: An IP over binary adjacency A, reachability r, integer distance d (with n standing for ∞), and on-path indicators δ^w_{u,v} (Table 2, p. 3). Eight necessary conditions C1-C8 are written in logic and then linearised with explicit big-M values (pp. 3-4) to give Eq. (1) (p. 4). Graph classes are obtained by adding linear constraints without new variables: undirected (3), connected/strongly connected (4), DAG (5), st-DAG (6), and weakly connected via a second copy on the underlying graph linked by (7) (p. 6). Table 1 maps each class to its encoding and OEIS sequence (p. 2). Under strong connectivity, a BFS-like single-source encoding (d_v, γ_{u,v}, Eq. 8) cuts the size from O(n³) to O(n²) "at the cost of losing control of the shortest paths between arbitrary pair of nodes" (pp. 2, 7-8). Strong connectivity is then the intersection of the encoding on G and on its transpose (Lemma 2, Eq. 10, p. 9). For symmetry, lexicographic constraints over neighbour sets (Eq. 14, linearised with "universal ordering vector" coefficients 2^{n−u−1}, Eq. 15, p. 11) replace the earlier work's need for an indexed node (p. 2). For DAGs, stronger constraints over successor sets via r (Eq. 21-22, p. 14).
  - Transferable: encode derived graph properties (reachability, distances, on-path indicators) as auxiliary variables tied to adjacency, then restrict to subclasses by adding constraints instead of writing a new model for each.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Nodes are labelled for the encoding sections, to avoid isomorphism (p. 2). Node counts lie in [n0, n], with existing nodes forced to have the smallest indices (Eq. 1a, p. 4). The symmetry analysis assumes all graphs have exactly n nodes and are connected or weakly connected (pp. 10, 14). All big-M coefficients are explicit (n, n−1, n−2, n+1, 2n; pp. 3-4). Remark 1 states that only A_{u,v} = A_{v,u} is needed for undirected graphs, and the other symmetric equalities "are introduced to tighten the continuous relaxation" (p. 6). The authors concede that the 2^{n−u−1} coefficients "might cause numerical instabilities" as graphs grow (p. 16).
  - Transferable: when you add a constraint that is logically redundant, label it as relaxation-tightening (Remark 1, p. 6) so readers can tell correctness constraints from strength constraints.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Necessity first, sufficiency second. Lemma 1 ("easy to check", p. 4) shows every graph gives a feasible point. Theorem 1 (bijection) proves injectivity by induction on min(d_{u,v}(G), d_{u,v}), with Case I (true distance = sd+1) and Case II (variable distance = sd+1), each closed by contradiction for the δ = 0 sub-case (pp. 4-6). (ii) The same induction for the single-source encoding (Theorem 2, pp. 8-9). (iii) A product construction via transpose for strong connectivity (Lemma 2, p. 9). (iv) For symmetry breaking, "does not remove a whole isomorphism class" is proved constructively. Algorithm 1 builds an indexing with temporary indices and ranks (p. 11). Propositions 1-3 (prefix monotonicity of LO under truncation) and Lemma 3 (final order implies temporary order) support it (pp. 11-12). Theorem 3 is proved by contradiction at the minimal violating index (pp. 12-13). Lemma 4 shows that Eq. (14) implies the older connectivity-ordering constraint (11) (pp. 13-14). (v) For DAGs, a reverse-topological Algorithm 2, Proposition 5 (topological order) and Proposition 6 give Theorem 4 (pp. 14-15). (vi) An honest open end: Conjecture 1 (ancestor-set tie-breaking, Eq. 23-24) is "verified" only numerically for n ≤ 7, and "we have not figured out a general way to construct a feasible indexing" (pp. 15-16).
  - Transferable: to prove that symmetry-breaking constraints keep at least one representative of every orbit, give an explicit indexing algorithm and prove that its output satisfies the constraints.
  - Transferable: prove an encoding exact by writing each constraint as a necessary condition, then proving the map from feasible points to graphs injective by induction on a natural graph quantity (here shortest distance).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There is no optimisation or timing experiment. The evaluation counts feasible solutions: Gurobi enumerates all solutions (PoolSearchMode=2, capped at 10^6 via PoolSolutions) and the count is compared with the OEIS counts for labelled graphs (no symmetry breaking) and unlabelled graphs (all symmetry broken) (p. 16; Table 4, p. 16). Examples at n = 7: connected undirected 1,866,256 labelled → 3,628 with Eq. (15) vs 853 unlabelled. Weakly connected DAGs 1,111,506,858 → 627,846 with Eq. (22) → 359,396 with (22)+(24) vs 237,317 unlabelled. For directed classes, Eq. (20) on the underlying graph removes far less (e.g. strongly connected n = 5: 565,080 → 84,481 vs 5,048) (p. 16). A worked example shows Eq. (14) reducing the indexings of one 6-node graph "from 720 to 4" (p. 11; Appendix A, pp. 20-23).
  - Transferable: evaluate symmetry-breaking constraints by exhaustive solution counting at small n, bracketed by the two known integer sequences (labelled = no breaking; unlabelled = perfect breaking). The gap to the unlabelled count measures the remaining symmetry exactly.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper is framed as consolidating her group's applied papers [45-48] (p. 2). It places itself in "the constraints-based symmetry-handling literature" (Margot, Liberti, Hojny, Kaibel-Pfetsch orbitopes), naming propagation during B&B [13, 36] as the alternative (p. 16). Eq. (14) is "a non-trivial generalization" of lexicographic column ordering for identical-machine assignment and orbitopes, "from a bipartite graph to an arbitrary undirected graph" (p. 16). McDonald et al.'s connectivity constraints are extended, not replaced (p. 10). ML graph generation is described neutrally, then its three limits are listed (p. 1).
  - Transferable: show that your constraint reduces to the known one on the special case (Algorithm 1 "will produce the same indexing documented in" the orbitope literature, p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The method sentence is "We first provide a list of necessary conditions ... Then we prove that these conditions are sufficient." (p. 2). Each condition is given in words, then in logic, then "could be rewritten as Eq. (...) with n as a big-M coefficient" (pp. 3-4). A design-principle paragraph sits before Lemma 1: insufficient constraints leave symmetric solutions, excessive ones cut feasible solutions (p. 4). Hedging is low. The paper has visible copy-editing slips: "are are" and "achieves" (p. 1); Seq(L, M) vs Seq(M, L) (p. 10); Eq. (11) printed with "> 1" where the words say "at least one" neighbour (p. 10); a reference to "Eq. (22)" where Eq. (21) seems meant (p. 14).
  - Transferable: put the encoding's catalogue up front as a table (graph class → constraint set → OEIS id, Table 1, p. 2) so readers can assemble the model they need without reading the proofs.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical and formulational: a general encoding, a simplified encoding, symmetry-breaking constraints with completeness proofs, and code at cog-imperial/graph_encoding (p. 2). Stated limits: the numerical instability of power-of-two coefficients (p. 16), Conjecture 1 unproven (p. 16), and experiments "in small scales (where we can exhaust all feasible graphs)" (p. 17). Future work: more graph properties, alternative implementations of lexicographic constraints, and more symmetry breaking (p. 17).
  - Transferable: after a run of application papers, write the "distillation" paper that states the general encoding with proofs and releases it as a separate repository.

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence, extending the symmetry-breaking practice (arXiv:2305.09420, cited here as [48]) from GNN inputs to general and DAG graph spaces, with the detector's completeness proved (Theorems 3-4, pp. 11-15) and its strength measured against exact counts (Table 4, p. 16). This is close to step 5, "validate the detector like an instrument", with OEIS as the labelled oracle. Method 3 ⚠ variant: the "trained model as constraints" step is pushed down a layer. What is encoded is the input domain (graphs with shortest paths) that GNN, kernel and molecular models need (pp. 1-2), and the paper generalises behind one encoding as step 7 does. Method 5: n/a (counting only, no solver comparison). New-pattern candidates: "exact-encoding bijection theorem" and "distillation paper after an application run".
**Transferable techniques**:
- Necessary conditions → linearisation with named big-M → bijection by induction on shortest distance (pp. 2-6).
- O(n³) → O(n²) by keeping only single-source distances when strong connectivity is assumed, and a transpose copy for the reverse direction (pp. 7-9).
- Lexicographic order over neighbour or successor sets, linearised with 2^{n−u−1} weights, plus a constructive indexing algorithm as the completeness proof (pp. 10-15).
- Solution counting with PoolSearchMode=2 against OEIS labelled and unlabelled counts (p. 16).
**Quotes**:
- "This paper aims to distill the fundamental contributions of our recent works [45, 46, 47, 48] and extend them from specific applications to general settings." (p. 2)
- "There are often multiple ways to encode a combinatorial problem, but insufficient constraints result in an unnecessarily large search space with symmetric solutions, while excessive constraints may cutoff feasible solutions from the search space." (p. 4)

### S067 · BoGrape: Bayesian optimization over graphs with shortest-path encoded (ICLR 2026, arXiv:2503.05642)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v2, file pp. 1-30: body pp. 1-10, references pp. 11-14, Appendices A-C pp. 15-30. Tables 3-4 were read; Figures 2-11 exist only as captions in the text layer).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yilin Xie and Shiqiang Zhang (equal contribution), Jixiang Qing, with Misener and Calvin Tsay as equal-contribution corresponding authors (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the trees → ENTMOOT → OMLT → tree-kernel BO line moved to graph domains. It builds on PK-MIQP (Xie et al. 2024, global acquisition optimisation, p. 3) and on her group's GNN and molecular MIP work (Zhang et al. 2023/2024, Limeade 2025, pp. 2, 10). S128 later generalises its shortest-path encoding.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: There are two kinds of graph BO, over nodes of a fixed graph or over graphs. The second is the target and is "usually more challenging" (p. 1). Existing graph BO limits the search space to a fixed graph, directed labelled or unlabelled graphs, or task-specific metrics (p. 2). Its acquisition step uses evolution or sampling, which cannot explore effectively, embed constraints, or guarantee "optimality in terms of acquisition function, which is essential for optimization convergence" (p. 2). GNNs are ruled out as surrogates on data needs, MIP size and lack of uncertainty quantification (p. 2).
  - Transferable: enter an ML subfield through its inner optimisation step (here acquisition optimisation) and show the three things a MIP gives that samplers cannot: coverage, constraints, certificates.
- **D2 Core idea**: A GP with a shortest-path graph kernel, whose acquisition (LCB) is optimised globally as a MIP. There are four kernel variants: SP, an unlabelled SSP, and exponentials ESP/ESSP (pp. 4-5). Structure and features are separated as k = α·k_G + β·k_F, an idea borrowed from Cui & Yang (p. 4). The graph domain is encoded with A, d, δ (MIP-SP, pp. 5-6). The kernel is linear in indicator variables d^s_{u,v} weighted by precomputed path-length counts D_s(G_i) of each data graph (p. 6). The non-constant self-covariance K_xx is linearised by a second layer of count indicators D^c_s (p. 7). Exponential kernels are handled by Gurobi's dynamic outer approximation (p. 7). What changes from PK-MIQP: Eq. (1d) and (1e) become graph kernel and graph search space. What stays: the LCB-as-MIQP template (p. 3, Eq. 1). Appendix B.1 says the kernel was chosen because it can be encoded: "it is not that we chose shortest-path kernel first" (p. 20).
  - Transferable: to put a kernel inside a MIP, rewrite k(x, X_i) as a linear function of indicator variables of x, with coefficients precomputed from each fixed data point X_i (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Connected graphs, undirected in practice (p. 3); fixed size n, or bounded size [n0, n] via MIP-SP-plus (pp. 5-6, 16-18). Nodes have M binary features with the first L a one-hot label (p. 5). PD is shown for all four kernels (Lemmas 3.1-3.2, pp. 4-5). GP settings: hyperparameter bounds [0.01, 100], noise variance 10^{-6}, β_t^{1/2} = 1 (p. 23). Regret theory assumes a global acquisition optimum (p. 3), yet every MIP is capped at 600 s because "good solutions [are] found early" (p. 9). The paper reconciles this only empirically (B.5, p. 21).
  - Transferable: if your motivation is a global-optimality guarantee but you run with a time limit, report the MIP gap at that limit and a time-limit ablation, as B.5 does (p. 21).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: PD of SSP as a special case of SP; ESP/ESSP as exponentials of kernels with a linear form (pp. 4-5). Theorem 3.5 (bijection between MIP-SP and connected n-node graphs) is proved by induction on shortest distance sd with Parts 1-2 and contradiction sub-cases (pp. 18-19). Theorem 3.6 reduces to Theorem 3.5 by fixing the node count n1 and checking that the constraints on non-existent nodes are slack (p. 19). The quadratic K_xx is linearised exactly by count indicators (p. 7). For undirected graphs every D_s is even, so odd indicators are fixed to zero (p. 20).
