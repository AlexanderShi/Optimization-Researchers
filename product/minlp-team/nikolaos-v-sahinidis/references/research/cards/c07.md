### S128 · A reformulation-enumeration MINLP algorithm for gas network design (Journal of Global Optimization 2024, doi:10.1007/s10898-024-01424-x; arXiv:2307.07648)
**Read level**: full. Read the arXiv v3 text (30 Jan 2024, p. 1): pp. 1-30 in full, including Appendix A (proof of Theorem 1, pp. 27-28) and Appendix B (MISOC relaxation, pp. 29-30); references pp. 30-32 skimmed. Page numbers are arXiv v3 pages, not JOGO pages.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yijiang Li (first author, Georgia Tech ISyE, p. 1; a 2023 PhD co-supervised with Dey per [04]), Santanu S. Dey; Sahinidis third author · **Place in trajectory**: Georgia Tech period; an application MINLP funded through IDAES (DOE Office of Fossil Energy and Carbon Management, acknowledgement p. 27). BARON appears as baseline and lower-bound source, not as the home of the method (pp. 17-19).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: The "design-from-scratch" gas network problem: choose discrete pipe diameters, the on/off states of compressors, valves and control valves, and the flows and node potentials that meet a given nomination at minimum pipe construction cost, with no existing pipes and no operating costs (pp. 2, 7-9). It is written as the nonconvex MINLP (13)-(36), whose nonconvexity is the potential-loss equation pi_v - pi_w = alpha|q|q (pp. 5, 8). The entry is a gap of scale and features: prior design-from-scratch work "has not considered any instance with over 500 nodes" and never handled active elements, discrete diameters and non-tree networks together (p. 2); the review lists each prior paper's restriction (Belgian network, at most 14 or 36 nodes, trees only, continuous diameters) (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: The classical equivalence between the nonconvex network analysis equations and a convex program over flows that minimizes the integrated potential-loss function (CVXNLP, named after Raghunathan 2013 and going back to Cherry 1951 and Collins et al.) (pp. 9-11, eqs. 44-47, Theorem 1) becomes the master problem for the discrete decisions. The master (Pm) minimizes the sum of alpha/3 q^3 over flows, diameter binaries and active-element binaries, under flow conservation and a budget constraint sum f z <= C (p. 11). The fixed-binary nomination-validation problem (Ps), still nonconvex, checks potentials and their bounds; an infeasible configuration gets an integer no-good cut (58) (pp. 12-13). A binary search on the budget C turns cost minimization into feasibility checks (Algorithm 1, p. 14). It starts from an "initial budget search" that solves the cheapest-diameter integer program (Im), checks it with (Is) and adds no-good cuts, which yields a valid lower bound (pp. 13-15). Changed relative to CVXNLP: binaries for diameters and active elements and the budget constraint are added, and the potentials leave the master and are checked only in the subproblem (pp. 11-12). The cubic master was implemented three ways (as is for BARON 22.9.1 and SCIP 7.0.1; in second-order cone form (62) for MOSEK 9.3.10; as a quadratic lifting q_qua >= q^2 with perspective strengthening q_qua z >= q^2 for Gurobi 9.5.1). Gurobi was kept because the others "tend to be slower due to the presence of the binary variables" (pp. 16-17). The master is solved only to a 60 s limit, because only its binaries are passed on (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Steady-state pipe model pi_v - pi_w = alpha|q|q with potential pi = p^2 (p. 5, eqs. 1, 3); q_max proportional to the pipe's cross-sectional area (p. 5); the compressor model of Babonneau, Nesterov and Vial (p. 6); resistors behave like pipes (p. 6); the cost function of [3] and [4] (eq. 12, p. 7); four diameter options per pipe, from multipliers {0.8, 1.0, 1.3, 1.5} of the GasLib diameter (p. 16); big-M on/off constraints ("M represents a large number") or their nonlinear equivalents (37)-(41) (p. 9). Theorem 1 needs a strictly increasing loss function with phi(0) = 0, which the authors say gas networks commonly satisfy (p. 11). Because the network analysis equations carry no potential bounds, the bounds must be verified in a separate step (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Theorem 1 (p. 11), whose proof is "adapted from a proof in [25]" (Raghunathan), is proved in Appendix A from the KKT system of CVXNLP (pp. 27-28). An exchange argument shows that q+ and q- cannot both be positive (strict monotonicity, eq. 71). The multipliers of flow conservation are identified with the node potentials (eq. 76). The converse constructs multipliers (78)-(82), and convexity of Phi, from monotone phi, gives sufficiency and uniqueness (p. 28). Appendix B builds the MISOC relaxation used for the adapted baseline: McCormick envelopes of (x+ - x-)(pi_v,i - pi_w,i), the convex relaxation gamma >= alpha q^2 and its perspective z gamma >= alpha q^2 (pp. 29-30). No convergence or finite-termination argument is given for the overall framework (none found, pp. 9-17). The paper's own diagnosis of its dual side: each no-good cut removes one binary point, so infeasible budgets cannot be proved at scale (p. 24).
- **D5 Experiments**: GasLib-11, -24, -40, -134 and -582; GasLib-4197 was dropped after a preliminary study as still too hard (p. 15). One nomination for each small network, two for GasLib-134, one per temperature class (warm to freezing) for GasLib-582, each scaled by stress levels 0.1-2.0 (p. 16; Tables 4-18; the text prints "{0, 1, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0}"). Protocol: Python and Pyomo with Gurobi 9.5.1 on an Intel i7 at 4.20 GHz with 16 GB (p. 17); initial budget search 10 min (longer runs gave no significant gain, p. 18), binary search 5 h, 45 min per primal bound loop (Algorithm 2, p. 17). Baselines: the two MINLP formulations solved by BARON, reporting the better one (p. 18), after ruling out CPLEX (objective-only nonconvexity), Gurobi ("at times returns solutions with large constraint violations") and SCIP (BARON "performed better in our tests") (p. 18); and an adaptation of the MISOC approach of Borraz-Sanchez et al., which found no feasible solution for nomination warm 31 at any stress level (p. 23). Metrics: upper and lower budget bounds, the gap (U - L)/L (eq. 63, p. 18), time, time to a 20% gap, budgets checked and no-good cuts added (pp. 18-19). Results: on the small networks, comparable to BARON up to stress 1.0 and larger gaps above (p. 19); BARON closes GasLib-11 and -24 at every stress level but not GasLib-40 at stress 1.5 and 2.0 (Tables 4-6, p. 20). On GasLib-134 the initial budget search alone solves the low-stress instances faster than BARON, while BARON reaches a 20% gap sooner at high stress (p. 19). On GasLib-582, BARON improves its lower bound but is slow to find feasible solutions, so BARON runs 45 min to supply the lower bound (p. 19). The framework finds a feasible budget for all 25 GasLib-582 instances, optimal for 12 and under 20% gap for 6 more (p. 23); gaps reach 255.99% without and 165.86% with perspective strengthening (Table 19, p. 26). Perspective strengthening: about 40% average improvement in a few small-network instances, but the master gets harder (fewer no-good cuts in the same time) (p. 19); no gain on GasLib-134 except in one instance (p. 19); on GasLib-582 never worse, about 55% average and 97% largest improvement, "all due to obtaining better feasible solutions" (p. 24). The instances with large gaps at the time limit are reported and marked in bold (p. 23). Reading note: in Table 6 (p. 20) the with-perspective rows at stress 1.5 and 2.0 list lower bounds 10.34 and 10.53 but repeat the gaps 32.78 and 82.01 of the without-perspective rows (lower bound 8.45); eq. (63) with 10.34 and 10.53 gives about 8.5% and 46%, and the text does not comment (the PDF's own table layout shows the same figures, so this is not an extraction error). BARON's time limit in the comparisons is not stated (pp. 18-19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Restates rather than criticizes prior work. Nomination validation: Pfetsch et al. compare four approaches on GasLib-582 and spatial branch-and-bound wins (p. 3). Reinforcement and design: Babonneau et al.'s two-stage convex NLP; Borraz-Sanchez et al.'s MISOC relaxation with perspective strengthening; bilevel, bundle and tree-contraction methods (p. 4). Water network design is noted as a sibling problem (pp. 4-5). The paper takes CVXNLP's name and proof from Raghunathan's water-network paper and moves them to gas (pp. 10-11), and adopts the cost function, compressor model and stress-level design of [3] and [4] (pp. 6-7, 16). Perspective strengthening is credited to Frangioni-Gentile and Gunluk-Linderoth (pp. 4, 17). Self-citations are limited to BARON (Tawarmalani & Sahinidis 2005; Khajavirad & Sahinidis 2018; the 22.9.1 manual) (pp. 16, 31-32).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions in one paragraph on p. 2, ending with a "first paper" claim. Constraint blocks are named once (Table 1, p. 10), and each master and subproblem is then defined as a list of blocks with listed modifications (pp. 11-15). Results are hedged by network and stress level ("comparable to BARON up to stress level of 1.0", p. 19). Limitations sit in the results sections (pp. 19, 24); the conclusion restates the method and claims effectiveness "when combined with BARON for obtaining lower bounds" (p. 24); one future direction in two sentences (p. 27). Minor slips: "Appendix 6" for the appendices (pp. 11, 23); nomination "cold 4218" (p. 19) vs "cold 4128" (Table 17, p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An algorithmic and computational contribution for one application class: a decomposition (convex master, nonconvex check, budget bisection, initial lower bound) that solves design instances on GasLib-582 (pp. 2, 24). The limits are specific: GasLib-4197 is still out of reach (p. 15); no infeasible budget is proved on GasLib-582, and the lower bounds are nearly constant across nominations (p. 24); gaps are large for high stress and cold nominations (pp. 23-24); the GasLib-582 lower bound depends on BARON (pp. 19, 24); operating costs are left out (p. 2). No code or data release is mentioned; the text says only that the framework "is coded in Python with Pyomo" (p. 17). Future: operating costs "with simple modifications" (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: three equivalent encodings of the convex master were implemented and the fastest (Gurobi on the quadratic lifting) was kept (pp. 16-17); perspective strengthening was judged both by the gap it closes and by the extra master cost (fewer no-good cuts per unit time) (pp. 19, 24); a solver that returned solutions with large constraint violations (Gurobi on the MINLP) was dropped as a baseline, in line with step 5's check of subsolver answers (p. 18).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: a whole public library (GasLib), named nominations, a stated stress-level design and the baseline in its better formulation (pp. 15-18), but no certified optima. Quality is shown by the framework's own lower bounds or BARON's, and the verdict is a two-tool combination, not a portfolio over many solvers (pp. 19, 24).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the method is a Python and Pyomo prototype on Gurobi (p. 17); BARON is a named baseline and lower-bound supplier, and the text says nothing about the method entering BARON (pp. 17-19, 24).
- Method 1: not exercised (the framework has no bound tightening); the stated weakness is on the dual side (p. 24).
- New-pattern candidate: tailored primal, general-solver dual. The framework finds incumbents and BARON proves bounds; the better bound of each is reported (pp. 19, 24).
- New-pattern candidate: a variational convex master. A physical network model with an equivalent convex minimization over flows becomes the master for the discrete design, and the nonconvex bounds are checked with the binaries fixed (pp. 10-13).
- Inner Tension 1 (general vs problem-specific): a tailored method that still needs the general solver for its lower bound (p. 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the physics of a network has an equivalent convex minimization over flows (here the integrated potential-loss function), put it in the master problem for the discrete design and check the nonconvex bounds in a subproblem with the binaries fixed. Prove the equivalence through the KKT system, identifying the flow-conservation multipliers with the potentials (pp. 10-13, 27-28).
- Bisect on the objective (here the budget): each feasibility check yields either an upper or a lower bound, and a cheap relaxation that ignores the physics (cheapest diameters plus no-good cuts) gives the starting lower bound (pp. 13-15, Algorithm 1).
- Solve a master problem only to a time limit when only its binaries are passed on (p. 17).
- Before choosing a solver for a convex subproblem, implement each equivalent encoding (native cubic, second-order cone, quadratic lifting with perspective) and keep the fastest (pp. 16-17).
- Report the time to a 20% gap beside the final gap, and count budgets checked and no-good cuts, so that a method that gets close fast but closes slowly is visible (pp. 18-19).
- Build instance families from a public library by multiplying demands by stress levels and generating discrete design options from multipliers of the given design (p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper that solves the gas network design problem on such large-scale instances." (p. 2)
- "While a large number of binary solutions are feasible to the master problem (Pm), each integer no-good cut only invalidates one of them." (p. 24)

### S242 · Surrogate modeling of the leaching process in a rare earth elements recovery plant (NETL 2024, no DOI or arXiv id; poster, AIChE Annual Meeting, San Diego, 29 Oct 2024)
**Read level**: full. The whole one-page poster text layer was read (p. 1). The plots (model comparisons, linear vs quadratic ALAMO fits, optimization results) are images whose data are not in the text, so every result below is the poster's own order-of-magnitude statement.
**Role**: core (thin: a poster) · **Coauthors**: Dimitrios Fardis (first author) and Donghyun Oh (Georgia Tech ChBE), Alejandro Garciadiego and Andrew Lee (NETL and an NETL support contractor) (p. 1); Sahinidis, as "Nick Sahinidis", is third of five · **Place in trajectory**: the ALAMO and surrogate line carried to a critical-minerals flowsheet under DOE's PrOMMiS initiative (acknowledgement, p. 1). [01] lists a 2025 journal version with a similar title (Comput. Chem. Eng. 197:109061); not read here.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: The poster opens with a question: "Is it possible to model and optimize chemical processes accurately using simple surrogate models?" (p. 1). The application is an acid leaching tank in a rare-earth recovery flowsheet (inputs: solid feed and sulfuric acid; outputs: leach solution and solid waste); a process simulator with complex units (reactors, separators) generates the data (p. 1). Sub-question: "What surrogate modeling techniques are the most appropriate for the leaching process?" (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: Train several surrogate families on simulator data (ANNs; LASSO regression; ALAMO linear, quadratic and PCE-3 models; polynomials, RBFs and kriging through PySMO), compare them on performance metrics, training time and model complexity, then embed the trained models in optimization (surrogate-based optimization, SBO) and compare with DFO and EO (p. 1). Relative to picking the most accurate model, the selection adds training cost and parameter count as criteria (p. 1).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Not stated in the text layer: no input dimension, sampling plan, noise model, variable bounds or optimization problem statement appears (p. 1). DFO and EO are not expanded or specified on the poster; DFO is read here as derivative-free optimization on the simulator, and EO is left unexpanded (p. 1).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a (a poster with no proofs or derivations, p. 1).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two evaluation panels, "Performance metrics" and "Model complexity" (p. 1). Findings as stated: all models have very good predictive ability; training times are 2-6 orders of magnitude larger for complex models; ANNs and RBFs have up to three orders of magnitude more parameters than the polynomials; kriging's complexity is not captured by its parameter count (p. 1). Optimization: SBO reaches the correct solution point with little deviation in the objective; DFO needs one order of magnitude more measurements and three more of CPU time than SBO; SBO is two orders of magnitude faster than EO (p. 1). The text gives neither the reference for "correct", nor the solvers, nor any metric values (p. 1).
- **D6 Positioning**: No references in the text layer. The comparison sets the group's own ALAMO against generic ML (ANN, LASSO) and the PySMO tool's polynomial, RBF and kriging models (p. 1); two co-authors are from NETL (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Question-led panels ("Is it possible ...?", "What surrogate modeling techniques ...?"), then bullet verdicts with order-of-magnitude comparisons; one categorical verdict without hedging, "ANNs, kriging, RBFs are not practical" (p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An application and evaluation contribution: for one leaching unit, quadratic surrogates are "the most appropriate", and surrogate-based optimization is up to three orders of magnitude cheaper than DFO and EO (p. 1). Limits: a single unit; numbers only as orders of magnitude. Future work: "more complex processes and additional surrogate modeling frameworks" (p. 1).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the simplest adequate algebraic model is chosen (quadratic over ANN, kriging and RBF), with parameter count and training time as criteria beside accuracy, and the surrogate is embedded in optimization instead of running DFO on the simulator (p. 1).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: every candidate technique is compared before one is recommended, but on a single unit, with no public library, no hold-out and no stated reference for the "Correct solution point" (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When choosing a surrogate that will be optimized, tabulate parameter count and training time next to accuracy; if the accuracies are similar, take the smallest algebraic model (p. 1).
- Validate surrogate-based optimization against both a derivative-free run on the simulator and the full model, counting simulator calls and CPU time (p. 1).

**Quotes**:
- "Is it possible to model and optimize chemical processes accurately using simple surrogate models?" (p. 1)
- "DFO requires 1 order of magnitude more measurements and 3 orders of magnitude more CPU time than SBO" (p. 1)

### S284 · Learning to Branch with Interpretable Machine Learning Models (NETL 2024, no DOI or arXiv id; talk slides)
**Read level**: full. All 26 slides' text was read. The charts on pp. 14-16 (deployment times), pp. 18-21 (large instances) and p. 24 (MINLP evaluation) are images, so only their titles and one-line headlines are available. The slides carry no date (the work list gives 2024).
**Role**: core (thin: a slide deck) · **Coauthors**: Selin Bayramoglu (first author), George Nemhauser (Georgia Tech ISyE) (p. 1); Sahinidis, as "Nick Sahinidis", is third author · **Place in trajectory**: the Georgia Tech "AI/ML for optimization" line (p. 2), acknowledged to IDAES (p. 26). [01] and [06] record a 2026 paper by the same three authors on sparse learning for branching (arXiv:2604.00094), not read here.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: Strong branching is the expensive target rule: it "Solves two LPs for each fractional binary at a node!" (p. 4). Prior learning-to-branch work imitates it with SVMrank, ExtraTrees, GNNs and DNNs (p. 5). The entry is to imitate strong-branching scores offline with interpretable sparse models (pp. 7-8), within a programme to "Systematize heuristics for tuning, customizing, adapting optimization algorithms" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: Offline learning: solve many similar instances with strong branching, record features and scores, fit a sparse linear model (score approximately beta1 x solution value + beta2 x objective coefficient + beta3 x number of rows + ...) by LASSO, L0L1 or L0L2, and branch in SCIP with the learned score (pp. 7-8, 11). Kept from the GNN line: the features of Khalil et al. 2016 and Gasse et al. 2019 (static and dynamic, plus quadratic transformations) and the four Gasse et al. benchmark families (pp. 10, 12). Changed: the model class (LASSO under 2,000 parameters, L0L1 and L0L2 at most 50, against 64,000 for the GNN, p. 13), the hardware (no GPU for training or deployment, p. 9) and the sampling ("effective sampling": 25K observations from instances of the test size, against 120K in the GNN literature, p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A score linear in the features (p. 8); penalties on the number of nonzero coefficients and on the coefficient norm (lambda0, lambda1, lambda2), fitted with glmnet and L0Learn (p. 11); offline learning assumes that test instances come from the training family (p. 7) and, for the -P models, have the training size (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a (no proofs; theory is only cited, e.g. Balcan et al. 2017 on learning branching-score weights, p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: Set covering, combinatorial auctions, maximum independent set and facility location at three sizes each (p. 12, from Gasse et al. 2019). Baselines: SCIP's default rule RPB (pp. 4, 24) and a GNN; on the large-instance slides the GNN is the one "trained on small problems" (pp. 18-21). Training hours: 0.02-1.14 h for the sparse models against 1.23-8.73 h for the GNN (p. 22). Headline results on large instances: LASSO-P 9% faster than RPB on set covering (p. 18), LASSO-P 27% faster on combinatorial auctions (p. 19), L0L2-P 81% less solving time on maximum independent set (p. 20), LASSO 5% faster on facility location (p. 21). An MINLP test: AC-network-constrained unit commitment built from a minlp.org instance (Anjos and Conejo 2020; six nodes, three generators, three demands), with instances generated by varying G1's start-up cost in [720, 880] (p. 23), run with a 5% gap limit and 1 h of CPU time against RPB, full strong, random, pseudocost, most-infeasible and CPU- and GPU-based GNN rules (p. 24); its results are in a chart not in the text. Reading note: the -P sparse models are trained at the test size (p. 17) while the GNN on the large-instance slides is trained on small problems (pp. 18-21), and the text does not separate the effect of the sampling scheme from that of the model class.
- **D6 Positioning**: Placed among AI/ML-for-MIP work (Bonami et al. 2018; Kannan et al. 2023; Khalil et al. 2016; Xavier et al. 2021, p. 3) and the branching literature from pseudocosts to hybrid branching (p. 4). The rival is the GNN, "a state-of-the-art ML rule" (p. 25), met on its own features and benchmark families (pp. 10, 12).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Results first: a "MAIN RESULTS" slide (p. 9) precedes the method details; each benchmark slide is titled with one percentage against the default rule (pp. 18-21); the conclusions are bullet claims without hedging (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and computational: sparse branching models that speed up SCIP, are faster than the GNN on a CPU-only machine and train in minutes with less data (pp. 9, 25). No limits are stated; the evidence shown comes from one benchmark source with training and test at the same size (pp. 12, 17), and the gains range from 5% to 81% by family (pp. 18-21). Future: "Understand why certain features are selected in the models" (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: Method 3's "keep the algebra small" carried into a learned solver component: from at most 50 to under 2,000 parameters against 64,000, fitted by sparse regression, CPU-only, data-lean and meant to be read (pp. 9, 11, 13, 17, 25). SKILL.md cites the same authors' 2026 arXiv:2604.00094 for this point; these slides state it in 2024 (p. 13).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: step 3's move (reproduce an expensive component with a cheaper object) applied to branching instead of relaxations: strong-branching scores, two LPs per candidate, are emulated by a linear score (pp. 4, 8, 13).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the lab notebook here is SCIP (the learned rule is judged inside SCIP against SCIP's default RPB, pp. 9, 24), not BARON: the open-solver "lone version" of Method 2.
- Heuristic 4 (each baseline in its strongest fair mode): the GNN is run on both CPU and GPU (GN-C, GN-G, p. 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before training a GNN to imitate strong branching, fit LASSO, L0L1 and L0L2 models on the same features (glmnet, L0Learn), and report parameter count, training hours and CPU-only deployment time beside solve time (pp. 11, 13, 22).
- Train on instances of the size you will solve, with a capped sample (25K observations), instead of training on small instances and transferring (p. 17).
- Put the ML rival on its best hardware (GPU) as well as on CPU, and keep the classical rules (full strong, pseudocost, most infeasible, random, solver default) in the same comparison (p. 24).

**Quotes**:
- "Models with fewer parameters can be trained with a smaller sample size" (p. 17)
- "L0L2-P reduces solving time by 81% compared to RPB" (p. 20)

### H007 · Nonconvex optimization problems involving the Euclidean norm: Challenges, progress, and opportunities (SIAM Review (accepted) 2025, no DOI or arXiv id)
**Read level**: full. Manuscript pp. 1-27 (body, sections 1-6) read in full; references pp. 27-34 skimmed. Figures 7, 8 and 10 are plots without data in the text layer, and the sentence describing Figure 8's y-axis is cut between pp. 14 and 17 by the figure and table pages. The file is undated, has no journal pagination, and cannot be checked against the accepted version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Anatoliy Kuznetsov (first author; per [04], a 2024 Georgia Tech ChBE PhD on convexification of Euclidean-norm problems); Sahinidis second and last author · **Place in trajectory**: funded by the Air Force Office of Scientific Research (grant GR00022273, p. 1); part of the norm-convexification turn of 2020- in SKILL.md's trajectory. SKILL.md's Honest Boundary lists this paper as "Unconfirmed and not used".

- **D1 Problem and entry**: Entry by a capability gap: "Yet, the solution of even small instances of many nonconvex optimization problems involving the Euclidean norm to global optimality remains beyond the reach of modern global optimization methods." (abstract, p. 1). The class is one formulation (1.1): positions of N points in R^d, constraints on their norms, on pairwise distances and on distances to fixed points, plus binary and continuous variables (p. 1). Global optimization "is not yet a mature technology" compared with linear, mixed-integer or quadratic programming (p. 1). The applications are chosen because they include famous open questions (kissing numbers, Euclidean Steiner trees, k-means, Lennard-Jones and Morse clusters, Thomson and Tammes, circle packing, planar facility location) (pp. 2-12). Physical evidence that some putative cluster optima may not be global (argon and fullerene cluster discrepancies, pp. 7-8) motivates certificates.
- **D2 Key idea and design**: Four moves. (i) Unify a dozen problems under (1.1) with MINLP formulations (2.1)-(2.11) (pp. 3-11). (ii) Build EuclidLib, 1,352 instances in GAMS, JuMP and Pyomo, sized so that solving it settles open questions; for example, the kissing-number instances run from the best known lower bound to one above the best known upper bound for d = 2-10 (pp. 12-13, 16). (iii) Benchmark five global solvers on it (p. 14). (iv) Diagnose the difficulty as a dual-bound problem caused by two structures: symmetry groups that include Euclidean transformations as well as permutations, and reverse-convex distance constraints whose single-row convexification is trivial (pp. 17-23); derive an agenda from this (pp. 25-27).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: (1.1) allows nonconvexity in the objective and in every norm constraint (p. 1). Library design choices: synthetic, uniformly distributed terminals, data points and customers for the Steiner, k-means and p-median families (p. 13); k-means in at most five dimensions (p. 13); Morse with rho = 6 (p. 13; Table 2 note, p. 16); obnoxious facility location with D in {1/N, 1/(2N)} following [40] (p. 16); Steiner instances up to 200 terminals, although exact methods in general dimension stop below 20 (p. 13). "Solved" means a zero gap within 3600 s (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) An orbit-size estimate: the number of Euclidean-symmetric optima of (4.1) is at least the number of triangles in a triangulation of the convex hull of the atomic positions; it is plotted for putative Lennard-Jones optima with 5 <= N <= 150 and illustrated by two explicit symmetric solutions for N = 5 (pp. 19-20, Fig. 10, Table 3). (b) A projection argument: for the non-overlap constraint of two circles in the unit square, with or without the ordering x_i <= x_j, the convex hull is obtained by dropping the constraint, so single-row relaxation loses it entirely (p. 23, Fig. 12). (c) conv(intersection of F_i) is contained in the intersection of conv(F_i), as the case for simultaneous convexification (p. 22). (d) Fundamental-domain inequalities alpha'x <= alpha'g(x), separated over the Birkhoff polytope for permutations and over the convex hull of rotation matrices for rotations (p. 26). (e) A vertex argument (a convex function on a polytope peaks at a vertex) giving x_j in X_j minus the intersection of the balls B(v_k), as a basis for geometric convexification (p. 27). Surveyed devices: Locatelli and Raber's sub-square assignment branching, which prunes symmetric nodes (p. 21, Fig. 11), and their "wave effect" of optimality-based domain reduction, run twice per node, which solved equal circles in a square up to N = 39 (pp. 24-25); Wang and Gounaris's inscribed-polygon branching (pp. 23-24); Khajavirad's clique inequalities for triplets of circles (p. 24).
- **D5 Experiments**: EuclidLib has 1,352 instances, with 6,395 variables and 4,873 constraints on average (p. 12), in 11 families sized as in Table 2 (p. 16). Protocol: BARON 24.4.30, SCIP 8.1, Gurobi 11.0.1, ANTIGONE 1.1 and LINDOGLOBAL 3.17P through GAMS 46.4.0, on a 4 GHz machine with 5 GB RAM and a 3600 s limit (p. 14). Results: BARON and SCIP each solve 38 instances (2.9%); 73 instances (5.7%) are solved by any solver; the solved instances average 42 variables and 237 constraints; geometric means are 2,891-3,357 s with timeouts counted as 3,600 s (p. 14, Table 1). Diagnosis: scaled-time convergence profiles of the solved instances show dual bounds that start weaker and improve more slowly than primal bounds, and for many instances the optimum is found by local search during preprocessing (p. 17); the authors warn that the profiles should not be used to compare solvers (p. 17). Only aggregate counts appear in the text; there is no per-family table (pp. 14-17). Reading note: Table 1 lists SCIP 8.1, while reference [17] is the SCIP 9.0 report (pp. 14, 28).
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits the tailored heuristics (basin-hopping, particle swarm, genetic, neighbourhood search) and custom branch-and-bound codes, and states each family's certified frontier: SAMBA up to d <= 5 and N <= 18 for Steiner trees (p. 5); k-means up to 800 data points (p. 5); Thomson for N in {2, ..., 6, 12} (p. 9); Tammes for N <= 14 and N = 24 (p. 9); 39 equal circles in a square and 11 unequal circles (p. 10); fewer than 5 obnoxious facilities (p. 12). One direct critique: in Maranas and Floudas's deterministic Lennard-Jones approach, "a non-physical choice of an algorithmic parameter invalidated optimality proofs" (p. 7). Draws on MILP symmetry handling (Margot; Liberti and Ostrowski; Pfetsch and Rehn) (pp. 18, 26) and on convexification results from teammates and their circles (Santana and Dey 2020; Anstreicher and Burer 2010; Munoz and Serrano 2022; Tawarmalani 2010) (pp. 22, 26); Misener and Floudas's ANTIGONE is one of the five solvers (p. 14). Claims transfer: the techniques "will likely be extensible" to (mixed-integer) quadratically constrained programs (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A review in five parts: applications (section 2), library and computational study (section 3), challenges and existing techniques (section 4), opportunities (section 5), conclusions (section 6) (p. 2). The community-challenge sentence appears three times: solving all of EuclidLib would be a significant or remarkable achievement for the field (abstract, p. 1; p. 13; p. 27). Each application paragraph ends with the largest certified instance and the method that certified it (pp. 5, 9, 10, 12). Opinions are flagged ("we believe", "In our view", pp. 2, 25-26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: a review, a public benchmark library (an artifact others can run, on GitHub), a computational study and a research agenda (pp. 2, 12-14, 16, 25-27). Limits: one 3600 s limit and 5 GB of memory (p. 14); synthetic instances for several families (p. 13); qualitative profiles that are not for solver comparison (p. 17); the proposed methods are directions, not implementations (pp. 25-27). Future: automatic detection and elimination of permutation and Euclidean symmetries, ideally down to one optimal solution (p. 25); fundamental-domain cutting planes (p. 26); simultaneous convexification of reverse-convex Euclidean sets through computational solid geometry ("safe" mesh approximations and their convex hulls), extendable to l1-norm variants (pp. 26-27).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the class is entered with a benchmark before any algorithm of their own: a public library of 1,352 instances in three modeling languages on GitHub, five named solver versions under identical limits, timeouts counted, and a covering count ("only 73 instances (5.7%) are solvable by any solver") (pp. 12-14, 16). Variant aspect: certified optima are exactly what the library lacks; only putative optima exist for many families (Cambridge Cluster Database, Packomania; pp. 7, 9, 14), and "solved" means a zero gap proved by a solver (p. 14).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: symmetry elimination is framed as domain reduction, "to reduce the domain X so the cardinality of its intersection with each orbit is as small as possible" (p. 18); coordinate bounds come from distance bounds (pp. 18-19); an incumbent is turned into a valid non-overlap constraint (r* = 0.3 gives 0.36, p. 23); the "wave effect" optimality-based reduction (pp. 24-25).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the case for stronger (simultaneous) convexification rests on the root-bound weakness of single-row relaxations and on qualitative profiles (pp. 17, 22-23), not on measured bound per second; the cost side is noted only for polygon branching ("at the expense of generating more child nodes", p. 24).
- Heuristic 1 (special classes first): a named class whose small instances defeat every solver, expected to generalize to QCQPs (pp. 2, 13). Heuristic 7 (keep evidence and proof apart): putative optima may not be global (p. 8), and invalidated optimality proofs (p. 7). Inner Tension 1: custom codes are "time-consuming to develop and have limited generalizability", so the agenda asks for automatic methods (p. 25).
- New-pattern candidate: primal-vs-dual bottleneck diagnosis from scaled-time convergence profiles of the solved instances (pp. 14, 17). This is a stated source for the test that SKILL.md's Workflow B1 step 3 marks as having no source.
- New-pattern candidate: a library sized to settle open problems: instance ranges chosen so that solving them would decide kissing numbers for d = 2-10 and cluster structures up to N = 150 (p. 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To show why a relaxation is weak, project the constraint onto the difference variables and show that its convex hull is the whole box (p. 23).
- Size the symmetry problem before attacking it: count the symmetric copies of a known (putative) optimum from its geometry (triangles of the convex hull) and plot the count against N (pp. 19-20).
- Diagnose whether the primal or the dual bound is the bottleneck by plotting scaled bound progress against scaled time for the solved instances, and state what the plot cannot be used for (pp. 14, 17).
- Choose instance ranges so that solving the library answers named open questions (kissing numbers from LB(d) to UB(d) + 1) and publish the instances in several modeling languages (pp. 13, 16).
- End each application paragraph with the largest instance ever certified and the method that certified it (pp. 5, 9, 10, 12).
