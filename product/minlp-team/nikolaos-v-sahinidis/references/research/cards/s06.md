### S183 · Agent-based modeling and simulation for an order-to-cash process using NetLogo (CMU Institute for Software Research, CMU-ISR-17-113, Pittsburgh 2017, no DOI)

**Read level**: full. All text pages were read: cover and abstract (pp. 1–3), contents (pp. 5–6), §§1–6 (pp. 7–28) and references (pp. 29–30); pp. 31–32 are blank. Figs. 1–18 are images, so only their captions and the values stated in the text and tables were read. First-page check: title and "Nikolaos Sahinidis" (fourth of four authors, CMU Chemical Engineering) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: John Villarraga (CMU Chemical Engineering), Kathleen M. Carley (CMU School of Computer Science), John Wassick (The Dow Chemical Company) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a CASOS technical report of 1 August 2017 (p. 1). Dow supplied the workflow (p. 8), and Wassick is thanked for his support and advice (p. 1). This fits the CAPD practice of member companies supplying case-study projects (S334 p. 2). The report does not say what Sahinidis contributed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: in an order-to-cash process many decision makers share information, and stock-outs, plant failures, illness or transport delays make an order's fulfilment time hard to predict (p. 7). The authors hold that the process resists mathematical equations because of its computational cost (p. 7). They choose agent-based modeling for what-if analysis of policy decisions (p. 7). Because of time constraints, only a simplified version of Dow's process is modeled (p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: a NetLogo 5.3.1 model (p. 13) with people, unit and information agents (pp. 9–12). Five "intelligent" agents (customer representative, planner/scheduler, distribution center, transportation specialist, carrier) process queues (p. 10), and an SAP agent stands for the ERP system (p. 12). The settings are order type, inventory cycle and agents per role. Queue discipline can be FIFO, LIFO, smallest-first or largest-first. Agents start at a set efficiency and earn learning rewards (Table 1), orders are routed by availability or by expertise, and a random error rate triggers rework, partial rework or return (pp. 13–14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: the people in each role are modeled generically "to reduce variability in the process" (p. 8). The plant is 100% efficient, the other agents start at 90%, and the error rate is 10% (pp. 14–15). The inventory cycle is set so that the distribution center never runs out (p. 13). Some agent actions were changed to reproduce the company's results (p. 9). Face validation and validation in parts are named as the strategy (p. 7), but no validation data appear in §§3–6 (pp. 9–28).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a (no proofs). The devices are designed sweeps with 10 runs per test case (Tables 2–5, pp. 15–17) and a two-sample t-test on training levels (Table 6, p. 24).
- **D5 Experiments**: the studies are a high-demand sweep of 10–300 orders with one agent per role, expertise versus availability, training versus hiring, and a role-by-role search for staffing levels (Tables 2–5, pp. 15–17). Performance is the share of orders completed faster than the average completion time (p. 17). The customer representative is the bottleneck (pp. 19–20). Two representatives raise performance from 22% to 47% but lengthen the average completion time per order. A second transportation specialist cuts the average time per order by about 13% without raising performance (p. 28). Raising training from 90% to 95% or 100% is not significant (p = 0.636 and 0.0921, Table 6, p. 24). Hiring, which the conclusion recommends, gets no significance test (pp. 20–24, 28).
- **D6 Positioning**: five agent-based supply-chain studies are summarized as inputs to the model, without critique (pp. 8–9).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: an agent-based model of a simplified Dow process, used to answer organizational questions (p. 28). The authors expect the best staffing to change if every combination of agents per role could be tested (p. 28). For future work they plan to move the model from NetLogo to C or C++, so it can grow to resemble the actual process (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction (weak, since his role is not stated): a simulator with integer decisions (1–5 agents in each of four roles, Table 5, p. 17) is searched one role at a time (pp. 19–28). The report expects the optimum to change under full enumeration, and the remedy it names is a faster code (p. 28). It neither fits an algebraic surrogate nor runs a derivative-free search, which is what Method 3 prescribes for a simulator with discrete decisions.
- Also Heuristics 3 and 10, and Workflow F step 4 (make the sponsor's engineer a co-mentor): the company supplied the process (p. 8), and its engineer is a co-author and is thanked (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Locate bottlenecks by tracking each agent's processing time as the load rises. After relieving one, measure again, because the next bottleneck appears elsewhere (customer representative, then transportation specialist, then carrier; pp. 19–27).
- Give every intervention you compare the same number of replications and the same significance test before ranking them. Here training was tested and hiring was not (pp. 24, 28).

**Quotes**:
- "Some of their actions have been modified with the purpose of increasing the complexity of the model so as to obtain results similar to the ones provided by the company." (p. 9)
- "However, we theorized that the optimal number of agents for the simulation will differ from our current results, if we had the computational speed to conduct an experiment testing every possible combination of agents in each role." (p. 28)

### S074 · Optimization of circuitry arrangements for heat exchangers using derivative-free optimization (Chemical Engineering Research and Design 2018, doi:10.1016/j.cherd.2017.05.015; arXiv:1705.10437)

**Read level**: full (all 23 pages of the arXiv v1 manuscript: §§1–6, Tables 1–9, the axes and captions of Figs. 6–9, references). First-page check: title and "Nikolaos V. Sahinidis" (fourth of four authors) match (p. 1). The journal version was not read; the manuscript's licence and its DOI link are on p. 1.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nikolaos Ploskas (CMU), Christopher Laughman and Arvind U. Raghunathan (Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: carries the 2013 comparison of box-bounded DFO solvers (ref. [37]) over to a mixed-integer constrained industrial problem (pp. 3, 9). The partner, MERL, is a CAPD member (S334 p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: engineers choose refrigerant circuitries from experience and from a set of enumerated simulations (p. 2). With N tubes, one inlet, one outlet and no branches, there are already N! circuitries (p. 2). Genetic algorithms produce infeasible circuits, and knowledge-based operators such as ISHED's are hard to define for each new type of heat exchanger (p. 3). The paper frames the task as a manufacturing engineer's: given a coil, specify the circuitry that optimizes a metric (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: a binary formulation over the upper triangle of the undirected adjacency matrix of the tubes, with (t²−t)/2 variables: 630 instead of 1,296 for 36 tubes (pp. 4–5). Inlet and outlet are left free, on the claim that a poor circuitry does not get much better when they change. The claim rests on "extensive computational experiments" that are not shown (pp. 4–5). Manufacturing rules become explicit constraints: fixed far-end pairs; at most two connections per tube, so no merges or splits; and no cycles, with one constraint for every combination of far-end pairs (pp. 6–7). CoilDesigner is called through its .NET interface as a black box (p. 7). Five solvers are compared: CMAES, MIDACO, NOMAD, TOMLAB/glcDirect and TOMLAB/glcSolve (pp. 9–10). For the solvers that use penalties, feasibility is checked before each simulator call. The capacity floor Q ≥ 3,900 W enters as a quadratic penalty with λ = 10^6 (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: geometry and inlet conditions are fixed (p. 3; Table 1, p. 11). Other rules, such as limits on tube distance or on the number of circuits, are left out "for the sake of algorithmic and computational simplicity" (p. 4). The simulator is treated as a deterministic function (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a (no proofs). Ground truth comes from enumerating every feasible circuitry with the Choco constraint-programming solver for 4–12 tubes. Each solution is then simulated for every inlet/outlet combination, except at 12 tubes, where one combination per solution is simulated (pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Experiments**: 17 coils of 4–36 tubes, each run limited to 2,500 evaluations and 86,400 s (pp. 10, 12). At 12 tubes there are 54,539 valid designs, and enumerating them took about 20 hours (pp. 10–11). Where checks were possible, glcDirect and glcSolve came out optimal or close to it: for Q, four optima and one design 0.05% off; for Q/ΔP, two optima and three designs 0.15% off on average (p. 16). CMAES solves no coil above 28 tubes, MIDACO finds no feasible design for Q above 24 tubes, and NOMAD is erratic on Q (p. 12). The number of runs and the seeds of the stochastic solvers are not stated (pp. 9–12).
- **D6 Positioning**: the paper fills a gap: no systematic comparison of mixed-integer constrained DFO solvers on industrially relevant problems existed (pp. 3, 9). CMAES is included because it was the best stochastic solver in the 2013 study (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing**: the abstract states a twofold aim, comparing solvers and showing that the formulation suits circuitry design (p. 1). The introduction names the contributions (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a formulation that needs little domain knowledge, plus a solver comparison (pp. 16, 20). The conclusion claims optimal or near-optimal designs "on all instances" (p. 16), although only the five smallest coils were verified (p. 16). Future work: other metrics (shortest joint tubes, production cost) and other design parameters (tube thickness, fin spacing, refrigerant temperature and pressure) (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the existing implementations are benchmarked on the industrial problem before any method of the authors' own. Budgets are equal (p. 12), the solver choice draws on the earlier benchmark (p. 9), small instances have exact ground truth (pp. 10–11, 16), and failures are marked per instance (pp. 13–19).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the objective is a black box, so no deterministic global solver can certify it. Ground truth is complete enumeration instead, available only for 4–12 tubes. For 14–36 tubes, "near-optimal" is measured against the best any solver found (pp. 12, 16). The stochastic solvers appear to have one run each (the number is not stated), so there is no median or best-run reporting (pp. 9–12).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the algebraic part of the problem, the manufacturing feasibility rules, is written out and enforced before every simulation (pp. 6–7, 10). Only Q and ΔP stay black-box. No surrogate is considered (pp. 7–8), although Method 3 step 1 warns against going straight to DFO when decisions are discrete. With 630 binary inputs at 36 tubes (p. 5), the problem is far beyond the input range the SKILL gives for ALAMO.
- Also Heuristic 7 ✗: the claim "on all instances" (p. 16) goes beyond the five verified instances.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode a connection design as the upper triangle of an undirected adjacency matrix. If direction changes performance little, drop it and recover it afterwards by enumerating inlet/outlet choices; this halves the binaries (pp. 4–5, 10). Unlike this paper, show the evidence that direction matters little.
- Write the feasibility rules as explicit constraints and screen candidates before calling the simulator, so solvers that handle constraints by penalties waste no simulations on infeasible points (p. 10).
- Score black-box solvers on small instances for which a constraint-programming solver has enumerated every feasible design (pp. 10–11, 16).
- Report the best value, time and evaluations per instance, mark runs that found no feasible point, and add geometric means (pp. 13–19).

**Quotes**:
- "We also use constraint programming methods to verify the results of the DFO methods for small heat exchangers." (p. 3)
- "Results showed that TOMLAB/glcDirect and TOMLAB/glcSolve can find optimal or near-optimal refrigerant circuitry designs on all instances." (p. 16)

### S103 · A machine learning approach to correlation development applied to fin-tube bundle heat exchangers (Energies 2018, doi:10.3390/en11123450)

**Read level**: full (all 16 pages: abstract, §§1–4, Appendix A, references). First-page check: title and "Nikolaos V. Sahinidis" (fourth of four authors, CMU Chemical Engineering) match (p. 1). File p. 4 also holds a stray block from the submitted version, headed "Version November 20, 2018 submitted to Energies" and carrying line numbers 103–131. It repeats Table 2 and §2.2 in the present tense; the cards use the published text.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Karl Lindqvist and Erling Næss (NTNU), Zachary T. Wilson (CMU) (p. 1). The author contributions credit him with conceptualization, methodology, software, review and editing, and supervision shared with Næss (p. 13) · **Position in trajectory**: an application of ALAMO version 2018.4.3 (p. 3), with travel support for Lindqvist's visit to CMU (p. 14).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: optimizing the volume and weight of offshore waste-heat recovery units needs accurate thermal-hydraulic correlations (p. 1). Optimizing directly with CFD is too costly (p. 1). Published correlations have a narrow or unknown range or rest on scattered pooled data, and tube diameters near 10 mm need an extended range (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: start ALAMO from 108 experimental points, filtered from 248 bundles in 21 publications (pp. 3–4). Choose the subset of basis functions by minimizing BIC "using a combination of enumeration, heuristics, and eventual global optimization using the BARON solver" (p. 4). Add CFD points where the model is most inconsistent, as located by derivative-free optimization (p. 5). Log-transform the outputs and the velocity input, so that the correlation becomes a velocity term times "geometry correction" terms (p. 5). Three tests follow: 30 Latin-hypercube CFD runs held out of training (pp. 3, 5); a weight-minimization case study whose optimum is checked by independent CFD (p. 6); and sensitivity plots against CFD (pp. 10–13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: the design space is a box (Table 2, p. 3). The layout angle is fixed at 30°; data from 25–35° are corrected by at most 5% for heat transfer and 9% for pressure drop (p. 4). The number of tube rows is not a parameter (p. 4). The CFD model is periodic RANS with the Spalart-Allmaras model in OpenFOAM v4.1, with constant properties (p. 5). The case study assumes a constant wall temperature, ignores the steam side and treats the number of tube rows as real-valued (p. 6). The correlations can be no more accurate than the CFD model (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a (no proofs). A scaling argument explains why the optimizer drives the tube diameter to its lower bound (pp. 8–9).
- **D5 Experiments**: on the validation set, the Euler-number correlation improved from R² 0.75 to 0.94 and from 28% to 15% RMSE. The Nusselt group improved from R² 0.70 to 0.76 and from 33% to 25% RMSE. The reference correlations had RMSE of 43–72% (Eu) and 33–35% (Nu) (Table 4, p. 7). At the case-study optima, the new Eu correlation is off from CFD by +48% and +72%, and the Escoa Nu correlation by −46% and +30% (Table 5, p. 9). The optimizer is fmincon SQP with 100 random starts (p. 6). No arm adds the same number of non-adaptive CFD points, and the number of sampled points is not stated. Fig. 3's 208 points minus 108 experimental and 30 validation points would leave 70 (pp. 3–4, 8; my arithmetic).
- **D6 Positioning**: presented as a contrast to the fully connected ANN, SVR and RBNN heat-exchanger models of Table 1, whose structure is less restricted (p. 2). The benchmarks are the Holfeld, Escoa and PFR correlations (pp. 7, 13).
- **D7 Writing**: the hypothesis is stated as a hypothesis, and the novelty as three bullets (p. 2). The abstract reports the shortfall at the optimum (p. 1).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: correlations with a wider validity range, and a public CFD dataset (CC-BY-SA; supplementary Tables S1–S2) (pp. 1–2, 13). The error at the optimum is as large as Escoa's (p. 1), and the Nusselt number is the harder output (pp. 7, 13). The authors note that dataset size limits how nonlinear the model can be (p. 13). Future work: larger datasets that combine Latin-hypercube and adaptive sampling, and experiments on untested geometries such as the case-study optimum (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: best-subset selection by BIC over a chosen basis, solved globally with BARON (p. 4). The model is kept readable through its multiplicative form (p. 5), its trends are read against CFD and physics (pp. 10–13), and points are added by error-maximization sampling (p. 5).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the surrogate is optimized by local SQP from 100 random starts, "to ensure that a global, feasible optimum point was reached" (p. 6). Method 3 step 7 calls for a deterministic global solver. Calling a multistart optimum global also goes against Heuristic 7.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: a hold-out set of 30 Latin-hypercube CFD runs is never used in fitting, and the optimum is checked by independent CFD (pp. 3, 5–6, 9).
- Bearing on the say–do note of Method 3 in the SKILL: ALAMO 2020.4.25 fixed a bug that had "effectively disabled adaptive sampling". This paper used ALAMO 2018.4.3 (p. 3) and credits its gain to CFD sampling (p. 1). Whether 2018.4.3 had the bug cannot be told from the paper. Without a non-adaptive arm, the data cannot separate adaptivity from simply adding points (Table 4, p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Seed a correlation with filtered public experiments, add simulator points where the fitted model is most inconsistent, and keep a separate Latin-hypercube set of simulator runs for validation only (pp. 3–5).
- Log-transform the outputs and any input with a known logarithmic effect. The fitted correlation is then multiplicative and reads as a base term times correction factors (p. 5).
- Check a surrogate-based optimum by running the full simulator at the optimum and at one-at-a-time perturbations around it. Judge the trends as well as the error at the point (pp. 9–13).
- When pooling literature data, map them to one configuration with a correction derived from an existing correlation, and report the largest correction applied (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "The quantitative error of predicted heat transfer and pressure drop coefficients at the case study optimum, however, was as large as those of the Escoa correlations." (p. 1)
- "The optimization was repeated 100 times for a given case, using random starting points within the design space, to ensure that a global, feasible optimum point was reached." (p. 6)

### S112 · BARON user manual (The Optimization Firm LLC 2018 [file: v. 17.4.1, April 2017], no DOI)

**Read level**: full (all 31 pages: contents, §§1–8 and the 41-item bibliography). Version: the file is BARON user manual v. 17.4.1 of April 1, 2017 (p. 1), not a 2018 release; its pages are cited as S112 v17.4.1. First-page check: title and "Nick Sahinidis, The Optimization Firm, LLC" match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (his name alone, in firm voice) · **Position in trajectory**: lies between the 2000 v4.0 manual (S077, card in s01) and the v. 2026.9.10 manual the SKILL cites. BARON is now licensed; the demo handles up to 10 variables, 10 constraints and 50 nonlinear operations (p. 2). It runs under AIMMS, AMPL, GAMS, JuMP, MATLAB, Pyomo and YALMIP (pp. 2–3), with CLP/CBC, FilterSD and IPOPT bundled and CPLEX optional (p. 3). Parts were created at UIUC and CMU (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: n/a as research. BARON finds global solutions of algebraic NLPs and MINLPs, with guarantees "under fairly general assumptions", among them finite bounds on nonlinear expressions (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: branch and bound "enhanced with a variety of constraint propagation and duality techniques for reducing ranges of variables" (p. 2). Five reduction switches are on by default: nonlinear and linear feasibility-based tightening, marginals-based reduction, optimality-based tightening, and probing, whose default is "-2: automatically decided by BARON" (pp. 23–24). Convex functions are outer-approximated in the relaxation (4 approximators, 4 cutting-plane rounds by default; p. 23). Branching and node selection default to BARON's dynamic strategies (p. 24). Other features: the best few or all feasible solutions from a single search tree rather than integer cuts (p. 16), a multistart-heuristic mode (p. 18), IIS search (p. 19) and automatic detection of complementarity (p. 20).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: functions must be algebraic and given explicitly. BARON handles exp, ln, x^α and β^x. It handles |x| and x^y automatically under AIMMS, AMPL and GAMS (p. 3), and also under MATLAB according to p. 6; elsewhere they need a reformulation (p. 6). Trigonometric functions are not supported (p. 3). This is the same function list as in 2000 (S077 p. 41). Users should give finite bounds for as many variables as possible. BARON otherwise tries to infer them, and if that fails the global guarantee is lost (p. 3). Default tolerances are EpsA 1e-6, EpsR 1e-4 and feasibility 1e-5. MaxTime is 1000 s, measured as CPU time for single-threaded runs and as wall-clock time otherwise (pp. 20–22).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a. The termination test is |U − L| ≤ εa or ≤ εr|L| (pp. 20–21). The status vocabulary distinguishes "solved within tolerances", heuristic termination, missing bounds (globality or infeasibility not guaranteed), numerically sensitive problems and resource limits (pp. 12–14).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a. Worked examples only: the gear-train log (pp. 9–11), the 14 solutions of the PUMA robot system (pp. 16–18) and an IIS (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: the bibliography is "a partial list of BARON-related publications that describe the algorithms implemented in the software": 41 items from 1995 to 2017, ending with Puranik and Sahinidis on domain reduction, bounds tightening and deletion presolve (pp. 28–31). The 1996 BARON note appears twice, as items 4 and 26 (pp. 28, 30).
- **D7 Writing**: every option appears with its range and default (pp. 20–28). Each termination message comes with what the user must do next (pp. 12–13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: the limits stated are limits of use. Missing bounds void the guarantee, and the relaxation bounds printed meanwhile may be invalid (pp. 3, 12). Under "numerically sensitive", the reported bound is only "likely correct" (p. 13). DeltaTerm may make runs nondeterministic (p. 21). IIS detection can slow feasible models (p. 19), and NumSol slows the search (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: range reduction is part of the definition (p. 2). The reduction switches are on by default (pp. 23–24), and the preprocessor's "extensive reduction of variable ranges" serves the multistart mode too (p. 18). Probing, off by default in 2000 (S077 pp. 49–50), is now decided automatically (p. 24), as Method 1 step 4 prescribes.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: every device has a numbered switch, so it can be ablated (pp. 20–28). The bibliography is declared to describe the implemented algorithms (p. 28). The log prints the version and build and asks users to cite the 2005 polyhedral branch-and-cut paper (p. 10).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the relaxations are LP/MIP with outer approximation (p. 23), and on problems with integer variables "most of BARON's time is spent on solving MIP relaxations" (p. 20). The local NLP solvers are chosen and switched during the search according to problem characteristics and solver performance, and CPLEX falls back to CLP/CBC (p. 26).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: AllowFilterSD defaults to 0, so FilterSD is not used for local search (p. 27), yet the sample log lists FILTERSD among the subsolvers the run may use (p. 10). This manual does not settle what the 2022.1.19 release note ("BARON's embedded FilterSD algorithm is now off by default") switched off.
- Also Heuristic 7: "Normal completion" means solved within tolerances, and missing bounds lead to "Globality is therefore not guaranteed" (p. 12). Heuristic 6: DeltaTerm's nondeterminism is flagged (p. 21). Heuristic 3: access violations are to be reported to Nick Sahinidis (p. 13). Heuristic 2: users should give finite bounds for as many variables as possible (p. 3).
- Variant of the s01 candidate *per-component CPU-time accounting by default* (S077 pp. 51, 65–66): in v. 17.4.1, when BARON runs on its own, the summary and times files that hold the iteration and time breakdown are off by default (pp. 13, 25).
- new-pattern candidate: *version, build and the paper to cite in every run log* (p. 10; ALAMO banner, S249 pp. 78–79).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give every reduction or relaxation device an integer switch with a documented default (off, on, automatic), so that users and reviewers can run the ablation themselves (pp. 23–24).
- Pair each termination status with what it certifies and what the user must change. Say outright when the printed bounds may be invalid (pp. 12–13).
- In the option table, flag any option that makes runs nondeterministic (DeltaTerm, p. 21), and state which clock enforces the time limit (p. 22).
- Offer a labelled heuristic mode of the global solver (multistart local search, iterations set to 0), so that users do not stop the search early and read the incumbent as global (p. 18).

**Quotes**:
- "Even though relaxation bounds are printed on the screen to give the user a feeling for convergence, these bounds may not be valid for the problem at hand." (p. 12)
- "Because it relies on CPU time measurements, which may depend on machine load, this option may result in nondeterministic behavior." (p. 21)
