### S046 · Domain reduction techniques for global NLP and MINLP optimization (Constraints 2017, doi:10.1007/s10601-016-9267-5; arXiv:1706.08601)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v1; body pp. 1-21 read in full, including §8 and §9; the tables on pp. 21, 24-25, 34-35 read; Figures 5-17 are performance-profile images of which only the captions are in the text file, so the curves were not read; reference list pp. 21-33 skimmed for self-citations).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Y. Puranik (first author, Department of Chemical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon University, p. 1; Sahinidis's PhD student, PhD 2016 per note 01) · **Position in trajectory**: CMU period; written alongside Puranik's two other 2017 papers, which it cites ([151] optimality-condition tightening, p. 14; [152] deletion presolve for IIS isolation, p. 5); the group's 2017 restatement of the 1995-96 range-reduction tests (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry from solver practice, not from an open theorem: solvers "routinely utilize presolve techniques", and domain reduction is "especially important" for nonconvex NLP and MINLP (abstract, p. 1). Two framings set the question: reduction is not needed for convergence but "typically" speeds it up (p. 2; again "not even necessary to prove convergence", p. 9), and "Smaller domains lead to tighter relaxations" (p. 2, Fig. 1). Stated aims: review the techniques, point to connections with constraint programming and interval arithmetic, and measure the impact on widely available global solvers on 1740 test problems (abstract p. 1; p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a component is not needed for correctness, say so first and make its value an empirical question (pp. 2, 9), then design the ablation that answers it (§8, pp. 15-21).
- **D2 Core idea**: A taxonomy by what a technique removes. §5 "Reduction of infeasible domains" (p. 5): bound problems at three cost levels, the exact nonconvex problem (11), a convex relaxation (12) and its linearization (13) (pp. 5-6); propagation (pp. 6-9); consistency (p. 9); MIP techniques: probing, shaving, aggressive bounds tightening, implications, coefficient reduction (pp. 9-10); conflict analysis (pp. 10-11). §6 "Reduction of suboptimal domains" (p. 11): techniques that may remove feasible points as long as one global optimum remains: duality-based cuts (22)-(25) from the relaxation's multipliers, an incumbent U and the relaxation value L (p. 11); the contraction subproblem with f_conv(x) <= U, (26)-(27) (p. 12); pruning, dominance and symmetry (p. 14); optimality conditions, including BARON's relaxation-only constraints (p. 14); the cluster effect (p. 15). A deliberate reclassification: the bound problems (11)-(13), usually called optimality-based bounds tightening, are filed under feasibility-based reduction because "they only carry out reduction of infeasible regions" (p. 6). §7 treats the reduction effort per node as the design variable (p. 15). No new algorithm is proposed; the new part is the cross-solver ablation (§8, pp. 15-21).
  - Transferable: sort a toolbox by what it removes (infeasible vs suboptimal points) rather than by the subproblem it solves (pp. 5-6, 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Problem (1) carries explicit bounds x^l <= x <= x^u on all variables (p. 1); relaxations come from factorable reformulation, represented as a DAG (pp. 2-3). The duality-based cuts need a convex relaxation with optimal value L, an active bound with multiplier lambda_j > 0 and a valid upper bound U (p. 11). Floating point is a stated hazard: rounding "can lead to solutions being lost even for simple problems", interval methods avoid it but are "often slower" (p. 4), and a safe floating-point implementation of duality-based reduction (Lebbah et al.) is cited (p. 11). Propagation may not reach a fixed point finitely (p. 9). Experimental settings: "default options" against "default options with domain reduction turned off" (p. 16), with the switch-off option lists in Tables 2, 4 and 6 (pp. 21, 24, 34).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: No new theorems; restated derivations and small counterexamples. (i) The range-reduction cut x_j^l >= x_j^u - (U - L)/lambda_j, attributed to "Theorem 2 in [158]" (Ryoo & Sahinidis), with a geometric reading through the relaxation's value function (eq. (22), p. 11; Fig. 4, p. 12); the mirror cut at a lower bound (23), the probing version obtained by fixing x_j at a bound and re-solving (24), and the constraint version (25) (p. 11); suboptimal multipliers "often provide greater reduction" (p. 12). (ii) A unified duality derivation (Tawarmalani & Sahinidis): a domain-reduction master problem built from Lagrangian subproblems, (29)-(31); choosing (a, a0) = e_j gives eq. (25), and LP duality on the one-row relaxation (32) gives the propagation rules (15)-(16) (pp. 12-13). (iii) Counterexamples: a four-constraint, two-variable system where one-constraint-at-a-time propagation reduces nothing while Problem (11) gives [-1, 3] (pp. 7-8); x1 + x2 = 0, x1 - q x2 = 0 with q in (0, 1), where propagation converges only in the limit and LP stops at once (p. 9). (iv) A worked propagation example (17)-(18) (p. 7) and the factorable example (2)-(4) carried through to propagation (pp. 3, 9).
  - Transferable: show that a family of heuristics are special cases of one dual master problem (pp. 12-13); bound the reach of a cheap method with a two-variable counterexample against the exact bound problem (pp. 7-8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 1740 problems: GlobalLib 369, PrincetonLib 980, CMU-IBM 142, MINLPLib 249 (Table 1, p. 21); results per library "so as to demonstrate that the observed trends are not dominated by any particular library, convexity, or integrality properties" (p. 16). Protocol: Intel Xeon X5650 2.66 GHz, CentOS 7, 500 s, absolute and relative tolerances 10^-6 (p. 16), all under GAMS (p. 15). Solvers: BARON, Couenne and SCIP, because they let domain reduction be switched off; ANTIGONE and LindoGlobal excluded because they do not (p. 15). Performance profiles through PAVER, where "solved" means the best solution among the solvers in that profile within a multiple of the fastest time (p. 16). Node and time increases with reduction off, on Global/Princeton/CMU-IBM/MINLP: BARON +1180/261/546/802% nodes and +67/70/75/47% time (Table 3, p. 24); Couenne +129/21/186/171% and +26/19/12/16% (Table 5, p. 25); SCIP +174/152/417/56% and +24/33/141/18% (Table 7, p. 35). Reading of the profiles: on the continuous libraries BARON's no-reduction profile "catches up", so its heuristics still find (near-)global solutions but the lower bounds cannot prove them; in the MINLP case it does not even find good feasible solutions (p. 16); Couenne and SCIP without reduction fail to find good solutions on the NLP libraries (p. 16). An aggregated profile over all libraries (Fig. 17, p. 24) carries the cross-solver remarks (p. 21). Not stated: how the % increases are averaged (all problems or commonly solved ones; arithmetic or geometric) (pp. 16, 24-25, 35); no list of instances on which reduction hurt (pp. 15-21).
  - Transferable: publish the exact option lists that switch a component off in each solver (Tables 2, 4, 6; pp. 21, 24, 34) so that the ablation can be rerun by anyone with the solvers.
- **D6 Positioning**: Bridges communities: SAT unit propagation, CP filtering and consistency, AI label inference, interval analysis (pp. 2, 6-7, 9), with explicit equivalences: "Probing and shaving techniques are equivalent to the singleton consistency techniques from CP" (p. 9); propagation "is equivalent to label inference in the AI community" (p. 7). Credits rivals' recent work (Belotti's pairs of inequalities, p. 8; Nannicini et al.'s aggressive probing, pp. 10, 15; Caprara-Locatelli NRDR, p. 12; Zamora-Grossmann branch-and-contract, p. 12) and presents Gleixner et al.'s Lagrangian variable bounds as an instantiation of a remark by Tawarmalani and Sahinidis (p. 13). Self-citation: at least 21 of the 202 references have Sahinidis as an author (my count of the list, pp. 21-33), including the "poor man's LPs and poor man's NLPs" terms [173, 160] (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Survey order from representation and interval arithmetic, through infeasible and then suboptimal reduction, to implementation trade-offs and computation (p. 2); each technique gets a toy example or a figure (Figs. 1, 3, 4; pp. 2, 7-8, 11-12). Reading instructions are given: profiles in different figures cannot be compared across solvers (p. 16). The computational prose is strong: "huge deterioration" (p. 16), a "huge impact" for Couenne (p. 16) and, in the conclusions, "huge reductions in computational time and number of nodes" (p. 21), while the time columns read +12% to +26% for Couenne (Table 5, p. 25) and +18% to +141% for SCIP (Table 7, p. 35); the profile curves themselves are not in the text file.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a survey that maps the domain-reduction literature onto one taxonomy and one dual derivation, plus evidence from three solvers that switching the component off costs nodes and time on every library (pp. 15-21). Limits stated: per-technique impact is "beyond the scope" (Kilinc & Sahinidis [104] is pointed to, p. 16); two solvers could not be tested (p. 15). Limits not stated (my reading): the profile yardstick is the best solution found among the compared solvers, not a certified optimum (p. 16); the averaging rule is not given; the sentence "Even without reduction, BARON dominates" (p. 21) is a cross-solver verdict by BARON's developers with no third-party numbers. Future work: reduction from sets of constraints (global filtering) for broad structured classes and for pooling, protein folding and network design (p. 21).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: duality-based reduction at every node from the relaxation alone, probing rationed to the root and some nodes, effort balanced per node, the need for a floating-point-safe implementation named, and the contribution shown by switching reduction off inside production solvers, library by library (pp. 6, 11-12, 15-16, 21; Tables 3, 5, 7). A refinement, not a contradiction: the paper says reduction is not needed for convergence (pp. 2, 9), so Method 1 is a speed claim. Method 2 evidence ✅: one protocol (machine, 500 s, 10^-6, GAMS), competitors chosen by the switch they allow, named option sets as ablation units, per-library reporting (pp. 15-16; Tables 2, 4, 6). Method 5 evidence ✅: bound problems ranked by cost, nonconvex (11), convex NLP (12), LP (13), with LP chosen for "efficiency and robustness" (p. 6); Lagrangian variable bounds approximate the effect of Problem (27) at every node without solving it (pp. 13-14), which is Method 5 step 3 (a cheaper object that reproduces most of an expensive one). Method 4 variant ⚠: whole public libraries, but the yardstick is the best solution among the compared solvers, not a certified optimum (p. 16), and the aggregated Fig. 17 supports a single-winner sentence about the authors' own solver (p. 21). Also a variant of Workflow D step 2, which asks that modest effects be called modest: see D7. Heuristic 2 evidence: optimality conditions passed as relaxation-only constraints (p. 14). New-pattern candidates: dual unification of the reduction toolbox (pp. 6, 12-13); profile-tail attribution of an ablated component, primal vs dual (pp. 16, 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ablate a whole component family inside several production solvers through their own options, print the exact option lists (Tables 2, 4, 6; pp. 21, 24, 34), and report node and time changes per library (Tables 3, 5, 7; pp. 24-25, 35).
- Include only competitors that let the same component be switched off, and name the ones excluded and why (p. 15).
- Read the right end of a performance profile: if the ablated variant catches up, the component mainly serves the proof (the lower bound), not the search for solutions (p. 16); a flip in two solvers' order when a component is removed shows which one depends on it more (p. 21).
- Rank bound-tightening subproblems by cost (exact nonconvex (11), convex NLP (12), LP (13)) and spend the expensive ones by a stated rule: root only, a subset of variables, or Couenne's rule of every node up to depth L and probability 2^(L-d) below it (pp. 5-6, 11, 15).
- Present a toolbox through one dual master problem and recover each known rule as a choice of its objective coefficients (pp. 12-13).
- Use a two-variable linear system to show the limit of one-constraint-at-a-time propagation against the full bound LP (pp. 7-8).

**Quotes**:
- "Thus, there exists a need to balance the effort involved in domain reduction in order to reduce the average effort per node." (p. 15)
- "Linear relaxations may provide weaker bounds than nonlinear ones, but can be solved very efficiently." (p. 6)

### S031 · QPLIB: a library of quadratic programming instances (Mathematical Programming Computation 2019, doi:10.1007/s12532-018-0147-4)

**Read level**: full (pp. 1-22 read in full; reference list pp. 22-33 skimmed; Appendix A, the per-instance feature table, pp. 33-38 skimmed; Appendix B, the file format with its worked example, pp. 38-42 read; Figures 1-5 are plots of which only captions and axis labels are in the text file).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: F. Furini, E. Traversi, P. Belotti, A. Frangioni, A. Gleixner, N. Gould, L. Liberti, A. Lodi, R. Misener, H. Mittelmann, S. Vigerske, A. Wiegele (author line p. 1, where he appears as "Nick Sahinidis", 11th of 13; after the first two the names are in alphabetical order, so the position says nothing about his share); the acknowledgments name funding for the 5th, 6th and 12th authors only and give no division of labour (p. 22) · **Position in trajectory**: CMU period; community infrastructure built with the MINLPLib/ZIB group (the website extends the MINLPLib 2 scripts, p. 20) and with team member Misener; "the BARON library" is one of the seven libraries gathered (p. 14).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry from a missing artifact: unlike MILP, there was "no single library devoted to all different kinds of instances of QP", and the existing ones serve one application or one structure (p. 2). The design question is what "interesting" means; the answer is "challenging for a significant set of solution methods": an instance that a large fraction of capable solvers solve in a "short" time is out, one that a single structure-exploiting solver solves quickly while most others cannot is in (p. 2). Motivation: QPs are the "least-nonlinear nonlinear problems" (p. 2) and, through polynomial-to-quadratic reduction, roughly as expressive as MINLP, a "sweet spot" between MILP and MINLP (p. 3); applications in Table 1 (pp. 3-4).
  - Transferable: turn a vague library goal, such as interesting instances, into a measurable property of solver behaviour before collecting anything (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: (a) A three-field taxonomy OVC: objective (L, D, C, Q), variables (C, B, M, I, G), constraints (N, B, L, D, C, Q), ordered so that problems get harder from left to right and a method for a class applies to every simpler class (pp. 6-7); reformulations that move instances between classes are discussed (binary iff x^2 = x; a linear objective without loss of generality; diagonalization of PSD Hessians, which adds linear constraints) (pp. 6-8). (b) A two-filter cascade with complete solvers under GAMS as the hardness instrument: about 8500 gathered, about 3000 discrete and 1000 continuous after the first filter, 319 discrete and 134 continuous (453) after the second (Table 3, p. 16; pp. 15-16). (c) No reformulation of contributed instances, so as not to add a bias toward one class of solvers (p. 8). (d) A new plain-text .qplib format (p. 20; Appendix B, pp. 38-42) and a website with per-instance metadata, sparsity plots, a sortable table and a CSV download (pp. 20-21).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: General MIQCQP, bounds possibly infinite (p. 5). Convexity is judged by eigenvalues with thresholds of +-10^-12 (p. 15) and by the sufficient condition only, so a quadratic constraint with two finite sides is nonconvex and SOC constraints may be labelled nonconvex, justified because "not all QP solvers are capable of detecting and exploiting these structures" (pp. 6, 8, 15). Completeness classes after Neumaier [110]: incomplete, asymptotically complete, complete, rigorous, assigned per solver and class in Table 2 (pp. 10-11); none of the solvers used is rigorous (p. 11). On "exact": against Turing-machine and Real-RAM models of computation, calling a floating-point solver exact is "stretching the meaning of the word" (pp. 12-13). Hardness thresholds: at least 30% of the complete solvers within 30 s for the first filter (p. 15), one complete QGQ solver within 120 s for the second (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: n/a for proofs (no theorems). Devices: the containment ordering of the taxonomy with a partial order on the variable field (C ⊂ M ⊂ G, B ⊂ M ⊂ G, B ⊂ I ⊂ G), used to propagate method applicability (pp. 6-7); complexity landmarks used to place classes: LIQ undecidable (Jeroslow [78], p. 7), QCQ NP-hard even for box constraints and one negative eigenvalue (p. 9), the decision version of QCL in NP (Vavasis [138], p. 12); the ordering C > A > I > blank used to fill Table 2 with each solver's strongest algorithm for a class (p. 11).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: No solver comparison, by design: "we will not report any data comparing them" (p. 2; repeated p. 21); performance questions are referred to Mittelmann's benchmark site (pp. 4-5). Computation serves instance selection only: the complete solvers in GAMS for each category (Table 2, p. 11; p. 15). Gathering: an online call opened in January 2014 and kept open ten months, plus seven existing libraries (BARON, CUTEst, GAMS Performance, MacMINLP, Maros-Meszaros, MINLPLib, POLIP) (pp. 13-14); all instances translated to GAMS (p. 14). Final split: continuous convex 32, continuous nonconvex 102, discrete convex 31, discrete nonconvex 288 (Table 4, p. 16); sizes up to about 10^6 variables and constraints (QPLIB_8547, QPLIB_9008, p. 17); at most 140,000 quadratic constraints (p. 18). The second filter includes a manual judgement: "We carefully analyzed the instances one by one", keeping a few larger and harder instances per donor (p. 16). Not stated: solver versions, GAMS version, machine, and which complete solver ran the 120 s pass (pp. 13-16). The website provides feasible solution points "for most instances" (pp. 20-21); no optimal values or bounds are given in the paper.
- **D6 Positioning**: Takes MIPLIB 2010 as the model of a mature library (p. 2, [84]) and reuses MINLPLib 2's tools (p. 20); defers performance to Mittelmann's benchmarks (pp. 4-5). Sahinidis's own work appears as the BARON references [133-135] (Table 2, p. 11) and as two applications in Table 1, chip layout [39] and portfolio optimization [122] (p. 3): 5 of 147 references.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A 13-author "we"; scare quotes mark the soft words ("interesting", "short", "natural", "problematic") (pp. 2, 8, 18); scope negations stated twice ("Providing guidance about which solvers are most suited to some specific class of QPs is entirely outside the scope of our work", p. 2; again p. 21). The process itself is offered as guidance: "We hope that our work can provide useful guidelines for other researchers interested in constructing benchmarks" (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: infrastructure, namely 453 instances, the taxonomy, a file format and a website (p. 21). Limits stated: the library is a "snapshot" to be extended dynamically; links to the literature are planned (p. 21). Limits not stated (my reading): hardness is relative to the solvers and time limits of the 2014-15 selection runs (pp. 13-16), and those runs cannot be repeated exactly (no versions or hardware); no certified optima are part of the library as described.

**Method links**: Method 4 variant ⚠: a large public library with written admission rules is built and a solver ranking is refused (pp. 2, 15-16, 21), but the library ships feasible points, not certified optima (pp. 20-21), and the filters go beyond Workflow C's rule of filtering only for acceptance, triviality and always-timeout cases: a hardness cut (an instance one complete solver proves within 120 s is dropped) and a manual near-duplicate cut (p. 16). Method 2 evidence ✅, joint and weak: instances from "the BARON library" flow into a community library (p. 14), as in Method 2 step 5 (instances turned into a public library); his personal role is unknown. Also evidence for Heuristic 7 and Integrity Rule 5: BARON and the other global solvers are called "complete", not rigorous, and calling them exact is called a stretch (pp. 11-13). Variant of Heuristic 6 (reproducible by construction): the selection runs name no versions or machine (pp. 15-16). New-pattern candidates: admission rule by solver-fraction thresholds (pp. 2, 15-16); natural-form instances, no reformulation, so no solver class is favoured (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the admission rule as solver-fraction thresholds: drop an instance if at least 30% of the complete solvers solve it within 30 s; after removing near-duplicates per donor, drop it if one complete solver proves optimality within 120 s (pp. 15-16).
- Store instances as contributed, never reformulated, and say why: reformulations favour one solver class (p. 8).
- Give every instance a three-letter class code ordered by containment (pp. 6-7) and publish a CSV of per-instance metadata so that users can build their own subsets (p. 21).
- Grade each solver per problem class as incomplete, asymptotically complete, complete or rigorous, and call floating-point global solvers "complete" (pp. 10-13).
- Define property labels by an explicit numerical test (eigenvalues beyond +-10^-12) and state the known misclassification it causes (SOC constraints labelled nonconvex) (pp. 8, 15).
- Ship a minimal text format of defaults plus non-default exceptions, with a worked example (pp. 38-42).

**Quotes**:
- "Thanks to these extensive preliminary tests, we discarded all instances that are solved by at least 30% of the complete solvers within a time limit of 30 seconds." (p. 15)
- "Since none of the solvers we are using can be classified as rigorous, we limit ourselves to declaring solvers complete." (p. 11)

### S182 · A discussion on practical considerations with sparse regression methodologies (Statistical Science 2020, doi:10.1214/20-STS806; arXiv:2011.09362)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v2; pp. 1-10 read in full, with footnotes and figure captions; references pp. 10-11 read; Figures 1-3 are plots whose values are not in the text file; the two discussed papers, their supplements and the authors' scripts were not read).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Owais Sarwar (first author) and Benjamin Sauk, both Carnegie Mellon (p. 1; his PhD students per notes 01 and 04); Sahinidis last, with a Georgia Tech affiliation (p. 1); funded through DOE's IDAES institute (pp. 9-10) · **Position in trajectory**: the CMU-to-Georgia-Tech transition (2020); the statistics-facing side of the ALAMO best-subset line (Cozad et al. 2014 and 2015 cited as [7] and [8], pp. 5, 7), written as a published discussion of Hastie-Tibshirani-Tibshirani and Bertsimas-Pauphilet-Van Parys (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry into someone else's debate: two new Statistical Science comparisons of the lasso, subset selection and alternatives reach conclusions that are "not completely aligned" (p. 2); the discussion sets out to "summarize and compare the two studies", examining agreement and divergence, "aiming to provide clarity and value to users" (abstract, p. 1). Framed historically from Gauss and Legendre through ridge regression and the lasso to best subset selection (p. 1). Restricted to regression, not classification (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Put the two studies' setups and conclusions side by side (Table 1, p. 3; Table 2, p. 4), then test points of divergence with small new experiments run on the authors' released code (pp. 2, 5): (a) the relaxed lasso, the "overall winner" of Hastie et al., run inside Bertsimas et al.'s "Medium noise, High correlation" setting (p. 5; Fig. 1, p. 6); (b) Bertsimas et al.'s case for cardinality-constrained formulations tested against the penalized L0Learn (with an l1 or l2 term) and forward stepwise in the same setting (p. 7; Fig. 2, p. 8), with training times (Fig. 3, p. 9). Plus design critiques: fixing the support size to the true one "somewhat exaggerates the lasso's relative deficiencies" (p. 5); the scaling of (p, n, ktrue) as a confounder between the studies (p. 5); a mismatched timing comparison (p. 6); the cost of a full hyperparameter path (pp. 6-7, fn. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Synthetic data from a linear model with Gaussian error (p. 2). The reproduced setting: (p, ktrue, SNR, rho, MIC) = (10^4, 50, 1, 0.7, true); validation on a separate set of size n; results averaged over 10 trials; CIO and SS values taken from Bertsimas et al.'s Figs. 6-7, not rerun (fn. 7, p. 5). R defaults for lasso and MCP; rlasso with nlambda 50 and 10 interpolation points (Fig. 1 caption, p. 6); forward selection up to 100 steps; L0Learn with (nLambda, nGamma) = (50, 10) and the "slower but stronger CDPSI algorithm" (Fig. 2 caption, p. 8); times include cross validation (Fig. 3 caption, p. 9). No hardware is stated (pp. 1-10). L0BnB is left out because "the implementation is currently in the prototype stage" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: n/a for proofs. Argument devices: (i) a like-for-like check of a timing claim: a single cardinality-constrained solve set against glmnet's full solution path (p. 6); (ii) a cost extrapolation: a 60 s limit per (gamma, k), 100 values of k and 10 of gamma, and, "charitably", 30 s per solve on average give more than 8 h of training before K-fold cross validation (fn. 8, pp. 6-7); (iii) a matched-scaling comparison: the configurations (10^3, 50, 5) and (10^4, 500, 50) have identical linear scaling in (p, n, ktrue), so a difference must come from the ratio that changes (p. 5); (iv) a transplant test: one study's winner run inside the other study's protocol (p. 5).
  - Transferable: before accepting a claimed speed, multiply the per-solve time by the number of grid points the method needs in practice (fn. 8, pp. 6-7).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Fig. 1 compares the relaxed lasso with the lasso, MCP and (from Bertsimas et al.'s figures) CIO/SS on Accuracy, FDR and RMSE: the relaxed lasso "appears to be superior to MCP in this setting", CIO/SS have the lower FDR, and the relaxed lasso is competitive with SS and more accurate than both (p. 5). Their MCP results differ from the original figures ("likely there is an implementation difference", p. 5). Fig. 2: regularized subset methods beat plain L0Learn and forward selection, most of all with little data; beyond a data threshold the unregularized ones match them in variable selection, but "lack of shrinkage in the coefficients" keeps their test error higher, and regularized L0Learn exceeds the cardinality-constrained algorithms "while being significantly faster and more convenient" (p. 7). Fig. 3 gives training times (p. 9). A tension left open in the text (my reading of the captions): the timing figure compares methods run over different tuning grids (50 x 10 for rlasso and L0Learn, 100 steps for forward selection, R defaults for lasso and MCP; pp. 6, 8, 9), the kind of mismatch the paper itself faults on p. 6; note 05 §8e records that a rejoinder, not read here, called this comparison misleading.
- **D6 Positioning**: Opens by commending both groups for open code, Hastie et al. "notably further" for releasing their simulation scripts (p. 2), says its own experiments "were actualized because the authors have provided open-source implementations" (p. 5), and posts its own scripts (p. 2). Takes sides point by point: agrees that the lasso's FDR is high (p. 5), disputes Bertsimas et al.'s timing basis (p. 6) and part of their design (p. 5). **Transferable techniques**:
- Before arguing with two competing studies, tabulate their estimators, scales, sparsity and correlation structures, SNR, metrics and experiments side by side, and then their conclusions (Tables 1-2, pp. 3-4).
- Run the overall winner of one study inside the other study's protocol with the released code, and report where your reproduction of a baseline differs from the original (p. 5).
- Check a timing claim for equal tasks (one solve against a whole solution path) (p. 6), and apply the same check to your own timing figure (pp. 6, 8, 9).
- Price the whole tuning path: grid points times the per-solve limit, before cross validation (fn. 8, pp. 6-7).
- Compare configurations with identical linear scaling of (p, n, ktrue) to isolate which ratio drives a difference between studies (p. 5).
- End with a convenience-ordered decision ladder for the typical user, with explicit "avoid" cases (pp. 8-9).
