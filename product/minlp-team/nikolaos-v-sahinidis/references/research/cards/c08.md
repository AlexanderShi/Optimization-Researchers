### S165 · Optimizing the design and operation of water networks: Two decomposition approaches (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2025, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2024.108897)

**Read level**: full (accepted manuscript, pp. 1-30 read in full, including Algorithms 1-4 and Tables 1-4; references pp. 31-34 scanned). Figures 1-6 (network, diameter choices, solution configurations, pp. 23-28) survive only as captions in the extracted text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yijiang Li and Santanu S. Dey (Georgia Tech ISyE), Naresh Susarla (NETL support contractor), Miguel A. Zamarripa (NETL support contractor and NETL), Markus G. Drouven (NETL) (p. 1); funded by the DOE Office of Fossil Energy and Carbon Management (p. 30) · **Position in trajectory**: Georgia Tech period; the sequel of the gas-network paper Li, Dey & Sahinidis 2024 (JOGO; INDEX S128), whose CVXNLP decomposition is carried over to water networks (pp. 3, 13); the case is the PARETO strategic case study (Drouven et al. 2023, an open-source produced-water optimization framework whose authors include the NETL co-authors Drouven and Zamarripa) (pp. 22-23, 32). INDEX also lists a 2023 NETL report with a near-identical title (S243) and a 2026 follow-up by Choudhary, Dey & Sahinidis (S238); neither was read here.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Application-driven. Produced water from oil and gas extraction grows while disposal capacity shrinks (p. 2); its networks need multi-period modelling (production declines over time), elevation-driven pressure changes and a cap on pump stations (p. 2). Design (pipe diameters, pump placement) and operation (pump and pressure-reduction-valve schedules) are put in one model because operators are often also the designers (p. 3). The research question is then set by a solver observation: in a preliminary study BARON and SCIP kept improving the dual bound but had trouble finding feasible points (abstract p. 1; p. 11), so the paper is about primal solutions, not bounds.
  - Transferable: run the compact model through two off-the-shelf global solvers before designing anything, and let the side that stalls (here the primal side) define the contribution (p. 11).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two primal heuristics, then a hand-off to the general solver. (i) CVXNLP-based decomposition, adapted from Li et al. 2024 and Raghunathan 2013 (p. 13): a budget-constrained MINLP master whose objective comes from a convex program whose first-order conditions reduce to the pressure-drop equations (pp. 13-14), with pump and valve terms absorbed into the pressures (eq. 26, p. 14); a feasibility subproblem with the diameters fixed (pp. 14-15); an integer no-good cut when the subproblem is infeasible (eq. 34, p. 16); budget doubling on time-out (p. 16); and an initial budget search whose master only picks the cheapest diameters (pp. 16-17). Algorithm 1 gives 10 min to the budget search and 45 min per budget to the main loop (p. 17). (ii) Time decomposition: because production falls over time, solve the problem over the first T~ periods to a target gap ε with the pump-hours cap scaled to Ma·T~/T (eq. 41, p. 18), fix diameters and pump locations, and check feasibility of the remaining periods after a boundary-fixing routine for min-up/min-down states (Algorithm 2, p. 21); scan T~ and ε over sets and keep the best solution (Algorithm 3, p. 22). What is not changed: the compact MINLP (6)-(20) stays the model; the decompositions only supply an incumbent, after which SCIP runs for the rest of 5 hours (Algorithm 4, p. 24).
  - Transferable: when a general solver bounds well but finds no feasible points, write a problem-specific decomposition whose only job is an incumbent, and pass its output to the solver as a starting point (Algorithm 4, p. 24).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Steady-state flow reduced to Hazen-Williams (η = 0.852, used in the main experiments) or Darcy (η = 1) pressure-drop equations (pp. 7, 11); a pump raises pressure from v to w whatever the flow direction (p. 8); valve pressure reduction unbounded (p. 8); at most N pump stations (eq. 6, p. 8); the one-variable min-up/min-down variant, chosen because start-up costs are ignored (p. 9); pump operating cost enters only as a cap on pump-on periods (eq. 10), since costing it would need monetary values relative to the other costs (p. 9); fixed reservoir pressures, constant over time in the experiments (pp. 9, 23); objective = pipe construction cost only (eq. 12, p. 10). The time decomposition assumes production decreases over time (pp. 17-18); non-monotone production needs a modified fixing procedure (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis and devices**: n/a for proofs (no theorems). Devices: (i) a convex program whose stationarity conditions reproduce the nonconvex pressure-drop equations, used as the master objective (p. 14; properties referred to Li et al. 2024 §4.1); (ii) cost-ordered enumeration as an optimality certificate: the initial-budget master is a relaxation (cheapest diameters only), so the first configuration the feasibility subproblem accepts is optimal (p. 16); this needs each infeasibility answer to be proven, and the solver used for the subproblems is not named beyond SCIP being used in the experiments (p. 24); (iii) integer no-good cuts and budget doubling (p. 16); (iv) a physical monotonicity argument (more flow, more friction loss, so pumps are needed when production is high) that justifies the peak-period restriction (pp. 17-18); (v) proportional scaling of the pump-hours resource across the split horizon (eqs. 41, 49; pp. 18, 20) and boundary fixing of min-up/min-down states (Algorithm 2, p. 21).
  - Transferable: when the objective is construction cost only, enumerate designs in cost order with a feasibility check and no-good cuts; the first feasible design is optimal, provided every infeasibility answer is proven (p. 16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: One network derived from the PARETO strategic case study: 19 sources, 7 sinks, 29 in-nodes, 58 pipes (Table 2, p. 23); T = 24 and T = 53 periods; base demand and supply plus stress factors 0.1, 0.5, 1.5, 2.0 (p. 23); four diameters, cost proportional to circumference (p. 23). Python/Pyomo, SCIP through GAMS, i9-10900K with 64 GB (pp. 23-24); SCIP was picked after testing BARON and SCIP (p. 24). No solver versions are given except Gurobi 9.5.1 (pp. 24, 27). Baseline: SCIP on the compact model for 5 h; pipelines: heuristic, then SCIP for the rest of 5 h; reported: primal bound, dual bound, gap (eq. 52, p. 24). T = 24 (Table 3, p. 28): SCIP alone finds feasible points only at stress 1.0 and 2.0 (gaps 16.63%, 11.54%); the time decomposition reaches every scenario with gaps 0-8.92%; the CVXNLP pipeline reaches every scenario but ends worse than SCIP alone at stress 2.0 (26.23% vs 11.54%), a loss the text does not comment on (pp. 26-27); SCIP improved the CVXNLP solutions by about 37.6% and 38.5% at stress 0.5 and 1.0 (p. 26); SCIP alone keeps the best dual bounds (p. 26). T = 53 (Table 4, p. 29): SCIP alone finds no feasible point; the CVXNLP pipeline fails at stress 2.0 and ends at 86.66% and 94.77% at 1.0 and 1.5; the time decomposition stays at or below 13.53%. A single Darcy-model run (an MIQP) with Gurobi at stress 0.5 found a feasible solution in similar time and with a gap comparable to the time decomposition's, but that solution violates the Hazen-Williams model (p. 27). Not reported: the T~ and ε sets used (pp. 22-24); time to the first primal solution, although Algorithm 4 records it (pp. 24, 26; the time advantage is inferred from dual bounds instead, p. 26); repeated runs; other networks.
  - Transferable: generate an instance family from one case study by multiplying every node's base demand and supply by stress factors (p. 23), and compare solver-alone with heuristic-then-solver under the same total wall-clock budget, with primal, dual and gap per scenario (pp. 24, 28-29).
  - Transferable: when a simpler physical law makes the model easier (Darcy turns it into an MIQP), check the easy model's solution against the original law before using it (p. 27).
- **D6 Positioning**: Placed among hydraulic water-network design papers (Bragalli et al. 2012 with Bonmin; Raghunathan 2013's linearization-based branch-and-bound; Rajagopalan 2018; Pecci et al. 2019 with tailored domain reduction; Caballero & Ravagnani 2019) and pump-scheduling papers (Ghaddar et al. 2015; Bonvin et al. 2021) (pp. 4-5); min-up/min-down polytopes are borrowed from unit commitment (Ostrowski et al. 2012; Lee et al. 2004; Rajan & Takriti 2005) and time decomposition from rolling horizon (Glomb et al. 2022) (p. 6). Prior work is restated, not criticised; the novelty claimed is joining design and operation, which the literature treats separately (p. 3). Self-continuation: Li et al. 2024 for gas networks (pp. 3, 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A two-item contributions paragraph with the headline (state-of-the-art solvers struggle to find a feasible solution in the same time) in the introduction (p. 3). Constraint blocks are named once in a table (Table 1, p. 12) and each subproblem is written as a block-by-block change to the compact model (pp. 15, 19-20). Explanations of SCIP's successes are offered as belief (heuristics possibly triggered by problem structure, p. 27); the conclusion is modest (good primal solutions for most scenarios, p. 30).
  - Transferable: name the constraint blocks of the compact model once and describe every decomposition subproblem as a list of block changes (pp. 12, 15, 19-20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: application modelling plus two primal heuristics (p. 3); the dual bound still comes from SCIP (p. 24). Limits stated: for larger networks or more periods the restricted problem may be too slow, and the CVXNLP decomposition could solve it instead (p. 29); non-monotone production needs a changed fixing step (p. 20). Future work: put pump-station and valve costs into the objective (p. 30). No code or instance release is mentioned (pp. 22-30).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (reduce before you branch), step 2 (get a good incumbent early): variant ⚠. The paper diagnoses the incumbent as the bottleneck (dual bounds improve, no feasible points, p. 11) and supplies one from outside, after which SCIP improves the dual bound and sometimes the primal (p. 24); the stated reason is feasibility, not duality-based reduction. It is a co-authored source for the diagnosis that Workflow B1 step 3 says no source states.
- Method 2 (the production solver is the lab notebook): variant ⚠. BARON was tested and set aside because SCIP did slightly better (p. 24); the new methods are Pyomo/GAMS procedures outside any solver, judged against the solver alone, not by switching a module off inside a production code (pp. 23-24).
- Method 4 (enter with a benchmark): variant ⚠. A two-solver preliminary study precedes the algorithm (p. 11), but there is no public library and no certified optimum: one network, stress-factor scenarios, gaps against SCIP's dual bound (pp. 23-24, 28-29).
- Heuristic 1 and Inner Tension 1 (general vs problem-specific): evidence ✅ for the problem-specific side: tailored decompositions find feasible designs where the general solvers do not (Tables 3-4, pp. 28-29).
- Heuristic 10 (keep the engine as the problem source moves): evidence ✅. The gas-network decomposition is moved to produced-water networks under a NETL/DOE programme (pp. 3, 13, 30).
- New-pattern candidate: "decompose for the incumbent, let the general solver bound". A problem-specific decomposition supplies only the primal solution; the general solver keeps the full model and produces the dual bound (pp. 11, 24, 26).
- New-pattern candidate: "peak-period restriction". Solve the high-load sub-horizon to a target gap, fix the design, check feasibility of the rest, scanning the split point and the gap (pp. 17-22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Diagnose a primal vs a dual stall with two global solvers before choosing what to build (p. 11).
- Budget loop: MINLP master under a cost budget, feasibility subproblem with the design fixed, no-good cut on infeasibility, budget doubling on time-out (pp. 13-16).

### S170 · Model-and-search: a derivative-free local optimization algorithm (Computational Optimization and Applications 2025, doi:10.1007/s10589-025-00686-9)

**Read level**: full (pp. 1-31 read in full: algorithm §2 with Algorithms 1-10, convergence §3, illustrative example §4, setup §5, results §6, conclusions §7; references pp. 32-33 scanned). Figures 2-7 (camel6 trajectory, performance curves, ablation) survive only as captions; the numbers used here are those stated in the text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Kaiwen Ma (CMU Chemical Engineering), Luis Miguel Rios (End-to-End Analytics), Hua Zheng (Airbnb), Sreekanth Rajagopalan (The Dow Chemical Company); Sahinidis is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the DFO line from the Rios & Sahinidis 2013 review and benchmark (its 501-problem library is reused, p. 24) through Branch-and-Model, a global DFO algorithm by the same group (Ma et al. 2023, COAP, ref. [22], p. 33), to this local solver, meant to refine the solutions of global-search methods and to become Branch-and-Model's local search (pp. 3, 31). Received 10 Feb 2024, accepted 9 Apr 2025 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Bound-constrained deterministic black-box minimisation with finite bounds (eq. 1, p. 2). The entry is a role rather than a gap in theory: a local DFO solver that reaches near-local minima within a small evaluation budget, so that it can refine the output of algorithms with a broader global search component (p. 3). Prior local methods are split into model-based (DFO, NEWUOA, BOBYQA, IMFIL, RBF and randomized models) and direct search (FMINSEARCH, DAKOTA/PATTERN, GPS, MADS/NOMAD, BFO) (pp. 2-3).
  - Transferable: define a new local solver by the job it will do inside a larger method (here a refiner under a small budget) and test it at that budget (pp. 3, 28-29).
- **D2 Core idea**: A hybrid that spends information before evaluations. Each iteration collects evaluated points in an annulus φ1·r_k to φ2·r_k around the incumbent (Algorithm 8, pp. 12-14); completes a linear basis only if needed, by solving small vector problems S, PB and B (Algorithms 4-5, pp. 8-10); builds a complete linear model and then an incomplete quadratic model one term at a time, adding the pair (i, j) with the largest |d_i| + |d_j| while the interpolation matrix stays full rank (Algorithm 6, pp. 10-11); minimises the quadratic model globally with BARON over the neighbourhood (p. 11); line-searches along any improving direction (Algorithm 2, pp. 6-7); and, if nothing improves, completes a positive basis with cosine measure at least γ* (Algorithm 7, p. 12). A MAS All step builds a model from all evaluated points every C_all evaluations (pp. 13, 15). The radius grows more when one line search finds several improving points (eq. 5, p. 16). Kept from trust-region methods: radius control by success and failure; changed: freer point geometry and model form and length (pp. 3, 23). Kept from direct search: positive spanning at unsuccessful iterations; changed: the directions also serve to build a linear model (p. 3).
  - Transferable: grow a quadratic model one second-order term at a time, ranked by the gradient components the term multiplies, and stop when the interpolation system loses rank (pp. 10-11).
  - Transferable: order candidate points by their angle with the previous gradient estimate so that the first evaluations are the likely improving ones (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Theory: f continuously differentiable and bounded below (Theorem 1, p. 17), ∇f bounded on C (Theorem 2, p. 19), forcing function with ρ(x)/x → 0 (eq. 3, p. 6; ρ(x) = ρx² with ρ = 1e-5 in Table 3, p. 25). Deterministic f, box constraints only (pp. 1-2). The tests include 178 non-smooth problems out of 501 (Table 1, p. 24), which the convergence theory does not cover; the paper does not discuss this gap (pp. 16-20, 24). Free variables get bounds [−10000, 10000], tightened where the function overflows (p. 24).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Stationarity measure χ(x) from Conn, Gould & Toint (eq. 6, pp. 16-17). Theorem 1 (r_k → 0) by two contradictions, on lim inf and lim sup, using sufficient decrease against boundedness below (pp. 17-18). Proposition 1 bounds χ(x) through the polar decomposition into the cone K(x, ε) of nearby active-bound normals and its dual, with the cosine measure κ(D_k) (pp. 18-19). Theorem 2 (χ(x_k) → 0) in two cases, all trial points on or outside the boundary, or at least one interior trial point; the second uses the cosine measure, the mean value theorem and the unsuccessful-iteration inequality (19), which is written for k ∈ U (pp. 19-20). The convergence hook is a design rule: every unsuccessful iteration ends with a positive basis whose cosine measure exceeds γ* (pp. 4, 12, 22). Asymptotic only; no complexity bound.
  - Transferable: in a hybrid DFO method, put the guarantee into one checkable state at the end of every unsuccessful iteration (a positive basis with cosine measure at least γ*) and reuse the generating-set-search proof from there, leaving the other steps free (pp. 12, 17-20).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 501 bound-constrained problems from the Rios & Sahinidis 2013 library, dimension 1-300: 238 convex, 263 nonconvex, 323 smooth, 178 non-smooth (Table 1, p. 24). Rivals: DFO 2.0, BOBYQA (2009), IMFIL 1.02, DAKOTA/PATTERN 6.9, NOMAD 3.9.1, chosen as local solvers that share design ideas with MAS (Table 2, p. 24). Budget 300 evaluations; Intel Xeon 2.67 GHz under Linux; MAS defaults in Table 3 (pp. 24-25). Ten random starting points per problem, the same for every solver, scored by the median run (p. 26). A problem counts as solved when f(x0) − f_η ≥ (1 − τ)(f(x0) − f_L), with f_L the best value found by any solver in the set, a relative yardstick rather than a certified optimum (eq. 23, p. 26); data profiles at τ = 1e-6, plus 1e-5 and 1e-4 (pp. 26-27). Results: MAS 35% at 100 and 46% at 300 evaluations, NOMAD and BOBYQA 29-32% and about 41%, DFO about 10% (pp. 26-27); MAS 48% and 51% at τ = 1e-5 and 1e-4 (p. 27); at 300 evaluations convex 20%, nonconvex 69%, smooth 59%, non-smooth 21% (pp. 28-29); dimension 1-10: MAS 78% vs about 74% for BOBYQA and NOMAD; dimension 11-300: 10% (pp. 29-30). The low convex figure fits Table 1, where 185 of the 238 convex problems have 11-300 variables (p. 24; my inference). Rival wins are reported: NOMAD finds better local optima on many nonconvex problems at larger budgets, attributed to its multistart (p. 28), and model-based rivals beat direct search in high dimension (p. 30). Ablation: without model-based search MAS loses about 10%, without MAS All about 2%, the gain concentrated on smooth problems (Fig. 7, p. 31). Not reported: CPU time, including the cost of the BARON and vector-subproblem solves; how the Table 3 defaults were set; any hold-out set (pp. 24-31).
  - Transferable: benchmark from ten fixed random starts shared by all solvers, score the median run, and split data profiles by convexity, smoothness and dimension at several tolerances (pp. 26-30).
  - Transferable: ablate your own components (no model step; no all-points model) on the same profiles and split the ablation by problem type (p. 31).
- **D6 Positioning**: Synthesis rather than critique: MAS is presented as a combination of earlier features, namely reuse of evaluated points [5], incomplete and complete models [27], positive spanning [12] and line search (p. 3); the proof follows [9] (p. 16). Rivals are restricted to local solvers with shared design ideas (p. 24); Cartis et al. [6] is cited in the introduction (p. 2) but not compared. The group's own library and data page ([30], [31]) supply the test set (pp. 24, 31); the data page is printed as sahinidis.coe.gatech.edu/bbo?q=dfo, dated 9 January 2025 (p. 33), not as the /dfo address SKILL.md lists.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Ten algorithms in pseudo-code (pp. 5-16) before a short theory section (pp. 16-20); a worked 2-D example (six-hump camel) traced iteration by iteration against the figure (pp. 20-23). Claims are strong and seldom hedged: MAS outperforms the rivals over the whole evaluation range (p. 26) and is called the best local solver to use for budgets under 100 evaluations (p. 29); the camel6 run is said to demonstrate the algorithm's effectiveness (p. 23). The single-winner framing sits next to reported rival wins (pp. 28, 30).
  - Transferable: trace one small instance through the first iterations in the algorithm's own vocabulary (neighbourhood, basis completion, model step, positive basis) so readers see each routine fire (pp. 20-23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithm, convergence theory and benchmark (p. 31). Limits shown: the curse of dimensionality, about 10% or less solved at 11-300 variables by the best solvers, MAS included (pp. 29-30); bound constraints and deterministic f only (pp. 1-2). Future: MAS as the local search of a global DFO framework [22] (p. 31). Data: the problems are available from [30] and [31]; code only on request from Sahinidis (p. 31).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (enter with a benchmark; own algorithms come later, on the same library): evidence ✅. The 2013 study's 501-problem library and data page are the test bed for the group's own local solver (pp. 24, 31), with fixed starts shared by all solvers and median scoring (p. 26).
- Method 4 (step 2, certified ground truth): variant ⚠. Solved is measured against the best value found by the solver pool, not against a certified optimum (eq. 23, p. 26).
- Method 4 (steps 6-7, portfolio verdict and hold-out): contradiction ✗. The verdict is a single winner (pp. 26, 29) although NOMAD wins on nonconvex problems at larger budgets (p. 28); no hold-out set or tuning protocol for the defaults is mentioned (pp. 24-26). With Sahinidis as corresponding author (p. 1), this extends the ⚠ the skill records for his own products (Inner Tension 5) to a 2025 algorithm paper.
- Method 3 (make the black box algebraic, keep the algebra small): variant ⚠. Inside the DFO loop the local model is a small algebraic model (an incomplete quadratic holding only the terms the data support) minimised globally by BARON (pp. 10-11); the terms are chosen by gradient sensitivity, not by best-subset integer programming, and the function itself stays a black box, which is Method 3 step 7's fallback (pp. 1-2).
- Method 2 (the production solver is the lab notebook): evidence ✅, including its stated limitation. BARON is the embedded subsolver (p. 11) and the code is on request only (p. 31), as in Method 2's limitations and Inner Tension 4.
- Heuristic 8 (keep the rival's insight, replace the part that clashes): evidence ✅. Radius shrinking on failure is kept from trust-region methods while their point-geometry and model-form rules are relaxed (pp. 3, 23).
- New-pattern candidate: "exact global solve of the surrogate inside a local method". Each model step minimises a possibly nonconvex quadratic with BARON (p. 11).
- New-pattern candidate: "build the component for the engine". The local solver is designed and judged for the small budgets of a refiner inside a global DFO method (pp. 3, 28-29, 31).

**Transferable techniques**:
- One checkable end-of-iteration state (positive basis, cosine measure at least γ*) as the convergence hook of a hybrid method (pp. 12, 17-20).
- Sensitivity-ordered incomplete quadratic models, grown until rank deficiency (pp. 10-11).
- Annulus neighbourhoods (0.3 r_k to 1.5 r_k) for choosing model points (pp. 12, 25).
- Radius update that rewards several improving points in one line search (eq. 5, p. 16).
- Shared-start, median-of-ten benchmarking with profiles split by convexity, smoothness and dimension, plus component ablation (pp. 26-31).

**Quotes**:
- "MAS outperforms the other DFO solvers over the entire range of function evaluations." (p. 26)
- "Code availability Interested readers should contact N. V. Sahinidis." (p. 31)

### S189 · Constructing tight quadratic relaxations for global optimization: II. Underestimating difference-of-convex (DC) functions (Journal of Global Optimization 2025, doi:10.1007/s10898-025-01567-5; arXiv:2408.13058)

**Read level**: full, as arXiv v1 of 23 Aug 2024 (p. 1), the indexed text; the JOGO version was not read. Main text pp. 1-22 read in full; appendix pp. 22-28: Tables A1-A3 and Figure A1 read, the problem definitions in Tables A4-A8 skimmed; references pp. 29-33 scanned. Figures survive only as captions and axis labels.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: William R. Strahl (CMU Chemical Engineering and CAPD; graduate student, fellowships p. 22), Arvind U. Raghunathan (Mitsubishi Electric Research Labs), Chrysanthos E. Gounaris (CMU, corresponding author) (p. 1); funded by MERL through CMU's CAPD (p. 22) · **Position in trajectory**: Part II of a two-paper series; Part I (Strahl et al. 2024, INDEX S211, the tight-quadratic-relaxations entry under SKILL.md Latest) built the cutting-plane construction for convex functions (pp. 1, 5). SKILL.md Method 5 cites the related convex-quadratic relaxation paper for MIQPs by Nohra, Raghunathan & Sahinidis (INDEX S133); this paper does not cite it (references pp. 29-33).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a change in subsolver technology: convex QCQP solvers have become faster and more robust (Mittelmann's benchmarks, p. 2), which makes convex quadratic relaxations worth building even though they cost more than linear ones, because they bound more tightly (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). The gap named: the αBB family uses quadratics but yields a quadratic underestimator only when the function itself is quadratic (p. 3); other quadratic underestimators cover restricted classes (Su et al. 2018; Buchheim & Trieu 2013; Olama et al. 2023), are not guaranteed convex (Streeter & Dillon), or apply only to box-constrained minimisation of a single function (Ouanes et al. 2015) (p. 3). D.c. functions are chosen for generality: any twice-differentiable function on a convex set is d.c. (pp. 3-4).
  - Transferable: open a relaxation paper with the subsolver advance that changes the cost side of the trade-off, then name the function class for which no relaxation of the new type exists (pp. 1-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: For f = h − g with h, g convex, checking whether q(x; α, x0) underestimates f becomes the minimisation over B of h − (g + q), with g + q convex; a partial epigraph reformulation turns it into minimising a concave function over a convex set (eq. 4), so Part I's cutting-plane algorithm runs with only the objective changed, keeping vertices for h alone (pp. 5-6). On top of that, a hierarchy (Fig. 3, p. 15): scalar α (S); diagonal scaling of the Hessian's eigenvalues (D); full matrix scaling kept PSD by diagonal dominance (M); each updated by a small LP that maximises the quadratic at 100n Latin-hypercube points (pp. 8-11), and each with an optional downward shift γ (SS, UDS, DS, MS), with which local convexity at x0 becomes sufficient, not only necessary, for a valid quadratic underestimator (pp. 12-14). The first LP includes all sample points to avoid greedy early commitments (p. 9). Unchanged: Part I's algorithm and its convergence argument (pp. 6, 12).
  - Transferable: to extend a convex-function construction to d.c. functions, move −g into the convex part of the check and reuse the concave-minimisation cutting-plane machinery unchanged (pp. 5-6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Twice-differentiable d.c. functions with a known split h − g (p. 5) on a box (p. 5); points of construction must be locally convex (p. 6; Fig. 1, p. 7). Choices made for convenience and said to be so: diagonal dominance as a sufficient PSD condition for method M (pp. 10-11), with the full PSD cone left to future work (p. 22); α capped at 1 in the experiments, although γ > 0 with α > 1 could be tighter (p. 13); a finite sample S (100n points) that can yield an inferior underestimator (p. 10); monotone parameter updates, required for efficiency and for the convergence proof (pp. 8, 12, 22). Underestimation is enforced to a tolerance: a point triggers a correction only when f − q < −ε (p. 7), with ε = 1e-3 (pp. 15, 19); how this tolerance enters the reported bounds is not discussed.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No new theorem; validity rests on devices that keep Part I's proof applicable. (i) The d.c. partial-epigraph reformulation keeps the cutting-plane lower bounds monotonically increasing, so Part I's convergence proof, which relies on Hoffman 1981, applies unchanged (p. 6). (ii) Observation 1, monotonicity of q in α (p. 8), generalised by LP constraints A_ii ≤ A_ii^(k) and γ ≥ γ^(k) (pp. 8-14). (iii) Diagonal dominance imposed on AΛ and on (A^(k) − A)Λ, which gives convexity and monotone updates at once (pp. 10-11). (iv) Observation 2: with infinitely many sample points the LP gives the tightest parameters by construction, which motivates the sampled objective (p. 9). (v) The tightness metric (6): fractional hypervolume reduction against the linear underestimator at the same point (p. 9). LP sizes are counted: |S| + 2n + 1 and then 2n + 1 constraints for D (p. 10); n² + 2n(n − 1) variables and 4n + 5n(n − 1) + 1 constraints for M′ (p. 11).
  - Transferable: generalise a parameterised underestimator family so that every update is an LP with never-increase constraints; one monotonicity argument then covers the whole hierarchy (pp. 8-14).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: (1) Hierarchy study: 3 one- and 7 two-dimensional d.c. functions from the COCONUT collection (GlobalLib, CUTE, CSTP), d.c. splits made by algebraic identities, missing bounds assigned, ranges scaled to [−1, 1], 25 locally convex points of construction per function by Latin-hypercube sampling, ε = 1e-3; Python with HiGHS via SciPy on a 1.80 GHz i7-8565U Ubuntu 22.04 virtual machine with 8 GB RAM (pp. 15-16; Table A1, p. 23). In 2-D the metric rises from 0.384 (S) to 0.449 (D), 0.464 (M), 0.467 (UDS), 0.499 (DS) and 0.502 (MS) at 35-133 ms per underestimator (Table 1, p. 18); in 1-D all methods tie at 0.559 (pp. 16, 18); where the shift is required, every shifted method succeeds at every point, and in 2-D the curvature kept cuts the volume to SS's linear underestimator by 9.4%, 11.5% and 12.2% for UDS, DS and MS (Table 2, p. 18); the matrix methods add only 3.3% and 0.6% over the diagonal ones (p. 18). Means and standard deviations of iterations, vertices, metric, CPU time and LP solves are in Tables A2-A3 (p. 23); a pathological function (sisser) is kept in the set (p. 19). (2) Root-node study: 24 generated d.c. problems, 6 for each n = 1-4, on [−1, 1]^n (pp. 19-20). Constraints are added in the order linear, convex, d.c., each right-hand side set by binary search so that the constraint removes 20% of the remaining sampled feasible points, and a quartic linking term prevents separability (p. 20); the problems are supplied as .nl files with the points of construction (pp. 21, 23). Method DS; the convex QCQP is solved by IPOPT 3.14.13 with MUMPS 5.6.2 and compared with BARON 24.5.8 at default settings, NoutPerVar = 4 matched by four underestimators per dimension, with BARON keeping its bound tightening (p. 19). The QCQP bound is better on 5 of 6 1-D and all 18 2-4-D problems; the average root-gap reduction is 78.8% (1-D, over those 5), 92.1%, 94.4% and 94.5% (Table 3, p. 21). Time: the QCQP route is admitted to take longer than BARON, without numbers, partly blamed on the Python implementation (p. 21). Not reported: how the optimal values were obtained (pp. 19-21), any branch-and-bound run, any check that IPOPT's answer is a valid bound.
  - Transferable: generate a test library with controlled difficulty: add constraints in a fixed order, set each right-hand side by binary search to remove a fixed share of sampled feasible points, add a linking term against separability, and publish the instances (pp. 20-21).
  - Transferable: when comparing with a solver's root bound, let it keep its defaults and auxiliary techniques, match its outer-approximation density, and say the setup is unfavourable to you (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Critical but factual about αBB and its variants, which produce a quadratic underestimator only for quadratic functions (p. 3); builds on Su et al. 2018 (co-authored by Grossmann, p. 32), named as the foundational study of the series (p. 3); separates itself from αBB, McCormick, RLT and the reformulation-perspectification technique, none of which gives convex quadratic relaxations of general d.c. functions (p. 4). BARON is the state-of-the-art comparator, cited as Sahinidis 1996 (pp. 4, 19). Gounaris & Floudas 2008 appears among the αBB uses (p. 3). The group's own convex-quadratic relaxation work for MIQPs (INDEX S133) is not cited (pp. 29-33).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Four contribution bullets and a roadmap (p. 4); the hierarchy drawn as a diagram with tighter but more expensive methods lower and to the right (pp. 14-15); figures of valid and invalid construction points (Figs. 1-2, pp. 7, 13). Results are quantified with absolute and relative changes (pp. 17-18). The bound result is stated strongly (substantially superior bounds, strong evidence of tightness, p. 21); the time question is deferred to a possible compiled implementation (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Relaxation method and computational evidence (pp. 4, 21-22). Limits stated: more computing time than BARON and a Python implementation (p. 21); restriction to diagonally dominant matrices and to updates monotone in the parameters (p. 22). Limits visible but not discussed: root node only, at most four variables (pp. 19-21). Future: matrices from the whole PSD cone, parameters on the first-order term, and dropping the monotonicity requirement while keeping efficiency and convergence (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (a relaxation's strength counts only at speed), step 7 (re-test the stance when subsolvers change): evidence ✅. The paper exists because convex QCQP solvers became faster and more robust (pp. 1-2); inside the hierarchy the cost per underestimator is measured (Tables 1-2, p. 18) and DS is chosen as the trade-off between quality and tractability (p. 19).
- Method 5 (steps 1 and 5, never rank by strength without time and failures; verify every subsolver answer): contradiction ✗. The BARON comparison ranks by root bound only; time is admitted to be higher but not measured, failures are not counted, and no check of the IPOPT bound is described (pp. 19, 21).
- Method 4 (judge by certified ground truth; publish problems): variant ⚠. Because d.c. benchmarks are scarce, a library is generated and published as .nl files (pp. 19-21, 23), but the source and certification of the optimal values are not stated (pp. 19-21).
- Method 2 (the production solver is the lab notebook): variant ⚠. The idea is judged against BARON's default root bound (p. 19), not built into BARON and switched off there; it is a Python prototype (p. 21).
- Heuristic 4 (each baseline in its strongest fair mode): evidence ✅. BARON at defaults, with its bound tightening and a matched outer-approximation density (p. 19).
- New-pattern candidate: "controlled-tightness instance generator". Test problems built by adding constraints in a fixed order, each removing a fixed share of sampled feasible points (p. 20).
- New-pattern candidate: "relaxation ladder with monotone LP updates". Nested parameterisations (scalar, diagonal, matrix; with or without shift) where every update is an LP that may never raise the quadratic, so one convergence proof covers all (pp. 8-14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn the check whether q underestimates f = h − g into concave minimisation over a convex set by a partial epigraph on h (pp. 5-6).
- A downward shift as the fix when the tangent at a locally convex point is not a global underestimator (pp. 12-13; Fig. 2, p. 13).
- Seed the first parameter LP with all sample points, later LPs with the violated point only (pp. 9-10).
- A hypervolume-reduction metric against the linear underestimator at the same point as the measure of tightness (p. 9).
- Controlled-difficulty problem generation plus release of the instances and points of construction as .nl files (pp. 20-21).
