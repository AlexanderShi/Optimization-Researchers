### S190 · New bounds and formulations for the deterministic global optimization of Lennard–Jones clusters (Journal of Global Optimization 2025, doi:10.1007/s10898-025-01476-7)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-18 read in full, all proofs included; reference list pp. 19-20 skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Kuznetsov (first author, Georgia Tech ChBE), N. V. Sahinidis (corresponding) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Euclidean-norm convexification line, AFOSR-funded (acknowledgements, p. 18); the molecular-cluster theme that 01-publications traces to 1992-93 talks (its reading of a 30-year deferral is marked inferred there); cites a Kuznetsov & Sahinidis SIAM Review paper as "Under review" (ref. 37, p. 20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The abstract opens with the physics question (minimum-energy configuration of N identical atoms) and names the gap: heuristics find low local minima, but certifying global optimality has stayed out of reach for all but the smallest clusters (abstract, p. 1). Putative optima sit in the Cambridge Cluster Database; the only earlier deterministic method (Maranas & Floudas 1992) produced incorrect certificates because of a non-physical parameter choice in the dual bound (p. 3). Stake: argon and fullerene clusters where experiment and putative optima disagree (e.g. N = 38, 75), which a certificate could confirm or rule out (p. 3). Problem stated as the NLP (2) with the standard fixings and x-ordering (p. 3).
  - Transferable: pick a class with a public database of heuristic "putative" optima and a documented wrong certificate; the contribution is a certificate for small instances, not a better point.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Three additions handed to general spatial B&B solvers, no new solver: (i) optimality-based upper bounds on the cluster diameter that improve Blanc's r^U ≤ N (Theorem 4.1 of ref. 11) by an iterative slice argument (§2, pp. 4-8); (ii) a lexicographic symmetry-breaking MINLP (8) with N(N−1) binaries u_ij, v_ij, big-M with M = r^U, and transitivity inequalities (Table 2) (§3, pp. 9-10); (iii) the convex envelope of V_LJ in coordinate space over one orthant intersected with distance bounds (Theorem 3), next to the 1-D distance-space envelope (Prop. 2) (§4, pp. 11-14). The envelopes become C cutting-plane routines inside BARON (p. 15). Kept unchanged: formulation (2) and off-the-shelf solvers (pp. 3, 14-15).
  - Transferable: derive the envelope on the node shape that branching actually produces (coordinate differences confined to one orthant by the binaries and spatial branching), not on a generic box (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Scaled potential with minimum −1 at r = 1, convexoconcave, increasing for r ≥ 1 (p. 2). The diameter argument needs every x-slice occupied at an optimum (Blanc's monotonicity argument, p. 4) and the best putative value V^put_N as incumbent (p. 7). The N = 5 sublevel bound needs a primal-dual energy gap below 1, so it fails for N ≥ 6 with current bounds (pp. 8-9). Theorem 3 assumes r^L ≤ 1 ≤ r^U and a single orthant (p. 12). Computations use squared distances d_ij (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Proposition 1: closed-form optimum of the 0-1 QP (4) that assigns atoms to slices: fix one atom per slice by symmetry, bound free-free pairs by −1, and move a free atom to the centre slice by an exchange argument (pp. 6-7, Fig. 3); Algorithm 1 decrements x_N while V*(x_N) > V^put_N (pp. 7-8). Interval-arithmetic sublevel set: with V^put_5 = −9.103852 and 10 pairs, every pair has V_LJ ≤ −0.103852, so r_ij ∈ [0.894922, 1.629842] (p. 8). Prop. 2: 1-D envelope of a convexoconcave function through a tangent root (12), "standard techniques" of the 2002 book (p. 11). Theorem 3: four-region envelope, proved in three steps (convexity by the gradient inequality region by region; underestimation; maximality by convex combinations of epigraph points) (pp. 12-13). Gap-closed measure (14) (p. 14). Aggregation of all M-subset inequalities (15) with the certified V*_6 into a per-atom bound (17) (p. 17).
  - Transferable: an optimality-based bound by contradiction: lower-bound the objective of any solution that violates the bound by a relaxed combinatorial problem with a closed-form solution, compare with the incumbent, tighten, repeat (pp. 4-8).
- **D5 Experiments**: The table has one instance (N = 5): formulations (2) and (8), old vs new distance bounds, with and without the Table-2 cuts, on BARON 24.5.10, Gurobi 11.0.1, SCIP 8.1 and LINDOGLOBAL 3.17P, GAMS 46.4.0, Xeon E5-1620 3.50 GHz, 16 GB, single thread (pp. 14-15; Table 3, p. 16). ANTIGONE 1.1 is named among the benchmarked solvers (p. 14) but has no row in Table 3 (p. 16), without comment. The time limit is not stated in the text (the 3600 s entries of Table 3 imply it); optimality tolerances are not stated. Only BARON finishes; the other three end with 3.1-50.5% gaps (p. 16). Bound and formulation effects are small and mixed, and the table shows the losses: BARON on (2) takes 396 s with the new bounds vs 378 s with the old; SCIP on (8) with cuts goes from a 12.0% to a 45.7% gap; LINDOGLOBAL on (8) without cuts from 6.3% to 9.0% (p. 16), while the text summarises slight improvements "in general" (p. 15). Cuts inside BARON on (2) with new bounds: distance-space 228 s (−42%), coordinate-space 185 s (−53%), both 194 s (−51%) (p. 15). N = 6 solved in 114,086 s (31.7 h), 588 times the N = 5 time; the reason is "not clear" (p. 17). A rerun note: under BARON 22.10.13 formulation (8) had cut run time by 85%, a gain absent under 24.5.10 (p. 15). GAMS models and code on GitHub (p. 18).
  - Transferable: print the full factorial (formulation × bounds × cuts × solver) including the cells that got worse, and say when a gain measured on an older solver version disappeared (Table 3, p. 16; p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Blanc's diameter bound (p. 4) and the lower-bound line (Xue, Vinkó, Schachinger et al., Yuhjtman; p. 3); corrects Maranas & Floudas directly (p. 3); treats the heuristic literature as the supplier of putative optima (p. 3). The factorable relaxation of Tawarmalani & Sahinidis (2004) is the baseline the new envelope beats (p. 14); symmetry framed through Liberti and the authors' SIAM Review submission (p. 9; ref. 37, p. 20).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Question-first abstract; contributions in one sentence (p. 3). The abstract's claim that the results "enable the proof of global optimality" for N = 5 and 6 by a general-purpose solver (p. 1) rests on BARON runs whose tolerances are not stated (pp. 14-17). Candid null results ("difficult to discern", p. 15) and hedges ("The precise reason is not clear", p. 17; "likely still significant room for improvement", p. 8). Conflict of interest disclosed: the second author holds a financial interest in the BARON company (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Layers: theory (bounds, envelope), formulation, solver cuts, two first certificates (N = 5, 6; abstract and p. 4) and new energy lower bounds for 7 ≤ N ≤ 35 (Fig. 7, pp. 17-18). Limits written in: the diameter bound grows linearly in N (slope about 0.798) while putative diameters grow like N^(1/3) (p. 8); the sublevel argument fails for N ≥ 6 (pp. 8-9); symmetric solutions survive formulation (8) (p. 10); the coordinate-space envelope is valid only in single-orthant nodes (p. 14); the new per-atom bound is weaker than the stability constant beyond 34 atoms (p. 17). Future: stronger diameter bounds, and symmetry elimination that handles Euclidean and permutational symmetry together (pp. 17-18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the first contribution is domain reduction driven by the incumbent: diameter bounds proved by comparing a relaxed energy bound with V^put (pp. 4-8) and, for N = 5, distance bounds from the incumbent energy by interval arithmetic that work only while the energy gap is below 1 (pp. 8-9), a problem-specific case of step 2 (duality-based reductions scale with U − L). The measured effect of the new bounds inside the solvers is small and mixed (Table 3, p. 16).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the envelope is judged first by gap closed against the factorable and the 1-D relaxations (Fig. 6, eq. 14, pp. 14-15), then by run time inside BARON; tightness is weighed against availability (distance-space cuts exist at every node, coordinate-space cuts only in single-orthant nodes, p. 14), and both cut families together (194 s) are slower than coordinate cuts alone (185 s) (p. 15).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the cuts land in BARON as C routines (p. 15), versions are named (p. 14) and an older-version result is reported as no longer holding (p. 15); but the protocol is not the 500 s / 10^-6 / X5650 one: E5-1620 3.50 GHz, an implied 3600 s limit, unstated tolerances, a single instance (pp. 14-16), and a named solver (ANTIGONE) missing from the table (pp. 14, 16).
- Heuristics 1 and 2 ✅ evidence: small instances defeat today's solvers (three of the four reported solvers time out on N = 5, p. 16), and problem knowledge (bounds, symmetry, envelope) is handed to general-purpose solvers (abstract; p. 3).
- Heuristic 7 ⚠ variant (Tension 7): "proof of global optimality" wording (abstract) for floating-point B&B runs with unstated tolerances (pp. 14-17).
- New-pattern candidate *report the vanished gain*: an 85% speed-up from a formulation under BARON 22.10.13 that is gone under 24.5.10 is reported with a hypothesis instead of being dropped (p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate *certified small instance as a bound for larger ones*: V*_6 aggregated over all 6-atom subsets bounds clusters with 7 ≤ N ≤ 35 (eqs. 15-17, Fig. 7, pp. 17-18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove a domain bound by contradiction against the incumbent through a relaxed assignment problem with a closed-form optimum; decrement until the relaxed bound no longer exceeds the incumbent (Prop. 1, Algorithm 1, pp. 6-8).
- When the primal-dual gap is smaller than the best value of a single term, derive per-term variable bounds from the sublevel set by interval arithmetic (p. 8).
- Break permutation symmetry with binary order variables plus transitivity inequalities, then re-test the formulation on the current solver version (Table 2, p. 10; p. 15).
- Map "percentage of gap closed" (eq. 14) over the domain against each competing relaxation before timing any cut (Fig. 6, pp. 14-15).
- Aggregate all M-subset inequalities with a certified M-instance optimum to bound larger instances (eqs. 15-17, p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "A deterministic global optimization method was developed in [24], but a non-physical choice of an algorithmic parameter in the dual bound calculation led to incorrect optimality certificates" (p. 3)
- "It is possible that part of this run time reduction was due to the spatial branching induced by branching on the binary variables, and that advances in spatial branching over the last few years have rendered this technique no longer necessary." (p. 15)

### S205 · Algorithm-Guided Experimentation for Optimization of High-Performance Perovskite Solar Cells (ACS Energy Letters 2025, doi:10.1021/acsenergylett.5c02477)
**Read level**: full for the letter (pp. 1-7 read in full; references pp. 8-10 skimmed). The Supporting Information (experimental Methods, SNOBFIT procedure and settings, Figs. S1-S6, Table S1) is not in the file: not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: D. Oh (first author, ChBE), S. Kim and C. A. R. Perini (Materials Science and Engineering), J.-P. Correa-Baena and N. V. Sahinidis (corresponding) (pp. 1, 7-8) · **Position in trajectory**: the DFO line of the 2013 benchmark and of BAM and HybridTuner (refs. 62, 76, 77, pp. 9-10) taken into a Georgia Tech materials lab; 06-trajectory lists 2026 talks on adaptive learning for self-driving laboratories.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: PSC optimization still runs largely by manual trial and error (p. 1). ML trained on compiled literature data inherits poor cross-lab reproducibility, and one lab's data are too few for complex models (pp. 1-2). BO, the usual choice, scales poorly with the number of variables (citing Rios & Sahinidis 2013) and has been applied to the perovskite or a single layer, not to interlayer interactions across the device stack (p. 2). Entry: state-of-the-art DFO solvers have not been used on fully assembled devices (p. 2).
  - Transferable: enter an experimental field by naming the scope gap of the dominant method (one layer, few variables) rather than claiming a better algorithm in general (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A batch loop: pick directly controllable processing variables and ranges, sample initially, fabricate and measure, feed median PCE to SNOBFIT, fabricate its suggestions (Fig. 1a, pp. 2-4). SNOBFIT is chosen for its local surrogate models and noise handling (p. 4). Schedule: in the first seven iterations, SNOBFIT's random initial sampling and size-based search of the largest subdomains, with one model-based point in each of iterations 2-7; in the last iteration a 0.67:0.33 model-based to size-based ratio (p. 5). The design space grows by layer: 2 perovskite variables (first-step spin rate [500, 1500] rpm, antisolvent quenching time [11, 27] s, resolution vector [1 rpm, 1 s], p. 4) → 4 with two ETL variables (ethanol-to-titanium ratio 10:1-20:1, gas flow 3, 4 or 5 L/min; 17 Latin hypercube points, p. 6) → 6 with two HTL variables (p. 7). Chemical ingredients and device configuration are not changed (abstract, p. 1; p. 2).
  - Transferable: grow the design space one device layer at a time, re-optimizing the earlier variables jointly with the new ones (pp. 4-7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Black box: PCE of fabricated devices, nonconvex and noisy (p. 5). Bounds and resolution set by the experimenters (p. 4); the resolution vector restricts spin rate and quenching time to whole numbers (p. 4), and the flow rate takes three levels (p. 6). Noise handled by replication and medians, not by a noise model (p. 4). Experimenters may move the bounds between batches (p. 4). The HTL step is not run through SNOBFIT: the three additives are raised or lowered together by 20% and the precursor solution is heated to 75 °C (p. 7); the authors plan to "integrate them into the systematic optimization problem" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a (experimental letter, no analysis). Design devices only: duplicate devices with eight pixels each, up to 24 pixels per condition, median PCE as the output (p. 4); SNOBFIT resolution vector (p. 4); fixed search-mix schedule (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: Fewer than 100 tested designs over the whole 2D→6D campaign (p. 7). 2D: best design (1289 rpm, 12 s); median PCE 19.6% → 20.2%, maximum 20.3% → 20.8%; fill factor below the reference (pp. 4-5, Fig. 3). 4D: the improved design (1300 rpm, 15 s, ratio 13.5, 4 L/min) was found during the 17-point Latin hypercube initial sampling; median 21.8%, maximum 22.3%; more data "required to continue optimization" (p. 6). 6D, with HTL changes made by hand: maximum 22.8%, 23.1% with an antireflective coating (p. 7, Table 1). GIWAXS and optical microscopy of high-, medium- and low-efficiency films point to surface morphology (p. 6). No comparison arm (no BO, design-of-experiments or manual-search run on the same devices) and no repeated campaign is reported (pp. 4-7); comparisons are to the lab's reference device (Table 1, p. 7) and, in the introduction, to other groups' BO results (p. 2).
  - Transferable: give a per-stage table (degrees of freedom, layer, variables, PCE, VOC, JSC, FF, stabilized efficiency) so readers see which stage produced the gain (Table 1, p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: BO precedents (MacLeod, Liu, Xu, Zhang) cited with their dimensions and test counts (p. 2). The DFO case rests on self-citations: Rios & Sahinidis (2013) for BO scaling, Ma et al. (2023, BAM) among state-of-the-art DFO solvers, Sauk & Sahinidis (2023, HybridTuner) for DFO beating BO in high dimension (p. 2); SNOBFIT's noise performance on a 2014 CMU thesis (Amaran, ref. 83, p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Letter format with the headline in the abstract: reverse-scanning PCE from 20.3% to 23.1% without altering chemical ingredients or device configuration (p. 1); the 23.1% includes an antireflective coating, and the summary gives both numbers (22.8% without, 23.1% with, p. 7). Hedged physics ("may be beneficial", p. 6). The framework is presented as general, "independent of specific techniques or manufacturers" (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: an application demonstration with a measured device gain (pp. 5-7). Limits stated: the 4D search is unfinished (p. 6); the HTL variables are not yet in the optimization (p. 7). Limits not stated (my reading): no baseline arm or repeat; the 4D gain appeared in initial sampling (p. 6), so the share due to model-based search is not separated. Future: more degrees of freedom, characterization of optimal and suboptimal designs, ML on the collected data afterwards (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the black box is kept black: SNOBFIT's local surrogates steer fabrication, no algebraic model is fitted, and ML is deferred to post-hoc analysis (pp. 4, 7). This is step 7's fallback (deterministic DFO when the function stays black-box) with the scarce-data argument against complex ML (pp. 1-2); the 2-6 variables carry no discrete design decisions beyond dial resolution and a three-level flow rate (pp. 4, 6).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the solver is picked from the group's earlier benchmarks (refs. 62, 77, 83; pp. 2, 4), as in step 7; but the campaign has no comparison arm and no repeat, and success is measured against the lab's reference device (pp. 5-7, Table 1).
- Heuristic 10 ✅ evidence: the DFO engine moves to a new problem source, a Georgia Tech materials group (p. 1).
- SKILL.md anti-pattern of algorithms detached from how experiments run, avoided ✅ (text only): the resolution vector matches dial settings, and the batch loop leaves room for lab judgment between batches (p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate *grow the design space by layer*: optimize one layer's variables, then add the next layer's and re-optimize jointly (pp. 4-7, Table 1).
- New-pattern candidate *replicate-and-median noise handling*: the optimizer sees only medians over up to 24 pixels per condition (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Restrict a DFO solver's proposals to settings the lab can dial, through its resolution vector (p. 4).
- Feed back the median over duplicate devices and pixels as the objective value of a design (p. 4).
- Fix the search-mix schedule in advance: space-filling batches with one model-based point each, then a stated model-based to size-based ratio (p. 5).
- Characterize high-, medium- and low-performing designs to locate where the gain comes from (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "The batch-based experimentation provided flexibility to make decisions between batches whether to continue sampling within the same bounds or to adjust the input space when certain regions show significantly lower performance than the rest of the design space." (p. 4)
- "We were able to identify a design with an improved median PCE during this sampling" (p. 6)

### S206 · Surrogate modeling and optimization of the leaching process in a rare earth elements recovery plant (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2025, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2025.109061; read as author manuscript)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-28 read in full; nomenclature, acknowledgment, disclaimer and code availability pp. 28-32 read; references pp. 32-36 skimmed). Version read: a 36-page author manuscript; the journal typeset version was not read. The online supplementary material (Fardis et al. 2024, GitHub) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: D. M. Fardis (first author) and D. Oh (Georgia Tech ChBE), N. V. Sahinidis, A. Garciadiego and A. Lee (NETL) (p. 1); no corresponding-author mark in the file · **Position in trajectory**: the ALAMO line (Cozad et al. 2014; Wilson & Sahinidis 2017; p. 10) applied under the DOE PrOMMiS initiative (acknowledgment, p. 31; Tarka et al. 2024 lists Sahinidis among its authors, p. 35).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a missing comparison: no systematic study of surrogate quality for complex critical-mineral and REE processes (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Data are scarce and leaching experiments slow, so the surrogate must work from few points (p. 2). Surrogate-selection aids exist (LEAPS2; selection rules of Williams & Cremaschi; a random-forest recommender by Williams et al. that focuses on relatively smooth functions), which motivates testing on a realistic process model (p. 3). DFO algorithms do not scale well with problem size (p. 3, citing Rios & Sahinidis 2013 and Ploskas & Sahinidis 2022). Stated aim: compare SBO with EO and DFO (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A study design, not a new algorithm. One leaching unit of the PrOMMiS flowsheet of the University of Kentucky pilot plant (shrinking-core kinetics fitted to eight long pilot runs, eq. 1, p. 6), 2 inputs inside the fitted range (Table 1, p. 7), 30 outputs (Tables 3-4, p. 8). Six techniques (ANN; LASSO with LassoLarsIC and BIC; ALAMO linear, quadratic and POLY-3 with BIC at defaults; PySMO polynomials, Gaussian RBF and kriging at defaults) (pp. 9-15). Surrogates enter problem (10) as equality constraints (p. 12), via OMLT for ANNs (full- and reduced-space for tanh and sigmoid; big-M and complementarity for ReLU) and IDAES for the rest, solved by BARON with a 500 s limit (p. 15). EO: BARON on the full PrOMMiS equations (p. 16). DFO: MCS, chosen as "the best open-source DFO solver" of the 2013 comparison (p. 16). Objective: total REE mass flow in the liquid outlet (p. 12).
  - Transferable: compare surrogate families by what happens after training: embed each in the same global solver and record solve time, feasibility and the true-objective deviation (pp. 15-16, 23-26).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Simulator taken as ground truth inside its fitted range (p. 7); fixed feed composition (Table 2, p. 7); data scaled to [0, 1] (p. 13). Data generation solves the square system with BARON because IPOPT 3.13.2 failed in over 15% of runs with incorrect infeasibility claims (p. 13). A separate 1000-point LHS test set is called unbiased "because of its density" (p. 13). ANN hyperparameters by grid search with 5-fold cross-validation, chosen to "reflect the typical approach taken by most users" (pp. 13-14), conceding that simpler ANNs with slightly higher loss might exist (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a (no theorems). Evaluation devices: R², RMSE and number of terms on the test set (p. 16); relative deviation between the true objective at the surrogate optimum and the surrogate's prediction (eq. 11, p. 16); the EO optimum from a global solver as ground truth (pp. 16, 23).
- **D5 Experiments**: Training sets of 10, 50, 100, 500 and 1000 LHS points, one independent 1000-point test set (p. 13). Accuracy: nonlinear surrogates R² > 0.99, linear ones about 0.98 on average (p. 17); ANNs need at least 50 points to match quadratics built from 10 (p. 21). Cost: LASSO and ALAMO linear and quadratic train in under a second, ALAMO POLY-3 in at most 2.1 s, ALAMO up to two orders of magnitude faster than PySMO, complex models two to six orders slower (p. 18); ANN, kriging and RBF have up to three orders more parameters (p. 18). Optimization: true optimum 5128.2 mg REE per hour with both inputs at their upper bounds (pp. 23-24); every surrogate finds that corner with deviations below 2% (p. 24); ANN big-M finds no feasible point for 50, 100 and 500 training points; kriging and RBF hit the 500 s limit beyond 10 points; polynomial models solve in 0.02-0.11 s (pp. 24-25). Adaptive sampling with ALAMO needs 253 measurements for an excellent quadratic without the large test set (p. 26). EO 2.81 s (p. 27); MCS 919 evaluations and 464 s (p. 27). Hardware 3.5 GHz CPU, 32 GB; BARON 24.5.8 (p. 17); thread count and optimality tolerances not stated (p. 17).
  - Transferable: nested training sizes against one independent dense test set answer the question of how many samples are enough for each model family (p. 13; Fig. 3, p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Places ALAMO among ANN, kriging and RBF as reviewed by Bhosekar & Ierapetritou (pp. 2-3); builds on Schweidtmann & Mitsos for embedded ANNs and on OMLT (pp. 3, 15). Self-citations carry the DFO side (Rios & Sahinidis 2013; Ploskas & Sahinidis 2022) and the method (Cozad et al. 2014; Wilson & Sahinidis 2017) (pp. 3, 10). Code availability names PrOMMiS and IDAES (free) and minlp.com for ALAMO ("free for academics") and BARON (free through NEOS) (p. 32); no conflict-of-interest statement appears in this manuscript file (pp. 31-32), unlike S190 (p. 18) and S212 (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contribution sentence stated plainly (p. 3); "surprisingly small number of measurements" (p. 4). Headline ratios in the abstract and conclusions: two orders of magnitude fewer measurements, up to four orders faster (p. 1; p. 28). My reading of the basis: §4.6 sets SBO's optimization time (0.03 s) against the whole MCS run (464 s for 919 simulations) (p. 27); the conclusions add training, reported only as under a second (pp. 18, 28); the time to generate the 10 training simulations is not reported separately; the fewer-measurements figure rests on 10-point models whose adequacy was established with the 1000-point test set, while adaptive sampling without that set took 253 measurements against MCS's 919 (pp. 26-27).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a comparison and a recommendation (ALAMO sparse quadratics from about 10 points) for one leaching unit (pp. 21, 28). Limits (my reading of the text): two inputs; an optimum at a corner of the box, so the optimization test separates methods mainly by time (p. 24); one objective, with the assertion that the models would serve other objectives unchanged (p. 24); one DFO solver; the ANN baseline in typical-user mode (pp. 13-14). Future: larger flowsheets and supply chains (p. 28); the benefits "are expected to be significantly greater" at flowsheet scale (p. 27), untested here.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (core case): best-subset sparse quadratics with BIC from 10 points match the accuracy of ANN, kriging and RBF at a fraction of their training and optimization time (pp. 17-26), are embedded in a deterministic global solver (p. 12), are argued to be interpretable (p. 21), and come with adaptive sampling (p. 26) and a direct comparison against DFO on the raw simulator (p. 27).

### S211 · Constructing Tight Quadratic Relaxations for Global Optimization: I. Outer-Approximating Twice-Differentiable Convex Functions (Journal of Global Optimization 2025, doi:10.1007/s10898-025-01565-7; read as arXiv:2408.13053v1)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-18 read in full, proofs and remarks included; function tables pp. 18-21 read; references pp. 21-23 skimmed). Version read: arXiv v1 of 23 Aug 2024 (p. 1); the journal version was not read (03-process-evidence quotes journal wording of the implementation sentence that differs from p. 13 here).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: W. R. Strahl (first author, CMU), A. U. Raghunathan (MERL), N. V. Sahinidis (third author), C. E. Gounaris (corresponding) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a CMU CAPD project funded by MERL (acknowledgments, p. 21); Part II on d.c. functions follows (p. 18). Voice: Gounaris's group with Sahinidis as co-author, so evidence for his methods is co-signed, not led.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Spatial B&B codes outer-approximate convex relaxations linearly for LP speed and robustness, and linear cuts approximate highly nonlinear functions poorly (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Progress of QP and QCQP solvers (Mittelmann's benchmarks) opens an intermediate option between LP and convex NLP (p. 2). Su et al. (2018) proposed the α-scaled second-order Taylor underestimator but gave the tightest α only when the gradient of the defining ratio is coordinate-wise monotonic (p. 3); Buchheim & Trieu and Olama et al. need a Hessian bound or strong-convexity constant known in advance; AutoBound gives no convexity guarantee (pp. 3-4). Gap: no general procedure for tight convex quadratic underestimators of twice-differentiable convex functions (p. 4).
  - Transferable: enter where a published construction leaves its key parameter computable only under a special condition, and make it computable in general (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Keep Su et al.'s form q(x; α, x0) (eq. 2) and compute the largest α ∈ [0, 1] with q ≤ f on the box (problem 3, p. 5). For fixed α the test is a d.c. minimization, recast as concave minimization over the epigraph (4) (p. 5); solve it by outer approximation with vertex enumeration (Chen et al. 1991): pick the vertex with the lowest t − q, find the boundary point by bisection, add the supporting hyperplane, and whenever a new vertex shows overestimation lower α to the value at which q touches f there; stop when LB > −ε (Fig. 1 and Steps 1-7, pp. 6-7). Extension: intersect the box with the problem's linear constraints, so the underestimator may overestimate in infeasible regions (pp. 15-16).
  - Transferable: turn the search for the tightest parameter into one monotone outer-approximation run that updates the parameter while it enumerates (pp. 5-7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f twice differentiable and convex on a box (p. 5); low dimension (functions of 1-4 variables in the study, p. 12); α ≤ 1, or the Taylor form would overestimate near x0 (p. 5). Assumption 1: α is lowered at least once, which the authors say generally holds; they name two exceptions, a too-large ε, or every Hessian in the domain dominating the one at x0 (p. 9). Termination allows overestimation of at most ε; subtracting the final lower bound restores a valid underestimator (Remark 4, p. 8). The extension needs the constraints' variables inside f's domain and a feasible x0 (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Observation 1: q decreases monotonically as α decreases (p. 6), which lets the algorithm drop vertices that already underestimate (Remark 5, p. 8). Convergence taken from Hoffman (1981): Lemmas 2-3 reused, Lemma 1 re-proved for the changing objective from shrinking outer approximations plus Observation 1 (pp. 8-9). Property 1 (at least two coincidence points) and Property 2 (the largest α consistent with all enumerated vertices, by contradiction) (pp. 9-10); Remark 11 explains why ε is kept out of the α update (p. 10). Degenerate vertices removed by perturbing b (Remark 7, p. 8). Qualitative convergence analysis: iteration counts depend on (i) distance from a quadratic and (ii) how well hyperplanes approximate the function: x1² + x2² takes 1387 iterations vs 45 and 36 for 4th and 6th powers; increasingly "more linear" quadratics take 1387, 804 and 46 (pp. 10-11, Figs. 2-3).
  - Transferable: when adapting a classical convergence proof to a moving objective, re-prove only the monotonicity lemma and reuse the separation lemmas unchanged (pp. 8-9).
- **D5 Experiments**: Library from the 233 MINLPLib problems with CONVEX==TRUE and ProblemType ∈ {MBNLP, MINLP}; bounds from Pyomo fbbt at defaults; separable expressions decomposed; over 9,000 candidate expressions of at most 4 dimensions reduced to 19 representative forms; 12 more forms from COCONUT (GlobalLib, CUTE, CSTP) with assigned bounds; 31 functions in all (pp. 11-13; Tables 2-5, pp. 18-21). Each scaled to [−1, 1], ε = 10^-3 (so relative), 5 LHS construction points each (p. 13). Metric: fractional hypervolume reduction against the linear underestimator at the same point (eq. 5), integrated on 100d LHS points (p. 13). Results: average metric 0.533, 0.575, 0.399 and 0.354 for 1-4 dimensions, with ranges down to 0.000; average CPU 4-51 ms, maximum 182 ms; up to 1951 vertices in 4D (Table 1, p. 14). Python on a laptop virtual machine (p. 13). The only baseline is the linear tangent: none of the quadratic constructions discussed on pp. 3-4 is run, and no B&B or OBBT run uses the new underestimators (§3). The extension is shown on one 2-D example (α 0.3456 → 0.4351 → 0.5261 as two constraints are added, p. 16).
  - Transferable: measure a new underestimator by hypervolume reduction against the standard cut at the same construction point, on functions whose source instance, constraint and bounds are tabulated (eq. 5, p. 13; Tables 2-5).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Su et al. (2018) directly, keeping their form and closing their open case (p. 3); contrasts with Buchheim & Trieu (reusable Q, cheap, "not as tight as possible"), Olama et al. (diagonal Hessian, restricted classes) and Streeter & Dillon (no convexity guarantee; analytic results for univariate cases) (pp. 3-4). Cites Tawarmalani & Sahinidis (2005) for linear outer approximation and Puranik & Sahinidis (2017) for OBBT and fbbt (pp. 2, 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Three bulleted contributions (p. 4); a generality claim covering "all contexts where outer approximation is relevant" (p. 3); "to the best of our knowledge" for the gap (p. 4). Limits voiced plainly: exponential vertex growth as "the main tractability limitation" (p. 14), a Python implementation with acknowledged compiled-language gains (p. 13), a "rudimentary implementation" (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: an algorithm with convergence and two properties, a function library, and an extension with linear constraints (pp. 4, 17-18). Limits stated: one scalar α scales every Hessian direction, so quality falls with dimension (p. 14); vertex enumeration grows exponentially (p. 14); exact quadratics are slow cases and should be detected and skipped (p. 11). Not addressed in this version: whether quadratic node problems pay for their extra cost in B&B or OBBT run time (§3 measures tightness and construction time only). Future: d.c. functions in Part II (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the premise is step 7 (re-test the linear-only stance as QP/QCQP solvers improve, p. 2) and step 3 (a cheaper object between LP cuts and convex NLP); tightness and construction cost are measured (Table 1, p. 14) but not the bound per second of quadratic node relaxations or their failure rate (no in-solver test, §3).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (weak): OBBT over a quadratic outer approximation is named as an application (p. 2), and fbbt supplies realistic bounds before any underestimation (pp. 11-12).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: public libraries with stated admission filters and full provenance (pp. 11-13, Tables 2-5), but the only baseline is the linear tangent at the same point; the competing quadratic constructions of pp. 3-4 are not run.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (co-authored, Gounaris-led): a Python prototype on a laptop (p. 13), not a module switched on and off inside a production solver.
- New-pattern candidate *underestimate only where feasible*: let the cut overestimate outside the polyhedron of known linear constraints, derived cuts included (pp. 15-16).
- New-pattern candidate *distil a benchmark library into representative functional forms*: extract, decompose and deduplicate expressions, recording instance, constraint, location and bounds (pp. 11-13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Cast the test whether q underestimates f on B as concave minimization over the epigraph and solve it by vertex-enumeration outer approximation (pp. 5-7).
- Use monotonicity in the parameter to prune vertices that can never violate again (Observation 1, Remark 5, pp. 6, 8).

- Scale every function into [−1, 1] so one ε acts as a relative tolerance across the library (p. 13).
- Show an extension on a 2-D example with constraints added one at a time, giving the parameter after each (p. 16, Figs. 6-7).
