### S239 · A novel linear optimization presolve technique based on Fourier-Motzkin elimination (Mathematical Programming Computation 18:345-378, 2026, doi:10.1007/s12532-026-00316-3)

**Read level**: full (all 34 file pages: text, Algorithms 1-5, Examples 1-2, Tables 1-14, Figs. 1-6 viewed in the PDF; reference list pp. 32-34 skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yi Zhang (The Optimization Firm), Nikolaos Ploskas (University of Western Macedonia); Sahinidis listed with The Optimization Firm and Georgia Tech (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the "own method later" end of the LP-presolve line that SKILL.md Method 4 dates to 2008-09; the code is "embedded in the BARON solver" (p. 32); submitted 20 March 2024, accepted 17 March 2026, handled by Jonathan Eckstein as Editor-in-Chief (p. 1) (SKILL.md lists Sahinidis as an editor-in-chief of this journal, so the venue routed his paper to another editor: inference); SKILL.md Method 2 records that this presolve shipped in BARON 2023.11.4, before submission.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: LP presolve is established and pays (over tenfold speed-ups reported for CPLEX and Gurobi, citing [1, 9], p. 3). Fourier-Motzkin elimination (FME), the classical projection algorithm, "has not been utilized as a presolve method in existing solvers due to its exponential row growth" (abstract, p. 1): eliminating x_j removes m+_j + m−_j rows and adds m+_j × m−_j (p. 5). Earlier redundancy rules (Chernikov, Kohler, Imbert), bit-pattern trees (Bastrakov et al.) and Balas-projection variants (Jing et al.) still "cannot guarantee a size reduction for a general LP" (p. 3; Theorems 1-3 and the closing paragraph of §2, p. 6). The pull comes from his own solver class: global MINLP solvers such as SCIP and BARON solve "a vast number of LPs" and so benefit from better LP presolve (p. 20).
  - Transferable: revisit a textbook algorithm that practice dropped because of a known blow-up, and ask whether a controlled, partial use of it pays as preprocessing (pp. 3, 6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Use FME partially and greedily, as one more presolve routine. Candidates are only the columns that appear solely in inequality rows (J≤), because FME on equality columns amounts to Gaussian elimination and duplicates aggregation and substitution (p. 7). Before each step the nonzeros of the new rows are predicted, ne_new = m+ ne− + m− ne+ − Σ_k p+_k p−_k (eq. 4, p. 8); the variable with the largest predicted nonzero reduction is eliminated (eq. 6), ties at zero are broken by the row reduction m+ + m− − m+ m− (eq. 7), and FME stops when every candidate is predicted to grow the model (Algorithm 1, p. 9; Algorithm 2, p. 10). Redundant new rows are dropped by bound propagation (Puranik & Sahinidis 2017), which also detects infeasibility (p. 8). Postsolve recovers primal values (zero if the inferred interval contains zero, else the bound nearest zero; §3.5.1 and Algorithm 3, pp. 10-11), duals (Algorithm 4, p. 12) and a basis (§3.5.3 and Algorithm 5, pp. 13-14). Changed: FME becomes a growth-controlled presolve step inside an ordered presolve (fast row/column → medium block → FME → aggregation → substitution, the expensive ones once per round; p. 17). Unchanged: the elimination step and the redundancy theorems, cited rather than re-proved (pp. 4-6).
  - Transferable: when a reduction can blow up, compute a cheap estimate of its size effect per candidate, apply the best, and stop when none is predicted to shrink the model (eqs. 4-7, Algorithms 1-2, pp. 8-10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: LP min cᵀx s.t. Ax ≤ b with bounds folded into the rows (p. 2); the objective becomes a row z ≥ cᵀx (p. 4). The size prediction assumes no new row is redundant (pp. 7-8); the cheaper estimate (5), without the Σ p+p− term, "may miss the variable with the maximum reduction" (p. 8). Numerical guards: FME only on variables whose coefficients lie between 10^-3 and 10^3, to avoid tiny coefficients and bound crossings in postsolve (§4.4, p. 19); each combined row is scaled by s = a_ij a_i'j / (a_ij + a_i'j) so that the user's feasibility tolerance δ keeps its meaning (§4.3, p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: No new theorem. Devices: Kohler's view of FME as projection and the Chernikov/Kohler redundancy and acceleration theorems, stated with sources (Theorems 1-3, pp. 5-6); a constructive dual-feasibility argument: from the duals α of the projected system, u_i = Σ α over the rows generated from row i (and v likewise) gives a feasible dual of the original (pp. 11-12); basis postsolve as the implicit inverse of a row-operation transform T, with the slack identity s_ii' = s_i/a_ij − s_i'/a_i'j and four propagation rules (nonbasic, basic, free variable, vanished constraint) (p. 13). That the recovered basis is nonsingular and primal-dual feasible is asserted, not proved (p. 13). One example carries the method end to end: Example 1 (pp. 4-5) returns as Example 2 with an objective, through elimination order, primal, basis and dual recovery (pp. 14-16).
  - Transferable: thread one small example through the whole paper, from elimination order to dual and basis postsolve, so every rule is checked on numbers (pp. 4-5, 14-16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The authors state that some algorithmic and implementation choices were made after experiments on the same collection of more than 1,900 LPs that §5 reports (p. 16). The order of the expensive routines (aggregation A, substitution S, FME F) was chosen on FME success rate and size reductions over five row/column-permuted copies of each LP with distinct seeds: A-S-F has the highest success rate (62.3%) and removes the most rows and nonzeros but the fewest columns; F-A-S/F-S-A, with FME first and among the top three in eliminated variables, were chosen (Tables 1-2, pp. 17-18). Test set: 1,931 LPs after removing infeasible and duplicate ones: 78 MittelmannLib, 355 MeszarosLib, 142 NetLib, 1,356 MINLPrLPLib, a new library of SCIP 8.0 root LP relaxations of MINLPLib problems published at minlp.com (p. 20). Protocol: BARON 25.3 parses the LPs into a standalone presolve module FMEpre; CPLEX 22.1.1, 3,600 CPU s, feasibility and gap tolerances 10^-6, Intel Xeon X5650 (p. 20); thread count not stated. Study (i): FMEpre with vs without FME, about 5% extra reduction in columns, rows and nonzeros over all problems (Fig. 1, p. 21). Study (ii): FMEpre applied to CPLEX-presolved models and fed back to CPLEX, because CPLEX's code is closed (p. 21); FME changes 1,114 problems (6%, 53%, 29%, 65% of the four libraries) and removes a further 8-10% on them (Tables 3-4, p. 21; p. 23). Stronger baseline: CPLEX's aggregator at 2× and 10× settings cuts nonzeros more but not variables and rows as FME does (Table 5, p. 22; p. 23). Timing: CPLEX's default algorithm choice excluded because it mixes methods; primal, dual and barrier without crossover run separately; trivial (both under 1 s) and always-timeout instances removed, leaving 207/186/127 (p. 23; Table 6, p. 24); shifted geometric means (shift 10 for time and iterations; improvement = shifted geometric mean of per-instance ratios, shift 100) by hardness groups ≥1, ≥10, ≥100 s (pp. 23-24). Results with losses: over all problems primal and barrier lose 2% in the ≥100 s group (Table 7, p. 24); on FME-impacted problems 2/3/6% faster (primal/dual/barrier), 6/8/11% on harder ones, with FME taking under 0.2% of solve time (p. 25; Table 9); win/tie/loss counts with "same" within 10% and "much faster" above 50% (Table 11, p. 26); the largest deteriorations are named, e.g. nuclear10a under dual simplex 6.9 s → 214.4 s (−97%) and stat96v5 under all three methods (Tables 12-14, pp. 26-27; p. 25); per-library performance profiles (Figs. 2-4, pp. 27-29) and overall ones (Figs. 5-6, pp. 30-31). Reading cautions: because the improvement column averages per-instance ratios it can disagree with the two means (Table 10, primal ≥1: 30,825 vs 28,265 iterations, reported 0%, p. 25); in the profiles the CANSOLVE envelope plateaus below 1 (about 0.8-0.95 by library, plot, Figs. 2-5) although always-timeout instances were removed from the timing set (p. 23), and the paper does not say which set the profiles use.
  - Transferable: when the rival's code is closed, apply your module after its presolve and feed the result back to it, so the comparison isolates your step (p. 21).
  - Transferable: test order-sensitive design choices on several row/column permutations of each instance and report the spread (Tables 1-2, pp. 17-18).
  - Transferable: report win/tie/loss counts with stated thresholds and name the worst cases next to the best (Tables 11-14, pp. 26-27).
- **D6 Positioning**: Placed in the LP presolve canon (Brearley, Mitra & Williams 1975; Andersen & Andersen 1995; Gondzio 1997; Mészáros & Suhl 2003; Achterberg et al. 2020; pp. 2-3) and in the FME literature from Fourier 1826 and Dines 1919 to Bastrakov et al. 2021 and Jing et al. 2020 (pp. 3, 5-6). Prior work is restated, not attacked, and its limit named in one sentence (p. 3). Claim of priority: "the first implementation of an FME-based presolve method in the context of general LPs" (p. 3). Self-citations are functional: bound propagation from Puranik & Sahinidis 2017 (p. 8), BARON from Zhang & Sahinidis 2025 (p. 20); presolve-order dependence from Elble's 2010 UIUC thesis (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Four contribution bullets plus a test library (p. 3); the core difficulty stated in one sentence before the method ("the primary challenge ... is the potential growth of the number of constraints", p. 6); gains reported as modest numbers ("up to 6%, 8% and 11%", p. 29) and the closing claim hedged ("highly likely to accelerate", p. 28). A "Code availability" line names where the code lives (p. 32). Small slips survive review ("probelms", "Table 13However", p. 25).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Levels: algorithm (growth-controlled FME), postsolve (primal, dual, basis), software (in BARON, p. 32), test library (MINLPrLPLib, p. 20). Limits stated in the text: estimate (5) may miss the best variable (p. 8); FME slows some hard problems under primal simplex and barrier (Table 7, p. 24); the three LP methods have "different preferences to FME" (p. 28); FME is not integrated inside CPLEX's presolve (p. 21). Not stated: no hold-out set although choices were tuned on the evaluation collection (p. 16); no thread count (p. 20). No future-work section; the last paragraph points to LPs inside MILP and MINLP solvers (p. 28). "No funding was received" (p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (reduce before you branch): evidence ✅. The reduction ethic carried to LP presolve: reductions ordered by cost, the expensive ones once per round (p. 17; step 1); cheap inference (bound propagation) drops redundant rows and detects infeasibility (p. 8); an automatic rule rations the expensive reduction by its predicted payoff (Algorithms 1-2, pp. 9-10; step 4); numerical guards keep it safe (coefficient window and tolerance scaling, p. 19; step 5); the stated motive is the LPs solved inside global MINLP solvers (p. 20).
- Method 2 (the production solver as lab notebook): evidence ✅. Code "embedded in the BARON solver" (p. 32); BARON 25.3 is the parsing front end and FMEpre with/without FME the ablation unit (p. 20); results per library because "the impact of FME varies significantly between different libraries" (p. 20); the test LPs become a public library (p. 20; step 5); deteriorations listed by name (Tables 12-14), the case SKILL.md Workflow D step 2 already cites.
- Method 4 (hold-out when your own algorithm enters, step 7): contradiction ✗. Implementation choices were made on the same >1,900 LPs used for the claims (p. 16) and no hold-out is mentioned; the disclosure itself is candid. Same pattern as SKILL.md Tension 5 and Method 2's development-set limitation.
- Heuristic 4 (each baseline in its strongest fair mode): evidence. CPLEX's aggregator pushed to 2× and 10× (Table 5, p. 22; p. 23); algorithms pinned instead of CPLEX's automatic choice so results stay interpretable (p. 23).
- new-pattern candidate: "compose after a closed rival's presolve" (p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: "permuted replicas for order-sensitive design choices" (Tables 1-2, pp. 17-18).
- new-pattern candidate: "any-solver envelope in profiles" (CANSOLVE line in Figs. 2-6, pp. 27-31; also S270 pp. 14-15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Predict each candidate elimination's nonzero change (eq. 4, or the cheaper eq. 5), apply the best, break ties by rows (eq. 7), and stop when all predictions are negative (pp. 8-10).
- Keep the predictions in a heap and recompute only the variables that share rows with the eliminated one: the update is linear in the affected nonzeros and selection is O(1) (pp. 19-20).
- Scale each combined row by s = a_ij a_i'j / (a_ij + a_i'j) so the feasibility tolerance means the same after elimination (p. 19).
- Recover the duals of eliminated rows by summing the duals of the rows generated from them (Algorithm 4, p. 12).
- In LP timing studies, pin the algorithm (primal, dual, barrier without crossover), drop trivial and always-timeout instances, and report by hardness group (≥1, ≥10, ≥100 s) (pp. 23-24).
- Build a test library from the root LP relaxations that an open MINLP solver generates on MINLPLib, and publish it (p. 20).

### S269 · Speeding Up Mixed-Integer Programming Solvers with Sparse Learning for Branching (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2604.00094)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v1, all 21 pages: main text pp. 1-16, feature appendix pp. 16-18, acknowledgements and disclaimer pp. 18-19, references pp. 19-21 skimmed; Figs. 1-2 viewed in the PDF).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Selin Bayramoğlu (first author), George L. Nemhauser (both Georgia Tech ISyE; p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Georgia Tech period, "ML for solvers" line (SKILL.md Research Trajectory, 2020-); funded as part of IDAES by DOE (p. 18); the source of SKILL.md Method 3's "fewer than 4% of the parameters" case.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Branching shapes the tree, and naïve strategies can raise solve times "by more than a factor of eight" (Achterberg & Wunderling 2013, p. 2). Strong branching (SB) gives small trees but solves two child LPs per candidate (p. 3). Learned SB approximations moved to GNNs that need large data and GPUs; Gupta et al. 2020 found that with CPU inference "the default solver was faster" (p. 5). The entry is the cost of the learned rule, not its accuracy: "we take a different path by developing interpretable models that are simple but effective" (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable: when a learned solver component is reported to win, ask what it costs per decision on the hardware the solver actually runs on (pp. 1-2, 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Keep the imitation-learning pipeline (collect SB data, build datasets, fit, deploy; Fig. 1, pp. 5-6) and change the model class: lasso on quadratic expansions (squares and pairwise products) of Khalil- and Gasse-type variable features (pp. 7, 9), with λ chosen by validation MSE along the glmnet path (p. 7). Feature changes: root ("static") features recomputed at every node (p. 6); the bipartite graph replaced by mean/max/min summaries of each candidate's constraints and edges (p. 7); features computed on the reduced formulation after removing fixed variables (p. 7); candidates restricted to fractional variables, since integral ones get near-zero product scores (pp. 7-8). Two data regimes: large-sample as in Gasse et al., and a small-sample regime of 25,000 candidate observations per problem size, size-matched (p. 8; Table 1, p. 9).
  - Transferable: before reaching for a GNN, test whether a sparse quadratic model on hand-crafted features, recomputed at every node, reaches the same decisions at a fraction of the cost (pp. 6-9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Four Gasse et al. generator families (set covering, combinatorial auctions, maximum independent set, capacitated facility location) with their sizes and generators "to enable direct comparison" (p. 6); 10,000/2,000/2,000 training/validation/test instances per family (p. 6). SCIP 8.0.0 with SoPlex 6.0.0, presolve restarts off, cuts only at the root (p. 7). SB data at 5% of nodes (every node for FL in the small-sample regime), vanilla full strong branching with scoreall and idempotent, nodes where SB raises numerical errors discarded (pp. 7-8); labels are SB scores divided by their ℓ2 norm per node (p. 7). Training and testing stay within one family (and, for QS, one size).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: No theorems. Analysis devices: accuracy as top-1 agreement with SB on held-out tuples (Table 3, p. 10); a model-size sweep with glmnet's dfmax at 25-1,000 coefficients (Table 5, pp. 12, 14); ablation of the quadratic terms (LL vs QL; Table 6, pp. 12, 15); interpretation by standardized coefficients β_i s_i / s_y and by the four features kept in all 20 models (p. 15); in Fig. 2 two terms keep one sign across all four families (solution value² negative, ceiling distance × solution value positive; plot, p. 16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 20 small, 20 medium, 20 large instances per family, each under 5 SCIP seeds, = 100 problems per cell (Lodi & Tramontani 2013; p. 11); 1 h limit, single thread, jobs in isolation, Intel Xeon Gold 6226, GNN-G with inference on a V100 GPU (p. 11); hyperparameters of all baselines tuned on the validation sets like the sparse models (p. 6). Metrics: solved count, 1-shifted geometric mean time (timeouts at 3,600 s) and nodes, with a stated rule for node counts of timeouts; problems no method solves are dropped (p. 11). Findings with losses: the GNN is more accurate on all four families (Table 3, p. 10) and often gives smaller trees, yet QL is fastest on small instances, ahead of GNN-G (p. 11); VFS gives the smallest trees at up to 65 times the runtime (p. 11); on large CA, QS is 25% faster than SCIP (p. 12; Table 4(b), p. 13: 103 s vs 137 s); peak memory up to 10% above SCIP vs 5-15 times for GNNs (p. 12); node counts are not compared with SCIP because reliability branching with side effects understates them (Gamrath & Schubert 2018; p. 12). Not discussed: on large independent set, QL solves 34 and QS 40 of 100 against SCIP's 41, although with lower shifted mean time (Table 4(d), p. 13). Reading caution: the drop rule depends on the set of compared methods, so the same QL row differs between tables (large IS: 974 s in Table 4, 728 s in Table 5, 663 s in Table 6; pp. 13-15).
  - Transferable: treat instance × seed as separate problems (20 × 5), and drop a metric (node count) where a baseline's side effects make it incomparable (pp. 11-12).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Khalil et al. 2016 (Nemhauser a co-author there, reference list p. 20) and Gasse et al. 2019 for features, generators and protocol (pp. 4-6); contrasts with Gupta et al.'s hybrids: "our approach does not depend on deep learning components" (p. 2); places the work among ML for node selection, heuristics and cut selection and among imitation and reinforcement learning to branch (pp. 4-5); cites Dey et al. 2024 on full strong branching (p. 4). No mention of best-subset selection, ALAMO or Sahinidis's own surrogate work (whole text checked).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Three contributions, each a claim the experiments test (simple models speed up SCIP; CPU-only beats a GPU GNN; small training sets suffice) (p. 2). The GNN's higher accuracy and smaller trees are conceded in the results and the conclusion (pp. 10, 16); the thesis sentence is the accuracy-versus-speed distinction (p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution: a learning design and an empirical study; no code or data release statement (whole text checked). No limitations or future-work section; the conclusion restates the gains (p. 16). Observable limits: four synthetic families only, no MIPLIB or heterogeneous set (pp. 6, 11); per-family models; fewer large IS instances solved than SCIP (Table 4(d), p. 13); the feature set was fixed "After exploratory experimentation" on data that is not specified (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (a relaxation's strength counts only at speed): evidence ✅, transposed from relaxations to learned branching rules: prediction accuracy (strength) is ranked below time per decision (pp. 10-11), and memory and training data are counted too (Table 1, p. 9; p. 12).
- Method 3 (make the black box algebraic, keep the algebra small): variant ⚠. The preference for the smallest readable model over a network holds (under 4% of GNN parameters, interpretation of terms; pp. 9-10, 15-16), but selection is by lasso along the glmnet path (p. 7), not by best-subset integer programming as Method 3 step 3 prescribes, and the object is a branching-score predictor inside a solver, not a simulator surrogate. SKILL.md files this paper under Method 3 evidence; read in full it is a variant.
- Method 2 (the production solver as lab notebook): variant ⚠. The module is built in SCIP through its C API (p. 11), not in BARON: SKILL.md's "lone version" (contribute modules to an open solver), here without a release statement.
- Heuristics 4 and 6: evidence. GPU inference for the GNN baseline and validation-tuned baselines (pp. 6, 11); five seeds, single thread, jobs in isolation (p. 11).
- Inner Tension 1 (general vs problem-specific): the learned rules are family-specific and, in QS, size-specific (pp. 6, 8, 11).
- new-pattern candidate: "deployment-cost accounting for learned solver components": CPU-only inference, training-data volume (Table 1, p. 9), model size (Table 2, p. 10) and peak memory (p. 12) reported beside accuracy and time.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Recompute root features at every node and summarize a candidate's constraint neighbourhood by mean/max/min instead of a graph (pp. 6-7).
- Restrict learned branching to fractional candidates; integral ones carry near-zero product scores (pp. 7-8).
- Train size-matched models on about 25,000 observations with per-instance node caps so no instance dominates (p. 8).
- Sweep model size with glmnet's dfmax to find the smallest model that still performs (Table 5, pp. 12, 14).
- Run the deep baseline with both CPU and GPU inference (GNN-C, GNN-G) and report peak memory (pp. 11-12).

**Quotes**:
- "prediction accuracy alone does not determine solver performance, especially as problem size grows." (p. 10)
- "faster branching decisions can outweigh modest increases in tree size." (p. 11)

### S270 · Algorithm-guided experimentation for autonomous AI systems in self-driving laboratories (talk slides, DOE NETL / PrOMMiS, 2026, no DOI)

**Read level**: full (all 21 slides: text layer, plus the page images of the local PDF for the plots, pie charts and success formula on slides 3-18; slides 20-21 are acknowledgements and disclaimer).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Donghyun Oh (first author, Georgia Tech ChBE; p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: 13-year sequel to Rios & Sahinidis 2013 (pp. 4-5), now including BAM, which SKILL.md (Latest; Inner Tension 5) records as The Optimization Firm's product launched June 2026; motivated by self-driving labs for critical-minerals separation under DOE's PrOMMiS programme (pp. 2, 20); title slide dated 23 March 2026 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two entries. An application: self-driving labs in which an algorithm proposes the next experiment, to "Accelerate identification of the most promising separation techniques for critical minerals production processes" (p. 2). A meta-observation: BBO performance depends on problem type, dimension and budget, and studies disagree (p. 3); a Scopus count (5 December 2025) shows benchmarking studies as a small fraction of DFO/BBO papers in 2013-2025 (plot, p. 3). Research question: "Can we evaluate BBO algorithms on a large-scale, unified, reproducible benchmark suite?" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: motivate a benchmark with a dated bibliometric count of method papers against benchmarking papers (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Re-run the 2013 protocol on its 502 problems with 42 implementations tagged as same (5), updated (14, counting DAKOTA and Powell sub-codes) and newly added (23: BAM, three Bayesian-optimization codes, BFO, three DAKOTA methods, eight DFL codes, COBYLA, LINCOA, UOBYQA, PARTICLESWARM, PATTERNSEARCH, SIMULANNEALBND, VRBBO) (p. 5; counts mine from the lists); classify them 28 deterministic / 14 stochastic and 23 global / 19 local (plot, p. 6); report data profiles overall (p. 9), by family (local: direct and line search, trust region; global: "branch-without-bound", others including BO; pp. 10-11) and by dimension band (pp. 12-13, 15); compute a minimal covering solver set (p. 16); demonstrate on an in-silico flow reactor (pp. 17-18).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: 502 problems with n from 1 to 300 (20% n ∈ {1,2}, 26% [3,9], 24% [10,30], 30% [31,300]), convex/nonconvex × smooth/nonsmooth; "All problems are available from minlp.com" (p. 7). Budget up to 2,500 evaluations; ten starting points for solvers that accept them; success when the median objective of 10 runs satisfies f(y) ≤ f(x*) + max(0.01|f(x*)|, 0.01) (p. 8; formula in the image). Not stated on the slides: constraint types, noise, how x* was certified, and whether any solver was developed or tuned on these problems.
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: n/a (no proofs). Devices: data profiles of the fraction solved against evaluations (pp. 9-15); the any-solver "Solvable" envelope drawn with the individual curves (pp. 14-15); a minimal solver set with each member's marginal count at the full budget (p. 16); how the minimal set was computed is not stated.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: BAM solves 81% overall, the best stochastic solver (CMA-ES) 31%, Bayesian optimization 9%, and some solver 88% (p. 14). ### S271 · MINLP for regularized symbolic regression with applications to data-driven discovery of physical laws (talk slides, DOE NETL / PrOMMiS, 2026 per batch list, undated, no DOI)

**Read level**: full (all 18 slides: text layer, plus the page images of the local PDF for formulas, flowchart and plots on slides 2-16; slide 18 is acknowledgements and disclaimer).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Dimitrios Fardis (first author, Georgia Tech ChBE), Radhakrishna Tumbalam Gooty (NETL and NETL support contractor) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the Method 3 line (ALAMO best subset → symbolic regression as MINLP, Cozad & Sahinidis 2018 → STAR randomized rounding, Sarwar 2022, and PySTAR, Kim et al. 2025) now regularized by BIC and applied to critical-minerals leaching under PrOMMiS (pp. 6, 8, 11, 18). The acknowledgement slide carries the label of the 2025 Joint IDAES/CCSI2/PrOMMiS/WaterTAP Technical Team Meeting (p. 18), which S270 also carries despite its 2026 title date (S270 pp. 1, 20), so the label does not date the deck.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Models from data trade complexity against accuracy: polynomials are fast and readable but "too simple or overfitted"; lasso, best-subset selection (ALAMO) and symbolic regression (SR) sit between linear models and kriging, neural networks and RBFs (p. 2; chart). SR learns form and parameters together (p. 4); genetic programming dominates SR but is called inefficient (Kronberger et al. 2024; p. 5); existing MINLP approaches to SR minimize the sum of squared residuals (SSR) (p. 7). Entry: put a complexity penalty into the MINLP objective (p. 9). Application: simplify the leaching model of a rare-earth recovery flowsheet (p. 11).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: SR as a generalized disjunctive program over the nodes of an expression tree: binaries z_n^o pick an operator or operand per node, big-M value-defining constraints give each node's value per data point, plus tree-defining constraints (Σ_o z_n^o ≤ 1) and redundancy-eliminating ones (no operations between constants) (pp. 6-7). The objective changes from SSR to BIC = n_data ln(SSR/n_data) + L ln n_data, with complexity = tree depth L written in the binaries: level l_n = ⌈log2(n+1)⌉ − 1 and L = max_n l_n Σ_o z_no (p. 9). Solved by STAR: solve the relaxed MINLP minimizing BIC, read assignment probabilities, round randomly m times, add a slope and intercept per input, fit the parameters of each tree by SSR, keep the tree with the lowest BIC; the best BIC so far is inverted to an SSR upper bound UB passed to the next tree's fit (flowchart, p. 10), though how UB enters that fit is not shown. Unchanged: the GDP formulation and STAR's rounding (pp. 6, 8); changed: the criterion (pp. 9-10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Binary trees of given depth over the operators +, −, *, ÷, sqrt, exp, log, sin, square (pp. 4, 10); finite bounds on every node value for big-M (p. 6). The n ln(SSR/n) form of BIC presumes a Gaussian error model (my inference; not stated). Case: two inputs (solid feed 50-100 lb/h, sulfuric acid 0.025-0.075 mol/L) and 30 outputs (p. 11), data generated from the leaching model (p. 12); noise and the train/test split are not stated. Notation shifts between slides: the penalty is L ln n_data on p. 9 and k ln n_data on p. 10, and the SSR inversion divides by n (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: n/a (no proofs). Devices: GDP to big-M value-defining constraints (p. 6); redundancy cuts on constant pairs (p. 7); depth as a maximum of level-weighted assignment binaries (p. 9); inversion of BIC to an SSR bound used as a cutoff (p. 10); randomized rounding of relaxation probabilities (p. 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Compared: star_bic, star (SSR objective) and alamo_quad (ALAMO sparse quadratics) (pp. 13, 15-16). Accuracy: R² per output near 1 for star_bic and alamo_quad, while unregularized star falls to about 0.74 (Gd) and 0.62 (Y) (plot, p. 13); parity plots for all 30 outputs from 10 training points (p. 14). Training time: alamo_quad near zero from 10 to 1,000 points; star about 550 s at 10 points and 4,300 s at about 50; star_bic about 1,300 s and 7,500 s; the SR runs stop near 50 points (plot, p. 15). Surrogate-based optimization: deviation from the equation-oriented (EO) optimum of 5,128.2 mg rare earths per hour stays below 1% for all; star_bic at 10 points about 0.04%, star at 10 points about 0.7%, alamo_quad 0.14-0.27% over 10-1,000 points (plot, p. 16). The "Correct solution point" is 45.36 kg/h solid feed and 0.075 M acid (p. 16); 45.36 kg/h is 100 lb/h, so the optimum sits at the upper bounds of both inputs (my arithmetic, ranges from p. 11), which limits how much this test separates the surrogates. Not shown: held-out error, the fitted expressions, repeats over samples.
- **D6 Positioning**: Against GP-based SR (Koza; Eureqa; SRBench; gplearn; Operon; AI Feynman; PySR) (p. 5) and within MINLP SR (Cozad 2014; Cozad & Sahinidis 2018; Austel et al. 2017, 2020; Kim et al. 2023) (p. 6). Relies on earlier results for two comparative claims: PySTAR's superior predictive performance over gplearn, Operon and PySR (p. 8; Kim et al. 2025, github IDAES/idaes-pySTAR) and ALAMO's balance of accuracy and efficiency against lasso, ANN, kriging and RBF (p. 13; Fardis et al. 2025).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Talk arc: trade-off chart → formulation → algorithm → case → accuracy → price → conclusions (pp. 2-17). The price is stated plainly (p. 15). Strong claims appear as bullets ("No overfitting", p. 17). The title's "discovery of physical laws" has no example in the deck; the conclusion names law discovery as a use (p. 17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution: BIC-regularized MINLP SR inside STAR, tested on one flowsheet unit (pp. 9-16). Limits stated: training cost, doubled by BIC (p. 15); "SR efficient for moderate numbers of training points" (p. 17). Future: other complexity metrics (depth, nodes, operators, complexity-weighted nodes) and criteria (BIC, AIC, HQIC) (p. 17).
