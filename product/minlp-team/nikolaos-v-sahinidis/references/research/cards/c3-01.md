### S025 · A Lagrangian approach to the pooling problem (Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research 1999, doi:10.1021/ie980666q)
**Read level**: full. All 17 pages: §§1-9 (pp. 1-17) read completely, with every proposition, theorem and proof (pp. 6-10) and Tables 1-15, including the data Tables 4-6 (pp. 3-4, 10, 13-16); Figures 1-14 are captions only; references (p. 17) read for sources and self-citations.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Nilanjan Adhya (first author) and Mohit Tawarmalani; Sahinidis corresponding (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: received October 1998, accepted February 1999 (p. 17); support from Mobil Technology, the DuPont Educational Aid Program and NSF DMII 94-14615 and CAREER DMII 95-02722 (p. 17); written while branch-and-reduce BARON was the group's instrument (refs 15, 34-36, 38; pp. 11-12, 17) and Tawarmalani a doctoral student (PhD 2001, SKILL.md Signature Work Anatomy).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Pooling and blending in refining, motivated by money: Texaco's nonlinear blending optimizer saved about 2.5 cents per gallon, more than 30 million dollars a year in the 1980s (p. 1). The bilinear quality terms make even Haverly's three-supply problem multi-extremal: infinitely many local solutions of value 0, a local minimum of -100 and a global optimum of -400 (p. 3). The entry is a gap in bounding theory rather than in algorithms: Lagrangian bounds are known to be no weaker than LP relaxations, but how to build one that is strictly stronger is not known (p. 2), and building one for the pooling problem is stated as the paper's primary purpose (p. 2). The object is formulation P (the p-formulation), argued to be the one most used in the process industries (pp. 3-4), with finite bounds on every flow and quality (eqs. 13-15, p. 3).
  - Transferable: enter a crowded application through a known inequality whose strict version is open (Lagrangian bound ≥ LP bound) and set out to make it strict for a named structure (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Dualize all four constraint families, mass balances (9), quality definitions (10), demands (11) and quality specifications (12), and keep only the variable bounds (LRP, p. 5). The Lagrangian subproblem splits into one LP over the inflows and one box-constrained bilinear problem per pool (Proposition 4.1, p. 6); its optimum sits at box vertices (Proposition 4.2, Corollary 4.3, p. 6), so each pool problem is solved exactly as a MILP with one binary per quality, in which the McCormick inequalities are exact because one factor is binary (Theorem 4.4, pp. 6-7). Solving a pool subproblem convexifies the pool's whole bilinear form Σ_k Σ_w R_kw x_k q_w over the box at once (Theorem 5.2, pp. 7-8), whereas the McCormick LP convexifies each product x_k q_w separately. With one quality, Rikun's lemma makes the termwise envelopes exact, so the Lagrangian and LP bounds coincide (Theorems 5.4-5.5 and 6.1, pp. 8-9); with several qualities the Lagrangian bound is at least as strong (Theorem 6.3, pp. 9-10) and strictly stronger on Example 1, -939.29 against -999.31 (Theorem 6.4, p. 10). The dual is solved by a cutting-plane master problem (pp. 13-14), and cuts are made reusable at child nodes by indexing each cut by the bounds that generated it (pp. 15-16), an answer to the paper's own complaint that McCormick envelopes must be regenerated at every node, four rows per bilinear term (p. 5). Unchanged: formulation P, the McCormick envelope as building block, branching on qualities only (p. 15).
  - Transferable: to see where a Lagrangian bound can beat an LP bound, write the LP's own Lagrangian dual (no duality gap) and compare the two subproblems; here the LP's subproblem is the continuous relaxation, y in [0, 1], of the Lagrangian's MILP subproblem (Theorem 6.3, p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linear mixing (p. 2). Finite lower and upper bounds on all f_ij, q_jw and x_jk (eqs. 13-15, p. 3), on which both the McCormick envelopes and the vertex argument rest (pp. 5-6). Qualities enter bilinearly in (10) and (12) (p. 3). Multipliers of the inequalities are nonnegative, those of the equalities free (LRP, p. 5). The theory is exact over the box and needs no regularity condition: a convex-hull fact for linear objectives (Lemma 5.1, from Bazaraa et al., p. 7) and Rikun's envelope lemma, which the authors state "without proof" (Lemma 5.3, p. 8). The structural parameter is the number of qualities l (single quality §6.1, multiple qualities §6.2, p. 9).
- **D4 Proof devices**:
  - Separability after dualization: once (9)-(12) are priced, no constraint couples pools, so the subproblem splits into p + 1 independent problems (Proposition 4.1, p. 6).
  - Vertex attainment by alternating linearity: for fixed qualities the objective is linear in the flows, so each x_k goes to the bound picked by the sign of its coefficient (eq. 17); for those flows it is an LP in q with an optimal vertex (Proposition 4.2, p. 6; Corollary 4.3).
  - Exact binarization of a variable known to sit at a bound: q_w = q_w^L + (q_w^U - q_w^L) y_w with y_w binary, and the product x_k y_w linearized by McCormick, which is exact for binary y; the equivalence is proved in both directions with equal objective values (Theorem 4.4, eqs. 18-28, pp. 6-7).
  - Lagrangian subproblem as convexification: minimizing a linear function over X equals minimizing it over conv(X) (Lemma 5.1), so solving the pool subproblem is minimizing over the convex envelope of its bilinear form (Theorem 5.2, pp. 7-8).
  - Envelope of a sum through Rikun's lemma: for terms that share one variable ranging over a simplex (here an interval) and are concave in it, the envelope of the sum is the sum of the envelopes, so termwise McCormick is exact for l = 1 (Lemma 5.3, Theorem 5.4, p. 8).
  - Bound comparison through the LP's own Lagrangian dual, whose subproblem is the y-relaxed MILP (Theorem 6.3, p. 10).
  - Strictness by one constructed instance: Theorem 6.4 is obtained from Example 1 (two pools, four products, four qualities; Figure 9), "Hence, theorem 6.4." (p. 10).
  - Cut reuse argued, not proved: every master cut comes from a subproblem solution at bounds, so cuts can be indexed by those bounds and transformed when branching changes one ("theoretically", p. 16); the argument cites "proposition 4.3", which is Corollary 4.3 (pp. 6, 15).
  - Transferable: when a nonconvex subproblem attains its optimum at box vertices, replace each continuous variable by a binary on its two bounds and linearize the products exactly; the subproblem becomes a MILP with as many binaries as variables binarized (Theorem 4.4, pp. 6-7).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 13 problems: eight single-quality ones from the literature (Haverly 1-3, Foulds 2-5, Ben-Tal 4), Ben-Tal 5, and Examples 1-4, built by the authors with 4-5 qualities, 2-3 pools, 5-8 raw materials and 4-5 products (Table 3, p. 10; as extracted, Table 3 has 12 rows and no Foulds 3); flowcharts and data for all of them are printed (Figures 3-12, Tables 4-6, pp. 10-14). Four protocols. (1) 100 MINOS local searches per problem, with and without BARON's range contraction, on an IBM RS/6000 43P: total CPU and number of distinct solutions (Table 7, p. 14) and the frequency of every local value (Tables 8-11, p. 14). (2) Root lower bounds, McCormick LP (MCP) against Lagrangian (LRP), beside the global optimum certified by BARON (Tables 12-13, p. 15; p. 12): identical on all eight single-quality problems and on Ben-Tal 5, stronger on Examples 1-4, e.g. -999.31 to -939.29 against a global -549.80 on Example 1 (Table 13, p. 15). From Table 13 the Lagrangian closes 13.4%, 9.4%, 5.6% and 17.8% of the LP's root gap on Examples 1-4 (computed for this card). No time to compute LRP is reported anywhere, and §7 does not say how LRP was solved (§8 describes a cutting-plane method, pp. 13-14). (3) BARON'99 against the published iteration counts and CPU times of Foulds'92, Ben-Tal'94 and Floudas'93/'96, obtained on other computers (CDC 4340, HP9000/730) with other tolerances (BARON 10^-6; 0.05% to 1% in ref. 41) (Table 14 and footnotes, p. 15; p. 12). In the last two columns as extracted, BARON closes every literature problem in at most 9 iterations and 2.59 s, and needs 1980 to 79944 iterations and 182 to 19314 s on Examples 1-4, for which no earlier result exists (Table 14, p. 15). (4) One Lagrangian branch-and-bound run on a one-pool, four-product, four-quality example (global -260): 23 nodes, branching on qualities only (p. 15), with 28 master cuts at the root, 14-43 at inner nodes and 1 at terminal nodes, against 64 McCormick rows per node for its 16 bilinear terms (Table 15, p. 16; p. 15). Losses: the text reports that range contraction raises local-search CPU in some instances, because MINOS finds feasible points less easily in the smaller box (p. 11). My tally from Tables 7-11 (p. 14): CPU rose on 7 of 13 problems (4132 s to 4296 s in total); the frequency of the best value found rose on 10 problems and fell on Foulds 2 (20% to 19%) and Example 1 (3% to 2%); on Example 3 the best value found got worse (-559.6 to -549.8); on Example 4 the best value -877.6 rose from 4% to 45% of the runs while infeasible runs rose from 3% to 19%. The verdict "It is clear that range contraction is beneficial" (p. 11) does not mention these exceptions.
  - Transferable: when your solver finds every published instance easy, build new instances along the structural axis your contribution targets (here the number of qualities), print their full data, and show the contribution where the old set could not (Table 3, p. 10; pp. 12-13).
  - Transferable: judge a preprocessing step for local search by the distribution of local values over 100 starts with and without it, plus total CPU, not by one run (Tables 7-11, p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: One table classifies prior work by method family (recursion, sensitivity and feasibility analysis, decomposition, branch-and-bound), number of qualities and local or global optimum (Table 2, p. 4; the text calls it "Table 9", p. 5). Each rival is criticized by a named failure: recursion often does not converge and becomes unstable as pools are added (p. 4); successive linear programming is local (p. 4); the Floudas-Aggarwal generalized Benders strategy gives no global guarantee and may stop at a local maximum or a non-KKT point, by his own earlier result with Grossmann (Sahinidis & Grossmann 1991; p. 4); finite termination of the GOP algorithm may need a modification noted by Gourdin et al. (p. 4); McCormick branch-and-bound regenerates four rows per bilinear term at every node, so node LPs grow with pools, products and qualities (p. 5). The paper builds on Ben-Tal et al.'s Lagrangian duality for the q-formulation and Dür & Horst's duality-gap result (p. 5), and on McCormick, Al-Khayyal & Falk and Rikun (pp. 5, 8). Seven of the 41 references include Sahinidis (refs 15, 24, 34-38, p. 17); refs 15, 24, 34-36 and 38 are cited for BARON's range contraction and branching (pp. 11-12).
- **D7 Writing**: Headline wording is stronger than the theorems: the abstract says the Lagrangian bound is tighter for multiple qualities (p. 1) and the introduction that the easier separable relaxation "does not provide bounds that are as tight" (p. 2), while Theorems 6.3-6.4 give "at least as strong" plus strictness on one instance (p. 10). Results sentences are categorical: BARON "presents a superior performance" (p. 12); range contraction is clearly beneficial (p. 11). The closing claim that the best local optimum of a popular NLP solver may be "very far from the global optimum" (p. 17) fits a typical single MINOS run (value 0 in 84-98% of the runs on Examples 1-3, Table 11, p. 14) but not the best of the 100 runs, which reached -549.8 on Examples 1-2 (global -549.80), -559.6 on Example 3 (global -561.05) and -877.6 on Example 4 (global -877.65) (Tables 11 and 13, pp. 14-15). The theory runs as a short Proposition-Theorem-Corollary chain, each section opening with what it will establish (pp. 6-10). The conclusion separates what is proved from what is conjectured from one example, and says what testing the conjecture would require (p. 16).
  - Transferable: in the conclusion, label each claim as proved, observed on one example, or conjectured, and name the implementation work needed to test the conjecture (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory first: an exactly solvable Lagrangian bound and its exact placement against the McCormick LP (pp. 6-10); then four constructed hard multi-quality instances and evidence that BARON, with the LP relaxation and range reduction, finds every literature pooling problem easy (pp. 10, 12-13). Stated limits: the emphasis is theoretical; cut reuse is a conjecture from one example; testing it requires solving implementation issues because the bounding procedure is "drastically different" from existing branch-and-bound codes (p. 16). Not stated: the time to compute LRP (Tables 12-13 report bounds only, p. 15), and how the subproblem grows with the number of qualities: with y fixed each pool problem is an LP in x (p. 7), so a pool costs at most 2^l LPs (my inference). Future: computational testing of the bound and of cut reuse inside branch-and-bound (p. 16); global optimization as the focus for refinery pooling (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅: BARON's range contraction in 1999 is feasibility-based (LPs that minimize and maximize each variable over the linear constraints) and optimality-based (feasible solutions with duality arguments) (p. 11), the order of Method 1 step 1. Applied before local search, it raises the frequency of the best local value on 10 of 13 problems, at some CPU cost and with the exceptions tallied in D5 (Tables 7-11, p. 14); inside BARON, five literature problems close in one iteration (Foulds 2-5, Ben-Tal 5; Table 14, p. 15).
- Method 5 variant ⚠: a relaxation argued by strength alone. Dominance is proved (Theorems 6.3-6.4, p. 10); cost is counted only as master cuts per node on one example against 64 McCormick rows (Table 15, pp. 15-16), with no times; the bound closes 6-18% of the LP's root gap on the four hard instances (computed from Table 13, p. 15); and BARON, the instrument that solved every problem, kept the McCormick LP (p. 12). The source of the extra strength, joint convexification of a pool's multi-term bilinear form (Theorem 5.2, pp. 7-8), is the kind of object Method 5 step 3 names (multiterm LP inequalities).
- Method 2 variant ⚠: BARON is the instrument of record (global optima, range contraction, the Table 14 comparison; pp. 11-12, 15), but the paper's own contribution lives outside it: the Lagrangian bound is computed separately, and the authors write that implementing it inside branch-and-bound raises "several implementation issues" (p. 16). Paper before module, the reverse of Method 2's order.
- Method 4 contradiction ✗ (dated 1999, before the 2013 protocol): the comparison takes rivals' published numbers from other machines and tolerances, sets node counts beside GOP iterations, and concludes with a single winner (Table 14, p. 15; p. 12), against Method 4 steps 3 and 6 (identical conditions, portfolio conclusion) and the skill's Research Taste warning sign 1; the authors defend it by the direction of their own handicaps, a slower computer and a stricter tolerance (p. 12), and on Examples 1-4 no rival result exists (Table 14, footnote a, p. 15).
- Heuristic 7 (keep evidence and proof apart) in practice: local values from 100 starts are reported beside BARON-certified optima, never as optima (Tables 8-13, pp. 14-15; p. 12).
- New-pattern candidates (existing names): *Transfer integer-programming devices into global optimization* (Held-Karp Lagrangian relaxation, an exact MILP subproblem and a cutting-plane dual carried into pooling; pp. 1, 6-7, 13-14); *Linearize through discreteness of the domain* (vertex attainment, then binaries on bounds; pp. 6-7); *Prove where the cheap relaxation sits* (Theorems 6.1, 6.3, 6.4; pp. 9-10); *price of local search on the literature's own instances* (100-start distributions against certified optima; pp. 11, 14-15, 17). New: *hand-built hard instances on the contribution's structural axis* (pp. 10, 12-13), close to *structural-knob instance generator* (S018) but hand-built along one axis.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Exact MILP for a box-constrained bilinear subproblem: show the optimum is at vertices, write each variable as lower bound plus range times a binary, and linearize the products exactly with McCormick (Proposition 4.2, Theorem 4.4, pp. 6-7).
- Compare a Lagrangian bound with an LP bound through the LP's own Lagrangian dual: if the LP's subproblem is the continuous relaxation of yours, yours is at least as strong (Theorem 6.3, p. 10).
- Explain a bound's strength by naming the nonconvex aggregate it convexifies jointly, then prove where termwise envelopes are already exact (Rikun's lemma for terms sharing one interval variable) (Theorems 5.2, 5.4, pp. 7-8).
- Prove a strictness theorem of the form "may provide a strictly stronger lower bound" with one constructed instance, and publish that instance's full data (Theorem 6.4, Figure 9, p. 10).
- When the literature's instances are easy for your solver, build harder ones along the axis your method targets and show the method gains there (Table 3, p. 10; pp. 12-13).
- Make Lagrangian cuts reusable down the tree by indexing them by the bounds that generated them, so a child transforms its parent's cuts instead of regenerating them (pp. 15-16).

### S010 · A critical review and annotated bibliography for heat exchanger network synthesis in the 20th century (Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research 2002, doi:10.1021/ie010389e)
**Read level**: full, in the authors' preprint of April 30, 2001 (p. 1), not the typeset article: §§1-5 (pp. 1-8), acknowledgements and notation (p. 9), all 446 annotated entries [1]-[446] (pp. 10-71; [346] is printed "[34 6]", p. 57), Tables 1-8 (pp. 72-79) and Figures 1 and 3 (pp. 80, 82) read; Figure 2 (papers per year, p. 81) lost its bars in extraction. The published version differs at least in its abstract (461 works, another section order; a2-02 card); its text was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Kevin C. Furman (first author); Sahinidis corresponding (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: UIUC Chemical Engineering with NSF CTS-9704643 (pp. 1, 9), the grant of the complexity paper S024 (c01 card); the middle of the Furman-Sahinidis HENS line: S024 is cited as accepted ([95], p. 23), and the approximation-algorithm paper S119 followed in 2004 (s02 card).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: HENS, introduced as the most commonly studied problem in process synthesis (abstract, p. 1) and stated as a Given/develop list (p. 2). Two thorough reviews already exist (Gundersen & Naess 1988; Jezowski 1994), so the paper does not supplant these "excellent reviews" but sets out to "complement them", with three aims: cover work they missed, give a critical review that becomes "the impetus for future research", and compile and categorize the century's HENS citations (p. 2). The coverage rule is stated at once: mainly English-language journal papers, plus important PhD theses and conference papers; non-English papers only with an English abstract (p. 2).
  - Transferable: state a survey's coverage rule (language, venue type, period) on its second page, so that omissions follow a rule the reader can check (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A four-layer map of one field. (i) Solution methods in two paradigms, each with its main drawback: sequential synthesis by targets (pinch-based evolutionary design; sequential LP, MILP and NLP models), which "does not guarantee" a network of minimum annual cost (p. 3), and simultaneous MINLP synthesis, whose hyperstructure still needs HRAT fixed in advance and whose stagewise superstructure rests on isothermal mixing, no split stream through more than one exchanger and no bypass (p. 4). (ii) 20 dated milestones from Ten Broeck 1944 to the fully simultaneous formulations of 1989-1991 (p. 5), condensed into a 10-item timeline (Figure 1, p. 80). (iii) Eight classification tables, by topic, heat-integration context, pinch technique, mathematical model, formulation, computer program, commercial package and review type, seven of them crossed with three periods: before 1980, 1980-1989, 1990-2000 (§4, pp. 6-7; Tables 1-8, pp. 72-79). (iv) One annotation per work (pp. 10-71). The critical assessment is concentrated in five numbered open problems (p. 8); the annotations are mostly descriptive.
  - Transferable: cross every classification table with period columns, so one table shows both what has been done and when the activity moved (Tables 1-6, 8, pp. 72-77, 79).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: For a review, the assumptions are its scope and its model of the field. Scope: the 20th century, English, journals first (p. 2). Model: HENS research as sequential vs simultaneous synthesis (p. 2). The paper carries the simplifying assumptions that make HENS formulations tractable as named limits: HRAT fixed by temperature-interval partitioning (Floudas-Ciric, p. 4); isothermal mixing, no split stream through more than one exchanger, no bypass (Yee-Grossmann, p. 4; [418], p. 66); linear area cost and arithmetic-mean temperature difference in the global algorithms of Grossmann's group ([317], p. 53; [427], p. 68). Open problem 4 names the Yee-Grossmann assumptions as the reason no "truly complete formulation" exists (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a for original theorems (a review). Argument devices:
  - Coverage gaps as the reason to write: complement two reviews by what they missed (p. 2).
  - Milestones as dated firsts (first known paper, first use of mathematical programming, first formal definition; p. 5; Figure 1, p. 80).
  - Field statistics as evidence for a trend: annual paper counts peak in the late 1980s and early 1990s, tied to the optimization advances and hardware that first made simultaneous synthesis solvable (p. 8; Figure 2, p. 81).
  - One-line assumption flags in the annotations: forbidden matches and multiple utilities "not considered" ([1], p. 10); "does not allow for stream splits" ([91], pp. 22-23); "not guaranteed optimal" ([399], p. 64); the restrictions of the Yee-Grossmann superstructure ([418], p. 66).
  - A hardness result turned into an agenda: strong NP-hardness of HENS [95] is the reason given for polynomial-time approximation algorithms with performance guarantees (open problem 2, p. 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (no computations). Bibliometrics: papers per year (Figure 2, p. 81, bars lost; the text's reading, p. 8) and per journal, 120 of 408 journal papers in Computers & Chemical Engineering and 110 under "Other" (Figure 3, p. 82). Test-problem provenance is recorded entry by entry: the papers that introduced the named test problems 6SP2, 8SP1, 12SP1, 14SP1 and 20SP1 ([111], p. 25), 4SP1 and 6SP1 ([223], p. 40), 5SP1, 7SP1 and 7SP2 ([263], p. 46), 10SP1 ([305], p. 51) and 4SP2 ([312], p. 52); a new low-cost solution of 7SP4 ([73], p. 20); a collection with "best known solutions at the time of publication" ([90], p. 22); Shenoy's listing of 43 test problems with GAMS code ([349], p. 57); an industrial retrofit benchmark ([273], p. 47); all gathered in a "Test Problem Collections" row (Table 8, p. 79).
  - Transferable: inside an annotated bibliography, record which paper introduced which test instance and who holds its best-known solution, so that the bibliography doubles as the field's benchmark registry (pp. 20, 22, 25, 40, 46, 51-52, 57, 79).
- **D6 Positioning**: Complements the earlier reviews rather than correcting them (p. 2), and praises other surveys in the annotations ("excellent review", [56], p. 17; "very thorough", [120], p. 27; "very well organized", [277], pp. 47-48). The two largest bodies of work are the pinch school (Linnhoff, about 33 entries) and the mathematical-programming school (Grossmann, about 36) (my count of entry headers, pp. 10-71); both are annotated descriptively, and the critique is spread over the open problems: the unknown solution quality of the pinch-variant heuristics (problem 1), local optima and simplifying assumptions in the simultaneous MINLPs (problems 3-4) (p. 8). Disputes are recorded neutrally: Linnhoff's claims on second-law analysis and Sama's rebuttal ([231], pp. 41-42; [343], p. 56), the Ahmad-Smith exchange ([10], p. 11; [367], p. 59). Hohmann's 1971 thesis is credited with the first annotated HENS bibliography, with the note that attempts to publish it in archival journals were "turned down twice" (p. 5; [137], p. 29). The only self-citation is the complexity paper [95] (p. 23); although open problem 3 calls for "more efficient global optimization algorithms" (p. 8), no BARON or other global-optimization paper of his is cited.
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract lists the parts in order: methods, milestones, classifications, assessment, then the works one by one (p. 1). The critical voice is held back to the last body page: a short assessment and a numbered list of five open problems (p. 8). Open problem 1 carries the yardstick sentence, analytic results over computational comparisons and case studies (p. 8). The hardness result is stated more strongly than in its source: [95] is said to refute the possibility of fast exact algorithms (pp. 8, 23), where S024 itself calls such an algorithm "highly unlikely" (c01 card, S024 p. 25); the condition P ≠ NP is dropped. Annotations run one to five sentences, in the present tense; errata, corrigenda, reprints, revised versions and replies get their own cross-linked entries ([67], p. 19; [85], pp. 21-22; [107], p. 25; [121], p. 27; [230], p. 41; [266]-[267], p. 46; [331], p. 55; [10], p. 11).
  - Transferable: give errata, reprints and revised versions their own entries cross-linked to the original, so that a reader knows which version carries a result (pp. 19, 21-22, 25, 27, 41, 46, 55).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A reference work and an agenda (pp. 2, 8): 446 annotated works, eight classification tables, a timeline and a directory of commercial and educational software with web addresses (Tables 6-7, pp. 77-78). Limits as stated: coverage restricted by language and venue (p. 2); software "outside the scope" beyond the tables (p. 7); the heat-integration table "not an all inclusive listing" (p. 6). Future directions, the five open problems (all p. 8): (1) the quality of existing heuristics, asked as worst-case questions (within a predetermined range of the optimum, or off by orders of magnitude?), with analytic results "long overdue"; (2) polynomial-time approximation algorithms with performance guarantees, given strong NP-hardness; (3) local optima in simultaneous formulations, calling for more robust formulations or more efficient global optimization; (4) a formulation without simplifying assumptions; (5) combining HENS with the rest of process synthesis. Problem 2 became the group's next HENS paper (S119, 2004, s02 card).
