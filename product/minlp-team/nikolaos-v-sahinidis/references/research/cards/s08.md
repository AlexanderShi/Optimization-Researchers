### S090 · On the impact of running intersection inequalities for globally solving polynomial optimization problems (Mathematical Programming Computation 2020, DOI 10.1007/s12532-019-00169-z)

**Read level**: full (all 30 pages: abstract, §1 introduction and contributions, §2 definitions, §3 NP-hardness proof, §4 separation algorithm with proofs, §5 implementation in BARON, §6 experiments, references). Version read: the author preprint dated 11 July 2019 (p. 1). First-page check: title and "Nikolaos Sahinidis", CMU Chemical Engineering, match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Alberto Del Pia (UW-Madison), Aida Khajavirad (CMU) (p. 1); the NSF support note covers the first two authors only (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a BARON cut module built on Del Pia and Khajavirad's polyhedral theory of the multilinear polytope (refs [12]-[15], p. 3) and on the multilinear cuts of Bao, Khajavirad, Sahinidis and Tawarmalani 2015 (p. 4; ref. [2], p. 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: global solvers rely on factorable relaxations, and the termwise bounding of multilinear terms by recursive bilinear envelopes, deployed in all general-purpose solvers, "in general" gives "very poor bounds" (pp. 2, 4). The entry is the separation problem for running intersection inequalities, a facet-defining family of the multilinear polytope introduced in [15] (abstract; p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: first prove that separating even the flower subclass is NP-hard (Theorem 1, p. 8); then use the fact that ranks are small in practice. For fixed rank r a reduced separation system (per center e0, one neighbor per intersection pattern, the one maximizing z̄_e, and per neighbor the node minimizing z̄_w) still contains a maximally violated inequality (Proposition 1, p. 12), which gives O(|E|^2) separation (Corollary 1, p. 14). In BARON the z̄-independent part (recognition: edge lists, admissible neighbor sets, running-intersection checks) runs once at the root and each cut round costs O(|E|) (pp. 14-16). Cuts use local bounds by scaling to [0,1] (inequality (7), p. 7), enter only the iterative cut rounds and not the initial outer approximation, and the cut pool keeps a share of the most violated ones (p. 16). Unchanged: BARON's factorable relaxation and its multilinear cuts stay, and the new cuts are added on top (p. 19).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: variables scaled to [0,1], or nonnegative finite bounds (pp. 2, 7); fixed rank, justified by "for all practical purposes we can assume that r ≤ 5" and by the test libraries (pp. 5, 11); the hardness result takes the rank as an input (p. 11). Problems with more than a few thousand variables fall outside the method: BARON switches its expensive cut routines off there (pp. 16-17).
- **D4 Analysis** (brief): polynomial reduction from 3-dimensional matching through perfect matchings with costs on a hypergraph augmented with loops (pp. 8-11); an exchange argument that shrinks the inequality family without losing its most violated member (Proposition 1, pp. 12-13); an induction bounding the neighbors of a center by |e0| − 1 (Lemma 1, pp. 13-14); operation counts against explicit enumeration, O(n^(r^2)) down to O(n^(2r)) when |E| = Θ(n^r) (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: a development version of BARON 18.6.19 with and without the cuts, GAMS 25.1.1, one thread, 10^-6 tolerances, 500 s, a problem trivial if all algorithms take under 0.5 s (p. 17), on 440 random multilinear and polynomial problems of degree 3 and 4 (the set of [2] extended) and 45 binary image-restoration instances (pp. 18-19). CPU time fell 60% (degree 3), 43% (degree 4) and 63% (vision); problems solved went from 208 to 216, 168 to 180 and 37 to 44, all 44 vision instances at the root node (pp. 20-24). Degradations are counted in factor bands, up to 6% of degree-4 problems at least twice slower because of larger LPs (Table 1(b), p. 22; p. 24); a 2×2 ablation BarBasic/BarMult/BarRI/BarBoth reports geometric means per test set (Table 3, p. 27). No other solver is compared and no hold-out set is mentioned (§6 read in full).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: complexity results (theory), a separation algorithm and its BARON implementation (software) (pp. 4-5, 14-17). Stated limits: the effect depends on the hypergraph, with no visible improvement on dense hypergraphs such as the autocorrelation set (p. 25); recognition and cut generation are too expensive above a few thousand variables (pp. 16-17); memory grows with the degree (pp. 22, 24). There is no separate conclusion section and no future work (pp. 25-27).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (steps 3 and 6): a polyhedral family is used because its separation can be made cheap; the LP is kept small by the cut-pool share (p. 16), the routines deactivate themselves on large problems (pp. 16-17), and the slowdowns from more expensive LPs are reported (p. 22; p. 24).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (steps 3-5): the module is judged as named builds inside BARON (BarBasic, BarMult, BarRI, BarBoth; p. 25) under the group's fixed protocol (GAMS, one thread, 10^-6, 500 s, Xeon X5650; p. 17), with results per test set (Tables 1-3, pp. 22-27), and the random test problems are released on the firm's site (p. 18; ref. [42], p. 30).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence, stated only: the contributions paragraph says the inequalities also serve "local feasibility-based and optimality-based range reductions" (p. 5); §5 describes only cut generation (pp. 14-17).
- Heuristic 1 ✅ (card only): a special class (multilinear and polynomial problems) is attacked through a parameter, the rank, that the libraries keep small (pp. 5, 11).
- New-pattern candidate: "parameterize hardness by a library statistic": NP-hard with the rank as input (Theorem 1, p. 8), polynomial for fixed rank (Corollary 1, p. 14), the fixed-rank assumption justified from the test libraries (p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a cut family's separation is NP-hard in general, find the parameter your test libraries keep small (here the rank, at most 5), give an algorithm polynomial for fixed values of it, and state the library evidence next to the assumption (pp. 5, 11, 14).
- Split separation into a point-independent recognition step done once at the root and a cheap per-round step; recheck a structural condition only at nodes where branching or range reduction has fixed a variable that can change it (pp. 14-16).
- Derive the cuts on [0,1] and scale them by the node's upper bounds, so they use local bounds without rebuilding the structure (inequality (7), p. 7).
- Test a new cut family against the incumbent one in a 2×2 design (neither, old, new, both) and claim complementarity only if "both" is fastest on every test set (Table 3, p. 27).
- Report effects in factor bands (at least 2× better … at least 2× worse) for time, iterations and nodes, so slowdowns are counted next to gains (Table 1, p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "Constructing strong and cheap to compute convex relaxations is key to the efficiency of global optimization algorithms." (p. 2)
- "For such problems BARON automatically deactivates its expensive cut generation routines, including the one proposed in this paper." (p. 17)

### S134 · GPU parameter tuning for tall and skinny dense linear least squares problems (Optimization Methods and Software 2020, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2018.1527331)

**Read level**: full (text pages 1-20: abstract, contributions, §2 QR background, §3 comparison of QR libraries, §4 DFO and SO classes, §5 tuning experiments, §6 conclusions, acknowledgments; the reference list, pp. 20-23, skimmed). Version read: the accepted manuscript dated 11 September 2018, "To appear in Optimization Methods & Software" (p. 1). First-page check: title and "Nikolaos Sahinidis" (corresponding author), CMU Chemical Engineering, match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Benjamin Sauk, Nikolaos Ploskas (CMU Chemical Engineering) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Sauk's GPU line, done within IDAES with DOE funding and an NVIDIA-donated Tesla K40 (p. 20); it carries the solver-comparison style of Rios & Sahinidis 2013 (ref. [59], pp. 2-3) to tuning a numerical library; LLSPs are motivated by, among others, best subset selection and the lasso (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: GPU codes are designed for one architecture and may be suboptimal or unusable on another; the map from tunable parameters to speed has "no explicit relationship to model", so the solver is treated as a black box (p. 1). Existing autotuning combines programmer insight with heuristic enumeration and cannot certify optimality (p. 2). Entry: tune MAGMA's QR-based LLSP solver for tall and skinny (TS) matrices, where solvers are communication-bound (pp. 5, 7).
- **D2 Core idea**: first compare four QR libraries (cuSolverDN, LAPACK, MAGMA, PLASMA) on square and TS matrices, and pick MAGMA, which led on square problems but lost speed on the tallest TS ones (pp. 6-8); then make block size and pinned versus malloc memory the decision variables, run 31 DFO solvers (26 continuous, 5 integer) and 4 SO solvers on the black box, and score them against complete enumeration of the 1000 combinations (pp. 12, 14, 18).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: performance in GFLOPs from timings, averaged over ten random uniform matrices per size, conditioning being irrelevant because the work depends only on size (p. 5); integrality handled by rounding inside the simulator for the continuous solvers (p. 12); timings are noisy because of OS scheduling (pp. 13, 15); the optima hold for one GPU, the Tesla K40 (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no analysis; odd results are explained by enumeration plots and profiling, pp. 15-17).
- **D5 Experiments**: 26 TS sizes (500 columns, 1000 to 126000 rows), 20 evaluations per solver chosen by a pilot on small problems, the same starting point, five trials with different starts, the best solver per size kept (pp. 7, 12, 14); 1.8× over MAGMA's defaults at 126000 rows (p. 13); optimal block size 8 from 41000 rows up against MAGMA's 64 (Table 3, p. 14); many solvers end worse than the default (BOBYQA and NEWUOA −73%, PRAXIS −74%; Table 4, p. 16); three of four SO solvers are worse than the default, also with 200 evaluations (pp. 17-18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a DFO/SO comparison, a 1.8× speed-up, and a five-solver set (GLCCLUSTER, HOPSPACK, SID-PSM, MCS, SNOBFIT) that finds the optimal parameters of all 26 problems an order of magnitude faster than enumeration (pp. 3, 15); a whole-set default (block size 8, pinned memory) better than MAGMA's (p. 17); a proposed finer lookup table (pp. 19-20). Stated limits: K40-specific optima (p. 17); SO codes "not mature enough yet" for this problem (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (steps 1-6): the existing codes are compared before anything is tuned (§3, pp. 5-8); all DFO and SO solvers get the same budget and starts (p. 12); the ground truth comes from complete enumeration instead of a global solver (p. 12), a variant of step 2 available because the space is finite and small; losses against the default are tabulated (Tables 4-5, pp. 16, 18); the verdict is a covering set of five solvers (p. 15). The headline 1.8× uses the best DFO solver for each size (p. 12), i.e. the portfolio's best, not one solver's.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (its last step): derivative-free search is used because there is "no explicit algebraic model" of the relation between tuning parameters and performance (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Pick a tuning space small enough to enumerate (here 1000 combinations), so that every black-box solver's answer is scored against the true optimum and against the default (p. 12).
- Compare the candidate host codes before tuning, then tune the one with the most headroom (§3, pp. 5-8).
- Report two numbers per solver, the mean improvement over the default and the number of problems on which it found the best option, and build the smallest covering set of solvers from the second (Table 4, pp. 15-16).
- When continuous solvers handle integer parameters by rounding, check whether they keep evaluating the same rounded point; the integer version of GLCCLUSTER did almost 60% better here (p. 15).
- Turn per-instance tuning into one default for the whole set, and state the hardware it was tuned on (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "Autotuning approaches cannot provide a certificate of optimality to ensure that they identify the best possible parameters since an exhaustive search of all parameter options is prohibitively expensive." (p. 2)
- "For the problem under study, there are 1000 possible combinations of parameters, thus affording us the possibility to determine an optimal solution via complete enumeration." (p. 12)

### S181 · An industrial application of deep reinforcement learning for chemical production scheduling (Workshop on Machine Learning for Engineering Modeling, Simulation and Design @ NeurIPS 2020, no DOI)

**Read level**: full (all 6 pages: abstract, introduction, problem, methods, results, conclusion, broader impact, references). First-page check: title and "Nikolaos V. Sahinidis", Georgia Tech, match (p. 1). Voice: an industrial deployment report; first author Hubbs (CMU and Dow), three Dow authors, Sahinidis fourth of five (p. 1); nothing in it is attributed to him personally.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Christian D. Hubbs (CMU Chemical Engineering and Dow), Adam Kelloway (Dow), John M. Wassick (Dow), Ignacio E. Grossmann (CMU) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: follows Hubbs et al. 2020 (Computers & Chemical Engineering), which benchmarked an actor-critic scheduler against MILP models (ref. [7]), and the OR-Gym library (ref. [8]) (p. 2); Dow funded the work (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: human planners schedule a continuous reactor under uncertain demand and outages; math-programming models are "not widely used" in industry because of stochasticity and model size, implemented schedules scarcely resemble the optimized ones, and such systems fall out of favor with planners (pp. 1-2). Entry: replace the schedule optimizer by a learned policy that reschedules daily (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: build a simulator of the plant and its planning process from historical, proprietary data; train a PPO policy (eight fully connected layers of 256 ReLU units, RLlib) that sequences the packaging stage, and back-calculate the reactor schedule from it (p. 3); meta-learn reward weights and hyperparameters by population-based training over 48 agents, scored by episode profit (revenue minus inventory and off-grade costs) (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: fixed run rates, so the sequence is the only decision; six products, forbidden and costly type changes, a seven-day frozen period, a daily data refresh (p. 2); all values rescaled for confidentiality (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: back-tests on consecutive historical 52-week windows started from the plant's initial conditions, plus simulated demand ("100 years" in the text, p. 3; "1,000 simulated years" in the Figure 3 caption, p. 4); product availability 85% for the human planners against 99% (p. 4); six months of live daily schedules under human review, with performance dropping in two periods, one coinciding with a larger distance from the training data in PCA space, attributed to an unplanned outage and to data errors that double-counted orders (pp. 3-5). This paper compares with no optimization model and no bound.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an application in operation (p. 5); limits shown: state drift and data errors (p. 5). Future work: alerts for human intervention when states stray, an ensemble of models, full automation, multi-agent RL across plants (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction (co-authored, industrial voice): the plant is a data-driven simulator and the decision maker a large network policy, with no algebraic model, no global optimization and no certificate (pp. 3-4), the reverse of Method 3 and of the taste warning against needless neural networks. Sahinidis is fourth of five authors, so this marks the range of lines he co-signs, not a method he states.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the yardstick is the owner's current practice (human planners over the same historical periods) and live operation (p. 4), not certified optima; the MILP comparison is in the earlier paper [7] (p. 2).
- Workflow F step 4 and Heuristic 10, consistent (card only): the sponsor's engineers are co-authors and the problem is the sponsor's (pp. 1, 5).
- New-pattern candidate (industry co-author voice; needs further cards): "deploy a learned policy with a drift alarm": live states are projected on the principal components of the training states and their distance from the training center is tracked against reward (pp. 4-5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Monitor a deployed learned policy by projecting live states onto the principal components of the training states and tracking their distance from the training center against reward; raise an alert for human review when it grows (pp. 4-5).
- Back-test a scheduler on consecutive historical 52-week windows started from the plant's own initial conditions, and compare with what the human planners did in the same windows (pp. 3-4).

**Quotes**:
- "The DRL system outperforms actual human planners over the same horizon with an average 99% Product Availability Rate compared to 85%." (p. 4)
- "We hypothesized that the DRL agent's performance would degrade as operational states becomes increasingly dissimilar from training states." (p. 4)

### S191 · BARON user manual v. 2021.1.13 (BARON user manual 2020, no DOI)

**Read level**: full (all 30 pages: contents, §1 introduction and licensing, §2 model requirements, §3 installation, §4 input grammar and sample file, §5 screen log, termination messages and output files, §6 features, §7 option tables, §8 bibliography pointer). Version read: v. 2021.1.13, dated 13 January 2021, with a log from BARON 21.1.13 (pp. 1, 10); the work list's year is 2020. First-page check: title and "Nick Sahinidis, The Optimization Firm, LLC" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author line, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the stand-alone manual of the firm era, sixteen years after the GAMS/BARON chapter of 2005 (S185) and about twenty after the UIUC v4.0 manual (S097), both carded in s03; parts of BARON are credited to UIUC and CMU (p. 2), and the log asks users to cite Kılınç & Sahinidis 2018 (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: users of algebraic NLPs and MINLPs need global optima, which traditional algorithms guarantee only under convexity; BARON's branch-and-bound guarantees them "under fairly general assumptions", the first being finite bounds on nonlinear expressions (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: branch-and-bound "enhanced with a variety of constraint propagation and duality techniques for reducing ranges of variables" (p. 2). All five reduction families are on by default: nonlinear and linear feasibility-based tightening ("poor man's NLPs" and "poor man's LPs"; TDo, LBTTDo), marginals-based reduction (MDo), optimality-based tightening (OBTTDo), and probing, whose amount is "automatically decided by BARON" (PDo −2) (pp. 24-25). Several or all feasible solutions come from one search tree without integer cuts (NumSol, IsolTol; pp. 16, 24); an IIS can be computed for every problem type by five algorithms (pp. 19-20, 29); complementarity relations, MPECs included, are recognized and exploited automatically (pp. 20-21).
- **D3 Assumptions (the contract with the user)**: functions algebraic and explicit: exp, ln, x^α and β^x, with |x| and x^y (both x and y variables) reformulated automatically only under AIMMS, AMPL, GAMS and MATLAB and by hand in the BARON language, and no trigonometric functions (pp. 3, 6). Finite variable bounds do not imply finite expression bounds (1/x on [0,1]); if BARON cannot infer them, "global optimality of the solutions provided cannot be guaranteed" (pp. 3-4). RELAXATION_ONLY_EQUATIONS and CONVEX_EQUATIONS can be declared, with no warning in this edition about wrong declarations (p. 8; whole manual read).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: none (a gear-train example and its log, and a PUMA robot system with 14 solutions found by NumSol; pp. 9-11, 16-19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: the statuses separate certified from uncertified outcomes: "Normal completion" means solved within tolerances; heuristic termination carries no guarantee; missing bounds lead to "*** Globality is therefore not guaranteed ***" or "*** Infeasibility is therefore not guaranteed ***", and the printed relaxation bounds may then be invalid (pp. 12-13). Defaults: EpsA 10^-6 and EpsR 10^-9 (p. 21); MaxTime 1000 s, on CPU time for one thread and wall clock for several (p. 23); one thread for the MIP subsolver (pp. 21, 30); DeltaTerm flagged as nondeterministic (p. 22). The bibliography is only a pointer to minlp.com (p. 30).
- **Cross-edition observation** (2021 values read here; the 2000 and 2005 values are those reported in the s03 cards of S097 and S185): probing went from off (v4.0) to all branching variables every third level (2005) to automatic (PDo −2, p. 25); local searches in preprocessing from 1 to 10 to automatic (NumLoc −2, p. 26); the reduction-pass caps (maxredpass, maxnodepass), the probing schedule (pfreq) and the NLP-relaxation switch (nlpdolin) listed in 2005 do not appear in the 2021 option tables (§7, pp. 21-30). The relative tolerance is 10^-9 here (p. 21) against 0.1 in the 2005 GAMS chapter, which ties it to GAMS optcr; the two interfaces are not like-for-like.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (steps 1 and 4): range reduction is part of BARON's one-paragraph definition (p. 2); every family is on by default with its own switch, and the costliest one, probing, is rationed automatically (pp. 24-25); log lines marked "+" are probing subproblems (p. 12).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (steps 2 and 4): for problems with integers "most of BARON's time is spent on solving MIP relaxations" (p. 21); convex functions enter as outer approximators (NOuter1, NOutPerVar, NOutIter, OutGrid; p. 24); NLP subsolvers are switched during the search "according to problem characteristics and solver performance" (p. 28). FilterSD is still allowed by default in this edition (AllowFilterSD 1, p. 28), i.e. before the 2022.1.19 change to off that SKILL.md Method 5 cites.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (step 4, the instrument): each reduction family, local search, subsolver choice and cut-round count is an option with a documented default (pp. 21-30), the switches that ablations such as S090 and S116 rely on; the log names a group paper to cite (p. 11).
- Heuristic 7 ✅ (card only): the statuses above (pp. 12-13); the multi-start mode is introduced with "To gain insight into the difficulty of a nonlinear program" (p. 19). The sentence "It does not prove global optimality." that SKILL.md quotes from manual §10.4 is not in this edition.
- Heuristic 6 ✅ (card only): DeltaTerm's nondeterminism is flagged with its cause (p. 22); MaxTime counts CPU time for one thread (p. 23).
- Heuristic 3 ✅ (card only): access violations are to be reported to the author (p. 13).
- Heuristic 2 ⚠ (card only): bounds are asked for "as many problem variables as possible" (p. 3), but the sentence on "physical or problem-specific knowledge" (manual §4.1 in SKILL.md) and the warning that convexity and relaxation-only declarations are believed without verification (manual §13.4 in SKILL.md Workflow B1) are not in this edition (declarations on p. 8; whole manual read).
- New-pattern candidate: "self-deciding defaults": many options default to a value meaning that BARON decides (PDo −2, NumLoc −2, NLPSol −1, LPSol −1, DoLocal 1, BrVarStra, BrPtStra and NodeSel 0; pp. 25-28), and the cross-edition trend above replaces fixed numbers by such values.

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a solver's output, separate certificates from estimates: print a distinct status when the premise of the guarantee fails (missing bounds), and say that the printed bounds may then be invalid (pp. 12-13).
- Give each expensive component an automatic default plus an explicit off value, so that users get adaptive behavior and experimenters a clean switch (pp. 24-26).
- Document which options make runs nondeterministic and why (DeltaTerm relies on CPU-time measurements) (p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "Even though relaxation bounds are printed on the screen to give the user a feeling for convergence, these bounds may not be valid for the problem at hand." (p. 13)
- "Because it relies on CPU time measurements, which may depend on machine load, this option may result in nondeterministic behavior." (p. 22)

### H012 · Announcement: Howard Rosenbrock Prize 2020 (Optimization and Engineering 2021, DOI 10.1007/s11081-021-09649-5)

**Read level**: full (both pages). Per the work-list note, an editorial announcement (CV item C4.10) whose correction notice is S297, which is not in this batch and was not read. First-page check: title and "Nikolaos V. Sahinidis", Georgia Tech ISyE and ChBE, match (p. 1). Voice: as in S304, the paragraph on the winning paper is the committee's citation (Luis Zuluaga, chair; Michel Gamache; Frauke Liers) (pp. 1-2).

### S116 · Spectral relaxations and branching strategies for global optimization of mixed-integer quadratic programs (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2021, DOI 10.1137/19m1271762; arXiv:2010.04822)

**Read level**: full (text pages 1-30: abstract, contributions, §2 review, §3 relaxations with all propositions and proofs, §3.4 recipe and Theorems 1-2, §4 branching, §5 implementation, §6 experiments, §7 conclusions; figure data and references only skimmed). Version read: arXiv:2010.04822v1, dated 13 October 2020 (p. 1); the SIOPT version was not read. First-page check: title and "Nikolaos V. Sahinidis", CMU Chemical Engineering, match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Carlos J. Nohra and Arvind U. Raghunathan (Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the journal paper behind the Nohra thesis items that SKILL.md already cites under Method 5 (the KKT check) and Heuristic 4 (solver options); both appear in the paper itself (pp. 22, 23). The test libraries are the authors' own (ref. [12], p. 32; p. 23).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: relaxation quality drives spatial branch-and-bound for nonconvex QPs and MIQPs; polyhedral relaxations are weak and SDPs strong but costly (pp. 2, 4, 10). Entry: a "very well-known" device, convexifying xᵀQx with the smallest eigenvalue of Q, already gives relaxations often much tighter than the polyhedral ones of state-of-the-art solvers "despite its simplicity" (pp. 2, 8).
- **D2 Core idea**: three convex QP relaxations in the original variables: EIG (a perturbation by −λmin(Q)), GEIG (the generalized eigenvalue of (Q, I + AᵀA), using the equalities) and EIGNS (λmin(ZᵀQZ) on the nullspace of A), approximated without computing Z by increasing δ in λmin(Q, I + δAᵀA) and dropping the term that would ill-condition the QP (pp. 7-14). Each equals a particular SDP (Propositions 2, 5, 9; pp. 8, 10-11, 14); EIGNS is the best of a four-step class of such relaxations (Theorem 1, p. 18) and lies below the SDPs (Theorem 2, p. 19). Unlike QCR, the perturbation is recomputed at every node on the unfixed submatrix and costs an eigenvalue problem rather than an SDP (p. 10). For binary QPs, branching takes the variable with the largest entry of the eigenvector of λmin, an O(|B|) proxy for the rule that maximizes the children's smallest eigenvalue (pp. 19-21). In BARON the QP class joins the LP/NLP/MILP portfolio; a QP solution is used only if it passes a KKT test; LP and QP solve frequencies adapt to the observed bound difference, and the MILP relaxation is skipped when the QP is stronger (pp. 21-23).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: finite bounds; Q symmetric and possibly indefinite; the inequalities Cx ≤ d are not used for convexification (p. 1); the strong-duality proofs need a point strictly inside the bounds and inequalities (pp. 8, 11); spectral branching only for nonconvex binary QPs (pp. 19, 22).
- **D4 Analysis** (brief): the Lagrangian dual of a generic SDP (Proposition 1, pp. 6-7); Slater's condition by an explicit strictly feasible pair (pp. 8, 11); where Slater fails (EIGNS), weak duality plus an explicit primal construction X̂ = x̂x̂ᵀ + γZv(Zv)ᵀ (pp. 14-16); Rayleigh-quotient orderings (pp. 9-10, 12) and a monotone limit in δ (pp. 12-13); a Gershgorin-circle argument behind the branching proxy (pp. 20-21).
- **D5 Experiments**: GAMS 30.1.0, Xeon X5650, one thread, 10^-6, 500 s, CPLEX and GUROBI forced into global mode, 1551 instances (960 CBQP, 30 QSAP, 246 BoxQP, 315 EIQP) (p. 23). Root gaps: SDPs tightest, spectral next, RLT and the separable QP weak; SDPs one to two orders of magnitude slower than the spectral QPs (p. 24). Builds BARONnoqp, BARONnosb and BARON (default) (pp. 24, 26): on 412 nontrivial instances solved by either, BARON is at least ten times faster than BARONnoqp on nearly 50% (p. 27). At least twice as fast as CPLEX on 80% of 445 instances (p. 28) and as GUROBI on more than 60% of 380, with similar gaps to GUROBI on most unsolved instances (p. 29). Against an in-house QCR build (BARONqcr): weaker root bounds on 85% (CBQP) and 93% (QSAP) of the instances, yet at least ten times faster on nearly 70% of 442 instances (p. 30). No hold-out set is mentioned (§6 read in full).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: four contributions: theory (SDP equivalences, best in class), algorithm (branching) and software (the default BARON) (pp. 2-3, 26, 30). Limits are shown rather than stated: many BoxQP and EIQP instances stay unsolved by every solver (p. 27); gaps similar to GUROBI's on most unsolved instances (p. 29); QCR's root bound is tighter (p. 30). The conclusion states no limitation and no future work (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (steps 1, 3, 4, 5): relaxations are compared on root gap and solve time together (pp. 24-25); a cheap QP class reproduces much of the SDP bound (Theorem 2, p. 19; p. 24); the relaxation class is switched per node from observed bounds (pp. 22-23); QP answers pass a KKT check (p. 22). The QCR comparison is the clearest case of strength counting only at speed: the stronger static root bound loses to cheaper relaxations re-derived at every node (p. 30).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (steps 3-4): the fixed protocol (p. 23); named builds, including a rival method rebuilt inside BARON (BARONqcr, p. 29); the feature is "the default version of this solver" (p. 26).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: other solvers are compared on the authors' own libraries without a hold-out (pp. 23, 27-29), the say-do gap SKILL.md records for his own products; the baselines get their strongest fair mode (p. 23), as in Heuristic 4 ✅.
- Heuristic 5 ⚠ variant (card only): the cheap branching proxy is scored against complete enumeration (the exact rule) and against the worst rule, by a normalized gap on random matrices, before use (pp. 20-21): a ceiling check for a hand-made rule rather than a learned one.
- New-pattern candidate: "re-derive cheap relaxations per node rather than adopt a stronger static reformulation" (pp. 10, 30).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before building an expensive relaxation, test whether a classical cheap one (smallest-eigenvalue convexification) already beats the solver's default, and refine it only then (pp. 2, 8; §6.1).
- Place a cheap relaxation exactly in the hierarchy by proving it equal to a specific SDP: Slater by an explicit interior point, and where Slater fails, weak duality plus an explicit primal construction (pp. 8, 11, 14-16).
- Approximate an expensive projected quantity by a monotone limit computed once at the root in a few multiplicative steps (δ times 10, at most 5 iterations, relative tolerance 10^-3) (pp. 12-13, 22).
- Score a cheap heuristic rule against the exact enumeration rule and the worst rule with a normalized percentage gap before putting it in the solver (pp. 20-21).
- Run two relaxation classes side by side and adapt their solve frequencies multiplicatively from the observed bound difference (pp. 22-23).
- To compare with a rival method, rebuild it inside your own solver as a named variant, so that only the relaxation and the branching differ (p. 29).
