### S063 · Review and comparison of algorithms and software for mixed-integer derivative-free optimization (Journal of Global Optimization 2022, doi:10.1007/s10898-021-01085-0)

**Read level**: full. Pp. 1-27 read in full, including Tables 1-5 (pp. 8-9, 14, 24-25) and figure captions. The figures are plots whose curves are not in the text file, so only the numbers stated in the text were used. The reference list (pp. 28-30) was skimmed. The Online Supplement (Tables S1-S26, Sections B-C) is not in the file and was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Nikolaos Ploskas (first author; University of Western Macedonia, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received 1 July 2020 and accepted 20 August 2021, with Sahinidis at Georgia Tech (p. 1). The paper says it extends Rios & Sahinidis 2013 (22 implementations, 502 problems) "from the continuous to the mixed-integer case" (p. 2). Three of its motivating applications are the group's own DFO uses: tuning BARON [41], GPU parameter tuning [63] and heat-exchanger circuitry [58] (p. 2; references pp. 29-30).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a benchmark gap, not an algorithm. Earlier works "compare several DFO solvers in the context of specific applications or small collections of test problems", so "a systematic comparison of solvers on a large collection of problems is missing" (p. 2). Problem (1) has bound constraints and both integer and continuous variables. Algebraic forms of f and its derivatives are assumed unavailable, so the problems are treated as black boxes (p. 2). The paper poses two questions: which solver is more likely to reach global or near-global solutions on mixed-integer and pure-integer problems, and how solution quality changes with problem size (p. 2). It argues that the field is immature from citation counts: the most-cited MIDFO papers have 138-210 Google Scholar citations as of 15 March 2021 (Table 2, p. 9), "suggesting that MIDFO is nowhere close to being a mature area" (p. 7).
  - Transferable: open a benchmark paper with the two or three questions it will answer. Support the claim that the field is immature with a dated, sourced count (Table 2, p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: The paper reviews the algorithms first (local, global and heuristic; §2, pp. 3-7) and gives a timeline (Table 1, p. 8). It then benchmarks against optima certified by a global solver. The rule for admitting solvers: open-source or commercial tools that "can handle discrete variables explicitly", with "reported computational experience in the literature for this class of problems" (p. 7). Footnotes exclude codes that cannot solve pure-integer problems: DAKOTA/EA, TOMLAB/EGO, ARBFMIP, GLCCLUSTER, and RBFSOLVE, which depends on GLCCLUSTER (pp. 9-10). Ground truth comes from BARON on each problem (p. 13). Solvers are judged on three yardsticks (p. 13):
  - whether they find a near-optimal solution;
  - whether they find the best solution among the solvers;
  - how much they improve a given starting point.

  The verdict is a minimal covering set of solvers (§5.4, pp. 19-21). Kept from 2013: the classification into model-based vs direct search, global vs local, and stochastic vs deterministic (p. 2). Changed: the problem class, the source of test problems (MINLPLib 2, p. 13) and the solver field (13 solvers, p. 1). The paper does not describe any other part of the 2013 design, and the 2013 paper is not in this batch.
  - Transferable: state in one paragraph what is inherited from your own earlier benchmark (taxonomy, design) and what is new (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Bounds must be finite (X is compact, p. 3). Local optimality depends on an integer neighbourhood that the user defines (p. 3). The stationarity definitions assume that f is continuously differentiable in the continuous variables (p. 3). The test problems are algebraic MINLPLib 2 models whose constraints were dropped and whose variables made redundant by that were eliminated. In addition, 79 continuous problems were given integrality constraints "to have a representative sample of non-binary discrete problems" (p. 13). Problems are capped at 500 variables because "most derivative-free optimization solvers are designed for low-dimensional unconstrained problems" (p. 13). Missing bounds were replaced by the box [-10,000, 10,000] (p. 13). For those problems, the certified optimum is therefore the optimum over an artificial box (inferred).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: There are no theorems or proofs, so D4 is n/a for proofs. The formal devices are four definitions of local optimality for mixed-integer problems: local minimum, stationary point, strong stationary point, and Newby and Ali's combined local minimum (Defs. 1-4, pp. 3-4). The evaluation devices:
  - a success test: within 1% or 0.01 of the global optimum, whichever is larger (p. 13);
  - Moré and Wild's starting-point improvement test, f(x0) - f_solver >= (1 - τ)(f(x0) - f_L), with f_L set to the global solution (pp. 18-19);
  - the minimal sufficient solver set, which leaves out any solver whose solved problems are a strict subset of another solver's (p. 20);
  - a scaled objective, 1 - |f_L - f_solver| / (1e-10 + |f_L|) clipped at 0, used to compare variance across problems of different scale (p. 21).
  - Transferable: when a certified optimum is available, use it as f_L in the Moré and Wild test, so that "improvement" means a share of the largest possible reduction (p. 18).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **Setup** (p. 13):
    - 13 solvers, with versions in Table 3. DFLBOX, DFLGEN and MISO have no version listed (p. 14).
    - 267 problems: 176 pure-integer and 91 mixed-integer (p. 3); by size, 52 small, 102 medium and 113 large (p. 17). Separately, 78 problems are binary and 74 non-binary discrete (p. 16). That split covers 152 of the 267, and the paper does not say how the rest are classified.
    - Hardware: Xeon W-2123 with 32 GB, CentOS 7.
    - Budget: 2500 function evaluations per run, with no reason given for that number.
    - Starting points: every solver gets the same five starting points, drawn uniformly from the box. MISO adds symmetric Latin hypercube points. DAKOTA/SOGA ignores the starts and runs five times. The deterministic TOMLAB DIRECT codes also ignore the starts and run once.
    - Settings: defaults throughout. The paper says "we did not tune solvers in any way to the problems at hand" (p. 13).
  - **Results by class**: median-case results are in Tables S2-S14 and best-case results in S15-S26. The figures show the best-case fraction of problems solved against the evaluation budget (p. 14).
    - All problems: NOMAD 77%, MISO 76%, TOMLAB/MSNLP 12% (p. 14).
    - Pure-integer: MISO 85% (p. 15).
    - Binary: MISO 81% (p. 16).
    - Mixed-integer: NOMAD 84% (p. 16).
    - Large: MISO 59%. TOMLAB/MSNLP solves none (p. 18).
  - **Other analyses**:
    - Two 2-D illustrative examples plot every evaluated point of every solver (Figs. 1-2, pp. 11-12).
    - Improvement of the starting point is plotted against τ (pp. 18-20).
    - Minimal covering sets: BFO, DAKOTA/SOGA, DFLBOX, MISO, NOMAD and SNOBFIT solve 93% of all problems together; DFLBOX, NOMAD and SNOBFIT solve 95% of the mixed-integer problems (p. 20).
    - Variance across runs is "small for all solvers" (p. 22).
    - Effort is reported as total evaluations, as the evaluation at which the best point was found (Table 4, p. 24), and in CPU seconds (Table 5, p. 25).
    - Because the algebraic test functions are cheap, CPU time here measures solver overhead. The paper notes that evaluation counts govern when the function is expensive (p. 22). Effort is also read as a Pareto plot of evaluations against success (p. 23).
  - **Not in the text**: no developer rounds and no held-out problem set.
  - Transferable: report two effort counts, total evaluations ("tot") and the evaluation at which the final best point was first reached ("opt") (Table 4, p. 24). The gap between them shows how much of the budget a solver spends after it has stopped improving.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper continues his own 2013 study [62] and adopts its taxonomy (p. 2). The review covers four groups of work:
  - the Audet-Dennis pattern-search line and its MADS extensions (pp. 4-5);
  - Liuzzi-Lucidi-Rinaldi line searches, Vicente's implicit and densely discrete problems, Newby and Ali's BOBYQA extension, and Porcelli and Toint's BFO (p. 5);
  - surrogate methods: Holmström, Davis-Ierapetritou, Müller's SO-MI, SO-I and MISO, Costa-Nannicini, and Larson-Leyffer-Palkar-Wild (p. 6);
  - metaheuristics (p. 6).

  The review is descriptive. It weighs method classes in general terms, for example local direct search being "highly dependent on the initial point" (p. 4), and leaves the verdict to the benchmark.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract leads with counts and named winners (p. 1), and the conclusion ends on a verdict about using solvers as a portfolio (p. 27; quoted below). Hedges are moderate, for example "for the problems considered in this study" (p. 19). Some numbers do not agree across the text, and the paper does not acknowledge the differences:
  - The 93% covering set has six solvers including DAKOTA/SOGA on p. 20, but five, without DAKOTA/SOGA, in the conclusion (p. 27).
  - MISO solves 59% of large problems on p. 18 but 54% on p. 20.
  - DAKOTA/SOGA is grouped with solvers reaching 40-42% on pure-integer problems on p. 15, but given 31% on p. 17. Its mixed-integer score is 13% and then 16% in consecutive sentences on p. 17.
  - The abstract names NOMAD the best performer on non-binary discrete and on small problems (p. 1). In the text, four solvers share 80-92% on non-binary discrete problems (p. 16), and DAKOTA/MADS ties NOMAD at 100% on small problems (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a survey, a benchmark and a public data set (data URL on p. 27). Stated limits: existing solvers "cannot solve large problems and pure-integer problems efficiently" (p. 26); algorithms for large problems are needed, and constrained problems are left to future work (p. 27). Unstated limits:
  - the 2500-evaluation budget is not tied to any application;
  - the test problems are cheap algebraic models with their constraints removed, some of them inside artificial boxes (p. 13);
  - there are no developer rounds and no hold-out set;
  - the numerical inconsistencies listed under D7 are not acknowledged.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): the method carried out step by step on a new class:
  - step 1: every available implementation with reported experience on the class (p. 7);
  - step 2: optima certified by BARON (p. 13);
  - step 3: identical starting points and bounding boxes for all solvers (pp. 3, 13);
  - step 5: an explicit success test; median and best runs; problems and results in the Online Supplement; a printed data URL (pp. 3, 13-14, 27);
  - step 6: the smallest covering set of solvers and a stated scope (pp. 19-21, 27).
- Method 4 (variant ⚠): the developer rounds and the structurally new hold-out of step 4 do not appear in the 2022 text: defaults only (p. 13). Step 3's budget tied to an application is replaced by a bare 2500-evaluation limit (p. 13). SKILL.md's say-do line "✅ for the 2013 and 2022 studies" (Method 4) holds for 2022 on steps 1-3, 5 and 6, but not on step 4.
- New-pattern candidate: *show the search before the score*. Before any statistics, the paper plots the evaluated points of all 13 solvers on two 2-D problems, to show local vs global and pattern vs random search (pp. 11-12).
- New-pattern candidate: *benchmark sequel on the adjacent class*. His own 2013 taxonomy and study design are carried over to the mixed-integer class (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a black-box test set with certified optima from an algebraic library. Drop the constraints, eliminate the variables this makes redundant, and impose integrality on continuous models to get general-integer cases. Then certify each optimum with a deterministic global solver (p. 13).
- Give every solver the same random starting points from the box. Run a solver that ignores the starts once if it is deterministic, and as many times as there are starts if it is stochastic. Report median and best runs (pp. 13-14).
- Count a run as a success when it is within 1% or 0.01 of the certified optimum, whichever is larger (p. 13).
- Report a minimal covering set of solvers for each problem class. Leave out any solver whose solved problems are a strict subset of another solver's (p. 20).
- Give the "tot" and "opt" evaluation counts next to CPU time, and say which one matters for cheap functions and which for expensive ones (pp. 22-25).

**Quotes**:
- "We used the general-purpose global optimization solver BARON [70] to obtain the global solution of each problem." (p. 13)
- "Overall, existing algorithms in this field are complementary in their performance. They should be used collectively rather than individually." (p. 27)

### S108 · Deterministic symbolic regression with derivative information: General methodology and application to equations of state (AIChE Journal 2022, doi:10.1002/aic.17457)

**Read level**: full (accepted author manuscript). The text pp. 1-16 was read in full. The references (pp. 17-20) were skimmed. The lists of figures and tables (p. 21) and Tables 1-8 (pp. 29-36) were read. The figure pages (pp. 22-28) were read for their captions only, because the plots are not legible in the text file. Pp. 37-50 hold only a citation line and figure file names.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Marissa R. Engle (first author; Carnegie Mellon, Department of Chemical Engineering, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: extends the Cozad-Sahinidis global MINLP formulation of symbolic regression (refs. 15-16, p. 18), which it reviews as its starting point (p. 4). Funded through IDAES by the DOE Office of Fossil Energy (p. 17); Sahinidis is at Georgia Tech (p. 1). SKILL.md Method 3 (Practice) cites it as the case where symbolic regression is posed as a global MINLP because the fixed basis is too narrow.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters from two limitations. Fixed-form regression restricts a model to known functional terms (p. 1). Deterministic symbolic regression "has been limited due to the complexity of the search space of existing formulations" (p. 1). Its proposal is to bring derivative values or constraints into the regression. This "reduces the search space and guides the model selection", constraining the regression "to functions that are more physically meaningful" (p. 3). The application is the alpha function of cubic equations of state. Thermodynamic consistency there requires alpha to be continuous, decreasing and convex, with a negative third derivative (Le Guennec et al.; p. 13). Neural-network property models are set aside because they need many measurements, and "the larger neural networks can exceed problem sizes for commercial solvers" (p. 12).
  - Transferable: tie the method to an application whose physics already fixes the signs of the derivatives (the rules for alpha functions, p. 13), so that the derivative constraints are not ad hoc.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The key idea is auxiliary expression trees, one per derivative order and per differentiation variable, built in parallel with the main tree. They share its binary operator choices, and the chain rule computes their node values from the main tree's node values (pp. 7-9; Fig. 1, p. 22; Tables 3-4, pp. 31-32). Derivative data or sign constraints are then imposed through optional constraints h' and h'', usually at the root (pp. 8-9). Kept from Cozad-Sahinidis: the disjunctive tree model, with one disjunction per node over operators and operands, and its big-M reformulation (pp. 4-6). The tree-structure constraints (1e)-(1f) come from Kim-Leyffer-Balaprakash (p. 6). New in this paper:
  - the derivative trees;
  - cuts against trivial operations on the same variable: x - x, x/x, x + x replaced by 2x, and x·x replaced by x² (p. 9; Table 5, p. 33);
  - a small penalty on the number of nodes, λΣy, that breaks ties between equally accurate trees (pp. 9-10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - The tree has a fixed depth D and N = 2^D - 1 nodes (p. 4).
  - The operator set is fixed: +, -, ×, ÷, exp, log, √, square and cube (p. 14).
  - Node values have bounds, from which interval arithmetic gives the big-M constants. A tolerance ε enforces the operator domains (p. 6). For the derivative trees, either more big-M values are computed or "a large bound" is applied (pp. 8-9).
  - Each auxiliary tree differentiates with respect to one predictor, and inputs that are functions of T get their own derivatives (pp. 7-8).
  - The loss is the sum of squared errors, which can be swapped for another (p. 5).
  - Derivative constraints act only at the data points, which the authors call a weakness (p. 15).
  - λ is "selected close to zero" (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: There are no theorems or proofs, so D4 is n/a for proofs. The devices are formulation constructions:
  - node disjunctions rewritten with big-M (p. 6);
  - forward differentiation written as constraints. Products, quotients and unary operators need the main tree's intermediate values (p. 7), and second derivatives need the values of both trees (p. 8);
  - constraints that shrink the search space, each listed in a table with an example and its source (Table 5, p. 33).

  The table also lists two rewrites that were not imposed, marked "Not constrained": multilevel logarithm and multilevel exponential.
  - Transferable: list every redundancy cut in a table with an example and its source, including the ones you chose not to impose (Table 5, p. 33).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: All runs use BARON 21.3.26 (ref. 22) on an Intel Core i7-5600U (p. 10).
  - **Toy 1** (p. 10; Table 6, p. 34): two points, x = -2.25 and x = 2, fit both z = x² - 2 and the line z = -0.25x + 2.5. Without sign constraints the regression finds the line in 8.31 s. With sign constraints on the derivative it recovers the quadratic in 45.63 s.
  - **Toy 2** (p. 11): the saddle z = x² - y², with 10 random points and 3 extra points for derivative signs. Each regression finishes in under 60 s. From samples on one axis only, the sign constraints recover the true surface (Fig. 3).
  - **Case study setup** (p. 14): argon and CO2 alpha functions in the Peng-Robinson equation of state, fitted to NIST saturated vapour pressure and saturated liquid isobaric heat capacity. Trees of depth 3-5 are used, with a node penalty. Runs last 1000 s, and each passes its improved upper bound to the next as a cutoff. When a run brings no improvement, a final 100,000 s run follows.
  - **Case study results** (pp. 15-16):
    - Consistent fits mostly use exponentials.
    - Without the constraints, the fits are polynomials or have discontinuities from divisions.
    - Reported failures: at depth 5, argon gives negative alpha values or rising slopes at high temperature. CO2 is asymptotic at low temperature and negative at high temperature.
    - The CO2 depth-5 fit is refitted with added points where it violates the constraints (Fig. 7, p. 16).
  - **Table 8** (p. 36) gives a "Training Error" and a "Test Error" without describing how the data were split. It shows that the corrected CO2 fit raises the training error from 0.00032 to 0.00516, a cost the text does not discuss.
  - **Not reported**: the number of data points; the optimality gap or global-optimality status of any case-study run; any baseline (genetic programming, AI Feynman, ALAMO).
  - Transferable: run a global solver in fixed time slices, passing each slice's incumbent as a cutoff to the next, and finish with one long run once a slice stops improving (p. 14).
- **D6 Literature positioning**:
  - Rival approaches: genetic programming, described as "the traditional way" (p. 2), Kaizen programming, and AI Feynman (pp. 2-3).
  - Its own deterministic lineage (p. 3): Cozad et al., the first deterministic MINLP for global symbolic regression; Neumann et al.; Kim et al.; Austel et al., a heuristic that works "at the expense of not guaranteeing global optimality"; and Wei and Yuan's generalized Benders decomposition.
  - The big-M form cites Grossmann's 2002 review (p. 6, ref. 21).
  - The thermodynamics literature serves as the source of the constraints, not as competition (p. 13).

  No competing method is run.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution is stated in the abstract as a "novel deterministic mixed-integer nonlinear programming formulation" (p. 1). The self-criticism is candid (p. 15; quoted below), and the hedging is open: "further evaluation is required" on which cuts help (p. 10). Promise and delivery differ in two places:
  - The introduction announces "constraints on the variance of intermediate values" (p. 3), but no such constraint is specified anywhere else in the text.
  - The closing claim that "overall, the functions obtained here adhere to the thermodynamic consistency constraints" (p. 16) comes right after violations outside the data range are reported (p. 15). It holds within the data range.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a formulation device for MINLP, plus insight into which alpha functions satisfy consistency (pp. 15-16). Stated limits: derivatives are constrained only at the data points, so extrapolated behaviour may break the physics (p. 15). The formulation needs a node penalty to be tractable (p. 14). Future work: more control of complexity, constraints that span more than the basic subtree of a parent node and its children, and use of the informational content of inputs and outputs (p. 16). Unstated: the problems are small (depth at most 5); no optimality gaps are given; there is no comparison with other symbolic-regression or surrogate tools.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (evidence ✅): the method carried out step by step:
  - steps 2-3: a basis seeded by physics, the alpha-function "motifs" T/Tc, 1 - T/Tc, ω and 1 - √(T/Tc) (Table 7, p. 35; p. 14), selected under a penalty for size (pp. 9-10);
  - step 4: first-principles constraints on the first and second derivatives, imposed during fitting (p. 14);
  - step 5: points added where the fitted model violates the physics, then a refit (pp. 15-16);
  - step 6: reading the model. The acentric factor drops out because it is zero for argon and constant for CO2, and exponentials produce the alternating signs of the derivatives (p. 15);
  - step 7: symbolic regression as a global MINLP solved with BARON (pp. 3, 10).
- Method 3 (variant ⚠): the "certify them" of step 4 is not met. Constraints hold only at sample points, and the fits break the physics outside the data range (p. 15). The case-study runs of the "global" MINLP are reported without gaps or termination status (pp. 14-16, 36). The added points carry constraints only and need no new experiment, unlike error-maximization sampling (p. 15).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the reduction of the search space moves into the model:
  - redundancy and symmetry cuts (p. 9);
  - derivative information as a way to shrink the search space (p. 3);
  - big-M constants from interval arithmetic (p. 6);
  - incumbent cutoffs passed between restarts "to fathom worse solutions" (p. 14).

  Range reduction at the nodes is not discussed.
- New-pattern candidate: *constraint-only knowledge points*. Derivative-sign constraints at extra inputs replace measurements (pp. 10-11). Points added where the fit violates the physics need no output value and stay out of the sum of squared errors (p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *time-sliced global solve with incumbent cutoff* (p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode each derivative order as an auxiliary expression tree that shares the operator binaries of the main tree. The derivative rule for each operator becomes a table entry (Tables 3-4, pp. 31-32).
- Impose qualitative physics, such as the sign of a derivative, at extra points instead of measuring more data (pp. 10-11).
- Where the fitted model breaks a physical rule, add constraint-only points and refit (p. 15).
- In expression-tree MINLPs, cut trivial operations on the same variable: x - x, x/x, x + x and x·x (p. 9).
- Compute big-M constants by interval arithmetic from bounds on the node values (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Enforcing gradient conditions replaces the need for additional measurements and results in an accurate model." (p. 10)
- "This highlights a weakness of our current symbolic regression formulation in that it only addresses derivatives at the data points." (p. 15)

### S133 · SDP-quality bounds via convex quadratic relaxations for global optimization of mixed-integer quadratic programs (Mathematical Programming 2022, doi:10.1007/s10107-021-01680-9; arXiv:2106.13721)

**Read level**: full (arXiv v1 preprint). Pp. 1-25 were read in full, and Appendix A (pp. 25-27) was read. The references (pp. 26-28) were skimmed. Figures 1-6 are plots whose curves are not in the text file, so only the numbers stated in the text were used.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Carlos J. Nohra (first author; Carnegie Mellon, Department of Chemical Engineering, p. 1); Arvind U. Raghunathan (Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the second of two papers with the same authors that put quadratic relaxations into BARON. The first, [12] (SIAM J. Optim. 2021), used uniform diagonal perturbations; this one generalizes them to nonuniform ones (p. 2). The authors publish the test libraries on MERL's FTP server [13] (p. 28). The paper cites the group's 2011 review and comparison of SDP relaxations, [2], among the SDP relaxations (pp. 2, 26). SKILL.md Method 5 (Practice) cites it for its "SDP-quality bounds".

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is min xᵀQx + qᵀx subject to Ax = b. Q may be indefinite, and each x_i lies in a bounded union of closed intervals (p. 1). The entry is an explicit trade-off between cost and strength. Branch-and-bound efficiency "depends to a large extent on the tightness and the computational cost of the relaxations" (p. 1). Their own spectral relaxations were "inexpensive to solve" and tight (p. 2). Saxena et al.'s projection of an SDP relaxation is "nearly as tight as the original SDP", but its separation "can be very expensive to solve" (p. 2). The starting point is Dong's semi-infinite view of a diagonal SDP (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The key idea is quadratic cuts v >= xᵀ(Q + diag(d))x - dᵀy, where y_i stands for x_i² inside conv(C_i) (pp. 3-4). The perturbations d are those that make Q + diag(d) + αAᵀA positive semidefinite. Because of the α term the cuts can be nonconvex, but they define a convex set on Ax = b (Prop. 1, p. 4).
  - **Changes relative to Dong**:
    - αAᵀA is added inside the semidefinite constraint, which gives bounds at least as tight (pp. 2, 6).
    - The term α‖Ax - b‖² appears only in the separation problem. This keeps the sparsity of Q and avoids ill-conditioning when α is large (p. 12).
    - Dong's fixed regularizer λΣ[d_i]⁺ is replaced by a smooth one, ρdᵀd, whose weight ρ adapts (pp. 14, 17).
  - **Kept from Dong**: barrier coordinate minimization on a log-det problem, with Sherman-Morrison updates (pp. 15-16), inside a cutting-surface loop (Algorithm 1, p. 12).
  - **Change relative to their own [12]**: uniform perturbations become nonuniform ones. The new relaxations are at least as tight by construction, because the loop starts from the spectral bound (p. 13).
  - **Inside BARON** (pp. 18-19):
    - The QCP cutting surface runs only at the root, for at most MaxNC = 20 iterations of one new cut each.
    - Every other node solves QPs that use the parent's perturbation, plus at most one new separation.
    - d_root is the root cuts' perturbations averaged with their Lagrange multipliers as weights (eq. 59). α is fixed at the root.
    - IPOPT solves the QCPs and CPLEX the QPs. An answer is used only if it satisfies the KKT conditions.
    - The dynamic relaxation selection of [12] sets how often polyhedral and quadratic relaxations run.
    - The QPs are used only if the root QCP bound beats BARON's LP bound.
  - Transferable: start each child node from its parent's relaxation parameters, so that its first bound is never below the parent's. Bounds then stay monotone at no extra cost (p. 19).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - A has full row rank m (p. 3).
  - Theorem 1 assumes that the nullspace SDP (25) has a strictly feasible point (p. 8).
  - Prop. 5 gives sufficient conditions for that point (p. 9). The first is e_iᵀZ != 0; otherwise x_i is fixed and can be eliminated (pp. 9-10). The second is an interior point with l_i(x̃_i) = x̃_i² < u_i(x̃_i).
  - The authors note that the second condition "fails to hold" for binary variables (p. 10). The SDP-equivalence result therefore does not cover binary instances, such as the test set's 960 Cardinality Binary Quadratic Programs (p. 20).
  - For general integers, l_i(x_i) = x_i² gives an outer approximation, not the convex hull of C_i (p. 19).
  - The new relaxations run only on models of form (1) (p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The proofs place the semi-infinite relaxation (6)/(20) relative to the SDPs it imitates. Each finite set of cuts in Algorithm 1 outer-approximates that relaxation (pp. 1, 12, 25).
  - Prop. 1: convexity, by projecting onto the nullspace of A (pp. 4-5).
  - Prop. 2: the bound grows with α, by inclusion of the perturbation sets; the limit α → ∞ gives the tightest member (pp. 5-6).
  - Prop. 3: the sandwich μSDPd <= μSICPda <= μSDPda, from the self-duality of the PSD cone (pp. 6-7).
  - Prop. 4: every feasible X has the form xxᵀ + ZWZᵀ (pp. 7-8).
  - Theorem 1: SDP (15) has no strictly feasible point (eq. 21). It is therefore rewritten in nullspace coordinates (25), where Slater's condition can hold, and strong duality gives equality (pp. 7-9).
  - Prop. 5: a strictly feasible point, constructed explicitly (p. 9).
  - Props. 6-7 and Theorem 2: (15) is the best of the SDPs that lift valid quadratic equalities implied by Ax = b (pp. 10-11).
  - Example 1: the infimum of the separation problem need not be attained. The dual shows why: when P is nonempty, there is no strictly feasible dual point (p. 14).
  - Prop. 8: under ρdᵀd the optimum is attained, shown by a strictly feasible primal-dual pair (p. 15).
  - Prop. 9: a closed-form coordinate step from a quadratic first-order condition (pp. 15-16).
  - Transferable: if the SDP you want to match has no Slater point because of equality constraints, restate it in nullspace coordinates, where an interior point can exist, and run the duality argument there (Theorem 1, pp. 7-9).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **Protocol** (p. 20):
    - GAMS 30.1.0 on a Xeon X5650, one thread.
    - Subsolvers in the §6.1 root study: CPLEX 12.10 for QPs, IPOPT 3.12 for QCPs, MOSEK 9.1.9 for SDPs. Inside BARON, IPOPT solves the QCPs and CPLEX the QPs (p. 19).
    - Solvers compared: ANTIGONE 1.1, BARON 20.4, COUENNE 0.5, CPLEX 12.10, GUROBI 9.0, LINDOGLOBAL 12.0 and SCIP 6.0, with tolerances of 10⁻⁶ and a 500 s limit.
    - CPLEX's optimalitytarget 3 and GUROBI's nonconvex 2 force a global search; all other settings are defaults.
    - Test set: 960 CBQP, 30 QSAP, 246 BoxQP and 315 EIQP instances, from the authors' own library [13] (p. 20).
  - **§6.1, root bounds**: four selected instances (Fig. 1) and per-library profiles of the root gap, (μSDP - μQCP)/(μSDP - μQP) × 100, show the new separation beating Dong's (pp. 20-22). Only bounds are compared: no times for the SDP, the QCP or the separation are reported.
  - **§6.2, ablation**: BARON against BARONnoqc, the same 20.4 build with the new relaxations off and the spectral ones of [12] kept (p. 21).
    - Filtering: 367 instances where the new relaxations never activate and 62 that both versions solve in under 1 s are excluded, leaving 1122 (pp. 21-22).
    - The 259 instances solved by either version are compared on time, nodes and memory. The 863 solved by neither are compared on the relative gap (eq. 61). Results within 10% count as "similar" (pp. 22-23).
    - Losses are reported: on a few instances that both solve in under 10 s, BARONnoqc is slightly better despite the tighter bounds (p. 22).
    - Shifted geometric means fall by 29.6% for CPU time, 57.7% for nodes, 47.7% for memory and 25.7% for gaps (Table 1, p. 23). These figures pool all four libraries.
  - **§6.3, solver comparison**: per-library profiles against all seven solvers (Fig. 4, p. 24). The text claims "BARON is faster than the other solvers" only for CBQP and QSAP. For BoxQP and EIQP it claims only smaller gaps (p. 24), and the Fig. 4 curves are not in the text file. One-to-one comparisons (pp. 24-25):
    - against CPLEX: in nearly 90% of 453 instances BARON is at least 1.1 times faster, and in more than 98% of 974 instances its gap is smaller;
    - against GUROBI: in more than 80% of 391 instances BARON is faster, and in nearly 90% of 977 instances its gap is smaller.
  - Transferable: in an ablation, drop the instances where the feature never fires and the trivial ones (under 1 s for both). Compare instances solved by either version on time, nodes and memory, and instances solved by neither on the final gap, with a 10% "similar" band (pp. 21-23).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper is "partially inspired by" Dong (2016) and refines "his approach in several directions" (p. 2). It then beats Dong's separation head-to-head (§6.1, pp. 20-22). Saxena-Bonami-Lee get credit for tightness and criticism for cost (p. 2). The families of relaxations get one sentence: factorable and RLT, SDP, and convex quadratic (p. 2). BARON's portfolio is cited through the group's own papers [8, 10, 12, 21] (p. 18). Faye-Roupin supply the characterization behind Theorem 2 (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contributions are numbered: the class of relaxations, the SDP equivalence, the attainment analysis, and the regularized algorithm (pp. 2-3). The "SDP-quality" of the title rests on Figs. 1-2: bound trajectories on four selected instances, plus per-library profiles of the root gap measured between the spectral and SDP values (pp. 20-22). Claims are scoped "for our test problems" (pp. 1, 25), and the new BARON "outperforms ... CPLEX and GUROBI for many of our test problems" (p. 3). "Significant" means outside a 10% band, not a statistical test (pp. 22-23). The theory sections pair short, complete proofs with a paragraph of notation (p. 3). The conclusion only summarizes; it gives no limits and no future work (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory (the family of relaxations, the equivalence, the best-in-class result, attainment), an algorithm (regularized separation solved by coordinate minimization), software (in BARON 20.4) and a public library of instances [13]. Stated limits appear in the body:
  - the binary case is not covered by Prop. 5 (p. 10);
  - general integers get only an outer approximation (p. 19);
  - QCPs run only at the root because they are "at least an order of magnitude more expensive" (p. 19);
  - easy instances get slower (p. 22).

  Unstated limits:
  - the cost claims ("an order of magnitude", p. 19; "only requires a few restarts", p. 16) come without data;
  - subsolver answers rejected by the KKT check are not counted;
  - MaxNC = 20 and the parameter values (p. 17) are not justified;
  - the ablation is not reported per library;
  - the evaluation libraries are the authors' own, with no hold-out;
  - there is no future work (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (evidence ✅): the method carried out in a production solver:
  - step 3: a cheap object approximates the SDP bound, as the title says. A few cuts give "a good approximation of the lower bounds given by the SDP relaxations" (p. 20; pp. 1, 21-22);
  - rationing by cost: QCPs only at the root and cheap QPs elsewhere, because QCPs cost at least ten times more and one cut often suffices (p. 19);
  - step 4: dynamic relaxation selection (p. 19);
  - step 5: subsolver answers checked against the KKT conditions (p. 19);
  - step 6: the QPs are switched off when the root QCP does not beat the LP bound (p. 19);
  - numerical safety: the α‖Ax - b‖² term is kept out of the cuts to avoid ill-conditioning (p. 12), and ρ is raised when entries of d blow up (p. 16);
  - the relaxation is judged on time as well as bound, and the losses on easy instances are admitted (p. 22).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): step 1 is not met at the level of the relaxation. §6.1 compares root bounds only, with no time or failure counts per relaxation (pp. 20-22). Cost enters only through BARON's end-to-end CPU time (pp. 22-23).
- Method 2 (evidence ✅): the component is built into BARON 20.4 and judged against a matched switch: BARONnoqc keeps the spectral relaxations of [12] (p. 21). The protocol is fixed: GAMS, one thread, 10⁻⁶ tolerances, 500 s (p. 20). The instances are published as a library [13] (p. 28). The work continues an earlier BARON module ([12], pp. 2, 18). The baselines run in their strongest fair mode, CPLEX optimalitytarget 3 and GUROBI nonconvex 2 (p. 20), as in Heuristic 4.
- Method 2 (variant ⚠): step 4's "report library by library" holds for the root-bound and solver profiles (Figs. 2 and 4) but not for the ablation. Figs. 3 and 5-6 and Table 1 pool all four libraries (pp. 22-25). The evaluation set is the authors' own library [13], with no hold-out.
- Method 1 (evidence ✅, weak): node relaxations are built from the unfixed variables only (p. 19). Variables that Ax = b fixes (e_iᵀZ = 0) are eliminated before the theory applies (pp. 9-10). Nodes that become convex get a plain continuous relaxation (p. 19).
- New-pattern candidate: *prove where the cheap relaxation sits*. The semi-infinite relaxation behind the cuts is sandwiched between two SDP bounds and equals the stronger one under Slater's condition; that SDP is in turn the best of its class (pp. 6-11).
- New-pattern candidate: *expensive once at the root, inherited below*. d_root is a multiplier-weighted average, α is fixed at the root, and bounds stay monotone because children start from the parent's perturbation (pp. 18-19).
- New-pattern candidate: *make the separation problem well-posed*. The paper shows non-attainment (p. 14), uses a smooth regularizer that guarantees attainment (p. 15), and adapts the regularizer's weight by restarting (pp. 16-17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Approximate an SDP bound with quadratic cuts from nonuniform diagonal perturbations. Separate them by barrier coordinate minimization, with closed-form steps and Sherman-Morrison updates (pp. 12, 15-17).
- Use the equality constraints only inside the separation problem (αAᵀA), never inside the cut (pp. 4, 12).
- Regularize a separation SDP with ρdᵀd so that its optimum is attained. When |d| exceeds 10 times the shift µ, raise ρ and restart (pp. 15-17).
- Run the expensive relaxation once at the root. Then pass a multiplier-weighted root perturbation, and each parent's perturbation, down the tree (pp. 18-19).
- Measure a root bound on a scale between a cheap reference and the target SDP: (μSDP - μQCP)/(μSDP - μQP) × 100 (p. 21).
- In ablation reports, filter for instances where the feature is active and that are not trivial; analyse solved and unsolved instances apart; use shifted geometric means with shifts of 1 and 10 (pp. 21-23).
