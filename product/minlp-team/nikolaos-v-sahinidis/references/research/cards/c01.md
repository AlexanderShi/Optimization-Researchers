### S024 · Computational complexity of heat exchanger network synthesis (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2001, doi:10.1016/s0098-1354(01)00681-0)
**Read level**: full. All 38 manuscript pages: §§1-5 (pp. 1-25) and the Appendix (pp. 36-38) read completely; Table 1 (p. 27) read; Figure 4 (p. 26) garbled except its caption; nomenclature (pp. 28-32) and references (pp. 33-35) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Kevin C. Furman (first author); Sahinidis is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: UIUC chemical engineering (p. 1), NSF CTS-9704643 (p. 25). Part of a complexity-and-approximation line in process systems engineering: it names Ahmed & Sahinidis on process planning as the only earlier complexity result in the field (p. 2), the same pair's annotated HENS bibliography is cited as "To be submitted, 2000" (p. 33), and the sequel on approximation algorithms for the matches problem is listed as submitted in S002 (S002 p. 28).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: HENS is stated as a Given/develop list: hot and cold streams, heat capacities and flow rates, utilities and their costs; find the network of minimum annualized investment and operating cost (p. 2). The entry is a gap in a crowded field: over 300 papers in four decades by the authors' own count [10], and "no studies of its computational complexity" (p. 2). More broadly, process-synthesis subproblems lack formal complexity characterizations, with Ahmed & Sahinidis as "a notable exception" (p. 2). The object of study is each published formulation, named by its source, not an abstract HENS: the sequential targets (minimum utilities, minimum number of matches, vertical-heat-transfer matches) and three simultaneous MINLP formulations (pp. 5-25, Table 1 p. 27).
  - Transferable: when a heavily studied applied problem has no complexity classification, classify each published formulation by its source, so that the result speaks to the models people actually solve (Table 1, p. 27).
- **D2 Core idea**: The minimum-number-of-matches model M (Papoulias-Grossmann transshipment form, p. 8) is restricted to one temperature interval (K = 1), unit match costs, 3n sources with sizes in (B/4, B/2) and n sinks of demand B (problem M1-1, p. 10). Its decision version with β = 3n is equivalent to 3-PARTITION (Theorem 2, pp. 11-12), so M is NP-hard in the strong sense (Corollaries 1-3, p. 12). Every richer formulation is then shown to contain this core: VM and EVM by setting the penalty weights and the lower bounds L_ij to zero with K = 1 (pp. 14-15, Corollary 4); the Floudas-Ciric match-network model MN, the Ciric-Floudas HENS without decomposition UMN and the Yee-Grossmann stagewise model S by taking one interval (MN, UMN) or one stage (S), keeping only the fixed exchanger costs (area-cost exponents β_ij = 0, or zero area coefficients in S), setting utility costs to zero and, where it appears, Nmax = |I| + |J| + 1 (Theorems 3-5, pp. 18, 20-21, 23-25). Contrast: the minimum utilities problems are min-cost network flows and hence in P (Theorem 1, p. 7). The Appendix adds a weaker (ordinary) NP-hardness proof by restriction to the minimum binary knapsack (Theorem 6, Corollaries 5-7, pp. 36-38).
  - Transferable: prove hardness once for the simplest restriction, then show that each richer formulation reduces to it by zeroing weights and deleting the constraint blocks that become redundant (pp. 14-25).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: P ≠ NP (p. 3; the sign is lost in extraction). The balance Σ s_i = Σ d_j (eq. (1)) holds because the utilities problem is solved first (p. 8). The hard instance is given a physical reading: one temperature interval, all cold streams needing the same heat, no cost difference between matches (p. 10). The simultaneous models carry their own simplifications, which the proofs inherit: temperature-interval partitioning for MN and UMN (pp. 17, 19); isothermal mixing, no split stream through more than one exchanger and no by-passes for S (p. 21). The restrictions used are stated explicitly: Nmax = |I| + |J| + 1, β_ij = 0, K = 1 (p. 18); zero utility costs (p. 21); |ST| = 1, zero utility and area cost coefficients and T_out,H ≥ T_out,C + EMAT (eq. (17), pp. 23-24).
- **D4 Proof devices**:
  - Restriction to 3-PARTITION through a bipartite graph G(I ∪ J, E): necessity builds the match set from a partition; sufficiency counts arcs, since B/4 < s_i < B/2 forces exactly three sources per sink and one sink per source, so no stream is split (Theorem 2, pp. 11-12).
  - Decision problem first: the optimization problem is "at least as hard" (p. 10), and the strong NP-completeness of M1-1D carries to M1-1 and to M (Corollaries 1-3, p. 12).
  - Redundancy after restriction: variables that leave the objective make whole constraint blocks removable ((7) and (11) in VM1, p. 14; (16) and (19) in S1, pp. 24-25).
  - A literature theorem as a reduction step: Floudas et al. proved that every match set feasible for M admits a feasible network, so the network constraints (14) can be dropped (Theorem 3 proof, p. 18).
  - The polynomial part is solved inside the reduction: the utility loads come from the polynomial problem U, are fixed as bounds, and force the utility binaries to 1 (Theorem 4 proof, p. 21).
  - Membership in P by recognizing a min-cost network flow (strongly polynomial, O(m̄ log n̄ (m̄ + n̄ log n̄))) and, alternatively, an LP (Khachian), with the remark that general LP algorithms have worse complexity (p. 7).
  - Knapsack equivalence whose sufficiency step redefines residual supplies and closes with a transportation problem made feasible by the Northwest Corner rule (Theorem 6, pp. 37-38).
  - Transferable: for strong NP-hardness of a fixed-charge flow model, use the 3-PARTITION size window (B/4, B/2), so that counting arcs forces unsplit assignments (pp. 11-12).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none; the paper is a complexity analysis (pp. 1-38).
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits the reviews (Gundersen & Naess; Jezowski) and every formulation's authors, several of them from Grossmann's group (Papoulias & Grossmann; Gundersen & Grossmann; Floudas, Ciric & Grossmann; Yee & Grossmann), without criticizing them: the claim is a classification (pp. 2-3, 5, Table 1 p. 27). Complexity theory is taken from Garey & Johnson, Papadimitriou & Steiglitz and Lewis & Papadimitriou (p. 3). The only process-systems predecessor named is his own group's planning paper (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract concedes the headline before sharpening it: the characterization "may not be surprising", but the hardness is in the strong sense and already holds for very simple subproblems (p. 1). A two-page primer on P, NP and strong NP-hardness, written for a chemical-engineering readership, precedes the proofs (pp. 3-4, Fig. 1). Each section follows the same order: published formulation, restriction, theorem or corollary. The results close as a table (problem, target, source, complexity) and a reduction map (Fig. 4 and Table 1, pp. 26-27). The conclusion turns hardness into a suggestion, heuristics from 3-PARTITION and Knapsack (p. 25), and the Appendix explains why a weaker proof is kept (p. 36).
  - Transferable: after a hardness result, say what it licenses (which source problem's heuristics or exact methods now apply) rather than stopping at intractability (pp. 25, 36).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical: a classification of ten problems, the utilities problems in P and the matches, vertical-matches, match-network, non-decomposition and stagewise formulations NP-hard in the strong sense (Table 1, p. 27). There is no limits section; the reach of the claim is stated as an efficient exact algorithm being "highly unlikely" (p. 25). Not addressed: approximability (no inapproximability result), and the network-derivation problem N (minimum area cost network topology, nomenclature p. 28) gets no row of its own (Table 1, p. 27). Future directions: heuristics from 3-PARTITION and Knapsack (p. 25); Knapsack-like properties to exploit "in the design of exact and approximate algorithms for HENS" (p. 36); the results as a basis for analysing more complex HENS problems (p. 1) and as evidence about general process synthesis (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 variant ⚠: he enters HENS the way Method 4 enters a method area, by surveying everything that exists before proposing anything of his own: every published formulation is classified (ten rows, Table 1, p. 27), beside an annotated bibliography of more than 300 papers (pp. 2, 33). The instrument is a complexity classification, not a computational benchmark.
- Heuristic 1 variant ⚠: special classes appear in the hardness direction. The paper names the simplest class that is already hard (one interval, unit costs, equal sink demands, p. 10). That is what Workflow A step 4 asks for ("name what makes them hard"), but established by proof rather than by solver failure.
- New-pattern candidate "complexity before algorithms, then bounded heuristics": the hardness proof is followed by pointers to heuristics and to exact and approximate algorithms (pp. 25, 36); S002 describes the same programme as polynomial-time heuristics with analytically derived quality bounds (S002 p. 18). This is the "science of heuristics" line that the skill records as left after 2004 (Honest Boundary).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Strong NP-hardness of a fixed-charge transshipment or transportation model: restrict to one period, unit fixed costs, 3n supplies in (B/4, B/2) and n demands of B, and prove equivalence with 3-PARTITION by counting arcs in the bipartite match graph (pp. 10-12).
- Carry one hardness proof across a literature: for each richer formulation, zero the extra cost weights, fix the extra structure (one stage, one interval), and show that the remaining constraints are redundant for the restricted objective (pp. 14-25).
- Drop a whole constraint block in a reduction by citing a feasibility theorem (any feasible match set admits a network, p. 18), and fix polynomially solvable subproblems before reducing (p. 21).
- Summarize a classification as a table (problem, target, source, complexity) plus a reduction map (pp. 26-27).
- Keep a weaker hardness proof when its source problem suggests algorithms, and say why in one paragraph (p. 36).

**Quotes**:
- "While HENS has received so much attention by the process systems engineering community, there have been no studies of its computational complexity and it is not known whether efficient (polynomial) algorithms might exist for the problem." (p. 2)
- "The proofs suggest that heuristic algorithms that have performed well for the 3-PARTITION and the Knapsack may be found useful when extended to HENS." (p. 25)

### S002 · Optimization under uncertainty: state-of-the-art and opportunities (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2004, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2003.09.017)
**Read level**: full. All 36 manuscript pages: §§1-8 and the acknowledgments (pp. 1-22) read completely; references (pp. 23-36) skimmed for sources and self-citations.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: written at UIUC Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering (p. 1), with ExxonMobil Upstream Research and NSF DMI 01-15166 support (p. 22), after his students Liu and Ahmed had worked on planning under uncertainty (pp. 7, 14, 18, 22) and while global optimization was his main line (the 2002 book with Tawarmalani is cited, p. 19). It "grew out of" his FOCAPO 2003 paper (p. 2; reference on p. 32).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Uncertainty governs fuel prices, electricity availability and chemical demand; the difficulty named is an uncertainty space that is "huge" and leads to very large models, made worse by integer decisions in multi-period or multi-stage settings (abstract, p. 1). The aim is set low: "a short overview" with "pointers to the literature", a detailed coverage being "beyond the scope" of the paper (p. 2). The motivation is borrowed from Dantzig, who still counts planning under uncertainty among the most important open problems (p. 2). The entry is a contrast of modeling philosophies: expectation minimization, deviations from goals, min-max cost and soft constraints (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A taxonomy by modeling philosophy, each with one standard formulation: two-stage recourse models in linear, integer and nonlinear form, robust stochastic programming and chance constraints (pp. 3-8); flexible and possibilistic fuzzy programming (pp. 8-12); stochastic dynamic programming (pp. 12-13). Applications and the computational state follow (pp. 13-16), then contributions from process systems engineering (pp. 17-19). The distinctive part is §7: models under uncertainty are often multi-extremal "even when this is not directly apparent", shown by two small examples, which makes them global-optimization problems (pp. 19-21). The philosophical differences are stated in one line each: recourse allows infeasibility at a penalty, while chance constraints target reliability (p. 7); stochastic programming models uncertainty by probability functions, fuzzy programming by membership functions (p. 8).
  - Transferable: close a survey of a neighbouring field with the smallest computed examples showing why your own field's tools are needed. EX1 has two first-stage variables and four scenarios, and its plotted value function has several local minima and a unique global minimum at (0, 3) (pp. 19-20); EX2 is a two-variable chance-constrained LP whose feasible set is the nonconvex union of two polyhedra (pp. 20-21).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Two-stage stochastic LP with polyhedral X and Y and ω on a probability space (Ω, F, P) (p. 3). Discrete distributions give a deterministic-equivalent LP; continuous ones need sampling, so convergence proofs "have to be probabilistic" (pp. 4, 6). The recourse model assumes a risk-neutral decision maker; the Mulvey et al. robust objective adds a variability measure weighted by λ (p. 7). A chance constraint with a log-concave right-hand side gives a convex feasible set (Prékopa 1971), but in general the set may be nonconvex (p. 8). Fuzzy programming uses linear membership functions and the Bellman-Zadeh max-min decision, which reduces to an LP (Zimmermann) in the flexible case and to a generally nonconvex NLP in the possibilistic case (pp. 9-12). Stochastic dynamic programming assumes Markov dynamics and additive costs and suffers the curse of dimensionality (pp. 12-13).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a for original theorems (a review). The argument devices are:
  - Each exact algorithm judged by its guarantee and range: the integer L-shaped method is not applicable when first-stage variables are continuous; the Carøe-Tind master has nonlinear, possibly discontinuous cuts with "no practical method" to solve it; Carøe-Schultz terminates finitely only with purely discrete first stages or ε > 0; the Schultz et al. enumeration is "computationally prohibitive" (pp. 5-6).
  - Constructed small examples for nonconvexity: EX1 is taken from Schultz et al., with the plot from Ahmed 2002 (pp. 19-20); EX2 is given without a source, with Sen & Higle cited for a similar one (pp. 20-21).
  - Reformulations reported: Zimmermann's λ-variable LP for flexible programs (eq. (10), pp. 10-11) and the possibilistic program as the NLP (12) (p. 12).
  - Transferable: in a review, rate each algorithm by what it guarantees (finiteness, the variable types it admits) and whether an implementation exists, not by its authors' reported performance (pp. 5-6).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none of its own. The computational state is reported from the literature (p. 16): stochastic LPs with up to one million variables solved exactly on parallel computers (Birge 1997); sample-average approximation on a computational grid for problems with up to "1081" scenarios (as extracted; probably 10^81 with the superscript lost, not checked) (Linderoth, Shapiro & Wright 2002); only small stochastic integer programs solved exactly (Ahmed et al. 2000); a stochastic routing problem with 21694 scenarios solved within an estimated 1% (Verweij et al. 2002). There is no widely available general-purpose software for fuzzy programming or stochastic dynamic programming, and commercial stochastic-programming software is emerging (p. 16). Clay & Grossmann solved deterministic equivalents with millions of rows and columns (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Textbooks and the Stochastic Programming Community Home Page get pointers, not verdicts (p. 2). Criticism goes by guarantee and by implementation status (pp. 5-6): the Higle-Sen and Sherali-Fraticelli convexification approaches are "in their formative stages and no implementations have yet been reported" (p. 6), and the straightforward deterministic reformulation of robust models may underestimate recourse costs (p. 7). Process systems engineering is credited with flexibility analysis, aggregation-disaggregation and multiparametric programming, efforts that "yielded general-purpose algorithms" (pp. 17-18). His group's work runs through the paper: the finite branch-and-bound of Ahmed, Tawarmalani & Sahinidis (pp. 4, 6, 18), robust and fuzzy planning (pp. 7, 14-15), the bounded heuristics (pp. 18-19) and the global-optimization book (p. 19). Fuzzy programming gets equal space and a neutral reading, including Delgado et al.'s finding that linear membership functions are often as good as nonlinear ones (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing**: A pointer-style survey: each paradigm gets one standard formulation, a short critique and an applications list (pp. 3-16). Hedging is sparing and calibrated in both directions: for exact integer and nonlinear methods "It appears unlikely" that general-purpose algorithms will do (p. 22), while global optimization is "very likely to play a major role" (p. 19). His own stochastic-versus-fuzzy comparison is called "A small step" (p. 21). The acknowledgments credit his former students Liu and Ahmed for introducing him to "many of the topics" and a referee for widening the coverage (p. 22). Text check against the skill: the p. 22 sentence "It appears unlikely that general-purpose algorithms will solve such problems exactly." is the one the skill quotes from FOCAPO 2003 (Inner Tension 1). The next sentence here reads "Instead, we anticipate the development of problem-specific approximation schemes" and cites Ahmed & Sahinidis (2003), whereas 02-methodology quotes the FOCAPO version as "we expect to see" with Ahmed & Sahinidis (2002).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a review and taxonomy plus one argument: uncertainty models are often hidden global-optimization problems (§7, pp. 19-21). The limit is stated up front: no detailed coverage (p. 2). The opportunities are concrete (pp. 21-22): systematic comparisons between modeling philosophies; the multi-stage case, which with integers after the first stage is "a conceptual in addition to computational challenge" needing deeper structural understanding; integer and nonlinear cases, where problem-specific approximation schemes are expected rather than general-purpose exact algorithms; neuro-dynamic programming for stochastic dynamic programs; global optimization for two-stage and chance-constrained formulations. The abstract adds polynomial-time approximation schemes for multi-stage programs (p. 1).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 variant ⚠: the review calls for "systematic comparisons between the different modeling philosophies" and names only his group's 1996 stochastic-versus-fuzzy planning study as a first step (p. 21); it rates algorithms partly by whether implementations exist (pp. 6, 16). That is the comparison instinct of Method 4, but as a call and a review rather than a benchmark, and about modeling frameworks rather than solver implementations.
- Inner Tension 1 evidence ✅, own voice: the sentence the skill quotes from FOCAPO 2003 stands verbatim in the journal manuscript (p. 22); the follow-up wording differs slightly (see D7).
- Heuristic 1 evidence ✅: for multi-stage integer programs, "Deeper understanding of problem structure and properties is required in order to devise applicable algorithms" (p. 22); the group's finite branch-and-bound works "for a class" by exploiting structural properties (p. 6) and by restating the problem in tender variables so that the discontinuities become orthogonal to the axes (p. 18).
- Heuristic 10 evidence ✅: the engine travels to a new problem source. Branch-and-bound handles stochastic integer programs after a reformulation (p. 18), and global optimization is proposed for two-stage and chance-constrained models (pp. 19, 22).
- Taste marks 1 and 3 evidence ✅: existing implementations count as a criterion (pp. 6, 16), and heuristics are acceptable when an analytical quality bound comes with them (p. 18). Taste mark 4, weak evidence ✅: he endorses Delgado et al.'s case for linear membership functions as an adequate, efficient simplification (p. 11).
- Workflow F, own-voice evidence ✅ (rare for that workflow): his former students "introduced me to many of the topics discussed in this paper" (p. 22).
- New-pattern candidates: "complexity before algorithms, then bounded heuristics" (pp. 18-19; with S024); "recast a neighbouring field's models as global-optimization problems" (pp. 19-22); "students as scouts into new topics" (p. 22, one instance).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Rate each algorithm in a review by its termination guarantee, the variable types it admits and whether an implementation exists (pp. 5-6, 16).
- Expose hidden nonconvexity with the smallest computed examples: a two-variable, four-scenario stochastic integer program whose value function is plotted (pp. 19-20) and a two-variable chance-constrained LP whose feasible set is drawn as a union of two polyhedra (pp. 20-21).
- Pair a polynomial-time heuristic with an analytical quality bound, e.g. LP rounding with bundling whose optimality gap vanishes almost surely as the number of stages grows, and still report small-instance behaviour (pp. 18-19).
- End a survey with open problems stated as gaps, each with a named first step (p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "It appears unlikely that general-purpose algorithms will solve such problems exactly." (p. 22)
- "The distinguishing feature of this solution paradigm is the analytical derivation of bounds for the quality of the solutions obtained by heuristics that run in polynomial time." (p. 18)

### S006 · Global optimization of mixed-integer nonlinear programs: A theoretical and computational study (Mathematical Programming 2004, doi:10.1007/s10107-003-0467-6)
**Read level**: full. All 29 pages (printed pp. 563-591): §§1-8 and Tables 1-4 read completely; references (pp. 27-29) read. Fig. 2 (p. 10) is garbled in extraction, but the proof it illustrates is legible. One row of Table 2 (m, n, k = 100, 100, 500; p. 25) does not parse cleanly and is not used.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Mohit Tawarmalani (first author, then at Purdue's Krannert School of Management, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: received January 24, 2000, accepted July 17, 2003 (p. 1), the 3.5-year review that note 01 of the skill records without a reason; funded by ExxonMobil Upstream Research and four NSF awards (p. 1). It condenses Tawarmalani's 2001 PhD thesis [38], cited for proofs, details and computational experience on "over 500 problems" (pp. 7, 11, 12, 15, 20, 21, 23), and is the algorithm-and-software paper of BARON that precedes the 2005 polyhedral branch-and-cut paper.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: MINLP methods had appeared "rather sporadically", mostly for problems that are convex without integrality or quadratic, while a few recent works found deterministic branch-and-bound promising for general MINLPs (p. 2). The thesis: branch-and-bound is viable for continuous, integer and mixed-integer NLPs if it is supplemented with range reduction, relaxations and branching strategies (p. 2). Three remarks name the obstacles (p. 3): branch-and-bound is finite for 0-1 programs but "merely convergent" in continuous spaces (Remark 1); dropping integrality may not give a useful relaxation of an MINLP, and the resulting NLP may even be harder than the MINLP (Remark 2); LP software is mature and robust, NLP solvers "often fail even in solving convex problems", and the KKT conditions are not even necessary without constraint qualifications (Remark 3). Branch-and-bound is "more of a prototype" than an algorithm, so every component and the data structures must be designed (pp. 3-4). The class is factorable programs (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open an algorithm paper with numbered remarks that give the structural reasons the obvious approach fails, then design one component against each (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Every branch-and-bound component is designed for factorable MINLPs.
  - (i) Lower bounding. Algorithm Relax decomposes functions recursively into univariate, bilinear and fractional pieces, with one auxiliary variable per distinct subexpression (p. 4). Products use recursive bilinear envelopes instead of the exponential multilinear description (p. 5), and fractions use product disaggregation (pp. 5-6). The convex nonlinear pieces are then outer-approximated by tangents, so that the relaxation is entirely LP (§3.1, p. 7). A new sandwich rule, the projective error rule, converges quadratically (Theorem 3, pp. 9-11).
  - (ii) Domain reduction. A Lagrangian with a homogenizing multiplier y0 defines a range-reduction problem (5), solved by Algorithm SimpleReduce (pp. 12-13). Optimality-based reduction (Theorem 4), marginals-based reduction, probing, the Zamora-Grossmann contraction and feasibility-based reduction all follow from it (pp. 15-16). New are duality-based reduction from stored dual solutions (p. 17) and a learning reduction heuristic that carries a fathomed child's information to its sibling and enlarges the region proven inferior (pp. 17-19).
  - (iii) Branching by violation transfer, forward and backward through the factorable relations and weighted by marginals and priorities (pp. 19-21); branching points under which the estimator gap of a univariate convex function falls as O(1/n²) (p. 21).
  - (iv) Node selection by a composite rule (minimum violation or least lower bound, LIFO under memory pressure), with heaps and postponed partitioning (pp. 21-22).
  - (v) BARON as the implementation (pp. 22-23).
  - Kept from the classical scheme: rectangular partitioning and the branch-and-bound prototype (pp. 3, 19). Changed: relaxations come from the factorable structure rather than from dropping integrality, and reduction is a node operation, re-run whenever bounds or the incumbent change (pp. 3, 17).
  - Transferable: build a relaxation by recursive decomposition with one auxiliary variable per distinct subexpression, then linearize each convex univariate piece by tangents, so that every node solves an LP (pp. 4-7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Factorable f and g (p. 2). Finite bounds are implicit in every envelope: the bilinear envelopes use y^l and y^u (p. 6), and the outer approximation is built over [x_j^l, x_j^u] (p. 7). Univariate functions are limited to monomial, power and logarithmic terms and their recursive sums and products, and functional properties are not detected (p. 6). Theorem 3 needs θ < π, "not a restrictive assumption" because the curve can be split (p. 11). The comparison of Theorem 4 with the earlier cut assumes constraint qualifications for strong duality of the convex relaxation (p. 15). The learning heuristic assumes that functions are represented exactly when a variable is at its bound, and that the two children's relaxations coincide at x_j = x_j^B with that bound active (p. 18). Convergence needs consistent bounding and bound-improving selection (p. 3). The computations use an absolute tolerance of 10^-6, and a relative tolerance of 0.1 for the large SCQP comparison (p. 24).
- **D4 Proof devices**:
  - Theorem 2 (p. 5): McCormick's composition bound (Theorem 1, the mid-function rule, pp. 4-5) is implied by Algorithm Relax. The proof builds the sets M, F and H in (n+2)-space, gets convexity from intersection and projection, and minimizes the quasiconvex envelope e over [c(x), C(x)].
  - Theorem 3 (pp. 9-11): at most N(k) supporting lines are needed for projective tolerance ε_p, with k = Lθ/ε_p; N = 0 for k ≤ 4 and √k − 2 for k > 4 (as extracted; any rounding brackets are not visible in the file). The proof is an induction on W_RS θ_RS/ε_p: the base case uses Lemma 1 (ac = bd implies ac + bd ≤ ad + bc, p. 9), the inductive step relaxes a max-program (p. 10) and closes with (√(L_RO θ_RA) + √(L_OS θ_AS))² ≤ (L_RO + L_OS)(θ_RA + θ_AS) (p. 11).
  - Homogenization: the extra multiplier y0 lets one procedure, SimpleReduce, cover the Lagrangian lower bound ((a0, a) = (1, 0)) and bounds on any constraint (unit vectors), and its cuts can hot-start reductions across nodes (pp. 12-13).
  - LP case: PD and PR arise from the same polyhedral cone with different normalizing planes, so any dual feasible solution of the node LP bounds the perturbation problem (pp. 14-15).
  - Theorem 4 (p. 15): for y_i ≠ 0, the cut g_i(x) ≥ (b0 − inf_x l(x, y))/y_i, proved as a corollary of the relaxed primal master. The Ryoo-Sahinidis 1996 cut g_i(x) ≥ (U − L)/y_i* is "either the same ... or weaker" (p. 15).
  - Feasibility-based tightening rederived as Fourier-Motzkin elimination on a one-constraint LP with explicit duals (p. 16).
  - Learning reduction: the sibling's dual is built from the fathomed child's dual by changing one multiplier; a bilinear-envelope example shows that this multiplier can increase, which does not happen when the children differ only in one bound, as in integer linear programming (pp. 18-19).
  - A cost argument for data structures: node selection is the one per-node step whose time is super-polynomial in the input size, so heaps are used, with O(log L) insertion and deletion and O(1) retrieval (p. 22).
  - Transferable: to unify a family of bound-tightening rules, write one range-reduction problem with a homogenized Lagrangian and show that each known rule is a choice of direction (a0, a) and of dual solution (pp. 12-16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: One machine (332 MHz RS/6000 43P, 128 MB, LINPACK 59.9); the measures are CPU seconds, total nodes and maximum nodes held in memory (pp. 23-24). Every reported problem converged within the tolerance (p. 24), and the tests are "three representative classes" from the thesis's 500-plus problems (p. 23).
  - (1) Thirteen small SCQPs ("the most difficult" of [35]) solved by the SCQP module with and without probing and by the general factorable NLP module without probing (Table 1, p. 24). The text reports that the general module needs about the same number of iterations as the specialized one; the table also shows it takes about 6 to 23 times the CPU time (e.g. FP7e 8.87 s vs 0.38 s), and that probing lowers node counts on all 13 problems but raises time on 8 of them.

- **D6 Positioning**: Earlier MINLP work, mostly convex or quadratic (McBride-Yormark, Lazimy, Gupta-Ravindran, Duran-Grossmann, Borchers-Mitchell, Bienstock), and recent global branch-and-bound work (Ryoo-Sahinidis 1995, Smith-Pantelides, Floudas, Sherali-Wang) set the stage (p. 2). The relaxation combines McCormick's factorable programming with the sandwich algorithm of Fruhwirth-Burkard-Rote and Rote (pp. 2, 7-8). On range reduction the move is restatement, not criticism: the schemes of nine cited works (Thakur, Hansen et al., Hamed-McCormick, Lamar, Savelsbergh, Ryoo-Sahinidis twice, Shectman-Sahinidis, Zamora-Grossmann) become special cases (pp. 2, 15-16), and the authors' own 1996 cut is shown to be the same or weaker (p. 15). Integer programming is credited for marginals-based techniques and probing (Nemhauser-Wolsey, Savelsbergh, pp. 15-16). The computational comparisons are with GOP (Visweswaran-Floudas) and CPLEX (pp. 24-26).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: quadratic convergence of the projective-error sandwich rule and a duality framework for range reduction. Algorithms: duality-based and learning reductions, violation-transfer branching, composite node selection. Software: BARON's core and modules. Computation: three classes, a continuous one (separable concave QPs), a pure-integer one (0-1 hyperbolic programs) and mixed-integer models from MINLPLib (pp. 23-27). Limits appear inline rather than in the conclusions: the simple multilinear scheme is chosen "To keep the implementation simple" (p. 5), and the univariate library for the same reason (p. 6); the sandwich bounds can be weak for small n (p. 8); the Lagrangian subproblem "may itself be NP-hard", in which case a weaker relaxation is substituted (p. 13); the bilinear violation transfer and the branching-point program are left to [38] (pp. 20-21). The finiteness that the abstract promises for "certain classes" (p. 1) has no theorem in the body; only Remark 1 cites finite schemes [35, 3, 1] (p. 3). The conclusions state no future work (pp. 26-27).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Factorable relaxation recipe: recursive decomposition with one auxiliary variable per distinct subexpression; McCormick envelopes for bilinear terms; products of t factors by recursive bilinear relaxation; fractions by product disaggregation; tangents for convex univariate pieces, so that every node solves an LP (pp. 4-7).
- Place tangents by the projective error rule (the curve point closest to the intersection of the endpoint tangents); at most N(k) lines are needed for projective tolerance ε_p, with k = Lθ/ε_p (pp. 9-11).
- Range reduction from any dual vector: for each constraint with y_i ≠ 0, add g_i(x) ≥ (b0 − inf_x l(x, y))/y_i (Theorem 4, p. 15). Keep the dual solutions generated by dual simplex or Lagrangian iterations, re-evaluate the bounds on all variables whenever a bound or the incumbent changes, and apply feasibility-based tightening to surrogate constraints formed with dual multipliers (p. 17).
- Learning reduction: when a child is fathomed with its branching bound active, derive the sibling's dual by changing only that bound's multiplier (watching for a switch of the active bilinear envelope), and cut the sibling's region next to the branching point (pp. 17-19).
- Violation-transfer branching: push nonconvexity violations forward and backward through the factorable relations, and weight them by marginals and branching priorities (pp. 19-21).
- Node bookkeeping: heaps for the priority orders; store the branching decision and partition the node only when it is selected again (half the memory, and the relaxation can be solved partially in the meantime) (pp. 21-22).
