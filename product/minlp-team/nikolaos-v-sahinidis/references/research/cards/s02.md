### S042 · Analysis of bounds for multilinear functions (Journal of Global Optimization 2001, doi:10.1023/a:1011295715398)
**Read level**: full. All 23 file pages: abstract, §1-§4, Theorems 1-12 with the argument of each proof, Example 1 with Table I, Conjectures 1-3. Version: author preprint (20/12/1999), not the journal print.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Hong Seo Ryoo (Sahinidis is the corresponding author, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Illinois branch-and-reduce years; the BARON 5.0 manual cites it for multiplicative programs and for polyhedral outer-estimators of multilinear terms (S162 pp. 2, 23; ref. [15], p. 48).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Multilinear functions are building blocks of bilinear, quadratic and multiplicative programs and of RLT (p. 1). Known envelope results (McCormick, Al-Khayyal-Falk, Rikun, Sherali, Crama) mostly hold on the unit hypercube only and need an affine transformation, which becomes "somewhat problematic in the context of branching" (pp. 2-3). Entry: extend Hamed's three schemes for products of three variables (arithmetic intervals AI, logarithmic Loga, exponent Expo) to p variables, add a fourth, recursive arithmetic intervals (rAI), and compare all four analytically (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: rAI is factorable programming: replace each bilinear product recursively by a new variable, bound it with the two McCormick planes, and get the new variable's bounds by interval arithmetic (p. 7). AI instead multiplies bound factors directly and then bounds every cross-product term (pp. 4-5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: comparisons on boxes with zero lower bounds, scaled without loss of generality to [0,1]^p (§3, p. 10); Loga needs strictly positive variables (pp. 3, 8). Reader's note: Table I on [1,2]^3 has AI above rAI at (1.5, 1.5, 1.6) (2.8 vs 2.6, p. 21), so the "positive orthant" of the abstract and conclusions (pp. 1, 22) should be read as boxes anchored at 0.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: counting linear pieces (Thm 1, p. 5; Remark 1: AI for p = 4 is the maximum of 536,870,912 linear functions, p. 7; Thm 3: rAI has 2^(p-1) pieces, yet needs only p-1 new variables and 2(p-1) inequalities, Remark 2(ii), pp. 7-8); envelope proofs by writing points as convex combinations of extreme points of a partition of the cube (Thm 5, pp. 10-12; Thm 6, pp. 12-14); induction on p for rAI (Thms 7-8, pp. 14-16); multinomial expansion with pairwise cancellation for Expo (Thm 4, pp. 8-9); non-dominance proved by a four-point table of values (Thm 12, Table I, p. 21).
- **D5 Experiments**: none; Conjecture 2 "was computationally verified" for p = 3,...,9 on [0.00001, 1] (p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: on [0,1]^p, rAI gives the convex and the concave envelope and AI the concave one (Thms 7-9, pp. 14-16); for p >= 4 Theorem 10's formula for AI's underestimator adds nonpositive terms to the envelope expression, and Theorem 11 states rAI >= AI (pp. 16-17, 20), so the scheme with the doubly exponential description is the weaker one. Off zero-anchored boxes no scheme dominates on [1,2]^3 (Thm 12, p. 21). Three conjectures are left open (pp. 21-22); no test inside a solver.

**Method links**: Method 5 evidence ✅, in analytical form: the four schemes are priced by the number of linear pieces and lifted variables (pp. 5-8), AI is declared usable only through column/row generation (p. 7), and the compact recursive McCormick construction reaches the envelope (p. 14). Cost is representation size, not measured time. Method 1 evidence ✅ (weak, motivation only): bounds must hold on whatever box branching produces, not only on the unit cube (p. 3), which is the premise of rebuilding relaxations on reduced boxes (Method 1 step 3).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Before ranking relaxations by tightness, count each one's linear pieces and the auxiliary variables needed to write it compactly; a scheme with exponentially many pieces can still be polynomial with lifted variables (Remark 2(ii), p. 8).
- Prove non-dominance between two bounding schemes with a small table of values at a few points of one box (Table I, Thm 12, p. 21).
- When the comparisons need known envelope results, re-prove them with a short self-contained argument and say that you did (pp. 3, 10).
**Quotes**:
- "This implies that AI may be practically useful only in the context of a column/row generation scheme." (p. 7)
- "Consequently, the use of the above-mentioned bounding schemes becomes somewhat problematic in the context of branching in branch-and-bound algorithms." (p. 3)

### S101 · Product disaggregation in global optimization and relaxations of rational programs (Optimization and Engineering 2002, doi:10.1023/a:1021043227181)
**Read level**: full. All 23 file pages: abstract, §1-§6, Theorem 3.3, Proposition 4.1, Lemmas 4.2-4.6, Theorem 4.7 and Corollary 4.8 with proofs, the three computational examples. Published version.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Mohit Tawarmalani, Shabbir Ahmed (Sahinidis is the corresponding author, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: convexification years with Tawarmalani; the 5.0 manual lists it among BARON's lower-bounding schemes (S162 p. 2, ref. [23] p. 48), and S035 lists product disaggregation among the developments behind its 45-minute BARON run (S035 p. 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the integer-programming lesson that larger formulations can be tighter (variable and constraint disaggregation, pp. 1-2) is carried to the continuous product of a variable and a sum, f·(b0 + Σ bk yk) (eq. 1, p. 1). The application is rational functions of 0-1 variables, for which earlier MILP reformulations (Williams 1974; Li 1994; Wu 1997) cover special cases only (pp. 2-3).
- **D2 Core idea**: distribute the product over the sum, then apply McCormick to each of the n bilinear terms: more variables and constraints than one auxiliary variable for the sum, but the result is the convex and concave envelope over the box (p. 2; Prop. 4.1, p. 9). The standard substitution w = Σ bk yk (Quesada & Grossmann 1995) is not the envelope: for f(2y1 + 3y2) over [0,1]^3 at f = 0.5, y1 = 1, y2 = 0 the envelope gives 1 and the standard bound 0 (p. 10). For rational functions, recursive multilinear enclosers plus bilinear envelopes give an exact MILP reformulation (R) for binary x and a linear relaxation for x in [0,1]^n (pp. 8-9).
- **D3 Assumptions**: finite bounds on all variables; results derived for general hypercubes, not the unit cube (p. 9); (R) needs valid bounds on every intermediate fractional term (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: NP-hardness of finite bounds on 1/(b0 + Σ bj xj) over binaries, by reduction from subset sum (Thm 3.3, p. 6); the envelope as the convex hull of the two faces f = f^L and f = f^U (proof of Prop. 4.1, p. 10); convex-extension theory from the companion paper (Defs 2.3-2.6, Thms 2.7 and 2.9, pp. 4-5); exact characterization of where the envelope strictly improves when the bounds on f shrink (Lemmas 4.3-4.6, Thm 4.7, pp. 12-13), and, with both bounds improved, exactly where some y_k lies strictly inside its bounds (Cor. 4.8 and its proof, p. 14); dominance by special case: the big-M relaxation (RM) behind Williams 1974 is the bilinear envelope with f^L = 0 and f^U = M (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: three examples in BARON's NLP module (CPLEX 7.0, MINOS 5.5, absolute tolerance 10^-6, p. 17): Hock-Schittkowski 62, root gap reduced 43%, 1,220 to 667 nodes (p. 17); nuclear reactor reload pattern, 1,110 s to 649 s, the "first successful application of global optimization" to it (pp. 18-19); catalyst mixing, unsolved after 100 h and 13 million nodes, solved in 112 min after disaggregation, root gap reduced 68% (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: tighter LP relaxations by product disaggregation, the exact region of improvement under bound tightening, and MILP reformulations that dominate earlier ones (pp. 3, 16). Stated limits: bounding fractional terms is NP-hard, so reformulating at every node "may not be computationally tractable"; efficient bounding exists only in special cases (Charnes-Cooper LP, Megiddo) (pp. 14-15); (R) is not the convex hull, because the hull of fy - 1 = 0 is non-polyhedral (p. 11). No conclusions section.

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: Theorem 4.7 and Corollary 4.8 prove where tighter bounds on f tighten the relaxation, and the paper concludes that the reformulation must be rebuilt at every node with the tightest derivable bounds (p. 14), the theory under Method 1 step 3. Method 5 evidence ✅: the larger LP relaxation is accepted with its time effect shown, as root gap, nodes, nodes in memory and CPU time on one machine and tolerance (pp. 17-21). Method 2 evidence ✅ (weak): tested inside BARON (p. 17) and listed as a BARON lower-bounding scheme (S162 p. 2). Also the primary source for Workflow B2 step 3 ("product vs variable disaggregation"). New-pattern candidate: **Transfer integer-programming devices into global optimization** (variable disaggregation turned into product disaggregation, pp. 1-2, 10; an older big-M reformulation shown to be a special case of the envelopes, p. 16).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When a relaxation adds variables and constraints, prove the envelope property of the bigger one and give a three-variable numeric point where the compact one is strictly weaker (p. 10).
- Characterize the region where a relaxation strictly improves when one bound is tightened (Lemma 4.3 gives a triangle in the (y, f) plane, p. 12); it says where bound tightening pays.
- Show that an older reformulation is your construction with a weaker bound plugged in (big-M = envelope with f^L = 0, f^U = M, p. 16): dominance in one line.
- Report every reformulation experiment as root gap, nodes, maximum nodes in memory and CPU time on the same machine and tolerance (pp. 17-21).
**Quotes**:
- "The main contribution of this paper is to show that this increase in the size of the relaxation comes with an improved tightness as it provides the convex and concave envelopes of (1) over the hypercube." (p. 2)
- "Therefore, it is critical that the reformulation scheme of Section 3 be applied at every node of the branch and bound tree using the tightest derivable bounds on each fractional term." (p. 14)

### S162 · GAMS/BARON 5.0: Global optimization of mixed-integer nonlinear programs (GAMS Corporation 2002, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 49 file pages: abstract; §1-§9 (problem classes, requirements, algorithmic overview with optimality- and feasibility-based range reduction, branch-and-reduce, lower bounding and the sandwich algorithm, branching, K-best solutions, two worked examples, log, termination messages, options, comparison with other GAMS solvers); reference list. Document dated 7 August 2002.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Mohit Tawarmalani · **Place in trajectory**: the year Illinois licensed BARON to The Optimization Firm (February 2002, p. 2); the manual is both user guide and the method statement of the solver.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: nonconvex NLPs and MINLPs arise across applications; local NLP/MINLP algorithms converge only under convexity, whereas BARON gives global optima "under fairly general assumptions, most notably the availability of finite lower and upper bounds on the variables and their expressions" (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: branch-and-bound enhanced by constraint propagation and duality-based range reduction (p. 2), applied "to every subproblem of the search tree in pre- and post-processing steps" (pp. 18-19; Fig. 6, p. 20): marginals-based reduction x_j >= x_j^U - (U - L)/λ_j and probing (pp. 12-16, Tables 1-2); feasibility-based tightening as "poor man's linear programs" against 2n exact LPs (pp. 16-18); factorable relaxations exact at the variable bounds (p. 19); an entirely linear outer approximation built with the sandwich algorithm (pp. 22-24); branching with periodic bisection, branching at the incumbent and ω-branching (pp. 24-25); all or K best solutions by changing the fathoming step (pp. 25-26).
- **D3 Assumptions**: factorable functions (p. 3); finite bounds on all nonlinear variables and expressions, otherwise "global optimality of the solutions provided is not guaranteed" (p. 8); no trigonometric functions (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: range reduction derived from the supporting hyperplane of the convex perturbation function (pp. 12-14, Figs 3-4). Example 1 deliberately uses a weak separable relaxation because the tree is "more interesting": 5 iterations; with marginals-based reduction and probing, optimality without branching; branching at the incumbent, 3 nodes instead of 5 (pp. 26-33). Example 2 (concave QP): bisection branch-and-bound 17 iterations, branch-and-reduce 7 (pp. 33-36).
- **D5 Experiments**: no benchmark, only the two illustrative examples and a screen log (pp. 26-38); §9 finds it "difficult to predict whether GAMS/BARON or another solver may be faster" and claims two unique capabilities: lower and upper bounds with a global guarantee, and K best or all solutions in one call (p. 46).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: history: 1,800 lines of GAMS in 1991-93; 10,000 lines of FORTRAN 77 in 1994-95, to be user-configurable "to allow experimentation"; now about 32,000 lines of FORTRAN 90 and 21,000 lines of C; executable of v2.0 in March 1995, v4.0 online in October 1999; 163 researchers and users submitted over 6,100 problems to the web portal between October 1999 and July 2002 (p. 2). Stated limits: missing bounds end in "Search Terminated by BARON", and the user must fix the formulation "or be content with the solution provided, which may not be globally optimal"; numerical difficulties "should be reported to the developers" (p. 39); the sandwich algorithm's guarantees are asymptotic and say little when few underestimators are added, so they proved one for a single added cut (p. 24); K-best works in continuous spaces only for isolated solutions (p. 26). Defaults: linear relaxation (nlpdolin 1), no probing (pdo 0), root preprocessing LPs and SDPs on (prelpdo 1, presdpdo 1), 1200 s, absolute tolerance 10^-6 (pp. 40-45).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: reduction at every node in pre- and post-processing (pp. 18-19), ordered by cost: cheap "poor man's" LPs at nodes and the 2n exact LPs once at the root, since the pathological case "rarely occurs in the context of branch-and-bound if all of the 2n LPs are solved initially" (p. 18); probing needs extra relaxations (p. 14) and is off by default (p. 43); passes per node are capped (maxredpass, maxnodepass, pp. 43-44). Method 2 evidence ✅: the production code grew from a GAMS prototype into a configurable experimentation system, its algorithms are listed by the group's papers (p. 2), and a callable library for experiments remains (p. 3). Method 5 evidence ✅: the entirely linear relaxation is justified by NLP unreliability (p. 23) and is the default (p. 45). Fact check against Heuristic 3: this manual counts 163 researchers and users (p. 2); the skill cites 164 users from the 2002 book acknowledgments, for the same period.
**Transferable techniques**:
- In a manual or tutorial, run the worked example on a deliberately weak relaxation and switch on one device at a time (marginals, probing, branching at the incumbent), so each device's effect shows in node counts (pp. 28-33).
- Pair an expensive exact reduction (2n LPs) run once at the root with a cheap iterated approximation at every node, and draw the pathological case where the cheap one fails (pp. 16-18, Fig. 5).
- When a guarantee is only asymptotic, prove one for the regime the code actually runs in (a single added cut) (p. 24).
- Give every way a run can end its own termination message, saying what it means for globality (p. 39).
**Quotes**:
- "Declaration of an inferior solution as optimal by a convex programming solver is fatal in the application of branch-and-bound to global optimization problems." (p. 23)
- "It still serves as a system that facilitates experimentation with novel branch-and-bound algorithms for global optimization." (p. 2)

### S029 · An approximation scheme for stochastic integer programs arising in capacity expansion (Operations Research 2003, doi:10.1287/opre.51.3.461.14960)
**Read level**: full. Body read (file pp. 2-27: abstract, §1-§5 with the formulation, the three-phase heuristic, Lemmas 3.1-3.4, Theorem 3.5, the probability model, Lemmas 4.1-4.3, Propositions 4.4-4.5, Theorem 4.6, Corollary 4.9 and the computational section) and Table 1 (p. 33); the figure pages (pp. 34-40) are axis labels only. Version: preprint dated 11 January 2001, revised 26 November 2001 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Shabbir Ahmed · **Place in trajectory**: extends the Illinois line of LP-based heuristics proven asymptotically optimal for deterministic capacity expansion (Liu & Sahinidis 1997; Ahmed & Sahinidis 2000a, b) to the multi-stage stochastic case (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: capacity-expansion models had been restricted to linear costs and/or limited uncertainty for tractability (abstract, p. 2; p. 3). With fixed charges in later stages, nested Benders and progressive hedging do not apply (p. 4); the deterministic problem is already NP-hard, and "no practicable general-purpose solution methodology exists" for the exact solution of multi-stage stochastic integer programs (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea**: a fast LP-based three-phase heuristic (pp. 9-16): (I) solve the LP relaxation, which respects non-anticipativity; (II) drop non-anticipativity and, scenario by scenario, shift capacity additions to earlier periods up to the bounds (temporal capacity shifting); (III) restore non-anticipativity by "capacity bundling" (the maximum over each scenario bundle) and round the binaries. Running time O(T_LP + mn^2 + Sn) (p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions**: fixed-charge costs; scenario tree fitted from sampled paths (p. 18); no inventory, no disposal (p. 8). For the analysis: bounded expansion limits, and demands and costs i.i.d. with finite second moments or with bounded supports (cases D1-D3, pp. 16-18); feasibility through an expensive artificial facility (p. 18); for the relative error, unit production cost >= 1 and some demand >= 1 in every period (Assumptions 4.7-4.8, p. 23).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: one combinatorial lemma carries the proof: shifting adds at most one expansion per facility beyond the LP solution (Lemma 3.4, p. 14), with a family of instances where naive round-up needs n setups and shifting at most 2 (p. 15). Extreme-order-statistics limits (Lemmas 4.1-4.2, p. 19) then give (z_H - z_IP)/n -> 0 almost surely (Thm 4.6, p. 22), and the relative error -> 0 through a feasible LP dual that shows z_IP = Ω(n) (Cor. 4.9, pp. 23-24).
- **D5 Experiments**: 170 random instances on four process networks (one an industrial chain with 38 processes and 24 chemicals), binary scenario trees with 2^(n-1) scenarios, CPLEX 7.0 at defaults on the deterministic equivalent as the exact baseline (pp. 24-25): about 1% from optimal on the large instances of networks 1-3, exact solves up to 14 times slower; for network 4 with n = 8 the IP was unsolved after 12 h while the heuristic was within 10.7% of the LP bound in under 16 min; naive LP rounding errors were "several orders of magnitude higher" (pp. 26-27).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm plus a probabilistic guarantee for a multi-stage stochastic integer program (abstract, p. 2). Limits: the guarantee is asymptotic in the number of periods (p. 22); for the industrial network only the gap to the LP bound is known (p. 26); named improvements are left undone (cost-aware shifting, combination with other heuristics, p. 16). No conclusions section.

**Method links**: none of Methods 1-5 directly (a MILP heuristic, not global MINLP). It fits Research Taste mark 3, a heuristic paired with a bound (the LP bound on every run and a convergence theorem, pp. 22, 26), and Heuristic 7 (the 10.7% is labelled as a gap to the LP bound, p. 26). New-pattern candidate: **Heuristics with proven guarantees, paired with relaxation bounds** (the "science of heuristics"): the paper belongs to a line of LP-based heuristics proven asymptotically optimal (p. 5). The skill's Honest Boundary lists this program as claimed but unverified ("no paper after 2004"); this card and S119 are its 2003-04 papers.
**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a feasible solution by relaxing the two hard constraint families in turn (integrality, then non-anticipativity) and repairing each in a separate phase (pp. 9-16).
- Isolate the one structural lemma that bounds the heuristic's excess over the relaxation (at most one extra setup per facility, p. 14), then let probabilistic limits do the rest (pp. 19-23).
- Show with a parametric family of instances that the naive rounding baseline is unboundedly bad (n setups against at most 2, p. 15), and confirm it on the test set (p. 27).
- When the exact solver fails, report the gap to the LP bound and say that it is that gap (p. 26).
**Quotes**:
- "Through probabilistic analysis tools, we prove that the optimality gap of the heuristic solution almost surely vanishes asymptotically as the problem size increases." (p. 2)
- "It should be noted that the gap of 10.7% is with respect to the LP relaxation value." (p. 26)

### S035 · Design of alternative refrigerants via global optimization (AIChE Journal 2003, doi:10.1002/aic.690490714)
**Read level**: full. Body read (file pp. 1-13: introduction, state of the art, formulation, structural constraints, algorithm, computational results, conclusions); the notation list and references (pp. 13-15) were not read. Equations are partly scrambled in the extraction; the prose is readable. Published version.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Mohit Tawarmalani, Minrui Yu (Sahinidis is the corresponding author, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: molecular-design application of the Illinois years (SKILL.md Research Trajectory, 1997-2007); an application that put a new capability into BARON (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: computer-aided molecular design meets a combinatorial explosion (with K = 40 groups, 12,300 candidate molecules for N = 3 and 2,054,455,593 for N = 9) and nonlinear property models with local optima (p. 2). Earlier MINLP approaches could at best guarantee local optima, and the Joback-Stephanopoulos interval bounds are weaker and slow (p. 3). Two questions are posed: how to search globally without explicit enumeration, and how to handle local-optima traps (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: two contributions (p. 2). An MINLP that keeps the Joback-Stephanopoulos property model but writes their structural constraints with integer and 0-1 variables "so that mathematical programming bounds can be developed" (p. 3), plus new constraints (one-bonded vs higher-bonded groups, clique constraints for rings), each introduced by a group combination that passes the older constraint sets but is chemically infeasible (pp. 7-9). A branch-and-reduce algorithm that finds all feasible solutions in one tree by changing the fathoming step (pp. 10-11), with a problem-specific bounding step: round the relaxation solution and evaluate all properties in one forward pass through the ordered constraints (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Joback-Reid group contributions and related correlations (pp. 4-5); property targets from R12 in an automotive cycle (p. 9); "fairly generous bounds" (up to 15 of each of 44 groups, p. 9; Table 3, p. 10); the objective ΔH/Cp was chosen "for consistency with the earlier articles" (p. 10); 52 integer, 14 binary and 21 continuous variables with 92 constraints, 236 after constraint disaggregation (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: counterexample group sets show each older set of necessary structural constraints to be insufficient, and each new constraint is shown to eliminate one of them (pp. 7-9); constraints are disaggregated because that "usually leads to tighter relaxations" (p. 10); in the all-solutions search, optimality-based reduction uses a bound from a linear relaxation instead of incumbents (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: BARON with two problem-specific modifications, CPLEX 6.0, IBM RS/6000 43P (p. 11): in the mid-1990s over 150 h gave a few feasible solutions and no proof (p. 11); now 45 min gives all feasible solutions, credited to seven developments (a presolver that cuts group bounds from 15 to 5 at the root, probing on five variables at every node, the LP-only relaxation, branching on expressions, product disaggregation, the specialized bounding step, single-tree enumeration) (p. 12); 44 solutions, 29 admissible, nine not found in earlier work (pp. 12-13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: global MINLP plus a suitable formulation has "reached the level of maturity required to solve realistic models for molecular design" (p. 13). Stated limits: the nine new compounds "seem to be unstable, toxic, or otherwise not fit" as refrigerants (p. 13); the model selects groups, not their bonding (p. 10); group contributions are inaccurate for some combinations (pp. 3, 12-13). Future: classes where the constraints suffice; solvents, polymers, pharmaceuticals; richer group-contribution models; larger search spaces; relaxed property targets (p. 13).

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅: the all/K-best capability was added to general-purpose BARON after it worked on this application (p. 11), and progress is told across solver generations (150 h to 45 min, pp. 11-12); variant ⚠ on step 6: the paper does not say whether the mid-1990s runs used the same machine. Method 1 evidence ✅: the root presolve cuts group bounds from 15 to 5, probing runs at every node, and interval propagation in constraint order collapses continuous bounds once integer ranges shrink (pp. 11-12). Method 5 evidence ✅: the LP-only relaxation "eliminated the need for the solution of convex nonlinear programming relaxations" (p. 12). Also Heuristic 1 (an application class first, then a general capability, p. 11) and Heuristic 2 (chemical knowledge as constraints: Nanda's constraints cut the time to about 30 min, p. 13). New-pattern candidate: **Transfer integer-programming devices into global optimization** (logical structural conditions written with integer and 0-1 variables so that mathematical-programming bounds exist, p. 3; constraint disaggregation, p. 10).
**Transferable techniques**:
- For each set of necessary-condition constraints, exhibit one infeasible object that passes it, then add the constraint that removes exactly that object (pp. 7-9).
- If fixing the integers makes the continuous variables computable in one forward pass, use rounding plus that pass as the node's incumbent search instead of a local NLP solve (p. 11).
- Enumerate all feasible (or the K best) solutions in one branch-and-reduce tree by fathoming only infeasible or point nodes, and switch optimality-based reduction to a relaxation bound (p. 11).
- Credit a speed-up to the list of developments that produced it (p. 12), not to the algorithm as a whole.
**Quotes**:
- "The capability to identify all, or the K best, solutions of an integer program was added to the general purpose BARON solver after successful implementation in the context of this molecular design problem." (p. 11)
- "Interval relaxations are weaker than mathematical programming bounds and, in general, require significant computational effort." (p. 3)

### S168 · An integer programming approach to the phase problem for centrosymmetric structures (Foundations of Crystallography 2003, doi:10.1107/s0108767303012972)
**Read level**: full. All 8 file pages (IUCr cover; printed pp. 452-458: abstract, §1-§5, Tables 1-5). Published version (author electronic reprint).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Anastasia Vaia (Sahinidis is the corresponding author, p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: chemistry informatics of the Illinois years (SKILL.md Research Trajectory, 1997-2007), with ExxonMobil and NSF support (p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the crystallographic phase problem through the minimal principle is a nonconvex trigonometric NLP, attacked with local and stochastic search such as Shake-and-Bake (p. 2). For centrosymmetric structures the phases can only be 0 or π, yet no method exploited this, although nearly 76% of the structures in the Cambridge Structural Database are centrosymmetric (p. 2). The trap is shown first: 1000 MINOS runs from random starts on a 12-atom structure never found the global solution (p. 4, Fig. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: replace each cosine of a triplet invariant by 1 - 2·(binary) and the phases by binary normalized phases, with one more binary family to fix the parity of each sum; the result is an equivalent linear integer program with 2T + M variables and T constraints (pp. 4, 6), solved by off-the-shelf LP-based branch-and-bound (CPLEX 7.0) with no starting point and a finite global guarantee (pp. 2-3, 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: equal atoms in Cochran's distribution (p. 3); centrosymmetry; atomic-resolution data; N:M:T = 1:10:100 (atoms, reflections, triplets) except where the data were insufficient (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: an exact equivalence reformulation; branching on the phases and one auxiliary family suffices, because the other family's positive objective coefficients make it integral at the LP optimum (p. 5; which family is which is lost with the Greek letters in the extraction); finiteness follows from the finite search space (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: a parametric study of the N:M and M:T ratios shows the time-quality trade-off (Table 1, pp. 4-5); 18 literature structures in five space groups, all correctly determined, about 2 min each on average (pp. 5-6, Table 3); against Shake-and-Bake at defaults with 1 to 1000 trials and an expected time to best (Te), and CRUNCH at defaults: the IP's average time is about 30% above SnB's expected time and 30% below SnB100, its objective on average 13 times smaller than SnB1000's, its crystallographic R 55% better than SnB1000 and 12% better than CRUNCH; SnB missed five structures and CRUNCH three (pp. 6-7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an exact, finite reformulation that resolves phasing for centrosymmetric structures entirely in reciprocal space (p. 7). Open: whether the global minimum of the minimal principle is the correct structure for larger and noncentrosymmetric cases; specialized combinatorial algorithms and the noncentrosymmetric extension are in progress (pp. 7-8).

**Method links**: Method 4 variant ⚠: the certified global optimum is the yardstick that shows the incumbent method returns local minima (p. 7), and the baselines run at defaults with a sensitivity check (10,000 trials; 1.5 times the invariants, p. 7), but here the authors' own method is the subject from the start, not a neutral benchmark that precedes it. Also Heuristic 1 (a special class first: centrosymmetric now, noncentrosymmetric next, p. 8), Heuristic 4 (baselines given more trials and invariants, p. 7) and Research Taste mark 3 (a finite global guarantee, p. 7). New-pattern candidate: **Transfer integer-programming devices into global optimization** (a discrete property of the application turns the nonconvex model into an exact ILP for a commercial MIP code, pp. 2, 4-5).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Before proposing a global method, show the local-optima trap: run many local searches from random starts and plot the distribution of their objective values (p. 4, Fig. 1).
- Look for a physical property that makes a continuous variable discrete (phases in {0, π}) and linearize exactly with binaries (p. 4).
- Fix the data-size ratios by a parametric study before the main runs, then use one ratio for all instances (pp. 5-6).
- For a stochastic baseline, report several trial counts and the expected time to its best solution, and test whether more trials or inputs would rescue it (p. 7).
**Quotes**:
- "While this, effectively, makes the phase problem a discrete optimization problem, no current solution strategy exploits the mathematical properties of the problem to effectively resolve the phase problem." (p. 2)
- "Obviously, SnB is reporting local minima of the minimal principle model for all of these 18 structures." (p. 7)

### S119 · Approximation algorithms for the minimum number of matches problem in heat exchanger network synthesis (Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research 2004, doi:10.1021/ie049978l)
**Read level**: full. Body read (file pp. 1-23: abstract, §1-§11 with all five algorithms, Theorems 1-3, computational results, conclusions); the reference list and Tables 1-5 (pp. 24-35) were not read cell by cell, and the numbers below come from the body text. Version: preprint dated 1 January 2004 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Kevin C. Furman (Sahinidis is the corresponding author, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: carries the group's line on "introducing rigor in the use and practice of heuristics" from process planning (Liu & Sahinidis 1997; Ahmed & Sahinidis 2000, 2003 = S029) into process synthesis (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: in process synthesis, heuristics are justified only empirically while optimization suffers combinatorial explosion (abstract, pp. 1-2). The minimum-number-of-matches subproblem of heat-exchanger network synthesis is strongly NP-hard (Furman & Sahinidis 2001) and well understood, so it is chosen to "illustrate" approximation algorithms, not to give a complete HENS method (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: five approximation algorithms on the Cerdá-Westerberg transportation formulation (pp. 6-16): LP rounding (binaries eliminated, objective Σ q/U_ij), dual LP rounding through complementary slackness, Lagrangean relaxation rounding with six multiplier rules, sequential and simultaneous primal-dual, and greedy.
- **D3 Assumptions**: sequential HENS with a fixed minimum approach temperature; feasibility is guaranteed by the preceding minimum-utility step; constant heat-capacity flowrates (p. 4); ΔTmin = 10 °C in the tests (p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 1 refutes the N - 1 targeting rule as an upper bound with a two-hot, two-cold counterexample without a pinch (p. 17); the count of nonzero flows in a basic transportation solution gives an upper bound and max{m, n} a lower bound, hence a performance guarantee PG for any heuristic, computable from the input (Thm 2, p. 18); an a posteriori ratio R_APX uses the best relaxation bound found (p. 18); with one stream per interval, PG < 2 and PG -> 1 (Thm 3, pp. 18-19).
- **D5 Experiments**: 29 literature problems (6 to 40 streams), Dell 1.7 GHz Xeon (p. 19): PG from 1.67 to 15 (mean 6.45) against R_APX from 1 to 2.93 (mean 1.79); R_APX = 1 certifies two problems without any IP (p. 20); CPLEX 7.0 with its default search stalls on five problems after 7 to 41 CPU hours (pp. 20-21); no algorithm dominates, the best of the suite averages within 25% of optimal, and all but the primal-dual algorithms take under 1 s (pp. 21-22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: "the first approximation algorithms for process synthesis", with a guarantee valid for any heuristic (pp. 2-3, 22). Open questions: best possible guarantees, approximation schemes independent of problem size, other HENS and synthesis problems, and special-purpose exact algorithms for the hard matches instances (pp. 22-23).

**Method links**: Method 4 variant ⚠: the verdict is a portfolio ("no single approximation algorithm dominates"; run the whole suite, pp. 21-22) and exact optima from CPLEX serve as the yardstick, with unproven cases marked (pp. 20-21), but the portfolio consists of the authors' own algorithms, not a benchmark of existing software. Also Research Taste mark 3 and Heuristic 7: every heuristic run carries a computed bound (R_APX, p. 18), and optimality is claimed only where R_APX = 1 (p. 20). New-pattern candidate: **Heuristics with proven guarantees, paired with relaxation bounds** (empirical, probabilistic and worst-case analysis presented as complementary, pp. 7-8; S029's line carried into synthesis, p. 2).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive a performance guarantee valid for every feasible solution from two bounds computable from the input (basic-solution count above, max{m, n} below), so that it can decide before solving whether a heuristic is good enough (p. 18).
- Report a priori and a posteriori ratios side by side; where the a posteriori ratio is 1, claim optimality without an exact solve (pp. 18, 20).
- Test a folklore target (the N - 1 rule) with the smallest counterexample before using it as a bound (p. 17).
- Introduce a new analysis paradigm to a field on its best-understood hard subproblem (p. 3).
**Quotes**:
- "Although the use of heuristics has been prevalent in the process synthesis literature, their justification has been exclusively based on empirical evidence obtained through computational testing." (p. 1)
- "As seen in Tables 3 and 4, no single approximation algorithm dominates all others for the entire data set." (p. 21)
