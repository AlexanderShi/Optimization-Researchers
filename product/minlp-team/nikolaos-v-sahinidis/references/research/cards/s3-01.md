### S032 · Semidefinite relaxations of fractional programs via novel convexification techniques (Journal of Global Optimization 2001, doi:10.1023/a:1011233805045)

**Read level**: full: all 22 pages (abstract, §§1-5, reference list). Because the displayed equations are partly garbled in the extraction, I followed the formulas through the prose and the worked examples (pp. 7-11).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Mohit Tawarmalani (UIUC Mechanical & Industrial Engineering; Sahinidis was in Chemical Engineering and is the corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received 13 June 2000, accepted in revised form 12 February 2001. Funders: NSF CAREER award DMI 95-02722, Mitsubishi Chemical Corporation and the UIUC Computational Science and Engineering Program (p. 1). The paper applies the convex-extensions framework of S028, which it cites as a 2000 Mathematical Programming manuscript (ref. 31, p. 22). It lists S053 as a 1999 JOGO manuscript (ref. 29, p. 22).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: fractional programs are NP-hard in general. They arise in location, economics, information theory, the chemical process industry and other areas (p. 1). Global methods replaced z = x/y by the bilinear equality zy = x and linearized it. Quesada-Grossmann and Zamora-Grossmann added nonlinear inequalities and a concave envelope for nonnegative x and positive y. Still, the envelopes of x/y over an arbitrary rectangle were "still open" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: locate the generating set, then convexify over it.
  - Concave envelope: the generating set is the four corners, because x/y is linear in x and convex in y. So the bilinear interpolant of the corner values has the same concave envelope, and McCormick gives that envelope in closed form, eq. (1) (pp. 4-5).
  - Convex envelope: the generating set is the two faces x = xL and x = xU. Their epigraphs are convexified by convex disjunctive programming, eq. (2). With yp = ya(1 − λ) and an auxiliary variable zp this becomes a system (R) that stays valid on the faces (pp. 6-7). Schur complements turn (R) into an SDP, eqs. (20)-(24), which can also be written with second-order cones, eqs. (25)-(26) (pp. 13-14).
  - The same recipe then covers the case 0 ∈ [xL, xU] (§3.4), (ax + by)/(cx + dy) (§3.6), functions f concave in x and convex in y through the perspective of f(·, y) (§4), and f(x)y², f(x)/y and sums (§§4.1-4.3) (pp. 11-20).
- **D3 Assumptions**: §§3.1-3.3 work on a rectangle in the positive orthant. §3.4 needs only 0 ∉ [yL, yU], since the envelopes become unbounded as y → 0 (pp. 11-13). §4 needs f lower semi-continuous, concave in x and convex in y, and bounded so that its perspective is closed (pp. 15-16). §4.1 assumes f concave and f ≥ 0; the sign condition is relaxed on p. 18. §3.6 assumes nonnegative coefficients and, without loss of generality, ad > bc (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis devices**:
  - S028's theorems, used without re-proof (Theorems 2.2-2.4, p. 3).
  - A disjunctive hull of the two faces with the perspective substitution (pp. 6-7, 16), and Fourier-Motzkin elimination of yp (p. 7).
  - A KKT analysis of the one-dimensional problem (C) in yp. It gives the closed forms (13)-(14), shows that (11) is never feasible and proves Theorem 3.4 (pp. 9-10).
  - Schur complements and the rotated-cone identity (25) (pp. 13-14), and Rikun's theorem for sums (Theorem 4.1, pp. 19-20).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The paper has worked numerical examples only (Examples 3.1-3.3 and 3.5, pp. 7-8, 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: written against the Grossmann group's relaxations of x/y; Grossmann was Sahinidis's PhD advisor (SKILL.md, Academic Lineage). Quesada and Grossmann's work "revealed that there is a considerable scope for improvement" (p. 2). Zamora and Grossmann's concave envelope is re-proved "using a completely different proof" (p. 2). Their nonlinear underestimator turns out to be one piece of the new convex envelope, and the whole envelope when y has no upper bound, which the earlier papers had not seen (Theorem 3.4, p. 10).
- **D7 Writing**: strictness is shown with example points at which each earlier inequality lies strictly below the envelope (Example 3.3, p. 8). Coincidences are flagged before they are explained ("There is something interesting that happens with the above inequalities", p. 8; explained on p. 10). Some claims go further than the paper shows. The result "provides a systematic means for constructing semidefinite relaxations for general factorable programs" (p. 14). The vector-x generalization is called "not only unnecessary but restrictive", with no proof (p. 16).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: the contribution is theory: closed-form and conic envelopes of x/y and of a few function classes, and an SDP relaxation for fractional programs (p. 20). There is no algorithm, implementation or computation. A global algorithm "may then be easily constructed" but "shall not be detailed" (p. 2), and SDP relaxations inside branch and bound are left as "an ongoing research area" (p. 20). Future directions: algorithms for factorable programs that solve SDPs instead of LP relaxations, and approximation algorithms for fractional programs (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (dated, 2001): the paper proposes that algorithms solve semidefinite programs "instead of the traditional linear programming relaxations" (p. 20). Its case rests on strength (strictly tighter envelopes, pp. 7-8) and on the complexity class: SDPs "are polynomially solvable using interior point techniques" (p. 2). It gives no evidence on time, failures or the search tree (p. 20). The 2005 paper reversed the stance on the grounds of speed and robustness (SKILL.md Method 5). The two stances also coexisted in time. S053, listed here as a 1999 manuscript (ref. 29, p. 22), chose an LP relaxation by gap and CPU time in its published version (see its card). In this period, then, the strength-only argument belongs to the theory papers. This updates the a2-02 abstract-level reading, which was "variant".
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: theory with no code. The paper names the algorithms the scheme would plug into ([30, 35]; [30] is the authors' own 1999 MINLP study, ref. 30, p. 22) but builds none (p. 2). Nothing in the text shows that the relaxation entered BARON.
- new-pattern candidate: *one framework re-derives the zoo, then extends it* (existing candidate, S006).
  - The Zamora-Grossmann inequality is recovered as one piece of the envelope, and as the whole envelope over [xL, xU] × (0, ∞) (Theorem 3.4, p. 10).
  - Their concave envelope is re-proved (pp. 2, 5).
  - Each earlier underestimator is shown to be strictly weaker at some point (pp. 7-8).
  - The framework is then extended to four more function classes (pp. 14-20).
- new-pattern candidate: *generating-set recipe for envelopes* (new). Find the generating set (Theorem 2.3) and view the envelope as the tightest convex extension over it (Theorem 2.2). Convexify it by disjunctive programming whenever it is a finite union of convex sets, then rewrite the result in a tractable cone (p. 3; pp. 6-7, 13-14). The theory is in S028 (Theorems 5-7, pp. 7-8), and S053 uses its vertex case (S053 pp. 3-7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Take a function over a box that is linear or concave in x and convex in the other variables. Its convex envelope is the disjunctive hull of the two faces x = xL and x = xU. Write the hull with the perspective substitution yp = ya(1 − λ), then solve or eliminate (pp. 5-7, 15-16).
- If a perspective form divides by zero on a face, add an auxiliary variable (zp here) so the system stays valid there (p. 7). Then write each constraint of the form zy ≥ x² as a 2×2 LMI or as the rotated cone ‖(2x, y − z)‖ ≤ y + z (pp. 13-14).
- To get an envelope in closed form, minimize over the single disaggregation variable by KKT. Interior stationary points give one family of pieces and the variable's bounds give the rest. Drop the stationary branches that are never feasible (pp. 9-11).
- Replace a hard function by a simpler one that agrees with it on the generating set: both have the same envelope. Examples are the bilinear interpolant of the corner values of x/y (p. 5) and, for f(x)/y, the linear interpolant of f between xL and xU (pp. 18-19).
- To claim strict dominance over earlier inequalities, give small numerical points at which each of them lies strictly below the new envelope. Then explain the cases where they coincide (Examples 3.1-3.3, pp. 7-8; Theorem 3.4, p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "As a result, new algorithms may be developed for factorable nonlinear programming problems incorporating these relaxations and solving semidefinite programs instead of the traditional linear programming relaxations." (p. 20)
- "A global optimization algorithm using this relaxation scheme may then be easily constructed along the lines of [30, 35] and shall not be detailed in this work." (p. 2)

### S028 · Convex extensions and envelopes of lower semi-continuous functions (Mathematical Programming 2002, doi:10.1007/s10107-002-0308-z)

**Read level**: full: all 17 pages (abstract, §§1-4 with all proofs, reference list).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Mohit Tawarmalani (then at Krannert School of Management, Purdue; Sahinidis at UIUC Chemical Engineering) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received December 2000, accepted May 2002, published online 5 September 2002. Funders: a Computational Science and Engineering Fellowship to Tawarmalani, and NSF CAREER award DMI 95-02722 and an NSF/Lucent Technologies Industrial Ecology Fellowship to Sahinidis (p. 1). It is the theory paper behind "companion works" on hyperbolic programs (S053, ref. 13, listed as accepted 2002), on pooling, and on envelopes of many function classes (S032) (pp. 2, 16-17). According to the metadata in the a2-02 card of S233, it was split off from a 1999-2000 manuscript that also covered rational functions of 0-1 variables.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the opening sentence makes tight convex relaxations central to the efficiency of global methods (p. 1). Crama had defined concave extensions of functions of 0-1 variables over the unit hypercube (p. 1). The paper generalizes the idea to lower semi-continuous functions that a convex function must match on an arbitrary subset (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Definition: a convex extension of φ restricted to X ⊆ C is a convex function on C that equals φ on X (Definition 1, p. 3).
  - Existence: an extension exists iff, at each point of X, φ is no larger than any convex combination of its values at points of X that average to that point (Theorem 2, pp. 3-4). Over a family of faces, it exists iff φ is convex on each face (Corollary 4, p. 4).
  - Tightest extension: it is the envelope of φ set to +∞ off X (Theorem 5, p. 7). It equals the envelope of φ over C iff the generating set (the projected extreme points of the envelope's epigraph) lies in X (Theorem 6, pp. 7-8). A point can be excluded from the generating set by a local argument, on any convex subset that contains it (Theorem 7, Corollary 5, p. 8).
  - Multilinear functions: the generating set is the vertex set (Theorem 8, pp. 9-10). So for any function given on the vertices of a hypercube, the tightest extension is the polyhedral envelope of its unique multilinear interpolant (Theorem 10, Corollary 6, p. 11).
  - §4 applies all this to x/y (pp. 11-16).
- **D3 Assumptions**: φ is lower semi-continuous, so its epigraph is closed (p. 2). The generating-set results need a compact convex C (pp. 7-8). Theorem 4 assumes no infinite two-sided directional derivative (p. 5). The second-order argument needs twice differentiability and existing multipliers (pp. 5-6). §4 works on a rectangle in the positive orthant (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis devices**:
  - Rockafellar's face theorem (Theorem 18.3) and his representation theorem for closed convex sets (pp. 2, 7); dimension counting by contradiction (Proposition 1, pp. 2-3).
  - A supporting-hyperplane test for where the envelope touches φ, with a second-order corollary (Theorem 4, Remark 1, pp. 5-6).
  - A one-line counterexample separating the envelope from the tightest extension: x² on [0, 1] with X = {0, 1} (p. 5).
  - Reduction of a multilinear form to a bilinear form with a matrix that is not positive semidefinite (Theorem 9, p. 10).
  - Closed-form maximum gaps, found by monotonicity in one variable (Theorems 11, 13, pp. 13-15) and by convexity of the gap on two quadrilaterals (Theorem 12, p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: one box, [0.1, 4] × [0.1, 0.5]. Maximum gaps: 3.38 for the envelope zc, 7.09 for the Zamora-Grossmann zu, 14.13 for the McCormick-based zf, and 5.72 for zg = max{zf, zu} (Table 1, p. 16). Gaps with no closed form come from three nonconvex max-gap models; in (CG) the inner envelope evaluation is replaced by its KKT conditions. All three models were solved to global optimality with the group's solver (refs. [15] and [4], BARON; pp. 15-17). Nothing is timed.
- **D6 Positioning**: the paper generalizes Crama's definition ("a generalized version of those used in [7]", p. 3). It recasts Rikun's polyhedrality result for multilinear envelopes as a statement about generating sets (p. 9). It measures itself against the classical outer-linearization of bilinear terms (McCormick, Al-Khayyal and Falk) and against Zamora-Grossmann (pp. 2, 12).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Theorem-proof throughout. x/y is the only worked application, and there is no conclusion section: the paper ends with Table 1 (p. 16).
  - The abstract's "much tighter" (p. 1) and the body's "significantly tighter" (pp. 2, 16) rest on this one box.
  - Table 1's maximum-gap column matches the closed forms (11) and (10) (7.0877 and 14.1337; my arithmetic).
  - Two entries of its x*/y* column do not match their points: 10.2077 at (2.05, 0.1), where x/y = 20.5, and 22.6014 at (4, 0.17968), where x/y ≈ 22.26 (p. 16).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: the contribution is theory: when convex extensions exist, which one is tightest, when it is the envelope, local tests for the generating set, and exact gap formulas for x/y. The general condition "is not easy to verify in general" (p. 8), hence the local tests. Applications are left to the companion papers (p. 2), and the paper makes no algorithmic claim.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: relaxations are ranked by maximum gap alone, in closed form (Theorems 11-13, pp. 13-15) and on one numerical box (Table 1, p. 16). Tightness is called central to efficiency (p. 1); time and failures are not considered. The winner, zc, is nonlinear, while the baseline zf is the LP outer-linearization (pp. 12-13). Unlike S032, the paper does not propose replacing LP relaxations.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (weak): in this theory paper the group's production solver is the measuring instrument. The max-gap models, one of them with an embedded KKT system, were solved to global optimality with it (p. 16).
- new-pattern candidate: *one framework re-derives the zoo, then extends it* (existing candidate, S006). Crama's extensions become a special case (p. 3), and Rikun's multilinear result becomes a statement about generating sets (p. 9). The McCormick-based zf and the Zamora-Grossmann zu turn out to be convex extensions over different sets of faces, and zc is the tightest over all proper faces (Corollary 7, p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate: *generating-set recipe for envelopes* (new; see S032). This paper supplies the theory (Theorems 5-7, Corollary 5, pp. 7-8) and applies it to x/y as a disjunctive hull of two faces (p. 12).
- new-pattern candidate: *add, don't replace (intersect a new relaxation with the incumbent one when neither dominates)* (existing, S109); the fit is partial. Here two older underestimators, neither dominating the other, are combined. zg = max{zf, zu} is an extension over the union of the faces on which they are exact, and it cuts the maximum gap to 5.72 (Corollary 7, p. 14; Table 1, p. 16). The new envelope still beats both of them and their maximum.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before computing an envelope, find where it can touch the function. A point is outside the generating set if it lies in the relative interior of a segment in the domain along which the function is concave (Corollary 5, p. 8). An interior point is also outside it if the Hessian there is not positive semidefinite (p. 6). Often only a union of faces is left.
- Explain each underestimator by the set of faces on which it is exact. The tightest extension over the union of those faces dominates all of them, and the maximum of two extensions is an extension over the union of their face sets (Corollary 7, p. 14).
- For a function known only on the vertices of a box, such as a function of 0-1 variables, build its unique multilinear interpolant with product weights. The polyhedral envelope of the interpolant is the tightest convex extension (Theorem 10, Corollary 6, p. 11).
- Compute a relaxation's maximum gap in closed form where monotonicity or piecewise convexity allows it (Theorems 11-13, pp. 13-15). Otherwise, pose a max-gap problem, replace the inner envelope evaluation by its KKT conditions, and solve it with a global solver (pp. 15-16).
- Separate two notions with the smallest counterexample: x² on [0, 1] restricted to {0, 1} has the convex extension x but the envelope x² (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "Central to the efficiency of global optimization methods for nonconvex mathematical programs is the capability to construct tight convex relaxations." (p. 1)
- "Models (OC), (OG), and (CG) were solved to global optimality using our nonlinear programming solver [15], [4]." (p. 16)

### S053 · Global optimization of 0-1 hyperbolic programs (Journal of Global Optimization 2002, doi:10.1023/a:1021279918708)

**Read level**: full: all 32 pages (abstract, §§1-8, Tables 1-10, reference list). The counts over Tables 7-10 below are my own tally of the 104 rows listed. The paper has no conclusion section.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Mohit Tawarmalani (Krannert School of Management, Purdue) and Shabbir Ahmed (Georgia Tech ISyE); Sahinidis (UIUC Chemical Engineering) is the starred author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: supported by NSF awards DMII 95-02722 and BES 98-73586 (p. 1). The file shows no received date. S032 lists it as a 1999 manuscript (S032 ref. 29, p. 22), and S028 lists it as accepted in 2002 (S028 ref. 13, p. 17). It uses the convex-extensions theory of S028 (ref. 43, p. 32) and a bound-sensitivity theorem from the product-disaggregation paper S101 (ref. 42, p. 32; Theorem 5.1, pp. 13-14). The Edmonton data come from Haque and Ahmed, then in preparation (ref. 21, p. 31).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: problem (H) maximizes a sum of ratios of linear functions of binary variables under linear constraints, with positive denominators (p. 1). Single-ratio problems were well studied. The constrained multiple-ratio problem had "received relatively little attention": there was Saipe's branch and bound for a cardinality constraint, and Li's and Wu's MILP reformulations (p. 2). The authors find that a "blind-folded application of branch and bound exhibits poor convergence" on such reformulations (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Write each ratio as a bilinear identity in a new bounded variable: either fi = 1/denominator (form M1) or gi = the ratio itself (form M2).
  - Replace each product of a binary and a bounded variable by its McCormick envelopes. They are exact at binary points, so the result is an exact MILP with no new binaries (Theorems 2.4-2.5, Remark 2.7, pp. 4-7, 10).
  - Combining the two forms with convex nonlinear cuts and with products of the original constraints and the bound factors gives eight reformulations, ordered by a dominance graph (pp. 7-11; Table 1 and Figure 1 on p. 11).
  - The relaxation gap is "extremely sensitive" to the bounds on the ratios. So at each node the algorithm recomputes these bounds over the node's subset and rebuilds the reformulation there, a step the paper calls node tightening (pp. 14, 17-18).
- **D3 Assumptions**:
  - Denominators are positive for every feasible x (p. 1).
  - Valid bounds on each ratio are needed. The tightest come from single-ratio subproblems, which are strongly polynomial only for special structures (Megiddo; Saipe's O(np) algorithm under a cardinality constraint). For general constraint sets the bounds fall back on the Charnes-Cooper LP over the continuous relaxation (pp. 14-17).
  - The strict inequality in Theorem 4.1 needs binary extreme points (p. 12). Remarks 3.1-3.3 drop extensions under sign conditions (pp. 8-9).
  - The implementation and all the tests cover only the cardinality-constrained case, CCH (§§7-8, pp. 19-30).
- **D4 Analysis devices**:
  - The envelope of φ(x, f) splits into a sum of bilinear envelopes, shown by restricting f to its bounds (Theorem 2.4, p. 4).
  - The envelope of a bilinear form is exact iff each product has a factor at a bound. The proof uses a strictly concave direction xi ± yj (Theorem 2.5, pp. 5-6).
  - The dominance graph follows from the construction (Figure 1, p. 11). Theorem 4.1 uses the quasi-convexity of the ratios on faces (pp. 12-13). Proposition 4.2 embeds the Charnes-Cooper bound by adding inequalities (p. 13).
  - Megiddo's parametric scheme with median finding gives a Θ(n) single-ratio algorithm, which reproduces the algorithm of Hansen et al. (Proposition 5.4, pp. 14-16).
- **D5 Experiments**: three parts.
  - Bounds: random CCH instances, with coefficients uniform on [1, 1000] and five instances per size. Optima come from solving the (R8) MILP with OSL; the models were run in GAMS with OSL and MINOS on an IBM RS/6000 (p. 20). Average gaps are about 62-208% for Li's reformulation and 0.3-46% for the eight new ones. MINOS failed on some (LR2) instances, and (LR6) could not be solved on any instance of size 20-20-12 (Tables 3-6, pp. 21-25).
  - Algorithm: BARON with node tightening (the (R7) LP, solved by CPLEX) against CPLEX 6.0 on the root (R7) MIP. BARON needed fewer nodes on 94 of the 104 instances listed and tied at one node on the other 5. CPLEX failed within 1,000,000 iterations on five instances with n = 50. CPLEX took less CPU time on 47 of the 99 instances it solved, all of them with m ≤ 10 and n ≤ 20. BARON took less time on every instance with n = 50 or m = 20 (Tables 7-10, pp. 23, 26-29).
  - Application: Edmonton, with 100 ratios, 58 binaries and p = 10, gives an (R7) MIP with 5900 continuous variables and 23501 constraints. It was solved globally in 5.5 hours and 79 nodes, with 97% of the CPU time spent in CPLEX LPs (p. 30).
- **D6 Positioning**: Li's and Wu's reformulations are special cases of (R1) with zero lower bounds (p. 8). On Li's own example, (R5) and (R7) cut his relaxation gap by 76.9% and 62.4% (p. 12). Saipe's bound is dominated (Corollaries 7.1-7.2, p. 19). On Saipe's Example 3, the (R7) LP gives the optimum at the root, where Saipe needed seven nodes (pp. 19-20).
- **D7 Writing**: claims are worded in nodes, not time: "In all cases, the proposed algorithm required fewer nodes than CPLEX" (p. 23), although five rows tie at one node. The tables "clearly demonstrate the importance of node tightening" (p. 24). The text does not discuss the instances on which CPLEX is faster. Table 10 lists only instances 2-5 of size 20-20-8, without comment (p. 29). The paper ends on the application, with no conclusions (p. 30).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: the contributions are reformulation theory, exact bounding subroutines, a node-tightening branch and bound built as a BARON module, and an application. No limits are stated, since there is no conclusion or future-work section (p. 30). Limits visible in the text:
  - the "off" arm is a different solver (pp. 22-23);
  - the bound comparison leaves out the time spent deriving the ratio bounds (p. 20);
  - only the cardinality-constrained case was implemented (§§7-8).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (practice; published 2002, an earlier manuscript listed in 1999, contents of that version not seen). The eight relaxations are compared on gap, CPU time and solver failures (Tables 3-6, pp. 21-25). The two nonlinear ones were slower and failed numerically in MINOS (p. 21). The LP relaxation of (R7) was chosen by "the average gap and the solution times" and solved with CPLEX (p. 22). (R8) had a smaller average gap in all 24 size rows, but at 2.4-5 times the CPU time (Tables 3-6; my arithmetic). This is the 2005 stance in practice before it was stated, from the same period as S032's SDP proposal.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: node tightening is range reduction of the auxiliary ratio variables over each node's feasible subset. Each bound is an optimality-based bound, computed exactly and cheaply by a single-ratio subproblem, and the relaxation is then rebuilt (Method 1 steps 1 and 3; pp. 17-18, 21). Theorem 5.1 characterizes exactly where the envelope tightens when a bound is tightened (p. 14). This updates the a2-02 abstract-level reading, which was "variant".
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the method is a module built on BARON's user-supplied bounding routines: "It is the latter feature of this code we build upon here." (p. 21). Compare the user exits of the 2000 manual (s01 card, S077 pp. 55-61).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the ablation is not a switch inside one code. The "off" arm is CPLEX 6.0 on the root reformulation (pp. 22-23), so the effect of node tightening is mixed with the differences between two solvers. To its credit, the baseline gets the strongest root formulation, (R7) rather than Li's (pp. 23-24; Heuristic 4).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the paper enters a class with its own method, not with a benchmark. It uses random instances with optima certified by an exact MILP solve (p. 20), two literature examples (pp. 12, 19-20) and one application (p. 30). There is no public library. The per-instance tables do show the losses: CPLEX is faster in CPU on the small instances, and its failures are marked "—" (Tables 7-10, pp. 26-29). The text does not mention the CPU losses (pp. 23-24).
- new-pattern candidate: *Linearize through discreteness of the domain* (existing, S198, S361). McCormick envelopes are exact iff one factor of each product is at a bound (Theorem 2.5, Remark 2.7, pp. 5-6). So ratios of binaries become exact polyhedral systems with no new binaries (pp. 7, 10).
- new-pattern candidate: *reformulate at every node, not only at the root* (existing, from this work's a2-02 card). The full text now supplies the mechanism (pp. 17-18). It is close to Method 1 step 3 and to the S116 candidate *re-derive cheap relaxations per node rather than adopt a stronger static reformulation*, so the synthesis can probably fold it into Method 1.
- new-pattern candidate: *one framework re-derives the zoo, then extends it* (existing, S006). Four earlier results reappear as special cases or dominated bounds of the framework: Li's and Wu's reformulations (p. 8), the Charnes-Cooper bound (Proposition 4.2, p. 13), Saipe's bound (Theorem 4.1, Corollaries 7.1-7.2, pp. 12-13, 19) and Hansen et al.'s Θ(n) algorithm (p. 16).
- Heuristic 1 ✅ (not counted as a method link): this is a special-class module, built alongside BARON's "several ready to use modules for certain classes" (p. 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a binary program with ratio terms, introduce one bounded variable per ratio and write the ratio as a bilinear identity. Then replace each product of a binary and a bounded variable by its McCormick envelopes. These are exact at binary points, so the reformulation adds no binaries (pp. 4-10).
- Generate a family of reformulations by combining forms with optional valid inequalities, and draw their dominance graph (Figure 1, p. 11). Then measure each one's gap and relaxation CPU time on random instances, and choose by both, not by the dominance order (Tables 3-6, pp. 21-25; p. 22).
