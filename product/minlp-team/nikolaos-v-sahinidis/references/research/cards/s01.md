### H010 · Book review: C. A. Floudas and P. M. Pardalos (eds.), State of the Art in Global Optimization. Computational Methods and Applications (Optima 52 1996, no DOI)

**Read level**: full. The review (pp. 12-13, signed by Nikolaos Sahinidis on p. 13) was read completely. For context, the opening of Pintér's software survey in the same issue was read (pp. 1-4). The other reviews and items of the issue were not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole reviewer) · **Position in trajectory**: 1996. The volume collects the papers of the Princeton conference of 28-30 April 1995 (p. 12), among them his finiteness paper with Shectman (p. 12). In the same issue Pintér, not Sahinidis, describes BARON as interval analysis and duality combined with branch and bound, with a library of specialized modules, and says that for more general problems the user must supply the underestimation routines (p. 4). This matches the core-plus-user-exits design of the 2000 manual (S077 pp. 55-61).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: n/a as research. A descriptive notice of a volume of 36 invited and refereed papers, the seventh in Kluwer's Nonconvex Optimization and Its Applications series (p. 12).
- **D2 Core idea**: the reviewer's selection is the content. Of the 36 papers he describes six. Horst and van Thoai get finiteness for separable concave minimization over totally unimodular constraints by integral branching. Ramakrishnan, Resende and Pardalos solve all QAPLIB problems with n ≤ 15 by branch and bound. Kearfott reports interval branch and bound with equality constraints. Van Voorhis and Al-Khayyal use range reduction to accelerate QCQP branch and bound. Shectman and Sahinidis show that exhaustiveness plus branching on the incumbent gives finiteness for separable concave programs (all p. 12). Tuy writes location problems as low-dimensional d.c. programs (p. 13). Two of the six are finiteness results and one is a range-reduction result. The rest are listed by author names only (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions**: n/a.
- **D4 Analysis devices**: n/a (no proofs; the two finiteness results are each restated in one sentence, p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a. The only computational claim relayed is that all QAPLIB problems of dimension n ≤ 15 were solved (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: uncritical praise of the volume and of the series (pp. 12-13). One of the six highlighted contributions is his own (p. 12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: n/a. No limitations or open problems are named. The review ends by agreeing with the editors that the book will be a valuable source of information (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (weak): one of the six contributions he chose to describe is range reduction used to accelerate QCQP branch and bound (p. 12). This is a reviewer's selection, not a method statement.
- new-pattern candidate: *finite branching by exhaustiveness plus incumbent branching*. His own paper is summarized by this result (p. 12), and the same rule is restated in S058 (pp. 8-9) and S077 (p. 24). All three are restatements of one result (Shectman and Sahinidis), not independent evidence.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When summarizing prior results in a review or a related-work section, give each in one sentence that names the observation that makes it work. Example: the problems can be viewed as integer programs, so integral branching gives finiteness (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "The key observation is that the underlying problems can be viewed as integer programs. Finiteness can, therefore, be achieved by integral branching." (p. 12)
- "J.P. Shectman and N.V. Sahinidis prove that exhaustiveness and branching on the incumbent whenever possible in tandem ensure finiteness of rectangular-subdivision-based branch and bound algorithms for global minimization if separable concave programs." (p. 12; the word *if* for *of* is in the original)

### S079 · Globally optimal robust process control (Journal of Process Control 1999, doi:10.1016/s0959-1524(99)00012-8)

**Read level**: full (all 9 pages: abstract, §§1-8, acknowledgements, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jeremy G. VanAntwerp, Richard D. Braatz (corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: all three authors at the UIUC Department of Chemical Engineering (p. 1). The branch-and-bound engine is cited to Ryoo and Sahinidis 1995 and 1996 (refs. [12], [13], pp. 3-4, 9). The BARON 4.0 manual lists robust controller design among the applications of the 1994-95 code (S077 p. 7). There, ref. [22] is a VanAntwerp-Braatz-Sahinidis paper whose title is cut off in the file (S077 p. 105).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: DK-iteration, the most popular robust design method, alternates between the controller and the scaling on a nonconvex problem. It is not guaranteed to reach a global optimum and can give conservative designs even for small processes (p. 1). Conservatism is costly because the uncertainty description is expensive to obtain (p. 1). The earlier global methods either solve iterated nonconvex feasibility problems by branch and bound (Goh et al.) or scale badly with the number of uncertainties (Yamada et al.) (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: pose the design directly as an optimization with bilinear matrix inequality (BMI) constraints (eqs. 9-13, p. 3) and solve it by spatial branch and bound. The lower bound is an LMI in which each bilinear product l_i r_j is replaced by a variable with interval bounds (eqs. 15-17), plus McCormick's four inequalities (eq. 18, p. 4). The upper bound is an LMI with some variables fixed (eq. 19, p. 4). Variable bounds come from auxiliary LMIs (p. 4). What is not changed is the constant-scaling formulation of robust performance due to Shamma (p. 3); what is changed is the alternating search, replaced by a global search over the joint problem.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: norm-bounded, nonlinear, time-varying, block-diagonal perturbations with constant scaling matrices (pp. 2-3). Controller order is unrestricted (p. 7). Time delays use a third-order Padé approximation, whose error the uncertainty weights cover (p. 7). Convergence of branch and bound needs three things: a relaxation whose gap does not grow as the region shrinks, a globally solvable (convex) relaxation, and lowest-bound node selection (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: McCormick envelopes of the bilinear terms sit inside an LMI, so each node bound is a polynomial-time convex problem (p. 4). NP-hardness of BMI problems is called straightforward to prove but is not proved (p. 2). Convergence is cited to [13] (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: on the mass-spring benchmark the design is about 50% less conservative than DK- and LR-iteration, with an order-4 controller (p. 5). A cited adhesive-coater study found only about 10% (p. 6). On the reactive ion etcher (48 states) the best performance value is 0.9834 < 1 with an order-48 controller (p. 7), and the set-point response is more than twice as fast as a published LQ design (pp. 7-8). The paper reports no CPU times, node counts, tolerances or hardware.
- **D6 Positioning**: it claims tighter bounds and a more direct approach than the two earlier global algorithms (p. 1), but compares only with DK- and LR-iteration (pp. 4-6).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: an application of global optimization to robust control. The conclusion is that global optimality matters for nonconservative controllers and that processes of moderate dimension are within reach (p. 8). Reduced-order robust controllers, called an even harder nonconvex problem, are left aside (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: range reduction is stated as the key to tight bounding, and each variable's bounds come from an LMI (p. 4). The device is borrowed from Yamada et al. [15], not the duality-based test.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the node relaxation is an LMI (semidefinite) problem, chosen because it is polynomial-time computable (p. 4). No time or failure accounting is reported anywhere (pp. 1-9), so bound per second is not measured.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the certified global design is the yardstick for the field's standard heuristic, but on one benchmark plus one cited case, not a library. The case where the heuristic is close is reported too (pp. 5-6).
- new-pattern candidate: *price of local search on the literature's own instances*. The published mass-spring benchmark is re-solved to global optimality to measure how conservative the standard designs are (pp. 4-6). The same move appears in S058 (pp. 13-15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a field's standard method alternates between two convex subproblems, write the joint problem with its bilinear coupling and branch on it. Relax each product by McCormick inequalities inside the field's native convex class (here LMIs), so every node bound stays a polynomial-time convex problem (pp. 3-4).
- Report the case where the incumbent heuristic is close (about 10%) next to the one where it is far off (about 50%) (pp. 5-6).

**Quotes**:
- "Reducing the ranges of all problem variable as much as possible is frequently a key to tight objective function bounding." (p. 4)
- "We would like to point out that the iterative methods do not always perform so poorly." (p. 6)

### S058 · Applications of global optimization to process and molecular design (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2000, doi:10.1016/s0098-1354(00)00583-4)

**Read level**: full, preprint. The file is the author manuscript dated 22 February 2000 (p. 1). All 30 pages were read: abstract, §§1-5, tables, acknowledgements, references. Page numbers are preprint pages.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Mohit Tawarmalani (UIUC Mechanical & Industrial Engineering, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: written from the UIUC Department of Chemical Engineering (p. 1) while the convex-extension papers with Tawarmalani and Ahmed were still under submission ([35], [36], p. 30). It restates the 1995-96 range-reduction rules and the 1998 finiteness result. It reuses two group applications: Gutierrez and Sahinidis 1996 ([12]) and a 1999 working paper by Sahinidis, Tawarmalani and Yu ([33]) (pp. 28-29).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: show with concrete examples that finding the global optima of MINLP formulations matters in practice (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Both problems had been attacked before with local search, and global optimization found better designs (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: no new method. The paper names three developments as the keys to BARON's success (pp. 4-5): duality- and feasibility-based range reduction, finite branching schemes for certain classes, and convex extensions for nonlinear functions of 0-1 variables. Range reduction is presented as cheap approximations of the 2n bound-tightening LPs (p. 5). One is feasibility-based tightening, called poor man's linear programs (p. 6). The other is marginals-based tightening x_j ≥ x_j^U - (U - L)/λ_j from the value function (pp. 6-7; Table 1, p. 8). The branching point is chosen in three ways: periodic bisection, the incumbent when it lies in the node, otherwise the relaxation solution (p. 9). The convex-extension results are stated as four numbered items (pp. 9-10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: in the JIT flowshop model the objective is convex and the constraints linear, so integrality is the only nonconvexity. Once the machine numbers are fixed, no degrees of freedom remain (p. 13). The instances have P = 3 products and S = 4 stages, hence four degrees of freedom (p. 13). The refrigerant model uses the Joback-Stephanopoulos group-contribution properties and structural constraints from [33], said to be tighter than earlier ones (p. 15). It has 44 groups with N_max = 15 (pp. 16-17) and finite bounds on all variables (Table 7, p. 23). The property estimates are admitted to be uncertain (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: the value-function and supporting-hyperplane argument for marginals-based reduction (pp. 6-7). The distinction between finite and ε-finite termination, with the remark that *finitely convergent* is a misnomer (p. 8). Convex-extension theory is stated without proofs, which are in [35] and [36] (pp. 9-10).
- **D5 Experiments**: JIT: nine literature instances, the base case plus machine costs scaled by 0.5-2.5 and lot sizes by 0.5-2.25 (p. 13). The global designs of [12] are compared with the direct-pattern-search designs of [11]: savings range from 11% to 26%, average 17% (pp. 13-15). Refrigerant: one MINLP with 48 integer, 14 binary and 21 continuous variables and 64 constraints. Its base case solves in about 1 CPU minute and 2570 nodes on a 332 MHz RS/6000, and 28 designs are ranked (pp. 24-25).
- **D6 Positioning**: earlier molecular-design work by many groups (p. 15) includes a genetic algorithm, simulated annealing and stochastic optimization (refs. pp. 28-30). All of it is grouped as search techniques that are only local in nature (p. 22).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: application evidence for the three BARON devices (p. 24). Limits: the stability of the novel molecules is open (p. 24). The claim that larger problems would show larger savings is not tested (p. 15). The claim that the MINLP could not be solved without the devices is not backed by a reported run (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: range reduction comes first among BARON's keys to success (pp. 4-5) and is restated with its formulas (pp. 5-8). Without range reduction, branching rules and relaxation rules the refrigerant MINLP could not be solved (p. 24). The three are bundled, so this is not an ablation of reduction alone.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (weak): both applications run on BARON, and the paper describes the code's components (p. 4). The applications come from group projects ([12], [33]).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the certified optimum is the yardstick for the literature's heuristic designs, but on nine small instances and one molecular benchmark rather than a large library, and with no portfolio conclusion (pp. 13-15, 24).
- new-pattern candidates: *price of local search on the literature's own instances* (pp. 13-15; also S079 pp. 5-6). *Ranked near-global list via integer cuts under model uncertainty* (p. 22; Table 8, p. 25). *Finite branching by exhaustiveness plus incumbent branching*, with the claim that finite algorithms beat convergent ones even at a positive tolerance (pp. 8-9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Re-solve the literature's own instances, with its parameters, to certified optimality. Tabulate per-instance savings split by cost term, so the reader sees where the local method lost (Tables 3-5, pp. 14-15).
- When the model's coefficients are estimates (here group contributions), return a ranked list of global and near-global designs. Re-solve with integer cuts that exclude every earlier solution (p. 22; Table 8, p. 25).
- Branching-point rule: bisect every few iterations for exhaustiveness. Branch at the incumbent when it lies in the node, which makes it gapless. Otherwise branch at the relaxation solution (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "Although [33] uses an algorithm that guarantees global optimality, we still obtain several non-global solutions to the problem in order to alleviate the difficulty due to uncertainty in the thermodynamic property estimation techniques that were used." (p. 22)
- "Without the range reduction, branching rules, and relaxation construction rules described in Section 2, we were unable to solve this MINLP model." (p. 24)

### S077 · BARON—Branch and reduce optimization navigator (User's Manual Version 4 2000, no DOI)

**Read level**: partial. Read in full: title page and contents (pp. 1-6), Chapters 1-7 (pp. 7-61) and Chapter 9 (pp. 96-102). The bibliography was scanned (pp. 103-105). Chapter 8 was read at §8.1-8.2 (pp. 62-66), the §8.8 log (p. 83), the §8.10 log and the start of §8.11 (pp. 89-90), and §8.12 (pp. 92-95). The other module input-format examples of Chapter 8 were skimmed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author; 17 students and two NYU colleagues thanked, pp. 8-9) · **Position in trajectory**: Version 4.0 of 29 March 1999, revision of 28 June 2000, from the UIUC Department of Chemical Engineering (p. 1). It is free for non-commercial use and comes with no warranty (p. 8). The title page dates Version 1.0 to 2 March 1995 (p. 1), the day the FORTRAN code went out by FTP (p. 8). The 1991-93 GAMS code of 1,800 lines is called the first version (p. 7), not Version 1.0.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: nonconvex problems with multiple local optima occur across engineering and science (p. 7). BARON is presented as a system for solving them to global optimality. Its name comes from combining interval analysis and duality in the reduce step with branch and bound (p. 7). The history ties each version to applications (pp. 7-8). Version 1 served engineering design, JIT manufacturing, circuit layout and process planning. Version 2 served polynomial and multiplicative programs, robust controller design, and separable concave and fixed-charge problems. Version 4 served pooling, 0-1 fractional and multiplicative programs.
- **D2 Core idea and design**: branch and reduce. Range-reduction tests run on every node in pre- and post-processing. Duality-based tests are used when the convex relaxation returns duals, and feasibility-based heuristics in addition (§2.4, pp. 19-20). The node is re-solved while reduction is significant, then branched (Fig. 2.6, p. 21; p. 50). The devices are marginals and probing (§2.2, pp. 12-17; Tables 2.1-2.2) and poor man's LPs as a cheap stand-in for the 2n bound LPs (§2.3, pp. 17-19). Relaxations are McCormick factorable relaxations, exact at the bounds, with underestimators for bilinear, linear-fractional, concave univariate and odd-power terms (§2.5, pp. 20-24). Branching is violation-based, with periodic bisection, incumbent branching and ω-branching (§2.6, p. 24). The architecture is a callable library: a core that calls seven user subroutines, 11 specialized modules, and a factorable NLP module reached through a parser (Chs. 3, 7, 9; pp. 36-41, 55-61, 96-102).
- **Defaults (D2, Ch. 6)**: absolute gap 10^-6, relative gap 0, 1200 CPU seconds (p. 46). Bounds tightening, marginals tests, poor man's LPs and optimality-based tightening are on. Probing is off (`pdo = 0`), and only feasibility-based tightening runs in preprocessing (pp. 49-50). The preprocessing LPs are solved at the root (`prelpdo = 1`, p. 52). The NLP module uses an LP relaxation by default (`nlpdolin = 1`), while the IQP module uses a quadratic relaxation by default (`qplin = 0`) (p. 53). A breakdown of CPU time by component is written to `tim.lst` by default (p. 51).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: the relaxation gap must not increase as the region shrinks (p. 11). The relaxation need only be much easier to solve than P (p. 11). Duality-based tests need a convex relaxation solved with its duals (p. 20). So the user's lower-bounding routine must return marginals and build its underestimators from the current bounds (§7.2.2, p. 59). Factorable functions are limited to exp, ln, x^α and β^x (p. 41). Modules carry conditions, e.g. qx + β > 0 on a compact set for fractional programs (p. 38). In the parser, variable bounds are optional (p. 100), although relaxation tightness depends on the tightness of the bounds (p. 20). The user picks the feasibility tolerance, commonly 10^-5, with scaling taken into account (§7.2.3, p. 59).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: the marginals rule κ_j = x_j^U - (U - L)/λ_j is derived from the value function and a supporting hyperplane (pp. 12, 15). Probing fixes a variable at a bound and re-solves (p. 15). A two-variable example shows where poor man's LPs fail (pp. 18-19). The manual argues that solving the 2n LPs once at the root leaves a nonredundant bound in every later subdomain, so that case does not arise inside branch and bound (p. 19). A tangent-point equation gives the convex envelope of an odd monomial (p. 24).
- **D5 Experiments**: worked examples only. On x1x2 ≤ 4, the separable relaxation needs 5 iterations, McCormick's relaxation solves it at the root, marginals plus probing prove optimality at the root even with the weak relaxation, and incumbent branching needs 3 nodes instead of 5 (pp. 25-31). A five-variable concave QP from the Floudas-Pardalos collection takes 17 iterations with bisection branch and bound and 7 with branch and reduce at tolerance 10^-6 (p. 32). Chapter 8 adds logs of the modules on toy instances.
- **D7 Writing**: manual voice. Each device comes with a figure and a worked example with node counts (pp. 12-35). Every option is listed with its default, and users are told they need not change any (p. 46).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a software contribution. Stated limits: the general factorable module does not exploit the structure the specialized modules use (p. 41). The code runs on UNIX only (p. 42). The fractional module runs only under OSL, and the MISDP module only with SDPA; more solver interfaces are planned (p. 43).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: reduction runs on every node in pre- and post-processing (p. 20), and the node is re-solved after significant reduction (pp. 21, 50). Cheap reductions are on and probing is off by default (pp. 49-50). The worked examples give 17 vs 7 iterations (p. 32) and a root-node proof with a weak relaxation (p. 31). This is a full-text statement of the marginals rule (p. 15), which SKILL.md cites only through later restatements.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: a dated version history with code sizes, from 1,800 lines of GAMS to about 26,000 lines of FORTRAN 90 and 17,000 of C. Each version takes in the group's techniques and applications (pp. 7-8). The acknowledgements credit 17 students with shaping, developing and testing the ideas in the code (pp. 8-9). Naming caveat: the 1991-93 GAMS code is the first version (p. 7), while Version 1.0 is dated 2 March 1995 (p. 1).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the version-4 factorable NLP module allows an entirely LP-based approach (p. 8) and uses an LP relaxation by default (p. 53). Per-component CPU times are reported by default (pp. 51, 65-66).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the IQP module defaults to a quadratic relaxation, and a mixed-integer SDP module ships with SDPA (pp. 40, 43, 53, 92-95). So in 2000, nonlinear relaxations were defaults inside specialized modules.
- Also Heuristic 1 (special classes first): 11 specialized modules sit beside a general module whose capabilities subsume them but which does not exploit their structure (pp. 36-41).
- new-pattern candidates: *per-component CPU-time accounting by default* (pp. 51, 65-66). *Finite branching by exhaustiveness plus incumbent branching* (p. 24; worked example p. 31).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Solve the exact 2n bound-tightening LPs once at the root, then use one-constraint interval propagation at the nodes. The root step protects the cheap test from its pathological case (pp. 18-19, 52).
- After a node's relaxation, run reduction and re-solve the same node, before branching, while the bound improves by more than a set tolerance, up to a pass limit (pp. 21, 50).
- Print a CPU-time breakdown with every run: preprocessing, relaxation, local search, tightening, marginals, probing. The cost of each device is then visible without a profiler (pp. 51, 65-66).
- In a tutorial example, use the weaker relaxation on purpose so each acceleration shows in the node count. Then show the strong relaxation closing the gap at the root (pp. 26, 30-31).
- Build the solver as a core with user exits for range reduction, lower bounding, feasibility checking, starting points, branching, local search and objective evaluation. A new problem class can then be prototyped on the same engine (pp. 55-61).

**Quotes**:
- "These tests are applied to every subproblem of the search tree in pre- and post-processing steps to contract the search space and reduce the relaxation gap." (p. 20)
- "In this way, the capabilities of this module subsume those of all other specialized modules. However, this module is not designed to fully exploit the special structure of the problems addressed by the above-described specialized modules." (p. 41)
