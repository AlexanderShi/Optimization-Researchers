### S272 · A large-scale benchmarking of deterministic and stochastic derivative-free optimization algorithms (National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL), DOE PrOMMiS talk slides 2026, no DOI; OSTI purl 3031240)
**Read level**: full. All 20 slides were read, both in the text layer and on the PDF page images, including the acknowledgements (p. 19) and the disclaimer (p. 20); nothing was skimmed. A slide deck carries no prose, so "full" means every slide, chart and legend. The study's raw results and any accompanying paper were not on disk and were not read.
**Role**: core (talk slides; group voice) · **Coauthors**: Donghyun Oh (first author, Georgia Tech School of Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the 13-year rerun of the 2013 benchmark (Rios & Sahinidis 2013, S001; a signature work in SKILL.md), inside the DOE Process Optimization and Modeling for Minerals Sustainability (PrOMMiS) initiative (pp. 2, 19). The group's own DFO solver BAM is one of the 42 competitors (p. 5). In 2013 no solver of the group took part (note 03 §B4). BAM is the black-box product of his company, The Optimization Firm (TOF) [01 §1.9], and the slides do not say so.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: There are two entries. The first is the application in the PrOMMiS program (p. 2 image):
  - A "Black-box System" of chemical reactions and processes maps an input x to an output y, and an AI component suggests the next x from the data.
  - The loop is drawn as a self-driving-lab cycle of experimentation, characterization and optimization.
  - The goal is to "Accelerate identification of the most promising separation techniques for critical minerals production processes" (p. 2).

  The second entry is methodological: DFO solver performance is "highly sensitive to problem type, dimensionality, evaluation budget, etc.", and "different studies often reach inconsistent conclusions" (p. 3). A Scopus chart (retrieved 5 December 2025) plots yearly publications matching "Derivative-free optimization" OR "Black-box optimization" OR "Zeroth-order oracle" against "Benchmarking studies". The first series rises from ≈100 in 2013 to ≈340-370 a year in 2022-2025; the second stays at ≈5-55 a year (p. 3 chart). The query behind the second series is not given. The research question is printed in red: "Can we evaluate DFO algorithms on a large-scale, unified, reproducible benchmark suite?" (p. 3). The starting point is the group's own 2013 study: 22 solvers, 502 problems, n from 1 to 300, up to 2,500 evaluations, ten starting points and 112,448 instances (p. 4).
  - Transferable: open a benchmark with a dated bibliometric count that sets the growth of the method literature against the number of benchmarking studies, with the query and the retrieval date printed (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: Rerun the 2013 design on a wider field of solvers. There are 42 implementations (p. 5 image):
  - 5 "Same algorithms": GLOBAL, MCS, SNOBFIT, TOMLAB-MULTIMIN, TOMLAB-GLCCLUSTER.
  - 14 "Updated algorithms": ASA, CMAES and FMINSEARCH; DAKOTA DIRECT, EA, PATTERN and SOLIS-WETS; HOPSPACK (printed "HOPSACK"), IMFIL and NOMAD; Powell's BOBYQA and NEWUOA; PSWARM and SID-PSM.
  - 23 "Newly added algorithms": BAM; the Bayesian-optimization codes BayesOpt, EGO under DAKOTA and Limbo; BFO; DAKOTA MOGA, OPTPP_PDS and SOGA; the Derivative-Free Library codes ACRS, DDFSA, DFLBOX, DFLGEN, DFSA, DIRDFN, SDBOX and SDPEN; Powell's COBYLA, LINCOA and UOBYQA; PARTICLESWARM, PATTERNSEARCH, SIMULANNEALBND and VRBBO.

  The 42 split 28 deterministic / 14 stochastic and 23 global / 19 local (p. 6 image). Three solvers in the 2013 legend are not among the 42, with no reason given: TOMLAB_OQNLP, TOMLAB_LGO and DFO (p. 4 chart legend vs p. 5).

  Kept from 2013: the 502-problem count, the 2,500-evaluation budget and the ten starting points (pp. 4, 7-8). The slides do not say in so many words that the problem set itself is unchanged.

  New in the protocol: "solved" is judged on the median of the 10 runs, within 1% relative or 0.01 absolute of the global optimum, f(y) ≤ f(x*) + max(0.01|f(x*)|, 0.01) (p. 8; the formula is in the image). Note 03 §B4 quotes the 2013 rule as the same threshold applied per run.

  New in the analysis:
  - Family panels, local "Direct and line search" (14 codes) vs "Trust-region" (5) (p. 10), and global "Branch-without-bound" (6: BAM, GLCCLUSTER, MCS, SNOBFIT, DAKOTA-DIRECT, DIRDFN) vs "Others (BO, stochastic, etc.)" (17) (p. 11). Together they partition the 42 as in p. 6's 19/23 split.
  - Four dimension buckets (pp. 12-13).
  - A virtual-best curve labelled "Solvable" (pp. 14-15, image legend).
  - A minimal covering set (p. 16).
  - An in-silico application (p. 17).
  - Transferable: when rerunning an old benchmark, tag every solver "same", "updated" or "newly added" (p. 5), so that a change in the ranking can be traced to new versions or to new entrants.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - **Test set.** 502 test problems; "All problems are available from minlp.com" (p. 7). n ranges over [1, 300] (p. 9 image). By dimension (p. 7 pie): 20% have n ∈ {1, 2}, 26% n ∈ [3, 9], 24% n ∈ [10, 30] and 30% n ∈ [31, 300]. Four classes are shown by example surfaces (convex or nonconvex, smooth or nonsmooth), with no count per class (p. 7 image).
  - **Computational model.** Budget only: up to 2,500 function evaluations, ten starting points "for solvers that accept user-specified starting points", 10 runs per problem (p. 8).
  - **Not stated on the slides:**
    - the constraint type (the 2013 problems were bound-constrained only, note 03 §B4);
    - noise;
    - how the global optima x* were obtained (BARON and LINDOGLOBAL in 2013, note 03 §B4);
    - what varies between the 10 runs of a solver that takes no starting point;
    - solver options, versions and hardware.
  - **Application model.** A published flow reactor (Hone et al. 2017; Felton et al. 2021) with four design variables: residence time, the concentrations of reactants A and B, and temperature. Two objectives are optimized separately, maximum space-time yield and minimum E-factor ("Waste") (p. 17; the objectives are in the image).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a. The deck has no theorems or proofs; its analysis devices are the evaluation metrics described under D5.
- **D5 Experiments**: All results are curves of the fraction of problems solved against the number of evaluations, from 0 to 2,500 (pp. 9-15 charts).
  - **Overall (n ∈ [1, 300]).** BAM ends at 81%: "BAM with an 81% success rate outperformed all other algorithms" (p. 14). It is at ≈0.44 after ≈100 evaluations and ≈0.59 at 500. MULTIMIN ends at ≈0.64, GLCCLUSTER ≈0.62, MCS ≈0.51, SID-PSM ≈0.41, NOMAD and SNOBFIT ≈0.37 (p. 9 chart).
  - **Local solvers (p. 10 charts).** The best direct and line-search codes are SID-PSM ≈0.41 and NOMAD ≈0.37. The best trust-region codes are UOBYQA ≈0.30, BOBYQA and LINCOA ≈0.28, NEWUOA ≈0.23 and COBYLA ≈0.12.
  - **Global solvers (p. 11 charts).** In the branch-without-bound family: BAM ≈0.81, GLCCLUSTER ≈0.62, MCS ≈0.51, SNOBFIT ≈0.37, DAKOTA-DIRECT ≈0.15 and DIRDFN ≈0.10. Among the others, MULTIMIN ≈0.64 and CMAES ≈0.31 lead. The three Bayesian-optimization codes (DAKOTA-EGO, BayesOpt, Limbo) all end below 0.10.
  - **By dimension.** The best rates, all BAM's, are 98% for n ∈ {1, 2} and 80% for n ∈ [3, 9] (p. 12 image), and 88% for n ∈ [10, 30] and 64% for n ∈ [31, 300] (p. 13 image). In the largest bucket the next best are GLCCLUSTER ≈0.33 and MULTIMIN ≈0.28. In both high-dimensional buckets BAM reaches ≈0.3 within its first ≈50-100 evaluations; for n ∈ [31, 300] no other solver has passed ≈0.05 at that point (p. 13 charts). The slides do not say what BAM evaluates first.
  - **Virtual best.** It solves 88% overall (p. 14), and 100%, 91%, 93% and 72% by bucket (p. 15).
  - **Minimal set (p. 16 image).** BAM (404), GLCCLUSTER (23), MULTIMIN (4), MCS (2), SNOBFIT (2), CMAES (2), BOBYQA (1), DFSA (1): "88% solvable by the minimal set of eight solvers using the maximum budget" (p. 16). BAM's 404 matches its own ≈81%, while GLCCLUSTER's 23 is far below its own ≈62%, so the counts read as marginal additions in sequence (my reading, pp. 9, 16). The slides do not say whether the set was found greedily or by an exact cover.
  - **Unchanged codes against 2013.** They end close to, and slightly above, their 2013 curves (p. 4 vs p. 9 charts). TOMLAB-MULTIMIN goes from ≈0.61 to ≈0.64, TOMLAB-GLCCLUSTER from ≈0.60 to ≈0.62, MCS from ≈0.50 to ≈0.51 and SNOBFIT from ≈0.33 to ≈0.37. The slides do not comment on this.
  - **In-silico demonstration (p. 17).** NOMAD, BAM, SID-PSM, MCS and SNOBFIT all reach the same plateau within about 50-100 evaluations: a space-time yield of ≈11,500 kg m⁻³ h⁻¹ and an E-factor of ≈8.5 ("50 evaluations (experiments) to optimize the system", p. 17). The TOMLAB codes ranked second and third overall are not in it, and no reason is given (p. 17 legend).
  - **Not shown anywhere (pp. 1-20):** CPU or wall time, solver versions and options, seeds, best-of-10 results, crashes or failures per solver, a hold-out set, and where the raw results are.
  - Transferable: put the virtual-best curve and the best single solver on one plot, overall and per dimension bucket, so the reader sees what the rest of the field adds: 88% vs 81% overall, and 72% vs 64% for n ∈ [31, 300] (pp. 14-15).

Its curves are reproduced, with the 2013 leaders TOMLAB_MULTIMIN and TOMLAB_GLCCLUSTER at ≈0.6 and MCS at ≈0.5 (p. 4 chart), and it is credited on the solver slide ("Rios and Sahinidis (2013)", p. 5). BAM is listed as one more "Newly added" code and sits with the DIRECT-type partitioning codes in the "Branch-without-bound" family (pp. 5, 11). The slides never say that BAM is the authors' own solver (pp. 1-20).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Talk format, with one framing question (p. 3), slide titles that name the finding ("Minimal Solver Set", p. 16), and headline numbers in color (pp. 12-14, 18 images). Hedging is low: BAM "outperformed all other algorithms" (pp. 14, 18), and the portfolio is a "Minimal, irreducible set of dominating solvers" (p. 18). The conclusion calls the success curves "convergence rates" (p. 18). There are small slips:
  - "HOPSACK" on p. 5, against "HOPSPACK" in the legends of pp. 4 and 9.
  - The p. 16 counts give 404/502 = 80.5% for BAM and 439/502 = 87.5% for the set. The printed 81% and 88% are about half a point higher, and the slides do not state the denominator behind the percentages (my arithmetic, pp. 7, 14, 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a benchmark update plus an application rehearsal.
  - **Stated conclusions (p. 18):**
    - 42 solvers were compared on 502 problems;
    - the top performers are BAM, GLCCLUSTER and MULTIMIN (BAM 81%; all solvers together 88%);
    - a minimal set of eight solvers covers what the field solves;
    - a self-driving lab was demonstrated in silico;

  - **Stated future work (p. 18):** more "State-of-the-art DFO solvers", and more realistic science and engineering problems, especially critical-materials and minerals separation and recovery processes.
  - **Limitations:** none are stated. Missing in what I read:

    - Versions, options, seeds and hardware are not named, despite "reproducible" in the research question (pp. 3, 5).
    - Only the median run is reported (p. 8).
    - Where the optima come from, and whether the problems have constraints or noise, is not stated (pp. 7-8).
    - The headline numbers hold "using the maximum budget" of 2,500 evaluations (p. 16), while the motivating laboratory use is shown at about 50 experiments (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- **Method 4 ✅ evidence.**
  - Every available implementation goes onto one large public library under identical conditions: 42 codes on 502 problems from minlp.com, all with the same budget and the same ten starting points (pp. 5, 7-8).
  - Success is defined explicitly against the global optimum, on the median of 10 runs (p. 8).
  - The verdict is a virtual-best ceiling and a minimal covering portfolio of eight solvers (pp. 14-16, 18).
- **Method 4 ⚠ variant.**
  - The group's own algorithm enters the benchmark and is headlined as the single winner (pp. 5, 14, 18).
  - It is tested on 502 problems from minlp.com (p. 7). Per note 03 §A2, the 502-problem set on minlp.com (the 2013 library) is one of the two libraries TOF says it routinely uses to develop BAM.
  - The slides mention none of the 2013 safeguards listed in note 03 §B4: no hold-out, no developer rounds, no certification source for the optima and no rationale for the budget.
  - This is slide-level evidence for the ⚠️ on BAM in SKILL.md Method 4 (step 7 applies the hold-out to one's own algorithm) and for note 03 §C1. The figures match the OSTI abstract quoted in note 03 §C2: 42 algorithms, 502 problems, BAM above 80%.
- **Method 2 ✅ evidence** (step 3: one protocol kept across papers). Thirteen years later the same scale, budget and starting points are reused, solvers are tagged same, updated or new, and the unchanged codes land near their 2013 curves (pp. 4-5, 8-9). Versions are not named, which falls short of Method 2's named builds and Heuristic 6.
- **Method 3 ⚠ variant.** The self-driving-lab framing sends the experimental black box straight to a DFO loop, and the demonstration optimizes a reactor simulator by DFO (pp. 2, 17). No step that first makes the system algebraic is shown, and the slides do not say whether BAM fits models internally. The deck therefore matches only Method 3's last clause: keep derivative-free search for what stays black-box.
- **Heuristic 10 ✅.** The problem source is now a DOE minerals program with national-laboratory partners (pp. 2, 19), and the engine kept is the group's own solver ("We are about to apply BAM to the optimization of critical minerals separation processes", p. 18).
- **New-pattern candidates:** in-silico rehearsal of a self-driving-lab campaign (pp. 17-18); count-based motivation for a benchmark (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Rerun an old benchmark with its problems, budget and starting points fixed, and tag every solver "same", "updated" or "newly added". The unchanged codes then act as a replication check: their curves should land where they did before (pp. 4-5, 8-9).
- Score multi-run solvers on the median run against a known optimum with a mixed tolerance, f(y) ≤ f(x*) + max(0.01|f(x*)|, 0.01) (p. 8), so that a stochastic or multistart code is judged by its typical run rather than its luckiest.
- Plot the virtual-best "Solvable" curve next to the best single solver, overall and per dimension bucket (pp. 14-15).
- Report the minimal covering portfolio as a stacked bar of marginal problem counts, and state the budget at which it holds (p. 16).
- Compare solvers within search-architecture families before the all-solver plot: among local solvers, direct and line search vs trust region; among global solvers, branch-without-bound vs the rest (pp. 10-11).
- Before a self-driving-lab campaign, run the shortlisted solvers on a published simulator of the experiment and read the evaluation axis as the number of experiments (p. 17).
