### H013 · Cooling Crystallization Solvent Design (Test problems for SO and DFO algorithms, group website 2014, no DOI)

**Read level**: full: all 3 pages (problem description p. 1, simulator interface p. 2, reference p. 3). The example in.txt and out.txt files are figures, and their contents are not in the extracted text (p. 2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nick Austin (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: dated July 28, 2014 (p. 1). The problem comes from the mixture-design work published as Austin, Samudra, Sahinidis and Trahan, AIChE J. 62, 1514-1530, 2016 (p. 3). The work list, not the sheet, places it among the group's test problems for SO and DFO algorithms.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: an application, not a method. The task is to design an ideal solvent for the cooling crystallization of ibuprofen (p. 1). The degrees of freedom are five solubility properties: the three Hansen parameters (δh, δp, δd), the octanol-water partition coefficient Kow and the aqueous solubility Cw. They are taken to be correlated with percent recovery, the measure of a solvent's quality (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: the problem is packaged as a black box. The executable MD 64 reads the five properties from in.txt, one per line in the order δd, δp, δh, Kow, Cw. It solves a molecular design problem for the structure closest to these property targets and writes that structure's percent recovery to out.txt. If no structure lies "within a reasonable distance", it returns −1 (p. 2). Each evaluation therefore hides an inner design solve over molecules.
- **D3 Assumptions**: none stated. The text gives no bounds on the five inputs, no noise model, no evaluation cost and no known optimum (pp. 1-3; the figure contents were not extracted).
- **D4 Analysis devices**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a. This is a problem sheet; results are in the referenced AIChE J. paper, which was not read (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: the contribution is a runnable test problem with a fixed file interface (p. 2). Limits visible in the sheet: "reasonable distance" is not defined, and failure is signalled by a value, −1, inside the objective channel (p. 2). My own reading, not the sheet's: a maximizer will treat a failed evaluation as a very poor point, not as a hidden-constraint violation, unless its wrapper translates the value. No bounds or starting point are given in the text.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak): a real application turned into a runnable test problem with a documented input/output interface (p. 2), which is the "publish problems" step (step 5). That it was posted as a benchmark comes from the work list, not from the sheet.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the black box is kept. The discrete choice (the molecule) is made inside the executable by a design solve, and the outer search runs over five continuous property targets (p. 2). The reference's title names derivative-free optimization in the space of individual component properties (p. 3). The sheet does not say which DFO method was used.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ship an application test problem as a file-in/file-out executable with a fixed input order and a documented failure value. Any derivative-free solver can then be wired to it without the source (p. 2). Do better than this sheet: return the failure as a separate flag, and print the bounds and a reference solution.

**Quotes**:
- "If no structure is found within a reasonable distance from the property inputs, a value of −1 is returned." (p. 2)
- "These properties will serve as the problem’s degrees of freedom and are considered to be correlated with percent recovery, a metric used to quantify a solvent’s goodness for this process." (p. 1)

### S109 · Relaxations of factorable functions with convex-transformable intermediates (Mathematical Programming 2014, doi:10.1007/s10107-012-0618-8)

**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract and §1 (pp. 1-3); the §2 statements with their short proofs (pp. 3-6); §3 in full (pp. 6-10); Props 10-11 with proofs (pp. 10-12); §4.2 up to the end of Prop 13 with Table 1 (pp. 13-18); the Prop 14-15 statements and the Table 2 summary (pp. 19-23); the §5 opening, Lemma 1, Prop 16 and Remark 6 (pp. 23-24); the §6 opening, Prop 19 and Examples 5-6 (pp. 27-30); §§7-8 in full (pp. 29-33). Not read: most of the proofs of Props 12, 14 and 15 (pp. 14-15, 19-21); Examples 1-4 and Props 17-18 (pp. 24-27); the reference list (pp. 33-34).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Aida Khajavirad (IBM T. J. Watson Research Center at publication) and Jeremy J. Michalek (CMU Mechanical Engineering and Engineering & Public Policy) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received 30 June 2010, accepted 25 November 2012; NSF-supported (p. 1). It belongs to the Khajavirad convexification line, whose envelope papers of 2012-2013 the 2015 manual lists next to it (S159 v15.2 pp. 29-30).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: factorable relaxations decompose a function until every intermediate has a known convex or concave envelope. Apart from univariate functions, few nonconvex functions have envelopes that are both known and practical to implement (p. 2). The question is whether nested decompositions can be "replaced by, or enhanced via" functional transformations that exploit the convex transformability of component functions (p. 2). Earlier transformation schemes covered signomials only (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: φ is G-convex if G(φ) is convex for some increasing G. For such φ, relax {φ(x) ≤ t} as {G(φ(x)) ≤ Ḡ(t)} with Ḡ a concave overestimator of G (Prop 7, p. 6). Among such relaxations, the least convexifying transformation G*, with Ḡ = conc G*, gives the tightest one (Cor 4, p. 7). The scheme is applied to signomials (§4), to products and ratios of convex and concave functions (§5) and to log-concave functions (§6), then inserted into a factorable decomposition (§7) (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions**: φ is continuous on a convex set and G continuous and increasing; the characterization needs C² (pp. 3, 5). The relaxation needs bounds on φ: the lower bound comes from a convex problem, the upper bound from evaluating φ at the box vertices. The latter is cheap because intermediates "rarely involve more than ten variables" (Remark 5, p. 10). Prop 14 needs nonnegative exponents and zero lower bounds (p. 21).
- **D4 Analysis devices**: positive semidefiniteness of the augmented Hessian ∇²φ + ρ∇φ∇φᵀ (eq. 3, p. 5), checked through principal minor signs, characterizes G-convex and G-concave signomials and their least transformations (Props 10-11, pp. 10-12). Which of two transformations dominates is read off the concavity or convexity of F = G2∘G1⁻¹ (Prop 8, p. 7). Closed-form maximum gaps of both relaxations are derived and compared (Props 12-13, pp. 13-16). An exchange argument finds the exponent pairing that minimizes the gap of the RT scheme (Prop 15, p. 20).
- **D5 Experiments**: no test library. Gaps are measured as maximum and total gap and percent reduction (eqs. 13-15, pp. 9-10). Combining the transformation and factorable overestimators cuts the factorable maximum and total gaps by 55% and 29% on average over 30 signomial-and-box cases (Table 1, p. 17). The RT scheme gives 28% and 18%, and 4% more total-gap reduction when combined (pp. 21-23). One branch-and-bound example runs BARON 9.3 at default options with a 10% relative tolerance: 99 nodes with the standard decomposition, 11 with the added G-convexity cuts, 99.83% less root gap; at 0.001% tolerance there are 85% fewer nodes (pp. 31-32).
- **D6 Positioning**: measured against "a widely used factorable scheme" and, in §5, against the tightest possible factorable relaxation, which Lemma 1 selects (pp. 23, 33). It presents itself as a bridge between generalized convexity, a theory with "limited applications in nonconvex optimization", and global optimization (pp. 2, 33).
- **D7 Writing**: the body reports where the new relaxation loses. The factorable overestimator globally dominates under case (ii) of Prop 11 (Prop 12, p. 14). One example has a larger maximum gap for the transformation (pp. 17-18), and another has 15% more total gap (p. 18). The conclusion then says the transformation relaxations were "considerably tighter" "In all instances" (p. 33), a stronger claim than the body supports. The computational claim is hedged (p. 32).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: theory plus new relaxations. The relaxations are not yet in a solver: "Future research will integrate the proposed relaxations into a global solver" (p. 33). Their functional form can be more complex, which matters to solvers that solve nonlinear relaxations, not to polyhedral ones (p. 33).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the paper argues that the more complex form does not matter in a solver that builds polyhedral relaxations and solves them with robust LP codes. BARON solves a crude LP relaxation at each node and adds supporting-hyperplane cuts only if they shrink the relaxation (p. 33).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the relaxations are ranked by gap closed on chosen functions and boxes (Tables 1-2, pp. 17, 22-23). Time and numerical failures are not measured. The only solver evidence is node counts in one example, which the authors themselves call preliminary (p. 32).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: here the paper comes before the code. The test feeds an alternative decomposition to a production BARON (9.3, default options) instead of switching a feature (pp. 31-32), and solver integration is left for future work (p. 33). The 2015 manual already lists the paper among BARON-related publications (S159 v15.2 p. 30), but that list covers "algorithms implemented in the software, the theory behind them, and some related applications" (S159 v15.2 p. 27). So the listing does not show the method was implemented.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (weak): Remark 5 ties the tightness of the relaxation to the bounds on the intermediate (p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: *add, don't replace*. Where neither relaxation dominates, keep the old one and intersect it with the new one: "include both relaxations" (p. 16), combined relaxations reduce gaps further (pp. 17, 22, 31), and the solver test adds cuts to the standard relaxation (p. 32).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To rank two relaxations of one function, define pointwise, maximum and total (integrated) gap and report percent reductions (eqs. 9-15, pp. 9-10). Report the pointwise minimum of the two as a third entry (pp. 17, 22).
- Compare a new relaxation with the tightest version of the old scheme, not with its default decomposition (Lemma 1, p. 23).
- Before claiming dominance, compute closed-form maximum gaps for both relaxations and publish the cases where the new one loses (pp. 13-18).
- To test inside a closed solver that has no switch for your idea, give it an alternative decomposition of the same model at fixed options and compare nodes and root gap (pp. 31-32).

**Quotes**:
- "Thus, it is mostly advantageous to include both relaxations in computational implementations." (p. 16)
- "Clearly, these are preliminary results and we do not attempt to make any conclusion about the computational benefits of the proposed relaxation in general." (p. 32)

### S039 · A combined first-principles and data-driven approach to model building (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2015, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2014.11.010)

**Read level**: full: the abstract and body pp. 1-37 (all sections, tables and examples; figures are not in the text). Of the reference list (pp. 38-40) only refs. [23]-[28] were read (p. 40). The file is the author manuscript under the Elsevier user license (p. 1); cite it as S039 manuscript.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Alison Cozad (CMU) and David C. Miller (U.S. DOE National Energy Technology Laboratory) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the second ALAMO methods paper. It uses ALAMO as described in ref. [24], Cozad, Sahinidis & Miller, AIChE J. 2014 (pp. 5, 40), as its test platform. The work was done under NETL's Regional University Alliance as part of the Carbon Capture Simulation Initiative (p. 37).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: modelers choose between first-principles models and empirical surrogates; the paper combines the two (p. 2). Earlier constrained regression restricted only the parameter (β) space, which ties a constraint to one functional form (pp. 3-4). The gap it names: "no investigation" of constraints that relate predictors to responses (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: impose prior knowledge as constraints f[x, ẑ(x;β)] ≤ 0 for all x ∈ X, in the space of predictors and responses (eq. 1, p. 2). Such constraints do not depend on the model's functional form (p. 4). The resulting semi-infinite problem is solved in two phases (pp. 7-9, Algorithm 1). Phase I fits on a finite point set. Phase II maximizes the violation over the whole domain with BARON and returns up to n_viol violating points, kept apart by an l∞ separation, for the next round. The method sits inside ALAMO's best-subset MIQP with corrected AIC (pp. 5-6, 23). The constraint classes are response bounds, relations among several responses, derivative and convexity limits, a wider enforcement domain (safe extrapolation) and boundary conditions (§4, pp. 14-22).
- **D3 Assumptions**: for models linear in β, Phase I keeps linear constraints. Phase II is nonconvex and needs a global solver (p. 7). A bound taken from a simpler system must be a theoretical limit, not an approximation (p. 16). Derivative constraints need once- or twice-differentiable models (p. 18). Convexity is imposed by det(H) ≥ 0 (eq. 9, p. 20). My own check, not in the paper: beyond one dimension, det(H) ≥ 0 is necessary but not sufficient for a positive semidefinite Hessian, since a negative-definite 2×2 Hessian also has a positive determinant. The worked convexity example is one-dimensional, where ẑ'' ≥ 0 is exact (p. 28).
- **D4 Analysis devices**: n/a (no theorems). Termination rests on the semi-infinite programming fact that β ∈ Ω(X) iff max_x f[x, ẑ(x;β)] ≤ 0 (pp. 4-5), checked by solving Phase II globally (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Illustrative fit of x⁵: each round raises training error and lowers test error; the test error factors are 48.9 unconstrained and 1 constrained (pp. 10-11).
  - Example 1, a batch reactor: the unconstrained test error is 7.25 times the constrained one. A wider enforcement domain cuts the error 55.2-fold against unconstrained and 7.61-fold against constrained, both at five terms (pp. 23-26).
  - Example 2, enforced convexity: test error 24% lower at 10% higher training error (p. 28).
  - Example 3, a 4-D logistic function: the unconstrained 7-term model ranges over [−1.35, 4.62] against physical limits [0, 1]. The constrained model has two terms and does not change with the data-set size (pp. 30-31).
  - Study: 120 generated problems (3 families × 10 random forms × 4 LHS data sets, pp. 32-33), three ALAMO variants, cumulative error-factor plots for training, original-domain test and two extrapolation domains (p. 34). Constrained regression is best or tied on the original-domain test in 48% of problems; unconstrained has the lowest training error in 87%. Enforcing over the wider domain is best in 58% and 60% of the lower and upper extrapolation tests (pp. 34-36). The extra cost (Table 12): means of 1.93 and 2.28 rounds and up to 18 extra regression solves (p. 36).
- **D6 Positioning**: extends the parameter-space restrictions of Rao, Bard and Korkhin (p. 3). Within semi-infinite programming, it replaces Žaković and Rustem's multistart search for violations with branch-and-reduce (pp. 7-8).
- **D7 Writing**: the Phase II solve by BARON is said to "guarantee rigorous global optimality" (p. 8), stronger wording than the skill's own "global within tolerances" rule. Results are reported as profiles of an error factor EF = RMSE_m/RMSE_best (pp. 11, 34).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a method, its implementation in ALAMO and a catalogue of constraint classes. Costs admitted: more regression re-solves and iterations, more again for wider domains (p. 36). The general multi-response case conflicts with ALAMO's treatment of outputs one at a time and is handled by a sequential adaptation (pp. 16-18). No external baseline or competing constrained-regression code is run, and the test set is generated from three function families (pp. 32-33). The conclusion claims the approach works for methods from ordinary least squares to subset selection and regularized regression (p. 37); regularized regression is not tested in the paper.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: this paper is behind step 4 ("impose first-principles bounds ... and certify them"). Bounds and physical relations are imposed during fitting and certified over the whole domain by a global solve (pp. 2, 7-8). The constrained models are also smaller when data are scarce (2 terms against 7, pp. 30-31). In the authors' words, limits have "the greatest impact when little initial data is available" (p. 31).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the method is implemented and evaluated inside ALAMO, the test platform (pp. 5, 23), by running the same data sets with the feature off, on and extended (p. 32).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: a profile-style comparison over a 120-problem library, but the library is generated from three families. There is no external competitor and no structurally new hold-out family; the hold-out is new points and extrapolation domains of the same functions (pp. 32-36).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write domain knowledge as constraints on the model's output over the input domain (bounds, sums, monotonicity, curvature), not on its coefficients, so the constraints survive a change of basis functions (p. 4).
- Enforce "for all x" constraints by discretize-and-certify. Fit on a finite set, solve the maximum-violation problem globally, and add several mutually separated violators per round to save refits. Stop when no violator remains (pp. 7-9, 11).
- Compare model-building variants by an error factor EF = RMSE/RMSE_best. Plot the fraction of problems within each EF separately for training, in-domain test and each extrapolation domain (pp. 11, 34).
- Test extrapolation on purpose: enforce limits over a wider domain than the data and validate on points drawn from the extension (pp. 21-22, 25-27).

**Quotes**:
- "To our knowledge, there has been no investigation into the enforcement of a priori information that directly relates predictors to regressors." (p. 3)
- "We propose a method that is similar to (b), but we employ a branch-and-reduce optimization solver—instead of a multistart local search—to guarantee rigorous global optimality." (p. 8)

### S159 · BARON user manual v. 15.6.5 (The Optimization Firm 2015, no DOI; read as v15.2)

**Read level**: full for the version read. The file is "BARON user manual v. 15.2" of February 27, 2015 (p. 1), with a sample log from BARON 15.2.0 built on 24 February 2015 (p. 9); all 30 pages were read. Version 15.6.5 itself was not read. Cite as S159 v15.2.
**Role**: supplement (software manual) · **Coauthors**: none; Nick Sahinidis, The Optimization Firm (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the BARON user contract of early 2015. It sits between the 2005 GAMS manual of v7.2.5 (S012) and the v17.4.1 manual dated April 1, 2017 (S112, first page checked only).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: n/a as research. The manual tells users how to state a model so BARON can return global optima. The guarantee holds "under fairly general assumptions" that include finite bounds on nonlinear expressions (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: the product is branch and bound "enhanced with a variety of constraint propagation and duality techniques for reducing ranges of variables" (p. 1). LP/MIP subproblems go to CLP/CBC, CPLEX or XPRESS; NLP subproblems to IPOPT, FilterSD, CONOPT, MINOS or SNOPT (p. 3).
  - User levers: variable bounds, branching priorities, RELAXATION_ONLY_EQUATIONS, CONVEX_EQUATIONS and starting points (pp. 6-7).
  - Features: no starting point needed (p. 15); the best few or all feasible solutions from a single tree, with an isolation tolerance (pp. 15-18); a multistart heuristic mode (p. 18); unbounded rays (p. 18); IISs for nonconvex models (pp. 18-19); automatic recognition of complementarity (p. 19); parallel MIP subproblems (p. 20).
- **D3 Assumptions**: allowed functions are exp, ln, x^α, β^x, products and ratios; |x| and x^y go through reformulations; there are no trigonometric functions (pp. 3, 5). If BARON cannot infer bounds that make every nonlinear expression finite, "global optimality of the solutions provided may not be guaranteed" (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis devices**: n/a (no proofs). The status vocabulary works as the certificate (pp. 12-14):
  - "Normal completion" means solved within tolerances; model status 1 reads "optimal within tolerances".
  - "Heuristic termination" means global optimality is not guaranteed.
  - Missing bounds lead to "Infeasibility is therefore not guaranteed" or "Globality is therefore not guaranteed".
  - A separate status flags numerically sensitive problems.
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no experiments). A sample log for gear.bar shows the run order: start-point check, local search, bounding LP, multistart, branch and bound. It then breaks time down by component (tightening, marginals, probing) and counts cuts by class (pp. 9-11). The PUMA robot example finds all 14 solutions (p. 18).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: limits stated in the manual:
  - The demo version handles at most 10 variables, 10 constraints and 50 nonlinear operations (p. 2).
  - There are no trigonometric functions (p. 3).
  - With missing bounds, the printed relaxation bounds "may not be valid" (p. 12).
  - DeltaTerm relies on CPU time and "may result in nondeterministic behavior" (p. 20).
  - Non-KKT points get no duals (p. 15).

  Defaults worth recording: EpsA 1e-6, EpsR 1e-4 and MaxTime 1000 s (pp. 20-22); one core (pp. 20, 27); AllowFilterSD 0 (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: range reduction is on by default in every form (pp. 22-23): nonlinear and linear feasibility-based (TDo, LBTTDo), marginals-based (MDo) and optimality-based (OBTTDo). The number of probing problems is "automatically decided by BARON" (PDo = −2). To sample local minima without reduction, a user must switch four options off (p. 18), so reduction is a switchable unit.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: node relaxations are LP/MIP with outer approximators of convex functions (NOuter1, NOutPerVar, NOutIter, OutGrid; p. 22). For integer problems "most of BARON's time is spent on solving MIP relaxations" (p. 20). The NLP subsolver is chosen, and may be switched during the search, "based on problem characteristics and solver performance" (p. 25). FilterSD is excluded from automatic local-search selection by default (AllowFilterSD 0, p. 26), yet the sample log lists it among possible NLP subsolvers (p. 10). The skill dates FilterSD's switch-off to release 2022.1.19. This file does not show what changed in between, or whether FilterSD had another role.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: every run prints the version and build date and asks users to cite Tawarmalani & Sahinidis 2005 (p. 9). The log gives time per component and cuts per class (pp. 10-11). The bibliography lists 37 publications "that describe the algorithms implemented in the software, the theory behind them, and some related applications", from Ryoo & Sahinidis 1995 to Bao et al. 2015 (pp. 27-30).
- Not counted as methods, since the digest counts only methods:
  - Heuristic 2 ✅: bounds and relaxation-only and convex declarations are the user's levers (pp. 3, 7).
  - Heuristic 6 ✅: the manual flags the nondeterminism of a time-based option (p. 20).
  - Heuristic 7 ✅: status messages keep "within tolerances" apart from "not guaranteed", and they were already in place in 2015 (pp. 12-14).
  - Workflow B1 step 5 ✅: the multistart mode (NumLoc with MaxIter 0) is documented as a way "to gain insight" into local solutions, not as a global solve (p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- End every solver run with a small fixed status vocabulary that separates "solved within tolerances" from "a feasible solution was found but global optimality was not proven" and names the missing ingredient, such as missing bounds (pp. 12-14). Users then cannot mistake a printed bound for a valid one.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the chapter states the division of labour of Method 3 step 1. DFO solvers are "not able to handle very large numbers of variables, as in mixed integer problems involving superstructure optimization" (p. 8). Algebraic surrogates let the superstructure be "rigorously solved to optimality" (p. 17), and ALAMO selects models by an information criterion through integer optimization (p. 7).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the chapter says, without caveat, that adaptive sampling "outperforms classical Latin hypercube sampling" (p. 7). The skill records that ALAMO's adaptive sampling was effectively disabled by a bug until release 2020.4.25, and that random sampling stayed the default until 2024.10.19 (SKILL.md, Method 3 say-do note). So in 2017 users may not have had what the chapter describes. The claim is attributed to Cozad et al. in a co-authored chapter. Workflow E's checkpoint warns against restating results more strongly than the papers.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: as in H006, ALAMO is a module of the sponsor's framework (p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: *per-unit surrogates*. Fit one surrogate per piece of equipment instead of one per flowsheet, so each simulation converges and each fit stays small, then assemble the superstructure MINLP (p. 17). Attribution is weak (13 authors).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Fit surrogates unit by unit and assemble them in the optimization model: unit simulations converge more often and each fit is smaller (p. 17).
- Before giving any UQ statistic, report how many sampled simulations converged (338 of 510; 9648 of 10,000) (pp. 19-20).
- When you still use a response surface for sensitivity analysis, say where it is weak (MARS at high COE) (p. 21).
