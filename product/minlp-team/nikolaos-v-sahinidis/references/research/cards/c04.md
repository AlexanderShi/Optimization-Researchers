### S022 · Computer-aided molecular design: An introduction and review of tools, applications, and solution techniques (Chemical Engineering Research and Design 2016, doi:10.1016/j.cherd.2016.10.014; arXiv:1701.03978)
**Read level**: full. The whole text, pp. 1-29, was read: Sections 1-6, equations (1)-(62), Figs. 1-13 as extracted, and Tables 1-3. The reference list (pp. 29-38) was skimmed; I read it closely only for the self-citations [3], [4], [146]-[148] and to check for duplicates. The file is the arXiv accepted manuscript of the journal article; p. 1 carries the CC-BY-NC-ND notice and the DOI.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Nick D. Austin (Carnegie Mellon University, first author) and Daniel W. Trahan (The Dow Chemical Company, Freeport, TX) (p. 1). The voice is joint throughout. · **Place in trajectory**: This is a late-CMU tutorial review, written with a CMU first author and an industrial co-author from Dow (p. 1). The text does not give Austin's status; the SKILL's mentorship notes cite an Austin 2017 thesis acknowledgement. The group's earliest paper it cites is the 2003 design of alternative refrigerants via global optimization, with Tawarmalani and Yu ([146], p. 36). It also places the group's 2013 framework [147] and 2016 mixture-design papers [3], [4] among the applications (pp. 23, 25-27). The arXiv posting is dated 15 Jan 2017 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The review presents chemical product design as a trial-and-error practice confined to small design spaces (p. 1). It sets this against a chemical space of over 115 million registered structures and an estimated 10^60 small drug-like molecules (p. 2). Two obstacles organize the whole article. There is no immediate map from an arbitrary structure to its performance, and the space is far too large to enumerate (p. 2). QSPRs answer both: they map structures to properties, and they break molecules into countable substructures over which combinatorial optimization can run (pp. 2-3). The entry is pedagogical rather than a gap in a method. The article is meant to be "approachable for the absolute beginner" (abstract, p. 1), and unlike the earlier reviews [52, 126] it "first motivates the field from the ground up" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open a review by naming the obstacles that every method in the area must overcome (here, a structure-property map and a searchable space), then sort each method by how it handles them (pp. 2-3).
- **D2 Core idea (organization)**: CAMD is presented as the "reverse problem" of QSPR (p. 10) and written as one general program (18)-(25) (p. 11). The program has an objective C(n, p), QSPR equalities p = f(n), property and process constraints h1 and h2, structural-feasibility constraints s1 and s2, and bounds. Each problem class is a specialization of it:
  - single-molecule design, in three forms: the feasibility problem, an exact performance objective, and distance to property targets (26) (pp. 12-13);
  - mixture design, which adds the mixture properties q = g(x, n, p) and Σx = 1, (27)-(32) (p. 14);
  - integrated product/process design, which adds the process variables μ, (33)-(36) (p. 14).

  The structural-feasibility block is then written out for each QSPR family: for group contribution, the Odele-Macchietto constraints (40)-(42) and an aromatic extension (43)-(47) (pp. 16-17); for topological indices, the Camarda-Maranas adjacency formulation (48)-(56) (pp. 17-19); for signature descriptors, the Chemmangattuvalappil et al. constraints (57)-(60) (pp. 19-20). Solution techniques fall into four groups: generate-and-test, decomposition, mathematical optimization and heuristics (pp. 20-24). The application papers are tagged by representation and method in Table 3 (p. 27). The review adds no formulation of its own and no computational comparison.
  - Transferable: write one master formulation first, and present each problem class as an added block of variables and constraints, so readers see what each class adds to the model (pp. 11-14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - **Group contribution (GC).** Properties are additive in group counts, sometimes through a transformation f, (1)-(3) (pp. 3-4). The groups are fixed before the regression and often do not overlap (p. 3), and "there is no guarantee that the set of groups used best captures the property they model" (p. 5). Group sets that differ across properties become "problematic for mathematical formulations" (p. 5).
  - **Topological indices (TIs).** They are restricted to classes of chemicals, often lack a chemical interpretation, and have been demonstrated in CAMD only on small problems (pp. 7-8).
  - **Signature descriptors (SDs).** The coloring scheme is the only subjective choice (p. 8), and using every signature risks overfitting (p. 10).
  - **Optimization.** With a nonconvex objective or constraints, the optimal molecule found may be only a local optimum, and a global algorithm is needed to get the best molecule (p. 12). Even the exact-objective form guarantees optimality "at least as judged by the provided performance function" (p. 12).
  - **Feasibility rules.** The Odele-Macchietto valence balance (40) admits sets of groups that cannot be assembled, for example one >C< group with two -Br groups at m = 0; inequality (42) excludes them (p. 16). The aromatic extension assumes benzylic rings, with no biphenyls and no fused aromatics (p. 17).
  - **Decomposition.** The process-first targeting decomposition always yields a feasible value but "may not always find the globally optimal structure(s)" (p. 22).
  - **QSPR error.** Error in the QSPRs enters only as a reason to solve feasibility problems "when the performance function C is not accessible or reliable" and to re-rank the surviving candidates with higher-order models or experiments (pp. 12, 23). The body never discusses uncertainty or robust formulations; the words appear only in the titles of references [93], [168] and [171] (pp. 33, 37).
- **D4 Proof and analysis devices**: n/a for proofs; the review has no theorems. It reproduces the following analysis devices:
  - a counterexample showing that a counting rule is necessary but not sufficient: one >C< and two -Br satisfy (40) at m = 0, and (42) is added to exclude them (p. 16);
  - the handshaking lemma combined with |E| = |V| - 1 + |R|, which gives the linear degree constraint (57) on signature counts, plus coloring-sequence balance (58), parity (59) and a connectivity count (60) (pp. 19-20);
  - a worked numeric check of each constraint family on one concrete molecule (Figs. 11-13, pp. 17-21) and of each index (Figs. 2-4, pp. 4-8);
  - a process-only relaxation that gives a lower bound and ideal property targets, after which the nearest real molecule gives a feasible point (p. 22);
  - branch-and-bound over underestimators "(linear in many cases)" (pp. 22-23), and target matching turned into an MILP with deviation variables d+ - d- = pT - p, (61)-(62) (p. 23).
  - Transferable: when a counting rule is necessary but not sufficient, exhibit a small set it wrongly admits and add the inequality that cuts it off (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a; this is a review. Its only empirical content is Table 3, which tags 73 application papers (my count of the distinct references) by QSPR type (gc/ti/sd/sel), method (d/gt/o/h) and scope (m/p), with no performance numbers (p. 27).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Earlier reviews.** It builds on the CAMD reviews [52, 126] and differs from them by its ground-up exposition (p. 2).
  - **Credits.** Odele and Macchietto [127] are credited with introducing "a general optimization formulation" (p. 22). Duvedi and Achenie [38] and Churi and Achenie [30] are credited with outer approximation (OA) (p. 22). Sinha et al. [154] and Sahinidis et al. [146] are credited with branch-and-bound (pp. 22-23). Maranas [111] and Camarda and Maranas [20] are credited with linearization and convexification (p. 23).
  - **Judgement by guarantee.** Methods are judged by what they guarantee, not by computational results. OA is "not guaranteed to find globally optimal solutions if the problem is non-convex" (p. 22), while branch-and-bound "guarantees a globally optimal solution, at least to within a user-specified tolerance" (p. 23).
  - **Method choice.** The review ties method choice to the size of the space: generate-and-test suits few descriptors, and optimization suits large spaces or nonconvex models (pp. 20, 22). It concedes heuristics for problems "too high-dimensional or non-linear to be practically considered by mathematical optimization approaches" (p. 23), and describes them without numbers (pp. 23-24).
  - **Gentle critique.** Of the ionic-liquid case studies (one of them a tabu search shown to reach the global optimum), it notes that they "could thus be alternatively addressed via exhaustive enumeration" (p. 25).
  - **Self-citation.** The group's own papers appear among many others (pp. 23, 25-27). [146] is described as "an improved CAMD formulation" (p. 26). The authors also revise their own earlier usage: "we alter a definition given in [4]" for consistency with the literature (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - **Style.** The register is tutorial throughout. The article opens with history (pottery 20,000 years old, p. 1), and every concept gets a worked figure (Figs. 1-13). Each QSPR family gets a paired strengths/weaknesses paragraph (pp. 5, 7-8, 10). Asides beginning "we note" are frequent (pp. 3, 7, 14, 22), and the review flags its own coverage as "non-exhaustive" (p. 2).
  - **Classification legend.** The single-letter legend of Table 3 (p. 27) makes the classification scannable.
  - **Minor slips.** "affect" for "effect" (p. 3), "pharamacological" (p. 7), "from from" (p. 13), "Macchieto" (p. 22), "the the the" (p. 25). [68] and [91] are the same 1993 paper listed twice with the authors in a different order (pp. 32-33). As extracted, the label in Fig. 11 prints (Φ - 2) where both (43) and the figure's own table compute (2 - Φ) (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a pedagogical review and taxonomy: one formulation, explicit feasibility constraints for three QSPR families, and a map of the applications (pp. 11-20, 27). There is no new method and no result. The limits are written generically: the review is non-exhaustive (pp. 2, 7), it makes no quantitative comparison of methods, and QSPR error is handled only by re-ranking (p. 12). The future directions are generic as well: "integrating quantum chemistry techniques, designing transition metal catalysts, and determining optimal structures of pharmaceutical compounds" (p. 28), and the field "is poised to play an integral role" (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. In CAMD, the algebraic QSPR is what makes rigorous optimization possible: "there is an exact algebraic relationship between the descriptor space n and the performance function C" (p. 12). Integrated product/process design is hard "because there is no easily discernible algebraic relationship between the descriptor variables n and process variables" (p. 14). The group's mixture-design work keeps derivative-free optimization for a reduced property space (Austin et al. [4], p. 26; [3], p. 25). (pp. 2, 12, 14, 25-26)
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. CAMD decomposition is framed as step-wise reduction of the feasible set before costly constraints or models are applied, and it "works best if the number of feasible structures is reduced to a reasonably small number" first (pp. 20-21). Samudra and Sahinidis's three steps (optimal group sets, then structure generation, then higher-order models) follow the same order (p. 23). The reduction acts on the design space, not on node boxes, and the methods are mostly other people's, as the review frames them. (pp. 20-23, 25)
- Research Taste mark 3 (a certificate) ✅ evidence. The review labels OA's lack of a global guarantee on nonconvex problems and branch-and-bound's tolerance-level guarantee (pp. 22-23), and it states that only a global algorithm yields the best molecule (p. 12). This is not a numbered Method, so it is not in the digest's `method_links`.
- New-pattern candidate: **screen with the cheap model, re-rank the survivors with higher-order models**. Feasibility problems shrink the candidates to a pool that is then examined with higher-order models or experiments (p. 12). Optimization returns "the best molecule or a ranking of the top solutions" (Fig. 7, p. 13). Samudra and Sahinidis's step 3 applies "higher-order models to feasible structures" (p. 23).
- New-pattern candidate: **enumeration as the baseline**. A design space small enough to enumerate tests neither a heuristic nor an optimizer (pp. 20, 22, 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In an integer model that assembles a molecule (or any graph) from counted building blocks, impose both the valence-balance equation and the inequality that guarantees enough partner groups: Σ_d n_d ≥ n_d'(Φ_d' - 1) + 2 (42), or its aromatic and aliphatic forms (46)-(47). The balance alone admits unbuildable sets (pp. 16-17).
- For overlapping descriptors such as atomic signatures, derive linear feasibility constraints from the handshaking lemma and |E| = |V| - 1 + |R|, then add the coloring-sequence balance and parity conditions (57)-(59) (pp. 19-20).
- For integrated product/process design, take a bound and a feasible point in three solves. First optimize the process with the properties as free continuous variables, which gives a lower bound and ideal targets. Then find the nearest real molecule. Finally re-optimize the process with that molecule's properties. Report the result as feasible, not global (p. 22).
- Model property-target matching as an MILP with positive and negative deviation variables, d+ - d- = pT - p, minimizing Σ w(d+ + d-) (61)-(62) (p. 23).
- Before crediting a heuristic's case study, check whether its design space is small enough to enumerate (p. 25).
- In a review, tag every application paper by representation, method class and scope in one table with a letter legend, so that rare combinations are visible (Table 3, p. 27).

**Quotes**:
- "This smaller pool of compounds can then be investigated using high-order models or experiments." (p. 12)
- "These case studies used a small number of possible groups and could thus be alternatively addressed via exhaustive enumeration." (p. 25)

### S017 · The ALAMO approach to machine learning (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2017, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2017.02.010; arXiv:1705.10918)
**Read level**: full. The whole text, pp. 1-19, was read: Sections 1-7, formulations (1)-(14), and Tables 1-3, with Table 3 read from the run-on line on p. 19. The references (pp. 15-17) were skimmed. Figures 1-7 exist only as captions (pp. 6, 10, 12, 14, 18), so whatever they alone show was not read, for example the fit in Fig. 2 and the per-problem point counts in Fig. 4. The file is the arXiv accepted manuscript; p. 1 carries the CC-BY-NC-ND notice and the DOI.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Zachary T. Wilson (Department of Chemical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon University, first author; p. 1). The voice is joint. · **Place in trajectory**: The paper reviews and evaluates the ALAMO approach introduced in [17] (Cozad, Sahinidis and Miller 2014) and extended to combine data- and theory-driven building in [18] (2015) (p. 2). The work was performed under NETL's Regional University Alliance (project 1042568), as part of the Institute for the Design of Advanced Energy Systems (p. 14). Every subproblem is solved by BARON 15.6 (p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper starts from a mismatch between data-rich machine learning and process modeling. Deep models handle huge data sets, but they "can be poorly suited to tasks involving optimization or design" (p. 1). In chemical kinetics, by contrast, data are expensive, which "places a high premium on the identification of simple models that do not overfit training data". It also places a premium on first-principles information and on choosing new data points intelligently (p. 2). Those three needs become ALAMO's three components: best subset, adaptive sampling and constrained regression (§3, pp. 5-8). The stated purpose is "to review, evaluate, and illustrate the ALAMO methodology" and to contrast it with machine-learning techniques (p. 2). The problem is supervised regression with a parametric model that is linear in its coefficients over explicit nonlinear transformations of the inputs (pp. 2, 5).
  - Transferable: derive a method's components one for one from the constraints of the application regime, and say so in the introduction: expensive data call for sampling, small data for parsimony, known physics for constraints (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - **Best subset.** Best-subset selection over a large basis of explicit transformations is solved exactly as an integer program (p. 5). The basis contains monomial, binomial, ratio, exponential, logarithmic and trigonometric terms, plus sigmoid, Arrhenius or Gaussian terms whose inner parameter is fixed as a hyperparameter.
  - **Fitness metrics.** Any of six metrics (AICc, HQIC, MSE, Cp, BIC and RIC; (2)-(7), pp. 4-5) is minimized through a sweep of cardinality-constrained MIQPs (8). For a fixed cardinality r the complexity penalty C(r) is constant, so each subproblem minimizes the sum of squared residuals (SSR) with Σy_j ≤ r and big-M links (pp. 5-7). Cp, BIC and RIC also admit a single convex MIQP, (9) (pp. 5, 7).
  - **Against the lasso.** What changes: l0 replaces l1, there is no shrinkage, and the trade-off between fit and complexity is "informed from theory instead of exhaustively solved for using warm-starting" (p. 7). What is kept: linear least squares in the coefficients, and a big-M value M = Σ|β_ols| found "using logic borrowed from the lasso" (p. 7).
  - **Error-maximization sampling (EMS).** A derivative-free solver maximizes the squared relative error between the true response and the surrogate, (10). New points are added until the maximum observed error is below δ (pp. 5, 7-8).
  - **Constrained regression.** Constraints restrict the model's response, not its coefficients, over a domain χ, (12)-(13). That turns the fit into a semi-infinite program whose inner maximum must be found by global optimization (p. 8).
  - Transferable: when a model-selection criterion is a nonlinear function of (SSR, number of terms), sweep the number of terms and solve one cardinality-constrained MIQP per value. Criteria that are additive in the count fit in a single MIQP (pp. 5-7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - **Model form.** The model is linear in its parameters over a fixed basis, and nonlinear inner parameters must be fixed (p. 5).
  - **Criteria.** The information criteria assume normally distributed residuals with zero mean, and σ̂² comes from the full k-term OLS model (pp. 4-5). Only one response is treated, "In the interest of simplifying the presentation" (p. 5).
  - **Big-M.** The value M = Σ_j|β̂_j,ols| is justified only by lasso logic (p. 7). Whether it bounds the coefficients of every subset model is not argued.
  - **EMS.** EMS treats the true response as a black box, hence the derivative-free search (pp. 7-8). Its objective divides by z(x) (p. 7), and the behaviour near z = 0 is not discussed.
  - **Constraint subproblem.** The maximization max_x f[x, ẑ(x; β)] is "in general nonlinear and nonconvex" and requires global optimization (p. 8).
  - **Test data.** One input, t ∈ [0.6, 10], noise-free closed-form responses, and fixed initial concentrations (p. 9). No noise model appears anywhere in the paper.
- **D4 Proof and analysis devices**: There are no theorems. The paper uses these devices:
  - all six fitness metrics reduce to one parametric family of cardinality-constrained MIQPs, because the penalty C(r) is constant at fixed r (pp. 5-7);
  - the metrics (5)-(7) are convex quadratic in the integer formulation, so a single MIQP (9) suffices, and it "considers all 2k possible regression models" (p. 7; the exponent is lost in extraction);
  - the semi-infinite equivalence: β ∈ Ω(χ) holds "if and only if" max_x f ≤ 0, so a bound certificate becomes one global maximization (p. 8);
  - the points EMS adds also serve as a "conservative estimate on the true value of (10)", i.e. a lower bound on the maximum error (p. 8);
  - the evaluation metrics: EF_m = RMSE_val,m / RMSE_best (14), and R²_val computed against the training mean, which can fall below 0 but not above 1 (p. 10);
  - a heuristic first phase that looks for an initial linear model, with some problems ending there if that model can be proven optimal; it is described in two sentences only (p. 10).
  - Transferable: certify a property of a fitted model over a whole domain, such as bounds, monotonicity or convexity via derivatives, by solving one global maximization of its worst violation (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - **Library.** Two batch-reactor schemes are used: in series (A→B→C) and in parallel (A→B, A→C). Five values each of ka ∈ [0.4, 3] and kb ∈ [0.9, 2.1] are paired into 25 systems, with three species each. That gives 150 problems with one input, t ∈ [0.6, 10] (pp. 2, 9; the 2 × 25 × 3 count is my arithmetic).
  - **Setup.** The basis has 13 features: t^±{0.5,1,2,3,4}, log t, exp t and 1 (p. 9). Every run minimizes BIC with (9), all subproblems are solved by BARON 15.6, and the machine is an Intel Core2 Quad Q9550 (pp. 10-11). No ALAMO version and no CPU times are reported. Validation uses 1000 random points, EF in a Dolan-Moré performance profile, and tables of R²_val and RMSE_val (pp. 10-12).
  - **EMS results.** Started from one random point with δ = 0.0001, EMS needed 14-46 points, mean 21.7 and median 21 (Table 1, p. 11). It beat a Latin hypercube (LHS) design of the same size in 120 of 150 problems. It was best overall in 45 problems against 83 for LHS-50, and it beat LHS-30 in 103 (p. 11). Its mean R²_val was 0.994 (minimum 0.906), against 0.988 (minimum 0.547) for same-size LHS; LHS-40 and LHS-50 reach R²_val = 1 (Table 2, p. 12).
  - **Constrained-regression results.** Training used a 25-point LHS, with bounds 0 ≤ [·] ≤ [A]0 and certification extended to t ∈ [0.4, 14] (p. 13). Best counts for UC/CR/ECR were 103/20/29 in the training domain, 59/57/36 above it and 9/29/117 below it (pp. 13-14). Mean RMSE_val for UC/CR/ECR was 1.1E-03/3.0E-03/3.1E-02 in the training domain, 4.4E-03/7.6E-03/4.1E-02 above it, and 1.95/1.98/0.27 below it. ECR's mean R²_val below the training domain is -5.2 (Table 3, p. 19).
  - **Baselines.** The body reports the losses, but no external method is run: no lasso, no neural network, no other surrogate tool. As the paper puts it, "all sampling schemes compared in this section are implemented in ALAMO" (p. 13). Model sizes are never reported for the 150 problems.
  - Transferable: compare an adaptive design with a space-filling one at the same sample count (n_EMS) and against fixed larger budgets (LHS-20 to LHS-50), and label the unequal comparisons as unequal (pp. 11, 13).
  - Transferable: build the test library from a parametric family of physical models with closed-form solutions. Ground truth is then exact and validation points cost nothing (1000 per problem) (pp. 9-10).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Where ALAMO sits.** The paper places ALAMO within subset selection: OLS, best subset, the lasso, ridge and the elastic net (pp. 2-4). It sets aside exhaustive search, whose popular R implementation "is limited to only 32 regressors" (p. 4), and stepwise methods, which "offer no guarantee of optimality" (p. 4). Regularization "is not equivalent to the problem of best subset selection" (p. 4).
  - **Superiority claim.** The claim that MIP subset selection gives models that are "smaller, and more accurate" than the lasso rests on the group's own [17] (p. 4), not on runs in this paper.
  - **Concurrent work.** Concurrent MIP subset-selection work (Bertsimas and King, Bertsimas and Shioda, Konno and Yamamoto, Miyashiro and Takano, Park and Klabjan) is cited but not compared (p. 3; refs pp. 15-17).
  - **Own benchmark used.** The derivative-free solver for EMS is chosen from the group's own comparative study [57] (Rios and Sahinidis 2013): SNOBFIT "is currently a highly effective DFO solver for minimizing problems such as 10" (p. 8).
  - **Deep learning.** The paragraph on deep learning, which describes it as techniques that typically address unsupervised problems (pp. 1-2), is background and plays no role later.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - **Structure.** Review, then method, then one illustrative example, then experiments and a short conclusion. The experiments section opens with the three claims it will demonstrate (p. 9).
  - **Caveats in the body.** "At face value, this might seem like an unfair comparison" (p. 11), and the EMS and LHS results "are not on even footing" (p. 13).
  - **Summary sentences stronger than the tables.** The abstract says constrained regression gives models that "perform better on validation data" (p. 1), and the conclusion says EMS "outperforms space-filling models with larger training sets" (p. 14). Yet UC wins both the training domain and the upper extension (pp. 13-14, 19). LHS-50 beats EMS, 83 against 45 best models (p. 11), and LHS-30 and LHS-40 have a higher mean R²_val than EMS (Table 2, p. 12). The text reads the upper-extension results as demonstrating "the utility of both constrained regression approaches" (p. 13), although UC has the most wins and the lowest mean RMSE there.
  - **Count and copy slips.** The per-domain best counts sum to 152, 152 and 155 rather than 150, and ECR's share is "the remaining 36" where 150 - 59 - 57 = 34 (pp. 13-14). EMS used fewer than 30 points in "147 of 150 problems" (p. 11), but p. 13 says more than 30 samples were needed in five problems. In Table 2, the LHS-40 row gives a mean RMSE (0.002) above its maximum (0.001) (p. 12).
  - **Misprinted model.** The printed A model equals B_ALAMO + C_ALAMO term by term (my check on p. 9). It is therefore a model of B + C = 1 - A, and it vanishes at t = 0 where A = 1; the text does not notice.
  - **Typos.** "leaning algebraic functions" (pp. 1, 14), "refereed" (p. 3), "out preformed" and "space-filling EMS methodologies" (p. 13).

- New-pattern candidate: **closed-form ground-truth test family**. Generate the test library from a parametric physical model whose solution is known in closed form, here 2 schemes × 25 rate-constant pairs × 3 species, and validate on 1000 random points per problem (pp. 8-10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To minimize an information criterion that is nonlinear in the fit and the number of terms (AICc, HQIC, MSE), solve min SSR s.t. Σy_j ≤ r with -My_j ≤ β_j ≤ My_j for r = 1, ..., p and take the best criterion value. Criteria linear in the count (Cp, BIC, RIC) go into one convex MIQP (pp. 5-7).
- Choose the next experiment by maximizing the current surrogate's squared relative error with a derivative-free solver. Stop when the largest error found is below δ, and treat the points found as a lower bound on the true maximum error (pp. 5, 7-8).
- Constrain a regression model's output, rather than its coefficients, over a domain, including outside the training range: bounds, monotonicity or convexity via derivatives. Treat this as a semi-infinite constraint, and certify each candidate by globally maximizing its worst violation (p. 8).
- Report a sampling method against a space-filling design at the same sample count and against fixed larger budgets, and say explicitly which comparisons are unequal (pp. 11, 13).
- Score each method by EF = RMSE_val / RMSE_best in a performance profile, and report R²_val computed against the training mean, which exposes models worse than the mean (p. 10).
- Make the test library a parametric family of closed-form physical systems (rate constants × schemes × outputs), so ground truth and validation are exact and free (p. 9).
