### S084 · Tuning BARON using derivative-free optimization algorithms (Journal of Global Optimization 2019, doi:10.1007/s10898-018-0640-3)

**Read level**: full. All 24 pages of the author preprint (abstract, §§1-6, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jianfeng Liu, Nikolaos Ploskas (both CMU Chemical Engineering); Sahinidis is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: CMU DFO line. It carries the 2013 Rios-Sahinidis comparison ([32]) from algebraic test problems to a real black box (pp. 2, 6) and runs on BARON 15.5 (p. 7). 04-mentorship lists an MS thesis with the same title (2012), which fits Heuristic 9; the paper itself does not mention a thesis.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: developers set defaults "based on limited computational experience", and defaults may do poorly on specific instances (p. 1). All earlier tuning work (MADS by Audet and Orban, STOP, ParamILS, random sampling for IPOPT) addressed MILP and local NLP solvers, never a global solver (p. 2). A second gap: the large DFO comparison of 2013 was "not performed on true black-box systems" (p. 6; also p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: tuning is min f(C, P) over the box of option values, with f = BARON's CPU time, treated as a nonsmooth black box (pp. 5-6). Eleven of BARON's more than 50 options were chosen after a pilot study: 8 discrete and 3 continuous, covering the LP warm-start basis, branching point and branching variable, outer approximators and cut rounds, probing count, frequency and distance, and node passes (pp. 3-5, Table 1). More than 3 million discrete combinations rule out enumeration (p. 5). Each of 27 DFO codes (Table 2, p. 7) gets 1,000 evaluations, with integer options rounded (p. 7). Two tasks: one configuration per instance, and one for the whole set, with each instance's time divided by its default time before summing (pp. 8, 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: only CPU time is tuned, because BARON finds the same solution whenever it solves within the limit (p. 8). The 126 GLOBALLib and MINLPLib problems all solve within 20 s at defaults (p. 7). Runtime changes within ±10% count as noise, and a result within 5% of the best counts as best (pp. 13-14). Machine: two Xeon E5-2660 v3, 128 GB, CentOS 7 (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a (no proofs). Measurement devices: a runtime-reduction score rr ∈ [−1, 1], positive when the tuned options are faster (p. 8; as printed in this preprint the two case conditions are swapped, since the stated range holds only with them exchanged); a minimal subset of tuners that covers every best result (p. 14); an effort-quality plane of average evaluations against average reduction (Fig. 2, pp. 16-17), redrawn for the problems solved within 1, 2, 5 and 10 s (pp. 17-19).
- **D5 Experiments**: per-instance tuning cuts CPU time by 57% on average, and 125 of 126 problems improve by more than 10% (p. 8); for nvs20 all four best configurations switch node probing off (PDo = 0, Table 4, p. 11). One setting for the whole set saves 34% (SNOBFIT); on all 580 library problems the TOMLAB/LGO setting saves 5% and solves 12 more problems within 500 s (p. 11). Global tuners average 49% against 30% for local ones, at 937 against 325 evaluations (pp. 20-21), and most tuners have at least one failure worse than −10% (p. 13).
- **D6 Positioning**: re-checks the 2013 ranking on the new black box. It is mostly consistent, but DAKOTA/EA and GLOBAL do much better here and FMINSEARCH much worse (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing**: the conclusion grades its findings by surprise: per-instance gains are "not surprising", the 57% average is "Somewhat surprising", and the 34% from one setting for a whole library "Even more surprising" (p. 23).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: an empirical study; no new algorithm. Written limits: tuning time (up to several hours) is often not recovered by the savings (a few seconds per instance), so tuning a small number of problems is not recommended, except for instances representative of many later solves (p. 9); tuning and evaluation use the same 126 problems (p. 11). Not written: the 20 s filter keeps hard instances out of the study (p. 7). Future work: tune other global solvers the same way (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: 27 codes that "cover most state-of-the-art DFO algorithms" (p. 6) on a public library under one evaluation budget (p. 7), failures counted per solver (Table 7, p. 14), and a portfolio verdict: "no single DFO solver dominated", so a minimal covering subset is reported (p. 14). The 2013 ranking is re-tested on a real black box and the disagreements are named (p. 15).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the tuning problem has no certified optimum, so "best" means best found, possibly a local solution (p. 13). There is no separate hold-out; the only wider check runs on the 580 problems, which contain the 126 tuning problems (p. 11).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: BARON has so many options because the project is meant as an experimentation platform for its developers (p. 3), and its defaults come from "years of extensive computational experimentation with a large set of benchmark instances" (p. 5). The gap between the per-instance best (57%) and one setting for all (34%) measures what a single default leaves on the table (pp. 8, 11), the comparison that Heuristic 5 later makes against a perfect-selection ceiling.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (weak): probing is rationed by two options because range-reduction subproblems "can be expensive if not done in moderation" (p. 4); on nvs20 the best settings switch it off (p. 11).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (weak): tree size does not track time. SNOBFIT's setting explores about as many nodes as the default and CMA-ES's about three times fewer, yet both save about 30% (pp. 11-12). CPU time is the yardstick.
- new-pattern candidate: *own solver as a true black box for benchmarking DFO codes* (pp. 2, 6).
- new-pattern candidate: *a gain counts net of the cost of finding it*: tuning effort is set against the time it saves, and tuners are compared by evaluations spent as well as by reduction (pp. 9, 15-17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Benchmark black-box optimizers on the option tuning of a solver you control: the inputs are option values, the output is CPU time and the default setting is the baseline (pp. 2, 5-6).
- Declare a noise band before comparing runtimes (here ±10%) and a tie band for "best" (here 5%) (pp. 13-14).
- When tuning one setting for a set, divide each instance's time by its default time before summing, so that a few long instances do not decide (p. 11).
- Report the tuning cost next to the savings and name the case where tuning pays: instances representative of many later solves (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "A primary objective of the BARON project is to provide its developers with a platform for experimentation with various branch-and-bound algorithms." (p. 3)
- "Finally, it is worth noting that runtime reduction due to optimized configurations is oftentimes insufficient to recover the computational effort required for solving the corresponding tuning problems." (p. 9)

### S104 · Automated learning of chemical reaction networks (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2019, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2019.05.020)

**Read level**: full. All 35 pages of the accepted-manuscript preprint (abstract, §§1-6, acknowledgments, disclaimer, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Zachary T. Wilson (first author; both CMU Chemical Engineering); Sahinidis is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: CMU, funded through IDAES by the DOE Office of Fossil Energy (p. 29). It carries the ALAMO line (refs [19]-[21]: Cozad et al. 2014 and 2015, Wilson and Sahinidis 2017, p. 31) from algebraic surrogates to kinetic networks, with BARON solving the MINLPs (pp. 20, 26).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: kinetic models need expensive data. Expert design, or exhaustive search over candidate reactions, becomes prohibitive as species and reactions multiply (p. 2). Factorial designs scale badly with input dimension and cannot use model knowledge (p. 2). Fisher-information MINLP designs suffer from the heavy correlation between rate coefficient and activation energy; the paper sidesteps it by maximizing a black-box model-disagreement objective (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: four features (p. 4): error-maximization sampling (EMS) as adaptive design; an MINLP that selects reactions and estimates parameters over alternative stoichiometries and mechanisms; piecewise ALAMO to estimate derivatives from dynamic data; Arrhenius and non-Arrhenius kinetics. The MINLP (7) has one binary per stoichiometry-mechanism pair, big-M links to k0 and E, at most one mechanism per stoichiometry and a cardinality bound K (pp. 7-8). Logic cuts forbid sets of linearly dependent reactions and admit a higher-order reaction only when its lower-order alternatives are absent (pp. 8-9). RIPE (Algorithm 2) grows K from the empty network and stops when BIC rises (pp. 14-15). EMS (Algorithm 3) refits, maximizes the scaled squared error between system and model with RBFOpt, and adds the point, until the error is below δ (pp. 14-17). Kept from ALAMO: best subset under an information criterion. Changed: the basis is a physical superset of reactions and the fit is nonlinear (Arrhenius), hence an MINLP (pp. 5, 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: rate laws have explicit algebraic forms (p. 5); Gaussian noise with species-specific variance (p. 6); the candidate set contains the true reactions (stated for case 1, p. 18); adsorption equilibrium constants are known in advance (p. 10). Big-M bounds come from scaling local regression fits of single sub-models (p. 8). That the BIC loop stops at a global optimum is asserted from the "convex nature" of BIC, not proved (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a (no theorems). Devices: the two logic-cut families, e.g. y1 + y2 + y3 ≤ 2 and y1 + y2 + 2y3 ≤ 2 (p. 9); the BIC stopping rule (pp. 14-15); recursive splitting of the time domain for derivatives, with n_min = 5 and δ set to R² = 0.95 (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: simulated CSTR with 12 candidate stoichiometries, 3 true, signal-to-noise about 10: the right network by iteration 4, tolerance met at iteration 6 with seven data points, each MINLP solved in seconds by BARON (pp. 18-20). Literature chemical-looping data: piecewise ALAMO changes the selected NiO/Al2O3 oxidation model and raises its fit (pp. 23-24). Expanded case with 35 candidate reactions: the true network from 16 points, minutes per MINLP (p. 26); error 5.98% against 3.92% for the true model, while six LHS points and ten random designs never find the right network (errors of 1.2×10³% and, on average, 1.8×10³%) (p. 27).
- **D6 Positioning**: against factorial and Fisher-information designs ([7]-[10]) and MINLP model-reduction work ([12]-[17]); Tsay et al. is named as the closest (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a methodology shown on three cases (pp. 28-29). Written limits: BIC-sized models need not satisfy species mass balances (p. 20); batch reactors in time only (p. 14); flow reactors need measured profiles (p. 29); the sampling objective is accuracy only, though costs could be embedded (pp. 16-17); the chemical-looping study is not compared with published mechanisms (p. 23). Future work: a confidence-level stopping rule (pp. 20-21) and more logic constraints (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a black-box reactor made algebraic and small. Best-subset selection by MINLP over a physics-built superset, BIC sizing, adaptive error-maximization sampling, and derivative-free search only for the black-box system itself (pp. 4, 7-9, 14-17). Equal-size LHS and random designs fail where EMS succeeds (p. 27), the same comparison design as the 2017 sampling study cited in SKILL.md (Signature Work Anatomy).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: first-principles consistency is not imposed on the fitted model (unlike step 4). RIPE may return models that violate mass balances, and the authors decline the extra logical constraints (p. 20). Physics enters only through the candidate set, built to satisfy elemental balances (p. 18), and through logic cuts (pp. 8-9, 11-12).
- new-pattern candidate: *logic cuts on selection binaries encode domain rules*: no linearly dependent sets, the simpler reaction first, side reactions only with their main reaction, no conflicting mechanisms together (pp. 8-9, 11-12, 28-29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write model discrimination as a superstructure: one binary per alternative mechanism of each stoichiometry, big-M links to its parameters, at most one mechanism per stoichiometry and a cardinality bound. Sweep the cardinality upward and stop at the first BIC increase (pp. 7-9, 14-15).
- Prefer the simpler of dependent alternatives with a weighted logic cut: y1 + y2 + 2y3 ≤ 2 admits the third-order reaction only if neither second-order one is chosen (p. 9).
- Get big-M bounds by scaling local least-squares fits of each sub-model (p. 8).
- Validate adaptive sampling on a case with a known true model against an equal-size LHS design and several random designs, and give the true model's own error as the floor (p. 27).

**Quotes**:
- "The fundamental advantage of a MINLP approach comes in the systematic consideration of many alternative models." (p. 5)
- "The randomly selected data does not succeed in selecting the correct model in any case." (p. 27)

### S130 · Evaluating and ranking patents with multiple criteria: How many criteria are required to find the most promising patents? (Computers & Chemical Engineering 2019, doi:10.1016/j.compchemeng.2019.01.011)

**Read level**: full. All 42 pages of the author preprint (abstract, §§1-6, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nikolaos Ploskas, Tong Zhang (CMU Chemical Engineering), Flor Castillo, Krishnan Sankaranarayanan (SABIC Americas); Sahinidis is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a CMU decision-support project with an industrial co-author team (p. 1). Ploskas also leads S214 and co-authors S084.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: citation analysis misses recently issued patents (pp. 2, 8). Every earlier multicriteria patent method needs expert estimates and "cannot be used for automatically ranking hundreds or thousands of patents" (p. 9). Expert weighting is subjective, biased by background, not repeatable across meetings and hard to automate (p. 12).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: eight criteria from Quid's semantic patent network, in three groups: connections (degree, betweenness, inter-cluster fraction), neighbours (flow, pagerank, triangles) and citations (forward, backward) (pp. 9-11). The weights come from a goal-programming LP. Group-level and within-group orderings, a 0.25 cap on citation weights (so that recent patents can rank high), bounds of [0.05, 0.33] on each weight and a sum of 1 (hard) are encoded as constraints; the 25 soft constraints get deviation variables whose sum is minimized, and strict inequalities use a 0.01 threshold (pp. 13-17). CPLEX 12.6.3 finds zero deviation and alternative optima A and B (pp. 17-18). Patents are ranked by TOPSIS (distances to ideal and anti-ideal after vector normalization) instead of a weighted sum (pp. 17-20). A web decision-support system wraps the method (p. 20).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: the constraints are "subjective" but "intuitive", and decision makers may change them (p. 17); the analyst removes irrelevant patents by hand before ranking (p. 20); a weighted sum is rejected because it assumes independent criteria and lets one large-valued criterion dominate (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a (no proofs). Devices: deviation-variable goal programming (pp. 15-17); paired weight perturbations at constant sum (pp. 27-29); exhaustive enumeration of criteria subsets of sizes 1-7, reporting maximum, minimum and average overlap with the full set (pp. 34-36).
- **D5 Experiments**: top-five lists for ammonia (166 patents), olefins (102) and polyethylene (116) under weights A, B and equal weights C, judged by domain reading (pp. 23-26). A weighted sum gives poor lists because betweenness dominates, over 95% of the sum in one case (pp. 32-33). On 22 capstone design projects, fewer than five criteria miss at least 15% of the top five and 25% of the top ten, and five criteria find 95% (pp. 34-37); 0.6 s against 0.5 s per run (p. 35).
- **D6 Positioning**: against citation-based ranking and expert-weighted multicriteria methods (Barbazza et al., Collan et al., Lawryshyn et al., Wang and Hsieh), with a table of the metrics used before (pp. 3-8). It claims to be the first multicriteria patent method that needs no decision-maker estimates (pp. 3, 9).
- **D7 Writing**: the title asks how many criteria are needed, and §5.6 answers with a number, five (pp. 1, 35).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a method and a software tool (p. 20). Written limit: the constraints are subjective (p. 17). Not written: the criteria-count study takes the eight-criteria ranking with equal weights as its reference (p. 34), so it measures agreement with the proposed method, not ranking quality, although §5.3 says equal weights "might mislead the decision makers" (p. 26). The conclusions name "direct propylene oxidation" as the third case while the body studies polyethylene (pp. 25-26, 37). The printed LP writes the stated rule wB ≤ wI as wB − wI + s6 ≥ 0 (pp. 14, 16). Future work: customized constraints (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the criteria-count study is exhaustive and reports the spread (pp. 34-36), but its yardstick is the paper's own ranking, not ground truth (p. 34), and the case studies are judged by domain plausibility (pp. 24-26).
- new-pattern candidate: *ordinal-constraint LP instead of expert weights*: the beliefs become LP constraints with deviation variables; zero total deviation shows they are jointly consistent, and the alternative optima show what they leave open (pp. 12-18). The method is argued to rest on constraints "easier to argue and negotiate between experts" (p. 3). Taste marks 1 and 2 fit: a web tool with default weights that others can run without experts (p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When weights must be set for a multicriteria ranking, encode the experts' qualitative beliefs as ordinal LP constraints with deviation variables and minimize total deviation; then rank with more than one optimal weight vector to see how much the beliefs leave open (pp. 13-18, 23-26).
- Before adopting a normalized distance method, show on the same data how a weighted sum fails, and name the criterion that dominates and its share of the sum (pp. 32-34).
- Size a criteria set by enumerating all subsets of each size and reporting the maximum, minimum and average overlap of the top-k lists (pp. 34-36).

**Quotes**:
- "Such a process is subjective and likely introduces biases based on experts backgrounds." (p. 12)
- "While subjective, the above constraints and goals are intuitive." (p. 17)

### S214 · Heat exchanger circuitry design by decision diagrams (International Conference on Integration of Constraint Programming 2019, doi:10.1007/978-3-030-19212-9_30)

**Read level**: full, of the MERL TR version: technical report TR2019-038 (28 June 2019), all 12 file pages (MERL cover, a blank page, then the paper's pp. 1-10 with references). The Springer LNCS chapter itself was not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nikolaos Ploskas (University of Western Macedonia), Christopher Laughman and Arvind U. Raghunathan (Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories); Sahinidis is last author, at CMU (TR p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: a CMU-MERL line. It extends the authors' 2018 paper that optimized circuitries with DFO ([1], TR p. 11; index S074, not read here).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: heat pumps have 60-200 tubes, and engineers pick circuitries by expert knowledge plus an enumerated set of simulations, with no guarantee of optimality (TR p. 4). The space is enormous and performance depends on the circuitry discontinuously and nonlinearly (TR p. 4). The authors' earlier binary formulation, solved by DFO, stopped at 36 tubes: a dense, memory-bound constraint matrix with cycle-exclusion inequalities (TR pp. 4, 6, 8).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: each pre-bent tube pair becomes a super-node. A circuitry is a set of super-node-disjoint paths that cover all super-nodes, represented by a relaxed decision diagram with n/2 layers and a 0-node that marks the end of a circuit (TR pp. 6-7). The MIP keeps flow balance at super-nodes and 0-nodes, enters each super-node exactly once (which rules out repeats, hence cycles) and bounds the number of circuits (TR pp. 7-8). It has |S|³ − |S|² + 3|S| − 1 variables and |S|² + 2|S| + 2 constraints (TR p. 8). The objective (heat capacity Q, or Q/ΔP) exists only in the CoilDesigner simulator. So the objective is set to a constant, CPLEX's solution pool produces 2,500 diverse feasible circuitries, and each is simulated (TR p. 9). Kept from [1]: the simulator and the manufacturing rules. Changed: a compact, sparse model of the feasible set, sampled instead of searched by DFO.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: geometry and inlet conditions are fixed (TR p. 5). Five manufacturing rules: adjacent tubes in each column are pre-connected, far-end connections cross rows only at the coil edge, no plugged tubes, inlets and outlets at the near end, no merges or splits (TR p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a (no proofs). Formulation-size comparison: at 40 tubes, 1,048,595 × 780 against 7,659 × 442, about 6 GB against 300 KB; at 16 tubes the new model is the larger one (−22%) (Table 1, TR p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**: seven coils of 4-128 tubes with identical structure and conditions; the baselines are the earlier approach [1] (up to 36 tubes) and the two-circuit design "typically used in practice today" (TR p. 9). On average Q rises 9% over the baseline and 4% over [1], and Q/ΔP rises 8,826% and 90% (TR p. 9). The Q gain shrinks from 26% at 4 tubes to 1% at 128 (Table 2, TR p. 10). The method is said to need "an order of magnitude less execution time" than [1], but no times are tabulated (TR p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning**: imports decision diagrams from the CP and OR literature ([18]-[31]), where such formulations have "vastly outperformed existing formulations" (TR p. 4).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a formulation that reaches 128 tubes (TR p. 4). Written limits: the 2,500-evaluation budget caps the results on large coils (TR p. 10); truncating the diagram for pressure drop and deleting forbidden arcs are left to future work (TR pp. 8-9). Not written: sampling gives no bound on the distance to the optimum, and the same two-circuit baseline serves both objectives, so the Q/ΔP gains of thousands of percent are measured against a design of two long circuits, while the best Q/ΔP designs use many short ones (TR pp. 9-10).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the simulator stays a black box. The algebra captures only the feasible set (a compact MIP of the manufacturable circuitries), and the black box is searched by scoring a diverse pool of MIP-feasible designs instead of by a DFO code (TR pp. 8-10). Problem knowledge sits in the model as constraints, as Heuristic 2 asks (TR p. 6).
- new-pattern candidate: *solution-pool sampling*: a constant objective plus a MIP solver's solution pool enumerates diverse feasible designs for a simulator to score (TR p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the objective exists only in a simulator but the feasible set is combinatorial and algebraic, give the MIP a constant objective and use the solver's solution pool (CPLEX PopulateLim, SolnPoolCapacity, SolnPoolReplace) to generate thousands of diverse feasible designs, then simulate each (TR p. 9).
- Model circuits or routes as flows through a layered decision diagram with an end-of-path node, and forbid repeats with one cover constraint per node instead of cycle-elimination inequalities, which keeps the matrix sparse (TR pp. 6-8).
- Report formulation sizes against the old formulation across instance sizes, including the size at which the new one is larger (TR p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Current design processes typically involve the manual choice of the configuration based upon expert knowledge and the results of an enumerated set of simulations." (TR p. 4)
- "We replaced the objective in (1) by a constant and applied the mixed integer programming solver CPLEX on (2)-(6) to produce 2, 500 feasible circuitry configurations." (TR p. 9)

### S318 · Announcement: Howard Rosenbrock Prize 2018 (Optimization and Engineering 2019, doi:10.1007/s11081-019-09458-x)

**Read level**: full (both pages).
**Role**: supplement; editorial announcement with a sole author line. Two voices: the announcement is his, as editor-in-chief; the prize citation (pp. 1-2) was produced by the selection committee, Makoto Ohsaki (chair), Marina Epelman and Luis Zuluaga (p. 1) · **Coauthors**: none · **Position in trajectory**: OPTE editor-in-chief (2018-2023 per 01-publications). The index holds the rest of the yearly series (H011, S304, H012, S297, S294, S289); none of them was read here.

- **D1-D5**: n/a for him (no research content).
- **What the item shows**: the prize honours the best paper in OPTE of the previous year, carries $500 sponsored by Springer, and the winners for 2014-2017 are named (p. 1). The 2018 prize goes to Hoeltgen et al. 2018 on sparse ℓ1 regularisation of matrix-valued models for acoustic source characterisation (pp. 1-2). ### S034 · Or-gym: A reinforcement learning library for operations research problems (arXiv preprint 2020, arXiv:2008.06319)

**Read level**: full for the body (arXiv v2, pp. 1-23) and Appendix A (p. 29); the reference list (pp. 24-28) was not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Christian D. Hubbs, Hector D. Perez, Owais Sarwar, Ignacio E. Grossmann (CMU Chemical Engineering), John M. Wassick (Dow Chemical); Sahinidis is fourth of six (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: CMU 2020, inside the group's deep-RL line, which 06-trajectory classes as a short visit with no journal version of this paper. The environments are open source (github.com/hubbs5/or-gym, p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: RL beats top human players in games but has few industrial or commercial uses (p. 1). Standard RL suites lack the structure and the industrial relevance of classic optimization problems (p. 2), and the MDP framing is unfamiliar to the optimization community (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: a library of OR environments with the OpenAI Gym interface: binary, bounded and online knapsack; virtual-machine packing on Azure data; multi-echelon inventory with backlog or with lost sales; multi-period asset allocation; plus the environments of Balaji et al. (pp. 2, 8). One algorithm for everything, PPO from Ray with 3 × 128 actor and critic networks, with tuning kept minimal "to reduce over fitting our results" (p. 4). Every environment is benchmarked against exact models and heuristics: MILPs (Gurobi 8.2 via Pyomo), shrinking-horizon MILP or LP, a perfect-information oracle LP, Powell-method DFO and a MIP for base-stock levels, and robust optimization solved with BARON 19.7.13 (pp. 3, 6, 9, 15-17, 21).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: MDP state, action and reward per problem (pp. 5-6, 9, 14-15, 20-21). Infeasible actions either end the episode with a penalty or are masked (pp. 8-9). Inventory episodes last 30 days (p. 15). Asset prices are Gaussian, and the reward comes only at the end, chosen to show RL in a hard setting (pp. 20-21).
- **D4 Analysis devices (brief)**: n/a (no proofs). Devices: a performance ratio against the MILP optimum or the oracle (Tables 1, 2, 4; pp. 7, 10, 18); a big-M reformulation of the min and max operators so that a MILP finds optimal static base-stock levels over a sample path (p. 16); action masking as a hard constraint (pp. 9, 23).
- **D5 Experiments**: offline knapsack: RL trails the greedy heuristic and the MILP (ratios 1.32 and 1.11), while online it beats the heuristic (1.8 against 2.02) (p. 7). VM packing: masked RL is about 9% better than First Fit and 16% worse than the shrinking-horizon MILP; unmasked RL reaches 2.37 (p. 10). Inventory: RL at 1.2 and 1.3 against the oracle beats the static DFO and MIP base-stock policies (1.5, 1.4) and loses to the shrinking-horizon LP (1.1) (p. 18). Asset allocation: RL's mean exceeds the robust policy's (about $865), with a wider, skewed spread; the robust model solves in minutes, RL trains for hours (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: builds on and absorbs the Balaji et al. benchmarks (p. 2); picks robust optimization over multistage stochastic programming on Ben-Tal et al.'s arguments (p. 21).
- **D7 Writing**: addressed to two communities, RL and OR (pp. 1-2); verdicts are given per problem class, with conditions (pp. 7, 23).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a library plus benchmarks. Written limits: RL is not worth it for offline knapsack (pp. 7, 23); with a 30-day episode, RL drains inventory at the end of the horizon (p. 18); training cost (p. 22). Future work: rules extracted from RL policies, hybrids of RL and mathematical programming, graph neural networks (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak; fourth of six authors): a new method class is entered by benchmarking it against exact optima and a perfect-information oracle, with heuristics as further baselines and losses reported class by class (pp. 7, 10, 17-18, 22-23). The oracle LP that knows the realized demand is the ceiling of Heuristic 5 (p. 17).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: one generated instance family per environment, averages over 100 episodes, no public library and no hold-out (pp. 7, 10, 18).
- The verdict on offline knapsack, that RL's extra complexity and cost "may not be worthwhile" (p. 23), matches Taste warning sign 2 (a complex model where a simple one would do).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Judge a learned policy by its ratio to the exact optimum and to a perfect-information oracle, problem class by problem class, and report where it loses (pp. 7, 10, 17-18).
- Use one architecture and minimal hyperparameter tuning across all environments, so that per-problem tuning does not flatter the method (p. 4).
- Enforce hard constraints by masking infeasible actions, and report the unmasked run that learns to end episodes early (pp. 9-11).
- Get optimal static base-stock levels over a sample path by writing the min and max operators of the inventory recursion with big-M binaries (p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "For simple, off-line cases it seems best to resort to heuristics or optimal solutions rather than RL." (p. 7)
- "Both approaches are compared to an oracle model, which is a LP model that determines the optimal dynamic reorder policy for that run by using the actual demand values for each time period." (p. 17)
