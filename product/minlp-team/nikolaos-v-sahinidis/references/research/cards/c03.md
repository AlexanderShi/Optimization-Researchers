### S018 · Semidefinite relaxations for quadratically constrained quadratic programming: A review and comparisons (Mathematical Programming 2011, doi:10.1007/s10107-011-0462-2)
**Read level**: full. pp. 1-27 read completely: §§1-7 with every proposition and proof, the computational section §6 and the conclusions. Figs. 1-6 were read from captions, axis labels and the text that describes them. References (pp. 27-29) were skimmed for self-citations and Burer's works. Received 3 July 2009, accepted 4 January 2011 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xiaowei Bao (UIUC Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering) and Mohit Tawarmalani (Purdue, Krannert School of Management); Sahinidis at CMU Chemical Engineering, corresponding author (p. 1). · **Place in trajectory**: the SDP entry of the relaxation line. It is cited next to the group's polyhedral work: Bao, Sahinidis & Tawarmalani 2009 on multiterm polyhedral relaxations [8] and Tawarmalani & Sahinidis 2005 [42] (pp. 27, 29). SKILL.md dates the group's own convex quadratic relaxations with SDP-quality bounds to 2021-22; this file does not cover them.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a practitioner's question about a crowded menu: "which relaxation is most appropriate in practice in terms of tightness and computational tractability" for the global optimization of general QCQP (p. 2). QCQP is stated with indefinite Q^k, linear equalities and finite bounds, scaled to [0,1]^n (p. 2). NP-hardness of QP is the stated reason why "the development of suitable relaxations is required for exact solution algorithms" (p. 2). The paper mixes known relaxations from the literature with new ones (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when an area offers many relaxations, frame the paper as the user's choice question (tightness and tractability) and answer it in two halves: theory first, then computation.
- **D2 Core idea**: Order the relaxations by proof, then price them by computation. The main result is stated up front as a chain, vLG = vShor = vDShor ≤ vSD ≤ vSC ≤ vSRLT = vDNN ≤ f*, plus vDShor ≤ vDLG1 ≤ vSRLT (p. 2). DLG1 and SD do not dominate each other. SD beats DLG1 on the one-variable instance min {−3x² + 2x | 0 ≤ x ≤ 1}; I checked this by hand: vSD = −1 = f*, vDLG1 = −3. The computations supply the other direction, with "many instances" where DLG1 beats SC and hence SD (p. 3). The building blocks are:
  - a partial first-level RLT that multiplies only bound and linear constraints (p. 4);
  - the Shor relaxation, obtained as the dual of the Lagrangian dual (pp. 5-6);
  - three strengthened Shor relaxations: SD (envelopes on diag X), SC (envelopes on all of X) and SRLT (Shor plus partial RLT) (pp. 8-9);
  - DLG1, obtained by squaring the linear equalities and adding x_i² ≤ 1 before dualizing (p. 7);
  - the doubly nonnegative relaxation DNN, derived from a completely positive representation (pp. 9-12);
  - the KKT-strengthened relaxations KKT1 and KKT2 (pp. 12-15);
  - for bilinear programs, a new relaxation BL with diag X = x (pp. 20-26).

  The unifying lens is that "all semidefinite relaxations can be regarded as Lagrangian relaxations obtained from certain reformulations of the primal problem". Which reformulation closes the gap "is not clear" (p. 8).
  - Transferable: to make a Lagrangian-derived SDP see linear equality constraints, square them, (a^T x − d)² = 0, and add the redundant x_i² ≤ 1 from the box before dualizing (p. 7). Fig. 3 shows that DLG1 then degrades less than expected as linear constraints are added (p. 19).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Every variable has finite bounds and is scaled to the unit box (p. 2). Q^k is symmetric without loss of generality (p. 2). From p. 6 on, a Slater-type Condition 1 (a strictly feasible Shor point) is assumed so that Shor equals the Lagrangian dual (p. 6). Proposition 11 assumes a nonempty relative interior (p. 23). The main chain holds "under mild technical conditions" (p. 2). The authors say why the lifted matrix must be bounded too: "the Shor relaxation may be unbounded since X may not be sufficiently bounded", even though x is bounded (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof devices**:
  - Weak duality plus conic duality (Theorem 2.4.1 of Ben-Tal and Nemirovski) identify the Lagrangian dual with the Shor SDP (Props. 1-2, p. 6).
  - Feasibility-mapping inclusions show that every feasible (x, X) of one relaxation is feasible for the other (Props. 4, 9, 10; pp. 8, 16). Implications such as "x − diag X ≥ 0 and diag X ≥ 0 imply x ≥ 0" order SD, SC and SRLT (Theorem 1, p. 16).
  - PSD square-root step: from a^T(X − xx^T)a = 0 and X − xx^T = B² ⪰ 0 it follows that Ba = 0, hence Xa = dx. This turns the DNN constraint aa^T • X = d² into the RLT product constraints (Prop. 9, p. 16).
  - The DNN relaxation comes from lifting with slacks, using Burer's completely positive representation of conv C+ (his Prop. 2.1), and then relaxing P ⊆ N (pp. 11-12). A separate exactness result: the convex relaxation is exact when every quadratic constraint is redundant over F or holds in reverse over all of F (Prop. 6, pp. 10-11).
  - KKT lifting: premultiplying stationarity by x^T gives an objective identity (Prop. 8, p. 13). The multipliers are lifted with x into one PSD matrix, and complementarity is dropped to shrink it (pp. 13-14).
  - Vertex-generated convex envelope of a multilinear function (Rikun): write x as a convex combination of box vertices and set X' = Σ φ_t π_t π_t^T, so that X' ⪰ xx^T and diag X' = x (Prop. 12, p. 24). SDP duality under Slater then passes from the Lagrangian subproblem to BL (Prop. 11, pp. 24-26).
  - The diagonal shift X' = X + diag z leaves a bilinear objective unchanged because diag Q = 0, so BL equals SD (Prop. 13, p. 26). The new relaxation collapses to an old one, and the paper says so.
  - Transferable: to show that two lifted relaxations are equal, map feasible points both ways. When a quadratic equality has to become linear products, use the square root of the PSD slack X − xx^T (p. 16).
  - Transferable: test a claimed dominance on a one-variable instance before computing anything; −3x² + 2x on [0,1] separates SD and DLG1 (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Test set: 700 random QCQPs with n from 20 to 60 and components uniform in [−1, 1] (b_k in [0, 100]) (p. 17). The constraints use four (m, q) combinations, from m = 1, q = n/10 to m = n, q = n/10. Matrix densities are 25, 50 and 100%. Dense matrices have 25-100% negative eigenvalues and are built as Z D Z^T with a random unitary Z. There are five instances per specification, and one relaxation got extra problems (p. 17); Fig. 6 shows 1,200 for DLG1 (p. 22).
  - Yardstick: the best feasible value found by BARON, MINOS, CONOPT and SNOPT under GAMS, at 10,000 s each, because some instances are "beyond the capabilities of exact solvers" (p. 17).
  - Pilot: SDPA 7.3.0, CSDP 6.0.1 and DSDP 5.8 on DNN up to n = 50 (480 problems, Fig. 1). DSDP missed the 1% tolerance, and CSDP matched SDPA's accuracy in less time, so CSDP at 1% was used. The solver's share of each gap is "no more than 1%" (p. 18).
  - Results: DNN/SRLT reaches about 3% gap at about 400 s per problem, SC 9% at 359 s, DLG1 13% and SD 19% (pp. 18-19). DLG1 was at least as tight as SD on every instance. It was about 4% tighter than SC on average but weaker on many instances. Adding diag(X) ≤ x to DLG1 gained only 0.16%. KKT1 and KKT2 were no better than SD and cost more (p. 19).
  - Breakdowns: linear constraints hurt DLG1 more than DNN (Fig. 3, p. 19). Density shows no clear trend, which the authors explain by dense Schur complements (pp. 19-20). Gaps peak at 50-75% negative eigenvalues for DNN/SRLT and near 10% for DLG1 (Figs. 5-6; pp. 20, 22).
  - Scope: root bounds only and no failure counts. The authors state that no effort went into implementation, for example no row generation (p. 17).
  - Transferable: before a relaxation comparison, pilot the candidate conic solvers on the hardest relaxation, pick one by accuracy and time, fix the tolerance, and state the solver's maximum share of every reported gap (p. 18).
  - Transferable: generate random nonconvex QCQPs with a controlled fraction of negative eigenvalues via Q = Z D Z^T, and vary constraint counts and density factorially so that each figure isolates one structural knob (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: The review recasts rather than criticizes. Burer's "recently developed" DNN relaxation is shown equal to the older Shor relaxation with partial first-level RLT (abstract, p. 16), and Burer's analogous result in [17] is credited (p. 16). Other works are drawn on as follows:
  - Anstreicher's finding that SDP plus RLT beats either alone frames the strengthened Shor family (p. 8).
  - Wolkowicz's view of Lagrangian relaxation as "the best possible SDP relaxation" is accepted, with the caveat that the right reformulation is unknown (p. 8).
  - KKT-based relaxations are cited as helping inside branch and bound although they do not tighten the root bound (Burer and Chen; Burer and Vandenbussche; p. 15).

  Burer's decomposition algorithm for bound-constrained DNNs is named as the way forward (p. 27).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract states the theorem and then the numbers: "three orders of magnitude more computational time", gaps averaging 3% against up to 19% on 700 problems with up to 60 variables, and a DLG1 bound "averaging 13% in a few CPU seconds" (p. 1). The ordering is announced on p. 2, before any relaxation is defined. Each relaxation gets a short code, and the proofs are a few lines each. The verdict is time-stamped and hedged: "At the time of this writing", DLG1 "may achieve the best balance between gap quality and solvability with current SDP solvers" (p. 27). The group's own new objects that lose are reported plainly (KKT1/KKT2, p. 19; BL = SD, p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory (the dominance chain, DNN = SRLT, BL = SD) plus a computational comparison that ends with two recommendations for two uses: DNN/SRLT for tight bounds and DLG1 for bound per second (pp. 19, 27).
  - Limits stated: DNN/SRLT is "time consuming for current SDP solvers" (p. 27); the comparison is root-only, with branch-and-bound comparisons left to future work (p. 27); the implementation was not tuned (p. 17).
  - Limits not stated: the instances are random rather than from a public library, and no instance release is mentioned. The root-only protocol cannot show the branch-and-bound benefit of KKT constraints that the cited papers report (p. 15 against p. 19).
  - Future directions: decomposition algorithms for DNN (Burer), approximate solution techniques for the dual of DNN/SRLT, and comparisons inside branch and bound (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 evidence ✅ (strong): proven dominance is explicitly not enough, so every relaxation is priced by time (p. 17). SC is set aside because its time follows that of the stronger DNN/SRLT (p. 19). The final recommendation is the balance relaxation DLG1, not the strongest one (p. 27). The SDP codes are chosen because they can be embedded in branch and bound (p. 18). Two departures from Method 5 step 1: there are no failure counts (only DSDP's missed tolerance in the pilot, p. 18), and the instances are random rather than a library.
- Method 4 variant ⚠: the area is entered with a systematic comparison of existing relaxations (pp. 1-2), but three things differ from the method. The yardstick is the best feasible value from four solvers (p. 17), not a certified optimum. The instances are random and their release is not mentioned. The group's own new relaxations sit inside the comparison and are reported as losing or equivalent (pp. 19, 26).
- Method 2 variant ⚠: the study runs on standalone SDP codes, outside BARON. The production solver enters only through the embeddability criterion (p. 18) and the deferred branch-and-bound comparison (p. 27).
- Workflow D step 2 evidence ✅: the group's own losses are reported (KKT1/KKT2, p. 19).
- New-pattern candidates: "dominance lattice, then price" (pp. 2-3, 16-19, 26); "calibrate the instrument before ranking" (p. 18); "structural-knob instance generator" (pp. 17, 19-22); "taxonomy-first review with one unifying lens" (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Present a relaxation zoo as a dominance lattice: prove equivalences by two-way feasibility maps and settle every incomparable pair with a small instance. Then let computation decide only among the non-dominated relaxations (pp. 2-3, 16, 19).
- Use the conic solver's dual value as the bound, and state the solver tolerance as an upper limit on its contribution to each reported gap (p. 18).
- When a stronger and a weaker relaxation cost about the same, drop the weaker one. When the strongest is orders of magnitude slower, add a cheap "balance" relaxation as a second recommendation (pp. 19, 27).
- Square linear equalities and add box-derived redundant quadratic constraints before dualizing, so that a Lagrangian-derived SDP keeps the information in the linear constraints (p. 7; Fig. 3, p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "The primary motivation is that, even when a dominance relationship has been established, the practical solvability of tight relaxations still needs to be investigated." (p. 17)
- "Comparisons of these relaxations in the context of a branch-and-bound algorithm should be addressed by future research." (p. 27)

### S001 · Derivative-free optimization: a review of algorithms and comparison of software implementations (Journal of Global Optimization 2013, doi:10.1007/s10898-012-9951-y)
**Read level**: full. pp. 1-41 read completely: the review (§§2-4), software (§5), the camel6 illustration (§6), all computational sections (§§7.1-7.12) and the conclusions (§8). Figs. 2-32 were read from captions, legends and the authors' descriptions. The acknowledgments (p. 42) were read; the references (pp. 42-47) were skimmed for the sources behind the protocol and behind the black-box application claim. The online supplementary material is not in the file and was not read. Received 20 December 2011, accepted 23 June 2012 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Luis Miguel Rios (CMU Chemical Engineering, p. 1); Sahinidis is the corresponding author (p. 1). · **Place in trajectory**: CMU era. The paper grows out of Rios's 2009 UIUC PhD thesis, cited as [120] (pp. 41, 46). It was funded "over a period of seven years" by NIH/NIGMS, NETL CO2-capture and NSF awards (p. 42). It is the anchor of Method 4, and S007 later names its library as the main source of noisy test problems (S007 p. 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is bound-constrained optimization of a deterministic f with no derivatives and no Lipschitz constants (p. 2). The entry is a counted gap. Mongeau et al. compared six solvers on eleven problems in 1998, and the number of solvers "has more than quadrupled" since. Other comparisons "are restricted to a few solvers related to the algorithms proposed in these papers" and use small test sets (p. 2). The paper states four questions (p. 2):
  - What solution quality do solvers reach under an evaluation limit, and how fast does it drop with size?
  - Which solver finds global solutions of nonconvex problems?
  - Does a small subset of solvers suffice?
  - Which solver refines a near-optimal point fastest?
  - Transferable: open a benchmark paper with a short list of questions a user would ask, and give each question its own experiment and set of figures (p. 2; answered in §§7.4-7.11 and on p. 41).
- **D2 Core idea**: Review the algorithms, then compare their implementations under identical conditions against known global optima. The review classifies algorithms as direct or model-based, local or global, and deterministic or stochastic (pp. 2-3). It then offers one lens for all of them: "every iteration of a derivative-free method can be viewed as a process the main purpose of which is to determine the next point(s) to evaluate". Under that lens it splits the methods into those that partition the search space and those that do not (pp. 11-13). The comparison runs 22 solvers (Table 2, p. 14) on 502 problems: 78 smooth convex, 161 nonsmooth convex, 245 smooth nonconvex and 18 nonsmooth nonconvex (p. 3; Table 3, p. 20). All solvers get the same starting points and boxes and are ranked by several criteria (abstract), with developer rounds and an unseen problem set (p. 3).
  - Transferable: in a review of many methods, find the one question every method answers (here, which point to evaluate next) and classify the methods by how they answer it (partition or not), not by name (pp. 11-13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is deterministic, the only constraints are bounds, and n is at most 300 "as most of the optimization packages tested in this study were designed for low-dimensional unconstrained problems" (p. 19). The test problems are algebraic (globallib, princetonlib, Lukšan-Vlček, Richtarik, Nesterov, ARWHEAD; p. 19), but the solvers see only function values (p. 21), so certified optima can exist. Missing bounds were set to [−10,000, 10,000], tightened where overflow occurred while still containing the best known solution (p. 20). Of the 502 problems, 357 lacked lower and upper bounds on at least one variable (Table 4, p. 21). Constraints and noise are excluded (p. 41).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a; the paper proves nothing of its own. The review reports these convergence facts from the literature:
  - McKinnon's convex C² example, on which Nelder-Mead converges to a nonstationary point (p. 4);
  - GSS converges to stationary points "which, could be local minima, local maxima, or even saddle points" (p. 4);
  - MADS converges to second-order stationary points and, under more assumptions, to a local minimizer with probability 1 (p. 5);
  - DIRECT's best points converge to a KKT point (Finkel and Kelley; p. 7);
  - MCS converges as s_max → ∞ (p. 8);
  - simulated annealing has asymptotic results but "no guarantee that a good solution will be obtained in a finite number of iterations" (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Setup: Intel 2.13 GHz, Linux, Matlab R2010a. Each run gets 2,500 function evaluations, justified by "about 200 CPU days" for a groundwater bioremediation model (p. 20). Each run is also capped at 10 CPU minutes, and the hits of that cap are reported by solver and size (Fig. 4, p. 22).
  - Starting points: ten random points, shared by all solvers that accept one; four solvers instead start at the box centre. In all, 112,448 runs (p. 21).
  - Fairness devices: solvers that cannot handle bounds get a constant large value outside the box, and SID-PSM's required constraint gradients are set to unit vectors (p. 21).
  - Success test: f(y) ≤ max(1.01 f(x*), f(x*) + 0.01) (p. 21).
  - Ground truth: BARON "was able to guarantee global optimality for most of the test problems". LINDOGLOBAL covered the trigonometric and other functions BARON did not accept (p. 21). The nonconvex figures speak of "the best known solution" (p. 26).
  - Settings: defaults with identical stopping rules, following Neumaier et al.'s comparison of complete global solvers [102]. Developers could revise or specify their defaults. There were no restarts after early termination (pp. 22-23).
  - Criteria:
    - fraction solved against evaluations, per problem class (Figs. 5-12, pp. 23-28);
    - fraction of problems on which a solver found the best solution among all solvers (Figs. 7-8, 11-12);
    - Moré-Wild improvement from the starting point, with τ down to 10^-6 (pp. 28-30);
    - minimal covering solver subsets per budget (Figs. 17-21, pp. 30-33);
    - results by size group (Figs. 22-25, p. 34);
    - variance, measured by a scaled objective that uses the run's worst value (p. 36);
    - results by availability of bounds (Fig. 27, p. 36);
    - refinement from an asymmetric box [−0.166 + x*, 0.033 + x*] (pp. 36-37).
  - Unseen set: 502 problems obtained by an affine change of variables with condition number c = 2 (p. 41).
  - Losses are explicit: six solvers solved no convex smooth problem, and fifteen solved under 10% of the convex nonsmooth ones (p. 23).
  - Transferable: define success with a mixed tolerance, max(1.01 f*, f* + 0.01), so that problems whose optimal value is near zero are not judged by a relative test alone (p. 21).
  - Transferable: tie the evaluation budget to the run time of a named real application, and report how often the CPU limit, rather than the budget, ended a run (pp. 20, 22).
  - Transferable: after giving developers several rounds on your test set, rerun on a transformed copy they never saw before you publish rankings (p. 41).
  - Transferable: conclude with minimal covering solver subsets as a function of the budget instead of a single winner (pp. 30-33).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper positions itself against small comparisons run by the authors of the methods compared (p. 2). It borrows its protocol from two neighbouring benchmark papers: the default-settings rule from Neumaier et al. [102] (pp. 22-23) and the improvement test from Moré and Wild [97] (p. 28). It reconciles terminology instead of imposing it: some authors reserve "derivative-free" for what the paper calls model-based methods, and the literature is "inconsistent and confusing" (p. 3). History is told with Google Scholar citation counts (Table 1, p. 13): stochastic methods are the most cited, and deterministic ones had little theory behind them until the 1990s (p. 11). The conclusion names model-based algorithms and convergence proofs as "the most important results" of the field (p. 41), even though model-based solvers did poorly on the convex nonsmooth problems (p. 24). The developers' comments on drafts shaped the paper (p. 42).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract carries the counts (22 solvers, 502 problems, 112,448 instances) and names the winners per criterion. It hedges with "For the problems used in this study" and "on average" (p. 1). Surprises are named and then explained by the test set:
  - "It is strange that model-based solvers ... solve a small fraction of problems", softened by "some of these solvers are old" (pp. 23-24);
  - nonconvex smooth problems are solved more often than convex ones because they are smaller, per Table 3 (p. 26);
  - inflated improvement rates come from polynomial and exponential terms that are easy to improve (pp. 28, 30);
  - the order of the bound-availability classes is attributed to "the corresponding problem sizes (see Table 4)", although Table 4 lists counts by class, not sizes (pp. 21, 36).

  One such sentence discusses convex nonsmooth problems but names "the nonconvex problems" (p. 30). A gallery of camel6 plots shows how each solver spends its 2,500 evaluations (pp. 18-19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: a review plus the largest DFO comparison at the time, with the problems and detailed results posted at http://archimedes.cheme.cmu.edu/?q=dfocomp (p. 20).
  - Findings: quality drops with size and nonsmoothness. Global solvers beat local ones even on convex problems, except NEWUOA and BOBYQA on convex smooth problems (p. 24). No solver dominates, and every solver gave the best solution on some problem. TOMLAB/MULTIMIN, TOMLAB/GLCCLUSTER, MCS and SNOBFIT together give the best results in most cases (p. 41).
  - Limits stated: constraints and noise "warrant further study on their own" (p. 41); only default settings and no restarts (pp. 22-23).
  - An outside claim: the statement that the best solvers suffice for true black-box applications rests on work "detailed elsewhere" (p. 41). Most of those references are CMU Chemical Engineering MS theses from 2008-2011; their advisor is not named in the references ([13], [26], [43], [139], [144]; pp. 42-47).
  - Limits not stated: the unseen set is the same 502 functions under an affine map. On my reading of the extracted formula, x = x' + (1 + t u^T x')u with t = (c − 1)/u^T u, the map stretches one random direction by c = 2 and shifts by u (p. 41). The TOMLAB family that leads the rankings is among the codes revised during the rounds (pp. 37, 41).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 evidence ✅ (strong; this is the source paper). Each step of the method appears:
  - the comparison comes before any own method (p. 2);
  - optima come from a deterministic global solver, with a second solver for functions the first cannot read (p. 21);
  - bounds, starting points and stopping rules are identical, and the budget is tied to an application (pp. 20-21);
  - defaults plus developer rounds, then an unseen set (pp. 3, 22-23, 37, 41);
  - an explicit success test, with the median and best of ten runs (p. 21);
  - the problems and results are published (p. 20);
  - the conclusion is a portfolio with a stated scope (pp. 30-33, 41).

  Two precisions for the skill. The optima are certified "for most", not all, problems (p. 21). The unseen set is a mild change of variables of the same problems (p. 41), which supports the reading of the hold-out as weak, one of the two readings Method 4 keeps as a disagreement.
- Method 2 variant ⚠: the production solver BARON serves as the judge of another field's benchmark (p. 21), not as the code being ablated.
- Workflow E step 5 evidence ✅ (adopt the testers' protocol): the default-settings rule follows Neumaier et al.'s comparison of complete global solvers [102] (pp. 22-23; reference p. 45), the study that SKILL.md's Signature Work Anatomy cites for BARON's reception.
- Method 3 variant ⚠ (weak): the paper runs Method 3 backwards, hiding algebraic problems behind a function-value interface to obtain a yardstick (p. 21). Its rankings are the evidence behind Method 3 step 7's fallback to a deterministic derivative-free method: deterministic global solvers (MCS, GLCCLUSTER, LGO) and the multistart TOMLAB/MULTIMIN are on top (p. 41). Whether MULTIMIN is deterministic cannot be checked; it is described only as "a multistart algorithm" (p. 17).
- Method 1 variant ⚠ (weak): there is no reduction here, but bounds decide success. Problems with bounds on all variables are solved much more often (Fig. 27, p. 36), and missing bounds hurt "particularly the stochastic ones" (p. 41), consistent with Heuristic 2.
- Heuristic 4 evidence ✅: excluded solvers are listed with reasons (PRAXIS, and four TOMLAB solvers built for small boxes; p. 17), and solvers without bound handling get a constant penalty outside the box (p. 21).
- Heuristic 9 and Workflow F step 1 evidence ✅ (medium): the true black-box applications were carried by MS theses in the same department (p. 41; references pp. 42-47).
- New-pattern candidates: "explain surprising rankings by test-set composition first" (pp. 26, 28-30, 36); "audit the measurement before the ranking" (pp. 22, 41); "questions-first benchmark" (pp. 2, 41); "taxonomy-first review with one unifying lens" (pp. 2-3, 11-13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Judge each run by a mixed-tolerance success test. Report the median of ten shared random starts as the average case and the best of the ten as the best case (p. 21).
- For solvers that cannot take bounds, return one constant large objective value outside the box, the same for all iterations and solvers (p. 21).
- Build an unseen copy of a test set by a random affine change of variables with a chosen condition number (c = 2). It keeps smoothness and convexity but moves the local solutions (p. 41).
- Report minimal covering solver subsets for each budget instead of one ranking (pp. 30-33).
- To compare variability across problems of different scale, use the scaled objective 1 − (f − f_L)/(f_W − f_L), with f_W the worst of the runs (p. 36).
- Test refinement from a box placed asymmetrically around x* (p. 37). The paper gives no reason; the evident effect is that solvers starting at the box centre (p. 21) do not start at x*.
- Before interpreting a difference between problem classes, check the size distribution of the classes in the test-set table (pp. 20, 26).

**Quotes**:

### S007 · Simulation optimization: a review of algorithms and applications (Annals of Operations Research 2016, doi:10.1007/s10479-015-2019-x)
**Read level**: full. The version read is arXiv:1706.08591v1, the accepted manuscript posted on 26 June 2017 under CC-BY-NC-ND, which names the Annals of OR article as the formal publication (p. 1). Pagination is the manuscript's own (pp. 1-28). The body, §§1-6 (pp. 1-18), was read completely, including Tables 1-5 and Algorithms 1-6. The references (pp. 18-28) were skimmed for self-citations.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Satyajith Amaran (CMU Chemical Engineering, "Now at Core R&D, The Dow Chemical Company"), and Bikram Sharda and Scott J. Bury (Core R&D, The Dow Chemical Company) (p. 1). · **Place in trajectory**: CMU era, with industrial coauthors. The paper is "an updated version" of the group's 2014 4OR review [4] (p. 2). It cites S001 as the first comprehensive DFO comparison (p. 15) and S001's test set as where most SO testing happens (p. 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a contrast with mathematical programming. SO optimizes objectives and constraints that are evaluated "through a stochastic simulation" (abstract, p. 1). There is no algebraic description, runs are expensive, outputs are noisy and derivatives are unusable (pp. 1-2). The general problem P1 has an expected-value objective and constraints, deterministic constraints h, bounds, continuous x and discrete y (p. 2). The update is justified by recent developments (the relation to DFO, simulation test beds, machine learning) and by the fact that "more than half of the works we reference were published in the last decade" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Organize the field by what is known about the model. Table 1 crosses "algebraic model available" with "uncertainty present", giving four cells: traditional mathematical programming, DFO, stochastic programming and robust optimization, and SO (p. 5). Algorithms are classified by discrete or continuous variables and by local or global scope (Table 3, p. 7). Each class gets basic pseudocode (Algorithms 1-6, pp. 8-14), and "the key lies in the statistics of how noise is handled" (p. 6). The review closes with software (Tables 4-5, pp. 15-17) and the state of comparisons (§5, pp. 15-18).
  - Transferable: start a survey with a 2×2 of what each neighbouring field assumes (here: algebraic model or not, deterministic or noisy), so the reader sees which tools are lost and which can be borrowed (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: P1 is stated with expectations (p. 2). The expected value is the usual objective, and risk measures are an alternative (p. 2). The scope is restricted to methods for which "all the optimization scheme has to work with are evaluations of f(x, y, ω) and g(x, y, ω), or simply, observations with noise" (p. 4). An iteration is a fixed number of evaluations, usually one (pp. 3-4). Discrete variables may be binary, integer-ordered or categorical. Integer-ordered variables should stay integer-ordered "to exploit correlations in objective function values between adjacent integer values" (p. 6).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a (no proofs). Reported results:
  - the best possible SO rate is O(1/√k) by the central limit theorem, so practical runs end on a budget (pp. 4-5);
  - SPSA's two-point gradient estimate has the same asymptotic rate as an estimate from n + 1 points (p. 11);
  - certifying a global optimum would require all feasible solutions to be evaluated "through infinite observations" (p. 6);
  - in SO, "global convergence" means convergence to a global solution, unlike in mathematical programming (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no experiments). §5 discusses comparison practice:
  - no comprehensive comparison on large test beds exists, so SO "lags behind" other fields, including DFO, "where the first comprehensive comparison appeared in 2013 [166]" (p. 15);
  - the obstacles are the unknown true optimum and the noisy estimates, plus a common wrapper for all algorithms, irreproducible randomized solvers and missing test simulations (p. 15);
  - current reporting (macroreplicate averages at the end of the budget, or per-problem evaluation counts) hides behaviour across budgets and differences in sample variance (p. 17);
  - SimOpt offered "just 20 continuous optimization problems", and most testing uses classical NLP problems with added noise, many of them compiled in S001 (p. 18).
  - Transferable: when judging a comparison of noisy solvers, ask for performance across budgets (profiles), not only the final macroreplicate average, and for the sample variances (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on prior reviews (Meketon, Fu, Tekin and Sabuncuoglu, Hong and Nelson; pp. 2-3) and claims novelty by recency and breadth (p. 3). It bridges communities: mathematical and stochastic programming, DFO, active learning, reinforcement learning, bandits, experimental design and statistics (pp. 4-5, 18). Others' claims are reported as claims; for example, the Adaptive Hyperbox algorithm "claims to have superior performance" (p. 9). The self-citations are the 2014 version [4], S001 [166] and Sahinidis 2004 on optimization under uncertainty [175] (pp. 2, 4, 6, 15, 18, 19, 26). The group's own surrogate line is absent: a search of the file finds neither "ALAMO" nor "Cozad", and neural networks are listed among the forms of RSM without comment (p. 9). The paper names a research-practice gap: commercial packages use metaheuristics without guarantees, while academic methods are hard to obtain (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing**: Survey register in the first-person plural. There are long tables of applications (Table 2, p. 7) and software (Tables 4-5, pp. 16-17). The pseudocode is there "to give researchers and practitioners unfamiliar with the field an idea of the general approach" (p. 6). One exhortation stands out: "We wholeheartedly encourage researchers to post their executable files, if not their source code." (p. 18). The closing list of directions numbers "(2)" twice (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: an organized, updated survey with a taxonomy, pseudocode and software tables, plus the diagnosis that the field lacks comparisons on large test beds (pp. 5-18).
  - Limits: no new method and no experiment. The scope is limited to methods that see only noisy observations (p. 4).
  - Future directions (p. 18):
    - large problems with combined discrete and continuous variables;
    - stochastic and deterministic constraints;
    - parallel computing at several levels;
    - multiple simulation outputs;
    - risk measures;
    - hybrid algorithms;
    - automatic differentiation of simulations;
    - convergence guarantees;
    - "the availability and ease of comparison of the performance of available approaches on different applications";

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before running any SO algorithm, list the domain knowledge to put in: screen the inputs, scale and reduce variable ranges, give good starting guesses, and use known structure such as derivative estimates (p. 4).
- Keep integer-ordered decisions integer-ordered instead of encoding them in binaries, so that a method can exploit the correlation between neighbouring values (p. 6).
- When reviewing benchmark practice in a noisy field, separate the yardstick problem (unknown optimum, noisy estimates) from the logistics (a common wrapper, the reproducibility of randomized solvers, test simulations) (p. 15).
