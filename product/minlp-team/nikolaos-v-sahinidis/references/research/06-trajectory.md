## 8. Candidate trajectory heuristics for the skill (all [inferred] from §§1–7)

1. **Change the problem source, keep the engine.** On moving to a new home, adopt its application stream (and funding) and pull its hardest instances into your own solver. Do not restart the method. Era caveat: this assumes you own a mature solver. Early-career analogue: own a reusable code base, however small (BARON 1.0 was 1,800 lines of GAMS).
2. **Enter an unfamiliar method area with a benchmark, not a new method.** DFO (22 solvers, 502 problems), mixed-integer DFO (13 implementations), SDP relaxations for QCQP (2011 review), simulation optimization (2014/2016 reviews), CAMD (2016 review). In the DFO study, developers tuned their codes over several rounds and were then tested on held-out problems "to confirm that the process had not led to overtraining of the software on our test problem collection" (*JOGO* 56:1249). [stated-joint, P for the procedure]
3. **Expect a decade between a promising algorithm and a product.** Run it through MS and PhD theses, publish when it is mature, ship it last: MAS 2010 → 2025, BAM 2015 → 2026. Resource caveat: this needs a company or long-lived code base to land in.
4. **Revisit an old hard problem when the relaxation toolbox has matured**: molecular global minima, 1992 → 2025.
5. **Use cheap student projects to probe options.** At CMU, 38 MS theses in five years tested ALAMO design choices, solver tuning and application domains. Resource caveat: this needs a large MS program. At GT he probes through co-supervised PhDs instead.
6. **Keep a public, dated record of solver progress and errors**: yearly INFORMS "BARON project" status talks (2020–2025) and release notes that name serious bugs.
7. **Exits need no announcement**: let a line end when its student graduates or its grant ends. Downside (not a recommendation): stated long-term goals on the website go stale (§6).
