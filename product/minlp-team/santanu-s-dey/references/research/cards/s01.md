### S026 · Facets of two-dimensional infinite group problems (Mathematics of Operations Research 2008, doi:10.1287/moor.1070.0283)
**Read level**: partial (abstract and §1 pp. 1–2, §2 pp. 2–4, §4 statements pp. 5–6 and 10–11, §5 pp. 11–15 statements, §6 pp. 16–22 constructions and Theorems 34, 42, §7 statements pp. 22–28, §8 pp. 29–31, §9 Conclusion pp. 31–32; long proofs skipped).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jean-Philippe P. Richard (Purdue, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: PhD-era foundation of the multi-row group-relaxation line (cited as Dey's thesis [6], p. 32).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Commercial cuts come from single-row relaxations, and multiple constraints "should help" (p. 1); single-row group cuts have weak continuous-variable coefficients per Dash–Günlük's computations (p. 2). The open object is the two-dimensional mixed integer infinite group problem (2DMIIGP), for which no facets were known (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable: use a computational study's diagnosis of where existing cuts are weak (here: continuous coefficients) to choose which higher-dimensional relaxation to study (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Tools first, then families. (i) Subadditivity of a continuous piecewise-linear function on I² is verified at finitely many boundary points/vertices (Prop. 6, p. 6; Props. 10, 12, pp. 7, 11). (ii) A two-dimensional replacement for Gomory–Johnson's Interval Lemma (Prop. 24, pp. 13–14) to show the equality system E(φ) has a unique solution. (iii) Family 1: aggregate the two rows and apply a 1D facet, κ(x, y) = ζ((x + y) mod 1) (Construction 31, p. 16), generalised by automorphism/homomorphism (Construction 38, Theorem 39, p. 20). (iv) Family 2: a three-gradient function ψ on five polytopes (Construction 44, pp. 22–23; Theorem 48, p. 28).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Infinite group I² with finite-support solutions (Def. 1, p. 3); functions continuous and piecewise linear over a polyhedral subdivision (pp. 5–6); mixed-integer extension by Johnson's limit formula for continuous coefficients (Theorem 51, p. 29). Three-gradient family needs f1 ≠ 0, f2 ≠ 0 (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Facet Theorem route (Theorem 17, p. 12): show minimality, then uniqueness of the solution of E(φ) via the 2D interval lemma applied region by region (Theorem 48 proof, p. 28). Characterisation by contradiction for the two-gradient case: no vertices, parallel edges, an edge through the origin, then reduction to Construction 38 (Theorem 42, pp. 21–22). Prop. 54 proves GMIC has the strongest continuous coefficients among 1D facets, included "for completeness" (p. 30).
- **D5 Experiments**: none; one numeric example (Example 55, p. 31) where the three-gradient cut is facet-defining and both single-row GMICs are not, and a coefficient-dominance picture (Fig. 9, pp. 30–31).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: first facet families of 2DMIIGP, plus verification tools; all continuous PL two-gradient facets characterised (Theorem 42, p. 21). The conclusion reads Family 1 as "a theoretical explanation for the success of" constraint aggregation in practice (p. 31). Future: computational evaluation inside branch-and-cut, more facet families, extension to n-dimensional groups (p. 32). No computational test in the paper.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: this is the pre-2019 side of the contradiction recorded in SKILL.md: single-row cuts are the limit to go beyond, and multi-row (two-row) relaxations are the remedy (pp. 1–2); the substructure is still MILP-internal.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: a completeness theorem marks the boundary of Construction 38 (every two-gradient PL facet arises from it, Theorem 42, p. 21), and a second family is shown to lie outside it (§7, p. 22).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: one sentence claims the construction explains why aggregation works in practice (p. 31), but no practice is measured.
- New-pattern candidate: finite-check reduction (reduce a property over an infinite domain, here subadditivity on I², to finitely many inequalities at boundary points) (Props. 6, 10, 12, pp. 6–11).
- New-pattern candidate: build high-dimensional strong inequalities by composing one-dimensional facets (Constructions 31, 38, pp. 16–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove subadditivity of a piecewise-linear function, show it suffices to check the inequality at pairs of breakpoints/vertices (plus "supplemental vertices" where none exist), turning an infinite check into a finite list (Prop. 6, p. 6; proof of Prop. 32, p. 17).
- When a one-dimensional uniqueness lemma does not generalise, state a set-sum version (U + V) that drops continuity and apply it to adjacent regions one at a time (Prop. 24, pp. 13–14; Theorem 48, p. 28).
- Close a theory paper with a tiny two-variable IP where the new cut is facet-defining and the classical cuts are not (Example 55, p. 31).

**Quotes**:
- "Proving that a subadditive function φ is facet-defining is typically difficult." (p. 11)
- "This method requires the verification of subadditivity of the function at a finite number of carefully selected points." (p. 2)

### S072 · Linear-programming-based lifting and its application to primal cutting-plane algorithms (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2009, doi:10.1287/ijoc.1080.0284)
**Read level**: partial (abstract and §1 pp. 1–3, §2.1–2.2 pp. 3–5 and pp. 9–10, §3 algorithm statement pp. 12–16, §4 experiments pp. 16–25, §5 Conclusion pp. 26–27; proofs in §2.3 and the appendix pp. 27–32 not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jean-Philippe P. Richard (Purdue, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the only computational paper of the Purdue lifting work.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Primal cutting-plane algorithms keep an integer feasible solution at all times and can re-optimise after objective changes (p. 2), but have been "dormant since the 1970's" (p. 2) and seem to work only with strong cuts (p. 2). Lifting for unstructured IPs with multiple constraints is hard (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Exact lifting problems (5)–(6) are IPs (pp. 4–5); relax integrality of all variables except the one being lifted, so each bound is an LP with one integer variable (7)–(8) (p. 5). Bounds from two LPs suffice (Prop. 7, p. 9); fixing the variable at a bound gives an integral coefficient from one LP (Corollary 8, p. 10). Applied to Gomory's fractional cut inside Glover's algorithm (MGA, steps pp. 12–13); primal-dual pivot sequences reuse bases across coefficients (§2.3, p. 3 overview).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Pure IP min{cᵀx : Ax ≤ b, x ∈ Z₊ᵐ} with rational data (p. 4); cut coefficients and rhs assumed integral (p. 4: "most of the results of this section still apply" otherwise); Glover's special row needed (pp. 12–14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Finite convergence of MGA (Prop. 12, p. 16) via preservation of special-row properties under integer-coefficient cut families (Prop. 11, pp. 15–16); proofs in appendix, not read.
- **D5 Experiments**: 50 Letchford–Lodi 0-1 multidimensional knapsacks, 50 self-generated general-integer knapsacks, MIPLIB p0033/p0040 from 20 starting solutions; MGA (four lifting orders) vs Glover's algorithm and the published Letchford–Lodi IYA results; % gap to the CPLEX 10.1 optimum, cuts used, time split lifting/total (pp. 16–25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and computational: MGA proved optimality on 48 instances vs 35 for IYA with 1698 vs 9236 cuts (p. 20); 97% of cuts improved (p. 20); lifting takes about 96% of run time and primal-dual pivots cut it by about 50% (p. 23). Lifting order matters: random and reverse orders are worse (pp. 20, 23). Future: larger study especially with dual cutting planes, combination with Letchford–Lodi ideas, heuristics to choose cuts and variables, coefficients for continuous variables (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: success is measured as gap to the true optimum computed by CPLEX (p. 20) and the instrument is a hand-written C implementation of a textbook primal algorithm (p. 17), but the baseline IYA is taken from published numbers, not rerun (pp. 19–20).
- Heuristic 6 ✅ evidence: the time spent in lifting is reported separately (96%, Table 4, p. 23).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the move is "use information from multiple constraints" instead of one-row lifting (abstract p. 1, p. 26), still inside MILP.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To approximate an intractable lifting problem, keep integrality only on the variable being lifted and relax the rest: each coefficient bound becomes a single LP (eqs. (7)–(8), p. 5).
- Design ablations of an ordering rule with a purpose for each arm: a near-identical order (robustness), a random order (does order matter at all), and the reversed order (is the chosen order merely poor) (§4.3, p. 19).
- Report what fraction of the run time the new component takes and how much a warm-start trick saves (Table 4, p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "Thus, approximately 97% of the cuts were improved using the algorithm of Section 2." (p. 20)
- "We also observe that MGA spends approximately 96% of the time in the lifting procedure." (p. 23)

### S046 · Relations between facets of low- and high-dimensional group problems (Mathematical Programming 2010, doi:10.1007/s10107-009-0303-8)
**Read level**: partial (abstract and §1 pp. 1–4, definitions §2 pp. 4–8 statements, §3 pp. 8–11 definitions and statements, Theorem 5 and discussion pp. 14–16, Example 1 pp. 17–18, §5 opening pp. 19–21, Theorem 6 p. 28, §6 Conclusion pp. 28–29).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jean-Philippe P. Richard (Purdue, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: journal sequel of S026; extended abstract at IPCO XII 2007 (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Numerical studies (Fischetti–Saturni, Dash–Günlük) suggest multi-row cuts and stronger continuous coefficients are more promising than new 1D facets (p. 2); quotes Aardal–Weismantel–Wolsey and Gomory–Johnson calling for higher-dimensional functions (pp. 2–3). No facets were known above dimension two, and 1D proof tools do not generalise (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: The sequential-merge g♦h: generate a cut from the last m rows in "lifting space", add it to the first row, then apply a 1D cut to the combined row (Def. 10 and its interpretation, p. 8). Facets of PI(r1, 1) and PI(r2, m) merge into facets of PI(r, m+1) (Theorem 5, p. 14). Reverse direction: duplicate a row with integer multipliers and restrict an (m+1)-dimensional cut to get m-dimensional cuts (eq. (24), pp. 19–20), facet-defining under restrictive conditions (Theorem 6, p. 28).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: g, h continuous, piecewise linear, facet-defining, E(g), E(h) unique up to scaling, lifting-space representations nondecreasing (Theorem 5, p. 14); continuous coefficients via Johnson's limit formula (Prop. 15, p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Facet Theorem with the equality set E (p. 5); prove equality with any ψ satisfying E(g♦h) first on a "spine" set S(g♦h), then everywhere (Theorem 5 proof, p. 14). Example 1 certifies a cut lies outside the first split closure by exhibiting a point of the rank-1 split closure that the cut removes (pp. 17–18).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: First family of facets for general high-dimensional infinite group problems (p. 3); relates to two-step MIR of Dash–Günlük and explains the three-gradient facets of S026 (p. 3). Limits stated: necessity of the conditions on h is open and conjectured not needed (p. 16); the projected construction fails to give facets even with GMICs (p. 20); all known high-dimensional facets still derive from 1D ones (p. 29). Future: find genuinely high-dimensional cuts; test computationally (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: sufficiency theorem paired with necessity of "g facet-defining", an explicit conjecture where necessity is unknown (p. 16), and counterexamples where the projected merge is not extreme (φ = ½φ₁ + ½φ₂, p. 20).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: same multi-row MILP programme as S026, motivated by computational studies (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: build high-dimensional strong inequalities by composing one-dimensional facets (sequential-merge, p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give every abstract construction a one-paragraph "tableau reading" (cut the last rows, add to the first row, cut again) so practitioners see it as an operation on simplex rows (p. 8).
- To show a cut is not a split cut of rank 1, exhibit one point of the first split closure that the cut separates (Example 1, pp. 17–18).

**Quotes**:
- "In fact, the result does not even necessarily hold when the constituent inequalities are GMICs." (p. 20)
- "we expect sequential-merge inequalities to also be successful in practice." (p. 29)

### S053 · Composite lifting of group inequalities and an application to two-row mixing inequalities (Discrete Optimization 2010, doi:10.1016/j.disopt.2010.06.001)
**Read level**: partial (abstract and §1 pp. 1–2, §2 overview pp. 2–3, §3 p. 4, §4 opening pp. 5–6, Corollary 5 p. 7, Theorem 8 statement p. 8, §5 statements pp. 11–13, Theorem 23 p. 17, §6 Discussion p. 18).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Laurence A. Wolsey (CORE, Louvain, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: CORE postdoc period; continues Dey–Wolsey work on two-row lattice-free cuts ([12], p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Given a valid but non-minimal inequality for the mixed integer infinite group relaxation, how to strengthen it: sequential lifting of integer variables is expensive, trivial fill-in may be weak (p. 1); no paper had asked what to do when trivial fill-in fails to be minimal (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Composite lifting: lift one integer variable x(a) by the modified lifting problem (10), then fill in all others (§3, p. 4). Two-row mixing inequality φ_MIX is not minimal (via Prop. 18, p. 12, and Theorem 19, p. 13); choosing a = (1 + r1/2 − r2/2, (r1+r2)/2) gives an extreme function (Theorem 23, p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: π extreme for the continuous relaxation MI({0}, Rᵐ, r) (p. 2); φ piecewise linear, continuous, zero only at 0 (Theorem 8, p. 8); r1 + r2 ≤ 1, obtained WLOG by negating rows (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Bound on the lifting multiplier n: if φ(qa) + φ(r − qa) = 1 then some optimal n ≤ q (Corollary 5, p. 7), which replaced a subadditivity check of about 350 cases (pp. 5, 18). Necessary conditions for minimality of the lifted function (Theorem 8, p. 8) used, with experiments over values of a, to pick a (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (informal "empirically experimenting with different values of a", p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: New class of extreme inequalities for the two-row mixed integer infinite group relaxation (abstract; Theorem 23, p. 17), closed form so no lifting problem need be solved (p. 17). Open: can a k-variable lifting sequence followed by fill-in always give a minimal function (p. 18)?

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 3 ✅ evidence: the abandoned proof route is written in with its cost ("approximately 350 different cases", p. 5; again p. 18).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: choice of a was "guided both by such empirical experiments and the necessary conditions" (p. 6), i.e. small computation before proof, but not reported as data.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: MILP-internal two-row lifting, the programme SKILL.md records as later disappointing.
- New-pattern candidate: finite-check reduction (a bound on the lifting multiplier turns validity into a short finite check) (Corollary 5, p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a direct subadditivity proof explodes into hundreds of cases, look for an a-priori bound on the lifting problem's integer multiplier and prove validity through it instead (Corollary 5, p. 7; p. 18).
- Use necessary conditions for minimality as a filter on candidate parameters before attempting a proof, and pair it with quick numerical trials (Theorem 8, pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "This involved verification of approximately 350 different cases and the resulting proof was extremely tedious." (p. 5)
- "The lifting and fill-in process applied together in the composite lifting approach represents a compromise between the strength of inequalities and the difficulty in deriving them." (p. 18)

### D001 · On the Chvátal-Gomory closure of a compact convex set (IPCO 2011, doi:10.1007/978-3-642-20807-2_11)
**Read level**: partial (author preprint dated November 7, 2010, 18 pp.; abstract p. 1, §1 pp. 2–3, §2 main result and proof outline pp. 3–4, §3.1 overview p. 4, §4 openings p. 13, §5 proof of the theorem pp. 15–17; technical lemmas of §3.2 and §4 not read).
**Role**: supplement (conference version of S025) · **Coauthors**: Daniel Dadush (Georgia Tech), Juan Pablo Vielma (Pittsburgh) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: closes the CG-closure series (ellipsoid, strictly convex sets [6, 7], p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A question left open in a discussion section of Schrijver (1980): is the CG closure of an irrational polytope a polyhedron (quote of Schrijver, p. 2)? Convex IP motivation: second-order cone sets inherit irrational generators (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Theorem 2.1: the CG closure of any compact convex set is finitely generated, hence a rational polytope (p. 3). Proof by induction on dimension: (1) CG cuts of an exposed face F_v lift to CG cuts of K, with aff(F) replaced by the largest integer subspace aff_I(H_v), via Dirichlet's approximation theorem (§3.1, p. 4); (2) finite approximations of the closure built by compactness that agree with CC(K) on the relative boundary (§4, pp. 3–4); (3) only finitely many CG cuts can separate a vertex in the relative interior (§5, pp. 15–17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: K non-empty, compact, convex in Rⁿ (Theorem 2.1, p. 3); boundedness chosen because conv of integer points in a general convex set need not be a polyhedron (footnote 2, p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Dirichlet simultaneous approximation to rotate face cuts into cuts of K (p. 4; Theorem 3.1, p. 6); notion aff_I(A) = aff(aff(A) ∩ Zⁿ) (p. 3); induction on affine dimension with a final radius argument: cuts with ‖w‖ ≥ 1/δ cannot cut a relative-interior vertex (eq. (29), p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Pure theory; resolves Schrijver's question for polytopes and generalises the rational polytope, ellipsoid and strictly convex cases (abstract, p. 1). The non-compact case is explicitly set aside (footnote 2, p. 2). No conclusion section in this version.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: step 1 of Method 3 ("Cutting plane closures ?" for the nonlinear class) carried out a decade before the QCQP work: a classical MILP closure theorem (Chvátal, Schrijver) asked for convex IPs (p. 2).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the hypothesis "compact" is justified by the reason the theorem cannot hold beyond it (footnote 2, p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: Diophantine approximation as the tool for irrational data in integer-convex geometry (pp. 3–4; also S043 pp. 13–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When extending a polyhedral result to convex sets, isolate the new obstacle by name (here irrationality) and look for the one classical tool that handles it (Dirichlet's theorem) (pp. 2–4).
- Publish a numbered proof outline (sections mapped to steps) before 15 pages of lemmas (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "The barrier in extending our understanding of the CG closure from the setting of the intersection of a strictly convex set and a rational polyhedra to the setting of a general convex set, is in dealing with irrationality." (p. 2)
- "New connections with diophantine approximations were necessary for the proof here." (p. 3)

### S043 · On maximal S-free convex sets (SIAM Journal on Discrete Mathematics 2011, doi:10.1137/100796947)
**Read level**: partial (abstract and §1 pp. 1–2, §2 key statements p. 3, Theorems 3.1, 3.2 and Prop. 3.3 pp. 4–5, §3.3 statements pp. 9–10, §4 Notes pp. 11–15; the case analysis of the proof of Prop. 3.3, pp. 5–8, not read).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Diego A. Morán R. (Georgia Tech student, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: early Georgia Tech work on intersection cuts for integer nonlinear programs (keywords "Integer nonlinear programming", p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Lovász and Basu et al. proved maximal lattice-free sets, and maximal S-free sets for S the integer points of a rational polyhedron, are polyhedra; ask whether this holds when S = P ∩ Zⁿ for an arbitrary convex P (p. 1). Motivation: S-free sets give relaxations conv(C \ int(B̂)) for mixed-integer convex sets, and polyhedrality makes them a finite disjunction, convenient for second-order-cone sets (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Every S-free convex set lies in an S-free polyhedron, a hyperplane or a halfspace (Prop. 3.3, p. 4), proved by induction on dimension, separating K from S carefully (pp. 4–5). Full-dimensional maximal S-free sets have at most 2^dim(conv(S)) facets, each facet having points of S in its relative interior or arbitrarily close outside (Theorem 3.2, p. 4; Prop. 3.10, p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: S ⊆ Zⁿ with conv(S) ∩ Zⁿ = S (abstract, p. 1); affine subspace W, V = aff(W ∩ Zⁿ) (Theorem 3.1, p. 4); relaxed in §4.2 to finite unions of such S (Corollary 4.10, pp. 14–15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Induction on dim W with Basu et al.'s affine-subspace theorem (Theorem 2.5, p. 3); Doignon–Bell–Scarf bound adapted when facets miss S in their relative interior (p. 9); Dirichlet property for strictly convex sets via Dirichlet's theorem (Prop. 4.9, p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory; generalises Basu et al. and gives an alternative proof of their result that does not use rationality of conv(S) (p. 1). Boundaries drawn by examples: recession-direction symmetry of Basu et al. fails in general (Example 4.2, p. 12), restored under a Dirichlet property (Def. 4.3, Prop. 4.4, p. 12); the facet bound fails for unions of S (hexagon example, p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a lattice-free/intersection-cut theorem from MILP asked for integer points in a general convex set, motivated by second-order-cone representable sets (pp. 1–2).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: each positive statement comes with an example where the rational-polyhedron property fails (Example 4.2, p. 12; hexagon, p. 15) and a sufficient condition restoring it (Prop. 4.4, p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: Diophantine approximation as the tool for irrational data (Prop. 4.9, p. 14; cf. D001).

**Transferable techniques**:
- After generalising a theorem, write a "Notes" section listing which properties of the special case survive and give a small explicit counterexample for each one that does not (§4, pp. 11–15).
- Note when the generalised proof also gives a new proof of the special case that avoids its key hypothesis (p. 1).

**Quotes**:
- "This motivates the search for maximal S-free convex sets." (p. 2)
- "The following example shows that this result is not true in general." (p. 12)

### S050 · A note on the split rank of intersection cuts (Mathematical Programming 2011, doi:10.1007/s10107-009-0329-y)
**Read level**: partial (abstract and §1 pp. 1–3, §2 pp. 3–4, §3 opening and outline pp. 4–5, Theorem 1 and the inductive step of its proof pp. 11–12, Prop. 2 p. 10, §3.2 statements pp. 13–16, §4 Discussion pp. 16–17).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: sole author (CORE address); thanks Laurence Wolsey (p. 17) · **Position in trajectory**: CORE postdoc note in the two-row/multi-row cut line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Split cuts are among the most successful general cuts, so compare other classes to them by split rank (p. 2); a high lower bound says it "may be better to apply this inequality directly" (p. 2). No general technique for split-rank lower bounds existed (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Build a polyhedral subset of the lattice-free set, the restricted lattice-free set Q̃ (Def. 3, p. 6); if l integer points on its boundary lie on pairwise different facets, the split rank is at least ⌈log₂ l⌉ (Theorem 1, p. 11). Applied to n-row mixing inequalities: ⌈log₂(n+1)⌉ (Prop. 3, p. 14), and to constant-capacity lot sizing (p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Corner relaxation x = f + Σ rⁱyᵢ, x ∈ Zᵐ, y ≥ 0, rational data, f ∉ Zᵐ (eq. (1), p. 2); intersection cuts from lattice-free P with f in its interior (p. 4); a "mild" condition on the columns (p. 2), stated as Assumption 1 (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Halving induction: a split disjunction partitions the tight integer points into two sides, so after t rounds a set of more than 2ᵗ points still yields a point strictly beyond the cut (γ^{I,w,t} > 0) (proof of Theorem 1, pp. 11–12); base case from the dimension of Q̃ (Prop. 2, p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: none; worked examples (Examples 1–3, pp. 5–15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: First general lower bound on the split rank of a generic cut class (p. 2). Limits stated concretely: tight on some examples, but weak when Q̃ has integral vertices and integer points in every facet; for the Li–Richard simplex the true rank is infinite while the bound is ⌈log₂(m+1)⌉ (Theorem 3, p. 17). Open: is split rank a function of Q̃ alone (p. 16)?

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper names the regime where its own bound is weak and gives the instance (Theorem 3 of Li–Richard vs ⌈log₂(m+1)⌉, pp. 16–17).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the measure (split rank) is chosen because split cuts are what works in practice (p. 2), but the question is comparative, not an explanation of a solver habit.
- New-pattern candidate: count tight integer points on distinct facets and halve per round to lower-bound a closure rank (Theorem 1, pp. 11–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a rank lower bound, find many tight integer points spread over distinct facets and argue by induction that each split disjunction can only separate them into two groups, so ⌈log₂ l⌉ rounds are needed (pp. 11–12).
- State in the discussion the family where your bound is weakest next to the strongest known bound for that family (pp. 16–17).

**Quotes**:
- "To the best of our knowledge there are no known general techniques to obtain lower bounds on the split rank of valid inequalities for mixed integer programs." (p. 4)
- "Therefore, if the lower bound on the split rank of an inequality is high, it may be better to apply this inequality directly to the linear programming (LP) relaxation of the problem instead of generating it using a sequence of split cuts." (p. 2)

### S063 · On mixed-integer sets with two integer variables (Operations Research Letters 2011, doi:10.1016/j.orl.2011.06.010)
**Read level**: full (main text pp. 1–8 read; Appendix Lemma A.1 pp. 8–9 skimmed).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Sanjeeb Dash, Oktay Günlük (IBM Research, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: follow-up to the 2010 crooked-cross-cut paper [4]; IBM faculty award acknowledged (p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Extend their 2010 result (crooked cross (CC) cuts give conv(P) with two integer variables and two constraints) to any number of constraints, to rank-2 integer coefficient matrices and to convex relaxations (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Any valid inequality cuts off a set whose x-projection is lattice-free in R², and every lattice-free convex set in R² sits inside a CC set, so the inequality is a CC cut (Lemma 2.1, pp. 2–3). Analogue of Cook–Kannan–Schrijver: CC closure = ∩ conv{(x, y) ∈ P^LP : π₁x, π₂x ∈ Z} (Theorem 3.1, pp. 3–5), giving dominance over 2-branch split cuts (Corollary 3.2, p. 5). Rank(A) = 2 reduces to two integer variables by a unimodular transformation (Theorem 4.1, pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Rational polyhedral P = {(x, y) ∈ Z^{n1} × R^{n2} : Ax + Gy = b, y ≥ 0} (p. 1); in §5, C closed, bounded, convex (Theorem 5.1, p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Rational description of a projection with strict inequalities (Lemma A.1 via Motzkin transposition, pp. 8–9) and integer rounding px < q + 1 to enlarge to a closed lattice-free set (p. 3); lifting to auxiliary integer variables z = πx to transfer Lemma 2.1 to closures (pp. 4–5); for convex C, strict separation plus a finite set of integer points in a bounding polytope (pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory. Limit stated: for compact convex C only strictly valid inequalities are shown to be CC cuts; a supporting hyperplane may need an infinite sequence of CC cuts (p. 7). Open: whether 2D lattice-free cuts give conv(P) for n1 = 2, m > 2, and whether rank-2 split cuts exist that are not CC cuts with more than two integer variables (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a MILP closure characterisation (Cook–Kannan–Schrijver) and a convex-hull result are carried to mixed-integer sets inside a compact convex set (§5, pp. 6–7).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper marks exactly where the convex-case result is weaker than the polyhedral one (p. 7) and lists the open cases (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To turn a "valid inequality" into a "cut of class X", show the region it removes projects to a lattice-free set, then embed that set in the class's lattice-free template (Lemma 2.1, pp. 2–3).
- Prove a closure equals an intersection of convex hulls by introducing auxiliary integer variables z = πx and applying the small-dimension result to the lifted set (Theorem 3.1, pp. 4–5).
