### S025 · On the Chvátal–Gomory closure of a compact convex set (Mathematical Programming 2014, doi:10.1007/s10107-013-0649-9; arXiv:1011.1710)

**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–17 (abstract, §1 introduction, §2 main result and proof outline, §3 lifting and separation, §4 two approximations of the closure, §5 induction proof) and the reference list (pp. 17–18). Version read: arXiv v1; the title and author list (Dadush, Dey, Vielma) match on p. 1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel Dadush, Juan Pablo Vielma · **Position in trajectory**: third step of a Dey line on CG closures of non-polyhedral sets: ellipsoids (Dey–Vielma, IPCO 2010, ref. [7]) → strictly convex sets (Dadush–Dey–Vielma, ref. [6]) → general compact convex sets (this paper) (p. 2). Part of the 2010-2014 "MILP theory to convex MINLP" period (SKILL.md Trajectory).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an open question stated by a classical author. Schrijver's 1980 paper ends with a discussion asking whether the CG closure is polyhedral "in real spaces", and the authors quote it verbatim (p. 2). The second motivation is convex integer programming: CG cuts defined through the support function h_K, ⟨a,x⟩ ≤ ⌊h_K(a)⌋, make sense for any closed convex K (eq. (1), p. 2). Their own earlier proof covered only intersections of a strictly convex set with a rational polyhedron, and "they do not capture the whole gamut of interesting convex sets that appear in convex IPs" (p. 2). The named obstacle is irrationality: the Lorentz cone has irrational extreme rays, and second-order-cone representable sets "naturally (not always) inherit irrational generators" (p. 2). Problem statement: show that CC(K) is finitely generated for every non-empty compact convex K (Theorem 2.1, p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when your own result covers a special class, name the single obstacle that blocks the general case (here irrationality) and show where it arises in sets people actually use (Lorentz cone) (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & proof design**: The CG closure of a compact convex set is a rational polytope, which also settles Schrijver's question for irrational polytopes (abstract p. 1; Theorem 2.1, p. 3). Kept from the rational-polyhedron and strictly-convex proofs: induction over faces and Dirichlet-based lifting of a face's CG cut to a cut for K (pp. 4, 16). Changed:
  1. the classical face property CC(F) = F ∩ CC(K) is replaced by the stronger hyperplane property CC(F_v, S) = H_v ∩ CC(K, S′) with S′ finite, because the classical one says nothing about neighbourhoods of integer points on the boundary of a strictly convex set (p. 4);
  2. a new object, the integer affine hull aff_I(A) = aff(aff(A) ∩ Zⁿ), carries the irrational part ("crucial for the proof", p. 3);
  3. the points of F_v outside aff_I(H_v) are handled separately with the Kronecker density theorem (§3.2, pp. 7–12).

  The proof outline is given as three numbered steps keyed to Sections 3, 4 and 5 (pp. 3–4). The final step uses the fact that only finitely many CG cuts can separate a vertex of the second approximation lying in the relative interior of K (p. 4, p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: if the standard inductive invariant is too weak for the general setting, strengthen the invariant (here from the face to its supporting hyperplane) so the induction carries the neighbourhood information you need (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: K ⊆ Rⁿ non-empty, compact, convex; no rationality assumed (Theorem 2.1, p. 3). The restriction to compact sets is justified in footnote 2: if the integer hull is not a polyhedron, the CG closure cannot be a rational polyhedron, and "we do not understand well when the convex hull of integer points in general convex sets is a polyhedron" (p. 2). Compactness is used for the finite subcover of the unit sphere of directions (Proposition 4.1, pp. 14–15) and for the boundedness of K in the lifting lemmas (Lemmas 3.1–3.3, pp. 4–6).
  - Transferable technique: state in a footnote why the scope stops where it does (here, what is not understood about integer hulls), so the restriction reads as a boundary rather than a convenience (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: An induction on the affine dimension of K (Theorem 5.1, pp. 15–17) built from these devices:
  - **Dirichlet lifting** (Lemma 3.3 and Proposition 3.1, pp. 4–7): Dirichlet's simultaneous approximation gives integer s_i and t_i → ∞ with ‖s_i − t_i v‖ → 0; then h_K(w + s_i) is within ε of t_i h_K(v) + h_{F_v}(w), and integrality of ⟨z_j, w_i⟩ on the integer points of aff_I(H_v) forces the lifted cut to agree with the face cut there (p. 7).
  - **Unimodular normal form** (Lemma 3.5, pp. 8–9): every v can be mapped by a unimodular T and rational scaling to (0,…,0,1,α_1,…,α_r) with {1, α_1, …, α_r} linearly independent over Q; D(v) = s + r is the dimension of the smallest rational subspace containing v.
  - **Kronecker separation** (Proposition 3.2, pp. 8–12): D(v)+1 integer vectors close in angle to v are built from scaled copies of v plus small "correction vectors" chosen with Kronecker's density theorem around the vertices of a small simplex; a conic-combination argument (Claims 3–4) shows their CG cuts separate F_v \ aff_I(H_v). The authors give the intuition in prose before the proof (p. 9).
  - **Measure-zero choice of an equation** (Lemma 4.1, pp. 12–14): integer points of {Ax = b} equal those of one equation a′x = b′, because the bad multipliers form a countable union of lower-dimensional subspaces.
  - **Compactness cover** (Proposition 4.1, pp. 14–15): neighbourhoods N_v of directions on which the face-based approximation's support function stays below h_K; a finite subcover gives CC(K, S) ⊆ K.
  - **Finitely many faces** (Lemma 4.2, pp. 15–16): a polytope inside K meets relbd(K) in finitely many of its own faces, each inside a proper exposed face of K.
  - **Finiteness by bounded norm** (pp. 16–17): any undominated new cut must separate a vertex of the approximation in relint(K); with δ the smallest inradius at those vertices, only lattice vectors of norm < 1/δ matter (eq. (29), p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: to show a closure is finitely generated, first build a finite approximation that is exact on the boundary, then show that every remaining useful cut must cut off an interior vertex, which bounds the norm of its normal (pp. 16–17).
  - Transferable technique: to approximate an irrational direction by integer vectors with control of the remainder, normalize it by a unimodular map into rational and Q-independent parts, then use Dirichlet (for one approximant) or Kronecker density (for several approximants spread around it) (pp. 5–11).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no computation).
- **D6 Positioning**: Generalizes Schrijver (rational polytopes), Dey–Vielma (rational ellipsoids) and Dadush–Dey–Vielma (strictly convex sets) (abstract p. 1; p. 2). Schrijver is credited twice: for the open question and for the observation that the intersection of the closure with the boundary matters, which "plays a crucial role in our proof" (p. 2). The relation to their own previous proof is stated plainly: "similar in parts", but "major components of the proof are new", with "new connections with diophantine approximations" (p. 3). CG cuts for convex sets are traced to Chvátal and Schrijver implicitly and Çezik–Iyengar and Dey–Vielma explicitly (p. 2); computational usefulness of CG cuts is cited, not tested (refs. [2], [9], p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Abstract of three sentences: result, open question resolved, special cases generalized (p. 1). Section 2 gives the definition, the theorem and a numbered proof roadmap with section pointers before any proof (pp. 3–4). Each section opens with what it constructs and why the classical tool is insufficient (pp. 4, 13, 15). Little hedging; the one evaluative claim is that the new techniques "we believe are important on their own" (p. 3). No conclusion section (the text ends with the proof, p. 17). Preprint typos remain ("clsoure", p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Structural theory: polyhedrality of the CG closure for every compact convex set (p. 3), a corollary generalizing CC(F) = CC(K) ∩ F to exposed faces (Corollary 3.1, p. 13). Limits: compactness (footnote 2, p. 2); the proof is existential, with no bound on the number of cuts and no algorithm (not discussed in the text). Future directions: not stated in this version.

**Method links**: Method 3 ✅ evidence: a MILP closure result ("cutting plane closures?" in the Method 3 step 1 list) carried to convex integer programs, with second-order-cone sets as the motivating case (p. 2). Method 4 ⚠ variant: the boundary is drawn as a scope statement with its reason (footnote 2, p. 2), not by tight examples or counterexamples. Method 1: not applicable (the entry is a classical open question, not a solver habit). Heuristic 9 (borrow one tool from a neighbouring field): evidence, diophantine approximation (Dirichlet, Kronecker) (pp. 3, 5, 7). New-pattern candidate: **generalization ladder to a classical open question** (ellipsoid → strictly convex → compact convex, each paper naming the obstacle the next must remove) (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Strengthen the inductive invariant from faces to supporting hyperplanes when the general setting loses the neighbourhood information (p. 4).
- Normalize an irrational vector by unimodular transformations into rational and Q-independent parts before approximating it (Lemma 3.5, pp. 8–9).
- Prove finite generation by "boundary-exact finite approximation + interior vertices bound the normal's norm" (pp. 15–17).
- Write a numbered proof roadmap keyed to section numbers right after the main theorem (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "The barrier in extending our understanding of the CG closure from the setting of the intersection of a strictly convex set and a rational polyhedra to the setting of a general convex set, is in dealing with irrationality." (p. 2)
- "New connections with diophantine approximations were necessary for the proof here." (p. 3)

### S052 · On the practical strength of two-row tableau cuts (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2014, doi:10.1287/ijoc.2013.0559)

**Read level**: full. Pp. 1–25 read closely (abstract, §1 overview and literature, §2 theory review, §3 heuristic, §4 experiments, §5 conclusions); appendix Tables 14–16 (pp. 27–30) skimmed; references (pp. 25–27) read. Version read: author preprint dated August 12, 2012; title and authors (Dey, Lodi, Tramontani, Wolsey) match on p. 1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Andrea Lodi, Andrea Tramontani, Laurence A. Wolsey · **Position in trajectory**: journal version of the IPCO 2010 paper [18] (p. 1, p. 3 fn. 2); the computational close of Dey's multi-row cut line (2005-2010 theory with Wolsey, refs. [20], [21]; Optima survey [19]). SKILL.md uses it for the "goal too ambitious" exit (Workflow F2) and the anti-pattern "Measuring cut strength with a selection rule in the loop".

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap between theory and practice in the authors' own subfield. Theory says the type 2 triangle closure is within a factor 2 of the two-row hull while the split closure can be arbitrarily far (Theorem 2.1 of Basu et al. [9], p. 5), so "at least theoretically, we expect triangle cuts based on type 2 triangles to perform significantly better than split (and therefore GMI) cuts" (p. 5). The first aim, showing that a small subset of two-row cuts closes significant gap on general MIPs, "was too ambitious" and was restricted (p. 2). The restricted question is printed as a displayed, italicised question: what characteristics must a MIP have for type 2 triangle cuts from the first tableau to be useful against or together with GMI cuts (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when the original computational goal fails, restate the paper as an explanatory question ("what characteristics of an instance make X useful?") and say in the overview that the goal was cut down (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & study design**: Two parts.
  - **Cut generator** (§3, pp. 6–9): a heuristic for type 2 lattice-free triangles. Step 1 finds two integer points on one side by solving tiny two-dimensional integer programs with an objective that bisects two rays, then iterates a validity test until the side is a facet of conv(Y²) (pp. 6–8); step 2 lifts a third continuous ray by locating the neighbouring lattice line with the extended Euclidean algorithm (pp. 8–9). Integer coefficients are strengthened by monoidal strengthening with the closed-form formula of Basu et al. [8] (p. 5). The authors admit that the bisector choice is "entirely based on the above described geometric intuition" without theoretical justification (p. 8), and that the cut is facet-defining for (2) only with three rays (§3.1, p. 9).
  - **Measurement design** (pp. 9–11): one round from the first tableau; all generated cuts added, no selection, so selection does not hide "the real strength of the cuts" (pp. 9–10). Three families: GMI (G), non-elementary two-row splits with coprime (π1, π2) ∈ [−3, 3]² (S, at most 14 per row pair), and type 2 triangles (T) (p. 10). Metrics: %gap closed and a marginal "relative importance" Imp(X) = (%gap(GST) − %gap(GST \ X))/%gap(GST) (pp. 10–11).
  - Transferable technique: report each cut family's marginal contribution to the full combination (leave-one-family-out), not only its standalone gap closed (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The relaxations (1) (integer non-basics kept) and (2) (all non-basics continuous) of two tableau rows with f fractional (pp. 1–2). The theory behind the triangles assumes continuous non-basics, unbounded basic integer variables and only non-negativity on non-basics; the paper lists these as hypotheses to test (pp. 12–13). Numerical safeguards: cuts only from rows with fractional part in (0.001, 0.999), and splits or triangles discarded if f is within 0.001 of their boundary (p. 10). Implementation: C++ with CPLEX 10.0 as LP solver (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Mostly cited theory: classification of maximal lattice-free sets in R² (Proposition 2.1, p. 4), intersection cuts (eq. (3), p. 4), fill-in / monoidal strengthening (p. 5), closure comparison (Theorem 2.1, p. 5). The paper's own analytic content is the construction of §3 and the facetness remark in §3.1 (p. 9). Otherwise n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: An instance ladder in five steps.
  1. **"Perfect" two-row instances** (Table 1, p. 11): 90 random instances where relaxation (2) is the whole feasible region; T alone closes 97.99% against G 75.10% and S 76.39%, so the heuristic captures the triangle closure well (p. 11). Sparsified versions keep the picture (Table 3, p. 13).
  2. **MIPLIB 3.0 subset** (Table 2, p. 12): 28 "manageable" instances, not selected by criterion (p. 11). The picture reverses: G 31.62%, S 33.15%, T 20.53%; adding T on top of G and S moves the average only from 39.93% to 40.49% (p. 12). The authors write that "Tables 1 and 2 present completely different pictures" and list four candidate explanations: integer non-basics, bounds, number of rows, tableau density (pp. 12–13).
  3. **Feature-isolating random families** from Atamtürk's multidimensional knapsack generator (pp. 14–15): sets A (continuous non-basics only), B (90% integer non-basics), C (as B with continuous objective coefficients divided by 100), D (as B with all non-basics continuous). The cuts for A–C are identical; only their impact changes, so the effect of integer variables is isolated (p. 15). Tables 4–7 (pp. 15–17): triangles lose to splits as integer variables gain importance; lifting helps splits more than triangles (pp. 17–18).
  4. **Bounds** (Tables 8–9, pp. 18–19): bounds "do not appear to significantly affect" relative strength (p. 19). **Density and rows** (Tables 10–12, Figure 2, pp. 19–23): a tableau "entanglement" statistic p01/p11 (elementary vs non-elementary column pairs over row pairs) is defined (p. 20); triangles beat splits only on almost fully dense tableaux (p01/p11 = 0.09), while MIPLIB averages 13.62 (p. 22).
  5. **Modified MIPLIB** (Table 13, p. 24): MIPLIB.D (five GMI rounds, binding cuts kept, to densify the tableau) and MIPLIB.D.C (non-basics relaxed to continuous) move real instances along the hypothesised axes; the direction of change matches the synthetic findings, but splits still beat triangles because density stays far from the synthetic dense case (pp. 23–24).

  Also reported: relative standard deviation of %gap per cell, to argue conclusions are stable (pp. 13, 16); number of cuts generated vs binding after reoptimization (e.g. 2740.8 triangles generated, 2.5 binding, Table 4, p. 15), read as "it may be of some importance to find the 'right' two-row cuts" (p. 16). Per-instance tables for all three MIPLIB sets in the appendix (pp. 28–30). Running times are not reported.
  - Transferable technique: derive several instance sets from one generator so that the cuts are literally the same and only one instance feature (here, which non-basics are integer, or their objective weight) changes (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: after a synthetic finding, modify the real benchmark to move it along the hypothesised axis (add binding GMI cuts to densify; relax non-basics) and check the direction of change (pp. 23–24).
  - Transferable technique: define a scalar structural statistic (here p01/p11) and plot each family's marginal contribution against it across instance sets (Figure 2, pp. 22–23).
- **D6 Positioning**: Two earlier computational studies are distinguished in three points each (p. 3): Espinoza [22] (2–15 rows, generic lattice-free shapes, CPLEX branch-and-cut; results "judged to be encouraging") and Basu–Bonami–Cornuéjols–Margot [8] (row pairs from degenerate tableaux, many rounds; warning that GMI implementations differ). The common third point: "understanding the characteristics of MIPs for which multi-constraint cuts lead to improved performance is our primary goal" (p. 3). The theory being tested is partly the authors' own (Dey–Wolsey [20], [21]; Dey–Tramontani survey [19]) and Basu et al. [9] (pp. 2, 5–6). Cut selection is set aside twice as "a crucial research topic by itself" (p. 2 fn. 1; p. 10).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The scaled-down question carries a self-aware "(simpler?)" (p. 2). Each table is followed by bullets headed "possible interpretations" (pp. 16, 18, 20–22), and the Table 1 / Table 2 contradiction is turned into four numbered differences, each ending in an explicit question (pp. 12–13). The abstract hedges one finding as "more subjectively" (p. 1) and states the negative outcome plainly: "Unfortunately these characteristics seem rarely to be present among real life instances" (p. 1). The conclusion repeats the negative verdict on real instances (p. 25) and claims a methodological contribution (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: a computational methodology for multi-row cuts and an empirical characterization (dense tableaux and "important" continuous non-basics) of when type 2 triangles help (pp. 24–25). Limits named: one class of facets only, "clearly not exhaustive" (p. 25); one triangle per pair of rays (p. 10); one round only. Future directions (p. 25): facets of (1) not obtained by lifting facets of (2); cut selection, since very few triangles bind; m-row relaxations with m > 2 for dense instances.

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ evidence: stripped instrument (one round, no selection, first tableau) and a ground-truth testbed where the relaxation equals the feasible region, then an instance ladder from synthetic to modified benchmark (pp. 9–15, 23–24). Method 1 ⚠ variant: the paper tests whether a theoretical closure ranking (Theorem 2.1, p. 5) explains practice and reports that it does not, naming the missing structure (pp. 12–13, 25); there is no proxy theorem, only a measured explanation. Method 3 ⚠ variant: the two-row relaxation is the MILP prototype of "convexify a multi-constraint substructure", and here its practical payoff is negative (pp. 24–25); this is the source of the contradiction kept in SKILL.md Method 3 (two-row MILP cuts disappointed). Heuristic 7 (theory paper followed by a measurement-only paper): evidence (pp. 1–2). Heuristic 3 / Workflow F2 (write the dead end in): evidence (p. 2, p. 25). New-pattern candidates: **feature-isolating synthetic families** (identical cuts, one changed feature, p. 15); **move the real benchmark along the explanatory axis** (p. 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure cut strength with one round, all cuts, no selection, and say why (pp. 9–10).
- Build a "perfect" instance class where the relaxation is exact to check that your separation heuristic reaches the closure before testing on real data (p. 11).
- Report Imp(X) (leave-one-family-out marginal) and generated-vs-binding counts (pp. 11, 15–16).
- Isolate features with derived instance sets whose cuts are identical (p. 15); then perturb the real benchmark along the hypothesised axis (pp. 23–24).
- Report relative standard deviation per table cell to show conclusions are not driven by a few instances (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "However such a goal was too ambitious and we have had to considerably restrict it." (p. 2)
- "Since many real life instances do not typically satisfy the above two criteria, the experiments in this paper suggest that type 2 triangle cuts are not competitive with split cuts." (p. 25)

### S084 · How good are sparse cutting-planes? (IPCO 2014, LNCS, doi:10.1007/978-3-319-07557-0_22; arXiv:1405.1789)

**Read level**: full. Pp. 1–17 (abstract, §1 preliminaries, measure and examples, §2 main results, §3 upper bound, §4 lower bound, §5 hard packing IPs, §6 extended formulations, references) and appendices A–D (pp. 17–20) read. Version read: arXiv v1 (8 May 2014), title and authors (Dey, Molinaro, Wang) match on p. 1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Marco Molinaro, Qianyi Wang · **Position in trajectory**: first paper of the sparse-cut line (SKILL.md Signature Work Anatomy: IPCO 2014 → Math. Program. → MOR 2018 → 2018 cut-selection review → B&B theory). Funded by NSF CMMI-1149400 (the CAREER award) (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: A solver habit: "most commercial MILPs solvers consider sparsity of cuts as an important criterion for cutting-plane selection and use", supported by personal communications from Achterberg, Gu and Narisetty (p. 1; refs. [1], [6], [10], pp. 16–17). Theory is missing: "Surprisingly, very few studies have been conducted on the topic of sparse cutting-planes", with only Andersen–Weismantel's zero-coefficient cuts as explicit prior work (p. 1). The real question is a trade-off between faster LPs and weaker gap closure, which is "challenging to simultaneously study" (p. 2). The paper keeps one side: how much gap closure is lost by using only k-sparse cuts, and states it as a purely geometric question about a polytope P ⊆ [0,1]ⁿ and its k-sparse closure P^k (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when a solver rule balances two objectives, keep one side of the trade-off and turn it into a geometric question about the hull (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & result design**: P^k = ∩_{|I|=k} (P + R^Ī), the intersection of all k-sparse valid cuts (p. 2). The measure is chosen first: the objective-value ratio is discarded because it can be infinite (counterexample with P = conv{(0,0),(0,1),(1,1)}), the volume ratio because P may be lower-dimensional; the Hausdorff-type distance d(P, P^k) = max_{x∈P^k} min_{y∈P} ‖x − y‖ is "well-defined for all polytopes contained in [0, 1]ⁿ" (p. 2). It bounds the depth of any normalized cut (Observation 1.1, p. 2) and is compared to √n, the cube diameter (Observation 1.2, p. 3). Three examples show one-, two- and three-phase behaviour in k (pp. 3, Figure 1). Then:
  1. **Upper bounds** (Theorem 2.1, p. 4) in terms of the number t of vertices; with polynomially many vertices and half sparsity, d(P, P^k) ≤ c√log n (p. 4).
  2. **Lower bound** for random 0/1 polytopes matching the first upper bound up to O(√log(tn)/(√log n √log t)) (Theorem 2.2, pp. 4–5).
  3. **Hard packing IPs** where d(P, P^k) stays large unless k is close to n (Theorem 2.3, p. 5).
  4. **Extended formulations**: sparse cuts in an extension are at least as good (Proposition 2.4) and sometimes much better (Proposition 2.5: d = √(n/2) for k ≤ n/2 in the original space, but 3-sparse cuts suffice in an extension) (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: before any theorem, show two or three tiny examples that exhibit qualitatively different behaviour of your measure, then make the theorem explain the "phases" (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: P ⊆ [0,1]ⁿ without loss of generality (translation and scaling), given as the convex hull of t points (p. 2, p. 4); the proofs work in [−1,1]ⁿ (p. 6). ℓ2 norm. Random models: t independent uniform points of {0,1}ⁿ with (0.5k² log n + 2k + 1)² ≤ t ≤ eⁿ and 64 ≤ k ≤ n (Theorem 2.2, p. 5); the (n, m, M)-PIP distribution of Kaparis–Letchford-type packing instances with right-hand side half the row sum (p. 5). Constants and lower-order terms deliberately not optimized (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Closest-point cut** (Observation 3.1, p. 6): the separating inequality with normal x̄ − ȳ, used in both upper bounds.
  - **Randomized sparsification of a dense cut** (Lemma 3.2, Claims 3.4–3.6, pp. 6–8): keep coordinate d_i if α|d_i| ≥ 1, else set it to sign(d_i)/α with probability α|d_i|, with α = k/(2√n); the random normal is unbiased (Proposition 3.3); Bernstein's inequality bounds sparsity and the change at every vertex, a union bound over the t vertices gives the log(4tn) term, and Markov's inequality keeps the far point separated. Observation 3.7 notes that sharper bounds for structured P could come from suprema of stochastic processes, "for instance, via the generic chaining method" (p. 8).
  - **Averaging sparse relaxations of a halfspace** (Lemma 3.8, pp. 8–9): averaging the k-sparse weakenings of ax ≤ b over all k-subsets gives (k/n)ax ≤ b + (1 − k/n)(…), the source of the 2√n(n/k − 1) bound.
  - **Lower bound by anticoncentration** (§4, pp. 9–11; Appendix C, pp. 18–19): an "α-tough" polytope definition; a new anticoncentration lemma for Bernoulli combinations far from the standard deviation, proved by bucketing coefficients of similar size and applying a known bound per bucket (Lemma 4.2); a finite set D of integer k-sparse facet normals (from Ziegler, Lemma 4.3) makes a union bound possible; the point (½ + α/(2√k) − α/k²)e lies in P^k while Σx_i ≤ n/2 + 3√(n log t) holds for P (Lemmas 4.5–4.6).
  - **Packing IPs** (§5, pp. 12–15; Appendix D): aggregate all rows into an almost-uniform cut (Lemma 5.1); order statistics of uniform variables plus Chebyshev bound the heaviest cn-subsets (Lemma 5.3); a coupling Y = ⌊(M+1)U⌋ moves from continuous to discrete (Lemma 5.4); "approximate containment" is turned into actual containment in the integer hull by averaging n² randomly thinned copies of a 0/1 point (Lemma 5.5).
  - **Extended formulation gadget** (§6.2, p. 16): a complete binary tree of flow variables with 3-sparse constraints represents Σx_i ≤ n/2.
  - Transferable technique: to prove a sparse (or otherwise structured) inequality exists, randomly round the dense separating normal so it stays unbiased, then use concentration plus a union bound over the vertices (pp. 6–8).
  - Transferable technique: to show a point is in a sparse closure, show it lies in P + R^Ī for every support I, then use one aggregated valid cut of P to bound its distance from P (pp. 11, 14–15).

### S095 · Design and verify: a new scheme for generating cutting-planes (Mathematical Programming 2014, doi:10.1007/s10107-013-0645-0)

**Read level**: full. Pp. 1–17 (abstract, §1 introduction and contribution, §2 general properties, §3 comparisons, §4 rank bounds, §5 structured problems, §6 concluding remarks) and references (pp. 17–18). Version read: author preprint dated December 29, 2012; title and authors (Dey, Pokutta) match on p. 1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Sebastian Pokutta · **Position in trajectory**: Georgia Tech colleagues (both affiliations ISyE, p. 1); journal version of the IPCO 2011 extended abstract [11] (p. 3). SKILL.md Heuristic 10 ("Design the cut you want, then verify validity") is drawn from this paper alone.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a dissatisfaction with how generic cuts are produced: a black box is applied and "cutting-planes are generated 'almost blindly'", guided only by separating the incumbent (p. 1). The proposed alternative is to design an integral inequality cx ≤ d first and then verify it by showing M(P ∩ {cx ≥ d + 1}) = ∅ with the same black box M (Definition 1, p. 1). The reason it can be stronger is the inclusion M(P ∩ {cx ≥ d+1}) ⊆ M(P) ∩ {cx ≥ d+1}, which can be strict: one can verify an inequality one cannot compute (eq. (1), p. 2). The only related prior idea is in Cook–Coullard–Turán [4], whose "theoretical and computational potential ... has not been further investigated" (p. 2). Problem: a systematic study of the verification closure ∂M (Definition 2, p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when a procedure is used as a generator, ask what happens if it is used as a certifier of a guessed object instead, and look for the inclusion that makes certification stronger (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & result design**: The verification closure ∂M(P) is the intersection of all verifiable inequalities (p. 2). Example 1 shows the gap at once: A_3 has split rank 3 but ∂SC(A_3) = ∅ (p. 2). The results are organized as a map:
  1. ∂M is "almost admissible" (all axioms except short verification) (Theorem 1, p. 5);
  2. for any admissible M, ∂M strictly refines M and is contained in GC∘M and N0 (Propositions 4–5, pp. 6–8), so even a weak operator becomes at least as strong as GC and N0;
  3. ∂GC strictly refines SC; ∂N0 is incomparable with ∂GC and with SC; ∂N strictly refines ∂N0 (Theorem 3, Propositions 6, 11–13, pp. 6, 8–10), summarized in Figure 1 (p. 7);
  4. rank: ⌊(n−1)/3⌋ ≤ rk_∂M(A_n) ≤ n − 2 (Corollary 1, Proposition 15, p. 12);
  5. structured problems: monotone polytopes have rank ≤ k+1 (Corollary 2, p. 13); clique, odd hole, odd antihole and odd wheel inequalities have rank 1 for any admissible M (Theorem 6, pp. 13–14); the subtour relaxation of the TSP keeps rank Θ(n) (Theorem 7, pp. 14–15); ∂GC(P) = P_I for every polytope in R² (Theorem 8, pp. 15–17).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The admissible cutting-plane procedures of Pokutta–Schulz [19] (validity, inclusion preservation, homogeneity, single coordinate rounding, coordinate flips and duplications, substitution independence, short verification) (Definition 3, pp. 3–4). For general IPs the authors add Strong Homogeneity and justify the addition: it holds for all well-known operators and is inherited by ∂M (Remark 1, p. 4). Every result is tagged with the axioms it uses, e.g. "(Properties 1, 2, 4)" (p. 4 and throughout). Rational polytopes throughout (p. 4); M(P) assumed closed convex; short verification for ∂M is not proved and "we do not need this property" (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: state results for an axiomatic class of operators and tag each result with exactly the axioms its proof uses (pp. 4, 6).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Perturbation to the face** (Lemma 2 and Theorem 1, p. 5): if M(P ∩ {cx ≥ d+1}) misses a face F = {ax = b}, adding τ(ax ≤ b) to the cut for large integer τ gives a verifiable cut for P that still cuts the point; this proves strong homogeneity of ∂M.
  - **Support functions on integer directions** (Lemma 3, p. 6): two compact convex sets compare through σ(c) for c ∈ Zⁿ, which lets ∂M (defined by integral cuts) be compared with M (possibly non-polyhedral).
  - **Canonical hard instance** A_n and its half-integral relatives A_n^k (pp. 2, 11): used for strictness (Proposition 4, p. 7) and for the rank lower bound by induction (Lemma 14 and Theorem 4, pp. 11–12).
  - **Lifting gadget** (Lemma 10, Proposition 9, p. 9): Q = conv(P × {1} ∪ [0,1]ⁿ × {0}) has ∂L(Q) = Q_I iff L(P) = ∅, so any polytope separating two closures yields one separating their verification closures.
  - **Face-fixing lemma** (Lemma 16, p. 12), analogue of Lovász–Schrijver Lemma 1.5: an inequality with c, d ≥ 0 valid on every face x_i = 1 is valid for ∂M; it gives the stable-set results in a few lines (pp. 13–14).
  - **Embedding by flips and duplications** of A_{⌊n/8⌋} into the TSP polytope for the Θ(n) lower bound (p. 15).
  - **Planar lattice geometry** for Theorem 8 (pp. 15–17): lattice-free parallelograms, splits containing unbounded lattice-free sets (Lemma 17, from Lovász), and "minimally infeasible points" to reduce the cases dim(P_I) = 1, 0 and P_I = ∅ to the full-dimensional case.
  - Transferable technique: to transfer a separation between two operators to their derived operators, build a gadget polytope whose derived closure is integral exactly when the original closure is empty (Lemma 10, p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no computation; the paper does not say how cuts would be designed in practice).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on the Pokutta–Schulz framework of admissible operators [19], [18] and rank results by Chvátal–Cook–Hartmann [3], Cook–Dash [5], Lovász–Schrijver [16], Eisenbrand–Schulz [13] (pp. 3, 11, 13–15). Credits [4] as the only related idea (p. 2). Uses Dadush–Dey–Vielma [9], [10] for GC closures of convex sets (pp. 4, 6). No criticism of predecessors; the "blind" generation critique is about practice in general (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The introduction motivates by practice, defines the scheme in one paragraph, and proves the payoff on a 3-dimensional example before any theory (pp. 1–2). The contribution paragraph walks through the sections and pairs strength with limits: the V-closures are strong "they not unrealistically so" (p. 3). A single figure summarizes all refinement and incomparability relations (Figure 1, p. 7). - **D8 Contribution & limits**: Theory: a new cut paradigm, its closure, its place in the hierarchy of operators and its rank on standard families (pp. 2–3). Limits: short verification not established (p. 5); no algorithm for the design step and no computation (not addressed in the text); rank stays Θ(n) for TSP (p. 14). Future direction: apply verification to mixed-integer problems to generate pure integer cuts, e.g. with projected GC cuts (p. 17).
