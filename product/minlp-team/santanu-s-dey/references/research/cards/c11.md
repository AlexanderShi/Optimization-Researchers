### S017 · Using ℓ1-Relaxation and Integer Programming to Obtain Dual Bounds for Sparse PCA (Operations Research 2022, doi:10.1287/opre.2021.2153; arXiv:1810.09062)
**Read level**: full. I read all of arXiv v3, pp. 1–42: main text pp. 1–27, appendices A–J pp. 30–42, including the appendix tables. There is no conclusion section, so the paper ends with tables and appendices. **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Rahul Mazumder (MIT ORC), Guanyi Wang (Georgia Tech, student) · **Place in trajectory**: the "certificate" line (Method 5) carried into a statistics problem. S048 cites it under its earlier title "A convex integer programming approach for optimal sparse PCA" (S048 p. 28, ref. [24]), so the retitling to name "dual bounds" can be seen.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: SPCA is NP-hard (p. 2). The existing methods are heuristics whose success conditions "make very strong and often unverifiable assumptions on the problem data", so their quality on a given dataset "is not clear" (p. 2). The only dual bounds come from SDP relaxations and low-rank spectral methods, and neither scales: Mosek fails beyond a few hundred rows, ADMM handles n ≈ 200, the spectral method can afford only rank 2, and specialised B&B handles n ≈ 100 (p. 3). The stated goal is "certificates of optimality via dual bounds, which make limited restrictive/unverifiable assumptions on the data" (p. 3). A statistics motive comes second: estimators from convex relaxations are statistically sub-optimal (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open with the certificate gap. Say what existing heuristics cannot certify and at what size the existing bounding methods stop, and give numbers for each (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: relax ‖x‖0 ≤ k to ‖x‖1 ≤ √k. The feasible region becomes convex and "all the nonconvexity is in the objective function" (p. 4). Then the objective is attacked with IP tools instead of lifting to X = xxᵀ. (i) Shift the spectrum at a heuristic lower bound λ and split it into a convex part (eigenvalues above λ) and a concave part (p. 6). (ii) For the few eigenvalues above λ, give gᵢ² a piecewise-linear SOS-2 upper approximation (pp. 6–7). (iii) Add an implied quadratic cut that "significantly improves the running-time" (pp. 7–8). The binaries number (2N+1)·|{i: λi > λ}|, which is ≪ n in experiments (p. 8). The classical model (1)–(7) has n binaries, a quadratic number of X variables and a rank constraint (p. 5). Speed-ups: perturb A so that all eigenvalues ≤ λ collapse to λ̄, which removes most g variables (Pert-Convex-IP, pp. 9–10); add a splitting point at each solution and iterate (p. 10); add a sparse cut vᵀy ≤ b(v) that is valid for SPCA but not for ℓ1-relax (p. 11).
  - Transferable: move the combinatorial constraint into a convex set so that all the nonconvexity sits in a low-dimensional part of the objective, then spend binaries only on that part (pp. 4, 6–8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A is a PSD sample covariance matrix, and λ comes from a primal heuristic, so λ ≤ λ^k(A) (p. 6). The perturbation needs λ̄ < λ; Algorithm 1 perturbs A upward to break ties (p. 9). Polynomial solvability (Prop. 3) holds only with N and |{λi > λ}| fixed (p. 8). N = 3 was chosen because θi is "significantly smaller than 1" in practice (p. 16). The authors say both Convex-IP and Pert-Convex-IP bound SPCA from above, and that Pert's bound is weaker by ρ²(λ̄ − λmin(A)) (Prop. 4, p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Theorem 1 (λ^k ≤ OPT_ℓ1 ≤ ρ²λ^k, with ρ ≤ 1 + √(k/(k+1))) is proved as a containment, T_k ⊆ ρ·conv(S_k) (pp. 11–14). The steps: write x as a sum of k-sparse pieces; an exchange argument shows that consecutive top-k blocks give the optimal decomposition (Prop. 6, p. 12); each later block is bounded by the average of the previous one (ineq. (15), p. 13); a two-case analysis on γ = ‖x_{I¹}‖₂ finishes the bound (pp. 13–14); and the maximum of a convex function over a scaled hull yields ρ² (p. 14). Prop. 2: the SOS-2 error between two active breakpoints is (Δγ)²η(1−η) ≤ (Δγ)²/4, and uniform breakpoints minimise the worst case (p. 32). Prop. 3: enumerate the (2N)^{|Ipos|} active SOS-2 patterns, each of which leaves a convex program (p. 32). Prop. 4 uses subadditivity of λ^k (p. 33). A corollary turns the theorem into a hardness statement: a θ-approximation for ℓ1-relax would give a ≈4θ-approximation for SPCA, so "the ℓ1-relaxation is also inapproximable in general" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: prove a relaxation's quality as a scaled-hull containment (relaxed set ⊆ ρ·conv(original)). For a maximised convex objective this gives the multiplicative bound in one line (p. 14).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: run on a MacBook Pro with Gurobi 7.0.2 and Mosek 8.0.0.60 (p. 14). The primal side is a truncated-power-like heuristic with 20 random restarts; Appendix I shows it matches TPM and GPM (pp. 14–15, 36–37). The limit is 7200 s, with k = 10 and 20 and three Pert settings (p. 16). Artificial instances use the generators of three earlier papers (spiked covariance, synthetic, controlling sparsity) with n = 200–1000 and m = 50 samples, two cases per size (pp. 33–35). Real instances are Pitprops, Eisen-1/2, Colon, Lymphoma and a 2000-feature Reddit stress matrix built for the paper and posted on Dey's website (pp. 35–36). The gap is relative to the heuristic primal value, gap = (UB − LB)/LB, because the optimum is unknown (p. 17). Results: the SDP solves nothing of size ≥ 300; Convex-IP works up to 500 and Pert-Convex-IP up to 2000 (p. 17). The SDP is weaker on the controlling-sparsity instances and on Eisen (p. 17). Every instance has a gap < 10% except controlling-sparsity n = 1000 (≈ 50–60%, p. 25) and Lymphoma (p. 18). On Pitprops the SDP is best (Table 10, p. 26). Ablations: a splitting-point study shows 3 → 5 points adds little (Table 1, p. 18), and ℓ1 vs an ℓ0 model with binaries shows ℓ1 is far faster and more robust in k (Tables 2–3, pp. 19–20). Baselines: Appendix J runs the published DADAL code and also a DADAL-SPCA variant the authors wrote themselves to give the SDP route its best shot; DADAL runs out of memory and DADAL-SPCA is weaker except on Pitprops (pp. 37–42). Reporting defects I noticed: in Table 2 the CovColon (5,3,1) and (10,3,1) Model-with-ℓ0 entries (and the (10,3,1) Pert entry) repeat the Eisen Data 2 numbers (p. 19). Table 16 lists CovColon at 2.37% in 28 s, while Table 11's best CovColon (Colon) gap is 6.89% (pp. 27, 41).
  - Transferable: when your relaxation competes with an established one, implement your own tailored version of the competitor's algorithm and report it next to the off-the-shelf version (pp. 37–42).
  - Transferable: when the true optimum is unknown, define the gap against your best primal value, and make the primal heuristic credible separately by comparing it with the standard heuristics (pp. 17, 36–37).
- **D6 Positioning**: places itself against the statistics literature on convex relaxations [4, 35] (p. 2) and against SDP and spectral dual bounds (p. 3). It calls its bounds "incomparable" to the SDP bounds (neither dominates, p. 3). It claims novelty for the use: "ℓ1-relaxation has never been used to computationally obtain dual bounds for SPCA" (p. 4). Theorem 1 is framed as an improvement on an exercise in Vershynin's book (p. 4). The convex/concave split is credited as "a well-studied approach" with citations (p. 6), not claimed as new.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: "2. Main results" comes on p. 3, and the proofs are deferred to §3 and the appendices. The worst case is stated candidly: an "almost 100% gap … far from ideal and would not be considered as "solving" the problem", followed at once by the empirical counterweight (p. 5). Results are hedged as "some preliminary observations" (p. 17). The abstract's superlative is qualified: "To the best of our knowledge, we obtain the best dual bounds" (p. 1). There are no closing conclusions or future work; the paper stops after the tables (p. 27).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: contributions at four levels: theory (Thm 1, Props 2–4), a model and algorithm (Convex-IP, Pert-Convex-IP, refinement, cuts), computation (bounds up to 2000 × 2000) and data (the Reddit matrix, p. 36). Stated limits: Theorem 1's tightness is unknown (p. 5), and the worst-case gap is close to 100% (p. 5). Unstated ones: only k ∈ {10, 20}; two random cases per size; the table inconsistencies noted in D5. No future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅: the dual bound is the deliverable. The tractable relaxation is made strong by structure (spectral split, SOS-2, an implied cut, sparse cuts), certified gaps are reported up to n = 2000, and the hardware is named (pp. 3, 14, 17).
- Method 4 ✅: the tightness gap is stated openly ("we do not know if this new bound is tight", p. 5), Theorem 1 is turned into an inapproximability statement (p. 5), and the paper explains why polynomial solvability of Convex-IP does not contradict that inapproximability (p. 8).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: IP machinery (piecewise-linear SOS-2 modelling, valid inequalities, a polynomial-time result for fixed dimension) is imported into a nonconvex statistics problem rather than a QCQP substructure (pp. 5–8, 11).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: no exact ceiling is available, so the yardstick is the heuristic primal value plus controlled ablations (ℓ1 vs ℓ0, number of splitting points) (pp. 17–20).
- Research Heuristic 2 (strongest baseline) ✅: a custom DADAL-SPCA was written for the competitor's SDP route (pp. 37–42). Heuristic 1 ✅: the Reddit matrix is released (p. 36).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Relax the combinatorial constraint to its convex ℓ1 surrogate, prove a multiplicative bound by scaled-hull containment, and model only the remaining nonconvex objective part with a few SOS-2 binaries (pp. 4–8, 11–14).
- Use a primal heuristic value λ to decide how many eigen-directions get binaries. Directions below λ are handled by one convex constraint, or after a perturbation by none (pp. 6, 9–10, 16).
- Run a cheap relaxation iteratively and add one breakpoint at each solution value, instead of solving one fine discretisation (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Most conventional methods for solving SPCA are heuristics with no guarantees such as certificates of optimality on the solution-quality via associated dual bounds." (p. 1)
- "While we have improved upon the bound presented in [34], we do not know if this new bound is tight." (p. 5)

### S035 · Sparse PSD approximation of the PSD cone (Mathematical Programming 2022, doi:10.1007/s10107-020-01578-y; arXiv:2002.02988)
**Read level**: full. I read all of arXiv v1 (7 Feb 2020), pp. 1–21, references skimmed. The journal version was not read. **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Grigoriy Blekherman (Georgia Tech math), Marco Molinaro, Shengding Sun (Georgia Tech, student) · **Place in trajectory**: the theory leg of the sparse-SDP cluster. Its matrix G(a,b,n) is reused as the worst-case example in S048 (S048 pp. 7–8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: an empirical surprise. Sparse SDP relaxations, which enforce PSD-ness only on k × k principal submatrices, are cheaper and "in some cases" give bounds close to the full SDP (BoxQP, OPF, p. 2), yet "theoretical understanding … remains quite limited" (p. 2). The authors say the real question (compare the objective values of (1) and (2)) "appears to be a very challenging problem". They therefore replace it with a data-independent one: how far can a unit-Frobenius-norm matrix in the k-PSD closure be from the PSD cone (pp. 1–3)? The motivation goes back to Dey's sparse-cut programme: sparse constraints are what LP solvers exploit (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when "why does this relaxation work on these instances" is intractable, drop the data and the instance-specific choice of submatrices, then ask a geometric question about the two cones with a fixed norm and normalisation (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: bound dist_F(S^{n,k}, S^n_+) above and below as a function of n and k (p. 3). Upper bounds: an averaging operator gives (n−k)/(n+k−2) (Thm 1, p. 3), and randomized sparsification of the most negative eigenvector gives 96((n−k)/n)^{3/2} for k ≥ 3n/4 (Thm 2, p. 4). Lower bounds: an explicit symmetric matrix family (Thm 3, p. 4), and a construction from restricted-isometry (RIP) matrices showing that the distance is a constant independent of n when k = rn (Thm 4, p. 5). Practice: O(n²/ε² · ln(n/δ)) randomly sampled k × k blocks achieve Thm 1's bound within a factor (1 + ε), and 2-designs achieve it with n(n−1) blocks (Thm 5, pp. 5–6). The paper notes that the SOCP relaxation of [26] is the case k = 2 and that the dual cone is the factor-width-k cone (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: no data. The measure is the Frobenius norm, used both for distance and normalisation (p. 3), and the distance is at most 1 because 0 is PSD (p. 3). Thm 2 needs n ≥ 97 and k ≥ 3n/4, and these are used inside the proof (p ≥ 1/2, Chebyshev's 16/n, pp. 11–12). Thm 4 needs r < 1/93 (p. 5). Constants are explicit and not optimised (96, 93).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - An averaging operator: average all k-block restrictions of M, which is PSD because each restriction is, then rescale to equalise the diagonal and off-diagonal shrink factors (§5, p. 8).
  - Cauchy interlacing: every M ∈ S^{n,k} has at most n−k negative eigenvalues (Prop. 2, p. 10). Combined with the exact projection formula dist = √Σλi² (Prop. 1, p. 9), this gives dist ≤ √(n−k)·λ1 (Prop. 3, p. 10).
  - Characterisation by k-sparse test vectors (Observation 1, p. 10).
  - The probabilistic method: randomly sparsify the negative eigenvector and check three properties with a binomial-median fact, Chebyshev and Markov, then take a union bound below 1 (Lemmas 1–4, pp. 11–13).
  - An explicit matrix family G(a,b,n) whose spectrum is known in closed form (Prop. 4, Cors 1–2, pp. 13–14).
  - A random Bernoulli RIP matrix W with M = −(1−δ)I + WᵀW, where the kernel of W supplies n−m negative eigenvalues (Lemmas 5–6, pp. 15–16). The paper adds that it can "numerically verify" that the RIP probability is ≥ 0.51 (p. 16).
  - Chernoff and union bounds over the pair-inclusion fractions f_ij for the sampling result (Lemmas 8–9, pp. 17–19).
  - Transferable: to prove a cone with only local constraints is close to the global cone, average the locally certified pieces into a globally certified object and measure the rescaling error (p. 8).
  - Transferable: to lower-bound such a gap, look for an object from another field that "approximately preserves" what the local constraints test (here RIP preserves the norms of sparse vectors) (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a. There are no experiments; the only numerical element is the check of the probability bound (12) at the stated parameters (p. 16).
- **D6 Positioning**: framed as the first theory for practice reported by others (Qualizza–Belotti–Margot; Baltean-Lugojan et al.; Sojoudi–Lavaei) and by the authors (Kocuk–Dey–Sun OPF; Dey–Kazachkov–Lodi–Munoz sparse QCQP cuts) (p. 2). It links to Dey's earlier sparse-inequality theory for polytopes and MILPs [16–18] (p. 2), to factor width and scaled diagonal dominance [7, 29] and to DSOS/SDSOS [1] (pp. 2–3). Kim–Kojima matrix completion [22] is named as a direction for choosing submatrices (p. 7). The tone is constructive: earlier work is described, not criticised.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the abstract uses "Surprisingly" for the empirical fact and states the simplification move openly (p. 1). All results come in §2 "Our results", each with a proof idea in plain words, before the background and proofs (pp. 3–6). The "Conclusion and open questions" section is §3, placed before the proofs, with a regime table (Table 1, p. 6). Tightness is assessed result by result ("It appears that for k close to n this upper bound is not tight", p. 4; "Unfortunately, our next lower bound shows that this is not that case", p. 5).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: theory only: matching bounds in two regimes, 2 ≤ k ≤ √n and k = n − c (p. 6), plus a sampling result. Stated open questions: the bounds are "not quite close when viewed as a function of the ratio k/n" (p. 6), and submatrices should be chosen deterministically, perhaps through 2-designs, for which no explicit construction is known (pp. 6–7). Limits: the data-independent measure says nothing about a particular SDP's objective, which the authors acknowledge when they simplify (p. 2). No return to the empirical instances in this paper.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅: a textbook instance. An unexplained practice (sparse SDP bounds close to the SDP) becomes a proxy theorem through an explicitly data-independent measure, because the real objective-value comparison "appears to be a very challenging problem" (pp. 1–3). Step 7 (return to data) is not done here; S048 does it.
- Method 4 ✅: upper bounds are paired with matching lower bounds by regime, untightness is flagged (p. 4), and the uncovered regime is left as an explicit open question (p. 6).
- Research Heuristic 5 ✅ (start from a result better than it should be, p. 1). Heuristic 9 ✅ (borrow one tool: RIP from compressed sensing, 2-designs from combinatorics, pp. 5–6, 14–16). Heuristic 8 ✅ (Blekherman as the algebra specialist; coauthor list p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Present results as a regime table with upper bound, lower bound and the simplified form in each regime, and mark the regime where they do not meet as the open question (p. 6).
- When a closure has exponentially many local constraints, prove that a polynomial random sample of them keeps the bound within (1+ε) with high probability, and point to designs for a deterministic version (pp. 5–6, 17–19).

**Quotes**:
- "In order to simplify the question, we arrive at a data independent version of it, where we compare the sizes of SDP cone and the k-PSD closure" (p. 1)
- "Ideally we would like to compare the objective function values of (1) and (2), but this appears to be a very challenging problem." (p. 2)

### S048 · Cutting plane generation through sparse principal component analysis (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2022, doi:10.1137/21m1399956)
**Read level**: full. I read the whole author preprint dated 18 Feb 2021, pp. 1–31, references skimmed. The journal version was not read. **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Aleksandr M. Kazachkov, Andrea Lodi, Gonzalo Muñoz · **Place in trajectory**: the computational leg of the cluster. It brings back the SPCA machinery of S017 (ref. [24]) and the worst-case matrix of S035 (ref. [9]) to make LP cuts that emulate the SDP for QCQPs.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: general QCQP methods "fail to scale … even with just a few hundred variables". The SDP relaxation is strong but "relies on memory-intensive algorithms" (p. 1). Eigenvector cuts linearise it, but "the vector v will typically be dense", which causes numerical instability (p. 4). The effect of cut sparsity is "largely underexplored for QCQPs" (p. 2). Goal: a linear outer approximation that matches the SDP and can be embedded in spatial B&B (p. 3).
  - Transferable: define the target as "emulate relaxation R with objects of class C". The yardstick (gap closed relative to R) then falls out of the problem statement (pp. 3, 10).
- **D2 Core idea**: the separation problem for a cut with at most k nonzeros is sparse PCA. Prop. 1 reduces decision-SPCA to it, so separation is NP-hard (pp. 6–7). Lemma 1 shifts by λmax to turn the most-violated-cut problem into an SPCA instance for any M̃ (p. 7). The SPCA heuristic TPower therefore becomes the oracle (p. 15). Prior methods sparsify dense eigenvectors (Qualizza et al.) or pick supports with a neural net (Baltean-Lugojan et al.) (p. 6). Multiple cuts per round without re-solving the LP come from Algorithm 1. It takes the oracle's support, adds the most negative eigenvector of that block, deflates M by a rank-one PSD update, and calls the oracle again (pp. 13–14). From each support, all eigencuts are added (Lemma 2, p. 13). HYBRID adds dense cuts until the LP solve time reaches a switching time, then switches to sparse cuts (p. 17).
  - Transferable: when your separation problem is NP-hard, look for an equivalent well-studied problem in another field and use its best practical heuristic as the oracle (pp. 6–7, 15).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: QCQPs with a McCormick LP as the base relaxation. The results hold for any valid linear starting relaxation (footnote 1, p. 3). The ceiling is the SDP + McCormick value (p. 10). Fixed choices: k = ⌊0.25(n+1)⌋ (p. 18); NumCutsPerIter = 5n and MaxNumSupports = 100; HybridSwitchingTime = min{10 s, 100 × initial LP time}; TPower initialised with the smallest-eigenvalue eigenvector; explicit numerical tolerances (p. 18). The authors say these "heuristic parameter choices can be better tuned for larger BoxQP instances" (p. 23).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: hardness by a one-line reduction ("The proof is trivial", Prop. 1, p. 6). The spectrum is shifted so that an indefinite matrix can be treated as SPCA (Lemma 1, p. 7). Worst-case examples come from the authors' own S035: G(a,b) has every k-block PSD but is not PSD, and a block-diagonal sparse version shows that sparsity in M does not help (Examples 1–2, pp. 7–8). Finiteness of Algorithm 1 is proved by counting matrix signatures under PSD rank-one updates (Lemma 3, pp. 13–14). The validity of the generated cuts and the order in which they cut are proved by induction (Lemma 4, pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable: after a negative worst-case result, state its scope in one sentence ("they are only informing us that we cannot hope to devise an efficient method that will work in the worst case", p. 8) and move to empirical evidence.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: first, enumeration on three BoxQP instances with n = 30 and k = 4. All 31,465 supports are enumerated and one round of cuts is added; gap closed is measured against the SDP (pp. 9–12). The design is organised by three questions (p. 9). Findings: a single support closes < 5% of the gap (Table 1, p. 10); all sparse cuts beat the 14–16 dense cuts, but number in the thousands (p. 12); the top 5% of supports give 60–77% of the full gain (p. 12). Algorithm 1 is benchmarked against the enumeration ceiling and comes within 1% of violation-based selection (Table 3, p. 15). Main study: 111 BoxQP instances, including n = 200 and 250 instances the authors generated "using the same instance-generation code" (p. 19); 135 Biq instances, with the excluded instances listed by name (p. 20); and 151 MaxCut instances (p. 20). Two metrics, gap closed and last LP time, are averaged by size group (Tables 4–6, pp. 21–25). The limit is 1 h, single-threaded, on the Béluga cluster (p. 16). Extended 1-day runs on one instance per family show DENSE stalling at 88.1% against 96.4% for SPARSE (pp. 23, 25). Failures are reported and explained: SPARSE lags on the largest BoxQP (p. 21); on three spar250 instances HYBRID trails DENSE because there are 10× more sparse cuts (pp. 22–23); on the largest MaxCut all methods close only 5–6% (p. 24). Preliminary design choices are written in: Barrier beats Simplex despite warm starts (pp. 17–18), and an orthogonal-support scheme was tried and dropped (p. 13). ILP approaches for BoxQP are deliberately not compared, because they cut through the SDP cone (p. 19).
  - Transferable: enumerate exhaustively on small instances first and let the ceiling decide the algorithm's knobs, such as cuts per round and whether dense cuts are allowed (p. 9). Then benchmark the scalable heuristic against that enumerated ceiling (p. 15).
  - Transferable: report two axes, bound strength and the cost of the last relaxation solved, by size group. Add one long-horizon run to separate "slower" from "stalled" (pp. 21–25).
- **D6 Positioning**: the literature review is limited to cuts that use X = xxᵀ (p. 5). Sherali–Fraticelli [49] and Qualizza et al. [45] are treated as predecessors, and the paper sets out to "substantially expand" on Baltean-Lugojan et al. [5] (p. 2). It is distinguished from chordal/matrix-completion sparsity (Fukuda et al.), which needs structured sparsity (p. 4), and from intersection cuts and ILP approaches, which cut through the SDP constraint and are called "rather complementary" (pp. 5–6, 19). The authors' own S035 and S017 are cited as working papers [9, 24] (pp. 27–28).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the contributions paragraph is placed early (p. 4). The design is driven by the three questions (p. 9), and an explicit "Summary of enumeration experiments" closes with what was not considered: interactions among supports (p. 12). Takeaways are hedged ("Our takeaway is that, although our heuristic parameter choices can be better tuned…", p. 23). One summary claim is slightly stronger than its table: the summary calls violation "a good and efficient heuristic for cut selection" (p. 12), but in Table 2 violation selection trails gap-based selection on 2 of the 3 instances (p. 11), as the same page itself notes "on average". The conclusion gives numbers (">90% of the gap" for n ≤ 100; the dense LP 2–3× slower, p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: contributions are a theoretical link (separation = SPCA, NP-hardness, worst-case examples), algorithms (Algorithm 1, HYBRID) and a computational study on three families. Stated limits and future work: "all entries in X are created and stored", which blocks scaling and embedding in spatial B&B (p. 26); interactions among supports are not scored (p. 12); and the parameters are untuned for large instances (p. 23). The paper reports cutting-plane relaxations only and no full B&B run.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅: the ceiling is computed first by exhaustive enumeration of all supports, with the SDP value as the reference. In the paper's words, "the enumeration experiments justify how many sparse cuts we should generate at each round" (p. 9). The heuristic is then reported as a ratio to that ceiling (p. 15).
- Method 5 ✅: the most tractable relaxation (LP) is made to emulate the SDP so that it can go into spatial B&B, and gaps are reported at benchmark scale with the hardware named (pp. 3, 16, 26).
- Method 4 ✅: NP-hardness and the authors' own worst-case matrices (pp. 6–8) are paired with the empirical study, and their scope is stated (p. 8).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the paper measures a trade-off (sparse vs dense cuts) that the companion theory paper S035 had made into a proxy theorem, so it serves as the Method 1 step-7 return to practice for that line.
- Research Heuristic 1 ✅ (BoxQP instances generated at n = 200, 250, p. 19). Heuristic 3 ✅ (dead ends written in: orthogonal supports p. 13, Simplex vs Barrier pp. 17–18). Heuristic 9 ✅ (TPower from statistics, p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use a deflation-style rank-one PSD update of the point being separated to get many distinct sparse cuts from one LP solution, with finiteness proved by counting signatures (pp. 13–14).
- Use a hybrid schedule: put the strong-but-heavy cut family first, then switch to the light family when the LP solve time crosses a threshold tied to the initial LP time (pp. 17–18).
- List every excluded instance by name in a footnote, with the exclusion rule (p. 20).

### Cross-card note (batch level)
The three papers form one loop, written within about 18 months. S035 (Feb 2020) gives the theory for a practice (sparse SDP relaxations). S048 (2021) cites S035's G(a,b) as its worst-case example and S017 (then titled "A convex integer programming approach for optimal sparse PCA") as working papers [9, 24] (S048 pp. 7–8, 27–28). S048 then turns S017's subject, SPCA, into its own separation oracle (S048 pp. 6–7). This is a candidate pattern: programme cross-feeding. A construction, problem or tool from one paper is reused as the counterexample, oracle or motivation of a parallel paper.
