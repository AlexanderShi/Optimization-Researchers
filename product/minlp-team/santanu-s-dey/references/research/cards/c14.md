### S076 · On sparse canonical correlation analysis (Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 37 2024, DOI 10.52202/079017-0342 / arXiv:2401.00308)
**Read level**: full. Read: the arXiv v1 text (pp. 1–24), including Appendix A (proofs) and Appendix B (greedy/local-search pseudocode). The NeurIPS camera-ready was not read, so any later changes to the experiments or theorems are unknown.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yongchun Li, Weijun Xie (both Georgia Tech ISyE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: continues the sparse-PCA dual-bound line ([10], [11] on p. 14 are Dey's own sparse PCA papers) and moves it to a three-matrix problem. The acknowledgement says Rahul Mazumder introduced the problem and shared data (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper starts from the classical CCA (Hotelling), which is hard to interpret on high-dimensional data, and from SCCA methods that use ℓ1 penalties, which "only approximate the sparsity requirement" (p. 3). The problem is stated with exact ℓ0 constraints, covariance blocks B, C, and A, and B, C allowed to be singular because covariance matrices are low rank when samples are few (pp. 1–2). The NP-hardness argument is that SCCA contains sparse PCA, sparse SVD and sparse regression as special cases (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: justify the hardness of a new problem and the use of an exact MIP approach in one paragraph, by listing the known NP-hard problems it contains as special cases and the parameter settings that recover each one (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: For a fixed support, the inner CCA has a closed form. The CCA and its SDP relaxation share the value σmax(√B†A√C†) (Prop. 1, pp. 4–5). SCCA therefore becomes a combinatorial support selection over three submatrices at once (Thm 1, pp. 5–6). The same closed form drives (a) greedy and local search heuristics (App. B, p. 24), (b) an exact MISDP with big-M links X_ii ≤ M_ii z_i (Thm 2, p. 7), and (c) a Benders-type branch-and-cut whose cuts come from a closed-form dual solution (Prop. 3, pp. 8–9). What changes from sparse PCA/SVD: three principal and non-principal submatrices are selected at once rather than one submatrix of A (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when the inner continuous problem has a closed-form value for a fixed support, write the problem as "max over supports of a closed-form score". That one formula then gives you heuristics (greedy, swap local search), an exact MISDP, and a cut-generation oracle (pp. 5–9, 24).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Only PSD of the joint covariance is assumed. B, C may be singular, which the paper names as the difference from prior SCCA work that assumed B, C positive definite (pp. 2–3, 5). The bounds M1, M2 on ‖x*‖², ‖y*‖² use the smallest nonzero eigenvalue and "the smallest nonzero singular value of all the submatrices of the zero eigenvectors" (Prop. 2, p. 7). The paper does not say how to compute s_min (reading note). The low-rank results assume r = rank(B) and r̂ = rank(C) are fixed (Thm 3, p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (1) Generalised Schur-complement PSD characterisation (Lemma 1 from [12], p. 4) plus Lagrangian duality. This gives exactness of the SDP relaxation of CCA without SVD and without positive-definiteness (Prop. 1; App. A.1, pp. 17–18). (2) Bounded-solution construction in the singular case: an optimal x is moved along null-space eigenvectors, solving a minimal nonsingular subsystem, to get a sparse solution that is "not too far" away (Prop. 2; App. A.2, pp. 7, 18–19). (3) A sparsity-from-rank argument: subtracting null-space directions zeroes n − r coordinates without changing the objective, because q_i⊤A = 0 (Thm 3; App. A.4, pp. 21–22). This gives an O(n^{r−1} m^{r̂−1}) algorithm (Cor. 2, p. 10). (4) Rank-one A separates into a product of two subproblems, each reduced to sparse regression, hence NP-hard (Thm 4; App. A.5, pp. 10–11, 22–23). (5) An ε-perturbed dual solution gives closed-form multipliers θ* = f(z)/2 and λ* as a largest eigenvalue, and therefore closed-form cuts (App. A.3, pp. 19–20). The final step "follows from [23][theorem 7]", the Li–Xie sparse-SVD paper (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: to prove that a QCQP's SDP relaxation is exact with singular data, use the Schur-complement lemma with pseudo-inverses and the range condition (I − BB†)A = 0. Do not assume positive definiteness to make the SVD go through (pp. 4–5, 17).
  - Transferable technique: to get closed-form Benders cuts for a big-M MISDP, find a dual solution in closed form with an ε-perturbation, then let ε → 0 using closedness of the dual feasible set (pp. 19–20).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Synthetic data only. B0 = B̂B̂⊤ + I, C0 likewise, A0 = λB0uv⊤C0 with planted sparse u, v; then 5,000 samples and sample covariances (p. 11). Setup: Python 3.6, Gurobi 9.5.2, MOSEK 10.0.29, "a PC with 10-core CPU, 16-core GPU, and 16GB of memory", 1 h limit (pp. 11–12). Table 1: greedy and local search reach the same LB on all instances; the SDP relaxation (5) gap is ≤ 8.16%. Exact branch-and-cut solves only n = m ≤ 20, with 3,562 s at 20/20/10/10. The authors attribute this to the weak relaxation of formulation (9) (p. 12). Table 2 (rank-one approximation of A): the perspective relaxation beats the SDP bound in gap and time, with all instances in ≤ 15 s and gaps ≤ 11.3%. The exact MIQP route solves 100×100 (pp. 12–13). Reading notes: Table 1 row 120/120/10/10 lists v̂ = 0.942 with LB 0.501 but gap 7.86%, which is internally inconsistent (p. 12). Where v* is unknown, the gap is measured against the heuristic LB (p. 12). No real data, no failure analysis beyond the one sentence on the weak bound.
  - Transferable technique: report three columns per instance (heuristic LB, relaxation UB with gap, exact optimum where reachable), so that the reader can see where the certificate stops scaling (Tables 1–2, pp. 12–13).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against the ℓ1/penalty SCCA line ([33], [35], [7], ...), which approximates ℓ0 (pp. 2–3). Against Watanabe et al. [34], whose branch and bound needs B, C positive definite (p. 3). Against sparse PCA/SVD algorithms, which "preclude the direct application" because SCCA selects three submatrices (p. 3). Prop. 1 is said to generalise [7] and to differ from the SVD-based proofs of [26, 31, 38] (p. 5). Self-citation of the sparse-PCA dual-bound work [10, 11] sits among the convex-relaxation references (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Numbered contribution list with a section pointer for each (p. 2). "To the best of our knowledge" hedges priority claims (pp. 2, 5). Each result is followed by a "Remarkably" paragraph or a positioning paragraph (p. 5). The text has many unrepaired slips. Theorem 1 swaps the index ranges S1 ⊆ [m], S2 ⊆ [n] (p. 5). Theorem 4 writes A := a⊤b (p. 11). The sentence after Thm 3 calls s1 ≤ r a "sufficient condition" where the theorem needs s1 ≥ r (p. 10). Algorithm 2 is titled "for SSVD" and reuses i1, j1 in its y-loop (p. 24). Table 2's text points to "the last column of Section 4.2" (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions are formulation-level (combinatorial, MISDP, MILP with closed-form cuts), complexity-level (low-rank polynomial cases, rank-one NP-hard) and computational (pp. 2, 10–13). Limits are stated only implicitly. The exact method stalls beyond n = 20 because of a weak relaxation bound (p. 12), and the scalable exact route needs a rank-one approximation of A, which changes the problem (p. 12). No future-work section in v1 (p. 13 ends with acknowledgements).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. Dual bounds are the deliverable next to heuristics. The SDP relaxation (5) gives gaps ≤ 8.16% (p. 12). The perspective relaxation is chosen because it is "stronger and easier to solve than the SDP relaxation" (p. 11), which matches the LP/SOCP-before-SDP taste. The hardware is named (p. 11).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The complexity picture is demarcated: polynomial when rank(B), rank(C) are fixed (Thm 3, Cor. 2, p. 10), NP-hard already for rank-one A (Thm 4, p. 11), and SCCA reduces to CCA when s ≥ rank (p. 10).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. There is no ground truth beyond n = 20. Larger-instance gaps are measured against heuristic lower bounds, and the stripped-instrument protocol of Method 2 is absent (p. 12).
- new-pattern candidate: closed-form inner value → support-selection reformulation (pp. 5–9, 24).
- new-pattern candidate: special-case embedding as the hardness argument (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Closed-form score per support → greedy + swap local search + exact MISDP + closed-form Benders cuts, all from one formula (pp. 5–9, 24).
- Schur complement with pseudo-inverses instead of a positive-definiteness assumption, for exactness proofs with rank-deficient data (pp. 4–5, 17).
- For a rank-one coupling matrix, check separability into a product of two simpler subproblems before attacking the coupled problem (pp. 10–11).
- Low-rank data → the sparsity constraint is redundant once s ≥ rank, by moving along null-space directions; this gives an n^{r−1} enumeration algorithm (pp. 10, 21–22).

**Quotes**:
- "It is seen that the greedy and local search algorithms are quite scalable, and the SDP relaxation (5) yields a tight upper bound with an optimality gap at most 8.16%." (p. 12)
- "One reason may be because SCCA (9) has a weak relaxation bound." (p. 12)

### S077 · Aggregations of quadratic inequalities and hidden hyperplane convexity (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2024, DOI 10.1137/22m1528215 / arXiv:2210.01722)
**Read level**: full. Read: the arXiv v2 text (pp. 1–27), all sections including the proofs in §§4–10 and the references. The SIAM published version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Grigoriy Blekherman and Shengding Sun (School of Mathematics, Georgia Tech, p. 27). The acknowledgement thanks Gonzalo Muñoz and Felipe Serrano (p. 26) · **Position in trajectory**: direct sequel to Dey–Muñoz–Serrano [8] (three quadratics under PDLC). It answers the finiteness question left open there (p. 2). The paper is part of the "QCQP rank-one substructures and aggregations of quadratics (2019–2024)" anatomy in SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is the Farkas lemma. In LP, every implied linear inequality is an aggregation, and aggregations are also used for cuts in IP ([2], Dey's own packing/covering paper) and MINLP ([10]) (p. 1). The question is when the convex hull of a set cut out by m quadratic inequalities equals the intersection of "good" aggregations, meaning combinations with at most one negative eigenvalue (pp. 1, 3). Three gaps are named: a general sufficient condition beyond m = 2 [22] and m = 3 with PDLC [8], finiteness of the aggregations, and closed inequalities without topological assumptions (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: open a convexification paper by naming the LP fact (Farkas) whose nonlinear analogue you study. Then list the known cases by number of constraints and say exactly which three gaps the paper closes (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Define hidden hyperplane convexity (HHC): the image of the quadratic map restricted to every linear hyperplane is convex (Def. 2.1, p. 4). The separation proof of [8] needs convexity only on the one hyperplane that separates a point x̃ from conv(S), and HHC supplies it for any m (Thm 2.9, pp. 6, 16–17). HHC is a property of the span of the matrices (Lemma 2.5, p. 5). It is equivalent to hidden convexity of all (n−1)-dimensional compressions (Obs. 2.6, p. 5), so Dines' and Calabi's theorems give the m = 2 and m = 3+PDLC cases at once (Cor. 2.7, p. 6). What is kept from [8]: the "same road-map", with only the S-lemma step replaced (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: when a proof uses a classical global property (hidden convexity) only on one hyperplane, define the "restricted-to-every-hyperplane" version as the hypothesis. Then show the old theorems are corollaries through a compression lemma (pp. 4–6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The main sets are open quadratic inequalities with m ≥ 2, n ≥ 3 (p. 3). Main theorem: HHC plus ∅ ≠ S and conv(S) ≠ ℝⁿ (Thm 2.9, p. 6). HHC is strong: linearly independent Q_i require n − 1 ≥ m (Remark 2.2, p. 4). Finiteness additionally needs PDLC for every triple (Thm 2.18, p. 10). The closed case needs HHC and Q_λ ≠ 0 for every nonzero λ ≥ 0. The authors call this "easy to check computationally using linear programming" (Remark 2.25, p. 13). They prefer it to the topological assumption of [14], which is "hard to check computationally" (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  1. Separation on the homogenised hyperplane (proof of Thm 2.9, pp. 16–17). A point outside conv(S) gives an affine separator, and its homogenisation H misses S^h (Lemma 5.4 from [8]). HHC makes image f^h|_H convex and disjoint from the open negative orthant. Separating them yields λ ≥ 0 and µ = 0, so Q_λ is PSD on H, and Yildiran's Theorem 5.1 then gives exactly one negative eigenvalue with H splitting the two cones.
  2. Non-trivial HHC class (Thm 2.8; §4, pp. 14–16). The image of (‖x‖², x1x1, …, x1xn) is the boundary of a Lorentz cone (Prop. 4.1). A linear map with nontrivial kernel sends the boundary of a line-free closed convex set onto the image of the whole set (Lemma 4.2), which is convex. Remark 4.3 is a counterexample when the set contains a line. Gram–Schmidt in the f0 inner product moves to the general case (Prop. 4.6).
  3. Tightness pair (p. 7). Example 2.11 shows hidden convexity is not sufficient: all good aggregations are multiples of f1+f2+f3, which leave x1, x2 unrestricted. Theorem 2.12 shows HHC is not necessary: diagonal maps can fail HHC (Example 2.4, pp. 4–5) yet always give the hull by finitely many aggregations.
  4. Diagonal case by substitution (§8, pp. 20–23). With y = x∘x, S becomes an open polyhedron. conv(S) = {x : x∘x ∈ P′} (Prop. 8.1), and LP duality on the lifted polyhedron shows that P′'s facets are nonnegative aggregations (Prop. 8.4). Farkas and Motzkin give the emptiness certificate.
  5. Finiteness by improvement and support reduction (§9, pp. 23–26). Adding a PSD combination θ keeps a good aggregation good and makes it tighter, with Weyl's inequality bounding the negative eigenvalues (Prop. 9.1). PDLC on triples lets the support be reduced to ≤ 2 (Prop. 9.2). On a two-element support, the good aggregations form ≤ 2 intervals whose endpoints are generalised eigenvalues, so the two outer endpoints suffice (Props. 9.5–9.6, using Yildiran's Lemma 9.4 hyperplane). The total is m² − m, and 6 for m = 3 (Cor. 2.20, p. 10).
  6. Closed inequalities via the interlacing theorem (Thm 2.24, pp. 12–13, 26).
  - Transferable technique: to prove a finite description, first show that any certificate can be improved monotonically inside the certificate cone (here by adding a PSD combination). Then use the improvement to push the certificate to a low-dimensional face where the candidates can be counted (pp. 10, 23–24).
  - Transferable technique: for a "sufficient but not necessary" theorem, give one example showing the weaker natural condition fails and one structured class showing the new condition is not necessary. Together they bracket the hypothesis (pp. 7, 13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a. The paper has no computations. The only plots are illustrations of Examples 2.16, 2.21 and 2.23 (Figures 1–3, pp. 9, 11, 13).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper is said to "simultaneously generalize" Yildiran [22] (m = 2) and Dey–Muñoz–Serrano [8] (m = 3, PDLC), and the authors claim it generalises "all known results regarding aggregations defining convex hulls" (pp. 1–2). It answers the finiteness question raised in [8] (pp. 2, 10). It drops the topological assumption of Modaresi–Vielma [14] (p. 2). It places itself in the hidden-convexity tradition: Dines, Brickman, Calabi, Polyak, the S-lemma survey of Pólik–Terlaky (p. 2). It relates the sphere/halfspace case to trust-region variants [21, 1, 12] and is explicit about what it does not do: conv(S) only, not the lifted hull and not algorithms (pp. 8–9). Two citations are unresolved "[?]" in v2 (pp. 9, 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The goals are a three-bullet list in the introduction (p. 2). The layout is "In Section 2 we present all our main results ... Proofs ... are given in Sections 4-10" (p. 3), with every theorem stated and explained before any proof. Each theorem is followed by its tightness example. A short conclusions section turns the gaps into three named conjectures (Conj. 3.1–3.3, pp. 13–14). Honest small admissions: "We do not know whether an example with n linearly independent constraints (without PDLC) exists." (p. 9), and the restrictive conditions are acknowledged (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is purely theoretical: a new sufficient condition (HHC), a finiteness bound (m² − m; 6 for three quadratics with PDLC; 2m when the span is three-dimensional, Prop. 2.22, p. 12), linear-plus-sphere sets with the bound |P||N| + |P| + |Z| (Thm 2.15, p. 8), and a closed-inequality result (Thm 2.24). A practical consequence is noted: finitely many good aggregations imply an SOC-representable closure of the hull (Remark 2.19, p. 10). Limits are stated concretely as open conjectures. Some HHC set may need infinitely many aggregations (Conj. 3.1). Six aggregations may be needed for m = 3, though the worst example found needs four (Conj. 3.2, Example 2.21). Non-emptiness certificates have an unresolved case (Conj. 3.3) (pp. 13–14). Reading note: the paper gives no general test for whether a given quadratic map satisfies HHC. Only sufficient classes are given (Cor. 2.7, Thm 2.8, Cor. 7.1).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. The IP aggregation paradigm (Farkas; [2] aggregation cuts for packing and covering IPs) is carried into QCQP (p. 1). Several quadratics are convexified jointly, not term by term. The real-algebraic step (hidden convexity, Calabi, Dines) is done with a mathematician coauthor, as in Method 3 step 6 / Heuristic 8 (pp. 2, 27).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence. The demarcation is part of the headline. Hidden convexity is shown not sufficient and HHC not necessary (pp. 7, 13). A counterexample with n+1 sphere/linear constraints without PDLC is given (Example 2.17, p. 9). Open boundaries are stated as conjectures (pp. 13–14). Unknown tightness is admitted (p. 9).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. There are no dual-bound computations. The tie to cheap relaxations is one remark: a finite number of aggregations ⇒ the closure of the hull is SOC-representable (Remark 2.19, p. 10). Checkability of conditions by LP or SDP is stressed (pp. 8, 13, 19), in line with Taste warning sign 1 (unverifiable assumptions).
- new-pattern candidate: hyperplane-restricted strengthening of a classical property so that an existing proof road-map extends (pp. 4–6, 16–17).
- new-pattern candidate: improve-then-reduce-support argument for finiteness of certificates (pp. 10, 23–26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Restrict a classical hypothesis to the object the proof actually touches (every hyperplane), and show it depends only on the linear span. Old theorems then follow as corollaries (pp. 4–6).
- Prove convexity of a quadratic image by recognising it as the boundary of a known cone (Lorentz) and projecting along a nontrivial kernel. Give the counterexample when the cone contains a line (pp. 14–15).
- Bracket a sufficient condition with a "weaker condition not sufficient" example and a "new condition not necessary" class, then turn what is left open into numbered conjectures (pp. 7, 13–14).
- Prefer hypotheses that can be checked by LP or SDP over topological ones, and say so explicitly when choosing them (pp. 2, 13).

**Quotes**:
- "To the best of our knowledge, this result generalizes all known results regarding aggregations defining convex hulls." (p. 1)
- "Unfortunately, this assumption is hard to check computationally, and it does not hold in some interesting cases." (p. 2)
