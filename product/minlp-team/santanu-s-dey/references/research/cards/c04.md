### S014 · Analysis of MILP techniques for the pooling problem (Operations Research 2015, doi:10.1287/opre.2015.1357)
**Read level**: full (author manuscript version; main text pp. 1–31 and Appendices A–C pp. 32–36 read in full; Appendix D result tables pp. 37–40 skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Akshay Gupte (Clemson, p. 1); thanks to Shabbir Ahmed and Myun-Seok Cheon, ExxonMobil support (p. 29) · **Position in trajectory**: the Oper. Res. paper of the 2013–2017 pooling line (SKILL.md Signature Work Anatomy "Pooling").

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is an empirical regularity with no theory: "there is significant amount of empirical evidence" that piecewise-linear (PL) MILP relaxations of the pq-formulation give good bounds, yet "no formal result regarding the quality of these relaxations is known" (abstract, p. 1; repeated §2.1, p. 5). The problem is stated precisely (network (I, L, J), specifications K, pq-formulation (1), pp. 2–3) and the object of study is defined as a whole class, "piecewise-linear relaxation scheme" (Definition 1, p. 4), so the theorem covers every scheme at once, including the plain pq-relaxation (McCormick) (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when practitioners refine a relaxation and report good bounds, define the whole family of refinements abstractly (Definition 1) and ask for a worst-case ratio that holds uniformly over the family.
- **D2 Core idea**: Relax the relaxation: drop the q variables and every constraint containing them to get the LP "Inconsistent Pool-Outflow Problem" (IPOP, p. 7); send all flow to the single most profitable output j* and the specification-consistency constraint (1b) holds trivially because each pool has at most one out-arc with flow (p. 7, construction (7), p. 8). This gives z_pq ≤ n z* (Prop. 1, p. 7). The same construction is an LP-based n-approximation algorithm (§4, p. 12), and the paper then "generalize[s] the key ideas behind the n-approximation algorithm" (p. 6) into a MILP restriction PQ(τ, λ): split each pool into τ copies receiving fixed fractions λ of its inflow, each copy sending to exactly one output (pp. 14–15, (16)). What changes relative to earlier discretizations (Alfaki–Haugland, Gupte et al., Pham et al.): it discretizes the outflow fractions at each pool, not problem variables, and keeps a network-flow structure (p. 16); earlier MILP restrictions have no quality guarantee, this one contains all approximation-algorithm solutions (p. 6).
  - Transferable: read the feasible point built in an upper-bound proof as an algorithm, then enlarge the set of such points while keeping it MILP-representable, so the heuristic inherits the proof's guarantee (pp. 6, 14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Standard pooling problem only (no pool-to-pool arcs), maximization with linear arc weights, in-/out-degree of each pool at least 1, consistent capacities assumed without loss of generality (p. 2, footnotes 1–2). The ratio guarantee is for a maximization objective with z* > 0 (Theorem 1, p. 6). Hardness rests on Håstad's clique inapproximability, i.e. holds unless NP-complete problems have randomized polynomial algorithms (Theorem 3, p. 12). Remark 1 (p. 11) states the tight instance can be made rational and with non-degenerate specification boxes, i.e. the assumptions a_j = b_j in the construction are only for simplicity.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Relaxation-of-the-relaxation (IPOP) plus "keep one output" rounding, verified constraint by constraint (pp. 7–8). (ii) Tightness for every PL scheme (Prop. 2, pp. 8–11): a finiteness argument on the slopes of the finitely many affine pieces of g and h picks ε so the line ζ = (1 − ε/(n−1))α is never matched exactly, giving a neighbourhood of α* where each bilinear term is relaxed with error ≤ δ/2 (Claim 1, pp. 8–9); a two-input, two-pool, n-output instance with unit-vector specifications and a penalised second pool makes z* = 1 (Claim 2, pp. 10–11) while the relaxation reaches n − ε (Claim 3, p. 11). Small per-term errors "propagate" to a factor n (p. 5). (iii) Inapproximability through an approximation-factor-preserving reading of the Alfaki–Haugland NP-hardness reduction from stable set (Prop. 3, p. 13; Theorem 2, p. 12). (iv) Restriction validity: for any feasible MILP point the ratio v_ilj/y_lj depends only on (i, l), so q can be recovered (eqs. (18)–(19), p. 17). (v) Tightness of the restriction too: a rationality argument (all outflow fractions are multiples of 1/κ) and two incompatible ratio inequalities (21)–(22) force flow to a single output (Claim 7, pp. 17–18). (vi) A stable-set-derived family (graph G(n′) of triangles joined in a cycle) where z*/z_U(τ) = n′/τ, labelled "probably not representative of the real-world problems" (Prop. 5, p. 20).
- **D5 Experiments**: No experiments on relaxations, because "computational study of MILP based relaxations of the above type have been extensively conducted" (p. 5). Restriction heuristics U(τ), τ = 1..5, and A(τ), τ = 3..5, solved by Cplex 12.2 for 1 h, single-thread, MIPEmphasis = Feasibility, against BARON 12.7.3 (6 h on NEOS, longer "since ... it is impossible to know exactly how much time it spent to only find feasible solutions"), SNOPT, Xpress SLP, alternating LP and the Alfaki–Haugland FlowAug heuristic (pp. 21–22). Common tolerances set across solvers (p. 22). Testbed: 20 Alfaki–Haugland std* instances plus 50 self-generated random instances, because classical instances "are solved in a matter of seconds by BARON" (pp. 22–23, Table 1; generator App. B pp. 33–35: sparse graphs because "real-world instances are typically sparse", negative-cost paths, specification windows that cut off the root LP solution). LP preprocessing removes outputs (App. C, pp. 35–36). Metric: % gap against BARON's best relaxation bound (eq. (23), p. 23), performance profiles over the 70 instances (Fig. 3, p. 24) and over the 44 instances with n ≥ 30 (Fig. 4, p. 26), geometric-mean gap and time by number of outputs (Tables 3–4, p. 25). Findings reported with their surprises: A(3) beats U(4) within 1 h "contrary to the expectation from the containment" (p. 24); BARON finds the best solution on the largest fraction overall but deteriorates for n ≥ 30, while its dual bounds are "very good" (pp. 25–26, 28). Dead ends reported in a sentence each: APOGEE gave no benefit over BARON (p. 21); alternating-LP variants did not help (p. 21); Cplex symmetry tuning gave no benefit (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on the pq-formulation lineage (Ben-Tal et al., Tawarmalani–Sahinidis, Quesada–Grossmann, Sherali–Adams RLT, p. 3) and treats the computational literature on PL relaxations (Gounaris–Misener–Floudas 2009; Meyer–Floudas; Wicaksono–Karimi) as the evidence to be explained, not as rivals (pp. 4–5). Reuses Alfaki–Haugland's NP-hardness reduction and strengthens what it yields (p. 12). Criticism is mild and structural: BARON's poor primal performance "is perhaps to be expected since it is a generic global solver" that may not exploit structure (p. 28).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: An "Overview of Results" section (§2, pp. 5–6) gives all results before any proof. Results are stated as two-sided theorems (upper bound + tightness; algorithm + inapproximability: Theorems 1, 2, 4, pp. 6, 12, 16). The weak-looking factor n is defended immediately ("At first sight, this appears to be a very poor approximation ratio. However ...", p. 6: independence from |K|, |I|, |L|). Hedging is concentrated in the empirical section ("perhaps", "seems", pp. 24, 26, 28). Explicit numbered open questions close the theory part (Questions 1–2, pp. 27–28).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory (tight ratio n for all PL relaxations; n-approximation; n^{1−ε} inapproximability; structural corollary that pooling with one output is LP-solvable and a t*-approximation by connected components, Corollary 1, p. 27) plus an algorithmic contribution (guaranteed MILP restriction that is strong in practice). Limits stated concretely: the restriction's guarantee is tight and practice is "significantly better than the theoretical performance guarantee of n" (p. 28), so the theorem does not explain the empirical quality; restrictions for different (τ, λ) are not comparable (Example 1, p. 19); a bad family exists (p. 20). Future: complexity for fixed number of specifications or bounded pool out-degree (Question 1, pp. 27–28), better-than-n MILP restrictions (Question 2, p. 28), polyhedral and symmetry structure of the restriction (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the paper starts from a practice that works (PL MILP relaxations) with "no formal result" (p. 1), proves a tight proxy statement (worst-case ratio), reports that practice beats the bound (p. 28), and only then proposes a rule built from the proof (the MILP restriction, pp. 14–16), which is exactly step 8.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: tight upper bound for every scheme (Theorem 1, p. 6), hardness of improvement (Theorem 2, p. 12), constructive object attaining the bound (Theorem 4.1–4.3, p. 16), tightness of the restriction itself (Theorem 4.4, pp. 17–18) and a negative instance family (Prop. 5, p. 20).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the reference point is BARON's relaxation bound (eq. (23), p. 23), not an exact optimum; only 4 of 70 instances are solved to optimality (p. 23, Table 2 p. 24). Ground truth is a certified gap, not a computed ceiling.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the certificate (BARON's dual bound) is borrowed from a general solver while the paper's own contribution is primal; the paper notes BARON's dual bounds are strong and its primal side is the weakness (pp. 26, 28).
- Existing heuristics shown: Heuristic 1 (self-generated harder instances because public ones solve "in a matter of seconds by BARON", p. 22; generator App. B), Heuristic 2 (BARON given 6 h vs 1 h with the reason, common tolerances, best of own and Alfaki–Haugland's implementation of FlowAug, p. 22), Heuristic 3 (APOGEE and alternating-LP dead ends in one sentence each, p. 21).
- New-pattern candidate: proof-to-heuristic (the constructive step of a bound proof becomes an approximation algorithm, then a MILP restriction inheriting its guarantee) (pp. 6, 12, 14).
- New-pattern candidate: relax the relaxation to an analysable LP by deleting the coupling variables (IPOP) (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To bound any refinement of a relaxation, drop the variables that encode the coupling (here q) and show a feasible point of the true problem can be recovered from the weaker relaxation with a loss factor (IPOP, pp. 7–8).
- For tightness over a whole class of piecewise-linear relaxations, use the finitely many slopes of the affine pieces to find a direction where no piece is exact, then exploit the resulting small slack in many terms at once (Claim 1, pp. 8–9).
- Turn an NP-hardness reduction from the literature into an inapproximability result by checking that it preserves the objective value in both directions (Prop. 3, p. 13).
- Build a MILP restriction whose feasible set provably contains the approximation algorithm's outputs, so the heuristic carries the guarantee for free (Theorem 4, p. 16).
- In experiments with a global solver, report gaps against the solver's best dual bound, split profiles by instance size, and break averages down by the structural parameter of the theorem (number of outputs n) (Tables 3–4, p. 25; Fig. 4, p. 26).
- Write a random instance generator that deliberately cuts off the root LP solution so a global solver cannot finish at the root (App. B, p. 35).

**Quotes**:
- "However in the worst case, these arbitrarily small errors in each bilinear term can propagate and lead to an overall error of approximately n for the entire piecewise linear MILP relaxation." (p. 5)
- "If we view the performance of the MILP based restriction of the pooling problem proposed in this paper on its own, we observe that the quality of solutions that are produced by MILP are significantly better than the theoretical performance guarantee of n." (p. 28)

### S022 · Forbidden vertices (Mathematics of Operations Research 2015, doi:10.1287/moor.2014.0673; arXiv:1309.2545)
**Read level**: full (arXiv:1309.2545v2 version, pp. 1–15; the paper has no appendix).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Gustavo Angulo (Georgia Tech and PUC Chile, first author, p. 1), Shabbir Ahmed, Volker Kaibel (Magdeburg) · **Position in trajectory**: a polyhedral/complexity side project of the stochastic-integer-programming collaboration with Ahmed; not part of the MINLP lines in SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is an algorithmic need: "enumerative schemes for stochastic integer programs [9], where a series of potential solutions are evaluated and discarded from the search space" (p. 1), plus the link to k-best basic solutions (abstract, p. 1). The problem is a new primitive: given polytope P, vertex subset X and c, optimise over vert(P) \ X (Definition 1, p. 1). The decisive framing move is to separate encodings: explicit (inequalities) vs implicit (separation oracle / extended formulation) for P, and explicit (list) vs implicit (a face) for X (Definitions 2–3, pp. 1–2).
  - Transferable: when a primitive's complexity is unclear, make the input encoding an explicit parameter and classify every combination.
- **D2 Core idea**: Complexity depends on encoding, and 0-1 structure is what rescues tractability (p. 2, Table 1). For 0-1 polytopes, cover {0,1}^n \ X by at most n|X| faces of the cube ("X-separating" family built from longest common prefixes with X, Definition 14 and Prop. 15, p. 8), then optimise over P ∩ F for each face, or take the disjunctive hull, giving polynomial solvability for tractable 0-1 polytopes (Theorem 16, p. 8) and xc(forb(P, X)) ≤ µ(X)(xc(P)+1) (Prop. 17, p. 8). For the cube itself, two O(n|X|) extended formulations: one via the binary-expansion bijection and intervals K(a, b) whose hulls have O(n) facets (pp. 6–7), one via the recursion of Prop. 13 (p. 7).
  - Transferable: to remove a few points from a structured set, cover the remainder by few faces/boxes on which the original structure survives, then use disjunctive programming (Lemma 4, p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Rational polytopes (p. 1); "tractable" means a separation oracle polynomial in facet complexity (p. 2); complexity of vertex enumeration in the output-sensitive sense (p. 5). Positive results need 0-1 vertices (Section 3) or box-integrality (Definition 26, p. 12); the authors state that "Having all vertices with at most two possible values for each component is crucial" when X is a list (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Polarity to show one removed vertex can blow up facets from n²+1 to 2^n (Prop. 5, p. 3; example credited to Marc Pfetsch, p. 14). (ii) forb(P, X) as the hull of the union of faces avoiding X, giving f(P)^{|X|+1} (Prop. 6, p. 3). (iii) Simplex-path connectivity of the upper level set to show independent parts of X can be removed separately (Prop. 7, pp. 3–4), with an explicit counterexample to the converse (simplex, p. 4). (iv) "Sandwich" hardness constructions: add easy points so optimisation is trivial and xc is polynomial, then removing a single vertex (cut polytope plus scaled unit vectors, Theorem 9, pp. 4–5) or a single facet (TSP tours sandwiched between edge sets of size n−1 and n+1, Theorem 18, pp. 9–10) exposes an NP-hard problem with exponential extension complexity (using Fiorini et al. and Rothvoß lower bounds). (v) Reductions from knapsack (Prop. 10, p. 5), the "is there another vertex" problem of Boros et al. (Theorem 11, pp. 5–6) and vertex cover (Prop. 20, p. 10). (vi) A TU tractable subclass for the open case (Prop. 19, p. 10). (vii) Exchange argument reducing binary all-different to a min-weight R-matching over k-best lists (Theorem 24, p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (purely theoretical paper).
- **D6 Positioning**: Generalises the main result of Lee's "cropped cubes" (Prop. 7, p. 4); re-derives Lawler's k-best tractability (originally via branch-and-fix, p. 11); answers an open question of Lee's all-different paper "in the case P_i = [0,1]^n" and more generally (p. 11). Leans on recent extension-complexity lower bounds (Fiorini et al. [5], Rothvoß [18]) and vertex-enumeration hardness (Khachiyan et al. [8], Boros et al. [3]) as tools (pp. 4–6, 9). Prior work is credited, not criticised.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions as three bullets plus a 2×2×2 classification table pointing to the theorem for each cell (p. 2, Table 1). One cell is honestly marked unresolved: "(∗∗)", "we were not able to establish the complexity" (p. 2; again p. 10). Intuition sentences precede refinements ("we can expect describing forb(P, X) to be easier when the vertices in X are 'far' ... and more complicated when they are 'close'", p. 3), and the paper warns against a tempting converse (p. 4). Short, theorem-dense paper (15 pages).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: a complexity map of a new primitive, compact extended formulations for the 0-1 and box-integral cases, and two applications (k-best, all-different) (pp. 2, 11–14). Limits stated: the complexity of explicit P with X a facet is open (p. 10); removing a list of faces is hard in general (Prop. 20, p. 10). Integral-case k-best and all-different results are asserted without proof ("can be shown to be polynomially solvable", p. 14). No future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: each positive result is paired with the hardness that appears when one hypothesis is dropped (explicit → implicit P, list → face X, 0-1 → general vertices), organised as a table (p. 2, Table 1; Theorems 9, 11, 16, 18); the unresolved cell is labelled as such, matching step 6 ("If tightness is unknown, say so") (pp. 2, 10).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the toolkit is the MILP one (disjunctive programming, extended formulations, extension complexity, Lemma 4, p. 2), but here it is used inside integer programming, not imported into QCQP/MINLP.
- Method 1: no link (the motivation is an algorithmic need in stochastic IP enumeration, p. 1, not an unexplained solver habit).
- New-pattern candidate: encoding-sensitivity classification (state complexity for each input encoding and fill a table, flagging the open cell) (pp. 1–2, Table 1).
- New-pattern candidate: sandwich construction for hardness (pad a hard set with trivial points to make it tractable, then show removing one vertex or facet restores hardness) (Theorems 9, 18, pp. 4–5, 9–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Classify a new problem's complexity by input encoding in a single table with a theorem reference per cell, and mark open cells explicitly (p. 2).
- To prove that a small modification destroys tractability, pad a hard family with easy points that dominate unless the objective is sign-balanced, so the padded set is easy and the modified set embeds the hard one (Theorem 9 pp. 4–5; Theorem 18 p. 9).
- Cover the complement of a finite set of 0-1 points by at most n|X| cube faces built from longest common prefixes; this gives both an algorithm and an extended formulation (Prop. 15, p. 8; Prop. 17, pp. 8–9).
- Use the connectivity of upper level sets along simplex paths to show that removing independent groups of vertices separates (Prop. 7, pp. 3–4).
- Reduce an "all-different" assignment over polytopes to a matching over each component's k-best list by an exchange argument (Theorem 24, p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "It follows from Theorem 9 and Proposition 10 that only when P and X are explicitly given there is hope for efficient optimization over forb(P, X)." (p. 5)
- "Having all vertices with at most two possible values for each component is crucial to retain tractability when X is given as a list." (p. 9)

### S028 · Approximating polyhedra with sparse inequalities (Mathematical Programming 2015, doi:10.1007/s10107-015-0925-y)
**Read level**: full (authors' preprint dated April 27, 2015, pp. 1–22; Appendices A–D read).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Marco Molinaro, Qianyi Wang (supported with Dey by NSF CMMI-1149400, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the journal paper of the sparse-cut line (IPCO 2014 → this paper → Dey–Iroume–Molinaro lower bounds [12], cited as follow-up p. 18) in SKILL.md Signature Work Anatomy "Sparse cuts".

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a solver habit: dense cuts are hard for LP solvers, so "most commercial MILPs solvers consider sparsity of cuts as an important criterion for cutting-plane selection" (p. 1, supported by personal communications with Achterberg, Gu and Narisetty, refs. [1, 15, 20], p. 21–22), while "Surprisingly, very few studies have been conducted" (p. 2). The real question is a two-sided trade-off (faster LPs vs lost gap closure) that is "challenging to simultaneously study" (p. 2); it is cut to one side: "How much do we lose in the closure of integrality gap if we only use sparse cuts?" and then abstracted to approximating a polytope P by its k-sparse closure P^k (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a practice trades off two effects, freeze one effect and study the loss on the other side as a clean geometric question (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Measure quality by the Hausdorff-type distance d(P, P^k) = max over P^k of distance to P, after discarding the objective-ratio measure ("can become infinity", with a two-dimensional counterexample) and the volume ratio (fails when P is not full-dimensional) (pp. 2–3). Main results: an upper bound depending only on the number of vertices t, which at half sparsity gives d ≈ c√(log n) when t = n^q (Theorem 1, pp. 4–5); random 0/1 polytopes show it is nearly tight (Theorem 2, p. 5); hard packing IPs are a class where sparse cuts fail unless k is close to n (Theorem 3, p. 6); sparse cuts in an extended space are never worse and can be much better (Props. 1–2, p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Polytopes in [0,1]^n "without loss of generality" by translation and scaling (p. 2); k-sparse closure defined as the intersection of P + R^{Ī} over all k-subsets I (p. 2). Upper bounds are deterministic over arbitrary vertex sets; lower bounds are probabilistic: uniform random 0/1 points with parameter ranges 64 ≤ k ≤ n/(8802 log n) and polynomial-to-exponential t (Theorem 2, p. 5), and the (n, m, M)-PIP distribution with n ≥ 50, 8 log 8n ≤ m ≤ n (Theorem 3, p. 6). Constants are not optimised ("we did not try to optimize constants and lower order terms", p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Separate a far point by the projection hyperplane (Observation 1, p. 7), then sparsify that hyperplane by randomized rounding: each small coordinate becomes sign(d_i)/α with probability α|d_i| (pp. 7–8, Prop. 3); Bernstein's inequality for sparsity (Claim 1, p. 8), Bernstein plus union bound over vertices for validity (Claim 2, pp. 8–9), Markov for separation (Claim 3, p. 9). The slack λ is what is spent on sparsification (p. 7). Observation 2 (p. 9) notes generic chaining could sharpen this for structured P. (ii) Second bound by averaging the k-sparse relaxations of one halfspace over all k-subsets (Lemma 2, pp. 9–10). (iii) Lower bound modelled on the "bad" Example 2: define "α-tough" polytopes (Definition 1, p. 10), a new anticoncentration lemma for Bernoulli sums far from the standard deviation (Lemma 3, p. 11; proof by bucketing coefficients and trimming small ones, App. C pp. 19–20), integrality of facet normals of 0/1 polytopes to make the union bound finite (Lemmas 4–5, p. 11, citing Ziegler), and a concentration bound for Σx_i (Lemma 7, p. 12). (iv) Packing IPs: aggregate the constraints into a near-uniform cut Σx_i ≲ n/2 (Lemma 9, p. 13; App. D.1), order statistics of uniforms for approximate containment (Lemmas 11–12, p. 14), and a randomized-rounding-and-averaging argument that moves approximately feasible points into the integer hull (Lemma 13, p. 15). (v) A binary-tree extended formulation with 3-sparse constraints whose projection has d = √(n/2) for k ≤ n/2 (Prop. 2, pp. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: Only small exact computation for intuition: Example 3, n = 10, 150 random binary points on Σx_i = 5, lower bounds on d(P, P^k) computed with PORTA facet descriptions of P + R^{Ī} plus a "shooting experiment" over 20,000 random directions and 2 special ones, because PORTA could not enumerate P^k's vertices (p. 4, Fig. 1(c); App. B pp. 18–19). Stated limit: "We were unable to experiment with larger dimensions and larger number of points" (p. 4). No solver experiments; these are listed as future work (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: Frames prior work as nearly absent: apart from single-row cuts, only Andersen–Weismantel's zero-coefficient cuts [3] explicitly target sparse cuts (p. 2). Uses solver-developer personal communications as the evidence for the practice (refs. [1, 15, 20], pp. 1, 21–22). Places the distance measure next to integer-programming ratio measures (Goemans; Basu et al.) and leaves those as open (p. 18). Points to the follow-up lower-bound paper [12] (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Motivation section first (§1.1, pp. 1–2), then a reasoned choice of measure with counterexamples to the alternatives (§1.3, pp. 2–3), then four worked examples showing that "different polytopes behave very differently" (§1.4, pp. 3–4) before any theorem. A "Main Results" section (§2, pp. 4–7) states and interprets all theorems (regimes of small, medium and large k, p. 5) before the proofs (§§3–6). Interpretation sentences translate bounds into a practical reading ("using half sparsity cutting-planes allows us to approximate the integer hull very well", p. 5; "suggests that denser cuts should be explored in this case", p. 6). Hedging is explicit and local ("We believe that as the number of vertices increase, the structure of the polytope becomes more important", p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory only: a measure, matching upper and lower bounds, a negative class and an extended-formulation result. Limits written concretely: Theorem 1 "only captures the worst-case scenario" (p. 5); the bound is not tight for Example 4 (p. 5); the lower bound holds with probability 1/4 and only in a parameter window (Theorem 2, p. 5). Future directions: ratio measures closer to IP practice for downward-closed P (p. 18), computational experiments "which could give further information on how good sparse cuts are in different situations" (p. 18), and packing IPs with sparse LP formulations (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: solver habit (sparsity as a cut-selection criterion, p. 1) → real question with two competing objectives judged out of reach (p. 2) → one-sided proxy (gap lost by sparse cuts) → geometric object P^k (p. 2) → measure chosen so it is "well-defined for all polytopes" after discarding ratios that can be infinite (pp. 2–3), i.e. steps 1–4. Step 7 is only partly closed: the paper says Theorem 1 "presents a theoretical justification for the use of sparse cutting-planes in many cases" (p. 5) but leaves the comparison with solver data to future work (p. 18), consistent with the SKILL.md limitation that the final step is weak.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: upper bounds paired with near-matching random lower bounds (match up to an O(√log(tn)/(√log n √log t)) factor, pp. 5–6), a negative instance class (packing IPs, Theorem 3, p. 6), positive and negative examples side by side (Examples 1–4, pp. 3–4), and an explicit note where the bound is not tight (Example 4, p. 5).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (small scale): exact facet computation with PORTA on n = 10 before/alongside the theory, with the stated size limit (p. 4; App. B pp. 18–19).
- Heuristic 9 (borrow one tool) shown: probabilistic method and a new anticoncentration lemma from probability (Lemma 3, p. 11), generic chaining named as the route to sharper bounds (Observation 2, p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate: randomized sparsification of a separating hyperplane (spend the separation slack on randomized rounding of the normal vector, certify sparsity, validity and separation by concentration) (pp. 7–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before fixing a quality measure, test each candidate on a tiny example where it breaks (objective ratio infinite on a triangle; volume ratio undefined for lower-dimensional P) and write the rejection in the paper (p. 2).
- Show a k-sparse cut exists by randomized rounding of the dense separating normal with keep-probability proportional to |d_i|, then union-bound over the vertices (Lemma 1 and Claims 1–3, pp. 7–9).
- Get a second, regime-specific bound by averaging the k-sparse relaxations of a single halfspace over all k-subsets (Lemma 2, pp. 9–10).
- Prove lower bounds on random polytopes by (a) restricting to finitely many integral facet normals (Lemma 4–5, p. 11) and (b) an anticoncentration bound for each normal, then a union bound (Lemma 6, pp. 11–12).
- Present four contrasting examples (one good, one worst-case, one computed, one structural) before the main theorems so readers see the range the theorems must cover (pp. 3–4).
- When exact vertex enumeration is out of reach, lower-bound a Hausdorff distance by a "shooting" experiment: maximise random directions over both sets and take the best gap (App. B, pp. 18–19).
