### S051 · Convexifications of rank-one-based substructures in QCQPs and applications to the pooling problem (Journal of Global Optimization 2020, 10.1007/s10898-019-00844-4; arXiv 1902.00739)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1–23 in full: main text, Tables 1–9 and the conclusion. Appendix A was skimmed: the Fourier–Motzkin proofs of Props. 6–11 on pp. 23–28 were read at the level of their structure only, and the proof of Theorem 4 on pp. 28–29 was read in full. Appendix B instance tables (pp. 29–30) were scanned. References were not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Burak Kocuk, Asteroide Santana · **Version read**: arXiv v1 preprint, dated 5 Feb 2019 on p. 1, with the preprint title (see page mapping).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper starts from the lifted QCQP form (2)–(5). After lifting, "all the non-convexity" sits in the rank-1 condition (p. 2). It notes that global-optimization work mostly convexifies functions (envelopes), even though convexifying sets gives tighter relaxations (pp. 1–2). It also notes that, apart from the boolean quadric polytope case (W_ij ≤ 1), "no other particular choice of structured linear side constraint has ever been studied" (p. 2). The concrete entry point is the row-bounded rank-1 set U^row of (7), which "comes up naturally for the pooling problem" (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: name an open gap by pointing to the one classical special case that has been solved (BQP) and asking what happens for the next natural family of side constraints.
- **D2 Core idea**: The paper studies U = {W ≥ 0 : ⟨A^k, W⟩ ≤ b_k, rank W ≤ 1} and characterizes when conv(U) is polyhedral or SOC-representable (§2, pp. 3–7). The pooling bilinear constraints x^s_ij = q^s_i f_ij are rewritten as rank-1 conditions on flow-decomposition matrices (47), (58), (67) (pp. 12, 14, 16). Row- and column-wise hulls of these matrices then give new relaxations F^S_2 and F^T_2. These reduce to the stp-relaxation for standard pooling but are new for generalized pooling (Table 1, p. 16). The extended formulation is read as McCormick applied to a ratio variable t_j (22)–(25) (p. 8). Discretizing that ratio variable gives a matched pair: MILP relaxations D^row (Prop. 4) and MILP restrictions (Prop. 5) (pp. 10–11). What changes relative to McCormick on the pq-formulation: the relaxation is built from a whole matrix-level substructure, and the pq-relaxation is recovered as F^S_1 (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: rewrite a family of bilinear equalities that share one multiplier as a single rank-1 constraint on a matrix of disaggregated variables. Then convexify that matrix set row-wise and column-wise.
  - Transferable technique: once a hull's extended formulation is understood as McCormick on an auxiliary ratio variable, discretize that variable. One binary expansion then gives both a relaxation and a restriction, so dual and primal bounds come from the same construction (pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The QCQP has x ∈ [0,1]^n and possibly indefinite Q (p. 1). Rank-1 sets are restricted to nonnegative matrices (6) (p. 2). Prop. 1 needs boundedness (p. 3). Theorem 1 needs A^k = α^k β^T with β > 0 and condition (8) (p. 3). Theorem 2 covers any two bounded side constraints (p. 5). Theorem 3 needs n1 = n2 ≥ 3 and A^k = α^k ββ^T + γ^k δδ^T with nonnegative coefficients (p. 6). Prop. 2 needs finite u_i (p. 7). For pooling, the objective is assumed linear (p. 11), and generalized pooling allows pool-to-pool arcs (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: (i) Extreme-point support bound by perturbing along a rank-1 direction. W± = x̂(ŷ ± ε1e1 ∓ ε2e2)^T keeps the active constraints tight, so an extreme point has at most one nonzero column (Thm 1, p. 4) or a support of at most 2×2 (Thm 2, pp. 5–6; Thm 3, pp. 6–7). (ii) The hull is the convex hull of a union of finitely many small-support pieces. Each piece is a single quadratic W11W22 = W21W12 over a bounded polytope, which is SOCr by the cited result [47] (p. 6). (iii) A disjunctive extended formulation with weights t_j is proved exact by showing that its extreme points have exactly one positive t (pp. 4–5). (iv) The hull is projected to the original space by Fourier–Motzkin, giving partition inequalities (18)–(19) that separate in O(n1 n2) (App. A.1, pp. 23–25). (v) Non-polyhedrality is shown by an explicit one-parameter family of extreme points [a, a²/(1−a); 1−a, a], using the strict convexity of u²/(1−u) (Prop. 12, pp. 28–29). Complexity: polynomial-time linear optimization in all cases, because there are (n1 choose 2)(n2 choose 2) supports, each with a fixed-size SOC program (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: to prove a hull is SOCr or polyhedral, first bound the support of extreme points with a perturbation that keeps the active constraints tight. Then convexify each small support pattern separately and take the union.
  - Transferable technique: to prove that a hull is not polyhedral, exhibit a curve of extreme points parameterized through a strictly convex function (p. 29).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Setup is Gurobi 7.5.1 on a Windows 10 PC (2.19 GHz, 32 GB RAM) (p. 17). There are three generalized-pooling sets (p. 17): 24 real Mining instances; 12 of the 40 Alfaki–Haugland instances (the 28 whose gap the pq-relaxation already closes are dropped); and 24 new Random instances, built with the Alfaki–Haugland recipe but larger and denser, and posted online. Primal bounds come from the MILP restrictions with H = 3 and an 1800 s limit; G^S_2 is best on average (Table 2, p. 18). Dual bounds come from nine LP relaxations grouped as Light, Medium and Heavy (Tables 3–5, pp. 19–20). F^S_2 ∩ F^T_2 beats the Heavy F^ST_1 of [12] in both average gap and time; F^ST_1 hits memory limits, and in those cases the best other bound is reported in parentheses (pp. 18–19). On Mining, the LP average gap falls from 18.65 (the relaxation of [14]) to 3.82 (Table 3, p. 19). MILP relaxations with H = 3 and 1800 s are reported in Tables 6–8 (pp. 21–22). M^S_3 wins on Mining and Random, while M^T_3 nearly halves the gap on Literature. The authors report: "it is difficult to advise a single method" (p. 20). Summary is in Table 9 (p. 23). The metric is the percentage duality gap against the best known primal bound. Dead ends are reported: row-wise variants are "consistently weaker" (pp. 13, 15), and F^ST_2 is "too big and numerically unstable" (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: discard benchmark instances that the baseline relaxation already solves (here 28 of 40), say so, and report only the instances with a nontrivial gap (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: when the heaviest relaxation fails on memory, report the best bound from the other methods in parentheses so that the averages stay comparable, and say explicitly that this was done (pp. 18–19).
  - Transferable technique: use the cheap LP ranking to predict which MILP discretization will win. Here the winning Light LP predicted the MILP winner on every set (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself against the function-envelope tradition (a long citation list, p. 2) and with the smaller set-convexification line (p. 2). It builds on the BQP hull result [18] and on the SOCr result for one quadratic over a polytope [47] (pp. 2, 6). It contrasts Theorem 2 with the two-quadratic SOCr results [17, 40]: the setting here adds W ≥ 0, and "the proof technique here is completely different" (p. 6). On pooling, Table 1 sorts relaxations into known and new and shows that pq, tp and stp are recovered (p. 16). Previous work is credited by restating it inside the new framework, not by criticizing it.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: §1.2 previews each section's contribution (pp. 2–3). The key move is flagged in plain words: "Our key observation is to rewrite the bilinear constraints" (p. 12). Negative findings are written in one sentence each, with the reason (pp. 13, 15, 16). An open problem is stated as a conjecture: optimizing over U^row ∩ U^col is NP-hard (p. 9). The conclusion's claim "consistently outperforms, on average" (p. 23) is a superlative hedged by "on average". Proofs are short in the body, and the Fourier–Motzkin details go to an appendix.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Theory: polyhedral or SOCr hulls with polynomial-time optimization for three families of side constraints (§2). Formulation: rank-1 formulations of generalized pooling and new relaxations F^S_2 and F^T_2 (§3.3–3.4). Computation: better dual bounds, plus a new and harder random benchmark (p. 23). Limits stated in the paper: there is no complete hull description for row ∩ column bounds when n > 2 (the NP-hardness conjecture, p. 9); the simultaneous row-and-column discretization was not tried because it is "very large" (p. 13); and no single MILP relaxation dominates (p. 20). No explicit future-work section exists beyond the conjecture.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the stated substructure paradigm (p. 1), the rank-1 lift that carries all the nonconvexity (p. 2), side constraints chosen by an application (pooling, p. 2), set convexification instead of envelopes (p. 2), and constructive extended formulations with their size stated (pp. 4, 7).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: LP relaxations ("Light" ones) preferred for dual bounds and certified gaps; hardware stated (pp. 17–20).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (partial): Theorem 4 marks the boundary (SOCr but not polyhedral, p. 9; extreme-point family, p. 29); the missing general description is admitted and posed as a conjecture (p. 9).
- Heuristic 1 and Heuristic 3 of SKILL.md are both visible. The paper builds a harder Random benchmark and posts it (p. 17). It writes dead ends in one line each (pp. 13, 15, 16).
- New-pattern candidate: "paired MILP relaxation and restriction from one discretized ratio variable" (pp. 10–11, 17–18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Disaggregate flows by origin or destination, then state the bilinear pooling constraint as rank(x^s_ij) = 1 per pool; one matrix constraint replaces many bilinear terms (pp. 12, 14).
- Support-bounding perturbation for extreme points of rank-1 sets with m linear constraints: a homogeneous system with more unknowns than equations has a nontrivial solution, so the support is small (pp. 5–6).
- Report three bound tiers (Light, Medium, Heavy LP; then MILP) on the same instances in one summary table of the best average gap and time per tier (Table 9, p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "Observe that all the non-convexity of the problem is now captured by the rank-1 condition." (p. 2)
- "It is well-known that it is possible to obtain tighter convex relaxations when convexifing a set directly rather than using convex envelopes of functions describing the set." (p. 2)

### S031 · Branch-and-bound solves random binary IPs in polytime (Proceedings of the 2021 ACM-SIAM Symposium on Discrete Algorithms (SODA) 2021, 10.1137/1.9781611976465.35; arXiv 2007.15192)
**Read level**: full, pp. 1–17 including the references. **Version read**: arXiv v4 (16 Sep 2021). On p. 4 the text says that it is a "significant improvement" over the SODA version [16]: Theorem 1 is unchanged, but the proofs are simplified, the §7 bound is sharpened by a factor of O(n^m), and §§3–4 are "revamped". The card therefore describes the revised (journal-track, cf. D003) argument, not the SODA proceedings text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yatharth Dubey, Marco Molinaro

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Every state-of-the-art MILP solver uses variable branching, and practical trees are small. Yet "there is no theoretical study" explaining that success (p. 2). Worst-case trees are exponential [10, 13] (p. 2). Existing polynomial results for random packing IPs rely on lattice basis reduction (Pataki et al.) or on custom enumeration schemes, and none of these is standard B&B (p. 3). The entry point is to escape worst-case lower bounds by analysing random instances, following smoothed analysis of simplex (p. 2): A and c are i.i.d. uniform on [0,1], and b_j = β_j n.
  - Transferable technique: when a solver component works in practice but has exponential worst-case lower bounds, keep the component exactly as implemented and change the instance model, not the algorithm.
- **D2 Core idea**: Theorem 1 (pp. 2–3): with best-bound node selection and any fractional-variable choice, the tree has at most n^{ā1(m+α log m)} nodes with probability ≥ 1 − 1/n − 2^{−αā2}. The chain of ideas has three steps. First, tree size ≤ 2|G|n + 1, where G is the set of 0/1 points whose reduced-cost loss Δrc is at most the integrality gap (Thm 3, p. 7). Second, good points can only disagree with the LP saddle point x* on items whose columns lie close to the separating hyperplane H (Lemma 6, pp. 8–9; Lemma 7, p. 10). Third, for random columns, few items lie near any hyperplane, uniformly over all hyperplanes (Thm 4, p. 11). Kept unchanged: textbook variable branching, even with adversarial variable choice (p. 3). Changed: only the instance distribution.
  - Transferable technique: prove the result for an arbitrary or adversarial choice inside the rule you are analysing, so that any practical rule (for example strong branching) inherits the bound (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Binary packing IP, max c^T x subject to Ax ≤ b (p. 2). Entries of A and c are i.i.d. uniform on [0,1]; b_j = β_j n with β_j ∈ (0, 1/2); m is fixed; n ≥ m+1 (pp. 2–3). Best-bound node selection (p. 3). The proof uses only pruning by bound (p. 3). The integrality-gap input is Dyer–Frieze Theorem 2 (p. 5). The constants ā1 and ā2 depend on m and β (p. 3). §7 drops randomness and states the bound for any "well-behaved" IP: small integrality gap and no concentration of columns near a hyperplane (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: (i) Best-bound never branches on a node with LP value below IP* (Lemma 1, pp. 4–5). (ii) A vertex has ≤ m fractional coordinates (Lemma 2, p. 5). (iii) A Lagrangian saddle point (x*, λ*) defines the reduced-cost loss Δrc(x) ≤ OPT(LP) − ⟨c, x⟩ (Lemma 3, p. 7). (iv) A charging map from internal nodes to good points: rounding the node's fractional coordinates to minimise Δrc gives an affine minimisation over a cube face (Lemma 4, p. 7). The map is at most n-to-1 by a lowest-common-ancestor argument, because all nodes mapped to one point lie on one root path (Lemma 5, pp. 7–8). (v) Geometric reinterpretation: x* is a linear classifier of the columns (c_j, A^j), and the cost of flipping item j is at least its distance to H (Lemma 6, pp. 8–9). (vi) Distances are bucketed dyadically into sets J_ℓ, and a counting product bounds |G| (Lemma 7, p. 10). This alone gives only a quasi-polynomial bound (p. 10). (vii) The data-dependent hyperplane is handled by a uniform slab bound over all hyperplanes, built from an ε-net of the sphere, discretized widths, Ball's cube-slicing volume bound √2 (Lemma 8, p. 12), Bernstein's inequality (Lemmas 9–10, p. 12), and a union bound (pp. 12–13). (viii) The pieces are assembled on a high-probability event E (pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable technique: to bound a B&B tree, count the near-optimal integer points rather than the nodes, then charge nodes to points with bounded multiplicity (pp. 6–8).
  - Transferable technique: when the object to control (here the hyperplane H) depends on the random data, prove a uniform statement over all such objects with an ε-net and a union bound, instead of conditioning (pp. 11–13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (no computations).
- **D6 Positioning**: Lenstra's fixed-dimension algorithm and Pataki et al. are described as "very heavy machinery" that solvers do not use (pp. 2–3). Lueker, Goldberg–Marchetti-Spaccamela, Beier–Vöcking and Dyer–Frieze are described as custom enumeration schemes, "not equivalent to the general purpose branch-and-bound algorithm" (p. 3). The paper builds on Dyer–Frieze's integrality-gap theorem (p. 5) and generalizes Lemma 3.1 of [19] (p. 8). Follow-up work is noted: Borst et al. (Gaussian data, n^{poly(m)}) and Frieze's exponential lower bound for asymmetric TSP via the assignment relaxation (p. 3). Daniel Dadush is thanked for improvements (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The main theorem appears on pp. 2–3, followed at once by three remarks arguing its practical relevance. Best-bound gives the smallest tree for a fixed partitioning rule. Any variable choice is covered, so good rules do better. Only bound-pruning is used, so real trees are smaller (p. 3). The claim is "the first known such result for a standard version of branch-and-bound" (p. 1), hedged as "provides an indication" (p. 1) and "compelling indication" (p. 3). A section-by-section road map is given (pp. 3–4), and the version difference from SODA is stated openly (p. 4). An intuition paragraph precedes the formal slab theorem (p. 11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The theoretical contribution is the first polynomial tree-size bound for standard variable-branching B&B on a random IP family (p. 1). Corollary 3 gives a deterministic bound for "well-behaved" IPs (p. 15). Limits (some stated, some implied by the hypotheses): m must be fixed, so the exponent grows with m (p. 3); the model is uniform packing (p. 2); the node selection is best-bound (p. 3); no lower bound is shown that would make the bound tight; and the authors say they could not find a good enough multiplicative uniform bound in the literature (VC-dimension-type bounds included), so they proved one with an ε-net (p. 11). There is no explicit future-work list. The open directions appear only through the cited follow-ups (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the object of study is a solver habit (variable branching) that works and has no explanation (p. 2). The question is cut down to a proxy that can be proved: random instances and best-bound (pp. 2–3). Step 5 of Method 1 (random instances when worst case is exponential) is followed almost word for word (p. 2). The return to practice is the "indication" remarks (p. 3).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the boundary is drawn through sufficient conditions (fixed m; Corollary 3's small gap and no column concentration, p. 15), not through a tight example or matching lower bound. Tightness of n^{O(m)} is not addressed.
- Heuristic 9 of SKILL.md (borrow a tool from a neighbouring field) is visible: high-dimensional probability tools (ε-nets from Vershynin, Ball's cube slicing, Bernstein) are brought into IP theory (pp. 11–12).
- Workflow E step 5 (treat the conference version as a draft for the journal) is visible: v4 states what changed from SODA (p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate: "count near-optimal solutions to bound enumeration effort" (pp. 6–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Lemma-chain design: tree size → number of good points → counting by distance buckets → geometric concentration. Each link is a separate statement, so §7 can reuse the deterministic links for general IPs (p. 15).
- Use the LP's Lagrangian saddle point to turn a 0/1 point's suboptimality into a sum of per-item reduced costs, each bounded below by a distance to a hyperplane (pp. 6, 8–9).

**Quotes**:
- "In order to avoid worst-case lower bounds, a standard idea is to consider random instances." (p. 2)
- "We believe that this result provides an indication as to why branch-and-bound with variable branching works so well in practice." (p. 1)

### S007 · Complexity of training ReLU neural network (Discrete Optimization 2022, 10.1016/j.disopt.2020.100620; arXiv 1809.10787)
**Read level**: full, pp. 1–21 including all of Appendix A (proofs A.1–A.8). References were scanned. **Version read**: arXiv v2 (3 Nov 2020), marked "Preprint submitted to Discrete Optimization" (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Digvijay Boob, Guanghui Lan

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The complexity of training multi-layer fully connected ReLU networks "has remained open" (p. 2). Classical hardness results cover threshold activations (Blum–Rivest; Megiddo) (p. 2). Earlier ReLU hardness results [11, 12] use a non-fully-connected architecture that restricts the second-layer matrix to be diagonal. "The architecture artificially restricts the form of the affine functions in order to prove NP-hardness" (p. 3). So it was unclear whether full connectivity makes training easier or harder (p. 3). The entry point is the exact architecture of Blum et al. (2 nodes, then 1 node) with ReLU everywhere (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: when a hardness result in the literature relies on an artificial restriction of the model, re-ask the question on the exact unrestricted model that practitioners use, keeping the classical graph fixed.
- **D2 Core idea**: Three results together mark the boundary (pp. 4, 6–7). (a) Training the 2-ReLU NN (exact-fit decision version) is NP-hard, by reduction from 2-hyperplane separability (Thm 3.1, Cor. 3.2). (b) For fixed input dimension d and fixed first-layer width k, training is poly(N), by enumerating activation patterns with the hyperplane-arrangement theorem and solving a convex QP for each pattern (Thm 3.3). (c) With N first-layer nodes for N data points, there is a poly(N, d) randomized algorithm, by reduction to one-hidden-layer interpolation [7] (Prop. 3.4). Relative to Blum et al., the graph is kept and the activation changed. The authors say a new technique is needed because ReLU has no combinatorial interpretation (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Decision version: exact fit F(x^i) = y^i with labels in {0, 1} (Def. 2.1, p. 5). Without loss of generality w1, w2 ∈ {−1, 1} by positive homogeneity (Obs. 1, p. 5). 0 ∈ S1 after translation (p. 8). Strict and non-strict separability are shown equivalent (Remark 4.1, p. 8). Thm 3.3 uses square loss, with d and k constant (pp. 13–15). Prop. 3.4 assumes distinct inputs and y ≥ 0 (p. 15). The paper distinguishes its worst-case NP-hardness framework from distributional and statistical-query hardness (pp. 3–4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: (i) Polynomial reduction that lifts the data with two extra dimensions and adds 18 fixed "gadget points" (p. 8; Fig. 3, p. 9). (ii) An intermediate combinatorial characterization, "hard-sorting": exact fit holds iff the first-layer output is constant on the label-1 points and strictly smaller on the label-0 points (Def. 4.2, p. 9; Lemma 4.5, p. 10, proof A.4, pp. 16–17). (iii) Monotonicity under taking subsets: if the gadget alone cannot be hard-sorted except in a specific way, the full data cannot either (Remark 4.3, p. 10; p. 11). (iv) The gadget forces w1 = w2 = −1, c = 0 and a1, a2 ≤ 0 on T1 (Lemma 4.7, p. 11). The proof uses pigeonhole and non-collinearity of T1 (A.5, p. 17), a case analysis on parallel normals (A.6, pp. 18–19), and T1 ⊂ conv(T0) (A.7, p. 19). (v) For Thm 3.3: hyperplane-arrangement enumeration of O(N^d) partitions per node, O(N^{kd+(2^k−1)d}) combinations in total, and 2^{k+1} sign cases, each a convex QP (A.1, pp. 13–15). (vi) Corollary 4.11 extends to j output nodes by padding the labels with zeros (A.8, p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: put an exact characterization lemma such as "hard-sorting" between the learning problem and the NP-complete source problem. The reduction is then proved about a geometric property, not about the network's weights.
  - Transferable technique: design a small, fixed gadget in extra coordinates that pins down the sign pattern and weights of any feasible solution. Then argue only about the original coordinates (pp. 8–12).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (no computations).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Blum–Rivest and Megiddo (p. 2). Criticizes Livni et al. and DasGupta et al. for an artificially restricted architecture (p. 3). Separates itself from distribution-specific and SQ hardness (pp. 3–4). Generalizes Arora et al. [15] from one hidden layer to the two-layer k-ReLU network (pp. 4, 6–7). Uses Zhang et al. [7] (over-parameterization) as a tool (p. 15). Concurrent work by Manurangsi–Reichman and by Dey–Wang–Xie on single-node ReLU is acknowledged as covering "different network architectures" (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions are bulleted early, with theorem numbers (p. 4). High-level proof ideas are given in prose before the formal statements, for example "each data point 'passes through' the three ReLU nodes" (p. 6). The main text is a chain of lemmas, and all technical proofs go to Appendix A (pp. 13–20). Figures illustrate hard-sorting (Fig. 4, p. 10). The discussion claims "in our opinion, this result resolves a significant gap" (p. 13). The reviewers are thanked for simplifying several results (p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contribution is theoretical: a complexity map with NP-hard at k = 2, polynomial for fixed (d, k), and polynomial at k = N (pp. 4, 13). Open questions are stated explicitly: the complexity for 2 < k < N, and generalizing the gadget to k-ReLU (p. 13). Unstated but visible limits: only the exact-fit decision version is treated (Def. 2.1, p. 5), not approximate loss minimization; the polynomial exponent in Thm 3.3 grows as (2^k−1)d (p. 15); and Prop. 3.4 uses a randomized algorithm (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the positive and negative results are paired to mark the boundary (NP-hard at k = 2, polynomial for fixed d and k, polynomial at k = N), and the uncovered middle range is named as open (pp. 4, 6–7, 13). Dropping a hypothesis (fixed d) flips the complexity (p. 6).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the motivation cites the empirical success of over-parameterized networks [7] (p. 2), and Prop. 3.4 gives a matching polynomial case (p. 7). The paper does not claim to explain why gradient training works, so the proxy-theorem loop is only partly present.
- New-pattern candidate: "gadget plus characterization lemma for hardness of a continuous model" (pp. 8–12). The same reduction style (fixed gadget plus exact characterization) could be checked against the MIQP and undecidability boundary work cited under Method 4.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Positive homogeneity normalization (w[ax+b]+ = sgn(w)[|w|(ax+b)]+) reduces the weight signs to ±1 before any case analysis (p. 5).
- For fixed-dimension polynomiality, enumerate the activation or sign patterns with the hyperplane-arrangement bound O(N^d). Each pattern turns the piecewise-linear model into a convex program with linear sign constraints (pp. 6, 13–15).

**Quotes**:
- "The architecture artificially restricts the form of the affine functions in order to prove NP-hardness." (p. 3)
- "Techniques used in the proof are different from earlier work in the literature because there is no combinatorial interpretation to ReLU as opposed to the threshold function." (p. 4)
