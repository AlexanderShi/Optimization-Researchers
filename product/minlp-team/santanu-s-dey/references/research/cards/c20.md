### S111 · Strengthening dual bounds for multicommodity capacitated network design with unsplittable flow constraints (European Journal of Operational Research 2026, doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2026.02.014; arXiv:2512.25018)
**Read level**: full. Main text pp. 1–27 read line by line; Appendices A–E (pp. 27–31) read; Appendix F (per-instance Canad table, p. 32) skimmed; reference list (pp. 33–37) scanned. Read version: arXiv:2512.25018v1 of 31 Dec 2025 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Lacy M. Greening (Arizona State, first and corresponding author), Alan L. Erera (Georgia Tech) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an applied logistics IP paper in the "dual bounds first" line (Method 5); it reuses his aggregation-cut work ([12], Bodur–Del Pia–Dey–Molinaro–Pokutta 2018, cited p. 16) and his GMI-closure / Lagrangian-selection preprint ([8], arXiv:2411.15090, cited p. 18). Which parts are Dey's is not stated in the text.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an industrial requirement that breaks a standard model: e-commerce fulfilment networks need all shipments of a commodity on one path ("unsplittable"), which forces binary flow variables (p. 2). Primal metaheuristics exist, but "assessing the quality of solutions is typically difficult ... due to weak dual bounds" (p. 2). The stated aim is therefore dual-bound strengthening, not better primal solutions (p. 2, four contribution bullets).
  - Transferable: frame an applied MIP paper around the missing certificate ("heuristics give solutions, nobody can say how good") rather than around a faster heuristic.
- **D2 Core idea**: (i) Replace integer capacities τ_a by a multiple-choice binary scheme y_at with Σ_t y_at ≤ 1 (BIN, eqs. (2)–(3), pp. 5–6). (ii) Study the single-arc relaxation S (eq. (6), p. 8) and prove that conv(S) is described by Gx ≤ Hy with G, H ≥ 0 and no constant term (Prop. 1, pp. 8–9), then mine that form for two cut families: SAC-Pack, a "smart re-aggregation" of the disaggregated linking constraints with α_t = how many selected commodities fit into t capacity units (Prop. 2, eq. (10), pp. 10–11), and Gen-SAC-Pack with integer coefficients up to B (eq. (12), pp. 13–14). (iii) Add "helper" metric inequalities, generated from LP-feasible capacity vectors and selected by a Lagrangian re-solve borrowed from GMI-cut selection (Alg. 1, pp. 16–19). (iv) Combine all three in an Integrated Cut-Generation (ICG) scheme: pre-solve helper rows plus root-node user cuts (p. 19). Unchanged: the commercial MIP solver does the branch-and-bound (p. 19).
  - Transferable: before inventing cuts, prove the *form* every facet of a small relaxation must take (signs, no constant), then restrict cut families to that form.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Path-based unsplittable MCND with a pre-given path set P_k per commodity, a single facility type installable in integer multiples, no pre-existing capacity (pp. 3–5). The no-constant, nonnegative form of conv(S) depends on exactly two assumptions, which the authors name: no pre-installed capacity and a unique capacity level per arc; with either relaxed "a constant term would be required" and monotonicity fails (p. 9). Integer demands and capacities in S (p. 8). For Canad instances the arc-based fixed-capacity model of [44] is used for comparability (pp. 21–22, App. E).
  - Transferable: state which modelling assumption buys the clean polyhedral form, and what form the inequalities take once it is dropped.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Prop. 1: write S as (0,0) ∪ ⋃_t (S^t, e_t), take the disjunctive extended formulation, project out copies by Fourier–Motzkin (all right-hand sides zero ⇒ homogeneous Gx ≤ Hy), then prove G ≥ 0 by a zeroing-out argument (replacing a negative coefficient by 0 gives a valid, dominating inequality) and H ≥ 0 by the points (0, e_p) (pp. 8–9). Prop. 2: validity by restriction to Z and the definition of α_t as a cardinality-constrained knapsack value (p. 10). Separation: an exact IP (11) with precedence constraints enforcing a non-decreasing-demand order (pp. 11–13); Gen-SAC-Pack separation by Boyd-style row generation between a cut LP (13) and a feasibility oracle (14) (pp. 14–15). App. B: (14) decomposes into one knapsack per t; with ||θ||_∞ ≤ B, solve a min-knapsack by DP in O(n²B) and bisect on the guessed value, total O(n²B log(nB)) instead of O(n q_a) (pp. 28–29). App. A: a two-commodity example showing BIN's linking constraints beat INT's when some demand exceeds one unit (pp. 27–28).
  - Transferable: when cut coefficients are bounded by a small B, swap the knapsack DP's dimension from capacity to objective value and bisect.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Python 3.10, Gurobi 11.0, 24-core Xeon Gold 6226, 192 GB (p. 20). Two test beds: synthetic path-based instances built from a U.S. e-commerce company's network and demand data, three groups × 5 instances, up to 4,402 commodities and 30,441 paths (Table 3, pp. 20–22); and the public Canad "C" set, 31 instances, arc-based (p. 21). Protocol: (a) INT vs BIN, 3 h best-bound focus, gap to the best of two 12 h runs: BIN improves the gap by 38.5%, 12.4%, 2.6% (Table 4, p. 23). (b) LP strength, cuts added until no LP change, baselines are disaggregated linking constraints and r-split c-strong inequalities (r ≤ 10) of Atamtürk–Rajan: Group 1 gap 8.7% → 5.0% (79.8% improvement), with generation times reported (p. 24, Fig. 2); Canad: SAC-Pack 85.3% vs 82.7% for c-strong, and SAC-Pack then c-strong 87.2% (Table 6, p. 25). (c) ICG: 4 of 5 Group 1 instances solved vs 1 for BIN (Table 7, p. 26); on Groups 2–3, 3 h ICG (generation time counted inside the 3 h) beats 12 h BIN by 26.5% and 22.5% gap reduction (Table 8, pp. 26–27). (d) Solver-independence check of helper metric inequalities with Gurobi, CPLEX, SCIP: 27.1%, 36.4%, 12.9% (Table C.9, p. 30). Negative findings reported: Gen-SAC-Pack "does not appear necessary" on Canad given its generation time (p. 25).
  - Transferable: count separation time inside the same wall-clock budget as the baseline (3 h ICG vs 3 h and 12 h plain solve), and run the "helper constraint" effect under a second and third solver before claiming it.
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on the arc-set polyhedron line (Brockmüller–Günlük–Wolsey c-strong, Atamtürk–Rajan r-split c-strong, Benhamiche et al. Min-Set, Chen et al. 2021 exact separation) (pp. 3–4). The closest work is Chen et al.; the difference is stated by model assumption, not by criticism: single facility, no pre-existing capacity, hence no constant term (p. 4, p. 9). Against Hewitt–Nemhauser–Savelsbergh's branch-and-price bound, root LP bounds win on 20 and 29 of 31 instances, flagged as "a somewhat unfair comparison" (pp. 25–26).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions as four bullets at the end of p. 2; the root cause of weak LPs drawn as a one-figure step-vs-linear cost picture (Fig. 1, pp. 6–7); every cut family introduced by a worked numerical example with an LP point it cuts off (Examples 1–2, pp. 10–11, 13–14). Results paragraphs open with "We first observe ... We next observe" and quantify each claim (pp. 22–25). Hedging is explicit where comparisons favour the authors (p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution levels: polyhedral (Prop. 1–2), algorithmic (separation IP, DP, ICG), computational (industrial-scale instances). Limits stated: BIN alone still cannot solve small instances (p. 6); Gen-SAC-Pack not worth it on Canad (p. 25); helper inequality generation takes about 1.5 h on Group 3 (p. 30). Future work is generic: components "can also be included into alternative bounding procedures" (p. 27). No theory explains why redundant metric inequalities help the solver; the paper only points to aggregation literature (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the deliverable is the dual bound and the certified gap on full-size industrial instances with hardware stated (pp. 2, 20, 26–27).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the substructure paradigm used in its home MILP setting: convexify the single-arc set with its capacity-selection constraint jointly and read cuts off the hull's form (pp. 7–10).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the comparison is against the strongest known cut family and a branch-and-price bound, with the unfairness disclosed (Heuristic 2), but there is no exact-ceiling instrument (pp. 24–26).
- New-pattern candidate: "redundant constraints that help a relaxation or solver" (helper metric inequalities, p. 16; cf. S117 encoding rows).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Binarize an integer capacity into a multiple-choice selector so that the relaxation exposes one knapsack per capacity level; then cuts can have level-specific right-hand coefficients (pp. 5, 8–10).
- Prove the sign pattern and homogeneity of the hull of a small relaxation by disjunctive extended formulation plus Fourier–Motzkin, then search cuts only in that cone (pp. 8–9).
- Select which redundant "helper" rows to generate by moving generated rows into the objective with their duals and re-solving the LP to get a new point (Alg. 1, p. 18).
- Bounded-coefficient knapsack separation via min-knapsack DP over objective values plus bisection (pp. 28–29).

**Quotes**:
- "assessing the quality of solutions is typically difficult for realistically-sized instances due to weak dual bounds, a common issue in network design problems caused by their weak linear programming (LP) relaxations." (p. 2)
- "While this is a somewhat unfair comparison (given advances in modern commercial solvers, which aid the separation routines of SAC-Pack inequalities relative to those available when [44] was written), we report this result simply to show the potential improvements our new valid inequalities could offer" (p. 26)

### S112 · Geoffrion's theorem beyond finiteness and rationality (Operations Research Letters 2026, doi:10.1016/j.orl.2025.107395; arXiv:2510.10966)
**Read level**: full. All 9 pages read line by line (pp. 1–8 text, p. 9 references). Read version: arXiv:2510.10966v1 of 13 Oct 2025 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Frédéric Meunier, Diego A. Morán R. (p. 1); work begun at a Collaborate@ICERM visit, August 2024 (p. 8) · **Place in trajectory**: the "duality and decomposition" line of 2023–2026 (SKILL.md Latest), and a direct reuse of Dey–Morán 2013 on convex hulls of integer points in general convex sets ([6], used on pp. 3, 4, 5, 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is an implicit hypothesis in a textbook theorem: Geoffrion's theorem (v^L = v*) is proved for X finite or the integer points of a rational polyhedron, and its proof "relies crucially on conv(X) being polyhedral" (pp. 1–2). The only place in the literature that flags the gap is one sentence by Guignard, quoted (p. 2). Problem statement: does v^L equal v* or v̄* (the closure version, problem (3)) when X is the integer points of an irrational or non-polyhedral set? (pp. 2, 5)
  - Transferable: search the literature for the one sentence that admits a classical theorem's hidden hypothesis, quote it, and make it the paper's opening gap.
- **D2 Core idea**: Two counterexamples first, then sufficient conditions. Ex. 1.1: with y = √2 x, v^L = −∞ < v* = 0 (pp. 2–3). Ex. 1.2: even v^L = −1 < v̄* = 0 (pp. 3–5). Diagnosis: both are "degenerate", the feasible set lies on the boundary of conv(X), since Slater's condition gives v^L = v̄* = v* immediately (Prop. 1.1, p. 5). So the question becomes the boundary case, answered by three theorems: conv(X) locally polyhedral and (3) attains its optimum ⇒ v^L = v̄* (Thm 1.2, p. 5); n ≤ 2 ⇒ v^L = v̄* (Thm 1.3, p. 5); rational single-row Ax ≥ b and finite inf over X ⇒ v^L = v̄* = v* (Thm 1.4, p. 5). Thm 1.4 inverts the usual assumption: rationality on the dualized constraint instead of on X (p. 5).
  - Transferable: once counterexamples are found, ask what they have in common (here: boundary of the hull), dispose of the generic case by a standard tool (Slater), and spend the paper on the remaining degenerate case.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Minimization IP min{c·x : Ax ≥ b, x ∈ X}, X ⊆ Z^n, Lagrangian dual over λ ≥ 0 (p. 1). Thm 1.2 needs local polyhedrality (every box intersection is a polytope) and attainment in (3); the authors call attainment "a crucial assumption in our proof" (p. 7). Thm 1.2's proof does not use integrality of X at all (p. 5). Example of a non-polyhedral X covered: {(x, y) ∈ Z² : y ≥ x²} (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Prop. 2.1 ordering v^L ≤ v̄* ≤ v* by weak duality (p. 6). Thm 1.2: localize to a full-dimensional box Q around the optimum, apply Farkas to the polyhedron {Ax ≥ b} ∩ Q ∩ conv(X), note that box facets are inactive at an interior point, so the active hull inequalities Dx ≥ e are valid for all of conv(X), which yields multipliers certifying v^L ≥ v̄* (p. 6). Lemma 2.2 (planar hulls are locally polyhedral) by counting extreme points of K ∩ P in three types, with a pointer that it fails in R³ (pp. 6–7). Thm 1.3 by a three-case split on dim Y (p. 7). Thm 1.4 by a three-case split on how X meets the integer hyperplanes a·x = β, using that b − a·x ≥ 1 off the hyperplane and a large λ (p. 7). Ex. 1.2's equality (6) via recession-cone equality and Rockafellar Cor. 9.8.1 (pp. 4–5). Ex. 3.1: a non-rational separating hyperplane shows the natural proof route for multi-row Thm 1.4 fails even where the conclusion holds (p. 8).
  - Transferable: to pass a global Lagrangian statement through a non-polyhedral hull, localize to a box where the hull is a polytope, use LP duality there, and show the box constraints carry no multiplier.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (no computation).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Geoffrion 1974 (p. 1) and corrects nothing in it; positions against an unproved remark of Guignard 1998 (p. 2). Tools are his own earlier results with Morán [6] and textbook convex analysis (Rockafellar, Ben-Tal–Nemirovski notes) (pp. 4–7). The closing link is to a 2025 De Loera et al. paper on finitely generated integer points under group actions (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A 1-page introduction that states the theorem, gives two worked counterexamples with figures, then announces three results (pp. 1–5). "For the sake of completeness" proofs of standard facts (pp. 5–6). Hedged precision about which equality holds: Ex. 1.1 shows that under Thms 1.2–1.3 only v^L = v̄* "must hold", not v^L = v* (p. 5).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution is theoretical and demarcating: two failure examples plus three sufficient conditions. Open questions named concretely: Thm 1.2 without attainment of (3), and Thm 1.4 for rational multi-row systems, with Ex. 3.1 showing why the obvious proof fails (pp. 7–8); a broader direction via Hilbert-basis finiteness (p. 8). No computational relevance is claimed.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper is a demarcation exercise: counterexamples for each dropped hypothesis (Ex. 1.1, 1.2), sufficient conditions, a remark that Lemma 2.2 fails in R³, and open questions where the proof's key assumption may be removable (pp. 2–8).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: concerns when the Lagrangian dual bound is as good as the convexified primal, i.e. the quality of a certificate, but at the level of theory rather than computation (pp. 1, 5).
- New-pattern candidate: "test a classical theorem's implicit hypothesis by counterexample, then give sufficient conditions" (pp. 1–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Separate the closure question from the value question: compare v^L with both v* (hull) and v̄* (closed hull); a counterexample may kill one equality and not the other (pp. 2–3, 5).
- Use Slater's condition to dispose of the interior case and reduce a duality question to the boundary of conv(X) (p. 5).
- Box-localization plus Farkas to transfer LP duality to a locally polyhedral convex set (p. 6).
- When stating an open case, give the natural proof strategy and an example where it fails although the conclusion holds (Ex. 3.1, p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "The challenge for extending Geoffrion’s theorem beyond finiteness and rationality is thus to understand integer programs whose feasibility set lies on the boundary of conv(X)." (p. 5)
- "We note here that (3) having an optimal solution is a crucial assumption in our proof." (p. 7)

### S117 · Decomposition of Sparse Integer Programs via Nonlinear Edge Encodings and Column-and-Row Generation (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2609.05665)
**Read level**: full. §§1–8 (pp. 1–32) read line by line, including the lower-bound proofs of §5.2–5.4 (pp. 14–19); figures (pp. 24, 26, 29) seen only through captions and text (images not in the extraction); references (pp. 32–35) scanned for [16], [22], [29]. Read version: arXiv:2609.05665v1 of 4 Sep 2026 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Gustavo Angulo (PUC Chile; Dey's former student per SKILL.md Lineage) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: computational follow-up of Cifuentes–Dey–Xu, IPCO 2025 ([16], "Lagrangian dual for integer optimization with zero duality gap that admits decomposition", p. 33), in the 2023–2026 duality and decomposition line; reuses Angulo–Ahmed–Dey's integer L-shaped work [3] as a baseline (p. 33).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a named tension: dual decomposition is parallel and gives bounds but leaves a duality gap; methods that close it give up separability, generality, or intermediate bounds (p. 2). Their own IPCO 2025 result closes the gap in principle by dualizing redundant nonlinear consistency constraints, but "A zero-gap Lagrangian dual is not yet an algorithm": the family is exponential and subgradient methods return no primal point to separate from (p. 2). Problem: make the zero-gap dual computable without building it (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when your own theorem is existence-only, write the follow-up paper around the two concrete reasons it is not yet an algorithm.
- **D2 Core idea**: Treat the Geoffrion-style primal characterization (4) as the computational object and solve it by Dantzig–Wolfe: columns are lifted local integer solutions (priced per block in parallel), rows are consistency constraints on functions of shared variables, generated by separation (pp. 3, 6–9). The master's convex weights give "boundary distributions" ν on each edge; a violated encoding is any function with different expectations under the two distributions (eq. (8), p. 8). Encoding families: Vertex and Monomial from [16], plus new Reflected and Generalized (products of literals) (§4, pp. 9–12). Claimed as the first decomposition that is at once exact, decomposable (valid dual bound every outer iteration) and structurally general (tree block graph, binary shared variables, nothing else) (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a strong dual has a convex primal form, generate both the columns and the strengthening rows of that primal, so separation replaces explicit enumeration of the family.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Block interaction graph is a tree; shared variables are binary; local sets otherwise arbitrary, no convexity or recourse structure (pp. 3, 5). Exactness needs a complete family and exact separation (pp. 8–9). Separation of Monomial/Reflected/Generalized rows is itself a MIP (pp. 10–11). In experiments: 15 blocks of 100 binaries, 500 random edges per block, coupling q ∈ {20, 30, 40}, four topologies, deterministic and stochastic objectives, 5 seeds (pp. 20–21).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Lemma 4.1: Monomial and Reflected span the same function space (inclusion–exclusion), giving exactness of Reflected and Generalized from Thm 2.1 (pp. 11). Gadget lemma 5.9: on a one-edge instance with X_i, X_j built from F and its complement, k rows close the gap iff there is a "margin separator" r(z) ≤ −1 on F, ≥ 1 off F built from k family functions plus an affine part, proved from the master's duals and reduced costs (pp. 13–14). This turns row counts into sign-representation questions. Complementation symmetry z ↦ 1 − z swaps Monomial and Reflected and transfers bounds (p. 14). Lower bounds: Minsky–Papert polynomial threshold degree of the group-covering function (Thm 5.10) and parity (Thm 5.12) combined with random restrictions that kill all high-degree terms while leaving enough free variables, plus a binomial tail bound (12) (pp. 14–18). Vertex lower bound 2^{n/3} by a four-point affine identity ℓ(u)+ℓ(v)=ℓ(m)+ℓ(m′) on disjoint quadruples (p. 17). Separations: Monomial vs Reflected 2^{Ω(n^{1/3})} each way (Thm 5.2, Cor. 5.3); Generalized beats both (Thm 5.4) and Vertex (Thm 5.5); Generalized needs (1/2)(4/3)^{⌈n/4⌉} rows on parity (Thm 5.6), which one parity encoding closes (Prop. 5.7) (pp. 12–19). Every instance is "tractable" (polynomial linear-optimization oracles) so that the hardness is about encodings, not blocks (p. 13).
  - Transferable: to lower-bound how many generated rows a method needs, reduce "rows close the gap" to an LP-duality certificate on a gadget, then to a sign-representation degree bound from Boolean-function complexity.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Python 3.13, Gurobi 13.0.1, EC2 c8a.4xlarge (16 vCPU, 32 GB), 1800 s limit; pricing of 15 blocks in parallel; CRG warm-started from a short monolithic solve and its LP bound (pp. 21–22). 180 instances (stable set and dominating set). Ranking criterion fixed in advance and lexicographic: solved count, censored time, final gap (p. 23). Results: stable set Hybrid 50/90, Monomial 49, Vertex 47, Generalized 32, Reflected 31, monolithic 14; dominating set Vertex 19/90, monolithic 0/90 (Table 1, p. 23). Breakdown by topology, coupling and stochastic flag; coupling is "the most visible difficulty driver" (Table 3, p. 27). Specialized baselines (integer L-shaped, scenario decomposition) run on star instances and reported as faster (stable set 4.6 s and 7.0 s vs about 20 s for CRG; L-shaped closes 20/30 dominating-set stars vs 11 for Vertex) (Table 4, pp. 27–28). Mechanism study: heatmaps of cuts per outer iteration and edge; row-diversity statistics (budget share, density, pairwise Jaccard distance, near-duplicate and nested fractions) show Reflected/Generalized fill 87–91% of their row budget with dense, near-duplicate rows while Monomial uses 14–20% (Table 5, p. 30); Generalized rows are mostly negative-only and match Reflected supports (Table 6, p. 31).
  - Transferable: when a richer family underperforms, log every generated row and measure diversity (Jaccard distance, near-duplicates, budget use) to explain why.
- **D6 Positioning**: Related work organized on three axes (exactness, decomposability, structural generality), with inexact methods (Lagrangian, progressive hedging), master–recourse methods (integer L-shaped, Benders and Lagrangian cuts, SDDiP), non-decomposable duals (augmented and semi-Lagrangian) and exponential-size exact methods (nonserial DP, Bienstock–Muñoz LP) each placed in one cell (pp. 4–5). Benders-type methods are credited as "particularly close in spirit" (p. 4). The scope claim is modest: CRG is meant "not to replace specialized methods when additional structure is available" (p. 32).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Bold paragraph heads carry the argument ("Decomposability against bound quality.", "A zero-gap Lagrangian dual is not yet an algorithm.", "Encoding choice is a modeling decision.") (pp. 2–3). Contributions as four numbered items (pp. 3–4). Section 5 states all separations up front as theorems before proofs (pp. 12–13). The headline computational lesson is stated against the theory: richer is not better (p. 32).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic (CRG), theoretical (encoding separations), computational (benchmark plus mechanism study). Limits stated: specialized methods faster on stars (pp. 27–28, 32); interface size drives difficulty (p. 32); Generalized family not universal (p. 13). Future work: adaptive encoding selection from boundary distributions, duals and row redundancy, and problem-specific encodings (p. 32). Remark 5.8 turns the gadget lesson into a modelling rule (expose cross-block implications as local constraints), implemented as conflict propagation and neighbourhood-dominance preprocessing (pp. 13, 19–20).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the selling point is a valid dual bound after every outer iteration and exactness without branching (pp. 3, 9, 31–32).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: each encoding family is demarcated by exponential lower bounds, and the strongest family is shown not universal with a one-row problem-specific counterexample (Thms 5.2–5.6, Prop. 5.7, pp. 12–19).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: no exact ceiling, but specialized baselines are given their natural setting (star topology) and reported as faster, and the mechanism is measured from logged rows (pp. 27–31).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the paper ends by reporting where its theory does not predict practice (the theoretically strongest family performs worse) and names what is missing (row diversity) (pp. 29–32).
- New-pattern candidate: "redundant constraints that help a relaxation or solver" (redundant encoding rows strengthen the Lagrangian dual; Remark 5.8's redundant local constraints strengthen pricing; pp. 2, 13; cf. S111 p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: "expressiveness vs usefulness: measure whether a richer cut/encoding family helps, and diagnose with row-diversity statistics" (pp. 29–32).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Gadget lemma: one edge, X_i built from F and X_j from its complement, so that OPT = 0 and the number of rows needed equals the size of a margin separator (pp. 13–14).
- Random restrictions with labels {free, fix0, fix1} and a binomial tail bound to kill high-degree terms while keeping n/4 free variables, then apply a sign-degree lower bound (pp. 17–18).
- Read violated consistency rows off the master as differences of boundary distributions over signatures (eq. (8), p. 8).
- Fix the comparison criterion (solved count, then censored time, then gap) before reporting, and report residual gaps on unsolved runs separately (p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "A zero-gap Lagrangian dual is not yet an algorithm." (p. 2)
- "greater expressive power does not necessarily translate into a better algorithm." (p. 32)

### S118 · Branching with a pre-specified finite list of k-sparse split sets for binary MILPs (Operations Research Letters 2026, doi:10.1016/j.orl.2026.107491; arXiv:2408.05392)
**Read level**: full. All 10 pages read line by line, including all case proofs (pp. 4–8). Read version: arXiv:2408.05392v1 of 10 Aug 2024 (p. 1); the 2026 journal version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Diego Morán (RPI), Jingye Xu (Georgia Tech) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: branch-and-bound theory line (2018–2026), joining the sparse-cut work ([12], [13]) and the B&B-on-random-instances and tree-size lower-bound papers ([10], [11], [14]) cited on pp. 1–2.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a practice in the literature that works without theory: several papers ([24, 20, 22, 26]) report much smaller trees when branching on split disjunctions of fixed sparsity with coefficients in {−1, 0, 1}, i.e. on a pre-specified finite list of dense disjunctions (p. 2). The question: is it enough to restrict to a finite list to get "the full power" of k-sparse branching? (p. 2)
  - Transferable: read a cluster of computational papers as one paradigm ("expanding the list of disjunctions") and ask the one geometric question that would justify it.
- **D2 Core idea**: Define dominance of split sets on the cube (S¹ ∩ [0,1]^n ⊇ S⁰ ∩ [0,1]^n), which cannot increase tree size (p. 2), and its relaxation to covering by p sets, which increases the tree by at most a factor 2^{p−1} (p. 3). Results: k = 2, every 2-sparse split is dominated by one in F₂ (Prop. 1, p. 2); k ≥ 3, no finite list suffices (Thm 2, p. 2); covering number of any finite list ≥ ⌊k/2⌋ (Thm 3, p. 3; floor stated in the abstract, p. 1); C(F_k) ≤ k − 1 for k = 2, 3, 4 (Prop. 4, p. 3). The contrast is spelled out: for cuts, finite lists of split sets do suffice (split-closure polyhedrality) (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: when an operation (branching) and its cousin (cutting) use the same objects, check whether a finiteness theorem for one transfers to the other; a "no" is itself a result.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Pure binary MILPs; disjunctions π^T x ≤ η ∨ π^T x ≥ η + 1 with integer π, η; sparsity ||π||₀ ≤ k (p. 1). Dominance is measured on [0,1]^n only, i.e. independent of the LP (p. 2). Tree-size consequences are worst-case factors, not measured (pp. 2–3).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: WLOG 0 ≤ π₁ ≤ … ≤ π_k by complementing and permuting variables, since F_k is closed under these (p. 4). Prop. 1 and Prop. 4 by exhaustive case analysis on where η sits relative to partial sums of π (3 cases for k = 2, 3 for k = 3, ten for k = 4; pp. 4, 6–8), with k = 3, 4 reduced to the previous k by conditioning on x_k ∈ {0}, (0,1), {1}. Thm 2 by an infinite family S_γ = {γ < x₁ + γx₂ + (γ+1)x₃ < γ+1}: a dominating split must contain specific cube vertices on its boundary, which pins π = (1, η, η+1) and then fails as γ → ∞ (pp. 4–5). Thm 3 by contradiction: pigeonhole gives p ≤ k−1 sets dominating infinitely many S^θ; a nonzero solution of k + p < 2k homogeneous equations gives a direction along which a cube vertex moves into S^θ while staying in one cell A_u of the arrangement (pp. 5–6). Only the k = 3 case of Thm 2 and the even case of Thm 3 are proved in full; the others are "similar" (pp. 4, 6).
  - Transferable: to show no finite list can work, build a one-parameter family of targets and use pigeonhole to fix finitely many candidates, then a dimension count to find a bad point.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (no computation; the motivating evidence is cited from [24, 20, 22, 15, 26], p. 4).
- **D6 Positioning**: Takes the side of the dense-branching literature against the 1-sparse default, while citing his own random-instance result that 1-sparse branching suffices there (p. 2). Uses the cutting-plane finiteness results (Andersen–Cornuéjols–Li, Averkov, Dash–Günlük–Morán) as the foil (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Short-note format: all results in §2 "Main results" before any proof, conclusions in §3 before the proofs (pp. 2–4). Each negative result is followed by "the next natural question" (p. 3). The k = 3 statement in the conclusions (any finite list needs at least 2, and F₃ attains 2) combines Thm 2 and Prop. 4; the conclusions cite Thm 3 for the lower bound, which by its floor gives only 1 at k = 3 (my reading, pp. 1, 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Theory justifying {−1, 0, 1} lists for k ≤ 3 (optimal for k = 3 by covering number) (p. 3). Open: whether F_k is optimal for larger k; both Thm 3 and Prop. 4 "may need to be tightened and generalized" (pp. 3–4). The result is also read as a warning: pre-specified lists "may not be the best way", and problem-specific dense disjunctions may be needed (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: an empirically successful branching practice is shrunk to a geometric proxy (dominance and covering number on the cube) with a measure defined for every split, and the paper says how much of the practice it justifies (k ≤ 3) and where it suggests a different practice (pp. 2–4).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: positive result at k = 2, impossibility at k ≥ 3, lower bound ⌊k/2⌋ vs upper bound k − 1, the gap stated as open (pp. 2–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define a dominance relation that provably cannot increase tree size, then a covering number that degrades it by an explicit factor (2^{p−1}), so that a geometric quantity has a tree-size meaning (pp. 2–3).
- Reduce case analysis by symmetry: complement and permute variables to sort coefficients, after checking the candidate list is closed under the same operations (p. 4).
- Write the conclusion section as both a justification of the practice and a warning about its ceiling (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "If one can show that a finite list of k-sparse disjunctions has a small covering number, then it could be considered a theoretical justification for using just this finite list of pre-specified k-sparse disjunctions instead of general k-sparse disjunctions." (p. 3)
- "More generally, Theorem 3 may also be an indication that the use of pre-specified list of disjunctions may not be the best way to generate small branch-and-bound trees." (p. 4)

### S119 · Copositive Matrices with Ordered Off-Diagonal Entries (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2605.15970)
**Read level**: full. All 17 pages read; reference list (pp. 15–16) scanned. Read version: arXiv:2605.15970v1 of 15 May 2026 (p. 1). The matrices of Ex. 4.4, 5.6 and 6.2 are partly garbled by extraction but readable.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Grigoriy Blekherman, Alex Dunbar (Georgia Tech Mathematics), Burak Kocuk (Sabancı; Dey's former student per SKILL.md Lineage) (pp. 1, 17); authors in alphabetical order, individual roles not stated · **Place in trajectory**: SDP and real-algebraic collaboration with Blekherman (2020–2026); resolves Conjecture 1 of Dey–Kocuk's 2025 preprint on separable functions over polyhedra ([DK25], arXiv:2510.16595, pp. 2, 13, 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two goals (p. 1): find conditions, depending only on off-diagonal entries, under which a copositive matrix is SPN (PSD + nonnegative), a classical question since Diananda's SPN = COP iff n ≤ 4; and use them to show the SPN/DNN relaxation of standard quadratic programs (StQP) is exact. The concrete trigger is an open conjecture from Dey–Kocuk: the DNN relaxation of separable StQP is always tight (pp. 2, 13).
  - Transferable: when your own applied paper leaves a conjecture about a relaxation's exactness, recast it as a structural statement about the cone (here COP vs SPN) and recruit the people who own that cone.
- **D2 Core idea**: An inductive framework (Thm 3.1, pp. 4–5): if every matrix of a class has a row with all off-diagonal entries of one sign, deleting a nonnegative row stays in the class, and the Schur complement on a nonpositive row stays in the class, then copositive ⇒ SPN by induction from n = 4, using Shaked-Monderer's block lemma (Lemma 2.7). Ordered off-diagonal entries (nondecreasing in rows and columns, class M_n) satisfy it (Lemma 3.6, Thm 3.5, pp. 5–6). Relaxed ordering keyed to the first row with a positive entry (Thm 3.9, pp. 6–8); block corollaries (Cor. 3.10, Prop. 3.11, p. 8). Optimization link: since M_n + λE = M_n, the SPN bound equals the COP bound for any Q ∈ M_n (Obs. 5.2, Cor. 5.5, pp. 12); separable StQP matrices permute into M_n by sorting α_i, which proves the conjecture (Prop. 5.7, p. 13).
  - Transferable: if a matrix class is invariant under adding multiples of the all-ones matrix, a cone-equality result on the class immediately gives exactness of the relaxation for every objective in the class.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Symmetric real matrices; StQP min{x^T Q x : x ∈ Δ_n} with its CP/COP reformulation and DNN/SPN relaxations, strong duality between both pairs (p. 11). The framework's condition (3.1.III) is recursive, and the authors note it is "difficult to check" for a class (p. 5); this is why concrete ordered classes are studied.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Induction on n through Schur complements and row deletions (Thm 3.1, p. 5); the key computation is that the Schur complement on a first row with negative entries preserves the ordering, s_{i,k} − s_{i,j} ≥ 0 (p. 6). Group-orbit extension: K_n = cone(G_n M_n ∩ COP_n) ⊆ SPN_n (Cor. 4.2, p. 9); membership by an LP for diagonal scaling (Prop. 4.7, p. 10) and by acyclicity of a digraph / topological sort for permutations (Prop. 4.8, p. 11). Demarcations: an SPN 5-cycle matrix outside the orbit (Ex. 4.4, p. 9); K_n ≠ SPN_n for n ≥ 4 via vv^T with v = (−1, −1, 1, …, 1) (Prop. 4.6, p. 10); sign graphs of orbit matrices are threshold (Prop. 6.1, p. 14), but threshold sign graphs are not sufficient, shown by a 6×6 matrix whose Schur complement is the Horn matrix (Ex. 6.2, pp. 14–15). A short alternative proof of Gökmen–Yıldırım's convex-objective exactness by an (n+1)-bordered matrix and Finsler's lemma, avoiding an explicit decomposition (Prop. 5.4, p. 12).
  - Transferable: to show a sufficient condition is not necessary, take a matrix with the same sign pattern and show its Schur complement is a known counterexample (Horn).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (no computation; one worked example, Ex. 5.6 from Gökmen–Yıldırım, permuted into M_5 with z_COP = 1, pp. 12–13).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Diananda, Shaked-Monderer's "SPN graphs" and Gökmen–Yıldırım 2022 (pp. 1–4, 12). Relation to the perfect-graph exactness result of Gökmen–Yıldırım is stated precisely: threshold graphs are a strict subset of perfect graphs, but the new conditions do not involve the diagonal (pp. 2, 15). Prior exactness results are re-proved through Observation 5.2 "for completeness" to show the lens "unifies and simplifies" them (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Main theorem stated in the introduction with a forward pointer (Thm 1.1 = Thm 3.5, p. 1); one figure for the relaxed ordering (Fig. 1, p. 7); explicit remark on the weakness of the general framework (p. 5). The paper ends on the comparison with perfect graphs rather than a conclusions section (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution is theoretical: a new SPN-exact class, its orbit, polynomial membership tests, new and simplified StQP exactness results, and a resolved conjecture (pp. 1–2, 13). Limits shown rather than stated as future work: orbit cone strictly inside SPN for n ≥ 4; threshold sign graphs not sufficient; Prop. 4.7–4.8 handle scaling and permutation separately while the full group G_n is larger (p. 11). No explicit future-work section (pp. 14–15).

**Method links**:
