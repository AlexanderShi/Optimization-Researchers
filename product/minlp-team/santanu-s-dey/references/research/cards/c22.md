### S124 · Mixed-Integer Programming for Change-point Detection (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2602.11947)

**Read level**: full. Version read: arXiv v2, titled "Change-Point Detection via Piecewise Linear Fitting Using MIP" (p. 1); same three authors. Pages 1–16 read closely (intro, §2 formulations, §3 LP-relaxation comparison with proofs, §4 experiment design, §5 results, §6 conclusion); Appendices A–C (big-M derivation, ℓ0 extension, feasibility construction for Prop. 2, pp. 18–21) read; Appendices D–F (per-instance runtime tables, pp. 22–24) skimmed; references (pp. 17–18) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Apoorva Narula, Yao Xie (both Georgia Tech ISyE) · **Place in trajectory**: a 2026 application of MILP formulation-strength theory to a statistics problem; it cites his own non-monotonicity paper (Shah, Dey, Molinaro, IJOC 2026) as the caveat on what a tighter LP relaxation buys (pp. 10, 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Offline multiple change-point detection cast as globally optimal piecewise-linear (PWL) fitting with K contiguous segments (pp. 1, 3). The entry is a known bottleneck of the existing MIPs: "weak linear programming (LP) relaxations, which admit fractional segment assignments and can lead to excessive branch-and-bound effort" (p. 1). The targets are the two existing contiguous-segmentation MIPs, Goldberg et al. 2021 ("Basic") and Rebennack–Krasko 2020 ("Alternative", the most effective in the Warwicker–Rebennack 2022 comparison) (pp. 2–4). On the statistics side, penalization methods "cannot model continuous piecewise linear fits or be extended to enforce shared segmentation across multiple dimensions" (p. 2), and Cao et al. 2018 fix the pre-change slope to zero, "Our model relaxes this assumption" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a field already has two competing MIP formulations, isolate the one constraint block they share (here, segment assignment) and attack only that block, so the comparison is clean.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Replace the segment-assignment block by nested, monotonically non-increasing binaries X_{j,t} = 1 iff observation t lies in one of the first j segments, and define δ as differences of consecutive X vectors ((2.3.1)–(2.3.3), pp. 7–8). What changes: only the assignment block (C1); what stays: value-assignment, breakpoint-localization and continuity constraints of each base model ("All remaining constraints of the respective formulations remain unchanged", p. 8). The block is modular, which gives Extended Basic and Extended Alternative (p. 8). Side results: Basic ≡ Extended Basic in feasible region (Remark 3, p. 8); Extended Alternative strictly contains Alternative because it allows unused segments (Remark 4, pp. 8–9), but all four have optimal solutions that coincide in (m, c, ŷ) (Remark 5, p. 9). Allowing unused segments makes K an upper bound and enables an ℓ0 penalty on segments used (Remark 6, p. 9; App. B, p. 20). Extension to D-dimensional responses with shared breakpoints by sharing the binaries (Remark 7, pp. 9–10).
  - Transferable: rewrite ordered assignment variables in cumulative ("first j of K") form; the ordering constraints become two-nonzero rows of opposite sign, so the block is TU.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: One-dimensional domain (d = 1), mostly uniformly sampled time index (p. 3); fixed K (or K as an upper bound with ℓ0, p. 9); ℓ1 or ℓ2 loss (p. 3); parameters in a data-driven box Θ with big-M constants copied from Rebennack–Krasko "To ensure a fair and consistent comparison across MIP benchmarks" (App. A, pp. 18–19); continuity optional via bilinear constraints (2.1.8) in Basic (p. 5) or via slope-direction indicators and linearized constraints in Alternative (pp. 5–7); no continuity in the multi-dimensional case, "consistent with the literature in this area" (p. 3; p. 10). The authors note that the discontinuous case is solvable by dynamic programming (Bai–Perron 2003) and say they do not run it because the focus is the MIP formulations (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: (i) Prop. 1 (pp. 10–11): the (C1) polyhedron is integral because the X-part has at most two nonzeros of opposite sign per row (TU) and the δ-part is an identity block; bounds append identity rows. Corollary 1: the projection onto δ is integral (p. 11). (ii) Prop. 2 (pp. 11–12): strict containment of the Extended Basic projection in the Basic one via a total-variation invariant. Nested monotone X gives TV(X_j) ≤ 1, hence TV(δ_j) ≤ 2 (Lemma 1, triangle inequality), and an explicit oscillating fractional δ (period-2 0.4/0.1 pattern) with TV > 2 is shown feasible for the Basic LPR by hand-built breakpoints r_1 = r_2 = r_3 = (x_1 + x_8)/2 (App. C, pp. 20–21). (iii) Prop. 3 (pp. 12–13): the Alternative assignment polytope has a fractional extreme point (T = 6, K = 4), certified by exhibiting 24 linearly independent tight constraints that uniquely determine the point. (iv) Contiguity of the Alternative block proved by backward propagation of non-contiguity to segment 1 (Remark 1, p. 6). The authors bound their own theory: "the polyhedral comparisons in this section are not necessarily conclusive with respect to overall computational performance" (p. 13).
  - Transferable: to separate two LP relaxations in a projected space, find a simple functional (here total variation) that is bounded on one projection, then exhibit a point of the other that violates it.
  - Transferable: certify a fractional vertex by listing n tight constraints and showing that they solve uniquely for the point (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Gurobi 13.0.2 on an Apple M3, one thread per run "to control for variability" from parallelism and frequency scaling, three seeds (0, 10000, 20000), arithmetic means, 2000 s limit, minimal non-default parameters, code and outputs on GitHub (p. 14). Data: daily closing prices of stock tickers from yfinance from Jan 1, 2015 (pp. 14–15). Grids: without continuity T ∈ {100,…,500}, K ∈ {2,…,6}; with continuity T ∈ {100,…,180}, K ∈ {2,…,5}, shortened because longer series "more frequently hit the runtime limit" (p. 14); multi-dimensional D ∈ {2,…,10} (pp. 14–15); 200 settings per class, 600 in total. Metrics: number of instances on which each formulation has the lowest mean runtime (ties at the limit broken by gap) (Table 1, p. 15), and cumulative runtime with time-outs counted as 2000 s (Table 2, p. 16). Results: Extended fastest on 375/600 vs Alternative 163 and Basic 62 (p. 15); cumulative 171,884 s vs 216,380 s (Alternative) and 246,307 s (Basic) (p. 16). Not uniform: with continuity under ℓ1, Basic is fastest on 42/100 (Table 1, p. 15), and in the multi-dimensional ℓ2 row Extended's cumulative time (35,069.37 s) exceeds Alternative's (32,891.84 s) (Table 2, p. 16). Per-instance tables (pp. 22–24) report gaps for runs that hit the limit; the "bold entries" mentioned in the captions are lost in the text layer. There is no detection-accuracy evaluation and no synthetic ground truth: only solver time is measured.
  - Transferable: report both an instance-wise win count and a cumulative-time total with time-outs charged at the limit; the two can disagree, and showing both exposes it (Tables 1–2, pp. 15–16).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Organised by what each line cannot do: Bertsimas–Shioda 2007 do not enforce contiguity; Toriello–Vielma 2012 need a fixed grid; Cui et al. 2018 give up global optimality (p. 2); penalization and BIC methods cannot handle continuous PWL fits or shared segmentation (p. 2); multivariate work focuses on mean shifts (p. 2). The paper builds directly on the PWL-fitting MIP line (Rebennack–Krasko, Kong–Maravelias, Goldberg et al.), takes the Warwicker–Rebennack comparison as the benchmark choice, and keeps the benchmark's big-M (pp. 2–3, 19). Formulation strength is grounded in textbooks (Nemhauser–Wolsey; Conforti–Cornuéjols–Zambelli) and hedged by the author's own non-monotonicity result (Shah, Dey, Molinaro 2026) (pp. 10, 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Three-bullet contribution list on p. 2; a terminology note separating "change-point" (signal) from "breakpoint" (decision variable) (p. 2); named formulations (Basic / Alternative / Extended) that make tables readable; feasibility relations stated as numbered Remarks with short proofs (pp. 6–9); a closing summary that states the caveat on polyhedral comparisons (p. 13). Claim strength varies across the paper: the intro says the formulations "consistently achieve global optimality faster than existing benchmarks" (p. 1), while Tables 1–2 show wins on 375 of 600 settings and one class where Extended is not lowest (pp. 15–16); the conclusion says the experiments "empirically verify the claimed improvements" (p. 16).
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: Contribution levels: formulation (extended assignment block), polyhedral theory (integral projection; non-integrality of the two benchmarks), a computational study with released code, plus extensions (multi-dimensional, ℓ0) (pp. 2, 16). Future work named: decomposition for scalability and online variants (p. 16). Limitations visible but not all stated: instances up to T = 500 (T ≤ 180 with continuity) (p. 14); only the assignment block is strengthened, while big-M value-assignment constraints stay (pp. 4–5); Extended Alternative is a relaxation of Alternative's feasible region, equivalent only in optimal (m, c, ŷ) (Remarks 4–5, pp. 8–9); no comparison with the DP baseline for the discontinuous case (p. 10); a single data family (stock prices).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the IP move of convexifying a recurring substructure jointly. The segment-assignment block, shared by both MIP(NL) formulations (Basic carries bilinear continuity constraints, p. 5), gets an extended formulation whose projection is the integer hull (Prop. 1, Cor. 1, pp. 10–11).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the positive result comes with negatives. Basic is strictly weaker (Prop. 2 with explicit point, pp. 11–12, 20–21), the Alternative block has a fractional vertex (Prop. 3, pp. 12–13), and the paper states that polyhedral strength is "not necessarily conclusive" for run time (p. 13).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: no ceiling and no stripped instrument. The evaluation is full Gurobi with minimal non-default settings, one thread and three seeds, and run time is the only metric (p. 14). Ground truth enters only as provable integrality of the LP projection, not as an optimal-tree or best-possible comparison.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the certificate is a globally optimal fit from a MIP with a stronger LP bound; the paper is about the speed of reaching that certificate, not about a cheap relaxation used as the deliverable (pp. 1, 15–16).
- Heuristic 2 ✅ evidence (give the baseline its best shot): the benchmark's big-M construction is reused "To ensure a fair and consistent comparison" (p. 19), and solver tuning is minimised (p. 14).
- Workflow D checkpoint ("hold a superlative") ✗ against: "consistently achieve global optimality faster" (p. 1) and "consistently outperform their respective benchmark formulations in terms of cumulative runtime" (p. 16) sit next to Table 1's 42/100 Basic wins and Table 2's multi-dimensional ℓ2 row (pp. 15–16). The second sentence is literally true for the per-norm continuity comparison it refers to.
- New-pattern candidate: "Hedge a formulation-strength claim with your own non-monotonicity theorem" (Shah, Dey, Molinaro 2026 cited at pp. 10 and 13 to say that a tighter LP need not mean faster B&B).
- New-pattern candidate: "Cumulative (nested monotone) reformulation of ordered assignment binaries to get a TU block" (pp. 7–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Nested cumulative binaries X_{j,t} with δ_{j,t} = X_{j,t} − X_{j−1,t} and two monotonicity families; a TU argument via rows with two opposite-sign nonzeros plus an identity block (pp. 7–11).
- Total-variation separation of two projected LP relaxations, backed by an explicit fractional point and a hand-built feasible completion in an appendix (pp. 11–12, 20–21).

- Experimental hygiene: single thread, three seeds, mean, fixed limit, time-outs charged at the limit, minimal tuning, code and raw outputs released (pp. 14, 16).

### S125 · Asymptotically tight Lagrangian dual of smooth nonconvex problems via improved error bound of Shapley-Folkman Lemma (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2601.19003)

**Read level**: full. Version read: arXiv v2 (9 Feb 2026). Pages 1–23 read closely (intro, §2 notation and measures, §3 duality-gap theorem with proof, §4 geometric Shapley–Folkman proof, randomized-rounding bound, hidden-convex-component theorem, remarks with figures, §5 application with Lemmas 8–10, Claims 5–6, Theorem 6); references (pp. 23–25) skimmed. The paper has no appendix and no conclusion section. Figures 1–10 are not visible in the text layer; only their captions and descriptions were read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jingye Xu (Georgia Tech ISyE, corresponding author) · **Place in trajectory**: part of the 2023–2026 Lagrangian-duality line. It cites Cifuentes–Dey–Xu IPCO 2025 (decomposable zero-gap Lagrangian dual) and Dey–Meunier–Ramirez 2025 (Geoffrion beyond finiteness) as its own context (pp. 2, 8, 23–25).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry from the Lagrangian dual of a block-structured nonconvex program (1)–(3) (p. 2). It plays two roles: a decomposable lower bound in optimization, and equilibrium prices in economics (pp. 2–3). The cut-in is that strong duality needs only convexity of the image set P = Σ P^(i) in (objective, constraint) space, not convexity of each X^(i) (p. 3, citing Ben-Tal–Nemirovski; the S-Lemma via Dines' theorem is given as the model example, p. 3). The classical Shapley–Folkman bound d_H(P, conv P) ≤ √n R does not vanish (p. 4). The question is posed explicitly: can the nonconvexity of the Minkowski sum vanish, rather than stay at a constant, as k grows (abstract, p. 1)? The analysis is done on the unnormalized sum because "its convexity directly reflects the duality gap" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: reduce "is this dual tight?" to "is this image set convex?", then ask how fast a measure of its nonconvexity goes to zero.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Four steps. (a) Duality gap ≤ E + OPT(b) − OPT(b + E·1) with E = Φ(P) (Theorem 3, pp. 8–9), via the primal characterization OPT_L = DUAL (Theorem 2 / Assumption 1, p. 8). (b) A new elementary proof of Shapley–Folkman under pointedness: every y in conv(Σ A_i) lies in a sum of faces F_i with Σ dim F_i ≤ n (Lemma 4, pp. 10–11), so at least k − n faces are extreme points lying in A_i. (c) Randomized rounding: sample each nonzero-dimensional summand from a Carathéodory distribution (Lemma 5, p. 12). Variances add, giving Theorem 4: Φ ≤ sup √(Σ Ξ²(A_i ∩ F_i)) over face choices with total dimension n (p. 13); for A_i = C \ int C this gives R instead of √n R (p. 13). (d) Main idea: "hidden convex components" (Def. 2, pp. 13–14), i.e. convex subsets of A_i around the extreme points. If the squared gauge of their sum H is L-smooth, the rounding error measured in the H-gauge is at most √(1 + (L/2) Σ Ξ²) − 1 (Lemma 7, pp. 15–16). With balls of radius r_i at extreme points, Φ ≤ √(r*² + nβ²/2) − r* → 0 as k → ∞ (Theorem 5, p. 16). Application: the epigraph of an L-smooth f contains a ball of radius 1/L touching each graph point (Claim 5, p. 21). The coupling map B turns it into an ellipsoid, which contains a ball of radius L_s(B)/L (Def. 6, Lemma 11, Claim 6, pp. 20–22). Theorem 6 then gives E → 0 for problem (4), including sparsity constraints (pp. 22–23).
  - Transferable: when a covering or rounding error accumulates over independent pieces, use a probabilistic rounding whose variances add, then absorb the remaining error into a large convex "slack" body (here the sum of hidden balls).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1, OPT_L(b) = DUAL(b), set up to "exclude these pathological instances" (p. 8). Assumption 2: each conv(A_i) closed and pointed (p. 9). Assumption 3: bounded inner radius Ξ(P^(i)) ≤ β (p. 19). Assumption 4: each f^(i) 1-coercive, closed, proper, f^(i)(0) < ∞ (p. 19). Each f^(i) L-smooth (Theorem 6, p. 22). Projection factor L_s(B^(i)) ≥ ω > 0, which needs s^(i) ≥ m and "holds generically" (Remark 4, p. 21). Which hypotheses are needed is argued with examples: singletons at extreme points do not suffice ({0,1}^n, p. 14); balls need smooth gauges (p-norm example, Remark 2, p. 17); components are needed only at extreme points (Remark 3, p. 17); global smoothness can be relaxed to a local quadratic upper bound (Remark 5, p. 23). Reader note (my reading, unconfirmed by the authors): Theorem 6 sandwiches DUAL between OPT_s(b + E1) − E and OPT_s(b) (p. 23). The gap itself therefore vanishes only if OPT_s is continuous in b at the right point, and the text does not state that condition. Reader note: Claims 5 and 6 call f "a smooth convex function" / "a L-smooth convex function" (pp. 21–22) although the setting is nonconvex; the proofs use only the quadratic upper bound (9).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: Face-dimension reduction: with Σ dim F_i > n, rank–nullity gives directions d_i in the face subspaces summing to 0 (Claim 1, p. 10). Moving along them keeps the sum fixed, and pointedness ensures some y_i reaches a relative boundary and drops to a lower-dimensional face (Claim 2, Lemma 4, pp. 10–11). Face heredity of the inner radius, Ξ(A ∩ F) ≤ Ξ(A) (Claim 4, p. 11), with the remark that this fails for general intersections. Carathéodory distribution with variance ≤ Ξ² (Lemma 5, p. 12); independence makes the variance additive and existence follows by averaging (Lemma 6, p. 12). L-smoothness of the squared gauge applied to the random rounding error; the gradient term vanishes in expectation (Lemma 7, pp. 15–16). Explicit inscribed ball in the epigraph of an L-smooth function by completing the square (Claim 5, p. 21). Closedness and pointedness of conv(epi) by 1-coercivity and a pigeonhole argument on a Carathéodory representation (Lemma 8, pp. 19–20); closedness of the linear image via Rockafellar Thm 9.1 (Lemma 9–10, p. 20). Inscribed ball of an ellipsoid (Lemma 11, p. 22).
  - Transferable: prove Shapley–Folkman-type statements by face-dimension counting plus a kernel-direction move, instead of Carathéodory in a lifted space; the face structure then feeds sharper bounds.
  - Transferable: to show smoothness is needed, give a two-line Minkowski-sum example (a two-point set plus k p-norm balls) where the hollow persists for p = 1 and vanishes for p > 1 (Remark 2, p. 17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none. Figures illustrate definitions and counterexamples only (Figs. 1–10, pp. 6, 14, 17–18).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Lineage stated: Starr 1969 (economics, quasi-equilibria) → optimization uses (Bertsekas et al. 1983; Aubin–Ekeland 1976; Udell–Boyd 2016) → recent refinements (Bi–Tang 2020; Kerdreux–Colin–d'Aspremont 2023; Nguyen–Vohra 2024) and a discrete analogue (Murota–Tamura 2025) (p. 4). Claims priority: "the first result establishing an asymptotically vanishing nonconvexity measure" (p. 4). Says Lemma 4 "can be used to strengthen the refined Shapley-Folkman Lemma recently proposed in [13]" (p. 11, not carried out). Theorem 3 is "similar to the gap formula in [24]" (Dubois-Taine–d'Aspremont) (p. 8). Hidden convexity work (S-Lemma, Chandrasekaran et al. 2025) is framed as the same "image convexity" viewpoint (p. 3). Acknowledges Cifuentes, Blekherman, Vempala and Murota for discussions (p. 23).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract poses a yes/no "geometric question" and answers it "affirmatively" (p. 1). Two-audience framing: each result is restated for economics (competitive equilibrium with smooth utilities, pp. 1–3). The informal main application (4) appears in the intro before the formal Theorem 6 (pp. 4–5). The roadmap paragraph names the theorem in each section (p. 5). The key insight is flagged in one sentence: "It is sufficient to instead focus on exploiting only the local geometric properties" (p. 13). Each hypothesis gets a Remark with a figure-backed example (pp. 14, 17). Averaged vs unnormalized sums are contrasted to sell the result as "superlinear" when averaged (p. 5). Typos remain in v2 (e.g. "We do not need to under the global structure", p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limitations**: Purely theoretical. It gives a new proof of a classical lemma, a refined bound (Theorem 4), a vanishing bound under local smoothness (Theorem 5), and asymptotic tightness of the Lagrangian dual for smooth block problems with sparsity (Theorem 6). Stated non-pursued extensions: p-th power gauges and other nonconvexity measures (Remark 1, p. 17); local rather than global smoothness (Remark 5, p. 23). There is no conclusion or open-problem section; the paper ends at Remark 5 (p. 23). Unstated: no explicit rate for the duality gap Δ itself (only for E). Reader derivation, not in the paper: Theorem 5's second bound behaves like nβ²/(4(k − n)r) for large k. Tightness of Theorems 4–6 is not discussed. No computation shows how large k must be for real block problems (e.g. unit commitment, the [10] application).
