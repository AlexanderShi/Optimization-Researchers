### S078 · Strengthened bounds for the probability of k-out-of-n events (Discrete Applied Mathematics 2016, doi:10.1016/j.dam.2015.05.023)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-13). Read: abstract, introduction, §2 PAM derivation and SPAM, Lemma 1, Proposition 2 with its proof, §3 numerical examples, Appendices 1-2. The paper has no conclusion section. The file is the 2013 preprint.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Feng Qiu, Shabbir Ahmed

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: If only moments up to level m < n are known, P(at least k of n events) can be bounded but not computed (abstract; pp. 1-2). The entry is a known LP: the partially aggregated model (PAM) of Prékopa and Gao, which uses partially aggregated moment information (p. 2). The authors first note that PAM extends to any k (p. 2, p. 3). The stated aim is "to identify inequalities that can be appended to PAM" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when an existing LP bound comes from aggregating an exponential exact model, look at what the aggregation threw away; that is where the strengthening inequalities live.
- **D2 Key idea**: Rebuild PAM from the exponential Boolean LP (2) by duplicating and summing rows, then aggregating the variables w_C into v_ij (pp. 3-4). PAM is then exactly the relaxation that drops w_C and constraint (5d) (p. 4). Instead of dropping w_C, project it out, which is possible because w_C does not appear in the objective (pp. 4-5). The projection of each layer W_i gives the inequalities (15)-(16), and adding them to PAM gives SPAM (Proposition 2, p. 5; p. 7).
  - Transferable: derive a known relaxation as a projection-with-dropping of an exact extended model; then do the projection exactly for one block and add the resulting inequalities.
- **D3 Assumptions**: Bernoulli events with known joint probabilities p_S for |S| ≤ m (p. 2). The projection result is stated per layer i = 2, …, n with separate forms for i = n−1 and i = n (Proposition 2, p. 5).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: a dimension lemma for the slice {x ∈ {0,1}^n : Σx = i}, proved with n explicit linearly independent vectors (Lemma 1, p. 5). The projection is computed with Farkas' lemma by listing the extreme rays of {u : uᵀG ≥ 0}, where G is the matrix of all 0-1 columns with i ones. Each candidate ray is shown extreme by counting its tight columns, and any other ray is excluded by a coordinate-swap contradiction (pp. 6-7).
- **D5 Experiments**: Prékopa-Gao's three 20-event examples (Tables 1-3, pp. 8-9). The k = 1 lower bounds improve on FAM, PAM and three-moment PAM (Table 4, p. 9), and the k = 3 bounds are also reported (Table 5, p. 9). "we do not observe improvement in the new upper bounds" (p. 10). For n = 10, m = 3 and k = 2-4, distributions maximising the SPAM-PAM gap were found with BARON (10 h limit). SPAM then equals the exact Boolean-model bound, while PAM is far below it (Table 6, p. 10; model in Appendix 2, pp. 12-13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an LP strengthening with a short polyhedral proof (theory plus a small computation). Limits stated in the text: no gain in upper bounds (p. 10). Inconsistencies in the file: Table 4 labels the rows as Examples 4-6 although the text says Examples 1-3 (pp. 8-9). In Table 5, for Example 1 SPAM (0.6745504) is below PAM(3) (0.6768353), while the text claims improvement over "both FAM and PAM" (pp. 9-10); that claim holds against two-moment PAM. No future-work section.

**Method links**: Method 5 evidence ✅: the cheap LP bound is strengthened by inequalities read off the structure (projection of the aggregated layers), pp. 4-7. Method 2 evidence ✅: the exponential Boolean LP is computed as the ceiling, and SPAM is judged against it on worst-case distributions (Table 6, p. 10). Method 4 evidence ✅ (weak): the negative finding on upper bounds is reported (p. 10). New-pattern candidate: "optimise the instance to maximise the gap between two relaxations" (Appendix 2, pp. 12-13: the inner LPs are dualised into a single max problem over distributions and solved with BARON).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To show where a new inequality family matters, write "max over data of (new bound − old bound)", dualise the inner LP so that it becomes a single nonconvex max problem, and hand it to a global solver (pp. 10, 12-13).
- To project out an exponential block, compute the extreme rays of {u : uᵀG ≥ 0} with Farkas' lemma, using tight-column counts to certify extremality (pp. 6-7).
**Quotes**:
- "Our key contribution is to identify inequalities that can be appended to PAM to strengthen the bounds." (p. 2)
- "However, we do not observe improvement in the new upper bounds." (p. 10)

### S089 · On a cardinality-constrained transportation problem with market choice (Operations Research Letters 2016, doi:10.1016/j.orl.2015.12.001; read arXiv:1412.8723v1)
**Read level**: full for the statements and the proof architecture (pp. 1-7, 11-13). Read: abstract, §1, Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Examples 1-2, the edge property, Proposition 2 with its proof, Proposition 3, Claim 1, the conflict-graph construction, Algorithm 1 and Claims 9-11. The case analysis on pp. 7-11 was skimmed only. Version note: arXiv v1 has three authors and does not include M. Walter.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Pelin Damcı-Kurt, Simge Küçükyavuz (arXiv v1); M. Walter on the published version (metadata only)

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a classical integrality result: the matching polytope intersected with a cardinality constraint is integral (abstract, p. 1). The question is whether the same holds for TPMC (the transportation problem with market choice) when all demands d_j ∈ {1, 2} ("simple TPMC"). That case is polynomial by a reduction to perfect matching, while TPMC in general is strongly NP-complete (p. 2). The constraint is motivated as a service level: at most k markets rejected (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: Theorem 1: for d_j ≤ 2, conv of the feasible set with Σz ≤ k equals conv(X) ∩ {Σz ≤ k} (p. 3). With the ellipsoid method this gives polynomial solvability (Corollary 1, p. 3). Matching embeds into simple TPMC (Observation 1, p. 2), so the theorem generalises the matching result (p. 4). The authors choose not to argue through the perfect-matching polytope, because a cardinality constraint on a subset of edges breaks integrality there (Example 1, p. 3; p. 4). They prove an "edge property" instead (Definition 1, p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: integral data, d_j ≤ 2 (p. 3). Supplies are reduced to s_i = 1 by node splitting, which is polynomial because s_i ≤ 2|V2| (p. 5).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: the edge-property lemma (Proposition 2, pp. 4-5), credited to an earlier technique ("similar to that used in [2]", p. 4): if every pair of optimal solutions whose cardinalities differ by at least 2 has an optimal solution of intermediate cardinality, then the cardinality-constrained hull is the hull cut by the constraint. Claim 1 reduces this to building two solutions whose objective values are ρ − δ and ρ + δ (p. 5). Those solutions come from a bipartite conflict graph of "full" and "partial" nodes (p. 6) and a path-marking algorithm with an induction-on-iterations correctness proof (Algorithm 1, Claims 9-11, pp. 11-13).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a polyhedral theorem with a complexity corollary. The boundary is stated by counterexample: with one demand equal to 3, a fractional vertex appears (Example 2, p. 4). The complexity of min-weight perfect matching with a cardinality constraint on a subset of edges is said to be open; solving it would solve exact perfect matching (p. 3). No conclusion section.

**Method links**: Method 4 evidence ✅: drop the hypothesis d_j ≤ 2 and exhibit the fractional vertex (Example 2, p. 4); Example 1 shows why the obvious route through the matching polytope fails (p. 3). Method 3 n/a (pure MILP polyhedral). New-pattern candidate: "pick the proof route by first testing it on a counterexample" (p. 4: a generic matching-polytope approach "appears to be less fruitful" because of Example 1).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To show that adding one cardinality constraint keeps a hull integral, prove the edge property (optimal solutions of intermediate cardinality exist), not the integrality directly (pp. 4-5).
- Before choosing a proof route through a known polytope, test whether the target statement already fails for that polytope (Example 1, p. 3).
**Quotes**:
- "Therefore a generic approach considering the perfect matching polytope appears to be less fruitful." (p. 4)
- "The next example illustrates that the statement of Theorem 1 does not hold in such case." (p. 4)

### S093 · Groves mechanisms and communication externalities (Review of Economic Design 2016, doi:10.1007/s10058-015-0180-y)
**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-11, 14-15, 19-22). Read: abstract, introduction, §2 model, §3 efficient assignments with the algorithm (Table 1) and the worked example, the §4 impossibility (Proposition 2), Proposition 3 with its examples, Propositions 4-5 statements, §7 concluding remarks. Not read: the §5-6 derivations and Appendices 1-2 (pp. 22-37). Economics paper outside the MINLP field; the note in works.json says it is his (homepage PDF).
**Role**: supplement (outside the field) · **Coauthors**: Efthymios Athanasiou, Giacomo Valletta

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Agents on two "platforms" benefit from communicating, and adopting the foreign platform has a cost (abstract; p. 2). The paper asks two questions: what the efficient adoption pattern is, and how a planner can implement it (p. 2). The efficiency question is posed as a discrete optimisation problem P(e) over {0,1}^N (p. 6).
- **D2 Key idea**: On the optimisation side, Lemmas 1-3 give a monotonicity property (higher-θ agents adopt first) and necessary optimality conditions. These prune the 2^|N| candidates to about |Nα| candidates, so the algorithm needs roughly |Nα||Nβ| arithmetic operations (pp. 6-8, Table 1). With more than two platforms the problem is said to be NP-complete, "a proof is available on request" (p. 7). On the mechanism side, no Groves mechanism is both individually rational and feasible (Proposition 2, p. 10). The minimal-deficit mechanism is characterised (§5), and for two agents a unique second-best-efficient feasible Groves mechanism is identified (Proposition 5, p. 20).
- **D3 Assumptions**: two platforms, linear communicative benefit θ_i per reachable agent, a platform-specific adoption cost, quasi-linear utilities (pp. 4-5). The authors say they made the assumptions "to intensify the effect of the externality" (p. 6).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: exchange argument for monotonicity (Lemma 1, p. 6); impossibility by a two-agent counterexample economy (Proposition 2, pp. 10-11); a perturbation argument (raise one agent's transfer at a single valuation) for the necessity of Condition A (pp. 18-19).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. A worked numerical economy traces the algorithm: 3 of 2^5 candidates are generated, and the optimum involves two-sided adoption (pp. 8-9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory (an algorithm plus mechanism-design characterisations). Stated limits: the two-agent restriction for Proposition 5, because "a more general result is out of reach" (p. 20). It is open whether an individually rational Groves mechanism can ever run a surplus (footnote 2, p. 11). Mechanisms outside the Groves class are left for future research (pp. 21-22). Dey's individual contribution is not stated in the paper. That the §3 algorithm is the operations-research part is my inference.

**Method links**: Method 4 evidence ✅ (outside field): the impossibility is paired with "any three of them" being achievable (p. 3), and with a sufficient condition (Proposition 3) for when the deficit occurs (p. 14). New-pattern candidate: none. The paper's methods are economic, and I do not count it as evidence for any MINLP method beyond that.
**Transferable techniques**:
- To make a 2^N assignment problem polynomial, first prove a monotonicity lemma (if an agent with a higher value does not act, nobody with a lower value acts); it turns the search into a short chain of thresholds (Lemma 1 and Table 1, pp. 6-8).
**Quotes**:
- "We first provide an algorithm that determines the efficient outcome." (p. 1)
- "The combinatorial nature of the problem makes this very difficult to ascertain." (p. 20)

### S116 · Closedness of integer hulls of simple conic sets (SIAM Journal on Discrete Mathematics 2016, doi:10.1137/140982210)
**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-6, 20, 23). Read: abstract, introduction, §2 all main results (Theorems 1-4, Corollary 1, Example 1), the §3.1 proof outline, the opening of §5 and the perfect-square step. The detailed proofs in §§3.2-3.8 and 4-6 (pp. 6-24) were skimmed only for structure. The file is the 2013 preprint.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Diego A. Morán R.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For MILP, Meyer's theorem says that the integer hull of a rational polyhedron is a rational polyhedron (p. 1). For conic sets that is "unlikely", so the authors "explore a more basic question: Is the integer hull of P_C closed?" (p. 1). The object is a simple conic mixed-integer set {(x,y) ∈ Z^{n1}×R^{n2} : Ax + By − b ∈ C}, where C is the conic hull of a strictly convex set (abstract; p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea**: Theorem 1 characterises closedness through four cases that depend on b ∈ L (a mixed-integer lattice), on dim(C ∩ V), on dim⟨B⟩ and, in one case, on whether the extreme rays of C ∩ V scale into the lattice {Ax} (p. 2). The proof maps P_C by T(x,y) = Ax + By − b. This trades a complicated set and a simple lattice for a simple set C ∩ V and a complicated translated lattice L − b (p. 4), after showing that this map preserves closedness (Proposition 2, p. 5). When C is "poly-checkable", each case can be checked in polynomial time (Theorem 2, p. 3). The Lorentz cone is poly-checkable: case 3 reduces to whether a number is a perfect square, via the Hermite normal form (Theorem 3, p. 3; p. 23).
- **D3 Assumptions**: rational A, B, b; C full-dimensional, pointed, closed, generated by a strictly convex set (p. 2). Theorem 4 (closedness preserved under intersection) holds only in the pure integer case and needs the lineality space to be generated by integer points (p. 3).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: an affine map that moves the difficulty from the set to the lattice (p. 4); generalisation of the authors' earlier pure-integer closedness results to mixed-integer lattices (p. 2); Dirichlet approximation for the counterexample (p. 4); Hermite normal form plus a perfect-square test for the Lorentz cone (p. 3, p. 23).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: structural theory plus a polynomial-time decision procedure. Boundary: Theorem 4 fails in the mixed-integer case (Example 1, with an irrational-slope ray, p. 4). Whether a rational-data version of Corollary 1 holds in the mixed-integer case is left open: "We have not been able to resolve this question." (p. 4). An extended abstract appeared earlier (p. 4).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅: start from what MILP knows (Meyer's theorem) and ask the weakest meaningful analogue for the conic class (p. 1). Method 4 evidence ✅: the positive intersection theorem comes with a counterexample showing where it stops (mixed-integer) and an explicitly open rational-data case (pp. 3-4).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When the MILP analogue of a structure theorem is out of reach for a nonlinear class, step down to a weaker property (closedness instead of polyhedrality) and characterise it completely (p. 1).
- Push the complexity of a problem from the set into the lattice by an affine change of variables, after first proving that the map preserves the property you study (pp. 4-5).
- Turn an existence condition into an algorithm by isolating one number-theoretic test (a perfect square through the Hermite normal form) for the specific cone (p. 3).
**Quotes**:
- "We find it interesting that it is possible to construct an algorithm (let alone one that runs in polynomial-time) to test the closedness of integer hulls of unbounded nonlinear sets." (p. 1)
- "We have not been able to resolve this question." (p. 4)

### S073 · Some cut-generating functions for second-order conic sets (Discrete Optimization 2017, doi:10.1016/j.disopt.2016.11.001; read arXiv:1606.00385v3)
**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-8, 14-15). Read: abstract, §1, all of §2 (Theorems 1-3, Proposition 1, Remark 1), the §3 lemmas and the Proposition 3 counterexample, the start of §4, and the end of the Theorem 3 proof. The proofs in §§4-5 (pp. 9-14) were skimmed only.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Asteroide Santana

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Subadditive duality for linear IP has a conic generalisation: under a "discrete Slater" condition, cuts from the class F_K of subadditive, K-nondecreasing functions give the closed integer hull (p. 2). For K = R^m_+ much more is known (the CG characterisation, 2n-row sufficiency, finite lists independent of b). The aim is to "similarly better understand structural properties of subsets of functions from F_K" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: Theorem 1: if the set of points cut off is bounded, the cut comes from one rational polyhedral outer approximation with p ≤ 2^n aggregated rows y^i ∈ K*. Linear IP cut-generating functions composed with these aggregations therefore suffice (pp. 2-3). The unbounded hyperbola branch x1·x2 ≥ 1 shows the limit: every polyhedral outer approximation contains integer points (0,k) not in the set, so x1 ≥ 1 is obtainable only as the limit of infinitely many cuts (p. 3; Proposition 3, p. 8). A new closed-form family f_γ (Theorem 2, p. 4) produces x1 ≥ 1 in one step. Neither conic CG cuts nor conic MIR obtain it (p. 4). Theorem 3: in R² the two families together give every face of the integer hull of an intersection of conic sections (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions**: K regular; T has nonempty interior; for Theorem 1, the set B cut off is bounded (p. 2). For Theorem 3: dimension 2, non-degenerate hyperbolas written in the Lemma 1 form, non-redundant constraints, rational asymptotes for Proposition 1 (pp. 5-6).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: conic strong duality to obtain y ∈ K* aggregations (Lemma 4, p. 6); a perturbation of the separating hyperplane by interior points of the dual recession cone to make it rational (Lemma 5, pp. 6-7); B bounded iff π lies in the interior of the dual recession cone (Proposition 2, p. 7); explicit counterexamples (Proposition 3, p. 8; Remark 1, p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory. The new family is said to be the first closed-form subadditive family in F_{L^m} (p. 4). Its scope is the second-order cone and, for the completeness result, R² (p. 6). No conclusion or future-work section.

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅: the MILP list (subadditive duality, CG characterisation, finite function lists) is taken as the template, and the same is asked of conic IP (p. 2). Method 4 evidence ✅: Theorem 1 is paired with Proposition 3, which shows that it fails without boundedness (pp. 3, 8), and Remark 1 shows that f_γ is not in the orthant class (p. 5). Method 3 variant ⚠: here the substructure is a single conic section in R², not a multi-constraint set.
**Transferable techniques**:
- Use the smallest unbounded example (one hyperbola branch) to show that a "finite polyhedral outer approximation" argument cannot work, then design the new object to close exactly that example (pp. 3-4).
- To show that a separating hyperplane can be taken rational, perturb it by a positive combination of affinely independent vectors from the interior of the dual recession cone (Lemma 5, pp. 6-7).
**Quotes**:
- "However, it would be much nicer if we could directly obtain x1 ≥ 1 without resorting to obtaining it as an implication of an infinite sequence of cuts." (p. 3)
- "Geometrically, Theorem 1 can be interpreted as the fact that if the set of points separated is bounded, then the cut can be obtained using a rational polyhedral outer approximation." (p. 3)

### S088 · Sparse principal component analysis and its ℓ1-relaxation (arXiv preprint 2017, arXiv:1712.00800)
**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-6, 11-14). Read: abstract, §1, §2 main results (Algorithm 1, Theorems 1-2, the proof idea), §3.1 preliminaries, §3.6 conclusion of the proof, §4 the Theorem 2 construction. The concentration lemmas in §§3.2-3.5 (pp. 6-11) were skimmed for structure only.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Rahul Mazumder, Marco Molinaro, Guanyi Wang

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A popular practice: replace the ℓ0 constraint in sparse PCA by an ℓ1 constraint (pp. 2-3). Under the ℓ2 ball, ‖x‖0 ≤ k implies ‖x‖1 ≤ √k, so the ℓ1 problem with δ = √k is a relaxation (p. 3). The literature treats the two separately and gives "no theoretical results comparing" them (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: For every semi-norm objective and k ≥ 15, OPT_ℓ0 ≤ OPT_ℓ1 ≤ 2.95·OPT_ℓ0, independent of the data (Theorem 1, p. 4). The proof analyses the unbiased randomized rounding of an earlier paper [11] (Algorithm 1, p. 4). The tail of φ(X) is controlled by bounding φ through ‖X‖0 and ‖X‖2 only (Lemma 5) and integrating over concentration bounds for both (p. 5). A lower bound of 1.32 comes from a rank-one matrix (Theorem 2, p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: none on the data A (PSD covariance, or any semi-norm), set against the RIP and irrepresentable conditions needed in sparse regression (p. 3); k ≥ 15 (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: Jensen's inequality on the unbiased rounding for the expected value (p. 5); conditional layer-cake decomposition (Lemma 2, p. 6); concentration for the ℓ0 and ℓ2 norms of the rounded vector (§§3.3-3.4, pp. 7-9); worst-case instance search: for a rank-one A, the ratio becomes an optimisation over x. This is reduced to a four-variable problem (16), solved by discretisation, and certified by an explicit feasible point (k = 10000, θ = 0.755), pp. 12-14.
- **D5 Experiments**: only "limited computational experiments" mentioned in one sentence; they showed ratios well below 1.32 (p. 5). Figure 2 plots the discretised worst-case ratio against k (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: the first formal ℓ0-ℓ1 relation for SPCA (abstract). The gap between 1.32 and 2.95 is stated as open, with the speculation that 1.32 is closer (p. 5); the constants improve for larger k (p. 4). Inconsistency in the file: Theorem 1 states γ = 0.4051, g = 2.996 (p. 4), while §3.6 uses γ = 0.44, g = 2.69 for the feasibility probability (p. 11). The text I read does not reconcile the two.

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: a widespread practice (the ℓ1 surrogate) is given its first theorem, with a data-independent measure (OPT ratio), pp. 1-3. Method 4 evidence ✅: upper bound 2.95 plus a constructed lower bound 1.32, with the gap explicitly open (pp. 4-5). Method 5 evidence ✅ (weak): the ℓ1 problem serves as a bound on the ℓ0 optimum, and the rounding turns it into a feasible solution with a constant guarantee (p. 3). Taste "warning sign 1" evidence ✅: the result is contrasted with sparse-regression results that need RIP-type data assumptions (p. 3). New-pattern candidate: "optimise the instance to maximise the gap between two relaxations" (pp. 12-14; also S078).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When concentration of a non-Lipschitz objective is unavailable, bound the objective by quantities that do concentrate (support size and norm), then integrate over their tails (p. 5).
- For a lower bound on a worst-case ratio, restrict to a structured family (rank-one, sorted nonnegative x*), reduce to a few aggregate variables, solve numerically, and certify with one explicit feasible point (pp. 12-14).
**Quotes**:
- "In this paper, we prove that, independent of the data, the optimal objective function value of the problem with ℓ0 constraint is within a constant factor of the the optimal objective function value of the problem with ℓ1 constraint." (p. 1)
- "In our limited computational experiments, we saw ratios significantly lesser than 1.32, so we speculate that the lower bound of 1.32 is perhaps closer to the actual constant." (p. 5)

### S039 · Aggregation-based cutting-planes for packing and covering integer programs (Mathematical Programming 2018, doi:10.1007/s10107-017-1192-x; read arXiv:1606.08951v1)
**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-9, 11-12). Read: abstract, §1, §2 definitions, all results (Theorems 1-5 with the Figure 1-2 proof maps), §2.2.3 experiments, §3 open questions, the §4 opening, and the start of the §4.1 and §4.3 proofs. The covering proofs in §5 (pp. 13-23) were skimmed only.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Merve Bodur, Alberto Del Pia, Marco Molinaro, Sebastian Pokutta

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Many of the most important solver cut families (CG, lifted knapsack covers, weight inequalities) are aggregation cuts, which have been studied empirically but, the authors say, not theoretically (pp. 1-2). The question is when aggregation helps and what its limits are (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: For packing polyhedra and for covering polyhedra with bounds, the 1-row closures (cuts from each original constraint alone) 2-approximate the CG and aggregation closures, and the factor 2 is tight (Theorems 1 and 4, pp. 4-6). For general IPs the 1-row closure can be arbitrarily bad (Theorem 2, p. 5). A rank lower bound of order log(integrality gap) holds for the k-aggregation closure (Theorems 3 and 5, pp. 5-6). The proofs route through a "pre-processed LP": for packing, set x_j = 0 when A_ij > b_i; for covering with bounds, add all knapsack-cover inequalities. The relations are drawn as an arrow diagram with tight-example arrows (Figures 1-2, pp. 5-6).
- **D3 Assumptions**: nonnegative rational data; for covering, variable bounds; A_ij ≤ b_i for the rank bounds (pp. 2, 5-6). The results also hold for packing and covering sets defined by infinitely many inequalities (pp. 5-6).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: α-approximation is characterised as the containment U ⊆ αV, so it survives the infinite intersections that define a closure (Proposition 1, p. 8). An intermediate relaxation is sandwiched between the two closures (Figures 1-2, pp. 5-6). For covering CG closures the KC-LP route provably fails (a snake arrow: L·z^C ≤ z^KC for any L), so a different proof is needed (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: exact CG closures computed with CPLEX 12.6 on random equality IPs (n = 10-16, a planted binary solution; instances with z^LP/z^I ≤ 2 discarded; mean z^1C/z^C between 11.46 and 15.16) and on 100 market-split instances, where the 1-row CG closure misses infeasibility in 50 of 90 infeasible cases (pp. 7-8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory with an empirical counterpart. Open questions: is the aggregation closure a polyhedron (shown only for dense A, Appendix A), and can multipliers be restricted by the sign pattern of A (p. 8). The cut-selection reading is given in the introduction (p. 2).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: a ubiquitous solver practice (aggregation-based cuts) is turned into a closure-comparison theorem with a data-independent factor, and is read back as cut-selection advice (p. 2). Method 4 evidence ✅: the factor 2 is tight, it is paired with a negative class (general IPs, Theorem 2), and the KC-LP route is shown to fail for covering CG (pp. 4-6). Method 2 evidence ✅: the experiments compute the exact CG closure (the ceiling) on small instances, with exclusions written into the generator (p. 7). Heuristic 4 evidence ✅: the multi-row rank result continues the 2009 multi-row programme (p. 2, Theorems 3, 5).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To compare closures defined by infinitely many cuts, use the containment form of approximation (U ⊆ αV); it is preserved under arbitrary intersections (Proposition 1, p. 8).
- Draw the proof as a diagram of ≤ relations between named relaxation values, marking tight examples and unbounded ratios; it shows the reader which link carries the theorem (Figures 1-2, pp. 5-6).
- Pair a positive theorem for structured IPs with a randomised experiment on unstructured ones that shows the factor blowing up (pp. 7-8).
**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, for these problems, in order to obtain cuts that are much stronger than original constraint cuts, one needs to consider more complicated cuts that cannot be generated through aggregations" (p. 2)
- "From cut selection perspective, the insight here is that for packing and covering problems, using aggregation cuts may provide limited benefit over using cuts generated from only the original constraints, while aggregation cuts may produce significant value for general IPs." (p. 2)

### S069 · Strong mixed-integer formulations for the floor layout problem (INFOR 2018, doi:10.1080/03155986.2017.1346916; read arXiv:1602.07760v2)
**Read level**: partial (pp. 1-7, 10-12, 14-16, 17-23). Read: abstract, §1 contributions, §2 literature review, §3.1, the §4 opening, the §5.1 Theorem 5.1 statement, the §5.3 opening, the §6 and §7 openings, all of §8 computational results with Tables 1-2, §9 conclusion. Not read: the formulation details of §§4.1-4.2 and 5.2, the inequality families of §§6.1-6.2 and 7.2, and Appendices A-L (pp. 24-39).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Joey Huchette, Juan Pablo Vielma

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The floor layout problem (non-overlapping rectangles with variable shapes, minimising weighted Manhattan distances) has many seemingly ad hoc MIP formulations and remains very hard (abstract; p. 1). The entry is methodological: use it as a "case study on systematic construction" of MIP formulations (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: The approach applies Vielma's embedding approach to the pairwise non-overlap disjunction, varying three inputs: the 0-1 encoding (unary, binary, Gray code), alternative disjunctions, and which common constraints go into the ground set. This recovers every existing formulation and yields new ones (pp. 2, 6-7). It gives a small ideal "unary" formulation for one substructure (Theorem 5.1, pp. 10-11). It also gives a "refined" disjunction that removes layouts satisfying several branches, which branch-and-bound would otherwise enumerate (§5.3, p. 12). Inequalities are translated between encodings (Proposition 6.1, pp. 14-15), and pairwise formulations are glued into N-box ones (Proposition 7.1, p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions**: fixed rectangular floor; box areas fixed, aspect ratio bounded; the area constraints are second-order-cone constraints, which CPLEX linearises (pp. 1, 3, 19).
- **D4 Proof devices (brief)**: ideality of a small substructure formulation (Theorem 5.1; proof in Appendix B, not read); inequality translation through encoding maps (Proposition 6.1, pp. 14-15).
- **D5 Experiments**: 11 literature instances, none solved to optimality before, expanded to 99 by aspect ratio α ∈ {4, 5, 6} and three data-noise levels. Four formulations × four inequality levels; CPLEX 12.6 via JuMP, 4 h limit. Gurobi 6.0 was tried and dropped as not competitive. Code and instances are released on GitHub. Reported: root relaxation gaps, node-count trajectories, and solve time or final gap (pp. 17-23). New techniques were best on 55 of 99 instances (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: formulations, inequalities and a public instance library. The authors state that no single formulation dominates (p. 20) and recommend trying both SP and RU with subsets of the new inequalities (p. 23). Memory errors on the Armour instances (p. 20, Table 2 p. 22). RU wins on node count but not clearly on time (§8.3, p. 20).

**Method links**: Method 3 variant ⚠: convexify a small recurring substructure exactly (an ideal formulation for Q^lb, p. 10), then build the full problem from pairwise pieces (p. 16). This is the MILP version of the substructure move, not an import into a nonlinear class. Heuristic 1 evidence ✅: a benchmark library assembled from the literature and released with the code (pp. 2, 19). Heuristic 2 evidence ✅ (weak): the second solver was tried and its omission explained (p. 19). Method 4 evidence ✅ (weak): the authors report that no formulation dominates (p. 20).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Treat the formulations in a literature as outputs of one generator (encoding × disjunction × ground set); recovering every existing one shows the generator is complete, and the unexplored settings give the new ones (pp. 2, 6-7).
- If a disjunction lets one solution satisfy several branches, refine it into a logically equivalent one with mutually exclusive branches, and measure the effect by node count before solve time (§5.3, p. 12; §8.2, p. 19).
- Build a test family by perturbing base instances with controlled noise levels and shape parameters (p. 18).
**Quotes**:
- "While the construction approach focuses on the FLP, it also exemplifies generic formulation techniques that should prove useful for broader classes of problems." (p. 1)
- "We observe that, although there is no clearly superior formulation or approach across all benchmark instances, it is often the case that one approach will be the clear choice for a particular instance or family of instances." (p. 20)
