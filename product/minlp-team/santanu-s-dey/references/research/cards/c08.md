### S024 · Analysis of sparse cutting-planes for sparse MILPs with applications to stochastic MILPs (Mathematics of Operations Research 2018, doi:10.1287/moor.2017.0866; arXiv:1601.00198)
**Read level**: full. Main text pp. 1–18 read line by line; proofs §5 (pp. 18–37): §5.1.1–5.1.4 (pp. 18–24), §5.2.1–5.2.2 (pp. 29–31) and the opening of §5.2.3 (pp. 31–33) read; the rest of §5.1.5–5.1.6, §5.2.3 and §5.3 (pp. 25–28, 33–37) skimmed for construction and lemma statements; Appendix A (cut-generation algorithm, pp. 39–41) read. The arXiv text carries a v1 stamp of 2 Jan 2016 and a title-page date of October 8, 2018 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Marco Molinaro (PUC-Rio), Qianyi Wang (Georgia Tech) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: second step of the sparse-cut line; it follows the geometric vertex-count analysis "[10]" (pp. 1–2) and is cited by S061 as [DMW16] (S061 p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a solver habit without theory: "lesser understanding has been obtained on the question of cutting-plane selection from a theoretical perspective", while "Empirically, sparsity of cutting-planes is considered an important determinant" (p. 1). Three supports are given (p. 2): (i) why solvers prefer sparse cuts (LP linear algebra; a cited study where making 9 constraints dense gave about 25% more time, p. 2); (ii) why real MILPs are sparse (MIPLIB 2010 mean nonzero density 1.63%, median 0.17%, p. 2, with the hedge "this is definitely debatable"); (iii) why the strength of sparse cuts should depend on the matrix sparsity (a fully decomposable example where sparse cuts give the hull, p. 2). The paper presents itself as "a mathematical explanation for the empirical observations" of Crowder–Johnson–Padberg [8] (p. 3). Compared with the earlier paper, the new parameters "can be determined more easily than the number of vertices of the integer hull" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open with a triangle of evidence (why the practice exists, why the structural assumption is realistic with a statistic from the benchmark library, a toy example showing the mechanism), then present the theorem as an explanation of a named classical computational paper.
- **D2 Core idea**: Assume the support of the cuts is fixed and that *all* valid inequalities on those supports are added (the "sparse closure", Def. 2, p. 4), and bound z^cut/z^I by a quantity that depends only on where the zeros of A are and on the support list: "these bounds are completely data independent" (abstract; p. 3). Packing case: build the packing interaction graph on column blocks (Def. 4, p. 5) and bound the ratio by a new fractional mixed chromatic number subordinate to the support list (Defs. 8–9, p. 8; Thm. 11, p. 9). Covering case: a "dual" row-block covering interaction graph (Def. 19, p. 12) and the mixed chromatic number (Thm. 22, p. 13). General A with c ≥ 0: a "corrected average density" of the support list (Def. 25, Thm. 26, p. 14). What stays unchanged: no particular cut family is studied ("we do not explore particular families of sparse cutting-planes", p. 3).
  - Transferable: replace "which cuts does a solver pick" by "what is the best bound obtainable from all cuts on a given support", then measure the gap with a graph invariant of the constraint matrix.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Packing (A, b, c ≥ 0, max), covering (A, b, c ≥ 0, min) and arbitrary A with c ≥ 0, x ≥ 0, maximisation (pp. 4, 11, 14). Mixed-integer variables allowed (p. 4). Sparse cuts are taken as the strongest possible on each support, which is an idealisation of any actual cut family (p. 3). Blocks may be singletons, so the partition is a free modelling choice (p. 5). Two-stage stochastic MILPs with finitely many scenarios are the running example: their packing graph is a star (Example 5, p. 6) and specific-scenario cuts give exactly the natural sparse closure (Example 7, p. 7); for covering, the row graph is a clique (Example 21, p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Upper bounds by "patching" conditional optima: a mixed stable set gives sets of variables that can be set independently and stay feasible (Example 10, pp. 8–9; Lemma 34, p. 19); Lemma 33 bounds the closure value on each support by the conditional IP optimum (p. 18); a fractional cover by mixed stable sets yields Thm. 11 (pp. 19–20). (b) Brooks' theorem converts the bound to max degree (Cor. 12, p. 9; Thm. 35, p. 20). (c) A BFS edge-labelling of trees with 2∆−1 labels covering each node exactly ∆ times gives η ≤ (2∆−1)/∆ (Lemma 36, pp. 21–22; Cor. 37, p. 23). (d) Tight instances: small explicit IPs with right-hand sides 2 − ε (Thm. 13 proof, pp. 20–21); affine n-designs for prime n, whose near-disjoint parallel classes let the closure "see" each block separately while their intersection kills solutions (Def. 38, Thm. 39, pp. 23–24; again for cycles, p. 27); for covering, a duplicated version of a classical set-cover integrality-gap instance (Def. 47, Thm. 48, Lemma 49, p. 29) and a product-set system G^1..G^n with non-empty intersections (Lemmas 53–54, p. 32) for the "most involved" construction (p. 14). (e) The cycle bound depends on the length modulo 3 (Thm. 17, p. 11).
  - Transferable: to show a closure is weak, build an instance from a combinatorial design whose blocks look feasible one support at a time but conflict jointly; to show it is strong, cover the variable blocks fractionally by sets that can be optimised independently and add the conditional optima.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Pure binary instances; the natural sparse closure is computed by cycling over supports and separating with an IP-oracle row-generation loop (Appendix A, pp. 39–41); CPLEX 12.5 (p. 15). Instances: two-stage stochastic (10 scenarios, 20 variables per block) and random-graph-based (nv = 10, p = 0.2), dense random blocks with entries unif{1, M}, right-hand side built from a random 0/1 point plus noise so the instance is non-trivial (pp. 15–16). Each table reports the average z^cut/z^IP next to the theoretical bound: packing 1.00038 vs 1.9, covering 1.009 vs 10, arbitrary 1 vs 10 (Tables 1–3, pp. 16–17); random graphs 1–1.0059 vs bounds 1.667–9 (Tables 4–6, p. 17). No solver run times, no real benchmark instances, no failure cases reported.
  - Transferable: when a theorem is a worst-case bound on a closure, compute the closure exactly on generated instances and print the observed ratio next to the bound in one table.
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues their own geometric analysis [10] "using completely different techniques" (p. 2); frames itself as explaining Crowder–Johnson–Padberg 1983 [8] with direct quotes from that paper (pp. 2–3); mentions the Gomory-cut/tableau-sparsity empirical study [9] only in passing (p. 3). Claims a new statement on the nonanticipativity Lagrangian dual: "there are no known global bounds" on its quality, and the natural sparse closure gives the same bound under complete recourse (p. 7). Relates Cor. 16 to approximation analyses of two-stage matching and defers the link to "a future paper" (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: All definitions and theorems first in a "Main results" section (§2, pp. 4–15); proofs collected in §5 (p. 4 outline). Each theorem is followed by the tightness theorem and a stochastic-programming corollary (e.g. Thm. 14 → Thm. 15 → Cor. 16, pp. 10–11). Worked toy example placed before the proof to explain the mechanism (Example 10, pp. 8–9). Hedges are explicit ("definitely debatable", p. 2; "in our limited experience", p. 2). Main message restated in the conclusion: "In many cases sparse cuts provide very good bounds for sparse IPs." (p. 18)
  - Transferable: give each positive theorem a companion tightness theorem with a matching number, then a corollary for the application class.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Theory contribution: data-independent, mostly tight bounds for packing, covering and general c ≥ 0 MILPs, with consequences for scenario-specific cuts (pp. 17–18). Stated limits: super sparse cuts can be arbitrarily bad for covering (Thm. 18, p. 12); covering requires supports at least as large as constraint supports (p. 12); the bounds are "worst-case" in nature and experiments beat them (p. 18). Not stated but visible: experiments are small and synthetic (pp. 15–17); no bridge from closure strength to an actual cut-selection rule. Future direction named: relation with two-stage matching approximation (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: unexplained solver habit (sparse-cut preference, pp. 1–2) → proxy (closure of all cuts on fixed supports) → data-independent measure (ratio that depends only on the sparsity pattern, p. 3) → return to data and report that practice beats the bound (pp. 4, 18).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: tightness theorems for nearly every bound (Thms. 13, 15, 17, 24, 28, 30; pp. 10–15) and an explicit negative class (super sparse cuts for covering, Thm. 18, p. 12).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the "ground truth" is the exactly computed sparse closure on small generated instances (Appendix A, pp. 39–41); it is compared with the theoretical bound, not with a solver or an optimal-ceiling heuristic (pp. 16–17).
- new-pattern candidate: structural graph invariant of the constraint matrix as the instance parameter (packing/covering interaction graphs, fractional mixed chromatic number; pp. 5–9, 12–13).
- new-pattern candidate: land each general theorem in a structured application class (two-stage stochastic MILPs: scenario-specific cuts, nonanticipativity dual; pp. 5–7, 11, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Fix the support, add all valid cuts on it, and bound the resulting closure. This separates "how strong can sparse cuts be" from "which sparse cuts does a code generate" (pp. 3–4).
- Prove the upper bound with a fractional covering by independently optimisable variable sets (mixed stable sets), so the bound becomes an LP over a graph (pp. 8–9, 19–20).
- Build lower-bound (tight) instances from affine designs for prime n, or by duplicating a known integrality-gap instance (pp. 23–24, 29).
- Generate random instances with a planted 0/1 point plus noise in the right-hand side so that they are feasible and non-trivial (p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "Indeed, one way to view the results we obtain in this paper is to attempt a mathematical explanation for the empirical observations of quality of sparse cutting-planes obtained in [8]." (p. 3)
- "Our main observation is the sparse cuts usually perform much better than the worst-case bounds we obtain theoretically." (p. 4)

### S061 · Improving the randomization step in feasibility pump (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2018, doi:10.1137/16m1095962; arXiv:1609.08121)
**Read level**: full. All of the arXiv v1 text read (pp. 1–18), including Appendices A–C (pp. 16–18). The file is arXiv:1609.08121v1 (26 Sep 2016; title-page compile date March 17, 2021, p. 1); journal-version changes are not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Andres Iroume (Georgia Tech), Marco Molinaro (PUC-Rio), Domenico Salvagnin (IBM Italy / Padova) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: same 2016 period and same structural lens as S024 (cited as [DMW16], p. 3); a primal-heuristic counterpart to the cut-strength line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Feasibility Pump (FP) works very well (96.3% of MIPLIB 2003 instances, p. 2), and its many variants change rounding and projection but "use essentially the same randomization step" (p. 2). There is also "no known bounds on expected running-time of FP" (p. 2). Entry: study the neglected component, the randomization step used when FP stalls (abstract; p. 2).
  - Transferable: in a successful multi-component heuristic, list which component the follow-up literature never touched and make that the target.
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace the "flip the most fractional variables" perturbation with a WalkSAT-style step, RandWalkSAT_ℓ: build a *minimal projected infeasibility certificate* in the binary space and flip ℓ variables sampled uniformly from its support (Def. 3.1, p. 4; Alg. 2, p. 5). Minimality guarantees the certificate never mixes blocks of a decomposable instance (Lemma 3.5, p. 6). Unchanged: the rounding and ℓ1-projection steps of the naive FP (Alg. 4, p. 7).
  - Transferable: import a local-search move from a neighbouring field (WalkSAT from SAT, p. 4) and adapt its "violated constraint" to the mixed-integer setting through a projected certificate.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Mixed-binary sets (p. 4); decomposable sets with k blocks as the structural model (eq. (1), p. 2), k = 1 allowed so the general case is covered (p. 2). The FP analysis is restricted to separable subset-sum sets (Def. 2.2, p. 3) and assumes ℓ1-proj returns a vertex (p. 3) and consistent rounding of 1/2 (p. 7). The authors say the restriction is forced: "Although the general case is elusive at this point" (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Schöning-style "lucky move" argument: track the Hamming distance to a fixed feasible x*; each step reduces it with probability ≥ 1/certSupp; restart blocks of n iterations give failure probability (1 − p)^⌊T/n⌋ (Thm. 3.4, p. 6). (b) Fourier–Motzkin plus Carathéodory give certificates with ≤ d+1 rows, computable by one LP (Lemma 3.2, p. 5; Appendix A LP and extreme-point argument, p. 16). (c) Per-block independence plus a union bound give a running time additive over blocks, about Σ 2^{n_i} rather than 2^{Σ n_i} (Thm. 3.3 proof, p. 7; discussion p. 2). (d) For FP: idempotence of "ℓ1-proj + round" on subset-sum instances, by case analysis on the single fractional vertex coordinate (Lemma 4.3, pp. 8–9); a coupling with a "compressed" algorithm that costs at most a factor 2 in iterations (Lemma 4.4, pp. 9–10); a two-flip lucky move, which is why ℓ = 2 is needed (Lemma 4.6, pp. 10–11; p. 8). (e) Counterexamples: original FP without restarts cycles on 3x1 + x2 = 3 (Remark 2.3, p. 3) and still stalls when zero-fractionality flips are allowed (Appendix B, pp. 16–17). (f) With consistent rounding, long cycles cannot occur (Thm. 4.1, p. 7; Lemma C.1 and proof, pp. 17–18).
  - Transferable: to analyse a randomized repair heuristic, fix one feasible target, show some move reduces Hamming distance with probability at least 1/(support size), and chain this in blocks of n steps.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The analysed WFP was implemented first and lost: "its performance was not competitive with FPorig" (p. 12), with three stated reasons (ℓ hard to fix, perturbing every time is overkill, minimal certificates need LPs) (p. 12). The implemented WFPbase keeps the original perturbation when enough fractional variables exist and otherwise adds random flips from the union of supports of violated constraints (p. 12). Instrument: the Feasibility Pump 2.0 code with CPLEX 12.6.3, all other features (e.g. constraint propagation) "left unchanged" (p. 12); identical Xeon machines, half-hour limit (p. 12). Testbeds: 50 random two-stage stochastic instances with planted feasible 0/1 point, chosen "to validate the hypothesis suggested by the theoretical results" (pp. 12–13); 82 MIPLIB 2010 models, filtered to those where some method needed more than 20 iterations (p. 13). Ten seeds, reported seed by seed; #found, shifted geometric means of time and iterations (pp. 13–14). Results: two-stage +28% found, −17% time, −33% iterations; MIPLIB +6%, −8.4%, −5.9%, more solutions in 7/10 seeds and a tie in 3 (p. 13; Tables 1–2, pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable: change one component inside the strongest public code, leave the rest untouched, and report seed by seed on (i) a testbed that matches the theorem's structure and (ii) a filtered general benchmark.
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits the FP line in full (FGL05, BFL07, FS09 and the MINLP pumps) and states the gap: they focus on rounding/projection (p. 2). Claims priority: "the first time any theoretical analysis of running-time of Feasibility Pump or its variants has been conducted" (abstract). Builds on WalkSAT and Schöning [Sch99] (p. 4). Uses the S024 decomposability lens as motivation for real instances (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A separate "Our contributions" section (§2, pp. 2–4) states every theorem informally with an interpretation paragraph ("There are a few interesting features of this bound", p. 2) before the proofs. A one-paragraph proof sketch is given before each long proof (pp. 3, 5–6, 8). The dead end is reported with its reasons (p. 12). Confident close: "it is a good idea to add it as a feature in all future feasibility pump codes" (p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic and theoretical: a new randomization step, the first running-time bounds for an FP variant (decomposable mixed-binary for mbWalkSAT; separable subset-sum for WFP), and a code-level improvement (pp. 2–4, 13). Limits: the FP-with-rounding analysis covers only subset-sum; bounds are exponential per block (p. 3); the implemented version is not the analysed one (p. 12). No future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: habit that works without theory (FP randomisation, pp. 1–2) → proxy class where the analysis is possible (decomposable sets, separable subset-sum, p. 3) → result explained in structural terms (sparsity and decomposability, pp. 2–3).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (on its "step 8 rarely closed" limitation): here the rule built from the theory is implemented in a state-of-the-art code and recommended for all FP codes (p. 13), but only after the analysed version failed and was replaced by a more conservative variant (p. 12).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: counterexamples where the original randomisation never converges (Remark 2.3, p. 3; Appendix B, pp. 16–17); the scope of the FP theorem is stated as subset-sum only, "the general case is elusive" (p. 3).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the instrument varies only the studied decision (FP 2.0 with everything else unchanged, 10 seeds, p. 12), but there is no exact ceiling or optimum to compare against.
- Heuristic links (not counted as methods): Heuristic 9 (borrow WalkSAT, p. 4), Heuristic 3 (dead end written in with reasons, p. 12), Heuristic 6 (certificate LPs judged too expensive, p. 12).
- new-pattern candidate: block decomposability / sparsity as the structural parameter carried from cut analysis to heuristic analysis (S024 cited as [DMW16], p. 3; testbed chosen to validate it, p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use a minimal certificate (minimal multiplier support) so that a randomized repair step automatically acts on one block of a decomposable instance, without being told the decomposition (pp. 3, 6).
- To get any running-time theorem for an alternating-projection heuristic, find an instance class where "project + round" is idempotent, then couple with a compressed algorithm (pp. 8–10).
- If the analysed variant loses in the real code, write the reasons, design a conservative hybrid that falls back to the old rule, and report both (p. 12).
- Filter out benchmark instances that are too easy to separate the methods (more than 20 iterations for at least one method) and state the filter (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "To the best of our knowledge this is the first theoretical analysis of the running-time of a variant of Feasibility Pump algorithm, even for a special class of instances." (p. 3)
- "In preliminary tests, we implemented the algorithm WFP as described in the previous section. However, its performance was not competitive with FPorig." (p. 12)

### S056 · Nonunique lifting of integer variables in minimal inequalities (SIAM Journal on Discrete Mathematics 2019, doi:10.1137/17m1117070; arXiv:1605.06686)
**Read level**: full. Main text pp. 1–19 read line by line (§§1–4, acknowledgements); appendices (pp. 21–28) skimmed: A (nonemptiness via Zorn, p. 21) and the statements and closing argument of E (pp. 23–27) read; B, C, D and the case analysis F (pp. 21–23, 28) only skimmed. File is arXiv:1605.06686v4 (11 Mar 2019).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Amitabh Basu (Johns Hopkins), Joseph Paat (ETH Zürich); authors listed alphabetically, with funding notes for Basu and Paat on one NSF grant and for Dey on another (p. 1). Which moves are Dey's cannot be read from the text. · **Place in trajectory**: continuation of Dey–Wolsey "Two row mixed-integer cuts via lifting" (2010): "Our work is motivated by Section 7 of [24]" (p. 3). Pure MILP cutting-plane theory, the source side of Method 3.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The usual recipe (maximal S-free set B → smallest representation ψ → pair (ψ, ψ)) gives only "partially minimal" cut-generating pairs, because the integer-variable coefficients π can be lowered (p. 2). Most of the literature treats B with the *unique* lifting property (R(B) = R^n, p. 2). This paper takes the non-unique case: fix the best possible coefficient V_ψ(p*) at one integer column p* and ask where all minimal liftings with that value agree, the "fixing region" F_{ψ,p*} (eqs. (1.4)–(1.6), p. 3). When F = R^n the set is "one point fixable", and a modified recipe gives a minimal pair (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a uniqueness property fails, fix one free choice optimally and study how much of the remaining freedom that one choice removes.
- **D2 Core idea**: Geometrise sequential lifting: V_ψ(p*) is the smallest λ for which a pyramid Pyr(B, λ, p*) with base B × {0} and apex (p*, 1)/λ is (S × Z)-free (Prop. 4, p. 8). Lifting a second vector q is the same operation applied with the pyramid as the new base (iterated pyramid (3.13), p. 12; Prop. 11, p. 12). Blocking points of the pyramid and the "spindles" of each blocking point give an explicit inner approximation X(B, p*) + W_S of the fixing region (eq. (3.10), Thm. 9, p. 12). A universal upper bound ψ*_{[p*,B]} on all liftings in the class (Thm. 7, p. 10) is attained there.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: General closed S ⊆ R^n \ {0} for §2 (p. 4); from §3 on S is a polyhedrally truncated affine lattice (b + Z^n) ∩ P, so maximal S-free sets are polyhedra (eq. (3.2), p. 8). Application section: n = 2, S = Z^2 + b, Type 3 triangles (pp. 14–15); "mixing set Type 3 triangles" need −1 < b2 < b1 < 0 and b1 − 2b2 > 1 (p. 17). Appendix E needs int(B ∩ conv(S)) ≠ ∅ (Prop. 23, p. 24).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) A new object, partial cut-generating pairs defined only on subsets R, P of R^n (§2, p. 4), with an extension theorem when R′, P′ ⊆ cone(R) (Thm. 3, p. 6). The authors compare it with Hahn–Banach and with lifting from faces (p. 4) and show by Gomory–Johnson discontinuous functions that extension fails in general (p. 6). (b) The infimum-over-conic-combinations construction ν_ψ, ν_π, then Zorn's lemma to reach a minimal pair below it (pp. 6–7). (c) Lifting p* turned into an extra continuous column (p*, 1) of an (n+1)-dimensional model, valid by an integrality-of-s argument (Claim 8, p. 10), followed by the extension theorem (p. 11). (d) A unimodular map U on S × Z × Z shows the lifting value does not depend on the integer height q̄ (Prop. 12, p. 13). (e) Cover of the plane: decompose a fundamental region K into five polytopes and place each inside a translated spindle (Claim 16, p. 16; Fig. 1, p. 17). (f) (S × Z)-freeness shown by covering each slice with three lattice-free split sets, case by case (Prop. 17, pp. 18–19). (g) Translation invariance: a piecewise map glued by a pasting lemma (Lemma 22, p. 24), local finiteness (Prop. 23, p. 24), closedness (Prop. 24, p. 25), then Brouwer's invariance of domain makes the image open, and open + closed + non-empty = R^n (p. 27).
  - Transferable: to prove that a union of polyhedra covers R^n, show it is closed (local finiteness), show it is the image of R^n under an injective continuous piecewise-affine map (open by invariance of domain), and conclude by connectedness.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (no computation in the paper).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends the unique-lifting literature (Basu et al., Averkov–Basu, Basu–Paat, Dey–Wolsey; p. 2) to its complement. Takes the algebraic lifting formula from Dey–Wolsey (eq. (3.1), p. 8) and the geometric pyramid characterisation from [2] (Prop. 4, p. 8), noting that their proof transfers to the new pyramid definition (p. 8). Re-derives one-point fixability of mixing-set Type 3 triangles, which "also follows from [24, Theorem 5]; we use different, more geometric techniques" (p. 4). Notes that partial cut-generating pairs "have been subsequently used in [5]" (p. 4). Motivates the mixing set by production planning, facility location, network design and stochastic IP (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A numbered "Statement of results" list with the place of each result (pp. 3–4); the guiding questions are written as questions ("What is a good description of the fixing region? How does the fixing region depend on p∗?", p. 3). Technical proofs are pushed to Appendices B–F with a one-line reason each time (pp. 12–14). Open points are named plainly (inner approximation equality unknown, p. 3). The acknowledgement says referees "helped us correct a flaw in one of the major results" (p. 19).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Theory only: a framework for non-unique liftings (fixing region, inner approximation, universal upper bound), partial cut-generating pairs, translation invariance, and an application to Type 3 triangles from the mixing set (pp. 3–4). Limits: it is "not known if this inner approximation" equals the fixing region (p. 3); the one-point-fixability condition is sufficient only and is verified only for Type 3 triangles in R^2 (pp. 15–18); no computational evaluation of the resulting cuts. No conclusion or future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: this is the MILP side of the paradigm (S-free sets, cut-generating functions, lifting of integer variables, two-row Type 3 triangles; pp. 1–4, 14–17), worked on inside MILP rather than imported into QCQP. It documents the toolbox that Method 3 step 1 lists ("closures, duals, single- and two-row relaxations, lifting").
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: a general negative case before the positive extension theorem (Gomory–Johnson functions cannot be extended, p. 6); the unresolved gap between the inner approximation and the fixing region is stated (p. 3); the sufficient condition is stated as such (Prop. 15, p. 15).
- new-pattern candidate: reopen a named section of one's own earlier paper as the next problem (Dey–Wolsey 2010 §7 → this paper, p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: re-prove a known result by a more geometric route to get a reusable tool (mixing-set triangles, p. 4; pyramids and spindles, pp. 8–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn "lift one integer variable optimally" into a polyhedral question: the smallest apex height for which a pyramid over the S-free set stays lattice-free (p. 8); iterate the pyramid for sequential lifting (p. 12).
- Define partial versions of your objects (functions valid only on subsets of columns) and prove an extension theorem under a cone condition; this turns lifting arguments into a reusable lemma (pp. 4–7).
- Show that a polyhedron in R^3 is (S × Z)-free by covering each integer-height slice with a few explicit split sets (pp. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "Our work is motivated by Section 7 of [24], which initiated the study of this problem." (p. 3)
- "Their suggestions helped us correct a flaw in one of the major results and make the material more presentable." (p. 19)
