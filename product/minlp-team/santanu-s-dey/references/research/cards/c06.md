### S009 · A cycle-based formulation and valid inequalities for DC power transmission problems with switching (Operations Research 2016, DOI 10.1287/opre.2015.1471, arXiv:1412.6245)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–33 read, including Appendix A (cycle-basis correctness proof, pp. 30–32) and the reference list. The figures (performance profiles, pp. 27, 29) are image-only in the text file, so only their captions and the prose description (pp. 24, 28) were read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: B. Kocuk, H. Jeon, J. Linderoth, J. Luedtke, A. Sun (Kocuk first author, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The DC precursor of the Kocuk–Dey–Sun power-systems line. The AC papers cite it as [29] (S005 p. 23) and reuse its cycle basis (S005 p. 15). It is a Georgia Tech–Wisconsin collaboration: the acknowledgement funds Jeon, Linderoth and Luedtke through DOE (p. 30).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry has two parts: a policy and an application (the USA Energy Policy Act of 2005 quoted in the abstract, p. 1), and a computational complaint from the application literature. DC-OTS MILP models with many "indicator constraints" have very weak LP relaxations, so earlier work turned to line-ranking heuristics or artificial cardinality limits (p. 2). The authors quote Hedman et al. (2010): techniques for improving the lower bound "are largely ineffective" (p. 3), and they say their primary focus is "an attempt to change this reality" with strong valid inequalities (p. 3). They also note that "The mathematical theory of the DC-OTS problem is less well-developed" (abstract, p. 1). They defend the DC model against the criticism of Coffrin et al. (2014) by pointing to planning uses where DC is acceptable (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: open on a quoted admission from the application community that its bounding techniques fail, and make "change this reality" the stated goal. The reader then judges the paper by the lower bound, not by the heuristic solutions.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key insight comes from Kirchhoff's Voltage Law: replace angle variables by the condition that flow/susceptance sums to zero around every cycle of a cycle basis (formulation (3), equivalence Prop. 2.1, pp. 5–6). With switching, KVL is enforced only when every arc of the cycle is on, through a big-M on Σ(1−x) (formulation (10), p. 7). Formulation (10) has exponentially many cycles, so it is not used directly. It serves "as the starting point for deriving strong valid inequalities", or as lazy cuts (pp. 7–8). The relaxation S_C keeps one cycle's KVL constraint, the flow bounds and the binaries (eq. (11), p. 10), and the paper gives the complete convex hull of S_C (Theorem 4.1, p. 11). The algorithm is cut-and-branch: five rounds of separation at the root of the standard angle formulation (9), then CPLEX (Algorithm 1, p. 19; "five times", p. 21). What changed relative to Fisher et al. (2008): only the root relaxation. What did not change: the big-M angle formulation (9) that is finally solved (p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: when a physical law has a cycle form (KVL), write a cycle-space formulation even if it is too large to solve. Use it only as a source of single-cycle substructures to convexify, and keep the compact formulation for the solver.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The network is connected (p. 4). There is at most one generator per bus, without loss of generality (p. 4). Generation cost is linear (p. 4), and the authors flag this as ignoring the convex quadratic part. They argue that the hull is still useful for increasing convex costs because optima lie on the boundary. They also concede that the bound "might be weak" when the optimum is not an extreme point, and leave numerical tests of this case as future work (p. 10). For the hull proof they scale to B_ij = 1 "without loss of generality" and assume C is a directed cycle (pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - Complexity demarcation: there is always an optimal solution whose on-lines form a connected network (Prop. 3.1, p. 8), so trees are polynomial (Cor. 3.1, p. 8). DC-OTS feasibility is NP-complete on series-parallel graphs with one generator and one load (Thm 3.1, pp. 8–9). The proof reduces subset sum to a two-terminal parallel-path gadget, with capacities a_i/b and susceptances 2a_i (p. 9).
  - Convex hull by disjunction: "all lines on" vs "at least one off". Each side is integral in x (pp. 11–12), and the disjunctive extended formulation (14) is shown to be integral (Prop. 4.1, p. 11). The auxiliary flows are projected out via a hypercube ∩ hyperplane feasibility argument (Prop. 4.2, pp. 12–14), then y_C is eliminated by Fourier–Motzkin (pp. 14–15). The result is the explicit family (12), indexed by subsets S with Δ(S) > 0 (p. 10).
  - Facetness: every inequality in (12) is a facet, shown by explicit affinely independent points χ¹–χ⁴. The case |C∖S| = 1 is handled separately by a linear-independence calculation (Thm 4.2, pp. 15–16).
  - Separation: it is a knapsack problem (31) (p. 17). There is a necessary condition K_C > 0 (Prop. 4.3, p. 17), and a closed-form optimal subset S*_C (Prop. 4.4, p. 18), which gives a linear-time test (32) per cycle (pp. 18, 30).
  - Cycle basis from the LU factorisation of the edge–node incidence matrix (Algorithm 5, p. 21; correctness Prop. A.1, pp. 30–32).
  - Transferable technique: to get a full convex hull of a "constraint active only if all binaries are 1" set, write the two-term disjunction (all on / at least one off) and show that each side is integral. Then project the copies out in two stages: continuous copies by an interval-feasibility argument, the disjunction weight by Fourier–Motzkin. The surviving inequalities are the hull.
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Instances: five sets of 35 random instances each, generated from case118B (Blumsack) and a modified case300. The variations are load perturbations, added 6- or 16-cycles with tight capacities, and relocated generators (p. 22). Instances were selected on purpose so that switching matters and the problems are "not trivial ... but small enough to not be intractable" (p. 22). They were released at a public URL (p. 22).
  - Baselines: Default CPLEX on (9), BasicCycles (cuts from one cycle basis) and MoreCycles. MoreCycles uses about 3,500 combined cycles on the 118-bus sets and a random 10% of more than 37,000 on the 300-bus set (p. 23).
  - Metrics: initial LP gap, gap closed by cuts alone, root gap closed with CPLEX cuts, time, nodes, # unsolved, and the gap on unsolved instances, reported as arithmetic and geometric means (pp. 23–24). Performance profiles follow Dolan–Moré (p. 24).
  - Setup: single thread, 0.1% gap, 1 h limit, stated hardware (p. 22). Over all sets, MoreCycles leaves 22 instances unsolved vs 43 for Default, and closes 24.78% vs 6.17% of the root gap (arithmetic means, Table 1f, p. 26).
  - Honest disaggregation: on the easy set 118_15, Default is fastest on 42.9% of instances (p. 24). On 300_5, BasicCycles is dominated (p. 24).
  - Negative result reported: a callback implementation was "around 30% worse" than cut-and-branch (p. 19). Algorithm 3 (all violated inequalities per cycle) beat Algorithm 2 in preliminary tests (p. 23).
  - Reviewer-suggested sensitivity study (p. 30): optimal solutions switch off 42 lines on average (118-bus) and 85 (300-bus), against the literature's "only a few lines". They explain this by many near-optimal topologies. With a cardinality budget, 11 lines (118-bus) or 15 lines (300-bus) capture (nearly) all the benefit. The LP bound does not move with the budget, which explains why budgeted MIPs are harder (pp. 28–29).
  - Transferable technique: when a cut paper finds that the optimal structure contradicts folklore (many lines switched off), add a budget sweep (cost vs number of allowed changes, with the LP bound on the same plot). It both reconciles the folklore and explains solve-time differences.
- **D6 Positioning**: The complexity result was "established independently" of Lehmann et al. (2014) and complements it (NP-hard on cacti there; series-parallel with one source/sink and the tree case here) (p. 8). It extends Bienstock–Mattia (2007), whose result needed a cardinality constraint (p. 8). The inequalities are placed in the integer-programming tradition of cycle-derived cuts: Padberg 1973, Barahona–Mahjoub 1986, Ferreira et al. 1996, Van Vyve 2005 (p. 4). Prior DC-OTS work is described as heuristic or applied (pp. 2–3), and the Coffrin et al. criticism of DC is restated fairly before being bounded (pp. 2–3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contributions are a four-bullet list (p. 3). Assumptions come with a cost statement (linear-cost caveat, p. 10). Dead ends and arbitrary choices are written in plainly: "This choice of algorithm was arbitrary. We have no reason to believe ..." (cycle basis, p. 21); callbacks 30% worse (p. 19). Reviewers are credited for Section 6.3 and for the flow-balance substructure idea (p. 30). Hedging is moderate: "promising computational results" (abstract, p. 1), and "can be quite useful" for the cuts (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution layers are theory (complexity, convex hull, facets), algorithm (linear-time separation, LU cycle basis) and computation (benchmarks released). Stated limits: linear cost only, with the convex-cost case untested (p. 10); the substructure ignores flow balance, so stronger relaxations could come from an extended formulation including it (reviewer's observation, p. 30). Future directions: other planning problems with line addition/removal, more complicated substructures, larger networks (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a small recurring substructure (one cycle's KVL with on/off arcs) is convexified exactly, with an extended formulation, facets and separation, explicitly in the line of integer-programming cycle inequalities (pp. 4, 10–18).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: complexity is demarcated on both sides, polynomial on trees and NP-complete already on series-parallel graphs with a single source–sink pair (pp. 8–9). The facet proof shows that every hull inequality is necessary (pp. 15–16).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the cuts are evaluated deliberately inside the full commercial solver ("cut-and-branch" with all advanced features on) rather than in a stripped instrument. The callback alternative, which switches some features off, is reported as 30% worse (p. 19). Here the question is solver improvement, not explanation, and the instrument changes accordingly.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (partial): the goal is the lower bound ("improving the lower bound", p. 3), and the root-gap-closed metrics are the main yardstick (pp. 23–26). The exact MIP is still solved, though, so no certified-gap-as-deliverable here.
- new-pattern candidate: "budget sweep against folklore". When the optimal solution contradicts a folk belief, sweep the budget that the folklore implicitly assumes and plot both IP and LP values (pp. 28–29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Two-term "all on / at least one off" disjunction for constraints that hold only when a whole cycle (or any set of binaries) is on. Each side is integral, so the disjunctive hull is exact (pp. 11–12).
- Fourier–Motzkin elimination of the single disjunction weight y_C to read off an explicit, facet-defining inequality family (pp. 14–16).
- Reduce a knapsack-looking separation problem to a closed-form threshold set, by showing that every positively contributing element must be in an optimal solution (Prop. 4.4, p. 18).
- Report arithmetic and geometric means side by side, plus per-set performance profiles. Say explicitly where the baseline wins (pp. 24–26).

**Quotes**:
- "We prefer to implement our separation in this “cut-and-branch” manner in order to investigate the utility of the cutting planes when combined with all advanced features of modern MIP software." (p. 19)
- "We would like to thank the reviewers for their constructive comments and in particular suggesting the experiments in Section 6.3." (p. 30)

### D002 · Relaxations and discretizations for the pooling problem (J. Glob. Optim. 67(3) 2017, DOI 10.1007/s10898-016-0434-4)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–28 read, including all tables and the reference list. The figures (value-function plots, p. 16; performance profile, p. 24) are image-only in the text file; only captions and prose were read. Per the work list note, this is the journal version of technical report S012 (same four authors); the file read is an undated "Noname manuscript" preprint (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Gupte (first author; Clemson), S. Ahmed (Georgia Tech), M.-S. Cheon (ExxonMobil Research and Engineering) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Part of the 2013–2017 pooling line (SKILL.md "Pooling: MILP relaxations and restrictions with guarantees"). It cites the companion analytical paper Dey–Gupte, "Analysis of MILP techniques for the pooling problem" (Oper. Res., [21], p. 26), for the |J| approximation theorem and the 70 test instances (pp. 14, 21, 25). Ref. [30] is Gupte's 2012 Georgia Tech PhD thesis on mixed-integer bilinear programming and pooling (p. 26). The industrial coauthor and the refinery motivation (p. 2) place it in an industry-funded collaboration; the funding acknowledgement is not in this preprint.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is consolidation plus a practitioner's need. The pooling literature "dispersed over different areas of optimization and chemical engineering" is gathered with "unifying arguments", together with new relaxation ideas and an empirical test of discretization (abstract p. 1; intro p. 2). Industrial relevance is argued in detail from refinery operations (crude scheduling, blending, refinery planning) (p. 2). The question for the upper-bound side is driven by solver maturity: "MILP solvers are more mature ... than global optimization solvers", so a MILP restriction may be solved faster than the BLP (p. 18).
  - Transferable technique: when a field is scattered, write the consolidation and the new results in one paper. Prove the equivalences others skipped and use them as the base for the new relaxations, so that the survey part does real work.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: (i) Explain why the pq-formulation is strong. Its LP relaxation equals convexifying each single-pool set Q_l, because the proportion variables live on a simplex and Gupte's theorem says that bilinear sets over a simplex × polyhedron are convexified by level-1 RLT (Thm 1, p. 11; Prop. 4, p. 13). The same relaxation equals a specific Lagrangian dual (Prop. 6, p. 17). (ii) New stronger relaxations: partial RLT R1–R3 (p. 15) and two new Lagrangian duals z^LAG2 and z^LAG3 (p. 17), ordered in (31) (p. 17). (iii) For upper bounds, discretize one variable of each bilinear term: either the flow y_lj (binary expansion) or the ratio q_il (n intervals, unary or binary). Strengthen the resulting MILPs with valid inequalities obtained by combining knapsack cover / divisible-knapsack facets with the simplex RLT theorem (Props 7–8, pp. 19–20). What is kept is the network-flow structure. What changes is which variable is discretized and how it is encoded.
  - Transferable technique: before inventing a new relaxation, prove an equivalence between the incumbent "strong" relaxation and a convex hull or Lagrangian dual. That theorem tells you which sets to enlarge (here, several pools sharing an output; the whole network) to get anything stronger.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The graph is acyclic; standard and generalized pooling are treated together (p. 3). Blending is linear (Note 1, p. 4). Spec values lie in [0,1] (p. 3). Nontrivial instances are assumed via Observations 2–5 (z* < 0, feasible outputs, at least one nontrivial spec bound) (pp. 4–5). Discretization assumes integer capacities, rounded down otherwise (p. 19), and the authors say there is "no clear intuition behind a suitable choice of n" for ratio discretization (p. 19). The hardness facts (NP-hard with one pool; polynomial cases) are summarised from others and from Dey–Gupte (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - Equivalence of P, Q, PQ, HYB for generalized pooling via path-flow decomposition. Proportions are built from flows along each directed path; this "tedious exercise was skipped in [4]" (Prop. 2, pp. 7–8).
  - The sum-decomposition rule shows that single-term McCormick is the best envelope over bounds alone (Prop. 3, p. 12). So strength must come from the linear constraints (p. 12).
  - The simplex RLT theorem (Gupte [32]) gives conv(Q_l) explicitly (eq. (22), p. 13). The linear map q→p transfers it to show PQ dominates P (Prop. 4, p. 13).
  - Remark 2 shows that DC-decomposition (Q = Q1 − Q2) relaxations are weaker than the envelope relaxation, because convex envelopes do not sum-decompose. Its stated conclusion: "this decomposition method for nonconvex QCQPs does not present any additional advantages" (p. 11).
  - The value-function argument φ(q) with saddle-point interchange proves z^LAG1 = z^pq (pp. 16–17). Disjunctive (exponential) LP representations of conv(S̃), conv(Š) are used to evaluate z^LAG2, z^LAG3 exactly on small instances (p. 21).
  - Valid inequalities for the binarised MILP combine the minimal-cover facets of a superincreasing knapsack [31, 33] and Marcotte's divisible-knapsack rounding with the simplex RLT theorem (Props 7–8, pp. 19–20).
  - Transferable technique: when one factor of a bilinear term lives on a simplex, any valid description of the other factor's set (knapsack facets, bounds) lifts to the bilinear set by multiplying through. So integer-programming polyhedral results transfer directly.
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Instances: the classic small instances are dropped as uninformative because BARON solves them in seconds; they serve only to test the Lagrangians (p. 21). Main set: 70 random medium/large standard instances (20 from Alfaki–Haugland, 50 from Dey–Gupte) plus 10 generalized instances (3 Meyer–Floudas, 7 new time-indexed) (p. 21).
  - Fairness protocol: BARON 6 h on NEOS vs CPLEX 1 h. The longer limit is given "for the sake of fair comparison", because BARON's time spent on feasible solutions cannot be separated out. Tolerances are aligned across solvers (p. 21).
  - Lower bounds: the Lagrangians are evaluated through exponential-size LPs on small instances only. z^LAG2 always equalled z^pq; z^LAG3 closed 12.5–100% of the gap on most instances (Table 2, p. 22). R1 gave nothing, R3 was too large, R2 helped on some instances at large time cost (p. 21; Table 3, p. 22).
  - Upper bounds: % gap to BARON's 6 h lower bound (p. 22). On generalized instances, the spec and ratio discretizations were mostly infeasible or found nothing within 1 h (p. 23). The time-to-best-solution vs gap table shows P-discretizations are fast but poor (gaps of about 57–70%) (Table 6, p. 24). The authors are explicitly surprised that the naive discretization with no mixing at pools works on random instances, and warn it "may well be an artifact" (pp. 23, 25). Best known upper bounds improved on 6 of 70 instances (Table 7, p. 25).
  - Scope disclaimer: "we do not implement our discretization strategies as part of a heuristic"; the aim is only to identify which discretization works (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: to judge whether a new dual bound is worth engineering, first compute it exactly with a brute-force exponential formulation on small instances. Only a large gap closed justifies looking for a separation algorithm (pp. 21, 25).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper extends previous surveys [7, 30, 50, 67] (p. 2). Its Prop. 4 result is claimed "stronger than previous results for standard [67] and generalized [4] pooling problems" (p. 13), and it extends Tawarmalani–Sahinidis Prop. 9.9 to generalized pooling (p. 16). Prior work is reframed rather than attacked: the "conventional wisdom" that PQ is strong is given a reason (p. 7). Others' omissions are noted neutrally ("This tedious exercise was skipped in [4]", p. 7). The SDP route is set aside on scalability grounds ("do not scale well", p. 10).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Contributions come as three numbered parts (pp. 2–3). Observations and Remarks are used to record easy facts before the propositions (pp. 4–5). Open ideas are labelled "We leave this idea open for future research" (p. 15). Negative computational findings are stated flatly ("R1 did not improve", "R3 was too large", p. 21). Hedging is calibrated ("seems to be a promising approach", p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution layers are unification and theory (equivalences, PQ = conv of single-pool sets = Lagrangian dual), new relaxations (R1–R3, LAG2, LAG3), MILP restrictions with valid inequalities, and computation (6 new best upper bounds). Stated limits: the Lagrangian bounds have no tractable formulation or separation ("significant hurdle", p. 25). There is no a priori rule for choosing a discretization (p. 23). Generalized instances are poorly served by PQ discretizations because of formulation size (pp. 21, 23). Future work: dynamic discretization inside branch-and-bound, and solver tuning (pp. 20, 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the paper takes a practitioner habit (prefer the pq-formulation; use MILP discretizations) and supplies the theorem explaining it ("an insight into the conventional wisdom behind (PQ) being a strong formulation", p. 7; Prop. 4, p. 13). The approximation-ratio theorem on piecewise-linear MILP relaxations is cited from the companion paper (p. 14), not proved here.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the strength of PQ comes from convexifying the single-pool substructure jointly over the simplex, not term by term (pp. 12–13). Stronger relaxations come from enlarging the substructure (pools sharing an output, R2; the whole network, R3) (p. 15).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the exact ceiling comes first. The new Lagrangian bounds are computed exactly by exponential disjunctive LPs before any separation is designed (p. 21).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: dual bounds are compared and the gap is measured against BARON's 6 h lower bound (p. 22). But the deliverable here is primal (good upper bounds from MILP restrictions), and the strongest new bounds are not tractable (p. 25).
- new-pattern candidate: "consolidate-then-extend". A survey with equivalence proofs serves as the platform for the new relaxations (pp. 2, 7–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use a simplex-domain RLT convex-hull theorem to show which "partial RLT" inequalities are already implied, and which enlargements could add strength (pp. 11–15).
- Give the global solver a longer time limit than the MILP heuristic, and state why (its primal-search time cannot be isolated). Align tolerances across solvers (p. 21).
- Report geometric mean "time at which the best solution was found" against geometric mean gap for each heuristic variant. This separates "fast but poor" from "slow but good" (Table 6, p. 24).
- Drop benchmark instances a global solver closes in seconds. Keep them only for the experiment they still inform (p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "Since these problems are solved in a matter of seconds by BARON, they are not of particular interest to us and are only used for demonstrating the strength of the Lagrangian relaxations in §5.1." (p. 21)
- "However, it is surprising that such a naive discretization gives fairly good solutions on random instances." (p. 25)

### S003 · Mixed-integer quadratic programming is in NP (Mathematical Programming 2017, DOI 10.1007/s10107-016-1036-0, arXiv:1407.4798)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–10 of arXiv v1 (17 Jul 2014) read, including the references. The published 2017 version was not read and may differ.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Del Pia, M. Molinaro (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: A complexity paper on the "MILP-to-nonlinear" axis (SKILL.md Method 4 lists it as the tight-in-the-number-of-quadratics example). It follows Del Pia–Weismantel's planar IQP result, cited as [2] (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: There was a gap in classical complexity. QP is in NP (Vavasis 1990) and ILP is in NP (Borosh–Treybig, von zur Gathen–Sieveking, Kannan–Monma, Papadimitriou), but MIQP, which contains both, had no membership proof (abstract and p. 1). The problem is stated as a small-certificate theorem: if the feasible set F(H,c,d,A,b) of one quadratic inequality over a mixed-integer polyhedron is nonempty, it contains a point of polynomial encoding size (Thm 1, p. 2). The introduction names the major open question beyond it: is IQP polynomial in fixed dimension? (p. 1)
  - Transferable technique: look for a class that sits at the join of two classical results (QP in NP, ILP in NP) and ask whether the join inherits the property.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Decompose the mixed-integer linear set into pieces P_i + int.cone(R_K), each a polytope plus the integer cone of a simple cone. Each piece is "affinely isomorphic to some Z^n′_+", and its product structure lets the quadratic be analysed one integer ray at a time (Prop. 4 and comment, pp. 3–4). If the recession cone contains a direction of negative curvature, march along it (Case 1, p. 7). Otherwise, refine the cones so that zero-curvature directions appear as extreme rays (Lemma 7, pp. 5–6), use rays where Q is linear and decreasing (Claim 1, pp. 7–8), and otherwise bound all solutions (Claim 2, pp. 8–9). Then fix the integer part and invoke Vavasis on the continuous QP (p. 9). The paper keeps Vavasis's continuous theorem and the classical MILP small-solution argument unchanged as black boxes (Thm 3, Prop. 4, p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Rational data. Exactly one quadratic inequality (any symmetric H, not necessarily convex) plus linear constraints (eq. (2), p. 1). The polyhedron is made pointed by splitting into 2^n orthants, at O(n) extra complexity (p. 6). "Small" means polynomial in the input encoding (definition of O-small complexity, p. 3). No convexity is assumed.
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - Simple-cone decomposition of a mixed-integer polyhedron via Carathéodory and fractional/integer splitting of multipliers (Prop. 4, pp. 3–4). This also gives "MIP is in NP" as a by-product (Prop. 5, pp. 4–5).
  - Normalizing hyperplane f^T x = 1 with polynomially bounded f and f^T x ≥ 1/R on unit vectors (Lemma 6, p. 5). It turns cone questions into compact optimisation, to which Vavasis applies.
  - Recursive cone refinement: by induction on dimension, each simple cone is split by coning a minimiser x̄ of x^T H x on C ∩ H with the facets not containing it. Every face whose minimum is zero then has a zero-curvature extreme ray (Lemma 7, pp. 5–6). The proof is "a constructive algorithm" (p. 6).
  - One-dimensional quadratic analysis along rays: the concave case gives an explicit λ̃ from the larger root (eq. (9), p. 7); the convex case bounds λ by the larger root of λ²v₁* + λv₂* + v₃* (Claim 2, p. 8).
  - Tightness by three counterexamples, each dropping one hypothesis (pp. 2–3). (1) With "many" quadratic inequalities, feasibility is undecidable (a reduction through a 58-variable quartic, giving 3,424 quadratics). (2) Two quadratic inequalities, via Pell-type equations, have exponential-size minimal solutions. (3) Many convex quadratics x_i ≥ x_{i−1}² give exponential size.
  - NP-hardness via max-cut written as one quadratic inequality over binaries (Cor. 2, p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: to prove a small-witness theorem for a nonconvex class, decompose into pieces isomorphic to Z^k_+ plus a polytope. Classify directions by the sign of the quadratic form on the recession cone. Refine cones until every zero-curvature face has a zero-curvature extreme ray. Then the analysis is one-dimensional along each ray.
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (pure complexity paper; no computation).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents itself as generalising and unifying QP ∈ NP and ILP ∈ NP (abstract, p. 1). It cites Koeppe's survey for the undecidability and Lagarias for Pell equations (p. 2), and Khachiyan for the convex example (p. 2). It relies on Schrijver for polyhedral complexity facts (pp. 4–5). Del Pia–Weismantel's planar IQP result is cited as the frontier (p. 1). No prior work is criticised.

### S005 · New formulation and strong MISOCP relaxations for AC optimal transmission switching problem (IEEE Transactions on Power Systems 2017, DOI 10.1109/tpwrs.2017.2666718, arXiv:1510.02064)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–25 of arXiv v3 read, including Appendices A–B (pp. 20–21) and the references. Figure 1 (p. 19) is partly garbled as text; its caption and numbers were read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: B. Kocuk (first author), X. A. Sun (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The switching extension of "Strong SOCP relaxations for the optimal power flow problem" ([27], p. 23) in the AC-OPF arc (SKILL.md Method 5). It builds on the DC paper S009 ([29], p. 23) for the cycle basis and the cycle disjunction.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a modelling gap in an application. OTS research relied on the DC approximation, which "may lead to inaccurate flow solutions and also overlook stability issues" (abstract, p. 1). The DC-optimal topology is not guaranteed to be AC feasible (p. 2), and AC OTS "is much less explored" (p. 2). The method entry is the authors' own OPF work: SOCP relaxations that are "not dominated by the SDP relaxations" and run "an order of magnitude faster" (p. 2) are extended to the harder switching problem (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: carry a relaxation toolkit proven on the continuous problem (OPF) to its discrete-topology extension (OTS). Ask which strengthening still works when every constraint becomes conditional on a binary.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: (i) A new exact MINLP formulation of OTS in c, s, x variables, with the angle condition θ_j − θ_i = atan2(s, c) multiplied by x (eq. (5), pp. 5–6). (ii) A MISOCP relaxation that drops the arctangent constraint, uses McCormick on c^j_ii = c_ii x_ij, and keeps the rotated-cone relaxation of c² + s² = c_ii c_jj (eq. (6), pp. 6–7). (iii) Three ways to put the dropped angle information back (pp. 7–11): polyhedral arctangent envelopes lifted by (2π − γ′)(1 − x) (Prop. 4.1, pp. 7–8); an SDP disjunction over cycles, "every line on" vs "at least one off" (pp. 8–9); a McCormick disjunction over bilinearised cycle constraints (pp. 10–11). The last two are combined as conv((S1 ∩ M1) ∪ S0) and separated by SDP (p. 11). (iv) Bound tightening by solving SOCPs on the r-neighbourhood of each line, with r = 2 and in parallel (pp. 11–13). The same SOCP is used to fix binaries (p. 13). (v) A two-phase algorithm: cut rounds on the SOCP relaxation, then MISOCP solves whose solution pools give topologies, each evaluated by IPOPT and excluded with no-good cuts (Algorithm 1, pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable technique: for a constraint that is too nonconvex to relax directly (the angle/arctangent condition), drop it from the base relaxation. Then re-inject it through several separate cut families, each from a different convex set (envelope, SDP cone, McCormick of a cycle polynomial). Combine the families at the separation level.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Full AC power flow in rectangular form with variables c_ii, c_ij, s_ij (eqs (1)–(4), pp. 3–5). c > 0 on the box for the arctangent envelope (p. 7). Variable bounds are crucial and are computed rather than assumed (p. 11). Generator cost linear or convex quadratic (p. 4). Finite convergence (Obs. 5.1, p. 14) requires a global OPF oracle, available only for some IEEE instances through moment/SOS. In practice IPOPT gives only a valid gap LB₁ ≤ z* ≤ UB (Obs. 5.2, p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - Arctangent envelope: planes through three of the four box corners, shifted by the maximum violation Δγ_k. That maximum is computed by enumerating the KKT points of a nonconvex 2-variable problem, and the shift is lifted with the binary through (2π − γ′)(1 − x) (Prop. 4.1, pp. 7–8).
  - Disjunctive convex hull of the union of two bounded conic-representable sets via the standard perspective/extended formulation (Appendix A, pp. 20–21). Cut separation from an extended conic set is posed as a conic program with the normalisation −e ≤ α ≤ e, −1 ≤ β ≤ 1 (Appendix B, p. 21).
  - The cycle condition is written as cos(Σθ) = 1 times Πx, expanded into a homogeneous polynomial of degree |C|, then "bilinearized" by splitting long cycles with artificial lines (pp. 10–11, details deferred to [27]).
  - No new theorems; the two Observations are immediate (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: to cut off a relaxation point from a nonconvex mixed-integer set over a cycle, build a convex outer set for each branch of a cheap disjunction (here SDP-representable when all lines are on, SOC-representable otherwise). Separate over the union's extended formulation with a normalised conic separation problem.
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Instances: 11 IEEE instances from MATPOWER and 16 NESTA 0.3.0 congested instances, up to 300 buses. Larger instances are excluded "since Phase II of the Algorithm 1 does not scale up well" (pp. 15–16).
  - Setup: three nested settings, SOCP → SOCPA (+ arctangent) → SOCPA Disj (+ disjunctive cuts) (p. 15). Metrics: %OG (proved optimality gap), %CB (cost benefit vs no switching), #off, total time including preprocessing. T1 = 5 cut rounds, T2 = 5 MISOCP rounds at 720 s each, 0.1% early stop, stated hardware (pp. 15–16).
  - Results: on IEEE, the average %OG is 0.05% for SOCPA Disj vs 0.12% for SOCP (Table 1, p. 17). On NESTA it is 1.16% vs 4.24%, with an average cost benefit of 6.21%. 118ieee is the hard case (7.50% gap, 39.09% saving) (Table 2, p. 17). Cases where the stronger method is slightly worse are acknowledged (189edin, 300ieee, MISOCPs hitting the time limit) (p. 18).
  - External comparison: the QC relaxation results of [18] are compared from the published numbers, not re-run. Their average gap of 0.14% is worse than any of the three methods, except on 118ieee (p. 16). The authors note "there is limited literature for this purpose" (p. 16).
  - Diagnosis: the gaps are split between integrality and power-flow nonconvexity. For case3lmbd the gap is due only to power flow, since all topologies were enumerated (p. 18).
  - Illustration: case6ww is worked by hand, showing why switching line (1,2) saves 7.74% (cheaper generator unlocked) (pp. 18–19). The best-single-line heuristic gives 19.52% saving vs 39.09% on 118ieee (pp. 19–20).

Prior AC-OTS approaches are described neutrally: QC relaxation + BONMIN [18], a ranking heuristic [42], DC-then-correct [5] (p. 2). The no-good cut cites Angulo–Ahmed–Dey–Kaibel "Forbidden vertices" [2] (p. 13).

- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution layers are formulation, relaxation plus valid inequalities, an algorithm with certified gaps, and an application insight (large savings under congestion). Stated limits: no scaling beyond 300 buses (p. 16), MISOCP solve time is the bottleneck (p. 20), some MISOCPs are unsolved within the time limit (p. 18), and global optimality requires a global OPF oracle (p. 14). ### S021 · Bounding the gap between the McCormick relaxation and the convex hull for bilinear functions (Mathematical Programming 2017, DOI 10.1007/s10107-016-1031-5, arXiv:1507.08703)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–12 of arXiv v3 (31 May 2016) read, including acknowledgements and references.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: N. Boland (Georgia Tech), T. Kalinowski, F. Rigterink (Newcastle, Australia), M. Molinaro (PUC-Rio) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: A relaxation-strength paper on the McCormick relaxation, the default device of global solvers (p. 1). It is a sibling of the "measure how good the solver's relaxation is" line (SKILL.md Method 1, Taste 2). Funding comes from an ARC Linkage grant with an Australian coal-chain coordinator and a software firm (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a named open question. Luedtke, Namazifar and Linderoth proved that the McCormick gap is at most 2 − 1/⌈χ(G)/2⌉ times the convex-hull gap for nonnegative coefficients, and only n in general. They asked whether a constant independent of n exists (pp. 1–2). The measure is fixed first: c*(b) = inf{c : mcgap ≤ c·chgap on [0,1]^n} (p. 2). The motivation is solver practice: "Many modern global optimization solvers ... follow a general approach, proposed by McCormick" (p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: take a ratio measure defined in a peer's paper, with their bounds and their open question, as the problem statement. Then a negative answer and a matching upper bound together settle it.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key insight is that at half-integral points the convex-hull gap equals half the range of cut weights (max cut − min cut) in the subgraph induced by the fractional coordinates. The McCormick gap equals half the total absolute weight (Lemma 1, p. 4; Cor. 1, p. 6). So the question becomes a graph-discrepancy question: how small can max cut − min cut be relative to Σ|a_ij|? Random ±1 complete graphs make it of order n^{3/2} against n², which gives the √n lower bound. A discrepancy theorem gives the matching O(√n) upper bound (Thms 1–3, p. 3). Kept from [10]: the three-step reduction to x ∈ {0, 1/2, 1}^n (pp. 3–4). Changed: step 2 is sharpened from a crude bound into an exact cut formula (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Bilinear b(x) = Σ a_ij x_i x_j over the unit hypercube with arbitrary real coefficients. The edge signs define a signed graph G (pp. 1–2). The lower bound uses random ±1 coefficients on the complete graph (Thm 1, p. 3); an explicit Hadamard-based instance is also given (Lemma 2, p. 7). No restriction to positive coefficients, which was the case of [10] (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof devices**:
  - LP duality for envelope values: vex[b](x) is written as an LP over distributions on vertices. A primal solution from a max cut and an explicit dual (y = −μ⁺/2, z_i = half the incident weights) certify the exact value (Lemma 1, pp. 4–6).
  - Lower bound: Chernoff plus a union bound over 2^{n−1} cuts gives |cut weight| ≤ 0.6 n^{3/2} with probability → 1 (pp. 6–7). An explicit instance comes from the Hadamard matrix H_ij = (−1)^{⟨i,j⟩} with an AM-GM bound, valid for n ≥ 18 (p. 7).
  - Upper bound (discrepancy of cuts, Thm 3): a random half-partition to get a heavy bipartite part (eq. (4), p. 8), the Paley–Zygmund inequality with the Khintchine–Haagerup fourth-moment bound for each vertex (Lemma 3, pp. 8–9), Cauchy–Schwarz (p. 9), and a three-case argument following Nikolov's Theoretical Computer Science Stack Exchange answer (pp. 9–10). The constant 600 comes out of this chain.
  - Equality characterisation (Thm 4): a re-proof of Misener–Smadbeck–Floudas through the cut correspondence, using quotient graphs of positive/negative paths, which must be bipartite (pp. 10–11). They say they give this proof "in order to show how this result can be derived from the correspondence" (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: translate an envelope-gap question into a combinatorial quantity through an exact LP-duality formula at a structured set of points. Then import the discrepancy-theory toolkit (Chernoff + union bound for lower bounds; Paley–Zygmund + Khintchine for upper bounds).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no computation; the random-instance result is a probabilistic theorem, pp. 6–7).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper "extend[s]" Luedtke–Namazifar–Linderoth [10] (p. 1) and answers their open question negatively (abstract; p. 2). It reuses their proof skeleton, credited step by step (pp. 3–4, 6). Theorem 4 is credited as "a direct consequence of Theorem 3.10 in [12]" (Misener et al.) (p. 3). The paper thanks Linderoth and Luedtke for discussions, and Aleksandar Nikolov for pointing to "old arguments by Spencer and Erdős" (p. 11). Cites the Stack Exchange answer as reference [14] (p. 12). Calls itself a "note" (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Four theorems stated together in "Main results" (p. 3) before any proof; proof roadmap paragraph (pp. 3–4). Theorem 3 is marketed as "of independent interest" (p. 3). Asymptotic tightness phrased crisply: "√n is the correct leading term for the asymptotics" (p. 3). No hedging.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Theory only: answers [10]'s question (no constant bound), improves the bound from n to O(√n), proves asymptotic tightness, and characterises equality (abstract p. 1; p. 3). The constants (1/4 vs 600) are far apart, and the paper does not discuss closing them. Limit: the results cover bilinear functions over the hypercube only, and the paper does not discuss extensions to multilinear functions or general polytopes. The bipartite remark shows that bipartiteness does not help with mixed signs (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (strong): an upper bound O(√n), a matching lower bound for almost all ±1 instances plus an explicit construction, and an exact characterisation of when the ratio is 1 (pp. 3, 6–11).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the object is the relaxation every spatial branch-and-bound solver uses (p. 1). The measure c*(b) is a well-defined ratio fixed before the analysis (p. 2), and the result quantifies how much worse than the hull the solver's default can be.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (contrast form): the paper quantifies the cost of term-by-term convexification (McCormick) vs convexifying the whole bilinear function. The √n gap is the quantitative case for joint convexification of substructures (pp. 1–3).
- new-pattern candidate: "answer a named open question". Take the ratio and open question posed in a peers' paper (Luedtke–Namazifar–Linderoth) and settle it in both directions (pp. 1–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Evaluate convex/concave envelopes at half-integral points through the vertex-distribution LP and its dual. The envelope becomes a max-cut/min-cut expression (Lemma 1, pp. 4–6).
- Use Chernoff + union bound over all 2^{n−1} cuts to show that a random signed instance has small cut range (pp. 6–7). Use a Hadamard matrix for an explicit deterministic instance (p. 7).
- Use Paley–Zygmund with a Khintchine fourth-moment bound to get constant-probability anti-concentration of a random signed sum (Lemma 3, pp. 8–9).
- Re-prove a known characterisation through your new correspondence to show its explanatory power, and credit the original (p. 10).
