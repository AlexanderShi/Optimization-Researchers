### S082 · Approximating the Gomory mixed-integer cut closure using historical data (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv:2411.15090)
**Read level**: full. Main text pp. 1–13 and Appendices A–C pp. 16–24 read in full. Supplementary tables pp. 25–68 skimmed: captions, Tables 5, 9, the start of Table 10, and the averages of Tables 12–14.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Berkay Becu (Georgia Tech), Feng Qiu and Álinson S. Xavier (Argonne) · **Place in trajectory**: joins the data-driven, recurring-instance MILP line with Argonne (cited own work: kNN for DC transmission switching [37], instance-similarity branching [53], MIPLearn [45], p. 13–16) to his older cut-closure and aggregation work (aggregation multipliers credited to Bodur–Del Pia–Dey–Molinaro–Pokutta [13], p. 4; cut selection [26], p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper starts from an operational setting. Operational MILPs such as unit commitment are re-solved hourly or daily with the same constraint matrix, and only the RHS and objective change (pp. 1–2). The target is the GMIC closure: it is strong (Balas–Saxena: over 70% of the gap closed on MIPLIB 2003, p. 5), polyhedral and numerically safe at rank 1 (pp. 3, 5), but separating over it exactly is NP-hard and existing heuristics are "too computationally expensive" for commercial solvers (pp. 3, 5). The question is posed as "What 'information' from historical data, if any, could be exploited to learn good aggregation multipliers?" (p. 5). It is sharpened by an obstacle: an arbitrarily small RHS perturbation can turn an integral LP into one with many useful GMICs (p. 5).
  - Transferable: before learning anything across instances, write down why the object you want to transfer could be wildly unstable under small data changes, then find the data model (here, RHS on a lattice) under which it is not.
- **D2 Core idea**: Learn aggregation multipliers λ, not cuts. Each λ gives a one-row relaxation IP_λ and hence a GMIC (eq. 3–4, p. 4). Theorem 1 (p. 6) says that for a fixed integral A and RHS ranging over a lattice generated by rational vectors, a single finite set Λ yields the full GMIC closure of every instance. Corollary 1 (p. 6) gives all RHS for pure IPs. Theorem 4 (p. 21) gives the same result for split closures. The theorem "suggests a simple learning heuristic" (p. 6). Collect multipliers on training instances with a simplified Fischetti–Salvagnin relax-and-cut procedure (p. 7; Appendix B, pp. 21–22), store them as basis plus tableau row (p. 10), then re-apply them on test instances chosen by k-nearest, k-farthest or k-random similarity in a scaled (b, c) feature space (p. 10). What stays from the classical approach: GMIC formula (1) and Gurobi defaults. What changes: where the multipliers come from.
  - Transferable: parametrise a cut family by its aggregation weights and transfer the weights across instances. They are invariant to the instance data in a way the cut coefficients are not.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A and b are integral without loss of generality (p. 6). The RHS family is b + Γ with Γ a lattice generated by rational vectors (eq. 5, p. 6). The paper says plainly that this excludes arbitrarily close RHS ("so not arbitrarily close-by right-hand-sides", p. 5) and that the all-RHS case for mixed-integer programs is open (p. 12). Remark 1 (p. 6) explains why the Dash–Günlük–Morán finite split-closure results and unimodular-transformation arguments do not apply directly. The experiments leave the theorem's model behind: RHS are perturbed multiplicatively by r ∈ [0.9, 1.1], which is not a lattice, and objectives by [0.75, 1.25] (p. 8; Appendix C pp. 23–24). The heuristic is thus motivated by Theorem 1, not covered by it.
- **D4 Proof devices**: (i) Split cuts in "legitimate (λ, v)" form, with each GMIC as the optimal v for a fixed λ (pp. 16–17, after Conforti–Cornuéjols–Zambelli §5.1). (ii) Lemma 1: an undominated cut has basis support (p. 17). (iii) A sign cone C_sign(u) that does not depend on the RHS (p. 17). The key step is to decompose u = u¹ + u² with u² integral and divisible by M(Γ), so u² does not change the fractional part on any lattice RHS. Then u¹ dominates u (pp. 17–18). (iv) Integral extreme rays of C_sign scaled by M(Γ). Carathéodory gives ‖u¹‖∞ ≤ H = (m+n)·M(Γ)·Δ, so the set Π of split directions is finite (p. 18). (v) Corollary 2 for pure IPs handles a fractional RHS row with λ = e_i, since then the instance is infeasible (p. 21). The authors credit the argument openly: "The proof above closely follows arguments presented in [19]" (p. 19); the MIR-closure argument of [24] is also named (p. 17). The bound H is not quantified for any instance. Defects in the text: Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 repeat the statement and proof of Theorem 1 word for word (pp. 17–20); Corollary 1/2 name the finite set "Γ" where Λ is meant (pp. 6, 21); Δ is used both for a sub-determinant bound and for a multiplier set (pp. 18, 21).
  - Transferable: to show that one finite family of cuts serves an infinite family of instances, find a cone of certificates that does not depend on the RHS. Split each certificate into a bounded part plus an integral part that leaves the fractional part unchanged on the whole instance family. Carathéodory then bounds the bounded part.
- **D5 Experiments**: Setup: Julia/JuMP within MIPLearn, Gurobi 11.0.2, single thread, 16 jobs in parallel, defaults including cuts and presolve, 3 seeds, and work units reported alongside time. Gurobi is given the optimal solution "since this manuscript focuses on the dual side" (pp. 7–8). Instances: 55 variations per MIPLIB 2017 benchmark instance (50 train, 5 test); 220 → 186 instances after the perturbation rules (p. 8). The perturbation quality was checked: 157 of 186 had five distinct optimal integer parts (p. 8, Table 5 p. 36), and the variations got harder on average (arithmetic mean 911 s → 1868 s, pp. 8–9). Ceiling: an "expert method" that runs cut collection on the test instance and does not charge its time (p. 9). After 48 more eliminations, mostly zero cuts from dual degeneracy (Table 9 p. 53: 149 of 190 failures), 135 instances remain: 50 positive, 36 neutral, 49 negative at ±1% work-unit thresholds (p. 9). An exp+col row then adds the collection time back (p. 11). Results on the 50 positive instances: every learning variant beats default Gurobi in work units, up to 1.49× (50-closest) and 1.40× on the 14 hard instances (≥5 min), and most beat the expert (pp. 10–11, Tables 1–2). Findings that go against the authors' expectations: more cuts were better, and "Instance similarity does not appear to be a critical factor", with 10-farthest the best of the three selectors (p. 11). In total time only 1-closest has a positive average speedup on all 50 instances; on the hard instances all variants win with 1.08–1.17× (p. 12). exp+col gives 0.46× (p. 12). Speedups are arithmetic means of per-variation ratios (p. 11; Table 12 "Arithmetic Average", p. 66). Reader's note, not stated by the authors: the 50 evaluation instances were chosen by the expert's results on the same test variations (p. 9), so the reported averages are conditioned on known headroom.
  - Transferable: give three rows. (1) A ceiling that does not charge its overhead. (2) The same ceiling with the overhead charged. (3) The learned method with all its overhead charged. Report work units (solver effort only) and wall time (everything) side by side.
  - Transferable: when you build perturbed instance families, show they are neither trivial nor degenerate: count distinct optimal solutions per family, and check that difficulty did not fall.
- **D6 Positioning**: A short survey of ML-for-cuts: RL (Tang et al.), imitation (Paulus et al.), multi-cut selection, learned parameters, sample-complexity theory (Balcan et al.; Cheng et al.), and generators (Chételat–Lodi; Dragotto et al.; Guaje et al.), with a pointer to the Deza–Khalil survey for more (p. 2). The paper sets itself apart by the strength of its claim, not by criticising these works: "the first work in the literature of data-driven cutting plane generation that is able to significantly accelerate the performance of a commercial state-of-the-art MILP solver, using default solver settings, on large-scale benchmark instances" (abstract; p. 3). A historical aside places closures as a practical matter. GMICs were thought "useless in practice" until Balas et al. 1996 added them from all fractional rows at once (p. 4). Remark 2 links Corollary 1 to Wolsey's 1981 finite list of subadditive functions for all RHS (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing**: Two contributions are stated in one paragraph on p. 3 and repeated in the abstract. Five numbered observations, each with a bold-faced thesis sentence, carry the results (pp. 11–12). Hedging is light and targeted: "we anticipate much lower overhead" if the method were integrated into Gurobi (p. 12). Proofs go to an appendix. The main text keeps only theorem statements and two remarks on related results (pp. 6). The appendix duplication (Theorems 2 and 3, pp. 17–20) and symbol slips mark this as an unpolished v1.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions are theory (a finiteness theorem), an algorithm (a reuse heuristic), software (released in MIPLearn, p. 3) and benchmark families. The limits are stated concretely. Overhead comes from recomputing the standard form and tableaux without access to solver internals (p. 12). Perturbations stay within ±10% RHS and ±25% objective (p. 13). Open questions: all RHS for MILP, and extension to group cuts (p. 12). A better expert that cuts from infeasible bases is also listed (p. 13). Not discussed: the gap between the lattice model and the multiplicative perturbations of the experiments, and the conditioning of the instance set on the expert.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the expert ceiling decides whether to proceed ("most positive instances become negative, which motivates our learning approach", p. 9). The exp+col row charges overhead (pp. 11–12), which is also Heuristic 6.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: there is no stripped instrument (step 3). The evaluation runs inside full default Gurobi, and only the primal side is neutralised by supplying the optimal solution (pp. 7–8).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: theory comes first and the rule follows from it ("Theorem 1 suggests a simple learning heuristic", p. 6). Step 8 of Method 1 is reached, but the theorem does not explain an existing solver habit; it licenses a new one.
- Method 4 ⚠ partial: the boundary is drawn as an open question (all RHS for MILP, p. 12) and a stronger corollary for pure IP (p. 6). There is no tightness example and no counterexample for the lattice hypothesis.
- New-pattern candidate "recurring-instance MILP with a structural license": take the fixed-A, varying-(b, c) operational setting (pp. 1–2), prove a structural statement that makes cross-instance reuse principled, then learn. Own related works are cited [18, 37, 53] (pp. 2, 13–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Learn the aggregation weights λ, not the cuts. Store λ implicitly as (basis, tableau row) so the cut can be recomputed on a new RHS and objective (p. 10).
- Ceiling without overhead / ceiling with overhead / learned method with overhead, reported in work units and in wall time (pp. 9–12).
- Isolate the dual side of a MILP experiment by handing the solver the optimal solution, and fix the thread count and seeds to tame variability (pp. 7–8).
- Instance-family generator with rules by constraint type: multiplicative on rows with continuous variables or inequalities, ±1 additive on all-integer equalities, combinatorial rows (set partitioning, covering, packing) left alone, families rejected if any perturbation is infeasible (Appendix C, p. 23).
- RHS-independent sign cone + integral/fractional split + Carathéodory as a finiteness argument for closures over lattice families of RHS (pp. 17–19).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, simple perturbation can change the GMIC closure dramatically." (p. 5)
- "However, when accounting for the time required to collect the cuts, most positive instances become negative, which motivates our learning approach." (p. 9)

### S097 · Lagrangian dual with zero duality gap that admits decomposition (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv:2411.12085)
**Read level**: full. All 38 pages read: main text pp. 1–13, references pp. 14–16, Appendices A–G pp. 17–38, including all proofs. Several displayed formulas are garbled in the text layer: the factors in Theorems 3–4 (p. 6), the iteration bounds in Propositions 6–7 (p. 8), and the closed form in Corollary 1 (p. 11). The card does not restate them exactly.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Diego Cifuentes, Jingye Xu (both Georgia Tech) · **Place in trajectory**: continues the Cifuentes–Dey–Xu line on monomial reformulations of mixed-binary programs (sensitivity analysis, IPCO 2024 [13], p. 5). It reuses the packing/covering analysis of Dey–Molinaro–Wang on sparse cuts for sparse MILPs [14] (p. 6) and his exact augmented-Lagrangian work with Gu and Ahmed [21] (p. 2). The SKILL.md cites the IPCO 2025 version (doi:10.1007/978-3-031-93112-3_14) under Method 5. That is S104, not this text.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The setting is block-structured MIPs with coupling constraints. Dualizing the coupling gives a Lagrangian relaxation that decomposes and runs in parallel, but MIP nonconvexity leaves a duality gap. Solving the full problem has no gap but does not decompose (pp. 1–2). The question is stated as twin goals, zero gap and decomposability at once (p. 2). Related approaches fall short of one goal or the other. DP decomposes along tree decompositions but gives no bound "until the algorithm terminates" (p. 2). Augmented Lagrangian reaches strong duality but not decomposability "as the penalty terms have to be norms" (p. 2). Specialised exact methods exist only for two-stage stochastic programs (p. 2).
  - Transferable: frame the contribution as two properties that existing methods each give one of, and name for each competitor which property it loses and why.
- **D2 Core idea**: Add primal-redundant constraints that become separable after dualizing (eq. 4–7, pp. 3–4). They do not change the primal but can raise the dual. Proposition 1 (p. 4) shows that if either side F^(i) is affine the dual does not improve, which forces non-affine choices. There are two of them (p. 4). (a) Monomial (M-Lagrangian): equate ∏_{j∈S} x_j^(1) = ∏_{j∈S} x_j^(2) for S in a down-closed family. This is an RLT-like hierarchy, but one that stays separable, which RLT's products of differences (x₁^(1) − x₁^(2))(x₂^(1) − x₂^(2)) = 0 do not (Remark 1, p. 5). (b) Vertex (V-Lagrangian): equate vertex indicator products for every vertex of [0,1]^n. This is "more like a 'column-generation' approach" (p. 5), solved in the original space with no-good cuts (Algorithm 1, p. 7). Results: Theorem 2 (p. 6) gives zero gap for M with S = 2^[n]; Theorem 5 (p. 7) gives zero gap for V. For general sparse MIPs the paper uses a tree decomposition of the intersection graph (Defs 1–2, p. 9; Theorem 8, p. 10). What changes from classical Lagrangian decomposition: the lifted variables and their copies. The price is subproblems with nonlinear objectives (p. 13).
  - Transferable: to strengthen a decomposable dual, add constraints that are redundant in the primal and separable after dualizing, and first rule out the classes that provably cannot help (affine ones, Prop. 1).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Two-block model (1) with binary linking x and nonnegative mixed-integer y (pp. 1–2). The coupling variables are binary throughout (p. 10). S is down-closed (Assumption 1, p. 6; Assumption 3, p. 10). The packing bounds need relatively complete recourse (Assumption 2, p. 6; Assumption 4, p. 10), with a note that it "is commonly made in the stochastic programming literature" and can be enforced by adding constraints (pp. 6, 11). The V-Lagrangian is generalised only to star (two-stage) instances, because Algorithm 1 "does not generalize very easily" (p. 8). Proposition 6 assumes nonnegative c, d (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (i) The primal (Geoffrion) characterisation of the Lagrangian dual as optimisation over the convex hulls of the blocks (eq. 11, p. 5; Prop. 1 proof p. 17). (ii) The vectors of all subset products of a 0/1 vector are affinely independent (Prop. 14, p. 19, proved via a maximal-support argument). With the simplex lemma this gives Proposition 13 (p. 19): convex hulls glue along shared affinely independent coordinates, so "intersection of hulls" equals "hull of intersection". (iii) Induction that removes a leaf of the tree decomposition (Lemma 2, pp. 20–21), then projection ⊆ intersection of projections (Prop. 11) for A(S,T) ⊆ B(S,T) (p. 22). (iv) The packing/covering multiplicative bounds come from a fractional-cover LP over "good"/"k-good" variable subsets (η_k, θ_k, eq. 23–24, p. 11), adapted from [14] (p. 6). Lemma 3 builds a feasible point by zeroing variables outside W (packing, p. 22–25). The covering proof rounds up by a max over copies and pays the factor τ, the maximal number of bags containing a variable (pp. 26–28). Symmetry reduces the cover LP to two dimensions for two-stage problems (Corollary 1, p. 29). (v) A counterexample: a 3-block cycle (17) where even all monomials give the classical dual value 0 against OPT 1 (p. 9). This is why a tree is required. (vi) Small worst-case instances for n = 2, k = 1 against the bounds: packing ratio ≥ 5/4 against the proven 4/3, covering ≤ 3/4 against 2/3 (p. 12; constructions p. 30). (vii) Subgradient complexity: ‖g‖² ≤ n+2, because each subproblem's w has support one (Cor. 2, p. 32). Explicit dual optima built from SDA's termination state bound ‖(λ*, μ*)‖² ≤ 8φ²t or 9φ²t (Props 16–17, pp. 33–36). A separation instance where SDA needs at least 2^{n−3} iterations (p. 37); Proposition 7 states this as "O(2^n)" (p. 8), which really reads as a lower bound.
  - Transferable: to prove that a lifted Lagrangian dual is exact on a tree, show that the lifted coordinates shared by two blocks are affinely independent. Then gluing the convex hulls along them loses nothing, and induction on leaves finishes the proof.
  - Transferable: get approximation factors for hierarchy levels as the value of a fractional covering LP over the variable subsets on which level k is exact.
- **D5 Experiments**: "Preliminary" (p. 12). A Windows PC with an i7 and 16 GB RAM, Julia 1.9, Gurobi 9.0.2, Clarabel for the bundle QPs. Two classes, STAR-STAB and PATH-STAB, with 10 blocks of maximum-cardinality stable set on random graphs, 10 instances each (p. 12). Methods: classical Lagrangian (L), M-Lagrangian with quadratic monomials only (QL), V-Lagrangian (VL, star only), SDA [2], and Gurobi on the monolithic problem. Each method gets 1200 s. QL and VL are warm-started from 600 s of L. Subproblems are solved sequentially, and no method terminates (p. 12). Mean primal–dual gap: STAR: Gurobi 10.0%, L 6.0%, QL 4.4%, VL 3.9%, SDA 8.6%. PATH: Gurobi 10.8%, L 3.5%, QL 1.2% (Tables 1–2, p. 13). Only averages are given, with no spread. The bundle variant is chosen per method from what was "empirically observed" (proximal for L and QL, bundle-level for VL), and the VL constraints are scaled by 2 for numerical reasons (Appendix G, p. 37). Inconsistency: §4 says random graphs on 100 nodes (p. 12), while G.1 says "75 nodes" but samples edges from a 100-node clique (p. 37).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on and extends rather than criticising. Proposition 1 is "a strengthening on a result from [26]" (Lemaréchal–Renaud, p. 4). The M-hierarchy is "similar to ... RLT ... but not the same" (p. 2), closer to Burer's and their own [13] reformulations, and DRL* (Minoux–Ouzia) is noted (p. 5). V-Lagrangian is positioned against Ahmed's SDA [2], with a proved at-most-polynomial-overhead relation (Prop. 6) and an exponential separation (Prop. 7, p. 8). Sun–Sun–Yin's decomposable AL variant [33] is acknowledged (p. 2). In the conclusion the authors caution that "significantly more sophisticated methods for star (2-stage stochastic) problems [23]" exist and were not compared (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing**: Five numbered contributions in the introduction (pp. 2–3). Every theorem in the main text points to an appendix proof. One cross-reference is wrong: Theorem 2's proof is said to be in "Appendix D" (p. 6) but is in Appendix C (p. 18). The negative example (17) appears before the general theory as motivation for tree decompositions (p. 9). Remark 2 says plainly that the bounds probably are not tight: "we don't expect that the multiplicative bounds ... are tight for general choice of k" (pp. 11–12). The conclusion opens with the cost of the method rather than its benefit (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: exactness theorems, hierarchy bounds, and complexity comparisons with SDA. Algorithm: Algorithm 1 in the original space. Computation is preliminary. Stated limits: harder nonlinear subproblems, so the method suits "a large number of sub-problems, but each sub-problem is not too large" (p. 13). V-Lagrangian does not extend beyond star structures (p. 8). Tightness of Theorems 9–10 is open (p. 12). Only a naive sequential implementation, not compared with specialised two-stage methods (p. 13). Future work: an exact decomposable dual with "easier" subproblems, real engineering of the implementation, and MIPLIB instances with detected tree structure [7] (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: dual bounds are the deliverable. The argument for Lagrangian duals over DP is that bounds are available at any stopping time (p. 2). Experiments report the primal–dual gap against Gurobi's best feasible point (p. 12).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper marks the boundaries of its own results. Prop. 1 shows affine redundant constraints cannot help (p. 4). The 3-block cycle counterexample shows strong duality fails without a tree (p. 9). The open tightness question comes with explicit small worst cases (p. 12). Prop. 7 gives an exponential separation from SDA (p. 8).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: an IP/polynomial-optimisation lifting (RLT-like monomials) is imported into the Lagrangian-decomposition setting. Sparsity is handled through the tree decomposition of the intersection graph (pp. 5, 9), matching Taste 5 (real instances are sparse).
- Method 2 ⚠ weak/variant: a small-case search for worst instances supports the open-tightness remark (n = 2, k = 1, p. 12). Otherwise there is no ceiling and no stripped instrument, and the authors note they did not compare with the strongest specialised methods (p. 13). This runs against Heuristic 2, but is labelled openly as a caution.
- New-pattern candidate "primal-redundant, dual-separable constraints": strengthen a relaxation by adding constraints that change only the dual side, and first prove which classes cannot help (pp. 3–4). Shares the monomial-lifting idea with Cifuentes–Dey–Xu [13] (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate "recycle own proof machinery": the packing/covering bound technique of Dey–Molinaro–Wang sparse cuts [14] is reused in a new setting (p. 6; pp. 22–29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove that a class of reformulations is useless before searching inside it (affine F gives DUAL = DUAL_ex, p. 4, proof p. 17–18).
- Show a cycle counterexample with three blocks and one shared variable per pair to motivate a tree-decomposition hypothesis (p. 9).
- Run a vertex-indicator lifted dual in the original space: split the subproblem into "x ∉ V" via no-good cuts plus enumeration over the few vertices v with μ_v ≠ 0. Cache y′(v), since it does not depend on (λ, μ) (Algorithm 1, pp. 7–8).
- Compare a new dual method with an existing exact algorithm in two ways: an upper bound on iterations relative to it (Prop. 6), and an instance where it is exponentially worse (Prop. 7) (p. 8; pp. 33–37).
- In a time-limited dual-bound comparison, warm-start the stronger dual from the classical dual's multipliers padded with zeros, and give every method the same wall clock (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "However, DP suffers from the disadvantage that we do not obtain any information on bounds until the algorithm terminates." (p. 2)
- "An open question is whether one can achieve Lagrangian duals with decomposability and zero duality gap while solving an "easier" subproblem in each iteration." (p. 13)
