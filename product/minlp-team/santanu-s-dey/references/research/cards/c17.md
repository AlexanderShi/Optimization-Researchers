### S129 · Convexification of a Separable Function over a Polyhedral Ground Set (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2510.16595)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-26: all sections, proofs, the computational section with all figure captions, the conclusion and the references). **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Burak Kocuk (Sabancı University, p. 1; earlier Dey's student and coauthor on the OPF papers, per the SKILL's AC-OPF anatomy, not this text) · **Place in trajectory**: part of the 2019-2026 line on QCQP and MINLP substructures (Method 3), now for power functions y_j = x_j^κ. It ties into his own gas and water network work ([20], [21], p. 25) and into the RPT/SOC line with Khajavirad ([11], p. 25).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The set is S^κ = {(x,y) ∈ G × R^n : y_j = x_j^κ}, with κ > 1 and G a polytope in [0,1]^n (p. 1). Two applications motivate it: separable standard quadratic programming (κ = 2, G = Δn, eq. (1), pp. 1-2) and a node-based substructure of potential-based gas and water flow networks, where p_j − p_0 = χ_j f_j^κ (Figure 1, p. 2). The stated gap: function convexification is an active area, but "the specific substructure we are interested in is less explored" (p. 2). The closest prior results cover only κ = 2. Burer's completely positive representation of the hull is intractable, Bomze-Locatelli give a polynomial-time algorithm without a hull description, and Gökmen-Yıldırım show the DNN relaxation is exact for sign-constant β (p. 2). Goal: conv(S^κ), or a tight outer approximation, in the original or an extended space (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open by showing that one nonconvex set covers two unrelated applications (StQP and flow physics), each reduced to the set by a change of variables (p. 2). The set then deserves study for its own sake, not as one model's detail.
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Build a ladder of conic relaxations and place each rung by theorem and by experiment. The ladder: the single-row power-cone relaxation P (x_j^κ ≤ y_j ≤ x_j, p. 4), then the lifted X = xx^T with first-level RLT (2), SDP (3) or 2×2-minor SOC (4) constraints, linked to y by the observation X_jj = y_j^{2/κ} (eq. (5), p. 4). This gives P, PR, PS, Ps, PRS and PRs (pp. 4-5), plus RPT-derived power-cone inequalities (13) (p. 18), for nine relaxations in the experiments (p. 19). Main structural findings: SDP alone adds nothing on top of P (Prop. 2, Cor. 1, p. 5), while RLT plus SDP is strictly stronger than RLT (Props. 5-7, p. 7). For κ = 2 and G = Δn, PR is the convex hull at n = 2 (Thm 1, p. 8) and PRS at n = 3 (Cor. 2, p. 8). The weakest relaxation P is exact with probability → 1 as n → ∞ for uniform objectives (Thm 4, p. 17). Unchanged from standard practice: the generic RLT and SDP machinery; new: the y-X link (5) and the RPT step through the power cone (p. 18).
  - Transferable: when a lifted matrix variable X ≈ xx^T is added to a relaxation of y = x^κ, connect y to diag(X) by the exact identity X_jj = y_j^{2/κ} written as a convex inequality pair per case (κ < 2, = 2, > 2). Without that link the lifted constraints do not reach y (eq. (5), p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: κ > 1 and G ⊆ [0,1]^n nonempty polytope (p. 1). All theory beyond §2 is for G = Δn (§3, p. 5). The exactness results need κ = 2 (§3.2, pp. 8-10). The probabilistic theorem assumes (α,β) iid Unif(−1,1) (Prop. 11, Thm 4, pp. 15-17); the standard normal case is only simulated (Table 2, p. 15). The conclusion claims that most results extend to any convex function of x_j that is non-decreasing on the ground set, without proof (p. 24). The RPT set is stated for Δn, with an extension remark for nonnegative A, b (Remark 3, p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) NP-hardness by a SUBSET-SUM reduction: min Σ(x_j − y_j) over S^κ is zero iff the instance is feasible (Prop. 1, p. 3). (ii) SDP adds nothing: Lemma 1 says extreme points of S^κ_P have y_j ∈ {x_j, x_j^κ}. At each extreme point, complete X = x̂x̂^T + D with a diagonal D chosen per case, which is doubly nonnegative (Prop. 2, p. 5). (iii) Strict containments from explicit small points in Δ2 and Δ3, with determinants and minors computed by hand (Props. 5-7, p. 7). (iv) Hull at n = 2 via an explicit convex-combination decomposition with λ = x̂_1²/ŷ_1 (Thm 1, p. 8). Hull at n = 3 from DNN = CP for matrices up to 4×4 (Maxfield-Minc, Cor. 2, pp. 8-9). (v) DNN exactness for three sign patterns of β (Thm 3, pp. 9-10). The proof argues on an optimal solution: a diagonally dominant 2×2 shift that keeps the row sums (Claim 2), a Schur complement giving |J_=^-| ≤ 1, and an explicit completely positive decomposition. The authors note that [15] got similar results "utilizing conic duality whereas our approach uses only the primal problem" (p. 10). (vi) Matching distance bounds: upper bound 1 − n^{1−κ} by plugging in a feasible point (Prop. 8, p. 11); the same lower bound even for conv(S^κ) via a Lagrangian relaxation and a one-variable calculus lemma (Lemma 3, Prop. 9, pp. 11-12). (vii) Objective-based exactness cases with the closed-form inexact value (Prop. 10, pp. 13-14). Exact probability of Case 3a by conditioning on |J+| = m, order statistics of uniforms and an integration-by-parts recursion for I_m (Prop. 11, pp. 15-16). Limit via binomial-identity bounds (7/8)^n and (3/8)^n (Thm 4, p. 17). (viii) RPT derivation: multiply Σx_j = 1 by x_i^κ, then rewrite each term as a power-cone perspective (Prop. 13, pp. 18-19).
  - Transferable: to show that a heavier conic layer adds nothing to a separable relaxation, first characterise the relaxation's extreme points, then complete each extreme point to a feasible matrix as "rank one plus a nonnegative diagonal" (Lemma 1 + Prop. 2, p. 5).
  - Transferable: to judge whether a weak relaxation is "reasonable", prove a worst-case distance upper bound for it and the same lower bound for the true convex hull. The gap is then intrinsic to the set, not caused by the relaxation (Props. 8-9, pp. 11-12).
  - Transferable: first estimate by Monte Carlo the probability that the closed-form exactness conditions hold (10^6 samples, Tables 1-2, pp. 14-15). When one case dominates and seems to tend to 1, compute it exactly and prove the limit (Prop. 11, Table 3, Thm 4, pp. 15-17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Python on two Xeon Gold 6248R CPUs with 256 GB. The nonconvex "NON" model is solved by BARON 25.7.29 via Pyomo 6.4.0; relaxations by MOSEK 10.0.40 via CVXPY 1.5.2, defaults (p. 19). G = Δn only. Design: 2 objective distributions × 6 values of κ × 9 values of n (2..10) = 108 settings × 1,000 random objectives, each solved by NON + 9 relaxations. Four instances with MOSEK UNKNOWN status are excluded (p. 20). Metrics: cumulative absolute gap to the BARON value, cumulative time, and number of exact instances (gap ≤ 10^-4) (p. 20). The results are plotted as gap against time, broken down by distribution, κ and n (Figs. 3-6, pp. 21-23). Findings: P is exact on 888 of 1,000 on average with gap 3.607 in 34.429 s. RLT has "the largest marginal effect" (924 exact, gap 1.160, about 4 s more). PRS reaches 941 exact with gap 0.672. RPT versions reach 959-960 at about 121-129 s (p. 20). PRS was exact at κ = 2 in every run, which becomes Conjecture 1 (pp. 10, 22). PRs and PRs3 are dominated by the cheaper PRS and dropped (p. 21). At n = 10 the RPT relaxations cost more than solving NON with BARON, hence "not advisable" in higher dimensions (p. 23). The P relaxation's gap peaks at n = 3 and then falls, as Theorem 4 predicts (pp. 22-23). Unstated (my reading): BARON's optimality tolerance is not given, and ground truth exists only for n ≤ 10.
  - Transferable: when testing a relaxation hierarchy, solve the exact nonconvex model with a global solver on small n as ground truth. Report three numbers per setting: cumulative gap, cumulative time and count exact. Plot gap against time so that dominated relaxations are visible at a glance and can be dropped with a stated reason (pp. 20-21).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper extends the κ = 2 literature: Burer [10], Bomze-Locatelli [7], Gökmen-Yıldırım [15] (p. 2). It credits [7]'s algorithm as adaptable to any κ > 1 (p. 3) and sets its own primal-only proof of Thm 3 against [15]'s conic duality proof (p. 10). Tools come from Sherali-Adams RLT [29] and from the RPT of Zhen-de Moor-den Hertog [35] and Bertsimas et al. [5], plus Dey-Khajavirad [11] (p. 18). Application refs are largely in-group (Li-Dey-Sahinidis [20], [21]; Okumusoglu-Kocuk [25]; Dey-Kocuk-Redder [12]) (pp. 2, 25). The tone toward prior work is non-critical: earlier results are described, not faulted.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A contributions list by section comes right after the introduction (p. 3). "Interestingly" marks each surprise (pp. 3, 5, 15, 20, 22, 24). Conjectures are explicitly derived from computation: "Motivated by the insights derived from Theorem 3 and our extensive computational experiments" (Conj. 1, p. 10; Conj. 2, p. 21). The conclusion ranks the relaxations in plain words, e.g. "the RLT relaxation is very important", and says when to use which (pp. 21, 23-24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions are theoretical (hardness, hull results, bounds, probabilistic exactness) plus a computational comparison. Limits are stated concretely as open problems: Conjectures 1 and 2, multiple exponents, κ < 1, other ground sets (p. 24). Unstated limits (my reading): all theory and experiments beyond NP-hardness use only G = Δn, although the title and §2 promise general polyhedral ground sets. The gas and water network motivation (general G, flow-direction disjunctions, p. 2) is never tested. The extension to general convex nondecreasing functions is asserted without proof (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: convexify a small recurring nonconvex set (one power equality per variable over a polytope) jointly rather than term by term. The set is chosen because it appears in applications (StQP, gas and water networks, pp. 1-2). Hull results come in low dimension with an extended formulation stated (Thm 1, Cor. 2, p. 8). The cheap single-row relaxation is tested against the hull (Props. 8-10, Thm 4, pp. 11-17).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: NP-hardness for general G (Prop. 1, p. 3); strictness of every containment by explicit points (Props. 5-7, p. 7); matching upper and lower distance bounds (Props. 8-9, pp. 11-12); the inexact case of P given in closed form (p. 14); open conjectures stated rather than claimed (pp. 10, 21).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: tractable before beautiful. The power-cone/SOC relaxation P is shown to be the right first object (exact w.h.p., SDP adds nothing on it), and SDP or RPT is recommended only under a stated time budget (pp. 5, 17, 21, 23).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: ground truth from a global solver on small n (BARON on n ≤ 10, 108,000 instances), with counts of exact instances against that optimum (pp. 19-20). Workflow B step 4 also applies: experiments → Conjectures 1-2 (pp. 10, 21).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the random-instance model (Method 1 step 5) explains why a weak relaxation works on average (Thm 4, p. 17), but the object explained is the authors' own relaxation, not a solver habit.
- New-pattern candidate: "rule out the heavier relaxation by an equivalence theorem" (Prop. 2 and Cor. 1, p. 5; this is the second instance after S105 Prop. 1 in c16).
- New-pattern candidate: "simulate the exactness condition, then prove the dominant case analytically" (Tables 1-3 → Prop. 11 → Thm 4, pp. 14-17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Extreme-point characterisation + diagonal completion to prove SDP = LP/SOC on a separable relaxation (p. 5).
- A matched upper bound for the relaxation and lower bound for the convex hull show the relaxation is not the bottleneck (pp. 11-12).
- Monte Carlo over objective vectors to estimate how often closed-form exactness conditions hold, then an exact formula for the dominant case (pp. 14-17).
- A gap-against-time scatter with the exact-instance count printed next to each point, used to prune dominated relaxations (Fig. 3, p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "Interestingly, adding the SDP constraint (3) to the P relaxation does not further strengthen this relaxation as proven below." (p. 5)
- "Interestingly, this weakest relaxation we consider is tight with high probability when the dimension n is sufficiently large, as proven in Theorem 4." (p. 3)

### S130 · Improving Full Strong Branching Decisions by Incorporating Additional Information (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2507.09455)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-39: main text, all tables, Appendices A-C read; Appendix D instance generators, pp. 34-39, read; figure images not in the text layer, only their captions). **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Prachi Shah (Amazon, p. 1; coauthor of the FSB paper [14] and first author of the non-monotonicity paper [40], pp. 28, 30; her student status is not stated in this text) · **Place in trajectory**: third paper of the full-strong-branching line (SKILL "Full strong branching and non-monotonicity (2024-2026)"). [14] measured FSB against optimal trees; this paper tries to close the gap with a better score. It is the "rule proposal" preprint that the SKILL's Method 1 limitation names.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a measured gap: FSB gives the smallest trees of all heuristics, yet the authors' own study [14] found FSB trees "25-75% larger than optimal trees on average" (p. 2). The goal is to "bridge this difference in tree size from optimal trees" (p. 2). Two reasons are given for counting nodes rather than time. FSB is never used in textbook form, but ML methods imitate it and need a better expert, since optimal trees can only be computed for tiny instances. And the authors want "the science behind making good branching decisions" (p. 2). Two shortcomings are diagnosed: LP gains overestimate global bound improvement when the child can be pruned, and FSB is myopic about infeasibility or integrality beyond the children (p. 1, p. 3).
  - Transferable: if a heuristic is known to be near the best, measure its gap to a true optimum first (here, a prior paper). Then frame the next paper as diagnosing why the remaining gap exists, one named shortcoming per section (pp. 2-3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Three successive modifications of the product score max{Δ0,ε}·max{Δ1,ε} (eq. (1), p. 2). (1) Efficacious gains q = min{max{Δ,ε}, Δ_p-d}, capping each LP gain at the current primal-dual gap; infeasible children get Δ_p-d (p. 5). Alone this is neutral on average (−1.0% to +0.3%) and hurts the infeasibility-heavy classes (Tables 1-2, p. 6). (2) The product is reweighted to q_min^0.3·q_max^0.7 after a grid search (Eff-SB (3,7), eq. (4), p. 8). The same exponents on raw LP gains make trees 3.8% larger, so the cap and the reweighting only work together (pp. 8, 10-11). (3) Asymmetry exponents a0, a1 (eq. (6), p. 13) are set by the "last assignment rule". It counts whether the last branching before each infeasible or integral leaf fixed a variable to 0 or 1, and sets the exponent from the normalised difference (Algorithm 1, p. 14). It is generalised to all leaves ("rebalancing", §4, p. 18) and switched by a 5% threshold on the fraction of integral or infeasible leaves (PA-LA-SB, p. 20). The variable-level ranking of FSB is kept; the global trend is applied to all candidates (pp. 13-14). The same caps are carried over to reliability branching through pseudocost estimates (§5.2, p. 25).
  - Transferable: when a score combines two quantities, first cap each quantity at the largest value that can still matter (here the primal-dual gap), then re-tune the combining exponents. Check separately that the re-tuning alone does not help, so the interaction is the finding (Tables 3-4, pp. 10-11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Mixed-binary LPs with variable disjunctions (p. 1). Best-bound node order, no presolve, no cuts (p. 3). FSB solves LPs to optimality on all fractional variables; ties among infeasible children follow a fixed rule (p. 4). The primal bound is set at 0/2/5/10% primal gap to simulate solver heuristics and is updated when better solutions are found (p. 5). The last-assignment rule is motivated for pure packing or covering structure (p. 13) and assumes "variables are similar" so that one global trend fits all candidates (p. 27). Depth-first order could mislead the rule (Remark 3, p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No theorems. Analytical devices: (i) two-variable toy examples that show a failure mode, then show the fix (Examples 1-4, pp. 4, 5-7, 31). Example 4 shows that the pruning-focused alternative has discontinuous scores in the primal bound (p. 31). (ii) A minimal-infeasible-subsystem argument justifies looking only at the last branch: it must be in every MIS, and for pure packing or covering every branching constraint in an MIS fixes the same value (p. 13). Appendix B gives a knapsack counterexample to counting all branchings on the path (pp. 31-33). (iii) A combinatorial subtree-size estimator for cardinality constraints, T(n,k) = T(n−1,k) + T(n−1,k−1) + 1 ≈ C(n,k), gives the branch weights f0 = (n−k)/n and f1 = k/n (App. C, pp. 33-34).
  - Transferable: to defend a summary statistic of a search tree, ask which constraint is necessarily in every certificate of the event (the last branch for an infeasible leaf). Record only that constraint, so the statistic is not polluted by incidental path history (p. 13, App. B).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Instrument: a Python 3.10 textbook B&B with Gurobi 11.0 as LP solver. FSB is "implemented purely as an oracle ... thus allowing fair node counting" (pp. 3-4). Development test bed: 20 random instances × 14 problem classes (knapsack variants, packing and covering, lot-sizing, facility location, network flow, chance-constrained portfolio, weighted coverage), sized so that trees are "neither too large nor too small" (p. 4; generators in App. D, pp. 34-39). Metric: shifted geometric mean of nodes, shift 100 (p. 4). Every claim is reported at 4 primal gaps; per-class fractions of infeasible or integral leaves are reported to explain the wins and losses (Tables 1-12, pp. 6-22). Parameters are grid-searched on that same test bed: a_min ∈ {0,...,1} (p. 7), η ∈ {0.05,...,0.25} with k_I = 10, updated every 50 nodes (p. 14). The random-instance gains (13.3% → 7.4% for Eff-SB (3,7); 14.6% → 10.6% for PA-LA-SB) are therefore in-sample (Tables 4, 12, pp. 10, 22). The best per-class parameters would give 18.8% (Table 6, p. 12). Held-out test: MIPLIB 2017 Benchmark, filtered to ≤ 2,000 binaries and no general integers after Gurobi presolve, plus SCIP root cuts (5 rounds), leaving 51 instances. 3 seeds, node limit 20,000, remaining gap relative to the root gap with a 1% shift. Five no-progress instances and four that did not finish within 14 days are excluded (pp. 23-24). Results: on the 10 solved instances, 35.4%-20.5% (Eff-SB) and 35.3%-22.8% (PA-LA-SB) fewer nodes; on the 32 unsolved, 3.1-6.0% less remaining gap (p. 23). Per-instance losses are visible in Table 13 but not discussed (e.g. dano3_5 134 → 244, sp150x300d 3.6% → 9.2%, p. 24). Reliability-branching extension: Eff-RB cuts nodes by 13.8% with the optimal bound but adds nodes at 2-10% gap; PA-LA-RB is better at every gap (p. 25). No running times are reported anywhere.
  - Transferable: give every branching experiment a known incumbent at fixed primal gaps (0/2/5/10%). Robustness to incumbent quality is then a measured axis instead of a confound (p. 5).
  - Transferable: design and tune rules on structured random families whose behaviour you can explain (fraction of infeasible leaves per class), then evaluate once on a filtered public benchmark with seeds and a node limit (pp. 4, 23).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds directly on own work [14] (FSB vs optimal trees) and [40] (best-bound order gives the smallest trees) (pp. 2-3). The score lineage is Applegate et al. [5] and Achterberg's product score [1], [3] (p. 2). Alternatives are surveyed without criticism: pseudocost and reliability (larger trees), lookahead (costly), SAT-style conflict rules, hybrid branching [2], information-based branching [26], backdoors [15], [25] (pp. 2-3). The difference is stated: "we use this information to infer global asymmetry trends ... and do not use it to make distinctions amongst variables" (p. 14). Priority is claimed with a hedge: solvers "might be implicitly using efficacious gains", but this is "the first systematic study" (Remark 1, p. 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Each table is followed by numbered "main insights"/"key observations" (pp. 5, 7-8, 11, 14-15, 18-20, 23). Every modification is introduced by a worked toy example before its formula (Examples 1-3, pp. 4-7). Systematic rule names carry their parameters (Eff-SB (3,7), LA-SB (3,7,0.15), PA-LA-SB (3,7,0.15)). Headline numbers do not agree across the paper: the abstract says 22-35% fewer nodes and 3.6-5.6% less gap on MIPLIB (p. 1), the contribution list says 15-26% and 4-6% (p. 3), §5.1 says 20.5-35.4% and 3.1-6.0% (p. 23). For RB the abstract says 5-13% fewer nodes (p. 1), but §5.2 reports node increases for Eff-RB at nonzero gaps and gives the PA-LA-RB percentages "relative to Def-SB" (p. 25). The conclusion calls the gain "a remarkable improvement" (p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and empirical. Limits are stated concretely (p. 27): best parameters depend on problem structure ((0.1,0.9) for independent set and matching, (0.4,0.6) for auctions and set covering); the RB gains are smaller because pseudocost gains are inaccurate; the last-assignment rule "may not be the most effective" detector of asymmetry; and the rule assumes similar variables. Future directions: exponents that depend on the primal gap, moving from (0.5,0.5) to (0.3,0.7); other asymmetry metrics; variable-specific and structure-specific scores (App. C) (p. 27). Unstated (my reading): there is no time-based evaluation, which the purpose (an expert for imitation) excuses but which leaves solver use open. Random-test-bed gains are in-sample. Dead ends are written into the paper: Def-SB (3,7) (p. 8) and the pruning-focused rule (App. A, pp. 31-32).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the object is a solver habit (FSB with the product score). The question is cut down to a proxy measure (tree size, not time, with reasons, p. 2), and the paper closes step 8: a concrete rule (Eff-SB (3,7), PA-LA-SB) derived from the diagnosis (pp. 8, 20). The theory here is diagnostic examples, not theorems (pp. 4-7, 13).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: a stripped textbook B&B, FSB as a non-interfering oracle for fair node counting, best-bound order, presolve and cuts off (pp. 3-4). Instance ladder: structured random families → filtered MIPLIB (pp. 4, 23). ⚠ variant: no optimal-tree ceiling in this paper; the ceiling is borrowed from [14] (p. 2).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (weak): failure modes are shown by examples (Example 2, p. 5) and per-class losses are tabulated (Tables 2, 8, pp. 6, 17). There is no tightness claim, and per-instance MIPLIB losses go unremarked (p. 24).
- Heuristic 3 (write dead ends in) evidence: Def-SB (3,7) is worse than default (p. 8); the pruning-focused rule is not robust to suboptimal bounds (App. A, p. 31). Heuristic 6 tension: time overhead deliberately not measured, justified by the ML-expert purpose (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: "control the incumbent: report every branching result at fixed primal gaps" (pp. 5, 23, 25).
- New-pattern candidate: "toy example per failure mode before each formula change" (Examples 1-4, pp. 4-7, 31).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Cap LP gains at the primal-dual gap ("efficacious gains") before scoring, then re-tune the product exponents (pp. 5, 8).
- Last-assignment statistic: count only the final branching before each infeasible or integral leaf to detect 0/1 asymmetry (p. 13, Algorithm 1 p. 14).
- Threshold switch between two rules on a measured tree statistic (5% integral or infeasible leaves), chosen from where each rule wins (p. 20).
- Subtree-size recursion T(n,k) for cardinality constraints to set branch weights in closed form (App. C, pp. 33-34).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, one of the motivations for this paper is to discover simple rules that can improve over FSB, which can then be used as expert for machine learning algorithms to imitate." (p. 2)
- "Our results indicate that asymmetry plays a critical role in many problems, yet the last assignment rule may not be the most effective approach for detecting and addressing it." (p. 27)

### S133 · Hyperconnected Facility Location Contracting (IISE Annual Conference & Expo 2025, doi:10.21872/annual2025_6556)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-10, whole paper including references; the model's equations on pp. 6-7 were extracted with garbled math glyphs and were read from the defining prose; Figures 1-3 are images, so only their captions were read). **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jiachen Shi (corresponding author, Georgia Tech, p. 1), Benoit Montreuil (Physical Internet Center) · **Place in trajectory**: outside Dey's MINLP and cutting-plane core. It is a student-led application paper in Montreuil's Physical Internet programme, with Dey as the optimization coauthor (affiliations, p. 1). The paper does not say what Dey contributed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Application-driven. In "hyperconnected fulfillment", retailers rent capacity in open-access fulfillment centres through contracts that differ in length, capacity, timing and how far in advance they are signed. Flexe and ES3 are named as industry examples (pp. 1-2, 4). Gap: Physical Internet work studies how to use open networks "instead of how to construct it" (p. 3). Dynamic facility-location models fix the number of facilities and cannot price per-use contracts (p. 3). The problem: choose contracts (start, end, capacity module) for each open facility over a multi-period horizon, and route multi-product demand within a coverage radius (pp. 4-5).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: A single MIP. Binary y for each contract (facility, start period, end period, module), binary z for each active facility-period-module, continuous x for the share of demand served (p. 6). Constraints: contracts end after they start (2); at most one active contract per facility per period (3); linking z and y (4)-(5); demand caps (6); module capacity (7) (pp. 6-7). ### D005 · Random projection with linear equalities and inequalities (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2610.09324)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-17: all sections, all proofs, Appendix A with Lemma 2.7 and Proposition A.1, references). **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jingye Xu (no affiliation printed on p. 1; relation to Dey not stated in this text) · **Place in trajectory**: newest theory work (submitted 7 Oct 2026, p. 1). It continues Dey's interest in cheap relaxations that keep dual bounds (Method 5) and is the case the SKILL's Heuristic 9 cites ("generic chaining (arXiv:2610.09324)").

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Random projection replaces m equality rows Ax = b by k ≪ m Gaussian combinations GAx = Gb; the result is a relaxation, so RPOPT ≤ OPT (pp. 1-2). The known guarantees (Vu-Poirion-Liberti [13] for LP over a scaled simplex; extensions to QP [2] and conic programs [5]) rest on the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma and "typically" assume a simplex-contained feasible region (p. 2). The entry is twofold. First, explain the phenomenon through suprema of random processes, a link "often left implicit" (p. 2). Second, reach the case where the JL framework seems to fail: inequality constraints, where projection with signed G is "neither necessarily a relaxation nor a restriction" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a technique's guarantees all go through one lemma (JL), ask which property of the lemma the proofs actually use. If it is weaker (unbiasedness), the technique may extend to settings the lemma does not cover (pp. 3-4, 8).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Through Lagrangian duality, the projection error is bounded by the supremum of a random process indexed by AS − b: ε(k) ≤ sup_x ⟨λ*, Ax − b⟩ − ⟨Gλ*, G(Ax − b)⟩ (p. 8). Generic chaining then controls it with γ2(AS) instead of covering numbers (pp. 2, 8). For inequalities, Gλ* is not dual feasible, so the authors project the dual certificate as 2G+λ* (entrywise positive part). This keeps λ ≥ 0 and still satisfies E[2(G+)^T G] = I, which preserves the bilinear form in expectation (p. 8). The price is weaker concentration: a product of sub-Gaussians, i.e. mixed tails, which adds a γ1 term (p. 8). Unchanged: the primal projection GAx ≤ Gb with Gaussian G. Changed: only the dual certificate used in the analysis.
  - Transferable: to prove a one-sided bound for a perturbed problem, do not try to keep primal feasibility. Build a feasible dual certificate for the perturbed problem whose expectation reproduces the original certificate's bilinear form, then bound the deviation uniformly over the feasible set (pp. 8, 12-13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f convex, S closed convex (p. 1); strong duality with an optimal multiplier λ* (λ* ≥ 0 for inequalities) (p. 8). G has iid N(0,1/k) entries (p. 1). The bounds scale with ‖λ*‖ (and ‖λ*‖1 for inequalities) and with rad(AS) and ‖b‖ (Thms 3.2, 3.4, pp. 10, 12). Universal constants are unspecified (p. 4). The biased variant (§3.3) additionally assumes 0 ∈ S and f(0) = 0, "essentially without loss of generality" (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Lemma 3.1, equality case: decompose each Gaussian row along q = λ/‖λ‖ and its orthogonal complement (independent parts). Bernstein's inequality handles the χ² term a; conditional on γ, the cross term ℓ is Gaussian with variance ‖γ‖²/k² and is bounded via Lemma 2.7 (Gaussian comparison + concentration, App. A) (pp. 8-10, 15). Theorem 3.2 then uses weak duality with certificate Gλ* and translation invariance of γ2 (pp. 10-11). (ii) Lemma 3.3, inequality case: Z(t) = Σ X_i(t) is centered because E[(g_i)_j (g_i)_j^+] = 1/(2k). Increments are sub-exponential via the product rule ‖XY‖ψ1 ≲ ‖X‖ψ2‖Y‖ψ2 (Lemma 2.2), with the mean of ⟨g+, λ⟩ bounded by ‖λ‖1/√k, so the process has mixed tails (Lemma 2.5). Dirksen's mixed-tail generic chaining (Lemma 2.6) and a separate Bernstein bound for the base point finish the proof (pp. 11-12). (iii) Theorem 3.4: 2G+λ* is dual feasible because G+ ≥ 0; the b-term is handled by applying Lemma 3.3 to the singleton {b} (pp. 12-13). (iv) Remark 3.5: for linear f, the infimum restricts to vertices, which can shrink γ1 (p. 13; Table 1, p. 4). (v) Impossibility example: S a singleton with slack r ≠ 0 gives P(Gr ≥ 0) = 2^-k, so the projected problem is infeasible w.p. 1 − 2^-k (p. 14). (vi) Prop. A.1, γ1(Δn) ≍ √n: upper bound via Schütt's entropy numbers of B1^n summed over three regimes; lower bound via a Varshamov-Gilbert packing at scale 1/√n (pp. 15-16).
  - Transferable: to bound sup_t ⟨(M − I)λ, t⟩ for a random matrix M, split each Gaussian row into its component along λ and the orthogonal rest. The two parts are independent, so you get one scalar χ² term plus a conditionally Gaussian process that a comparison lemma handles (Lemma 3.1, pp. 8-10).
  - Transferable: when a sign constraint breaks an unbiased construction, replace the random matrix by its positive part scaled by 2. By sign symmetry the expectation identity survives, and the extra non-centred part costs only an ℓ1 norm (Lemma 3.3, pp. 11-12).
  - Transferable: show that a desired two-sided guarantee is impossible with a one-point feasible set before weakening the claim (p. 14).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a (no computations; the paper is purely theoretical).
- **D6 Positioning**: The authors say they generalise Vu et al. [13]: their bound "matches" [13] on the simplex and is strictly sharper on the ℓ2-ball ∩ orthant, O(‖λ*‖√(n/k)) vs O(‖λ*‖√(n log n/k)) (p. 3). [1] (statistical dimension) and [5] (JL) are reread as implicitly chaining-based (p. 2). Random embedding is linked as the dual-side twin of projection (p. 2).
