### S092 · A second-order cone representable class of nonconvex quadratic programs (Mathematical Programming 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10107-026-02364-y; arXiv:2508.18435)
**Read level**: full. arXiv v2 pp. 1–27 read in full, including all proofs; references pp. 28–29 read for positioning.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Aida Khajavirad (Lehigh) · **Position in trajectory**: continues the QCQP-convexification line (it cites Dey's own "convex hull of a quadratic constraint over a polytope", ref [24], p. 29, p. 27) and moves it toward Khajavirad's multilinear-polytope decomposability theory (refs [10–13], p. 28). It is the first Dey result on a polynomial-size *exact* SOC description of a nonconvex QP class (abstract p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Box-constrained nonconvex QP min xᵀQx + cᵀx over [0,1]ⁿ (p. 1). The entry is an analogy: for binary QP, Padberg's Boolean quadric polytope BQP(G) of a sparse graph has a polynomial-size LP extended formulation exactly when the treewidth is bounded (Props. 1–2; [9, 1, 19, 18, 6]; p. 4). The continuous counterpart QPₙ is barely understood: SDP+McCormick is exact for n = 2 but not n = 3, and "To date, obtaining an explicit characterization of QP3 remains an open question" (p. 2). The question is a sufficient condition, in terms of the sparsity graph G = (V, E, L) with plus and minus loops (sign of qᵢᵢ), under which QP(G) has a polynomial-size SOC-representable formulation (pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable technique: take a mature polyhedral theory for a discrete set (here, BQP of sparse graphs plus treewidth) and ask for the same theorem for its continuous relaxation, using the next-cheapest cone (SOC) in place of LP (pp. 3–5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: An extension of RLT to continuous quadratics (§3, p. 10). For a plus loop {i,i} and M ⊆ N(i) with i ∈ M, multiply zᵢᵢ ≥ zᵢ² by the partition of unity Σ_K Π_{j∈K} zⱼ Π_{j∈M′\K}(1−zⱼ) = 1 and write each term as a perspective (ℓ_d(J, M\J))² / ℓ_{d−1}(J\{i}, M\J) of linearized RLT products. This gives inequality (12) together with the RLT inequalities (13) (Prop. 5, p. 11). The system is rotated-SOC representable (Observation 3, p. 13) with Θ(2^|M|) variables, so |M| ∈ O(log|V|) is needed for polynomial size (p. 13). Larger M dominates smaller M (Prop. 6, p. 12), which gives an RLT-like hierarchy indexed by r = |M| (p. 13). What changed relative to Sherali–Tuncbilek RLT and the Reformulation-Perspectification Technique: those give relaxations, but "it remains unclear whether and under what conditions the resulting relaxations constitute extended formulations for QP(G)" (p. 10). This paper proves exactness: one plus loop on a complete hypergraph gives the convex hull (Thm. 1, p. 17). If the plus-loop nodes V⁺ form a stable set, QP(G) is SOC-representable (Thm. 2 / Cor. 4, pp. 20–22). With a tree decomposition satisfying (C1) at most one plus-loop node per bag, (C2) width O(log|V|) and (C3) spread of each plus-loop node O(log|V|), the formulation has polynomial size and can be built in polynomial time (Thm. 3 / Cor. 5, p. 23). Continuous analogues of Padberg's acyclic and chordless-cycle results follow (Props. 8–9, pp. 25–26), plus a class where the plus-loop nodes lie in a large stable set, i.e. a small vertex cover (Prop. 10, p. 26).
  - Transferable technique: to get a convex inequality from a univariate convex constraint zᵢᵢ ≥ h(zᵢ) in a multilinear setting, multiply it by an RLT partition-of-unity identity and rewrite each product term as the closed perspective of h composed with linearized RLT factors (Prop. 5 p. 11; generalized to any convex hᵢ in §6, pp. 26–27).
  - Transferable technique: prove dominance between members of a hierarchy by the subadditivity of the perspective (convex + positively homogeneous), after splitting each factor as ℓ_d = ℓ_{d+1}(·∪{k}) + ℓ_{d+1}(·, ·∪{k}) (Prop. 6, p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Q is not PSD (p. 1); box [0,1]ⁿ only (p. 1). The graph has a loop for each nonzero qᵢᵢ, split into plus loops (qᵢᵢ > 0, giving zᵢᵢ ≥ zᵢ²) and minus loops (zᵢᵢ ≤ zᵢ²) (p. 4). Minus loops are harmless: they add only zᵢᵢ ≤ zᵢ, 0 ≤ zᵢ ≤ 1 (Lemma 5, p. 8), so all difficulty sits in the plus loops. The key structural hypotheses are that V⁺ is a stable set (Thm. 2) and (C1)–(C3) (Thm. 3). The authors say the single-plus-loop hypothesis of Thm. 1 is necessary for the method (Observation 6, p. 19). Whether the stable-set hypothesis is necessary is left open (p. 27). The size result needs a tree decomposition to be given; whether (C1)–(C3) can be checked in polynomial time is open (p. 25).
  - Transferable technique: isolate the hypothesis that the construction needs (one plus loop per bag), show by a known result why it cannot simply be dropped (Burer–Anstreicher n = 2, Observation 6 p. 19), and state the rest as open (pp. 25, 27).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The proof is a chain of small structural lemmas followed by inductions.
  - Closedness of PP(G) via a Minkowski split S = S₀ ⊕ S∞ (compact + recession cone) (Lemma 2, p. 7).
  - Extreme points (Lemma 3, pp. 7–8): Straszewicz (extreme = closure of exposed) plus an explicit linear function with unique minimizer at each candidate point, where Σ(zᵢᵢ − 2z̄ᵢzᵢ) reduces to Σ(zᵢ − z̄ᵢ)².
  - Adding hyperedges gives an extended formulation, since projection and conv commute (Lemma 4, p. 8).
  - Decomposability when G₁ ∩ G₂ is a complete hypergraph without plus loops (Cor. 1, p. 9). This builds on Del Pia–Khajavirad: the multilinear polytope of a complete hypergraph is a simplex, so the shared variables have a unique convex combination (Lemma 1, p. 6; eq. (6), p. 8).
  - Strictness against SDP+MC+Tri (Prop. 7, pp. 13–14): an explicit point z̃ (15) satisfies McCormick, triangle and PSD constraints (PSD certified by an explicit LDLᵀ factorization) but violates (12) (3/16 vs 1/4). Corollary: even one square term makes SDP+MC+Tri inexact for n = 3 (Cor. 2, pp. 14–15). The same point satisfies the Anstreicher–Puges extended triangle inequalities (Observation 4, p. 15). Their SOC relaxation is implied by |M| = 3 via a switching argument and the identity R₂ − R₁ = 1 − 2z₁ (Observation 5, pp. 15–16).
  - Theorem 1 (pp. 17–19): induction on n. Write PP(G) = conv(face zₖ = 0 ∪ face zₖ = 1) (22), apply Balas's disjunctive programming (23)–(25), then project out λ and the copies using homogeneity of ℓ_n and the variable substitutions of Lemma 6 (pp. 16–17).
  - Theorem 2 (pp. 20–22, Fig. 1 p. 21): induction on |V⁺|. Complete the neighbourhood of one plus-loop node into a clique, split into G₁ (one plus loop, Thm. 1) and G₂ (one fewer plus loop), and glue by Cor. 1.
  - Theorem 3 (pp. 23–25): consolidate all bags containing a plus-loop node into one bag (Lemma 7, p. 23), then peel leaves of the tree decomposition (Lemma 8, p. 24). Each leaf bag is either loop-free (2^{n₁}-size RLT simplex) or has one plus loop (Thm. 1), and n₁ ≤ width = O(log|V|).
  - Corollaries via tailor-made tree decompositions: edge bags for trees, a "fan" of triangles {vᵢ, vᵢ₊₁, vₙ} with a non-plus hub for chordless cycles, and a star around V\S (Props. 8–10, pp. 25–26).
  - Slip: §6 asks whether "Theorem 5" is tight with respect to spread (p. 27), but the paper has only Theorems 1–3; Theorem 3 is meant.
  - Transferable technique: to certify that a point lies in an SDP relaxation without a solver, give an explicit LDLᵀ factorization with nonnegative D (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: to extend a hull result from one "hard" node to many, combine (i) a clique-completion trick that makes the interface a complete hypergraph and (ii) a decomposability lemma for complete interfaces, then induct on the number of hard nodes (Thm. 2 pp. 20–22).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a. There are no experiments. Examples 1–2 (pp. 12, 20) are worked by hand. An extensive computational study inside MINLP solvers is named as future work (p. 27).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues Padberg [22] and the treewidth extended-formulation literature [19, 18, 6] and the extension-complexity lower bounds [9, 1] (p. 4). Builds on Del Pia–Khajavirad decomposability [10–13] (pp. 5–6) and Sherali–Adams RLT [26] (p. 9). The comparison with prior RLT extensions [25, 28] is a reframing, not a dismissal: the paper concedes they exist and says what they lack (extended-formulation guarantees) (p. 10). Against Burer–Letchford [8] and Anstreicher–Burer [3] it sharpens their n = 3 inexactness (Cor. 2, p. 14). Against the 2025 Anstreicher–Puges preprint [2] it shows that their SOC relaxation is implied (Observation 5, p. 16). Dey's own SOC-based method for solving QP [24] is contrasted: it "cannot be used to characterize QP(G)" (p. 27).
  - Transferable technique: when a concurrent paper proposes a related relaxation, prove containment explicitly (here, an algebraic identity between switched inequalities, pp. 15–16) rather than only compare numerically.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Contributions are listed per section with a "Roughly speaking" plain-language gloss of the main condition (p. 5). Examples come right after constructions (Example 1 p. 12, Example 2 p. 20), and Figure 1 illustrates the Theorem 2 proof (p. 21). Hedging is precise and sparse ("this result suggests that, in this case, PP(G) may not be SOC-representable", p. 19). The final section separates what extends easily (general convex hᵢ, p. 26–27) from four open questions (p. 27). The abstract presents the paper as a beginning ("starting point", p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution type: theory (convex-hull characterization and an extended-formulation size bound). Limits stated concretely: the formulation "may be of exponential size in general" (abstract p. 1). Polynomial size needs plus-loop degree O(log|V|) (p. 22). The (C1)–(C3) check has unknown complexity (p. 25). Open questions are concrete and checkable: necessity of the stable-set hypothesis; whether SOC extension complexity must grow exponentially with spread; whether solving QP needs representations as large as QP(G) (p. 27). Future work: put the relaxations into MINLP solvers and run an extensive computational study (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: MILP/IP-style substructure theory (BQP of sparse graphs, RLT, treewidth) is carried over to a nonconvex continuous class, convexifying the joint set QP(G) rather than single terms, with a constructive extended formulation whose size is stated (pp. 3–5, 13, 22–25).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: strictness over SDP+MC+Tri with an explicit violated point (pp. 13–15); necessity argument for one plus loop (p. 19); open gaps said plainly (pp. 25, 27). Partial only: there is no lower-bound (extension-complexity) result for the SOC case, which is posed as open (p. 27).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the "LP or SOCP before SDP" choice drives the paper (an SOC-representable exact formulation, pp. 1, 13), but there is no certified gap, no computation and no benchmark. The certificate deliverable of Method 5 is deferred to future work (p. 27).
- New-pattern candidate: "discrete-to-continuous lift of a polyhedral theorem". A known extended-formulation theorem for a binary set (Padberg/treewidth) is restated and proved for the continuous counterpart, recovering the discrete results as special cases (Props. 8–9 generalize Props. 1–2) (pp. 4–5, 25–26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Multiply a convex univariate constraint by an RLT partition of unity and rewrite term by term as perspectives to get SOC-representable, hierarchy-indexed inequalities (pp. 11–13, 26–27).
- Prove that a new relaxation is not implied by an SDP-based one with an explicit point plus an LDLᵀ PSD certificate (pp. 13–14).
- Get polynomial-size results from tree decompositions by (i) consolidating all bags of each "hard" node, (ii) bounding a "spread" parameter rather than only the width, and (iii) peeling leaves by induction (pp. 22–25).
- Reduce a class of minus-type nonconvexities to a few linear inequalities first (Lemma 5, p. 8), so that the analysis focuses on the one hard class.

**Quotes**:
- "Our results serve as a starting point for bridging the gap between the Boolean quadric polytope of sparse problems and its continuous counterpart." (p. 1)
- "In contrast, as we detail in the next section, our relaxation technique yields an exact characterization of QP(G) for a large class of graphs G." (p. 10)

### S103 · Aggregation of bilinear bipartite equality constraints and its application to structural model updating problem (Journal of Global Optimization 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10898-026-01607-8; arXiv:2410.14163)
**Read level**: full. arXiv v1 pp. 1–32 read: main text pp. 1–14 in full, proof of Theorem 1 (pp. 14–22) and Theorem 2 (pp. 22–26) in full, Appendix A (p. 32) in full. The proof of Theorem 3 was read for its structure and device (pp. 26–27 in full; the case-by-case algebraic verification pp. 27–30 was skimmed). References pp. 30–31 read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Dahye Han (Georgia Tech ISyE), Yang Wang (Georgia Tech Civil and Environmental Engineering) · **Position in trajectory**: third step of the bipartite-bilinear arc. Dey–Santana–Wang 2019 (SOCP relaxation + branching rule, ref [7]) and Santana–Dey 2020 (hull of one quadratic over a polytope, ref [18]) gave the one-row hull (p. 2). The aggregation theme continues Dey–Muñoz–Serrano [6] and Blekherman–Dey–Sun [2] for quadratic inequalities (p. 3). The FEM application continues Otsuki–Li–Dey–Kurata–Wang 2021 [17] (p. 2, p. 31).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a cheap step from known to unknown. The hull of a bounded set with *one* bilinear bipartite equality is known and SOC-representable via a disjunctive procedure (refs [7, 18], p. 2), so "a natural approach is to consider aggregation of the two constraints to obtain a one-row relaxation and then convexifying it" (p. 2). The object is the aggregation closure ∩_{λ∈T} conv(S_λ) (2) (p. 2). Existing results on aggregations giving hulls of quadratic sets (Yildiran; Dey–Muñoz–Serrano; Blekherman–Dey–Sun) concern direct intersections of aggregations, not their convex hulls, and "do not hold when bounds are added to the variables" (p. 3). The application, finite element model (FEM) updating in structural engineering, is where better relaxations of such sets "can improve the performance of the branch-and-bound process" (p. 2). Contributions are framed as three questions: finite? infinite? not at all? Then: useful anyway? (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when the hull of a one-row object is known, define the "aggregation closure" of a multi-row set and ask the three-tier question: finitely many aggregations suffice / infinitely many are needed / even infinitely many fail (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Theory: for n₁ = n₂ = 1, at most three aggregations give conv(S) (Thm. 1, p. 4). For n₁ + n₂ ≥ 3 there are sets needing infinitely many aggregations (Thm. 2, p. 4) and sets where the full closure is strictly larger than the hull (Thm. 3, p. 5). Computation: add the hull of *one* aggregated constraint per selected pair of rows, because of the size–strength trade-off (p. 6). Weights come from three heuristics (pp. 6–7):
  1. grid search over 20 weight vectors (1, ±2^k), (±2^k, 1), maximizing the distance d(λ) from the current relaxed point to conv(S_λ) (4);
  2. "simple search": fix y = ŷ to get a hyperplane in x and maximize its distance to x̂ over λ (5);
  3. surrogate search, adapting Algorithm 1 of Müller et al. [16].

  The one-row hulls are implemented as polyhedral outer approximations "in the same way as in section 5.2.2 of [7]" (p. 7). In the FEM model only consecutive rows are aggregated, because the Kⱼ are tridiagonal and denser rows make the disjunctive convexification blow up (p. 11). The aggregations are embedded in the custom branch-and-bound of [7]: rows chosen once at the root; outer approximations recomputed at each node; branching on the x variable that minimizes the sum of volumes of the resulting 2-D hulls; OBBT before solving (p. 11; App. A p. 32).
  - Transferable technique: choose aggregation (or cut) weights by maximizing the distance between the current relaxation optimum and the hull of the aggregated set over a small grid, which is embarrassingly parallel (pp. 6, 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: S is nonempty and the two constraints are independent (Assumptions 1–2, p. 4). Bounds are scaled to [0,1], and inequalities are turned into equalities with bounded slacks (pp. 1–2). The computational relaxations are polyhedral outer approximations of the SOC-representable one-row hulls, so the experiments test approximations, not exact hulls (p. 7). Random instances are small and dense, (n₁, n₂) ∈ {(2,2), (3,3), (5,5)} with every variable in each constraint, and coefficients drawn "from discrete uniform distribution on {−10, 10}" (p. 8; whether this means the integer range or the two-point set is not stated). FEM instances are simulated shear-frame structures "similar to the 18-story structure in [17]" (p. 10). The parameters t = 2 (12-story) and t = 5 (16-story) for the number of aggregations kept in the tree were chosen after "preliminary experiments" (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Theorem 1 (pp. 14–22). Constructive case analysis. An aggregation is used to make one constraint linear (8), followed by an affine change to (x − r)y = τ (9). Cases: conv(S) is a segment or a point, and the hyperbola is non-degenerate (τ ≠ 0) or a pair of lines (τ = 0) (p. 15). In the point case, an ε-perturbed aggregation produces a hyperbola branch whose hull meets the line only on the "opposite side" of the singleton (pp. 16–20). This is proved with monotonicity and convexity of hyperbola branches (Observation 1, p. 14) and a mean-value argument (p. 17). The proof is a recipe: it "explicitly provides recipes of the three or fewer aggregations needed" (p. 4).
  - Theorem 2 (pp. 22–26). Minimal example: xy₁ = xy₂ = 0.5 (17). Since x ≥ 0.5, y₁ = y₂ on S (Observation 2, p. 22). For any finite T, a perturbed point (3/4, 17/24 + ε, 17/24 − ε) lies in every conv(S_λ). This is shown by exhibiting points of S_λ for each θ-regime and taking convex combinations with a point of conv(S); ε₀ is the minimum over the finite T (pp. 22–23). For the full closure, a family of half-spaces H±_λ shrinks to y₁ = y₂ as θ → 1 (Claim 2, Fig. 14, pp. 24–25). Every point of the resulting set is written as an explicit two-point convex combination of points of S (pp. 25–26).
  - Theorem 3 (pp. 26–30). A point p̂ = (7/10, 7/8, 1/6) is separated from conv(S) by the hyperplane −2x + 10y₁ − 10y₂ = 5. To verify the separation, S is parametrized as a curve y(x), the feasible x-interval is restricted, and g(x) is maximized (pp. 26–27, Fig. 15). Membership of p̂ in every conv(S_λ) is certified by explicit points (k ≤ 4) and weights rational in θ over six θ-intervals (pp. 27–30). The authors note that finding the example was hard (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: to prove that a closure over a continuum of parameters is weaker than the hull, give one separating hyperplane for the hull and, for each parameter regime, an explicit convex-combination certificate whose points and weights are rational functions of the parameter (pp. 26–30).
  - Transferable technique: to prove that finitely many members of a closure never suffice, find a hyperplane the hull lies in and show that each single member admits a perturbation off it of size ε(λ) > 0; the minimum over a finite set stays positive (pp. 22–23).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The environment is stated in full: Julia 1.7, Gurobi 10.1 (LPs), Ipopt (node upper bounds), BARON 24.5.8 as benchmark, BranchAndBound.jl, a laptop with a 1.8 GHz processor and 16 GB (p. 8). The metric is relative gap improvement Δρ against a base relaxation (McCormick or one-row) (pp. 7–8).
  - Random instances: 10 per size. The one-row relaxation already closes much of the McCormick gap; one aggregation adds "more than 10%" for (2,2) and (3,3) and "almost 8%" for (5,5) on average (p. 8). Figure 3 repeats the comparison after excluding instances that the one-row relaxation already solves (ρ < 0.01%) (p. 9). Grid search is best (p. 8). Weight-search times are reported (Table 1, p. 10), including a "Grid (parallel est.)" column, which is an estimate assembled from component times, not a measured parallel run (p. 9).
  - FEM, 12-story and 16-story, 10 instances each. One instance per set was excluded because all methods solved it fast (p. 12). Root gap improvements over one-row: 2.94/2.98/6.82% (12-story) and 2.56/2.84/4.88% (16-story) for simple/surrogate/grid (p. 11). Grid-weight search times go up to 1,441 s sequential vs an estimated 8.36 s fully parallel (Table 3, p. 11).
  - Branch-and-bound, 1 h limit vs BARON. BARON is given the same OBBT-reduced bounds (p. 11). Average final gap: BARON 4.02%, one-row 1.33%, aggregation 0.75% (12-story); 10.16 / 7.11 / 5.93% (16-story) (Tables 4–5, p. 13). Aggregation explores fewer nodes (957 vs 1,553; 449 vs 561) (p. 13). Failures are reported. Aggregation loses on instance 3 in both sets (2.43 vs 1.53; 4.55 vs 3.91). This is explained by citing Dey's own non-monotonicity result [19]: "adding cuts may increase the size of the branch-and-bound tree due to wrong branching decisions" (p. 12). On 16-story instance 8 the one-row relaxation is worse than BARON (Δρ = −39.57%) (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: give the commercial baseline the same preprocessing (OBBT-reduced bounds) as your own method, and say so (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: when a cheap selector ranks candidate constraints, keep only the top-t by the same separation score used to generate them, and report the slow-down that forced the cap (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the MILP aggregation lineage: Marchand–Wolsey MIR from aggregations, GMI cuts as aggregations (Cornuéjols–Li), the aggregation closure for packing and covering (Bodur–Del Pia–Dey–Molinaro–Pokutta), and Dey–Molinaro on cut selection (p. 2). It also places itself in the surrogate-relaxation lineage (Glover, Balas, Geoffrion, Greenberg–Pierskalla, Müller et al.) (pp. 2–3). Quadratic-aggregation results are reframed, not criticized: they "are not directly applicable" because of bounds (p. 3). McCormick is chosen as the base, citing Gu–Dey–Richard that for bipartite bilinear problems McCormick can beat SDP (p. 7). Dey is a coauthor on 9 of the 21 references (pp. 30–31).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: All theorems are stated in §2 with a worked example (Example 1 with Fig. 1, pp. 4–5) and the plan of each proof. The proofs are moved to §§5–7 (organization p. 3). Results are framed as answers to questions (p. 3). The writing is open about difficulty ("it was not straightforward to find such an example", p. 5) and about negative computational cases ("It is possible that adding aggregated constraints may not benefit the branch-and-bound process", p. 12). The conclusion does not overstate: it says the exact hull "may not be achieved" (p. 13). It lists no future work (pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable technique: put every theorem with one concrete example and a one-sentence proof idea in a results section, and move all proofs to the back (pp. 3–5, 14–30).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution types: theory (a sharp three-tier characterization of aggregation-closure strength) plus computation and application (FEM model-updating bounds). Stated limits: only two rows are treated; one aggregation per pair; computational slow-down when all nm/2 aggregations are added, which forced t = 2 or 5 (p. 12); the parallel timing is estimated (p. 9). Not stated: no extension to more than two rows, and no explanation of which instances benefit. The conclusion names no future direction (pp. 13–14).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a MILP device (aggregation closure, MIR/GMI as aggregation) is imported into bilinear sets. It is applied to the cheap practice "intersect one-row hulls" and tested against the true hull, with counterexamples where it fails (pp. 2–5). This is step 5 of Method 3 almost verbatim. The application (FEM) chooses the row pairs (p. 11, step 3).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the positive result for n₁ = n₂ = 1 (≤ 3 aggregations, constructive) is paired with two negative results at the very next dimension (n₁ + n₂ = 3): infinitely many needed, and not achievable at all (pp. 4–5). This is a three-level demarcation.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the deliverable is a smaller certified gap at a time limit vs BARON (Tables 4–5, p. 13). But the relaxations are polyhedral approximations of SOC hulls inside a custom B&B, and no gap is closed to certified optimality on most instances (p. 12).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: a stripped-down custom B&B isolates the effect of aggregation (same OBBT bounds, same branching rule, p. 11). But there is no ground-truth ceiling such as an exact hull computation, and weight selection is compared only heuristic to heuristic (pp. 6–9).
- New-pattern candidate: "explain a negative instance with your own theory paper". The aggregation loss on instance 3 is attributed to branch-and-bound non-monotonicity under added cuts, citing [19] (Shah–Dey–Molinaro) (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Three-tier closure question (finite / infinite / impossible) with the smallest dimension where each tier first appears (pp. 3–5).
- Distance-maximizing aggregation-weight grid search, parallelizable, used both to generate and to rank aggregated constraints (pp. 6, 12).
- Parameter-wise convex-combination certificates for membership in every member of a parametric family of hulls (pp. 22–23, 27–30).

### S109 · Water network design and operation optimization: Leveraging approximations (EURO Journal on Computational Optimization 2026, DOI 10.1016/j.ejco.2026.100145)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–27 read, including Appendix A (piecewise-linear formulation, pp. 25–26) and Appendix B (Lemma 1, p. 27); references pp. 23–25 read for positioning. Version: the extracted text has no journal header, so it is likely a preprint or author version; it could not be matched to the published version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Sourabh K. Choudhary (Georgia Tech ISyE; first author), Nikolaos V. Sahinidis (Georgia Tech ISyE and ChBE) · **Position in trajectory**: application paper from a DOE-funded produced-water project (Acknowledgment, p. 23). A companion paper with the same team, Li–Dey–Sahinidis et al. 2024 on decomposition approaches, is cited (p. 3, p. 24). It reuses the pooling-era stance "explain why a MILP approximation works" (Dey–Gupte 2015 cited as the model, p. 2).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The application drives the paper. Produced-water networks (oil and gas) and urban water network design both contain the Hazen–Williams friction loss (d)^{4.87} H = γ q^{1.85}, which is the main nonconvexity (pp. 1, 3, 7). BARON fails to find good feasible solutions (pp. 3, 17). The literature gap is stated as missing theory: MILP approximation approaches for water networks exist, but "These works do not present theoretical guarantees on the performance of approximations" (p. 2). Dey–Gupte 2015 on pooling is cited as the model for "global guarantees of a linear approximation in MINLP" (p. 2). The second entry is a known failure. For urban networks, direct piecewise-linear (PWL) approximation gives MILP solutions infeasible for the MINLP unless breakpoints are very dense, and the MILP is then intractable (Bragalli et al. 2012, pp. 3, 14).
  - Transferable technique: split one application into the sub-problem where a standard approximation provably works (prove it) and the sub-problem where it is known to fail (design something new for that one), and say which is which in the abstract (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Two separate devices.
  1. A standard PWL λ-formulation of q^{1.85} with only four intervals (pp. 8–9). Diameter × friction products are linearized exactly by McCormick, since one factor is binary (p. 9). The pump electricity cost is modeled by the maximum flow of the active interval via big-M (p. 10). Then three propositions. Prop. 3.1: any PWL solution can be turned into an MINLP solution of no higher cost by keeping flows, diameters and pumps fixed and adjusting only the valve variables (p. 10), so z_MINLP ≤ z_PL (p. 11). Prop. 3.2: near-optimality bound C₂Q_max ε|E|T + C₂(p_max − p_min)ΔQ|E|T under chordless cycles and pressure slack (p. 11). Prop. 3.3: a weaker assumption-free bound C₁|E| + C₂εTQ_max|E| (p. 13).
  2. The Continuous-Variable-Diving (CVD) heuristic for urban networks (pp. 14–15, Alg. 1). Replace each nonlinearity by a least-squares linear fit over uniformly sampled points within the current bounds and solve the MILP under a time limit. Stop if the max discrepancy is within tolerance; otherwise shrink each continuous domain to [x̄ − l/η, x̄ + l/η] around the MILP solution, with η > 2 and domains possibly not nested, and repeat (pp. 14–15). It is positioned against mesh refinement (Burlacu et al.; Nagarajan et al.), which refines intervals in place, and against integer diving, which fixes variables. CVD instead recentres and shrinks continuous domains (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: for a nonlinearity in continuous variables only, use a "continuous dive": a single global linear fit plus geometric domain shrinkage around the last MILP solution, instead of refining a piecewise mesh (pp. 4, 14–15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The full MINLP (1)–(19) has big-M flow-direction and pressure rules (pp. 5–7). Prop. 3.2 assumes chordless network cycles and an optimal MINLP solution whose pressures are at least 2|L||E|ε above the minimum and |L||E|ε below the maximum (p. 11). The authors assert "these assumptions hold true for all instances we solved in this work" (p. 11), but no check is shown. Prop. 3.1 uses that the PWL secants overestimate the convex q^{1.85}. The text says the PWL "tends to overestimate" (p. 11); nonnegativity of the adjusted valve variable needs this, and the proof (p. 10) does not state it. Prop. 3.3 is assumption-free because it installs a pump on every arc (v̂ = 1), hence the C₁|E| term (pp. 13–14). CVD assumes all continuous variables in nonlinear terms are bounded (p. 14). Prop. 4.1 assumes unidimensional Lipschitz nonlinearities (p. 16). It bounds the number of MILPs only if no MILP is infeasible, since an infeasible MILP makes the heuristic fail (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Repair proofs (Props. 3.1–3.3, pp. 10–14). Fix the discrete decisions and flows from one model, set the friction variable by the other model's formula, and absorb the difference in slack-like physical variables (valve or pump head).
  - Cycle balancing in Prop. 3.2 (pp. 11–12). In a unidirectional cycle, summing (12)–(13) shows a pump must exist, and the excess friction R_l ≤ ε|L_c| is added to its head. In a mixed-direction cycle, R_l is added to a valve on a counter-flow arc.
  - Pressure reassignment from source nodes (pp. 12–13): tentative pressures propagated along fixed differences, then one global downward shift D and valve adjustment at source neighbours.
  - Cost bounds read physically: "the cost incurred if |E| pumps were to operate over all the time periods at a small pressure difference ϵ but with a maximum flow" (Remark, p. 14).
  - Prop. 4.1 (p. 16): an intermediate-value argument gives a point where the least-squares fit equals f. Lemma 1 (App. B p. 27) proves by a rotation contradiction that the fit's slope is at most the Lipschitz constant γ. Hence |f − L| ≤ 2γl, and geometric shrinkage by η/2 gives ⌈log(2D₀γ/ε)/log(η/2)⌉ MILPs.
  - Transferable technique: to bound how well an approximation model represents a physical network, take an optimal solution of the exact model, keep its decisions and flows, and repair feasibility only through slack-like physical variables (valves, pump head); the cost of the repair is the bound (pp. 11–14).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Intel i7-1165G7, 16 GB; Gurobi 12.0 and BARON 25.2 (p. 16).
  - PARETO case studies (Toy, Small, Permian, Treatment; Table 2 p. 17). Variants: stress factors 0.8/1.2/1.6 and pump cost ×5/10/15 (p. 16).
  - PWL results. Stopping gap 4%; solve times from seconds to about 20 minutes (p. 17). Gaps 0.1–0.3% vs BARON failing to give a good UB in 3 h on all but Small (Table 3, pp. 17–18). The gaps are measured against a lower bound from the MILP with nonlinear constraints dropped, which the authors observe to be tight (p. 17; footnotes 2–3, p. 19).
  - Theory-directed stress test: "The propositions in Section 3 show that the optimality gap can be sensitive to the relative magnitude of the pump cost", so pump costs are scaled. Gaps grow with pump cost, all below 5% (Table 5, pp. 17–18).
  - CVD on 9 MINLPLib urban instances, with η = 3, tolerance 10⁻⁵ and MILP time limits tiered by root-LP time (p. 19). Compared with best-known solutions from minlp.org and a CMU dissertation: better on fosspoly1, pescara and modena; worse on blacksburg, fossiron, fosspoly0 (−7.9%) and hanoi; equal on shamir. New York fails and is solved only by a two-piece variant, reported as "best of two" with summed time (Tables 6–7, pp. 19–20). Gaps to BARON's LB reach 8.2% (pescara) and 16.1% (modena) (p. 19).
  - CVD on PARETO: 0.2–2.2% gaps, except Small at 24–30% (Table 8, p. 20).
  - Sensitivity: η ∈ {2.5, 3, 4} and three time settings, run on the same benchmark. The paper concludes "η = 3 is optimal value" (pp. 21–22) and recommends tuning on a smaller instance (p. 21). Figure 3 shows infeasibility decreasing with iterations on Shamir (p. 22).
  - Transferable technique: scale exactly the parameter that your approximation bound depends on (here, pump cost), and show that the empirical gap grows as the bound predicts (pp. 17–18).
- **D6 Positioning**: Reviews water-network optimization broadly (pp. 1–2). The MILP approximation literature is criticized on one axis only, missing guarantees (p. 2). Bragalli et al. are accepted as the authority on PWL failure for urban networks (pp. 3, 14). CVD is placed between mesh refinement and integer diving, with an explicit statement of the difference (p. 4). Dey's own pooling paper is the template for guarantees (p. 2). The bilinear water-treatment literature is set aside as a different model (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Each proposition gets a plain-language paragraph before its proof (p. 10, p. 11), and remarks give the bounds a physical reading (p. 14). The contributions are two bullets (p. 4). The claims are not matched to the tables in one place. The contributions bullet says "Our primal heuristic consistently delivers near-optimal solutions for all our water network design instances" (p. 4), while Tables 6 and 8 show a 16.1% gap (modena), a 30% gap (PARETO Small) and a failure on New York without the modified variant (pp. 19–20). The abstract's "three of the nine" is accurate (p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution types: application, approximation guarantees and a general-purpose primal heuristic. The limits that are stated are mostly implicit: Prop. 3.2 assumptions (p. 11), η tuning (p. 21), New York failure (p. 19). There is no limitations paragraph; the conclusion only proposes testing CVD "in addressing various other MINLP problems with nonlinearity in continuous variables" (p. 23). The guarantees have no tightness example and no lower bound on the approximation error.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the paper takes an engineering practice (coarse PWL MILP approximation of Hazen–Williams), proves why it works on produced-water networks (Props. 3.1–3.3), and tests the prediction (pump-cost sensitivity) (pp. 2, 10–14, 17). It explicitly continues the pooling "why do MILP relaxations work" line (p. 2).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the demarcation is two-tier (strong bound under assumptions vs weak general bound, pp. 11–14) plus an application boundary (urban networks, where PWL fails, p. 14). But there is no tightness example, and the stated assumption check is unshown (p. 11).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: gaps are certified against a MILP lower bound obtained by dropping the nonlinear constraints (pp. 17, 19), but the main deliverable is primal solutions. The urban-network gaps rest on BARON's lower bounds (Table 6, p. 19).
- Method 2 ✗ contradiction (weak): η and MILP time limits were tuned on the same instances that are reported. The paper concludes "η = 3 is optimal value" from that benchmark (pp. 21–22), against Method 2's no-extrapolation rule and the Taste quick-check item asking for instances larger than those used for tuning.
- New-pattern candidate: "theory-directed stress test". The parameter that the approximation bound depends on is scaled to check the bound's prediction empirically (pp. 17–18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Repair-based proofs that an approximation's optimum is near the true optimum, absorbing approximation error in slack-like physical variables (pp. 10–14).
- Two-tier guarantees: a sharp bound under checkable structural assumptions and a weaker unconditional one (pp. 11–14).
- Continuous-variable diving: least-squares linearization plus geometric domain shrinkage around the last MILP solution, with an iteration bound from Lipschitz continuity (pp. 14–16, 27).
- Stress the cost parameter that appears in the bound (pp. 17–18).

**Quotes**:
- "These works do not present theoretical guarantees on the performance of approximations." (p. 2)
- "Our primal heuristic consistently delivers near-optimal solutions for all our water network design instances" (p. 4)

### S110 · Distributed Linear Programming on GPU Clusters at Extreme Scale (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2609.09108; OPT2026 workshop)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–18 read, including Appendices A–E (pp. 9–18) and the references (pp. 7–9).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Arnaud Deza (first and corresponding author; Georgia Tech), Pascal Van Hentenryck (Georgia Tech) · **Position in trajectory**: a new line for Dey in 2026: GPU first-order LP solvers at extreme scale. The paper is from the OPT2026 Workshop on Optimization for Machine Learning (p. 1). Dey's individual contribution cannot be identified from the text. The future-work link to discrete optimization ("using SHARDLP as a subroutine inside algorithms for very large discrete optimization problems, such as decomposition methods or large-scale heuristics", p. 6) is the closest tie to his MINLP and MILP programme.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: A systems bottleneck. PDHG-based LP solvers replace factorizations with GPU-friendly matrix–vector products, but "other solver phases can reintroduce a single-node memory limit" (abstract p. 1). The closest systems: D-PDLP distributes the two products across eight GPUs in one node, and MPAX leaves distributed sparse sharding as future work (p. 1). The question is how to turn the distributed iteration into a complete solver when "neither the matrix nor the primal–dual state fits on one node" (pp. 1–2). Three research questions: RQ1 benchmark coverage, RQ2 scale, RQ3 communication (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: "Persistent ownership". The authors keep D-PDLP's R × C matrix decomposition through every stage (input, scaling, restart, termination, singleton-zero presolve, feasibility polishing, recovery, output, checking) so that no stage gathers the full problem (pp. 3, Fig. 1; Table 7 p. 13). What did not change: "SHARDLP does not introduce a new PDHG variant" (p. 2); the optimization logic follows cuPDLPx's reflected-Halpern update (4) (p. 2). The second idea is support-aware communication. With column partitions, the reduction for row i involves only the GPUs whose column shard has a nonzero in row i (participant set Pᵢ, (7)), and the exact-arithmetic PDHG iterate is unchanged (p. 5; App. D pp. 16–17).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: LP in interval form (1) (p. 2). The input is assumed already sharded; offline shard construction is excluded from timing (pp. 3, 6, 13). General presolve is disabled; only a distributed singleton-zero elimination is used, and only in the RQ3 runs (p. 3). Recovery covers primal–dual solutions, not infeasibility or unboundedness rays (p. 13). Support-aware communication is implemented only for 1 × p column partitions with fixed sparsity (pp. 6, 16).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Mostly n/a: this is a systems paper. The one argument is an exact-arithmetic equivalence of dense and support-aware executions by induction over "participant epochs" between synchronization points (p. 16). Floating-point differences are then measured: at most 1.87×10⁻¹² in relative primal residual (p. 17). A logical "scalar-hop" ring communication model (8) gives the 92.97% reduction on Design Match. It is explicitly "not measured bytes on physical links" and "need not predict elapsed time" (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: when an implementation shortcut is meant to be exact, state the invariants under which it equals the reference execution, prove equality at synchronization points by induction, and then report the measured floating-point drift (pp. 16–17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - RQ1, Google PDLP benchmark (11 instances). 9 reach the published criterion vs 8 in the published CPU study. TSP-Gaia-100M (1.185 B variables, 6.338 B nonzeros): 593 s solver / 933 s end-to-end on 8 H200 GPUs vs a published 21.06 h on a CPU (Table 1, p. 4). The comparison is explicitly limited: the Google study used a six-day limit, "accordingly, Table 1 compares coverage and reports raw runtimes rather than a controlled hardware comparison" (p. 15).
  - RQ2, scale: up to 13.604 B variables and 40.807 B nonzeros on 76 mixed GPUs across 29 nodes in 1,905 s (Table 2, p. 5). Public-data cases (KDD12 L1-SVM, QAPLIB tai256c, MS1 matching) show that the method is "not limited to the synthetic flow family" (p. 4).
  - RQ3, communication. A fixed-work 4,000-iteration test shows the solver loop slightly *slower* on four GPUs (0.95×) while end-to-end improves 1.67× (p. 5, Fig. 2a). Support-aware communication gives 1.269× and 1.518× on Design Match but only 1.024× on QAP-WIL, where the modeled reduction is 38.35% (pp. 5–6).
  - Validation protocol (pp. 3–4, 13–15). Every non-benchmark solve is checked by a separate checker in the original unscaled space with long-double accumulation, using nine criteria (normwise and coordinatewise primal feasibility, stationarity, dual-sign admissibility, relative gap) at 10⁻⁶ (eq. (6)). A capacity run (8.5 B variables) that did not export full vectors "is not counted as a solve" (pp. 12, 18).
  - Timing rules: medians of three runs for some instances, one run otherwise (p. 15). Paired dense vs support-aware runs share an allocation, alternate their order, and are summarized by geometric means (pp. 9–10). A shared-node run is labelled "descriptive rather than a scaling measurement" (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: accept a reported solution only after an independent checker re-evaluates it in the original (unscaled, unpresolved) coordinates with explicit normwise and coordinatewise tolerances, and exclude runs that cannot be checked (pp. 13–15, 18).
  - Transferable technique: for A/B timing on shared hardware, run each pair sequentially on the same allocation, reverse the order across pairs, and report geometric means (pp. 9–10).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on PDHG/Chambolle–Pock and PDLP [3–5, 12], the cuPDLPx logic [22] and the D-PDLP decomposition. The paper says where borrowing stops: "Up to this point, the decomposition is D-PDLP's" (p. 3). It does not claim to have invented sparse collectives: "We do not claim a new generic collective" (p. 17). Appendix E compares scope with D-PDLP, MPAX, cuOpt, a matching-specific solver and ECLIPSE, and says what the scale claim is not (it is not "first multi-node", and there is no matched speedup) (pp. 17–18).
  - Transferable technique: in a systems paper, write an explicit comparison-scope appendix that defines what counts (general sparse solver, complete validated primal–dual export) before making a "largest" claim (pp. 17–18).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: RQ-structured (p. 3). Contributions are three sentences (p. 2), and the conclusion distils "two distinct scale bottlenecks: memory ownership and communication" (p. 6). Claims are hedged on almost every number (modeled vs measured, descriptive vs scaling, coverage vs speed; pp. 10, 15, 17). Numbers are given to several significant digits (92.9748%, p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution types: software/systems plus empirical scale. There is a dedicated limitations paragraph: offline sharding, and support-aware communication only for column partitions with fixed support (p. 6). Future work: better partitioning, distributed presolve and postsolve, general process grids, QP and SDP, and use as a subroutine in very large discrete optimization (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (transposed to systems): each claim is bounded explicitly (scale claim vs priority claim, modeled vs measured communication, descriptive vs scaling timing, uncounted capacity run) (pp. 10, 15, 17–18).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: a controlled instrument isolates one decision. The fixed-work iteration count separates the solver loop from end-to-end time (p. 5), and paired dense vs support-aware runs on the same allocation hold everything else fixed (pp. 9–10). But there is no ground-truth ceiling, apart from the exact-arithmetic equivalence (p. 16).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the "certificate" idea appears as independent primal–dual validation of every counted solve (pp. 13–15), not as a relaxation dual bound.
- New-pattern candidate: "independent original-space checker as the acceptance gate". A run counts only if a separate checker accepts the exported primal–dual solution (pp. 3–4, 13–15, 18).
- New-pattern candidate (trajectory, not method): GPU-scale first-order LP as infrastructure for large discrete optimization (p. 6). This is a 2026 line with a student first author; Dey's own role is not identifiable.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Independent original-space validation with nine normwise and coordinatewise criteria; uncheckable runs are not counted (pp. 13–15, 18).
- Fixed-work scaling tests that separate the solver loop from end-to-end time (p. 5).
- Order-reversed paired timing on the same allocation, with geometric means (pp. 9–10).
- Explicit comparison-scope appendix before any "largest" claim (pp. 17–18).
