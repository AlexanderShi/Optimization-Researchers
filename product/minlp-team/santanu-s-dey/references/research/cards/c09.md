### S023 · Approximation algorithms for training one-node ReLU neural networks (IEEE Transactions on Signal Processing 2020, doi:10.1109/tsp.2020.3039360; arXiv:1810.03592)
**Read level**: full. I read the arXiv v2 text titled "An Approximation Algorithm for training One-Node ReLU Neural Network" (p. 1), all of pp. 1-27 (body, proofs, references). I skimmed Appendices A-F (pp. 28-38): algorithm boxes A-D in full, the figure pages 31-32 by caption only, and realizable-case Tables 1, 2, 11 and 15 (pp. 33-38) by spot check. The published IEEE TSP version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Guanyi Wang (PhD student), Yao Xie (Georgia Tech ISyE) · **Place in trajectory**: the 2017-2024 "statistics and ML through MINLP" turn (SKILL.md trajectory table, T10). Ref. [2] is Boob-Dey-Lan's companion hardness paper on two-layer ReLU training (p. 26). The paper cites itself under its v1 title, "Relu regression: Complexity, exact and approximation algorithms", for the sorting and iterative methods (ref. [4], p. 26; App. A-B, p. 28), so the title changed twice: v1 → v2 → published.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters at the smallest neural network that is still an optimization problem: least-squares fitting of a single ReLU node, written as min over (β, β0) of ‖max{0, X^Tβ + β0·1} − Y‖² (pp. 1-2). It fences off the ML meanings of "complexity of training" (sample complexity, hypothesis finding) and says it "solely deals with the computational complexity" of this optimization problem (p. 2). It opens with a demarcation: nonconvex is not the same as hard, since PCA is nonconvex and polynomial (p. 3). It then asks three questions in order: is the problem NP-hard, is there an approximation algorithm, and how good is it in the worst case and under a statistical model (p. 3).
  - Transferable: before an algorithm paper on an ML training problem, pin the problem down as a precise optimization problem and list explicitly the neighbouring "complexity" notions you are *not* addressing (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Split the samples into Y_i > 0 (the set [m]) and Y_i ≤ 0. The Y ≤ 0 part φ is convex (Prop. 1, p. 4). The Y > 0 part becomes a two-phase problem: an outer choice of the "active set" I ⊆ [m] and an inner convex problem (p. 4). The inner problem is made unconstrained with a convex upper surrogate σ(x, y), equal to (x − y)² if x > 2y and y² otherwise. The surrogate is never below the true loss and is exact at the optimal active set (Prop. 2, pp. 4-5). The outer search covers only O(n^k) active sets, guided by the intuition that a larger Y_i is more likely to be active (Algorithm 1, pp. 5-6). In practice the authors use a cruder "sorting" version (k = 1, N = 10 splits) followed by an iterative active-set update, or by gradient descent started from the sorting solution (§3.3, pp. 11-12; App. A-B, pp. 28-29).
  - Transferable: for a piecewise-linear nonconvexity, enumerate the activation pattern (outer, combinatorial) and solve a convex inner problem. Replace the pattern constraints by a convex surrogate that upper-bounds the loss everywhere and is exact at the right pattern, so that each inner problem is unconstrained (pp. 4-5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Two regimes (p. 3). (i) Arbitrary data, no assumptions: Theorems 1-2. (ii) A statistical model (Assumption 1, p. 7): Y_i = max{0, X_i^Tβ* + β0*} + ε_i with Gaussian ε; X_i i.i.d., mean zero, covariance Σ; a "unique optimal property" on the support; (β*, β0*) in a compact convex Θ. The algorithm needs no tuning except k (abstract, p. 1). In contrast with Soltanolkotabi and Kalan et al., it does not need i.i.d. Gaussian data and allows a nonzero bias β0 (p. 7). The asymptotic result relies on classical uniform-law and nonlinear least-squares consistency theorems (Mickey 1963, Jennrich 1969; pp. 22-23).
  - Transferable: state the worst-case result first with no data assumption. Bring in a statistical model only for the second, sharper guarantee, and name which prior results need stronger data assumptions (pp. 3, 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) NP-hardness by reduction from a {±1} subset-sum variant (Prop. 5, p. 17). The reduction instance uses a gadget θ(x, β0) = (max{0, x+β0} − 1)² + (max{0, −x+β0} − 1)² that forces β_j ∈ {±1}, a heavy sample X = 0 with Y = 10p that pins β0, and a four-case analysis on β0 (pp. 17-19). (b) The n/k ratio (proof labelled "5.4 Proof of Proposition 2", pp. 19-21, though it proves Theorem 2). If |I^OPT| ≥ m − k + 1, the algorithm enumerates the optimum exactly. Otherwise, take the k largest inactive indices, split the misclassified actives into Ĩ+ and Ĩ−, and bound ΣĨ− Y_i² ≤ ((n−k)/k)·D using the sorted Y (p. 21). (c) The realizable case is exact (Cor. 1, pp. 6-7), a free consequence of the multiplicative ratio. (d) The asymptotic bound γ² ≤ z_asy ≤ 3γ²/2 + (2+2Δ²)/√(2π)·γ comes from splitting the expected surrogate loss into seven conditional terms (T1)-(T7), bounding (T5) with erf/erfc inequalities, and taking y = 0 (Thm. 3, p. 8; Lemma 1, pp. 23-25). Errors spotted while reading (mine, not the authors'): sample 2 of the reduction is labelled "Y1" (p. 18); in App. B the "decreasing objective" claim is written with ≤ in the increasing direction (p. 28).
  - Transferable: a multiplicative approximation ratio automatically gives "exact when the optimum is zero" (the realizable case). State that corollary separately, because it is often the result readers remember (pp. 6-7).
  - Transferable: to prove an enumeration-based ratio, pick one specific enumerated candidate built from the unknown optimum (here the k largest inactive indices) and bound its value. You never need to know which candidate the algorithm actually returns (pp. 19-21).
- **D5 Computational experiments**: Synthetic instances only (§3.2, p. 10). X_ij takes values in {−1, 0, 1} with a sparsity level P ∈ {0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9}. Noise is set by SNR in dB ∈ {6, 10, 20, 30, ∞}. Sizes are (p, n) = (10, 200), (20, 400), (50, 1000). In realizable instances the first p samples are perturbed so that the global optimum is unique (p. 10), which makes the planted β* the exact ground truth. Five methods are compared: sorting, sorting+iterative, GD from 0, sorting+GD, SGD (pp. 11-12). There are four measures (prediction, objective, recovery and generalization error) plus time, each averaged over 20 instances (pp. 12-13). Hardware: a MacBook Pro with Gurobi 7.0.2 (p. 10). Findings: sorting-based methods have the lowest prediction error. Sorting+iterative and sorting+GD give the lowest objective. GD becomes best on recovery error at ρ = 0.32 (p. 15). In the realizable tables, all three sorting variants show 0.0 prediction and recovery error on every instance while GD and SGD do not (Tables 1, 2, 11, 15, pp. 33-38). Inconsistency in the stated generator: §3.2 says β* ~ N(0.5·0_p, 10·I_p) for Figures 3, 5 and 6 (p. 10), but the Figure 3 caption says N(0.5·1_p, 50·I_p) (p. 14) and Figure 6 says N(0.5·1_p, 20·I_p) (p. 32). No failure cases or runs are reported as excluded. Conclusions are called "preliminary" (p. 13).
  - Transferable: in a realizable test, perturb the data so that the planted parameter is the *unique* global optimum. Then "recovery error = 0" certifies global optimality per instance, and a local method's nonzero error is a measured failure, not a tie (p. 10; App. F, pp. 30-33).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Hardness: Boob-Dey-Lan show the two-layer case is NP-hard, and this paper shows "even a more simplified structure" is. Manurangsi-Reichman gave an independent reduction and an inapproximability bound, n^{1/(log log n)^{O(1)}} (pp. 3, 6). The exact O(n^p) algorithm of Arora et al. is credited, and the paper's own algorithm is placed in the p ≫ n regime (p. 6). Prior GD/SGD guarantees are rephrased as needing i.i.d. Gaussian inputs, realizability, additive error and special initialization (p. 7). Further prior work (Hinton et al.; Zhang et al.; Du et al.; Goel-Klivans-Meka, which assumes no bias term and a centrally symmetric input) is described as needing a good initialization or carrying "no theoretical guarantees" (p. 9). The tone is neutral restatement, not attack.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Each theorem is followed by a "Comparison with related results from literature" paragraph (pp. 3, 6, 7, 9). All proofs are deferred to §5 after the computations (p. 2). The paper is frank about limits. The ratio "goes to infinity" as noise vanishes, although z_asy → 0 (p. 9). The upper bound is far from the lower bound (p. 6). The conclusion presents an empirical observation as the paper's lesson (p. 16): approximation algorithms are worth having as starting points for GD. Typographical noise is heavy ("deiced" p. 10; "Y1" for sample 2, p. 18; a mislabelled proof heading, p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Three layers of contribution: a complexity result (NP-hard), an algorithm with a worst-case ratio and exactness in the realizable case, and an n-independent asymptotic ratio under noise (p. 16). The authors state their best-guarantee claim with a hedge: "To the best of our knowledge, these are best theoretical performance guarantees" (p. 16). Open questions named (p. 16): the gap between n/k and the n^{1/log log n} lower bound; a conjecture that the algorithm is optimal in the statistical model; extension to multi-node networks. Limits not named: the n/k ratio is not shown to be tight; O(n^k) is impractical beyond k = 1, so the experiments run a cruder variant of Algorithm 1, not Algorithm 1 itself (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: hardness (Thm. 1) is paired with an approximation result and an explicit statement that the gap to the known lower bound is open (pp. 3, 6, 16). The ratio's degeneration as γ → 0 is written next to the corollary (p. 9). This matches step 6 ("If tightness is unknown, say so"). No tight example is given for n/k.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: realizable instances are built with a planted, unique global optimum, so every method is measured against exact ground truth (p. 10; App. F, pp. 30-33). The generator mismatch between text and captions (pp. 10, 14, 32) repeats the "generator does not match the stated distribution" defect noted in Method 2's limitations [03 C2], in a different paper.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the paper keeps the certificate stance but changes its form. Instead of a per-instance dual bound from a relaxation, it gives an a priori multiplicative guarantee, and it criticises GD/SGD results for their Gaussian/realizable data assumptions (p. 7). This is evidence for Taste warning sign 1 (guarantees resting on unverifiable data assumptions).
- New-pattern candidate: "approximation algorithm as warm start for a local method": a guaranteed combinatorial algorithm supplies the starting point, gradient descent polishes it, and the combination is reported as the main empirical lesson (abstract p. 1; p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: "active-set enumeration + exact convex surrogate": a piecewise-linear nonconvex loss becomes an outer enumeration over activation patterns with an unconstrained convex inner problem (pp. 4-6). This is the MINLP outer/inner decomposition move applied to an ML training problem.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use a convex upper surrogate that is exact at the optimal discrete pattern (Prop. 2), so the inner problems are unconstrained convex fits and the outer search is purely combinatorial (pp. 4-5).
- Prove an enumeration ratio by bounding one specific enumerated candidate built from the unknown optimum (pp. 19-21).
- Construct realizable test instances whose planted parameter is the unique global optimum, so that "recovery error = 0" certifies optimality (p. 10).
- After each theorem, add a "comparison with related results" paragraph that names exactly which assumption of each prior result the new one drops (pp. 6-7).

**Quotes**:
- "There is a significant gap between upper bound from Algorithm 1 and this lower bound." (p. 6)
- "since such algorithms due to their theoretical guarantees provide a good starting point for gradient descent, usually a requirement for the gradient descent algorithm to work well." (p. 16)

### S032 · The convex hull of a quadratic constraint over a polytope (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2020, doi:10.1137/19m1277333; arXiv:1812.10160)
**Read level**: full. I read every page of the arXiv v1 text (25 Dec 2018, pp. 1-14), including all proofs and the reference list. The published SIAM version was not read; its content may differ.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Asteroide Santana (PhD student, first author) · **Place in trajectory**: the QCQP substructure convexification line (2017-2026, T9). It generalizes one main result of Dey-Santana-Wang on bipartite bilinear sets over a box (ref. [20], p. 2) and is listed in Method 3's practice evidence in SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a gap in the global-optimization literature (p. 2). Function convexification (McCormick and its descendants) "does not necessarily yield the convex hull" of {x : f(x) = b}, and there has been "much less work on explicit convexification of sets". Then comes a representability question: is the exact hull LP, SOCP or SDP representable (p. 2)? The authors' own earlier result (bipartite bilinear ∩ box is SOCr, with encouraging dual bounds) supplies the special case to generalize (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when your earlier paper proved a hull result for a special structure that also worked computationally, ask at once how far the structure can be dropped while keeping the same representability class (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Theorem 1 (p. 2): for any quadratic equation x^TQx + α^Tx = g and any polytope P, conv(S) is SOCr, with no assumption on Q. The key observation (p. 3): after diagonalization the quadric surface either (1) is a union of two convex pieces (as with a two-sheet hyperboloid), whose hull is SOCr directly, or (2) is ruled, with a straight line through every point lying in the surface. In case (2) no interior point of P is extreme, so the hull is generated by the intersections with the facets of P. Each of these is a set of the same form one dimension lower, so the proof recurses (pp. 3, 9-10).
  - Transferable: to find the hull of a compact nonconvex set, first ask where its extreme points can lie. If a line segment inside the set passes through every relative-interior point, push the problem to the facets and recurse on dimension (pp. 3, 6-7, 9-10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: One quadratic *equation*; P a bounded polyhedron. Boundedness is defended as "not very restrictive" because global optimization bounds variables for branch-and-bound (p. 2). There are no structural or sign assumptions on Q or α (p. 2). Quadratic inequalities and multiple quadratics are not treated in this version (not stated as a limitation).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Hull through a cover of extreme points. If B_1, …, B_k ⊆ S cover extr(S), then conv(S) = conv(∪ conv(B_i)), and SOCr is preserved under the convex hull of a finite union (Ben-Tal-Nemirovski) (§3.1, pp. 3-4). (b) Affine invariance of hulls and of SOC representability (Obs. 2, p. 4). (c) Canonical form by the spectral theorem and completing squares: Σw_i² − Σx_j² + Σy_k = g with free z, and g ≥ 0 without loss of generality (§3.2, pp. 4-5). (d) Reduction to a full-dimensional P by substituting the equality system (Lemma 1, §3.3.1, pp. 5-6). (e) Four non-extremality lemmas, one per sign pattern (no, nl, nq+, nq−) (Lemmas 2-5, pp. 6-7). In the hardest case a line in the surface is constructed by intersecting a unit sphere with a hyperplane at distance |b1|/‖a‖ ≤ 1 (p. 7). (f) Tawarmalani-Richard: conv(G ∩ {f = 0}) = conv(G ∩ {f ≤ 0}) ∩ conv(G ∩ {f ≥ 0}), plus Hillestad-Jacobsen: a reverse-convex set ∩ polytope has a polyhedral hull. Together these give the base cases (Lemmas 6-7, pp. 8-9). (g) Induction on dimension over the facets of P (pp. 9-10).
  - Transferable: the decomposition conv{f = 0} = conv{f ≤ 0} ∩ conv{f ≥ 0} (for G convex, f continuous) turns an equation into two inequalities: one convex side with an SOCr hull, and one reverse-convex side with a polyhedral hull (p. 8).
  - Transferable: reduce a general quadratic to a signed sum of squares plus linear terms (spectral theorem plus completing squares) and case-split on the counts of each sign (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational experiments**: n/a. There are no experiments. The paper points to the encouraging dual bounds in [20] as computational motivation (p. 2).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: It names three relaxation families: LP, SOCP, SDP; RLT and Lasserre hierarchies; DSOS/SDSOS (p. 1). It concedes that SDP is strong but scales poorly and that SOCP relaxations are often derived from SDP ones (p. 2). It lists about 27 function-convexification papers against 10 set-convexification papers (p. 2). It frames the result as "somewhat unexpected" against the sum-of-squares view, citing Fawzi's result that even the 3×3 PSD cone is not SOCr (p. 2). It credits the classical ruled-surfaces theory (Edge) after the fact (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A short note: the abstract states the result, then "We present a simple constructive proof" (p. 1). The main result is stated as Theorem 1 on p. 2, before any proof. Two figures carry the geometric intuition (hyperboloids of one and two sheets, p. 3). There are two candid narrative moves. The proof is "fairly straightforward" (p. 3). The authors learned only after proving the theorem that the property was classical ("ruled surfaces"), and they claim novelty only for its use in global optimization (p. 3). There is no conclusion section.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: an exact SOCr hull for one quadratic equation over any polytope, with a constructive procedure (pp. 2, 4). The cost of the construction is written next to the theorem: since linear optimization over S is NP-hard, the construction needs an exponential number of variables (p. 2). No future work or open questions are stated (no conclusion section). It does not discuss how to use the hull computationally, given the exponential size.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the paper explicitly contrasts set convexification with function convexification and chooses the set (p. 2). It convexifies a small recurring substructure (one quadratic ∩ polytope) toward the exact hull with a constructive proof, generalizing the authors' own bipartite-bilinear result (p. 2). This is a clean instance of steps 2-4.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper does what step 5 asks ("State the cost of a constructive proof"), with the exponential number of variables tied to NP-hardness (p. 2). Tightness of the exponential size is not shown.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the target representability class is SOCr, not SDP, and SDP's poor scaling is the stated reason (p. 2) (Taste 4, "LP and SOCP before SDP").
- Heuristic 9 ⚠ variant (not a Method): the neighbouring-field tool, ruled surfaces, was found *after* the proof (p. 3), not borrowed in advance. This is consistent with the "Literature search" row of the stages without a Dey method ("He finds prior work after the result" [03 §2.3]).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use the extreme-point cover identity conv(S) = conv(∪ conv(B_i)) with B_i ⊆ S covering extr(S) to prove that a compact set's hull stays in a representability class closed under finite unions (pp. 3-4).
- Argue "no interior point is extreme" with a line inside the surface (ruled-surface argument), then recurse over the facets of the polytope by induction on dimension (pp. 6-7, 9-10).
- Turn an equality into two inequality hulls: conv(G ∩ {f = 0}) = conv(G ∩ {f ≤ 0}) ∩ conv(G ∩ {f ≥ 0}) (p. 8).
- Put the size cost of a constructive hull proof next to the theorem, justified by the NP-hardness of linear optimization over the set (p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "Note that optimizing a linear function over S is NP-hard, therefore, while the convex hull is SOCr, the construction involves the introduction of an exponential number of variables." (p. 2)
- "To the best of our knowledge, however, no one from the global optimization community has ever exploited such results for convexification." (p. 3)

### S044 · A k-nearest neighbor heuristic for real-time DC optimal transmission switching (arXiv preprint 2020, arXiv:2003.10565)
**Read level**: full. I read every page of the arXiv v3 text (14 May 2021, pp. 1-32): body, Appendices A-D including the proof of Proposition 1, and the reference list. The boxplot pages 7-13 were read by caption and axis labels only (the plotted values are not in the text layer).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Emma S. Johnson (first author, Georgia Tech / Sandia), Shabbir Ahmed (deceased at submission, p. 1), Jean-Paul Watson (LLNL). Dey is third of four authors, so the voice is the team's, and Dey's individual role is not identifiable from the text. **Place in trajectory**: OPF / power-systems line (T6) crossed with ML-for-MIP. SKILL.md names arXiv:2003.10565 as an unpublished ML line under U2. It is also an early instance of "reuse information across re-solves" (U4, Anti-patterns table).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an operational time limit (pp. 1-2). ISOs re-solve DCOPF every 5 minutes. DC optimal transmission switching (DCOTS) is NP-hard and not approximable within a constant (citing Lehmann et al. and Kocuk et al., including Dey's own cycle-based formulation), so no MIP formulation or cutting-plane approach scales to real time (p. 2). The opening is the observation that the same network is solved repeatedly with load and cost varying only about 10% and 5%. So historical solutions are plentiful, and "similar load profiles and generation cost profiles will likely turn off similar sets of lines" (abstract p. 1; p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a hard MIP is re-solved on a fixed structure with a slowly varying right-hand side and costs, treat the archive of past optimal integer solutions as the heuristic's search space (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Represent an instance by its vector q of generation costs and demands (p. 4). Normalize it, find the k nearest solved instances in ℓ_p distance, fix each neighbour's switching vector in the new instance, solve k DCOPF LPs, and return the cheapest (Algorithm 1, pp. 3-4, 29). Change relative to Yang-Oren 2019: learn *topologies directly* rather than line priorities fed to a greedy algorithm, adopting Xavier et al.'s initial-solution learning (p. 3). Online cost is independent of the switching cardinality and nearly independent of network size, because there are only k LP solves and no loop over lines (pp. 3-4, 6).
  - Transferable: design the online phase so that its cost is k LP solves with the integer part fixed. Network size then enters only offline (training) and through the LP solve time (pp. 4, 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A single-period B-θ DCOTS MIP (Fisher et al. 2008) with McCormick-linearized Ohm's law (big-M on angle differences), a ±π/6 angle limit, a cardinality K of opened lines, and load-shed and over-generation slacks penalized at M = 10^6 so that every topology is feasible (App. A, pp. 27-29). Only loads and costs vary; all other parameters are fixed per network (p. 4). Returned solutions are *not* guaranteed feasible, and this is defended because DC solutions need post-processing anyway (p. 4). K is 5 or 10 in practice, because many switches hurt AC feasibility (citing Coffrin et al.) and most savings come from few switches (citing Kocuk et al.) (pp. 28-29).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: There is one proposition (Prop. 1, p. 18). If the integer part x* of an optimum is unique and stays feasible in a neighbourhood of b, then x* stays optimal for all nearby b̂. Proof device (App. D, p. 32): write g(b) = min over x ∈ {0,1}^n of f_x(b), where each f_x is an LP value function, piecewise linear and convex in b. Show that each competitor x̂ is dominated in a neighbourhood, either because infeasibility is preserved (the feasible right-hand sides of an LP form a closed polyhedron) or by continuity in the interior of the domain. Then intersect finitely many neighbourhoods. The authors concede that this says nothing about the small training sets actually used (p. 18). My note, not the authors': as printed, step 2(b) states f_x*(b) ≤ f_x̂(b) + ε, but the argument needs strict separation from uniqueness (p. 32). The empirical "why it works" section (§5, pp. 18-25) replaces theory with three data claims: few lines are ever opened; neighbours' solutions are near-optimal; buses act in regional congestion classes.
  - Transferable: to justify nearest-neighbour reuse of integer solutions for a MIP, use the finite-min-of-LP-value-functions argument. The unique integer optimum is locally stable in the right-hand side (pp. 18, 32).
- **D5 Computational experiments**: Seven networks, 118 to 3,375 buses, from Blumsack, Kocuk et al., PEGASE and PGLib "api" heavily loaded cases (Table 1, p. 5). Selection rule: networks are kept only if switching has a cost benefit; for example, nominal 1951rte is dropped (p. 5). Per network, 300 instances are generated (demand ×U[0.9, 1.1], cost ×U[0.95, 1.05], following Xavier et al.) and solved to 1% optimality or 0.5 h; 270 are used for training and 30 for testing (pp. 5-6). Setup: k = 10, ℓ2 and ℓ∞ norms; cardinality 5, 10 or none (p. 6). Baselines: the four sensitivity heuristics of Ruiz et al., given a 10-minute limit "to forgive inefficiencies in our implementation"; Gurobi with Heuristics = 1, warm-started at all-lines-closed, given 5 minutes; and greedy local search on 118 buses only, because it is intractable larger (pp. 5-6). Platform: Pyomo with Gurobi 9.0.2, 8 threads on a 96-core server (p. 6). Metric: relative gap to the *best-known* solution across training and all heuristics, not to a proven optimum (p. 6). Results: boxplots of gap and time (Figs. 1-7, pp. 7-13). A count of best-solution wins gives KNN-ℓ2 231, Gurobi 152 and Line Profits 122 over all cases (Table 2, p. 14). KNN takes at most 40 s even on 3375wp (p. 6). Dollar savings are given to show when quality differences matter (p. 15). A load-shed analysis compares heuristics against training solutions (Fig. 8, p. 16). There are parameter sensitivity tests (|Q| = 100/270/500; k = 3/5/10) and leave-one-out cross-validation with a mean gap of 0.75% and a worst case of 17.4% (Table 3, p. 17). Finally, an aggregated-feature experiment on 118 buses (118 → 30 dimensions) matches the original (Fig. 13, p. 24).
  - Transferable: report a "number of instances on which each heuristic finds the best solution" table with ties counted for both, next to gap and time distributions (Table 2, p. 14).
  - Transferable: give the baseline you implemented yourself extra time and say why (10 min vs 5 min, p. 6).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: MIP formulations, including Kocuk-Dey et al.'s cycle-based cuts, are acknowledged to be too slow for real time (p. 2). The sensitivity heuristics of Ruiz and Fuller are "effective in terms of solution quality" but scale with lines × budget (p. 2). The closest ML work (Yang and Oren) is differentiated by what is learned (p. 3). The method is presented as an application of Xavier, Qiu and Ahmed's learned initial solutions for unit commitment to DCOTS (p. 3). The tone is neutral throughout.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract ends with "Last, we present an empirical study of our training data to understand why the heuristic works well" (p. 1), so explanation is promised as a deliverable alongside performance. The paper reports a finding that goes against its own premise ("Surprisingly, the best solutions do not tend to be among the closest", p. 20) and then resolves it with a second plot: the closest 1%-optimal solution lies within the k = 10 neighbourhood (Fig. 11, pp. 20-22). Hedging is moderate and explicit ("We suspect", p. 18; "We have no reason to believe that this method is an improvement", p. 22). Section headings are questions ("Is Distance the Correct Metric?", p. 20). Minor inconsistencies: the 300-bus case is labelled "300kocuk" in tables and "300burak" in Figures 10, 11 and 14 (pp. 21-22, 31), and p. 20 refers to "Figure 2" where Figure 9's data are meant.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and empirical. A fast heuristic usable within real-time limits, with an analysis of the training data (p. 25). Limits written concretely: no feasibility guarantee, and all k neighbours can be infeasible, which is attributed neither to M nor to k (p. 16); distance is "an imperfect metric" (p. 25); the class-aggregation variant does not scale (p. 22). Future work named (p. 25): characterize how demand changes drive congestion; apply the method to AC optimal transmission switching; use it as warm starts for planning and day-ahead problems. There is no dual bound or optimality certificate anywhere.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Treat an archive of past optimal integer solutions as the candidate set: online cost is k fixed-integer LP solves (Algorithm 1, p. 29).
- Use a local-stability proposition for MIP value functions (finite min of convex piecewise-linear LP value functions) to justify reusing solutions near a right-hand side (pp. 18, 32).
- When distance-based reuse works but the closest neighbour is not the best, plot both "rank of the best" and "rank of the closest ε-optimal" solution. The second plot explains why the method works anyway (Figs. 10-11, pp. 20-22).
- Probe which features matter by grouping inputs that induce the same optimal decision (bus classes) and rerunning with aggregated features (pp. 21-24).
