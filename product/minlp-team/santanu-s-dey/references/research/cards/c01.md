### S079 · Recent developments in multi-row cuts (Optima 80 2009, no DOI)
**Read level**: full for the article (pp. 2-8) and for the discussion column (pp. 8-10, other authors' voice). The MPS Chair's column (pp. 1-2) was skimmed; the prize citations and the MINLP-cybersite note (pp. 10-12) were not read.
**Role**: core (survey) · **Coauthors**: Andrea Tramontani (Bologna) · **Place in trajectory**: written as Dey moved from CORE to Georgia Tech (affiliation p. 8); it summarises the 2007-2009 multi-row programme in which his own S013 and S016 sit (refs [21], [23], p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is the limit of the single-constraint paradigm: for a general MIP "little structure can be assumed", so one relaxes the feasible region, and the standard relaxation keeps one row (GMI, MIR, cover cuts), p. 2. Recent work uses several tableau rows at once (p. 2). The problem is stated as a design tension: which relaxation of the tableau (1) is both analysable and strong (p. 2).
  - Transferable: organise a survey (or a research programme) around one explicit trade-off, and classify every result by the relaxation it analyses.
- **D2 Core idea**: The review is built as a ladder of relaxations. Corner relaxation → master group relaxation, chosen because it "contains all possible interesting columns and is effectively 'data independent'" (pp. 2-3) → drop integrality of the nonbasics, R(f,∅,W), which ties extreme inequalities to maximal lattice-free convex sets through intersection cuts (pp. 3-5) → add back bounds on the nonbasics (§3.4, p. 5), constraints AxB ≤ b on the basics through maximal S-free sets (§3.5, pp. 5-6), and integer nonbasics through lifting and fill-in (§3.6, p. 6). Strength is then judged by closures and split rank (§4, pp. 6-7).
  - Transferable: when presenting a family of relaxations, write down what each step drops or adds back, and say which characterisation theorem survives each step.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A tableau with an integer-infeasible basic solution; f ∈ Q^m \ Z^m; rational columns; finite support (pp. 2-3, 5). The S-free results need S to be the integer points of a rational polyhedron with full-dimensional conv(S) (Theorems 3.7-3.9, p. 6). The survey says the clean results need the all-columns model: Theorem 3.5's result is "'cleaner'" because the model "has all possible columns and is effectively data independent" (p. 5).
- **D4 Proof devices**: This is a review, so it reports devices rather than proving anything: the intersection-cut coefficient formula (5) (p. 4); Gomory-Johnson-type minimality conditions (Theorem 2.1, p. 3); the Cornuéjols-Margot reduction algorithm and "ray condition" (p. 4); closure comparison through scaled sets αQ (p. 7: triangle/quadrilateral closures lie within a factor 2 of the hull, while the split closure can be arbitrarily bad, Theorem 4.12); and Dey's own split-rank results: finite split rank iff Lπ is not a type-1 triangle (Theorem 4.15, with Louveaux), and a log2(l) lower bound from integer points on distinct facets of a "restricted lattice-free" set (Dey [16]), p. 7.
  - Transferable: compare two cut families by the factor α by which their closures can exceed the convex hull, since that factor does not depend on the instance (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**: None by the authors. They report Espinoza [24]: multi-row cuts added through CPLEX 10.2 callbacks at the root after the default cuts, m from 2 to 15, two classes of lattice-free sets, 87 MIPLIB 3.0/2003 instances, a 31% geometric-mean speed-up on instances solved to optimality, negative examples acknowledged, and more gap closed with more rows (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: Multi-row work is presented as "revisiting and recalling other important results discovered more than 40 years ago" (Gomory, Balas, Gomory-Johnson), p. 2. The scope is restricted explicitly to group-relaxation-based approaches (p. 2). Dey's own papers ([16]-[23]) are cited in the same neutral voice as everyone else's (pp. 3, 5-7).
- **D7 Writing**: A theorem-by-theorem tutorial with one toy MIP (the Cook-Kannan-Schrijver example, Example 3.1) showing that a triangle cut gives in one step what split cuts cannot give in finitely many (p. 4). Evaluative sentences are hedged: "at least theoretically" (p. 7), "may hold some potential" (p. 7), "we hope that much progress will be made" (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is expository. Theoretical open questions: maximal lattice-free sets in dimension ≥ 3, and "how many of the relaxations ... can be revoked" while keeping a complete characterisation of extreme inequalities (pp. 7-8). Practical obstacles are named concretely: cut selection (O(m²) row pairs times many triangles or quadrilaterals per pair) and density, which "can affect the performance of other components" of branch-and-cut (p. 8). In the discussion column (other voices): Weismantel asks which lattice-free bodies avoid tailing off (p. 9); Cornuéjols argues that the key relaxation is dropping integrality of the nonbasics, not the group relaxation, citing Dey-Wolsey [10] (= S016) (p. 9); Wolsey raises three-way branching versus triangle cuts (p. 9).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅: the "art lies in obtaining a relaxation" sentence (p. 2) is the stated root of Method 3, and §§3.2-3.6 are the MILP substructure toolkit that Method 3 later carries into QCQP. Method 1 variant ⚠: the preference for data independence is already here (pp. 3, 5), but it guides the choice of model (the all-columns relaxation), not the choice of a measure. Heuristic 4 evidence ✅: the two named obstacles, cut selection and density (p. 8), are the ones Heuristic 4 says became the sparse-cut and cut-selection programme (that continuation is not in this text). New-pattern candidate: "relax, then add back one constraint at a time" (§§3.4-3.6, pp. 5-6).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Lay out a relaxation ladder (what is dropped, what is re-added) and attach to each rung the theorem that holds there (pp. 2-6).
- Use one toy instance that separates two cut families (finite versus infinite convergence) as the running motivation (p. 4).
- End with open questions split into theoretical and practical, and quantify the practical obstacle (O(m²) row choices × shapes), p. 8.
**Quotes**:
- "Another practical difficulty with this class of cutting planes is that they tend to be dense." (p. 8)
- Wolsey, discussion column, not Dey's voice: "Later when Santanu Dey came to Core, my main concern was how to deal with integer non-basic variables, which again led him far beyond what was initially asked." (p. 9)

### S013 · Two row mixed-integer cuts via lifting (Mathematical Programming 2010, doi:10.1007/s10107-010-0362-x)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-29). The proofs of §§4-7 were read for statements and proof structure; the area computations (pp. 22-23), the case checks in the proof of Theorem 5 (pp. 27-29) and Appendix 1 (pp. 31-32) were skimmed because the extracted formulas are fragmented.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Laurence A. Wolsey (both at CORE, UCLouvain, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the journal version of an IPCO 2008 extended abstract (p. 3); companion of S016; this is the "integer non-basic variables" question Wolsey recalls in S079 p. 9.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a computational regularity. Single-row group cuts other than GMIC were tested and "the results have been disappointing"; GMIC "seems to be the most effective", and the paper offers a possible reason, its continuous coefficients (p. 2). Meanwhile, the new two-row results of Andersen et al., Borozan-Cornuéjols and Cornuéjols-Margot characterise only a model with continuous nonbasics (p. 3). Goal: cuts from any two rows "that are strong in a well-defined sense and that have similar properties to the GMIC" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when one member of a cut family dominates in practice, name the property you think explains it, then design the higher-dimensional object to keep that property.
- **D2 Core idea**: Read GMIC as a three-step recipe (relax to a one-row group problem; take an extreme inequality in the continuous variables; lift the integer variables) and carry out step 3 for two rows (p. 3). The continuous part π stays fixed, so the continuous variables keep the strongest coefficients, and only the integer coefficients are chosen. The candidate is the trivial fill-in φ(u) = inf{π(w) : P(w) = u} (Prop. 2, p. 8). Results: (φ,π) is minimal iff the fractional parts of a region D(π) cover I², and it is then the unique minimal lifting (Theorem 2, p. 8); for type-1 and type-2 triangles (splits give GMI) the fill-in is extreme (Theorem 3, p. 9); for type-3 triangles and quadrilaterals it is not (Theorem 4, p. 9); a general fill-in after exactly lifting a subset G (pp. 9-10) comes with a sufficient extremality condition (Theorem 5, p. 10); a five-step cut-generation recipe follows (pp. 10-11).
  - Transferable: when an exact lifting problem is a nonlinear integer program, look for a "middle path" coefficient (fill-in) and characterise exactly when it is already optimal.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The model is the mixed-integer infinite group problem MI(I², R², r) (Defs. 1-4, pp. 4-5). f is assumed in the interior of the maximal lattice-free set; the boundary case is set aside ("we focus", p. 6). The continuous columns are taken as W = R² rather than rational, "only a minor technical assumption", handled in Appendix 1 (p. 5, fn. 2). Most proofs need a bounded set (triangle or quadrilateral), pp. 14-15.
- **D4 Proof devices**: (i) The minimality test (7), which must hold for every u ∈ I², is replaced by a geometric covering test: D(π) is the union of parallelograms spanned from f to each integer point on an edge, and (φ,π) is minimal iff P(D(π)) = I² (Def. 6, p. 15; Props. 5 and 7, pp. 15-18). (ii) The fill-in is evaluated finitely by bounding the integer shifts (Prop. 3, p. 14). (iii) Unimodular normalisation to "standard" triangles before any casework (Props. 8, 9, 13, pp. 19-21). (iv) An area argument: Area(D(π)) is affine in f (Prop. 12, p. 21), so it is maximised at a vertex, and Area < 1 means no covering and hence no minimality (Prop. 14, pp. 22-23). (v) For the implicitly defined general fill-in, an explicit piecewise upper-bound function φ̃ is built and shown to satisfy the symmetry and subadditivity conditions, so it coincides with the fill-in (proof of Theorem 5, pp. 27-29). (vi) Uniqueness of the minimal lifting plus extremality of π gives extremality of the pair (Corollary 1, pp. 18-19). The fill-in is also read as Balas-Jeroslow coefficient strengthening (p. 14).
  - Transferable: to prove that a function defined by an infimum is minimal, build an explicit upper bound that satisfies the minimality conditions; it must then equal the function (pp. 27-29).
- **D5 Experiments**: None; "Computational experiments are under way" (p. 29). Worked Example 1 runs the recipe on an explicit two-row instance: with a type-2 triangle the fill-in suffices; with a type-3 triangle one exact lifting lowers two coefficients (27/35 → 3/5 and 2/3 → 8/21), pp. 11-13. Example 2 gives a type-3 triangle with two distinct extreme liftings (pp. 23-24).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself among three derivations of the same GMI/MIR/split cuts and "pursue[s] the group relaxation approach" (p. 2). It builds on Gomory-Johnson and Johnson, and on ALWW, Borozan-Cornuéjols and Cornuéjols-Margot (pp. 2-3). Fill-in is credited to Gomory-Johnson and linked to Balas-Jeroslow (p. 14). It cites Dey-Richard's earlier two-row group facets (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: All results are stated in §3, "The Principal Results", and every proof is deferred to §§4-7 (roadmap p. 3; §3 pp. 8-11). Practical remarks follow the theorems ("of practical interest", p. 9). A figure of two one-row extreme functions, one of which is not even minimal on its continuous part, motivates the whole design (Fig. 1, p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory plus a cut-generation recipe. The authors list the limits plainly: "only a very special set of inequalities", yet their number is "huge", and how to select triangles or quadrilaterals is open (p. 29). Future work: lifting for type-3 triangles and quadrilaterals, using bounds on the integer variables, closed forms of the fill-in, higher dimensions (p. 29).

**Method links**: Method 3 variant ⚠: the same move as Method 3 (copy the structure of a successful lower-dimensional object into a harder class) but inside MILP, from one-row GMIC to two rows (pp. 3, 8). Method 4 evidence ✅: an iff characterisation (Theorem 2), a positive class (Theorem 3) paired with a negative class (Theorem 4) and an explicit non-uniqueness example (Example 2), pp. 8-9, 23-24. Method 1 variant ⚠: the paper starts from an empirical regularity (GMIC is the best single-row cut, p. 2), but the explanation is used as a design rule, not proved as a proxy theorem. New-pattern candidate: "relax, then add back one constraint at a time" (integer nonbasics are added back to the continuous model, p. 3).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn an infinite pointwise check into a covering condition on a geometric region, then use area to show where covering fails (pp. 15-23).
- Normalise by unimodular transformations to a few "standard" shapes before casework (pp. 19-21).
- Pair every characterisation theorem with a computed small example that walks through the cut-generation steps (pp. 11-13).
**Quotes**:
- "One possible explanation for this is the fact that the GMIC has the strongest coefficients for the continuous variables among all single row group inequalities." (p. 2)
- "As GMIC is known to be an effective single row general cut, this suggests trying to mimic the above property in the construction of inequalities for two row group problems." (p. 8)

### S016 · Constrained infinite group relaxations of MIPs (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2010, doi:10.1137/090754388)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-23, including Appendix 1, pp. 20-23).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Laurence A. Wolsey (CORE); Dey at Georgia Tech (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: circulated as CORE DP 33 (2009) (S079 ref. [21], p. 8); companion of S013. In S079's discussion column Cornuéjols calls it "even more surprising" than the lifting paper (S079 p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The continuous group relaxation drops the nonnegativity of the basic integer variables, yet in practice the basics are "typically non-negative but are not necessarily bounded" (p. 2). The paper asks which of the lattice-free results survive when the basics are restricted to S = P ∩ Z^m, S finite or infinite (p. 2), and lists four numbered "natural questions" (polyhedrality, the correspondence between sets and minimal functions, a facet bound, a two-dimensional classification), each mapped to a section (pp. 2-3).
  - Transferable: open a theory paper with the list of questions the known theory raises for the new model, one per section.
- **D2 Core idea**: Lattice-free sets are replaced by maximal S-free convex sets. The function π_K is built as the support function of a subset K° of the polar of K − f (Construction 3.1, p. 6); it can be read as a disjunctive cut, it is at most the gauge function, and it takes negative values on the interior of the recession cone (p. 6). Results: π_K is minimal and is the only minimal function with P(π) = K (Prop. 3.2, pp. 6-7); extremality on the infinite model is equivalent to extremality on a finite set T of generators (Prop. 3.6, p. 8); taking f in the interior loses nothing (pp. 9-10); a new invariant, the "order" t of K with respect to a formulation, bounds the facet count by 2^m − t (Def. 4.1, Prop. 4.4, pp. 10-13); and in R² the extreme cases are classified by facet count and order (Theorem 5.3, p. 16).
  - Transferable: define an integer invariant that interpolates between the known case (order 0 = lattice-free) and the new one, and let it carry the generalised bound.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: S is the set of integer points of a rational polyhedron with full-dimensional conv(S) (p. 2); W = R^m, no integer nonbasics (G = ∅), no bounds (T = +∞), p. 4. The polyhedrality of maximal S-free sets is proved only under technical conditions (K ∩ conv(S) full-dimensional; rational lineality space of the common recession cone), p. 5 and Prop. 6.4, p. 22; the authors note that Basu et al. later removed them (pp. 2, 5). Boundedness of K ∩ conv(S) is argued to lose no generality (p. 15).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Polar/support-function construction with a separation-theorem argument that P(π_K) = K (p. 6). A finite generator set T built from the cones C^j, with extremality shown by linear independence of tight points (Prop. 3.6, p. 8; Props. 5.2, 5.4, 5.8, pp. 15-18; the determinant in (5.4), p. 18). Facets are added one at a time to turn an S-free set into a lattice-free one, which gives the facet bound (Lemma 4.3, pp. 12-13). Non-extremality is shown by perturbing a facet in two directions (K1(ε), K2(ε)) and writing π_K as their average (pp. 15, 17). Polyhedrality uses compactness (Heine-Borel) on the unit sphere of the recession cone (Prop. 6.3, pp. 21-22), "essentially the same steps as used in Lovász" (p. 20).
  - Transferable: to prove that a cut is not extreme, perturb the defining set in two opposite directions so that the cut is the midpoint of two valid cuts (pp. 15, 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no computation). Example 1.2 gives an explicit S, an explicit π and the resulting facet 3y1 − 0.5y2 + 0.5y3 + 1.5y4 ≥ 1 of a small MIP (p. 3).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends ALWW, Borozan-Cornuéjols, Cornuéjols-Margot and Zambelli (pp. 1-2). Johnson [20] is credited with the finite-S case and a similar construction; the paper presents its own result as the converse (pp. 3, 6). Basu et al. are credited with the general version (pp. 2, 5). The acknowledgements thank Ellis Johnson for references and Basu, Cornuéjols, Conforti and Zambelli for corrections to two statements (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing**: One example front-loads every new phenomenon (negative coefficients, an S-free set, three facets, order 1), with bullets that forward-reference the sections (pp. 3-4). The intro states the version change: the general result was verified by others "since the original submission" (p. 2). A referee's observation is credited in the main text (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory. The authors name the main gap: integer nonbasic variables (G ≠ ∅) are not handled, although "almost all practical instances" have them (p. 19). They sketch a Balas-Jeroslow monoid argument for a valid φ, which requires solving a MIP "which may not always be efficiently solvable", and leave open when φ is efficient and strongest (p. 19).

**Method links**: Method 4 evidence ✅: Theorem 5.3 is an iff classification with conditions for each facet count, and the paper shows that a two-facet set of order 2 "cannot yield an extreme inequality" (p. 16; Example 4.2, p. 11). Method 3 variant ⚠: Johnson's 1981 finite-S facet theory is redone in the modern lattice-free language, but the work stays within MILP. Workflow E checkpoint evidence ✅ (a version change stated in the text, p. 2). New-pattern candidates: "relax, then add back one constraint at a time" (constraints on the basics are added back, p. 2); "prove on the all-columns infinite model, then transfer to finite" (Prop. 3.6, p. 8).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Reduce extremality on an infinite model to a finite set of generator columns, then use linear independence of tight points (pp. 8, 15-18).
- Bound the complexity of a new family by repeatedly adding a hyperplane until a known family is reached (pp. 12-13).
- In the intro, state which of your results were since generalised by others (p. 2).
**Quotes**:
- "Since the original submission of this paper, Basu et al. [7] have verified that the above result is true in general without requiring any technical conditions." (p. 2)
- "The referee made the following very important observation." (p. 16)

### S019 · On the extreme inequalities of infinite group problems (Mathematical Programming 2010, doi:10.1007/s10107-008-0229-6)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-25; the references on pp. 25-26 were not read). The proofs in §3 (pp. 15-19) were skimmed where the extracted formulas are fragmented.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jean-Philippe P. Richard, Yanjun Li, Lisa A. Miller (Dey, Richard and Li at Purdue, Miller at Minnesota, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the Purdue PhD period; it cites Dey's PhD thesis for the merit-index computations (p. 21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap in the theory. Every function proven extreme for the infinite group problem is continuous; several discontinuous minimal functions (Letchford-Lodi, Dash-Günlük, Richard-Li-Miller) are known, but whether they are extreme is open, and the existing tools need continuity (p. 4). A second open point is how finite and infinite group problems relate (pp. 1, 4).
  - Transferable: when every known example of a class has a property your tools assume, ask whether the property is necessary, and build the tool that does without it.
- **D2 Core idea**: Gomory-Johnson's Interval Lemma assumes continuity. It is replaced by a version that needs only nonnegativity near 0, obtained from Aczél's result on Cauchy's functional equation (Prop. 2, Corollary 1, p. 6). The Continuity Theorem then says: if φ is piecewise linear, linear with positive slope near 0, and a convex combination of two subadditive valid functions, those functions are continuous wherever φ is (Theorem 2, p. 7). Extremality then reduces to the classical Interval Lemma (p. 8). Consequences: (a) facets of the finite group problems on the subgroups C_{2^k n} give an extreme interpolation (Theorem 6, p. 10), which proves the Aráoz et al. three-slope functions extreme (Example 1, pp. 10-11); (b) sufficient conditions for the limit of extreme functions to be extreme (Theorem 7, pp. 13-14); (c) three discontinuous extreme families for PI(r,1) and one for PI(r,2) (pp. 14-22); (d) extreme functions of the mixed-integer group problem are always continuous (Theorem 13, Corollary 2, p. 25).
  - Transferable: when a key lemma needs a regularity property you do not have, look in functional-equation theory for the weakest hypothesis that still forces linearity.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The pure-integer infinite group problem PI(r,m) on the torus I^m with a modulo-1 metric (pp. 3, 5). Theorem 2 needs φ piecewise linear with positive slope at 0 (p. 7). Theorem 7 needs piecewise-linear continuous extreme f_i, a finite positive right derivative at 0, and finite subgroups containing the breakpoints (pp. 13-14). κ is extreme only for r < 1/3 (Theorem 9, p. 18). Rational breakpoints are needed for the Theorem 7 route, but direct proofs cover irrational ones (pp. 14-15).
- **D4 Proof devices**: The functional-equation lemma (pp. 5-6). The subadditivity squeeze: an equality for φ = ½(φ1 + φ2) forces the same equality for both parts (eq. (2), p. 7; eq. (12), p. 16). An ε-δ contradiction for continuity (p. 7). Agreement on a dense subgroup plus continuity gives identity (Theorem 6 proof, p. 10). A counterexample shows that the limit of extreme functions need not be extreme (Example 2, pp. 11-12), and the paper later diagnoses which condition of Theorem 7 it violates (p. 14). Mixed-integer continuity follows from bounded directional derivatives plus subadditivity (Lemmas 1-2, pp. 24-25). A two-dimensional extension sets φ(x,y) = π(P(x+y)) (Prop. 6, p. 22).
  - Transferable: after proving sufficient conditions, go back to your own counterexample and show exactly which condition it violates (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: No MIP experiments. The Gomory-Johnson merit index is computed for the ψ_p family: it is convex in p, jumps at the GMIC endpoint, and is largest at the discontinuous limit (Fig. 8, p. 21). The general pattern was "observed" for "all sequences of extreme functions that we have considered" but is "difficult to prove in general" (p. 20). The paper notes that the shooting experiment cannot evaluate discontinuous functions because they cannot be replicated on a finite grid (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Gomory-Johnson and Johnson and settles the status of earlier discontinuous valid functions (pp. 1-2, 4). New families are hedged: "To the best of our knowledge, this family ... has not been presented before" (pp. 10, 16), and ζ "has not been previously described" (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing**: Strong claim words in the abstract: "for the first time" (p. 1), "Surprisingly" (p. 2). The tools section is presented as reusable, and the conclusion says the results "are general" and apply to continuous functions too (p. 25). Each tool is followed by an example of its use (Example 1, p. 10; Example 2, p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: tools plus new extreme families. The conclusion stresses the structural difference between pure and mixed infinite group relaxations (p. 25). Open: the monotonicity of the merit index along limits is not proved (p. 20). The background open question, whether all continuous extreme functions are piecewise linear, is left untouched (p. 4).

**Method links**: Method 4 evidence ✅: a negative example (the limit of extreme functions is not extreme) paired with sufficient conditions and a diagnosis of the failure (pp. 11-14); pure versus mixed as a boundary result (pp. 22, 25). Method 2 variant ⚠: a strength proxy (merit index) is computed over a family and the pattern is recorded as an observation, not a theorem (pp. 20-21); there is no ground-truth optimum, unlike Method 2. Method 1 variant ⚠: extremality and the merit index serve as the yardsticks of cut strength (p. 20, citing an empirical correlation shown by Gomory et al.); the later taste that questions facetness (Research Taste, warning sign 2) is not yet present. Heuristic 9 evidence ✅: one tool borrowed from a neighbouring field (Aczél's functional equations, p. 6). New-pattern candidate: "prove on the all-columns infinite model, then transfer to finite" (Theorem 6, p. 10).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace a continuity hypothesis by nonnegativity via Cauchy-equation results (p. 6).
- Prove a heredity lemma (parts of a convex combination inherit regularity) so that classical tools apply to non-regular objects (pp. 7-8).
- Build discontinuous objects as limits of known good ones, show by counterexample that limits can fail, then give sufficient conditions (pp. 11-14).
**Quotes**:
- "Surprisingly, we prove that the functions defining extreme inequalities for infinite group relaxations of mixed integer programs are continuous." (p. 2)
- "Further, most of the tools used in the past to prove that functions are extreme are not suitable to study these discontinuous functions." (p. 4)

### S018 · The split closure of a strictly convex body (Operations Research Letters 2011, doi:10.1016/j.orl.2011.02.002)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-10, including Appendix A, pp. 9-10, whose formula-heavy algebra was skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel Dadush, Juan Pablo Vielma (Dadush and Dey at Georgia Tech; Vielma at IBM and Pittsburgh, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the first item in this batch on nonlinear integer sets; it follows the same authors' Chvátal-Gomory closure paper [6] (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a MILP closure theorem. CG and split closures of rational polyhedra are rational polyhedra, and the CG closure of a strictly convex body was recently shown to be a rational polytope (abstract, p. 1). For a non-polyhedral C the paper argues that the right analogue is "a finite number of split disjunctions is sufficient to describe the split closure" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when moving a polyhedral theorem to convex sets, first restate what the theorem "really" says (finitely many disjunctions) so that it can survive outside polyhedra.
- **D2 Core idea**: Theorem 1.3: the split closure of a bounded strictly convex set with rational affine hull is finitely defined (p. 2). Corollary 1.5: the split closure preserves conic-quadratic representability (p. 2). Lemma 3.6 gives a closed-form conic quadratic split cut for an ellipsoid with no auxiliary variables (p. 6), and Example 3.8 shows a split closure of an ellipsoid in R³ that is not polyhedral (pp. 6-7); in R² the split closure is polyhedral (Corollary 3.2, p. 5). Proof idea: SC(C) ⊆ CGC(C) = T, a polytope; points of T on bd(C) are integral; the vertices of T inside int(C) have ε-balls inside C; a split disjunction can cut a ball only if it is thin, and only finitely many are (Lemma 2.5 and the proof, p. 4).
  - Transferable: prove finiteness of an infinite cut family by sandwiching it inside a closure already known to be finite and showing that only finitely many members can cut the remaining points (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: C bounded and strictly convex with rational affine hull (p. 2); reduced to the full-dimensional case by Hermite normal form (p. 4). Intersections of strictly convex sets with rational polyhedra are not covered; the authors explain why the proof breaks there (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Nested closures (SC ⊆ CGC ⊆ C, Lemma 2.4, p. 3); the thin-split ball lemma (Lemma 2.5, p. 4); Hermite normal form and the integer Farkas lemma to reduce dimension (Lemma 2.6, Theorem 2.7, p. 4); a planar case analysis showing at most two nontrivial split cuts per disjunction in R² (Lemma 3.1, p. 5); an explicit counterexample whose non-polyhedrality is certified by checking a finite list of candidate splits (p. 7); convexity and concavity arguments for the ellipsoid split cut (Appendix A, pp. 9-10).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a as numerical experiments. One step is computer-assisted: the splits with ‖π‖ ≤ 1/0.34, a finite list, are checked with "a simple Mathematica [10] program" to show that none separates the three test points (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Cook-Kannan-Schrijver and later polyhedrality proofs (p. 2) and builds on the authors' own CG-closure paper (p. 3). The closed form is motivated by efficiency: expressions without auxiliary variables "can result in more efficient cutting plane methods", citing Saxena-Bonami-Lee and Atamtürk-Narayanan (pp. 2-3).
- **D7 Writing**: A short note whose abstract states the positive and the negative result in one sentence (p. 1). It adds a remark on the weakest condition under which the proof works (p. 4) and thanks a referee for suggesting Corollary 3.2 (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory. Open: extending the result to strictly convex sets intersected with rational polyhedra, which would need a convex set T with two stated properties; "the existence of such set remains an open question" (p. 8). Observation: unlike the CG closure, some strictly convex sets have no polyhedral approximation with the same split closure (p. 8).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅: a MILP closure theorem is carried over to convex nonlinear integer sets, and the paper asks about representability (SOC-representability preserved), the same questions as Method 3 step 1's list (p. 2). Method 4 evidence ✅: the positive finiteness theorem comes with its boundary, polyhedral in R² but not in R³ (pp. 5-7), and with a named open extension (p. 8). Method 2 variant ⚠: a computer check over a provably finite candidate list completes a proof (p. 7). It also fits Research Taste 4 (stay SOC-representable, p. 2).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Reduce to full dimension with Hermite normal form before the main argument (p. 4).
- Close a counterexample by proving that only finitely many candidates matter, then checking them by computer (p. 7).
- Give closed-form cuts in the original variable space when a lifted representation exists only with auxiliary variables (p. 6).
