### S008 · Matrix minor reformulation and SOCP-based spatial branch-and-cut method for the AC optimal power flow problem (Mathematical Programming Computation 2018, DOI 10.1007/s12532-018-0150-9; arXiv:1703.03050)
**Read level**: full — arXiv v1 pp. 1–43 read, including Appendices A–C (pp. 34–38); the reference list (pp. 38–43) was read for positioning.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Burak Kocuk (student), X. Andy Sun (co-advisor) · **Position in trajectory**: final paper of the AC-OPF arc. It builds on their Strong SOCP paper (Oper. Res. 2016, ref [27]) and the SDP-inexactness paper (TPWRS 2016, ref [28]) (p. 41) and turns the root-node strong SOCP relaxation of [27] into a full spatial branch-and-cut (pp. 3–4).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is the gap between what SDP relaxations promise and what they deliver on hard instances. SDP relaxations were popular after IEEE cases up to 300 buses were solved exactly, but exactness needs restrictive conditions (resistive networks, virtual phase shifters, radial networks without generation lower bounds), primal recovery is hard when inexact, and SDP does not scale (p. 2). This "motivates researchers (including the present paper)" to pursue SOCP and LP relaxations (p. 3). The prior global method of Phan [43] is faulted for testing only IEEE instances "solvable in the root node" (p. 3). Problem statement: solve AC OPF to global optimality, i.e. near-optimal primal solutions plus high-quality dual bounds, on the hard NESTA archive (abstract p. 1; §1.2 p. 3; §5 p. 25).
  - Transferable technique: choose the benchmark on which the accepted method's dual bound is weak (NESTA rather than IEEE), and say explicitly why the easy benchmark proves nothing (p. 3, p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: After lifting, the only nonconvexity is rank(X) = 1 (p. 5–6). Prop. 3.1 replaces it by the condition that all 2×2 minors vanish and the diagonal is nonnegative (p. 7), which gives an exact minor-based reformulation (4) (p. 7). The minors are classified into three types (p. 8–9):
  1. Type 1, edge (principal) minors: the boundary of a rotated cone in R^4 (7);
  2. Type 2, 3-cycle minors: two bilinear equations in R^7 (9);
  3. Type 3, 4-cycle minors: two bilinear equations in R^8 (11).

  Types 2 and 3 are physically "voltage angle differences of edges in a cycle sum to zero" (p. 3), shown via the arctangent condition θj − θi = atan2(sij, cij) (Props. 3.7–3.8, pp. 15–16). What changed relative to the literature: [9, 10, 19] use only principal minors; this paper adds the non-principal ones (p. 3, p. 9). What did not change: the SDP constraint is deliberately avoided; everything is LP or SOC representable (p. 8, §3.3). The algorithm (§4, pp. 21–25) is: local solver for UB → cycle basis → optimization- and dual-based bound tightening → SOCP with edge cuts (EC), arctangent envelopes (AT) and separated cycle cuts (Algorithm 1, p. 23) → spatial branch-and-cut that branches on cij or sij of the line with the largest angle violation (44), bisection, best-bound node selection, local bound tightening and cuts only on cycles containing the branched line (Algorithm 2, pp. 23–24).
  - Transferable technique: when a lifted model's nonconvexity is a rank-one constraint, rewrite it as vanishing 2×2 minors, classify the minors by the index pattern they touch, and map each class to an object of the application's graph (edge, 3-cycle, 4-cycle) (pp. 7–9).
  - Transferable technique: do not add all cubic/quartic numbers of minors; triangulate a cycle basis into 3- and 4-subcycles and use the cycle relaxation only as a separation oracle (p. 13, Fig. 1 p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Rectangular AC OPF (1a)–(1g) with linear or convex quadratic costs, demand at every bus, voltage magnitudes near 1 p.u. (pp. 4–5). The relaxation machinery rests on bounds on cij, sij and angle bounds θij (37); the paper says the McCormick and arctangent constructions have the availability of such bounds as "one of the standing assumptions" (p. 19), which is why §3.8 (bound tightening) exists. The arctangent envelopes assume c > 0 (p. 16). §3.6 replaces complex matrices by real ones, citing Taylor [48] for "using real matrices is equivalent to complex matrices" (p. 18). No assumption is attached to the claim that the four edge-cut inequalities and the McCormick set S^M_a approximate the hulls well; it is backed only by "Our extensive experiments" (p. 11) and "Our extensive tests" (p. 13), with no reported measure. Parameters (T = 5, TC = 1, r1 = 2, r2 = 4, ε = 10^-3, B_max = 118) were set "After initial calibration" on the same instance family that is then reported (p. 14, p. 26).
  - Transferable technique: list the bounds every envelope depends on as a standing assumption and give a dedicated, parallelizable bound-tightening stage that produces them (pp. 19–21).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: Mostly constructive convexification, with the heavy cases in appendices.
  - Prop. 3.1 (p. 7): rank-one ⇔ zero 2×2 minors + nonnegative diagonal, via rank = size of the largest invertible submatrix and X = xx*.
  - Hull of the edge set K=_ij (pp. 10–11): conv(K=) = K≤ ∩ conv(K≥) by Tawarmalani–Richard [47] (13); conv(K≥) is a polytope because it is a polytope minus an open convex set (Hillestad–Jacobsen, Thm. 3.1, p. 10); its extreme points lie on the 32 edges of the 4-D box and are found by fixing three variables at bounds (Algorithms 3–5, pp. 35–36), giving the extended formulation (16) with up to 32 λ's (Prop. 3.2, p. 11).
  - Compact outer approximation (pp. 11–12): over-estimate the convex f = √(c²+s²) and under-estimate the concave g = √(cii cjj) by hyperplanes (Props. 3.3–3.5), giving the four edge cuts (18) (Prop. 3.6).
  - Theorem 3.2 (p. 12; proof App. C pp. 36–38): conv{Σ ai xi yi = 0, box} is SOC representable. Device: an extreme-point perturbation argument (move xi by ε and yj by δ in compensating directions, Cases 1a/1b/2/3, pp. 36–37) shows that in each pair of indices one of (xi, yi) or (xj, yj) sits at bounds (Cor. C.1, p. 37). So all but one product is fixed at a box vertex, each disjunct projects to Tα = {xy = α} ∩ box, whose hull is SOC representable (Prop. C.2, p. 38, stated without proof). The paper states the cost: disjunctions "exponential in N" (p. 12).
  - Arctangent envelopes (pp. 16–17, App. A pp. 34–35): planes through three of four box corners, shifted by the worst violation Δγ (29), computed exactly by enumerating KKT points: the Hessian of f is indefinite so the maximizer is on the boundary, two boundary segments are excluded because f is linear there, leaving four 1-D problems.
  - Separation (p. 19): for any conic-representable S = {x : ∃u, Ax + Bu ⪰_K b}, the most violated cut with ‖α‖∞ ≤ 1 is the dual program (36).
  - Dual-based bound tightening (p. 21): reuse the duals of each bounding SOCP to propagate other edges' improved bounds without re-solving ((41)–(42)).
  - Prop. 3.8's proof is omitted "due to its similarity" (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: to show conv of a bilinear equation over a box is conic representable, characterize its extreme points by a two-coordinate perturbation argument, then take the disjunction over which coordinate is free (App. C pp. 36–38).
  - Transferable technique: make any piecewise-smooth envelope valid by computing the exact maximal violation of a plane through the corners, using second-order conditions to push the maximizer to a few 1-D boundary pieces (App. A pp. 34–35).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: NESTA 0.3.0, Typical, Congested and Small Angle Operating Conditions, 3 to 2746 buses (pp. 25, 27–29). Code in C#, MOSEK 8 for all LP/SOCP/SDP subproblems, Intel Core i5 2.50 GHz, 16 GB (p. 25). Baselines: plain SOCP; SDP via the sparsity-exploiting OPF Solver of Madani–Ashraphijuo–Lavaei, modified by the authors to include angle-difference constraints (45) and run under both MOSEK and SDPT3; comparisons use SDPT3 because it is more accurate (1.61% vs 1.98% average gap) though slower (pp. 25–26, p. 30); and "Best of [27]", their own earlier strong SOCP settings (p. 26). Metric: %gap = 100 (zUB − zLB)/zUB against a MATPOWER local solution (p. 26). Three variants SEP(M), SEP(S), SEP(M,S) (pp. 25–26). Reporting:
  - per-instance tables with time (Tables 1–3, pp. 27–29) and an average gap/time scatter (Fig. 2, p. 30);
  - branch-and-cut with 15 and 30 minute budgets, per instance and averaged by operating condition (Tables 4–5, p. 32);
  - a cumulative curve of the fraction of instances solved up to a given gap (Fig. 3, p. 33).

  Results read off the tables: SEP(S) and SEP(M,S) give about half the SDP average gap at lower average time (p. 30, p. 33). But on Typical instances the SDP average gap (0.35) is below every proposed variant (0.53–1.09) (Table 1, p. 27), so the dominance holds on the class average, as the authors' own summary sentence on p. 30 concedes. Root averages 1.49/0.97/0.96 fall to 1.21/0.78/0.87 after 15 minutes of branching (p. 31). Negative results are reported: [27] is 2–3 times worse in gap (p. 30) and "not very competitive" with SDP on easy instances (p. 31); Chen et al.'s inequalities gave no extra gap closure and were dropped (p. 12); plain McCormick of cycles was inaccurate, which motivated discretization (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: when a competitor's relaxation code lacks a constraint your model has, add it to their code, run it under two back-end solvers, and compare against the more accurate run (pp. 25–26, p. 30).
  - Transferable technique: report the relaxation comparison as a cumulative distribution of certified gaps across instances, so dominance (a curve everywhere below) is visible at a glance (Fig. 3, p. 33; explanation p. 31).
- **D6 Positioning**: Three-part map of the literature: local methods, relaxations, global methods (pp. 2–3). Prior work is criticized directly but narrowly: SDP exactness conditions "quite restrictive" (p. 2); Phan's global method only on root-solvable instances (p. 3); [9, 10, 19] use only principal minors (p. 3, p. 9). Strong SOCP relaxations are cited as "not dominated by SDP relaxations" (p. 3). The paper is equally direct about its own predecessor [27]: faster but 2–3 times worse in gap (p. 30), and not competitive with SDP on easy instances (p. 31). Ideas are credited to the pooling literature (discretization, [35, 14, 18], p. 14) and to LP-based global solvers (dual bound tightening, [45], p. 21).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Contribution claim in the abstract and §1.2 is computational: the algorithm "significantly outperforms the state-of-the-art SDP-based OPF solver" and reaches "on average a 0.71% optimality gap in no more than 720 seconds" (p. 1, p. 4). Table 5 shows the source: 0.71% is SEP(S)'s 30-minute average gap and 719.26 s its average run time over runs capped at 30 minutes (p. 32; many runs end near 1800 s, Table 4 p. 32). So "no more than 720 seconds" reads as a bound but is an average (conclusion p. 33 says "on the average"). Hedged accuracy claims ("Our extensive tests show", p. 13) carry no reported number. The physical reading of each algebraic object (minors ↔ cycles, p. 3) is placed in the introduction before any mathematics. The v1 text has formula typos: in (11) the first product is written XijXlk − XijXlj (p. 9), and Prop. 3.8 contains "θl − θl" (p. 16). Section roadmap at the end of the introduction (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution layers: a reformulation theorem (Prop. 3.1), two convex-hull results (Prop. 3.2, Thm. 3.2), envelope and cut families, bound tightening, and a complete SOCP-based spatial branch-and-cut with computational evidence (pp. 3–4, p. 33). Limits are stated concretely: SOCPs have no efficient warm start, so each node is rebuilt from scratch with explicit tree handling, which "may" cause issues on large problems (p. 25); exact hulls are too large to use (p. 11, p. 12). Future work: multi-period AC OPF, optimal transmission switching, and an LP outer-approximation branch-and-cut that trades strength for warm starts and more nodes (pp. 33–34). Not stated as a limit, but visible: on the five largest Small Angle instances the 30-minute SEP(S) gaps stay at 2.50–4.56% (Table 4, p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the cheapest relaxation that can be made strong (LP/SOC only, "avoid the SDP constraint", p. 8), strengthened with structure (edge cuts, arctangent envelopes, cycle cuts; §3 pp. 9–19), gap certified against a local solution (p. 26) on a stated PC (p. 25), and closed with their own spatial branch-and-cut (step 6; §4 pp. 21–25). The limitation paragraph of Method 5 is also visible here: SDP keeps the better gap on Typical instances (Table 1, p. 27), and §3.6 rests on the real/complex equivalence cited from [48] (p. 18) that the Method 5 limitation records as later contested.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: "the only nonconvex constraint is a requirement that the rank of the matrix variable is one" (p. 5–6). The rank-one condition is split into small recurring substructures (edge set K=, bilinear set Sa, cycle sets QC and SC), and these sets are convexified jointly, not term by term (pp. 9–18). This is the OPF precursor of the 2019+ rank-one QCQP programme.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: only step 5 appears: the cost of the constructive hull is stated (32 λ's, p. 11; disjunctions exponential in N, p. 12), and a compact outer approximation is used instead. There are no tight examples or hypothesis-dropping results: in a computational paper the boundary is drawn by the instance tables, not by theorems.
- Heuristic 2 evidence (strongest baseline: competitor code modified, two solvers, more accurate run tabulated, pp. 25–26, p. 30) and Heuristic 3 evidence (dead ends in one sentence with the reason, p. 12, p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: **exact hull for the theorem, few-inequality outer approximation for the code** (Prop. 3.2 → four edge cuts, pp. 10–12; Thm. 3.2 → McCormick S^M_a + discretization, pp. 12–14).
- New-pattern candidate: **algebra-to-physics dictionary** (each class of 2×2 minors ↔ an edge/3-cycle/4-cycle with angle-sum meaning, p. 3, pp. 8–9, pp. 15–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Rank-one ⇒ vanishing 2×2 minors ⇒ classify by index pattern ⇒ convexify each small class over a box (pp. 7–13).
- Hull of a box minus an open convex set: enumerate the box's edges, intersect, take the convex hull (Thm. 3.1 + Algorithms 3–5, pp. 10, 35–36).
- Conic separation with ℓ∞-normalized multipliers as a generic cut generator for any LP/SDP-representable outer set (36) (p. 19).
- Reuse bounding-problem duals to propagate bound improvements across edges without re-solving (41)–(42) (p. 21).
- Branch on the edge variable whose relaxation violates the physical (angle) condition the most (44), bisect, re-tighten nearby edges only (pp. 23–24).

**Quotes**:
- "In general, we should note that our approaches are much faster on large instances, more accurate on hard instances, and comparable to the SDP relaxation on small or easy instances." (p. 30)
- "Strong SOCP relaxations from our previous paper [27] improves the plain SOCP relaxation considerably but it is not very competitive against the SDP relaxation, especially for easier instances." (p. 31)

### S011 · Theoretical challenges towards cutting-plane selection (Mathematical Programming 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10107-018-1302-4; arXiv:1805.02782)
**Read level**: full — arXiv v1 body pp. 1–22 read in full; the reference list (212 entries, pp. 22–36) was skimmed for self-citations and coverage.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Marco Molinaro (PUC-Rio) · **Position in trajectory**: the 2018 review that closes the sparse-cut line (it summarizes his papers [112], [113], [116], [117] and [56] with Bodur, Del Pia, Molinaro and Pokutta, pp. 11–20) and opens the branch-and-bound/learning line through its "need to ask different questions" section (p. 7) and its machine-learning question (p. 21). It is a survey with the authors' own results, not a primary research article.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a solver practice that works without a theory. Many cut classes exist, but "our scientific understanding is far from complete" on selecting which cuts to add at a node (abstract p. 1). An anecdote frames it: general-purpose cuts were "mostly of theoretical interest" until Balas–Ceria–Cornuéjols–Natraj made them work in the mid-90s, largely by selecting cuts carefully (pp. 1–2, p. 7–8). The real target (solver performance) cannot be analysed directly, so proxies are needed (p. 4). The review then argues that the classical proxies do not address what selection needs (§1.2, pp. 7–10).
  - Transferable technique: open a theory review with the historical moment when practice changed for a non-theoretical reason (cuts "used selectively"), then ask what theory would have predicted it (pp. 1–2, pp. 7–8).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Three moves.
  1. A taxonomy of cut sources: geometric/lattice-free, structured relaxations (single- and multi-row), subadditive, and extended-formulation/algebraic (pp. 2–4). Then a taxonomy of strength measures: finite cutting-plane algorithms, closure inclusion, rank, the blow-up (α-approximation) measure, and minimality/facetness (pp. 4–7).
  2. A list of the knobs a solver actually turns, each with what is known and the admission when nothing is known: effectiveness measures (depth of cut can rank a dominated cut higher, p. 8), cuts from multiple bases, parallelism ("no theoretical results/understanding", p. 9), how many cuts per round, facets vs minimal inequalities, sparsity, numerical safety and stability, instance-class dependence, and balancing goals (pp. 8–10).
  3. Two of their own lines presented as first steps: sparse cuts (§2, pp. 11–17) and aggregation (knapsack-relaxation) cuts (§3, pp. 17–20).
  - Transferable technique: for a design knob with no theory, write one paragraph each on what practitioners do, which papers measured it, and the explicit sentence "to the best of our knowledge" no study/theory exists. The list becomes a research agenda (pp. 8–10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Sparse-cut analysis: P ⊆ [0,1]^n, called "WLOG and just a normalization" (p. 11); worst case over polytopes with t vertices, distance measure d(P, P^k) compared with √n (pp. 11–12). Sparse formulations: packing problems with nonnegative A, b, c (p. 15), with the formulation's sparsity encoded in a packing interaction graph of column blocks (Def. 1, p. 15) and a support list (Def. 2, p. 15). Aggregation: "pre-processed" packing polyhedra 0 ≤ Aij ≤ bi (p. 18), sign-pattern IPs (p. 20). Each restriction is chosen to make a specific practical intuition provable (for example "loosely decomposable", p. 14). The authors flag the stylization: Theorem 1 "considers a very stylized situation" (p. 13), and worst-case families "could be very artificial" (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Theorem 1 (p. 12): d(P, P^k) ≤ min{(8√n/√k)√(2 log 4tn), 2√n(n/k − 1)}; tight for random 0/1 polytopes (p. 12). Proof sketch (pp. 12–13): take the separating direction d from the projection of u onto P and randomly sparsify it (coordinate i set to sign(di)/α with probability α|di|). Three properties (k-sparse; still valid up to λ/2 on every vertex; still separates u) hold with positive probability by Bernstein's inequality, a union bound over the t vertices, and Markov's inequality applied to n − D̃d.
  - Complement [112] (pp. 13–14): polytopes where sparse approximation stays poor even after adding many dense cuts, under every rotation, and in almost all directions.
  - Theorem 2 / Corollary 1 (pp. 16–17): the sparse closure is within the fractional (mixed) chromatic number of the interaction graph, independent of dimension and data. In the 2-stage stochastic case the factor is 2. Proof device: build two integer solutions from a stable-set cover of the block graph (first-stage block alone; all second-stage blocks together) and use the projection property of block-supported cuts (p. 17).
  - Lemma 2 (p. 19): if every relaxation in a family is an α-approximation of its own integer hull, adding all their cuts improves P by at most α. Device: the scaling characterization V ⊆ αU of α-approximation for packing sets. Theorem 3 (p. 18) follows because a pre-processed knapsack's LP is a 2-approximation (p. 19). Theorem 4 (p. 19) gives a family where 1-row closures are arbitrarily worse than the aggregation closure for general MILPs.
  - Specialization as exposition: "Since the definitions involved in this theorem are a mouthful" the paper proves the 2-SSP corollary instead of Theorem 2 (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: to show a sparse (or otherwise restricted) inequality nearly separates, randomly round the dense separating normal coordinate-wise with probabilities proportional to |di|, then check sparsity, validity and violation by concentration plus a union bound (pp. 12–13).
  - Transferable technique: to bound the gain of all cuts from a family of relaxations, bound each relaxation's own integrality gap and use Lemma 2 (pp. 18–19).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Almost none of their own. A computational check reported from [56] compares 1-row CG cuts with general CG cuts on random equality and market split instances: 1-row CG cuts are "significantly weaker" (p. 20, details deferred to [56]). Empirical context is borrowed: MIPLIB 2010 constraint-matrix density, 1.63% average and 0.17% median (p. 11); the dense-equality experiment of [202], about 25% slowdown with 9 dense rows (p. 11); computational studies showing triangle/quadrilateral cuts underperform split cuts despite better worst-case blow-up (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: when a worst-case separation result (Theorem 4) might be "very artificial", run the cheapest experiment that checks whether the gap appears on natural instance families, and say which way it came out (pp. 19–20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The review positions itself against the habit of judging cuts by worst-case strength alone. Worst-case results "only provide a coarse indication" (p. 10). Facetness is questioned because extreme inequalities are dense among minimal ones for the infinite group relaxation (p. 7). The depth-of-cut measure is shown to prefer dominated cuts (p. 8). Credit goes to practitioners and computational papers (Balas et al. [29, 30]; Andreello–Caprara–Fischetti [13]; Achterberg [4]; Crowder–Johnson–Padberg's sparsity remark [97], p. 14). Solver developers Achterberg, Salvagnin and Wunderling are thanked (p. 22). The authors' own works [56, 112, 113, 116, 117] are presented as "preliminary theoretical underpinning" (abstract p. 1).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Every one of the nine issues in §1.2 ends with an open question or a "to the best of our knowledge" gap statement (pp. 8–10). Own results are introduced with plain-language corollaries ("if the number of vertices is small, independent of the location of the vertices, using half sparsity cutting-planes allows us to approximate the integer hull very well", p. 12) and an honest hedge on the bound's scope (it "only captures the worst-case scenario", p. 12). A proof is distilled into a one-line design slogan in quotation marks: relaxations with small integrality gap may yield weak cuts (p. 18). §4 contains an explicit "word of caution" paragraph (p. 21) and then asks whether the whole programme is worth it, answering yes with an argument from hardware change (pp. 20–21). The paper ends on an open question about machine learning (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable technique: after each theorem, write one sentence that says what it means for the practical decision, and one that says where it stops (pp. 12, 20).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution level: survey + agenda + exposition of the authors' theory. Limits are stated concretely and strongly: the existing theory has "so far failed to directly help in actual cutting-plane selection" (p. 20); their own results are "very preliminary" and "we are very far from answering the questions raised in Section 1.2" (p. 21); a selection rule may help one code and fail in another, and dual bounds are not every user's top priority (p. 21). Concrete take-aways are offered anyway: for structured sparse packing IPs, select aggregation cuts from single original constraints, and use the formulation's sparsity pattern to choose cut supports (p. 21). Future directions: theory for parallelism, number of cuts and stability (pp. 9–10), the reverse-rank question (p. 10), and machine learning for cut selection working "in concert" with theory (pp. 21–22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the solver habit (cut selection, sparse cuts) is the object of study. Solver complexity rules out a direct analysis, so "proxy measures are required" (p. 4). The two-sided sparsity trade-off is cut to the one-sided question of how much gap is lost with only sparse cuts (p. 11). Step 7 appears in its report-the-mismatch form: the theory has not helped selection yet (p. 20), and the results are "very preliminary" (p. 21). This is the 2018 source of the Inner Tension *theory for solvers vs I do not know*.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: Theorem 1 is tight for random 0/1 polytopes (p. 12) and is paired with lower-bound polytopes [112] (pp. 13–14). Positive and negative instance classes are paired (packing/covering: aggregation adds at most a factor 2, Thm. 3; general MILPs: arbitrarily large gain, Thm. 4; sign-pattern IPs in between, pp. 18–20). Tight examples exist for the sparse-formulation bounds (p. 17).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (weak): the only ground-truth comparison is a measurement against the full CG closure (1-row vs general CG, p. 20). The review does not build a stripped instrument.
- Research Taste warning 2 and Anti-pattern *Facetness or depth of cut as proxies* ✅: facet density casts doubt on facetness (p. 7); depth of cut may rank weaker cuts higher (p. 8). Taste warning 3 ✅: "miserably fails" with other codes (p. 21). Inner Tension *tight worst case vs worst case is coarse* ✅: "only provide a coarse indication" (p. 10), against the tight worst-case Theorem 1 in the same paper (p. 12).
- Heuristic 4 evidence: section 1.2 is titled "the need to ask different questions" (p. 7) and turns the obstacles of the strength-measure literature into the next questions.
- New-pattern candidate: **proof distilled into a selection slogan** (Lemma 2 → "relaxations with small integrality gap may yield weak cuts", pp. 18–19).
- New-pattern candidate: **review as agenda** (known theory → list of solver knobs, each with a no-theory-known sentence → own results as first steps → word of caution → ML question, pp. 4–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Randomized sparsification of a separating normal, with Bernstein + union bound + Markov, to prove sparse-cut approximation bounds (pp. 12–13).
- Encode formulation sparsity as a block interaction graph and bound restricted-support closures by a (fractional) chromatic number via stable-set decompositions (pp. 15–17).
- The scaling (blow-up) characterization of α-approximation for packing/covering sets as a ready-made strength measure that compares closures through objective ratios (p. 6, p. 19).
- Pair a cuts-help-little theorem on a structured class with a cuts-help-arbitrarily-much family on the general class, and test the general-class gap on natural instances (pp. 18–20).

**Quotes**:
- "given the complexity of solvers one cannot expect to be able to perform a theoretical analysis of this performance metric directly, and thus proxy measures are required." (p. 4)
- "these results have so far failed to directly help in actual cutting-plane selection." (p. 20)
