## 1. Outer approximation (1994) and its theory

Era and resources: Dundee PhD work, Fortran codes, small test sets, no mature MIQP solver for the quadratic master problem (§1.4). The critics write 15 to 30 years later, with CPLEX- and Gurobi-era MILP solvers, MINLPLib with hundreds of convex instances, and conic solvers.

### 1.1 A characterization theorem stated too strongly (judgement shown wrong · layer 5)

- **Critic and source.** Bonami, Biegler, Conn, Cornuéjols, Grossmann, Laird, Lee, Lodi, Margot, Sawaya, Wächter, IBM Research Report RC23771 (4 Nov 2005) [src 2]; journal version Discrete Optim. 5(2) (2008) 186-204, DOI 10.1016/j.disopt.2006.10.011. [observed · primary]
- **What they say** (report p. 4; their ref. [10] is Fletcher and Leyffer 1994): "Theorem 1 is weaker than Theorem 1 in [10], where it is incorrectly stated that (under the conditions of Theorem 1) [a point] is optimal for P if and only if it is optimal for [the OA master MILP]." and "Indeed, although all optimal solutions of P are optimal solutions of [the OA master MILP], the converse is not true as the following example shows." Their Example 1 (pp. 4-5) is an MINLP over a ball in R^3 with one integer variable; a variable that the linearizations leave unconstrained makes infeasible points optimal for the MILP. The same sentence, in the journal's notation, appears in a Semantic Scholar citation context of the 2008 journal version [src 42], so the correction survived into print.
- **What they did not dispute.** The finite-termination result stands (p. 5): "As shown by Theorem 2 in [10], the algorithm converges to an optimal solution of P in a finite number of iterations provided that assumptions on convexity, differentiability and constraint qualifications of Theorem 1 hold." [observed · primary]
- **Answered? Yes, by acknowledgement and absorption.** 2017 (OS Today, p. 5): "Later, Bonami et al. corrected our convergence proof [4], and Pierre developed a much nicer example that showed that outer approximation can take an exponential number of iterations [26]." [src 23; stated · primary]. The 2013 survey he coauthored states the corrected version and credits the example: "We can show that (3.6) and (1.2) have the same optimal value and that any solution of (1.2) is an optimal solution of (3.6). However, the converse is not true, as the following example from Bonami et al. (2008) shows:" (preprint p. 29), and keeps the original termination proof: "A proof of Theorem 3.2 was given by Fletcher and Leyffer (1994)." (p. 31) [src 11; stated · primary, coauthored].
- [inferred] What was wrong is an equivalence statement (an "if and only if"), not the finite-termination argument. Leyffer's phrase "corrected our convergence proof" is broader than what Bonami et al. wrote (Contradiction C3).

### 1.2 Worst-case iteration count: exponential (limit of applicability · layer 5)

- **Critic and source.** Hijazi, Bonami, Ouorou, "An Outer-Inner Approximation for separable MINLPs", preprint (Optimization Online 2012/08/3581) [src 3]; INFORMS J. Comput. 26(1) (2014) 31-44, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1120.0545. [observed · primary]
- **What they say.** "The problem (sMINLP) is a simple form of MINLP and one could expect outer approximation to perform well on it. We show that this is not the case and even a problem with one quadratic constraint can require an exponential number of iterations to be solved by outer approximation." (preprint p. 2). Journal abstract: "As a side result, we exhibit a simple example where a classical implementation of the outer approximation would take an exponential number of iterations, whereas it is easily solved with our modifications." Their remedy is an extended (univariate, separable) formulation plus an inner approximation.
- **Relation to his own result.** The 1994 abstract already gave an example "for which it visits all integer assignments" (01:F1). Hijazi et al. turn this into an exponential count on a problem with a single constraint and show that reformulation removes it.
- **Answered? Yes, adopted.** Survey: "In practice, outer approximation often works efficiently. However, Hijazi et al. (2010) provide an example where outer approximation takes an exponential number of iterations." (p. 31). The survey also adopts the global-optimization school's remedy: "Tawarmalani and Sahinidis (2005) give an example that shows that the outer approximation (3.21) is tighter than (3.20). Moreover, for the two outer approximations to be equivalent, we may require an exponentially larger number of linearization points x(l) in (3.20) compared with (3.21)." (p. 40) [src 11; stated · primary, coauthored]. 2017: "a much nicer example" (§1.1). He also teaches it: in Lecture 29 of his January 2024 IIT Bombay course ("Exploiting Separability for Cuts & Presolve"; YouTube auto-captions, not checked against the audio) he walks through the example, "because there's two to the N of these you have a counter example that outo approximation visits all 2 to the N vertices okay so the authors who did this also studied this in a more general setting" ("outo" is the captioning's error), and presents the remedy: "separating out in this way um is actually stronger it can be shown to be tighter" [src 56; stated · primary, ASR transcript].

### 1.3 Standing assumptions: constraint qualifications and exact NLP solves (blind spot · layer 5)

- **Critic and source.** Erik Tamm and Jan Kronqvist, "Separation, Constraint Qualifications, and Cycling in Outer Approximation", arXiv 2606.26897v1 (25 Jun 2026) [src 4]. [observed · primary]
- **What they say.** Abstract: "While the algorithm is well established in theory and practice, certain assumptions underlying its convergence are rarely discussed in the literature. In this paper, we examine two such assumptions: that a constraint qualification holds at the optimal solution of each nonlinear programming subproblem, and that these subproblems can be solved exactly." p. 2: "The issues of constraint qualifications and non-exact solutions are not commonly discussed in the existing literature, but they can lead to convergence failure. The assumption of a constraint qualification is usually included without much discussion, and the exactness of the solution is rarely mentioned." On the 1994 paper (p. 6): "In Fletcher & Leyffer [9], on the other hand, the assumption is that some constraint qualification holds, with Abadie's constraint qualification given as an example." and "For more general constraint qualifications, like Abadie's condition, checking whether they are satisfied is often computationally intractable in practical applications." Their fix, an extended-cutting-plane fallback when cycling is detected, "yields a finitely convergent algorithm under relaxed assumptions regarding constraint qualifications, thereby generalizing the convergence theory of the outer approximation algorithm." (abstract).
- **Answered?** Not yet; the preprint is three and a half months old. [observed]

### 1.4 The quadratic master problem (limit of applicability, era-bound · layers 3-4)

- **Critic and source.** Kronqvist, Bernal, Grossmann, preprint dated 14 Dec 2017 [src 5]; Math. Program. 180(1-2) 285-310 (online 2018), DOI 10.1007/s10107-018-1356-3. [observed · primary]
- **What they say** (their ref. [20] is Fletcher and Leyffer 1994; [ε] marks the Greek letter lost by the text layer): "To avoid this, the method presented in [20] uses an [ε] improvement strategy, where the next solution must reduce the linearly approximated objective by a small [ε]-value." Then: "With this approach [ε] must be chosen smaller than the desired optimality gap. Thus, it will only result in a small reduction requirement. Therefore, the quadratic outer approximation method in [20] will rely heavily on the second order approximation of the Lagrangean. In case the approximation point [...] with the corresponding multipliers [...] is not the optimal solution to the MINLP, then the Lagrangean might not give a good approximation of the original problem and this might cause slow convergence." (p. 12). And: "A similar quadratic approach was presented in [20]. However, the level constraint used in the level method provides a more robust way of enforcing an improvement and avoiding cycling." (p. 2). They still rely on the 1994 paper for a lemma: "For more details see [20] Lemma 1 page 331." (p. 16).
- **Era-bound reason.** Su, Tang, Bernal, Grossmann [src 6] (submitted version of Comput. Chem. Eng. 109 (2018) 77-95, DOI 10.1016/j.compchemeng.2017.10.011), p. 11: "One drawback of this approach is that at the time there was no software available to efficiently solve MIQP problems (Fletcher and Leyffer, 1994)." Grossmann's 2002 review [src 7] (Optim. Eng. 3(3) 227-252, DOI 10.1023/A:1021039126272), preprint p. 13: "Also, if the function f(x,y) is nonlinear in y, and y is a general integer variable, Fletcher and Leyffer (1994) have shown that the original OA algorithm may require a much larger number of iterations to converge than when the master problem (M-MIQP) is used. This, however, comes at the price of having to solve an MIQP instead of an MILP." [observed · primary]
- **Answered?** No reply found. His thesis had already reported numerical trouble with the quadratic code (round-off growth in the QP solver bqpd; 04-mentorship §1.2). [practice · primary, via note 04]

### 1.5 Differentiability and the nonsmooth extension (limit of applicability)

- Lubin, Yamangil, Bent, Vielma, arXiv 1511.06710v3 (IPCO 2016, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-33461-5_9) [src 15], p. 2: "With the recent development of conic solvers supporting nonsymmetric cones [27,26], it may be advantageous to use these solvers over derivative-based NLP solvers, in which case the standard convergence theory for outer approximation algorithms no longer applies." They claim (abstract) "the first outer approximation algorithm with finite-time convergence guarantees" for mixed-integer conic problems. [observed · primary]
- Wei, Ali, arXiv 1502.06315 (J. Glob. Optim. 63(2) (2015) 213-227, DOI 10.1007/s10898-015-0284-5) [src 18], p. 2, describe the 1994 paper as covering "convex MINLPs with convex and continuously differentiable objective constraint functions" and add: "It is noted that differentiability of functions plays an important role in the construction of relaxation and is proved to be an important matter for allowing to solve these relaxed subproblems efficiently." Their contribution is OA with "subgradients obtained from KKT conditions" (abstract). [observed · primary]
- Wei, Liu, Zeng, arXiv 2602.04122 (2026) [src 19], p. 3: "Fletcher and Leyffer [15] in 1994 advanced the method to handle convex MINLPs with continuously differentiable functions, also considering a non-smooth extension within a penalty function context." [observed · primary]
- de Oliveira, TOP 24(3) (2016) 665-692, DOI 10.1007/s11750-016-0413-4 [src 20], S2 context [secondary]: "The outer-approximation method presented in [10,15] and [12] require differentiability of both functions f and c in order to efficiently solve the problem". Which of [10], [12], [15] is the 1994 paper was not checked.
- The 1994 abstract (Springer page, fetched in this run) places its nonsmooth theory inside the exact-penalty alternative: "An alternative approach is considered to the difficulties caused by infeasibility in outer approximation, in which exact penalty functions are used to solve the NLP subproblems. It is possible to develop the theory in an elegant way for a large class of nonsmooth MINLPs based on the use of convex composite functions and subdifferentials, although an interpretation for the l 1 norm is also given." [stated · primary]
- [inferred] Wei, Liu, Zeng describe this accurately; Wei and Ali leave it out and describe the paper as differentiable-only; de Oliveira (S2 context) treats OA as requiring differentiability (Contradiction C4). No source I read says the 1994 nonsmooth claim is wrong. The general nonsmooth OA with KKT-based subgradients was built later by others; Eronen, Mäkelä, Westerlund 2014 is the earliest such treatment these authors name, and it was not read (Gaps).
- **Answered?** No reply found.

## 2. Nonlinear branch-and-bound, the SQP integration (2001) and the MINLP software

Era and resources: MINLP_BB and filterSQP were Fortran codes he co-owned and did not open-source; Bonmin (COIN-OR, 2005 on) was open and built on Ipopt; FilMINT was built on MINTO; Minotaur (2010s) was written mainly by a postdoc and later his students (01 §1.8). The 2019 benchmark ran on a desktop with CPLEX through GAMS, AIMMS and JuMP.

### 2.1 The warm-start premise (contested · layers 4-5)

- **Critic and source.** Bonami et al. 2005 [src 2]. p. 8 (their [19] is Leyffer 2001): "The same applies to NLPs, to some extent, when they are solved using active set algorithms [19]. Nevertheless, we chose the interior point NLP solver Ipopt as our solver, as a suitable open source active-set NLP solver was unavailable." p. 15: "Since in those two algorithm the bulk of the time is spent solving NLPs, this indicates that the warm-starting advantage of the active set solver used by SBB over the interior point solver used by I-BB does not confer any advantage in terms of computing time in our test set." p. 14: "It is clear from the plot that I-Hyb dominates I-BB and I-OA." [observed · primary] [inferred] SBB ran with CONOPT, not filterSQP, so this is evidence against the warm-start premise of the 2001 paper, not a test of MINLP_BB itself.
- **Licensing as a limit on uptake.** Bonami et al. chose Ipopt because no open active-set NLP solver was available (above). In the 2018 benchmark filterSQP appears as the NLP subsolver of GAMS/SBB, listed as "filterSQP20010817" (preprint Table 1) [src 8]. [observed · primary]
- **Answered? In several ways, not all consistent.**
  - Coauthored 2011 preprint [src 10]: "In general, active sets methods have better warm-starting properties and therefore seem preferable." (p. 3), but also "Unfortunately, the efficiency of the dual simplex method for hot-starting LPs does not have an analogue for general convex NLPs." (p. 10). Their remedy: strong branching on hot-started QP approximations with BQPD. [stated · primary, coauthored]
  - Survey [src 11]: "Though some attempts have been made to use warm-starts in MINLP, see (Bonami et al., 2011; Mahajan et al., 2012), they generally have not been as successful in MINLP, mainly because the Jacobian and Hessian matrix change from one node to another so that factorizations are always outdated." (p. 24), and "One drawback of nonlinear branch-and-bound is that it requires the solution of a large number of NLPs that cannot be easily hot-started (unlike MILP where we can reuse the LP basis factors after branching)." (p. 28). [stated · primary, coauthored]
  - QP-diving [src 12] (Mahajan, Leyffer, Kirches, ANL/MCS-P2071-0312, 26 Mar 2012): "Our numerical experience shows that the new algorithm allows us to exploit warm-starting techniques from quadratic programming, resulting in a reduction in solve times for convex MINLPs by orders of magnitude on some classes of problems." The survey repeats it with the same qualifier: "Numerical experience shows that this approach can reduce the CPU time of branch-and-bound by several orders of magnitude for some problems." (p. 26). [stated · primary, coauthored]
  - **Failure or abandonment to record.** QP-diving never appeared in a journal: no DBLP or Crossref record (checked 2026-10-10). The 2020 Minotaur preprint still cites it as preprints only: its ref. [58] is "Solving convex mixed-integer nonlinear programs by QP-diving. Preprint ANL/MCSP1801-101, Argonne National Laboratory, October 2010." (number as printed there), and ref. [59] is the 2012 version. It lists QP-diving as a Minotaur approach: "For example, our QP-diving approach [58] solves QP approximations and NLP relaxations." (PDF p. 5) [src 13]. [practice · primary; why it was not published is not documented]

### 2.2 NLP relaxations inside the tree: the rival schools' objection (taste · layer 1)

- Tawarmalani, Sahinidis, Math. Program. 103(2) (2005) 225-249, DOI 10.1007/s10107-005-0581-8 [src 9], abstract: "Unfortunately, state-of-the-art nonlinear programming codes are significantly slower and numerically unstable compared to linear programming software." Their answer is polyhedral relaxations (BARON). [observed · primary; the abstract names no one, so the link to Leyffer's NLP-based branch-and-bound is inferred]
- Lubin, Yamangil, Bent, Vielma, Math. Program. 172 (2018) 139-168, DOI 10.1007/s10107-017-1191-y [src 16], abstract: "mixed-integer convex optimization possesses broad modeling power but has seen relatively few advances in general-purpose solvers in recent years." They report "superior performance compared with state-of-the-art solvers like Bonmin, SCIP, and Artelys Knitro"; Minotaur and FilMINT are not named. [observed · primary; field-level critique]
- Applied community, S2 context [secondary]: Soler, de Sousa, da Costa 2012 [src 52]: "Methods such as Outer Approximation (Fletcher and Leyffer, 1994), Benders (Poojari and Beasley, 2009), Branch-and-Bound (Leyffer, 2001), and Cutting-Plane Algorithms (Westerlund and Petersson, 1995) are non-polynomial and consequently, slow and intractable for large-scale problems, making them unsuitable for OPF problems."
- **Answered? By a change of practice, then a partial defence.** His group moved to LP/NLP-based branch-and-bound (FilMINT 2010; Minotaur-QG), and in 2017 he calls the Quesada-Grossmann algorithm "arguably the best approach to solving MINLPs" (p. 5) [src 23]. The coauthored 2011 preprint [src 10] concedes: "Though largely given up on, there is the possibility that B&B can still be made viable for convex MINLP, and that is the subject of the present investigation." (p. 2), and "In previous experiments, OA-based methods usually outperform B&B-type methods [9, 2]." (p. 17; [2] is the 2006 FilMINT preprint). Its defence of branch-and-bound rests on a test set it calls biased: "Recall, that our test set is a biased subset of a larger set of problems, specifically chosen to include only the 88 problems on which at least one B&B approach succeeds. Nevertheless, it is important to note that the outer-approximation-based method failed on 27 of these problems." (p. 17). Conclusion: "This shows that a sophisticated NLP-based B&B code is an indispensable tool for solving some difficult MINLPs." (p. 18) [stated · primary, coauthored].

### 2.3 Independent benchmark: Kronqvist, Bernal, Lundell, Grossmann (2019)

Source [src 8]: Optim. Eng. 20 (2019) 397-455, DOI 10.1007/s11081-018-9411-8 (open access; journal version read); preprint dated 3 Jun 2018 on the CMU server. Setup: 335 convex MINLPLib instances in the journal (366 in the preprint), 900 s limit, up to 8 threads, defaults plus listed options. Minotaur version "05-21-2018" with CPLEX 12.6.3 (preprint Table 1). MINLP_BB and FilMINT are described (journal pp. 416-417) but were not run. Grossmann, the leading author of the rival decomposition school, is a coauthor; he also sat on the 2016 Farkas Prize committee that honoured Leyffer (committee list per note 01 §2). [observed · primary]

- **Counts** (p. 422): "Out of the BB-based solvers, BARON is able to solve the most instances (306) within the time limit followed by SCIP (295) and Minotaur-BB (258). SHOT is able to solve the most problems out of the MILP decomposition-based solvers (312), closely followed by AOA (310) and Muriqui (301)." In the snapshot table (Table 3) Minotaur-QG reaches 280 solved instances within 590 s.
- **Split verdict on nonlinear branch-and-bound.** p. 428: "The NLP-BB-based solvers, BONMIN-BB, Juniper, Knitro-BB, Minotaur-BB, and SBB solve significantly fewer problems with a large gap than the solvers based on either an ECP, ESH or OA (AlphaECP, BONMIN-OA, DICOPT, and SHOT)." p. 431: "However, for problems with a high degree of nonlinearity they are outperformed by the NLP-BB-based solvers BONMIN-BB, Knitro-BB, Minotaur-BB, and SBB." Conclusion, p. 438: "For example, the solvers based on NLP-BB were strongly affected by both the continuous relaxation gap and the degree of nonlinearity."
- **On Minotaur's design.** p. 428: NLP-BB-based solvers "often need to manage branching, handling the BB tree, cut generation, and other techniques on their own." pp. 428 and 431: "As an example of the impact on the performance of a MILP decomposition-based solver that handle the integer requirements itself, consider Minotaur-QG (as it only uses CPLEX as a LP subsolver): The performance difference between Minotaur-QG and AOA are significant, even though they utilize the same basic algorithm."
- **Vindication of his team's stated position** (p. 438): "The results show the benefits of such a solution technique and support the strong belief in the single-tree approach by Abhishek et al. (2010) and Belotti et al. (2013)."
- **Caveat on test sets** (p. 438): "However, it should be noted that many of the test instances are quite small and simple compared to industry-relevant problems."
- **Answered?** No public reply to the Minotaur-QG remark was found. [inferred] The remark names the trade-off behind Minotaur's stated aim of a common infrastructure for developing algorithms (01:S4): owning the tree buys flexibility and costs speed against solvers that sit inside a commercial MILP code.

## 3. Filter methods (1997-2002) and their successors

Era and resources: Fletcher's Dundee group, filterSQP in Fortran; rival codes at Northwestern (KNITRO), Oxford and Namur (GALAHAD), Princeton (LOQO), CMU/IBM (IPOPT). The 2020s comparison runs inside Uno, a C++ framework whose lead developer is Vanaret.

### 3.1 The Maratos effect: self-reported, then answered by others

- The weakness was first reported by the authors themselves (01:F4) and is widely relayed. S2 context [secondary] of Wächter and Biegler's local-convergence paper [src 43]: "As mentioned by Fletcher and Leyffer [6], the filter approach can still suffer from the so-called Maratos effect [8], even though it is usually less restrictive in terms of accepting steps than a penalty function approach."
- **Alternative remedy by a peer.** Ulbrich, Math. Program. 100(1) (2004), DOI 10.1007/s10107-003-0491-6 [src 21], abstract: "Transition to superlinear local convergence is shown for a modified version of the trust-region filter-SQP method for nonlinear programming introduced by Fletcher, Leyffer, and Toint [8]. Hereby, the original trust-region SQP-steps can be used without an additional second order correction. The main modification consists in using the Lagrangian function value instead of the objective function value in the filter together with an appropriate infeasibility measure." Wächter and Biegler [src 25], abstract: "A new alternative filter approach employing the Lagrangian function instead of the objective function with identical global convergence properties is briefly discussed." [observed · primary]
- **Answered? Yes, by declining the modification, then by a third route.** 2006 history [src 22], p. 7: "We currently prefer to use SOC steps to obtain fast local convergence because this approach allows us to keep the original filter definition with f (x), rather than the Lagrangian L(x, y). This approach also avoids the need for a multiplier function." 2017 [src 23], p. 5: "Originally, we had believed that filter methods avoid the Maratos effect [34], which rejects Newton steps arbitrarily close to a solution, destroying the second-order convergence properties of Newton's method. However, Nick and Philippe developed an example that showed that filter methods also suffer from the Maratos effect", followed by "a more appealing approach is to use a nonmonotone filter" (the Shen, Leyffer, Fletcher method listed above). [stated · primary]

### 3.3 The feasibility-restoration phase: the main design criticism (blind spot · layer 4)

- **Byrd, Curtis, Nocedal**, SIAM J. Optim. 20(5) (2010) 2281-2299, DOI 10.1137/080738222 [src 26], pp. 2281-2282: "One way to contend with the possibility that a problem could be infeasible is to employ a switch to decide whether the current iteration should seek a solution of the nonlinear program or, alternatively, a minimizer of some measure of infeasibility. Such an approach has been advocated by Fletcher and Leyffer [18] and has the benefit that infeasibility can be declared when a minimizer of the infeasibility measure is found that violates one or more constraints. The main difficulty faced by this type of approach, however, lies in the design of effective criteria for determining when such a switch should be made, since an inappropriate technique can lead to inefficiencies. In particular, since the objective function is ignored during iterations that care only about minimizing a measure of infeasibility, the iterates may stray from an optimal solution, thus delaying the optimization process." Their alternative is a single exact-penalty SQP strategy. [observed · primary]
- **Gould, Loh, Robinson**, preprint RAL-P-2014-012 [src 27] (SIAM J. Optim. 25(3) (2015) 1885-1911, DOI 10.1137/140996677), p. 25: "Third, a key motivation for our work is the design of a filter method that does not use a restoration phase since it is generally considered to be the most dissatisfying aspect of such filter-based methods." Yet, same page: "We suspect that a disadvantage of our approach is that methods, such as filter-SQP [16], would typically perform better on infeasible problems, a feature directly attributed to feasibility restoration." Journal abstract: "This contrasts previous filter methods that require a separate restoration phase based on subproblems solely designed to reduce infeasibility." [observed · primary]
- **Hao Wang's dissertation** (Lehigh, 2015) [src 45], S2 context [secondary]: "The one solver that does produce acceptable-looking results is Filter, it reports correct information rapidly but fails to provide an accurate infeasible stationary point."
- **Answered?** No direct reply found. In practice he keeps restoration: the 2024 funnel paper is a "funnel restoration SQP" in which "Infeasible quadratic subproblems are handled by a feasibility restoration strategy." (arXiv abstract) [src 29; practice · primary]. Contradiction C1 keeps the three peer views apart.

### 3.4 Rival globalization schools, and his revision

- **Penalty school.** Byrd, Nocedal, Waltz, Optim. Methods Softw. 23(2) (2008) 197-213, DOI 10.1080/10556780701394169 [src 44], S2 context [secondary]: "The difficulties of choosing appropriate values of νk in penalty methods [20] caused nonsmooth penalty methods to fall out of favour during the early 1990s and stimulated the development of filter methods which do not require a penalty parameter [21]." Their steering rules revive penalty methods.
- **His answer** (2017, p. 5) [src 23]: "Following the development of filter methods, modern penalty functions methods emerged. Most notable are the steering methods developed by Byrd, Nocedal, and Waltz, see [6, 5]. These methods adjust the penalty parameter to ensure sufficient reduction in a measure of constraint violation. At this point, I do not believe that there exists a big difference between filter methods and modern penalty function methods, and the choice of method is really a question of personal preference." In 2006 the coauthors had written "We believe filter methods achieve this goal" (01:F11). [stated · primary]
- **Funnel school.** Gould, Toint, Math. [observed · primary]
- **His answer: adoption.** Kiessling, Leyffer, Vanaret [src 29], arXiv 2409.09208v1, p. 20: "The performance profiles demonstrate that the funnel method slightly outperforms the filter method in terms of Hessian and constraint evaluations." p. 24: "An implementation of the funnel strategy in the Uno solver proved to slightly outperform its filter counterpart with respect to constraint evaluations for a subset of CUTEst instances, while being also easier to implement." ## 8. How he answers critique (pattern, [inferred] from the cases above)

1. **Names and credits the critic** in his own retrospective (2017: Bonami et al.; "Pierre"; "Nick and Philippe"; Byrd, Nocedal, Waltz). No case of rebuttal, reply article or public dispute was found.
2. **Writes the correction into the field's reference text and his teaching** (Acta 2013: the Bonami counterexample, the Hijazi worst case, the Tawarmalani-Sahinidis extended formulation; the 2024 IIT Bombay lecture on the Hijazi example and disaggregation).
3. **Changes method instead of arguing**: nonlinear branch-and-bound to LP/NLP-based branch-and-bound (2001 to 2010-2017); filter to funnel (2024); SQP for MPECs to LPEC-based B-stationarity (2025).
4. **Recruits critics and rivals as coauthors** (Bonami 2011-2013; Gould and Wächter 2002; Nurkanović 2025; Kirches and Manns on function-space methods).
5. **Leaves some answers unfinished**: QP-diving (2010, 2012) and the 2007 B-stationarity filter preprint never reached journals; the reasons are not documented.
6. **Keeps the contested element when he judges it worth it**: second-order corrections over the Lagrangian filter (2006), feasibility restoration despite its critics (2024).

## Contradictions (kept, not reconciled)

- **C1. The restoration phase.** Byrd, Curtis, Nocedal: the switch to infeasibility minimization can make iterates "stray from an optimal solution" (2010). Gould, Loh, Robinson: restoration is "the most dissatisfying aspect" of filter methods, yet filter-SQP "would typically perform better on infeasible problems, a feature directly attributed to feasibility restoration" (2014). Hao Wang (2015, S2 context): Filter "reports correct information rapidly but fails to provide an accurate infeasible stationary point".
- **C2. Warm starts in nonlinear branch-and-bound.** Bonami et al. 2005: the active-set warm-start advantage "does not confer any advantage in terms of computing time in our test set". Bonami, Lee, Leyffer, Wächter 2011: "active sets methods have better warm-starting properties and therefore seem preferable". Survey 2013, p. 24: warm-start attempts, QP-diving included, "generally have not been as successful". Survey p. 26: QP-diving "can reduce the CPU time of branch-and-bound by several orders of magnitude for some problems".
- **C3. What Bonami et al. corrected.** Leyffer 2017: "Bonami et al. corrected our convergence proof". Bonami et al.: Theorem 1 was "incorrectly stated", while Theorem 2 (finite convergence) is cited as valid. The 2013 survey keeps "A proof of Theorem 3.2 was given by Fletcher and Leyffer (1994)."
- **C4. Scope of the 1994 nonsmooth theory.** The 1994 abstract develops theory for "a large class of nonsmooth MINLPs" within its exact-penalty alternative; Wei and Ali (2015) describe the paper as covering continuously differentiable functions only; Wei, Liu, Zeng (2026) credit "a non-smooth extension within a penalty function context", which matches the abstract; de Oliveira (2016, S2 context) says the OA method requires differentiability.
- **C5. The quadratic master problem, within one school.** Grossmann 2002: Fletcher and Leyffer "have shown that the original OA algorithm may require a much larger number of iterations to converge than when the master problem (M-MIQP) is used". Kronqvist, Bernal, Grossmann 2017: the 1994 quadratic OA "will rely heavily on the second order approximation of the Lagrangean" and this "might cause slow convergence".
- **C6. Preprint versus journal numbers in Kronqvist et al.** Preprint (3 Jun 2018): 366 instances, Minotaur-QG 315 and Minotaur-BB 288 solved. Journal (2019): 335 instances, Minotaur-BB 258 solved; Minotaur-QG at 280 in the snapshot table.
- **C7. Nonlinear branch-and-bound, split verdicts.** Kronqvist et al.: weak on large-gap and low-nonlinearity problems, strong on high-nonlinearity and high-discrete-density ones. Bonami, Lee, Leyffer, Wächter: "largely given up on" and "an indispensable tool for solving some difficult MINLPs".
- **C8. MPECs as NLPs.** Benson et al. 2003: filter "quite successful, particularly on the MacMPEC set". Leyffer 2017: reliable NLP solvers "are still the best approach for solving MPECs". Nurkanović and Leyffer 2026: the plain NLP reformulation (with IPOPT) is "less competitive than the MPEC-tailored methods", and "Many MacMPEC problems are small or easily solved by all examined algorithms". Solvers and success criteria differ across these statements.
- **C9. MIQP.** 1998: branch-and-bound "superior to other approaches in solving MIQP problems". Wei and Küçükyavuz 2023: "significant advances have been accelerating the OA algorithm to solve MIQP".
- **C10. The thesis lesson versus later practice.** Lubin et al. and Grossmann credit his 1993 thesis with evidence that polyhedral (LP/NLP-based) approaches often beat NLP relaxations; his 2001 sole-authored method went back to nonlinear branch-and-bound; from 2010 his team returned to LP/NLP-based branch-and-bound.
- **C11. Filter versus its rivals.** Benson, Vanderbei, Shanno 2002: filters "more efficient than using a merit function alone". 2006 coauthors: "We believe filter methods achieve this goal". Leyffer 2017: no "big difference" from modern penalty methods. Kiessling, Leyffer, Vanaret 2024: the funnel "slightly outperforms" the filter in Uno.
