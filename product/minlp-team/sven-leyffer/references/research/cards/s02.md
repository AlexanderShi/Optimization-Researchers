### S061 · Constrained nonlinear programming for volatility estimation with GARCH models (SIAM Review 2003, doi:10.1137/S0036144501400115)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-19: abstract, model sections, formulations, results, figures, tables, conclusions, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Aslıhan Altay-Salih, Mustafa Ç. Pınar (Bilkent); Leyffer at Argonne (p. 1). part of the research was done while Pınar visited Dundee in July 2000 (p. 1); the acknowledgements thank Fletcher (p. 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A received modelling convention: GARCH maximum-likelihood models are "usually presented ... as unconstrained optimization models with recursive terms" while they are really nonconvex, nonlinearly constrained NLPs (p. 2). Multivariate GARCH variants (diagonal VECH, BEKK) impose restrictions "for computational tractability" (p. 2). The goal is to test "practical solvability (i.e., computing a Karush-Kuhn-Tucker point)" of the general constrained form through AMPL and NEOS (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when an applied field solves a recursion-defined likelihood by unconstrained quasi-Newton, write the recursion as equality constraints and hand the full constrained model to an NLP solver; test whether the field's simplifying restrictions are still needed.
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep the full VECH recursion as constraints, impose positive semidefiniteness of H_t either through a determinant constraint (bivariate) or an LDL^T parameterisation with d_t ≥ 0, and compute H_t^{-1} ε_t by "implied" variables that perform forward substitution, diagonal solve and back substitution inside AMPL (pp. 6-10). The stationarity condition becomes a side constraint (3.2) in the bivariate case (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: express a matrix inverse or factorisation needed in the objective as defining equality constraints (triangular-solve variables) instead of a closed-form inverse.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Normal conditional distribution; strict stationarity inequality relaxed to ≤ "in the interest of computational tractability", checked afterwards to be inactive (p. 5). Objective sum of quasi-convex terms, so no hidden convexity; only a KKT point is claimed (p. 5); multivariate objective noted as a difference of convex functions (p. 6). A and B taken symmetric "for tractability" (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no theorems); the convexity remarks are the only analysis (pp. 5-6).
- **D5 Experiments**: S&P 500 univariate (2000 points), S&P 500/FTSE bivariate (1500 points; 4506 constraints, 4525 variables), trivariate with DAX (500 points); FILTER and SNOPT on NEOS against the S-PLUS BHHH modules; log-likelihood, AIC, SIC, volatility plots and MSE (pp. 10-18). The constrained NLP wins on likelihood/AIC/SIC (p. 11, p. 15) but has higher MSE in all three bivariate series (Table 3, p. 18), which the text reports (p. 15). Run times (2.5 h and 23 h on NEOS vs minutes for S-PLUS) are reported with a caveat that they are not comparable (p. 16). Reader's note: the constrained-NLP bivariate estimates include c11 = -0.198775 and b22 = -8.53698 (Table 2, p. 11), signs the text does not discuss beyond saying the coefficients are hard to interpret (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution is a formulation approach plus evidence of better optimisation quality, not forecasting accuracy; the forecasting question is explicitly left to "further research" (p. 3, p. 16, p. 17).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ (filter/SNOPT NLP base carried into econometrics; the field's object re-cast into constrained NLP, pp. 2, 10). Method 6 ✅ (formulation work decides solvability: determinant vs LDL^T, implied variables, relaxed strict inequality checked inactive, pp. 5, 8-9). Method 5 ⚠ variant (competitors are the S-PLUS defaults, not rebuilt at best settings; losses on MSE and run time are reported, but the summary calls solution quality "clearly superior" on likelihood alone, pp. 15-17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Validate a new formulation on the univariate case against the standard package before scaling up ("validates our formulation prior to an application to the multivariate setting", p. 10).
- Replace a strict inequality by a nonstrict one for the solver and report afterwards that it is inactive at the solution (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "However, the purpose of our tests was not so much computational efficiency as the quality of the solution, which seems to be clearly superior in the constrained NLP approach to the competing specifications." (p. 16)
- "Therefore, the times are not directly comparable, and thus should give only a rough indication to the reader." (p. 16)

### S031 · On the global minimization of the value-at-risk (Optimization Methods and Software 2004, doi:10.1080/10556780410001704911; read as arXiv:math/0401063v1)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v1 preprint, pp. 1-21: all sections, proofs, numerical example, smoothing section, conclusion).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jong-Shi Pang (RPI) (p. 1). Dedicated to Mangasarian's 70th birthday (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: VaR minimisation is nonconvex, and the CVaR-based algorithms of the literature "offer no guarantee of global optimality of the computed VaR" (p. 1). MPEC solvers are local, so "the computation of globally optimal solutions to MPECs remains elusive" (p. 2). The authors refuse the "routine adaptation of the existing MPEC solvers" and aim at global optimality (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when an application is a special case of a class whose solvers are only local, ask what structure of the special case permits global bounds.
- **D2 Core idea**: With scenarios, VaR minimisation is a bilevel LP, written as an LPEC via the lower-level LP optimality conditions (4) (pp. 3-4). Upper bounds come from restricted LPs that fix a complementarity piece at a degenerate feasible point (pp. 5-7) or from the NLP form (7) solved by standard solvers (p. 7); lower bounds from LP relaxations (12) after the substitution z^i = λ_i x with three-way disjunctive cuts on λ_i (pp. 8-11), and a McCormick-type convex-hull relaxation (20) (pp. 11-12). These feed a branch-and-cut that verifies a candidate (pp. 12-13). A smoothing alternative replaces t+ by ρ_ε (pp. 15-17).
  - Transferable technique: for a candidate from a local solver, build the smallest tree that proves its optimality, fixing complementarity pieces by bound tightening against the incumbent (pp. 12-13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: X a compact polyhedron; finite scenarios with positive probabilities (p. 3); for the smoothing result, a VaR minimiser whose argmin set M_β is a singleton (p. 17) and a globally optimal solution of each smoothed problem (p. 17), which the authors say cannot be guaranteed in practice (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis**: finiteness of a strictly improving restricted-LP sequence because the LPEC feasible set is a finite union of polyhedra (Theorem 3.1, pp. 6-7), with the remark that degeneracy is what lets the search continue (p. 7); validity of disjunctive bounds (Proposition 3.3, p. 10; Corollary 3.4, p. 11); smoothing convergence through a boundedness lemma and the uniform approximation |t+ - ρ_ε(t)| ≤ cε (Lemma 6.1, Proposition 6.2, pp. 16-17).
- **D5 Experiments**: one constructed example (n = 3, k = 27, β = 0.9): six LPs prove that 4.2652 is globally optimal (pp. 13-15); the two lower bounds are nondominating (β = 0.8 vs 0.9, p. 15); five NEOS NLP solvers on the smoothed problems mostly fail (exponential overflow, infeasible, iteration limit), only filter gets close (4.265827) (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The upper bounding extends easily to general LPECs, while the lower bounding "is very much problem dependent" (p. 18). Theorem 3.1 is "mainly of theoretical interest" (p. 7). The evidence is a single illustrative example (p. 13).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ (LPEC/MPEC and NLP-solver base carried into risk management, p. 2, p. 7). Method 6 ✅ (formulation choice decides solvability: the log-exp smoothing fails because "the exponentials cannot be evaluated or blow up", the square-root form works better, p. 18). Method 3 ⚠ variant (worst-case thinking appears as the global-optimality proof demand, not as a counterexample; the empirical base is one example, which the paper calls an illustration, pp. 1, 13). New-pattern candidate: certify, do not just solve (a local NLP/MPEC answer plus a small verification tree, pp. 12-15, 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Combine two non-dominating relaxations and report on which instance each wins (β = 0.8 vs 0.9, pp. 12, 15).
- Use an AMPL presolve to discard infeasible children instead of solving them, and count the LPs actually solved (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "this is one of the rare instances in mathematical programming where degeneracy actually helps: it enables one to continue the search for an improvement in a global optimization procedure." (p. 7)
- "while the NLP solvers are generally very robust, one still requires a proof such as the one given in Subsection 5.2 to ascertain the quality of the solutions they produce." (p. 18)

### S130 · Biomolecule electrostatic optimization with an implicit Hessian (Modeling and Simulation of Microsystems, NSTI-Nanotech 2004, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-4, the whole 4-page proceedings paper).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. P. Bardhan, J. H. Lee, M. D. Altman, S. Benson, B. Tidor, J. K. White (MIT and Argonne) (p. 1). Leyffer's part is not identifiable from the text; he and Benson are acknowledged as Argonne-supported (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Optimal ligand charges solve a convex QP whose Hessian L = M3 M2^{-1} M1 needs one boundary-element solve per column; standard methods "typically require the expensive calculation of an explicit Hessian matrix" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: A primal-dual interior-point method whose Newton system is solved as an expanded system containing the integral operators, never forming L (Hessian-implicit, system (6), p. 2); a block-diagonal preconditioner from diagonal blocks of M2 (p. 2). New in this paper: an adaptive centering-parameter rule driven by the previous step length (Algorithm 1, p. 2), a preconditioner for equality-only problems (8) (p. 3), and a full-scale pFFT++/PETSc implementation (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when the Hessian is an operator product with an inverse inside, embed the inner solve into the outer Newton system and precondition the expanded system.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: continuum electrostatics under "certain assumptions" giving a convex QP with L positive semidefinite (pp. 1-2); box and sum-of-charge constraints (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no theorems; the σ rule is "determined by practical experience", p. 2).
- **D5 Experiments**: cost metric is the number of M2 matrix-vector products (p. 3); old vs new formulation, explicit Hessian, and KNITRO whose cost is estimated, not run on the operator form (pp. 3-4); accuracy against an explicit-Hessian solution on chorismate mutase (26 variables, about 130,000 in the implicit system, pp. 3-4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: about a factor of two over the original formulation (p. 4); remaining error from numerical Hessian asymmetry, which cannot be symmetrised in the implicit method (pp. 3-4); future work on the asymmetry and on convex constraints (p. 4).

**Method links**: Method 5 ⚠ variant (the fair unit is stated, M2 products, p. 3, but the KNITRO baseline is an estimate, p. 3). Method 1 ⚠ variant (integration at the linear-algebra level inside the Newton step, p. 2, but the paper does not show this as Leyffer's design).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure solver cost in the dominant expensive operation (here M2 products) rather than time or iterations, and translate a competitor's iteration counts into that unit (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "This schedule was determined by practical experience with different model problems." (p. 2)
- "Inaccuracies are due largely to numerical Hessian asymmetry in the implicit method" (p. 3)

### S048 · A trust-region algorithm for global optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2006, doi:10.1007/s10589-006-8716-2; read as preprint ANL/MCS-P1190-0804)
**Read level**: full (preprint dated August 6, 2004, pp. 1-19: method, algorithms, numerical section, appendix tables).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Bernardetta Addis (Firenze) (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two-phase global methods minimise the piecewise-constant local-minimisation map L(x) (pp. 1-2); the ALSO method of Addis, Locatelli and Schoen smooths L(x) with Gaussian kernels in a ball of fixed radius ∆, and "it is not clear a priori what value ∆ should take" (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Interpret ALSO's arbitrary radius as a trust-region radius and adapt it with the actual/predicted reduction ratio ρ (3.12) (pp. 3, 8). Because shrinking the region is not guaranteed to help in global optimisation, a "global quality" measure q (3.13) (fraction of samples with the same L value) triggers a model-improvement step that discards duplicates and enlarges the region; the radius shrinks only after two consecutive poor-agreement steps with good global quality (pp. 8-10). Smoothing width tied to radius by σ = ∆/K^{1/n} (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: when a local-optimisation safeguard (trust-region shrink) is wrong for a global goal, add a diagnostic that decides whether to shrink or to diversify.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f sufficiently smooth on a simple compact set (box) so that uniform sampling and bound-constrained local solvers are easy (p. 1); funnel structure (p. 3). The cited unimodality theorem holds only in one dimension (Theorem 1, p. 5), and the authors say these results are "of limited usefulness" (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no convergence theory for the new method; the trust-region motivation is heuristic, p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**: Rastrigin, Levy, Ackley, Schwefel, scaled Rastrigin in several dimensions; 1000 random starts per test; MBH, adaptive MBH and ALSO run with initial radii chosen to optimise MBH; percentage of successes and average local searches per success with ∞ for no success; performance profiles of success rate (reciprocal, 1/0 set to 10^8) and of local searches (pp. 10-14, tables pp. 17-19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: "up to 20 times" fewer local searches than ALSO and less sensitivity to the initial radius (pp. 12, 15); AMBH is better on Ackley and Levy (p. 12); a nonmonotone version is in progress (p. 12). The tables also show lower success rates than ALSO for TRF in several rows (Rastrigin n = 50 at ∆ = 1.8-2.0, Table 2 p. 17; scaled Rastrigin n = 20 at ∆ = 0.6-0.8, Table 9 p. 19), which the text does not discuss row by row; it states that success "does not deteriorate" relative to ALSO (p. 12).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ (trust-region globalisation from local NLP carried into stochastic global optimisation, p. 7). Method 5 ✅ with a variant (baselines given their best radii, failures kept as ∞ in the statistics, losses to AMBH stated, pp. 11-12; the robustness wording is broader than some table rows, p. 12 vs pp. 17, 19). New-pattern candidate: turn an arbitrary method parameter into a controlled trust-region radius (also S108, pp. 10, 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give the baselines the parameter values that are best for them and say so ("competes against “optimal ∆ values”", p. 11).
- Fold success rate into a performance profile by taking reciprocals with a large sentinel for zero success (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "First, we remove the need for the arbitrary parameters in [2] by interpreting these parameters as a trust-region radius." (p. 3)
- "In other words, our new algorithm competes against “optimal ∆ values” of the other methods." (p. 11)

### S042 · A globally convergent filter method for MPECs (Preprint ANL/MCS-P1457-0907, Argonne 2007, no DOI)
**Read level**: full for introduction, motivation and examples, algorithm, assumptions, main theorem and proof outline, Section 5 and conclusions (pp. 3-13, 15-16, 21-23); the lemma proofs of §4.4 skimmed (pp. 17-21: Lemma 4.2 statement and opening of its proof read, p. 17-18; the end of the main proof read, p. 21).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Todd S. Munson (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: NLP approaches to MPECs (including his own) "can be very successful" but do "not preclude convergence to spurious stationary points"; failures observed on PJM electricity-market problems (p. 3). The paper argues that A-, C- and M-stationarity are useless because they allow trivial descent directions (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Treat complementarity "as a structural constraint, not as a nonlinear equation" (p. 4): each iteration solves an LPEC in a trust region, whose d = 0 is exactly B-stationarity (p. 10), then an EQP on the predicted active set for speed (pp. 9, 21-22). A three-dimensional filter separates general infeasibility h_c and complementarity violation h_F, with a margin proportional to the total violation (pp. 10-11).
  - Transferable technique: choose the step subproblem so that its trivial solution is the stationarity notion you want (here LPEC d = 0 ⇔ B-stationary), so termination certifies the right concept.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: iterates in a compact set, C² data, MPEC-MFCQ at limit points (Assumptions 4.1-4.3, p. 15), no MPEC-LICQ (p. 15); plus Assumption 4.4: the LPEC solver explores all ε-active complementarity partitions and takes the largest predicted reduction (p. 16). The authors call 4.1 the strongest assumption and give a bilevel sufficient condition (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis**: two-variable examples that show C- and A/M-stationary points with descent directions (pp. 6-7); Proposition 2.1, explicit SQPEC iterates converging quadratically to a spurious M-stationary point, with a hypothesis check that it is not a counterexample to Scholtes' local result (p. 8); global convergence (Theorem 4.1, three outcomes, p. 16) by the Chin-Fletcher filter-SLP proof pattern: LPEC bound lemma, f-type step near nonstationary feasible points, finite inner loop, feasible limit, case split on h-type steps (pp. 16-21).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (no numerical results; computational cost discussed in words, pp. 22-23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: only a linear rate for SLPEC; EQP steps added for speed (p. 21); worst case 2^d LP pieces per iteration, argued not to be prohibitive under MPEC-LICQ (pp. 22-23); outcome O3 (restoration failure) cannot be excluded (p. 16); extensions to penalty functions, star-shaped problems and vanishing constraints (p. 23). The text promises "In Section 6 we give a computationally more efficient way" (p. 16), but that material is in §5.2 (pp. 22-23).

**Method links**: Method 3 ✅ (smallest examples break three stationarity concepts and SQPEC, the SQPEC iterates are computed in closed form, and the hypotheses of the attacked result are checked before calling it wrong, pp. 6-8; his own NLP approach is among the targets, p. 7). Method 2 ✅ (filter and SLP-EQP base carried to MPECs, pp. 4, 10). Method 1 ✅ (the method is designed so the SLQP code under development can be reused "by simply replacing the step-computation through an LP by an LPEC", p. 22). Theory-before-numbers: matches the contested side of Heuristic 3 (no numerics, p. 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before introducing a method, audit the stationarity concepts in use with two-variable examples and keep only the one that excludes first-order descent (pp. 5-7).
- Prove a counterexample's iterates in closed form (a recursion with an explicit limit and rate), not by a run log (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Our main contribution is to regard the complementarity constraint as as a structural constraint, not as a nonlinear equation." (p. 4)
- "We note that this is not a counterexample to the results by Scholtes (2004), because Scholtes investigates only local quadratic convergence." (p. 8)

### S108 · A filter active-set trust-region method (Preprint ANL/MCS-P1456-0907, Argonne 2007, no DOI)
**Read level**: full for introduction, algorithm (§2), assumptions, theorem and proof outline (§3.2), §4 and conclusions (pp. 3-14, 19, 21-24); the preliminary lemmas of §3.1 read as statements only (pp. 14-19).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael P. Friedlander (UBC), Nick I. M. Gould (Oxford), Todd S. Munson (Argonne) (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Active-set SQP/SLP methods pivot one activity at a time and factor dense reduced Hessians or Schur complements, which does not scale (pp. 3-4). Why active-set at all: MINLP needs warm-started NLP subproblems and PDE-constrained problems need iterative solvers without barrier ill-conditioning (p. 4). Polyhedral trust regions in SLQP spoil active-set prediction and warm starts (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: justify a method by naming the downstream consumers (B&B warm starts, iterative PDE solvers) whose needs the dominant method cannot meet.
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace the LP trust-region subproblem by a regularized LP min µg'd + ½d'd (RLP), whose dual is a bound-constrained QP solvable by projected-gradient methods; the regularization parameter µ plays the trust-region role (pp. 7, 10-11); an EQP on the predicted active set gives fast steps (pp. 6-8); a filter with switching condition globalises (pp. 8-10); µ is reduced by a parametric ratio test to the next breakpoint (2.6) (p. 11); multipliers come for free from systems already solved (p. 12).
  - Transferable technique: replace a hard step bound by a penalty-type regularization whose dual has simple constraints, then prove the regularization parameter maps to a radius.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: C² functions, iterates in a compact region, enforceable by bounds (A1, A2, p. 14). Active-set identification needs LICQ and strict complementarity at the solution (Proposition 4.1, p. 21).
- **D4 Analysis**: four outcomes OR/OK/OF/OH; global convergence to a Fritz-John point (Theorem 3.9, p. 19) along the pattern of [25], via a piecewise-linear RLP solution path in µ (Lemma 3.2, p. 15; Corollary 3.3, p. 17), finite inner loop (Lemma 3.5, p. 18), and a Cauchy-step argument for EQP steps (Lemma 3.8, pp. 18-19); identification of the optimal active set for µ in an interval derived from primal and dual feasibility bounds (Proposition 4.1, pp. 21-22); RLP and ℓ2 trust-region LP equivalent by comparing KKT systems (Lemma 4.2, p. 23).
- **D5 Experiments**: none; the authors say only experiments can justify the name (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory only; "Some open questions remain, not least a successful implementation"; the main open question is a preconditioner for the BQP (p. 24).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ (filter SLQP base rebuilt for large scale, pp. 6-8). Method 1 ✅ (decomposition into computational kernels with parallel iterative solvers, TAO and PETSc named, pp. 4, 23). Method 5 ✅ (claims held back until experiments exist, p. 12). Heuristic 7 evidence (MINLP warm starts and PDE solvers choose the NLP method, p. 4). New-pattern candidate: a method parameter reinterpreted as a trust-region radius (also S048, p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Compute the largest parameter change that keeps the active set fixed by a ratio test, instead of halving blindly (p. 11).
- Obtain first-order multipliers as a linear combination of two linear-system solutions already computed for the subproblem (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "of course, only thorough numerical experiments can show whether this name is justified." (p. 12)
- "Interior-point methods are not readily warm-started and are not as efficiently warm-started as are active-set methods, despite recent efforts in the area" (p. 4)

### S199 · A Filter Active-Set Trust-Region (FASTr) Framework (Technical Memorandum ANL/MCS-TM-298, Argonne 2007, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-15: problem statement, termination conditions, algorithm, API, AMPL options, output, ifail codes, planned extensions).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, p. 1). A software manual in Leyffer's own voice.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A flexible SQP framework in C "designed to be flexible and allows a range of subproblem solvers to be implemented" (p. 3); available on request, needs Fletcher's BQPD (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: An ℓ∞ trust-region QP subproblem solved by BQPD (p. 8), a nonmonotone ("shadow") filter in which a point is acceptable if at most M filter entries dominate it (pp. 6-7), a switching sufficient-reduction condition (p. 7), and a feasibility restoration that reuses the same code with a second filter on (h_J, h_J⊥), the partition J determined by BQPD's Phase I (pp. 7-8, 9). Despite the name it shares with S108, the subproblem here is a QP trust-region step, not the regularized LP of S108; SLP-EQP steps are a planned extension (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: implement feasibility restoration as the same solver loop with a different filter and objective, switched by a flag, so one code base serves both phases (pp. 8-9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: smooth C² NLP with general, linear and bound constraints (p. 3); linear constraints assumed consistent and checked at the start (p. 4); infeasibility reported as a local minimum of the violation (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (manual; no proofs).
- **D5 Experiments**: none; one sample run log on a 2-variable problem (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: planned hooks to other QP solvers, SLP-EQP steps, COIN-OR/CUTEr/AD interfaces (p. 15); the 14 ifail codes separate normal outcomes (0-4) from failures, and user-function failures (7, 11, 12) from algorithmic ones (p. 14).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ (own framework with a solver API meant for swappable subproblem solvers, pp. 3, 8, 15). Method 6 ✅ (the termination codes read the solver's numbers: ifail = 4 "Indicates failure of constraint qualification", and ifail = 5 points to wrong gradients, p. 14). Method 5 ✅ (an explicit failure taxonomy in the output, p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Publish a termination-code table that maps each failure to its likely cause (CQ failure, wrong derivatives, evaluation errors), so users can diagnose from the log (p. 14).
- Print one line per major iteration with radius, step norm, violation, objective, acceptance and phase (pp. 13-14).

**Quotes**:
- "The framework is designed to be flexible and allows a range of subproblem solvers to be implemented." (p. 3)
- "We note also that ifail=5, the trust-region converging to zero, frequently occurs if the gradients are wrong." (p. 14)
