### S041 · Branch-and-refine for mixed-integer nonconvex global optimization (Preprint ANL/MCS-P1547-0908 2008, no DOI)
**Read level**: full for the main text (file pp. 1-23: §1-§5, Algorithm in Fig. 7, Theorem 4.1 and its proof). The Appendix proofs (pp. 23-31: area integrals for Proposition 3.6, the proof of Proposition 4.2) were skimmed for their method, not checked line by line. References (p. 31 on) not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Annick Sartenaer, Emilie Wanufelle (both FUNDP Namur, p. 3). Several proofs are delegated to Wanufelle's 2007 thesis (pp. 9, 11, 22). · **Place in trajectory**: his move from convex MINLP (OA, SQP-B&B) to deterministic global optimization of nonconvex MINLP, driven by an optimal power flow (OPF) application.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an application that the existing tools cannot handle. OPF problems have discrete variables and trigonometric equality constraints (p. 3). BARON "cannot be directly applied to problems with trigonometric functions": Bussieck replaced the trig terms by degree-7 polynomials to "fool" BARON, which still could not solve the problem in reasonable time (p. 4). The SOS piecewise-linear approximation of Beale-Tomlin and Martin et al. "did not converge on the OPF problem": the MILP was declared infeasible although the NLP was feasible (p. 6). Example 1.1 shows why: sin(x)=0 and a quadratic constraint have two common roots in [0, 2π], but their SOS approximations have no common point (p. 6, Fig. 2 p. 7). Second defect: p^n SOS variables for a function of n variables (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three changes to SOS approximation. (i) Decompose every function on its computational graph into unary and binary operations, "similar in spirit to automatic differentiation", so a function needs k1·p + k2·p² SOS variables instead of p^n (pp. 6-8). Bounds are propagated through the graph (Prop. 2.2, p. 9) and common subexpressions are stored once (p. 10). (ii) Replace each piecewise-linear interpolant by a piecewise polyhedral envelope, shifted by the maximum over- and under-estimation error on each piece (eq. 3.1, pp. 10-11). Closed-form errors are derived for x², sin and cos (Props. 3.1-3.3, pp. 11-12). For xy the four-corner convex combination is exact (Theorem 3.4, pp. 12-13), so no error terms are needed, but the SOS adjacency condition then can no longer be enforced (p. 13). (iii) Branch-and-refine: branch on the original integer or continuous variables, never on the SOS variables, and at every branch reset the breakpoints while keeping their number fixed (pp. 16-17, Fig. 6 p. 18). Integrality alone does not fathom a node; the envelope must also be close enough (F1-F3, p. 18; algorithm, Fig. 7 p. 19).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: g_i twice continuously differentiable, possibly nonconvex; finite bounds on all variables; linear constraints in a polyhedron P (p. 4). The method is developed for equality constraints because OPF has only equalities; inequalities "can be easily included" (p. 4). Trig domains are taken in [0, 2π] without loss of generality (p. 9). Theorem 4.1 needs the discrete variables and those appearing nonlinearly to be bounded (p. 20).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Convergence (Theorem 4.1, p. 20) has two short steps. First, every LP over the envelope problem is a valid lower bound and every fixed-integer NLP an upper bound, so F1-F3 never fathom the optimum. Second, integer branching is finite, and differentiability makes the envelope error drop below ε as the boxes shrink with a fixed number of breakpoints. Tightness is argued by explicit comparison. The SOS bilinear envelope equals McCormick and the convex hull (Theorem 3.5, p. 14; proof in the Appendix, p. 23). When x also appears in x², the shared SOS set is tighter than McCormick: it reproduces xy = −0.75 exactly, where McCormick gives −3 (p. 14). The area between sin/cos and their SOS underestimators with five breakpoints is 0.451 and 0.313 times the area for the Caratzoulas-Floudas trigonometric underestimators (Prop. 3.6, p. 15). Refining after branching beats SOS branching in envelope area, by a closed-form ratio > 1 (12/11 for p=5, j=3), and branching at the midpoint is optimal (Prop. 4.2, pp. 20-21). For xy, diagonal SOS branching leaves a volume three times larger (p. 22, cited to Wanufelle 2007).
- **D5 Experiments**: none in this paper. "early experiments" favour branching on problem variables, and the "careful numerical study" is deferred to a companion paper (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and theoretical: a refined-envelope relaxation, a finite ε-global convergence proof, and tightness comparisons (abstract, p. 3; conclusion pp. 22-23). Limits: no numbers here; error formulas are derived only for the four OPF functions, with "Generalizations to other nonconvex functions are straightforward" asserted, not shown (p. 11); the decomposition loses accuracy, since for x1x2x3 the decomposed SOS value is 0 where the full SOS approximation is exact at 4 (p. 8). Open questions: where to place breakpoints and branch points, adding cuts, including McCormick in the framework (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a one-dimensional, two-constraint example shows exactly how the received SOS method fails (pp. 6-7), and the paper also states the accuracy cost of its own decomposition with a three-variable example (p. 8).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the restriction they remove belongs to the existing global solver (BARON's lack of trig support, p. 4), but the remedy is a new relaxation framework, not iteration-level integration with an inner solver.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the home branch-and-bound/linearization competence goes into nonconvex global optimization, but the carried-in toolkit here is B&B and SOS modelling, not filter-NLP methods.
- New-pattern candidate: "compare relaxations by a measured gap (area/volume) and by closed-form ratios" (Props. 3.6, 4.2, pp. 15, 20-21).
- New-pattern candidate: "branch on the original variables and refine the approximation, keeping its size fixed" (pp. 16-17).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To make a piecewise-linear approximation a valid relaxation, shift it on each piece by the analytically computed maximum interpolation error, so the MILP can no longer be wrongly infeasible (eq. 3.1, pp. 10-12).
- Factor nonlinear functions on their expression graph into unary/binary pieces before building SOS or envelope relaxations; this turns p^n auxiliary variables into k1·p + k2·p² (p. 8), and the paper reports the accuracy cost alongside (p. 8).
- Show that a new relaxation is no weaker than the standard one by proving set equivalence (with McCormick and the convex hull), then give a tiny NLP where it is strictly tighter (pp. 14, 23).
**Quotes**:
- "In a preliminary implementation, the resulting MILP was wrongly declared infeasible, even though the nonlinear problem was feasible." (p. 6)
- "The key to its convergence is the fact that the envelopes are refined as we move down the tree." (p. 17)

### S071 · Global and finite termination of a two-phase augmented Lagrangian filter method for general quadratic programs (SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 2008, doi:10.1137/060669930)
**Read level**: full for what the supplement mode needs: abstract and §1-§2 (pp. 1-12) including Algorithms 1-3, §3 statements and the proofs of Theorems 3.4, 3.7 and Lemmas 3.2-3.6 (pp. 12-15), §4 with the finite-identification proof (pp. 17-19), §5 experiments, §6 conclusions and the Appendix Theorem 7.1 (pp. 19-22). The proof of Theorem 3.8 (pp. 15-16) was not read. The text is the preprint ANL/MCS-P1370-0906 "To appear in SIAM J. Sci. Comp., Revised August 30, 2007" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael P. Friedlander (UBC). A summer student, Anatoly Eydelzon, wrote the initial QPFIL version on TAO and PETSc (p. 22). · **Place in trajectory**: an early (2006-2008) forerunner of the augmented-Lagrangian filter that SKILL.md Method 2 step 6 lists among the 2016-2026 rebuilds of the base.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The downstream use defines the problem: "a QP algorithm that may be used effectively within an SQP framework" for large-scale NLP (p. 1). Active-set QP methods warm-start well but change one index per iteration; interior methods scale but produce KKT systems that are "inherently ill-conditioned" for iterative solvers (pp. 1-2). The target is matrix-free use, for example PDE-constrained problems with inequalities (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two phases per iteration. First, gradient projection on the bound-constrained augmented Lagrangian estimates the active set, allowing large active-set changes. Second, an equality-constrained QP on that active set gives a second-order step (pp. 2, 4, 6). A filter on (ω, η) = (dual infeasibility measured with the ordinary Lagrangian gradient, primal infeasibility ‖Ax−b‖) replaces the two forcing sequences of BCL methods: subproblem tolerance and the multiplier-update threshold (pp. 4-5, Def. 2.1 p. 5). The filter axes are deliberately reversed relative to the usual filter (p. 6). The QP structure gives an explicit penalty threshold: Lemma 2.2, sharp, and the computable bound ρ_min < max{0, −λmin(H)}/σmin(A)² of Lemma 2.3 (pp. 7-8). A norm-based proxy is used in practice (p. 9). When the active Jacobian is rank-deficient, the inner loop switches to a minimum-infeasibility problem (p. 9; Algorithm 2 p. 10). Outer loop: Algorithm 3 (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: General QP with H possibly indefinite, feasible, with a KKT point (pp. 2-3). Iterates in a compact set (Assumption 3.1, p. 12). Full rank of A* for the multiplier bounds (pp. 12-13). Global convergence assumes a single limit point (Theorem 3.7, p. 14) or a second-order sufficient condition (Theorem 3.8, p. 15). The authors say the single-limit-point assumption "considerably simplifies the analysis" and that the alternative assumption "cannot be verified in practice" (pp. 14-15). Finite identification needs strict complementarity and Cauchy decrease (Theorem 4.2, p. 17). Finite termination also needs exact EQP solves (Cor. 4.3, p. 19).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Global convergence is proved for a first-order version that skips the second-order steps, which "serve only to accelerate convergence" (pp. 12, 14). The filter lemma is borrowed from Chin-Fletcher with f = η, h = ω (Lemma 3.6, p. 14). The penalty is updated finitely often because there are finitely many active sets (Lemma 3.5, p. 14). Least-squares multiplier estimates are bounded by the dual residual (Lemmas 3.2-3.3, pp. 12-13). Finite active-set identification orders the breakpoints of the projected-gradient path: with strict complementarity, active-index breakpoints are O(ε/τ) and inactive ones O(τ/ε), so the first minimizer falls in between (pp. 17-19).
- **D5 Experiments**: CUTEr QPs in AMPL with up to 20,000 variables or constraints, solved with TAO 1.8.1 (TRON) through a user-defined filter termination, plus PETSc 2.3.1 GMRES. They are compared with KNITRO and LOQO, by design, on major iterations as the comparable unit, with CPU time also reported (pp. 19-20, Table 5.1). QPFIL needs fewer major iterations but more CPU time; profiling puts almost 90% of the time in the KKT solver (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm plus theory: global convergence and finite active-set identification, with filter-controlled inexactness (pp. 2, 21). Limits are stated plainly: CPU performance is "disappointing" and the cause is traced to GMRES on the KKT system (pp. 20-21); "We are not entirely satisfied with (2.9)" because σmin is expensive (p. 9). Open questions: whether gradient projection until a filter-acceptable point is enough, whether the second-order step is useful far from the solution, and inexact KKT solves (p. 21). The referees' comments led to Theorem 3.8 (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the filter, his home globalization, is carried into bound-constrained Lagrangian methods, and global convergence is "enforced by the filter and not through the augmented Lagrangian" (p. 4).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the comparison unit is chosen and justified (major iterations, because each solves a structurally similar KKT system), the CPU loss is reported, and its source is found by profiling (pp. 19-20).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the filter test is put inside TAO through its user-defined termination interface, a minimal change to the inner solver (p. 19); the algorithm is described as two swappable kernels, TAO/PETSc (pp. 4-5).
- (Heuristic 7, downstream use chooses the upstream research: the QP is designed for its use inside SQP, p. 1.)
**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove global convergence on the plain first-order version of your method, and treat the fast second-order step as an add-on that the filter may reject (pp. 12, 14).
- Replace hand-tuned forcing sequences (subproblem tolerances, multiplier-update thresholds) by a filter on (dual infeasibility, primal infeasibility) (pp. 4-5).
- For linear constraints, use the decoupling of penalty and multipliers to compute a penalty threshold from λmin(H) and σmin(A) instead of increasing ρ blindly (pp. 7-8).
- When comparing a research code with production codes, choose the unit in which the algorithms do comparable work, say so, report the other unit anyway, and profile to explain the loss (pp. 19-20).
**Quotes**:
- "At this stage we are not interested in a direct comparison between QPFIL and these production-quality interior implementations; rather, we are interested in the number of iterations it takes for QPFIL to identify the optimal active set—KNITRO and LOQO serve as relative benchmarks." (p. 19)
- "In particular, the referees’ comments led us to Theorem 3.8 as well as refinements of Algorithm 2." (p. 22)

### S029 · Applications and algorithms for mixed integer nonlinear programming (Journal of Physics: Conference Series 2009, doi:10.1088/1742-6596/180/1/012014)
**Read level**: full (all 6 file pages: abstract, §1-§4, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jeff Linderoth (corresponding, e-mail on p. 2), James Luedtke, Andrew Miller, Todd Munson · **Place in trajectory**: a SciDAC 2009 overview, the first short programme statement of the Argonne-Wisconsin MINLP collaboration that leads to the 2013 Acta Numerica survey.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: MINLPs model dichotomies plus physics (pressure, stress, temperature, equilibrium), but "current algorithms and available software are often unable to solve practically-sized instances" (abstract and p. 2). The stated aim is to close the mismatch between natural models and robust solvers (p. 2). Two showcase applications: thermal insulation for the LHC, where MINLP found designs "as much as 4% better" by handling more intercepts (p. 3), and nuclear core reload, where rounding the NLP relaxation does not give a valid pattern (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Algorithms are organized as "relax and search": NLP-based branch-and-bound for convex MINLP, convex-envelope relaxations plus spatial branching for nonconvex MINLP, and linearization (OA-type) methods (p. 4), with a software table (Table 1, p. 5). The main message is that strong reformulation decides tractability. The perspective reformulation gives conv(S) for an indicator set (p. 5): on an SQUFL instance with 30 facilities and 100 customers, Bonmin needs 16,697 s and 45,901 nodes on the original formulation and 23 s and 44 nodes on the perspective one (p. 5). For multilinear terms, the convex hull obtained from the vertices of the box gives a bound of 36.33 against 2.33 for McCormick on a bilinear reformulation, and from the hull solution IPOPT finds a feasible point (72.1, confirmed optimal by BARON) where it fails from the McCormick point (pp. 5-6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, g continuously differentiable; X, Y compact polyhedral (p. 2). The bounds of the multilinear example are printed with 8 lower but only 7 upper entries (p. 6), as printed.
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a. The hull results are cited ([8] Günlük-Linderoth, [13] Sherali, p. 5), not proved here.
- **D5 Experiments**: two illustrations, not a study. The SQUFL figures put together runs from two different papers ([10] and [8], p. 5), and the multilinear test is a single example (pp. 5-6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A survey-level overview of applications, algorithms and software (abstract, p. 2). The hedging is calibrated to the evidence: "This example suggests that the convex hull approach may be useful" (p. 6). No limitations or future work are spelled out beyond the stated aim (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: formulation decides what can be solved, shown twice: a speed-up of over 700× from the perspective reformulation (p. 5), and a hull relaxation that gives both a better bound and a feasible start for the NLP solver (p. 6).
- (Taste mark 2, a received design practice made obsolete by formulation plus solver: the thermal-insulation MINLP finds designs up to 4% better because it can handle more intercepts, p. 3.)
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the evidence for the hull approach is one example, offered with the hedge "suggests" (p. 6); the SQUFL speed-up compares numbers from two papers rather than one controlled run (p. 5).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To show a reformulation matters, report the same instance and solver before and after, in both time and nodes (16,697 s / 45,901 nodes vs 23 s / 44 nodes, p. 5).
- Judge a relaxation twice: by its bound, and by whether its solution is a good starting point for a local NLP solver (p. 6).
**Quotes**:
- "Our research is aimed at correcting the mismatch between natural optimization models and available robust optimization solvers." (p. 2)
- "The speedup factor is more than 700." (p. 5)

### S047 · Optimization of 3D plasmonic crystal structures for refractive index sensing (The Journal of Physical Chemistry C 2009, doi:10.1021/jp9024552)
**Read level**: full (all 7 file pages: abstract, methods, §3.1-§3.4, conclusions). Supporting Information figures cited in the text were not available.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Joana Maria, Tu T. Truong, Jimin Yao, Tae-Woo Lee, Ralph G. Nuzzo, Stephen K. Gray (corresponding), John A. Rogers (corresponding) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an applied physical-chemistry collaboration at Argonne. Leyffer's part is not stated; that it is the derivative-free optimization step (pp. 5-6), the only optimization-method content, is an inference from his MCS affiliation (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Maximize the refractive-index sensing figure of merit (FOM) of a gold-coated nanowell crystal over several geometric parameters whose combined effect cannot be predicted "based on simple physical principles" (p. 1). Each FDTD simulation can take up to 6 h on 128 processors (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: First, one-parameter-at-a-time sweeps of relief depth, periodicity, diameter (also with diameter tied to 0.6× periodicity) and the three gold thicknesses (pp. 3-5) give a best FOM ≈ 16,600 nm/RIU (p. 5). Then derivative-free optimization: a quadratic surrogate with 15 parameters is fitted inside a trust region by Powell's least-change Hessian interpolation, seeded with data from the coordinate-wise search; each predicted maximum is simulated, and the step is accepted or the point added to the model (pp. 5-6). After 8 iterations the FOM is 18,700 nm/RIU (+13%); 10 more iterations give no improvement (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The FOM is the slope of the integrated normalized transmission change versus refractive index (eq. 1, p. 4). The FDTD resolution is 4 nm, said to be converged on tests (p. 2), so the simulated gold thicknesses only approximate the measured ones (p. 3). Gradients are taken as unavailable because "the FDTD simulations preclude" their accurate computation (p. 5). The parameter vector labels x1 as "well depth" although its value (330 vs 320 nm) matches the diameter (p. 6), as printed.
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a. Stopping is argued, not proved: the local model keeps one positive eigenvalue, and proving local optimality would need 3⁴ = 81 FDTD runs, "which is prohibitive" (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: simulations checked against experiment where fabrication allowed; experimental FOMs show the same trends but are up to a factor 2 smaller (pp. 4-5). Control structures (no gold, top only, no sidewalls, plain holed film) separate the contributions (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is applied: about an order-of-magnitude predicted gain in sensitivity (abstract, p. 1; p. 7). Limits stated: the best gold geometry "cannot be easily implemented experimentally" (p. 5), quantitative agreement is only qualitative (p. 7), and the optimum is local (p. 6). Future work: nanowell shape, longer-wavelength resonances, extending the DFO approach (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the home NLP/DFO toolkit (trust-region quadratic models after Powell) is carried into an expensive physics simulation (pp. 5-6).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: a calibrated stopping claim: "We believe that it is unlikely", with the reason, and with what a proof of local optimality would cost (p. 6).
- (Heuristic 8, brute-force arithmetic first: 81 simulations to certify local optimality is judged prohibitive, so the claim is weakened instead, p. 6.)
- New-pattern candidate: "seed the surrogate with the sweep data": run coordinate sweeps first, then reuse their points to start a model-based DFO search (pp. 5-6).
**Transferable techniques**:
- For a simulator taking hours per run, start with cheap one-parameter sweeps, then fit a trust-region quadratic surrogate to those same points and iterate only at the surrogate's predicted optima (pp. 5-6).
- When you cannot certify optimality, say what certification would cost (here 81 runs) and phrase the claim as a belief (p. 6).
**Quotes**:
- "However, we cannot apply gradient-based optimization methods, because the FDTD simulations preclude the accurate and efficient computation of derivatives." (p. 5)
- "We interpret the fact that our local surrogate does not improve the FOM over 10 consecutive iterations as an indication that the design cannot be improved locally." (p. 6)

### S051 · Mixed-integer support vector machine (NIPS workshop on optimization for machine learning 2009, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (all 6 file pages: abstract, §1-§5, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Wei Guan, Alex Gray (Georgia Tech) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an early attempt to carry MINLP into machine learning, using the Günlük-Linderoth perspective relaxation, which S029 (the same year) also promotes.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Feature-selecting linear SVMs. L1-SVM selects features only "as a by-product" of sparsity, and L1 penalties bias estimates; earlier L0 work uses smooth approximations or scaling parameters (pp. 1-2). They choose the exact L0-norm SVM (5), which is NP-hard (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Indicator variables z_j and perspective constraints w_j² ≤ z_j u_j turn (5) into an MIQCQP (6) (p. 3). Solving (6) directly with Bonmin, CPLEX or MINLP_BB "fails": the result is infeasible or has a single nonzero indicator (p. 3). Diagnosis: when z_j = 0 the relaxation contains w_j² ≤ 0, which violates Slater's CQ. Preprocessing it to w_j = 0 would fix this, "current nonlinear solvers do not perform this operation" (p. 3). Remedy: relax to big-M constraints |w_j| ≤ M z_j with M = 10⁴, giving the MIQP (P1) solved by CPLEX 11.1 (pp. 3-4). MI-SVM2 refits a standard SVM on the selected features (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linear binary classification; at least one feature selected (p. 3); fixed big-M (p. 3). As printed, (P1) keeps the term ½eᵀu in its objective although the constraint that defines u has been dropped and u is not among the listed variables (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a. The CQ diagnosis is an argument, not a proof (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**: four UCI sets (Ionosphere, WPBC24, WPBC60, SPECTF); 10-fold CV, except the given split for SPECTF; baselines SVM, RFE, L1-SVM, FSV, R2W2 (RFE, FSV, R2W2 coded by the authors in MATLAB). Parameters are chosen by averaged training accuracy over the CV folds (p. 4). Tables 1-2: MI-SVM2 has the best average accuracy (77.7%) and, tied with MI-SVM1, the smallest average number of features (20.8) (pp. 4-5), but on WPBC60 both MI-SVM variants are below plain SVM (60.9/63.6 vs 66.2) (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a formulation plus a demonstration that "MINLP techniques which have not previous been widely used in machine learning" work (p. 5). Open questions: whether big-M is the best approximation of the conic constraints, and scalability limited by CPLEX (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (strong): solver failures are read as a CQ failure (Slater at z_j = 0), and the formulation is repaired before any algorithm is changed (p. 3). This is the SKILL.md step 3 pattern, in print, in 2009.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: a machine-learning object, the sparse SVM, is recast into a home class (an MIQCQP/MIQP with indicators and a perspective) (p. 3).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: baselines are self-built and the tuning rule is written down (p. 4), but the conclusion that MI-SVM representations are "generally more predictive" (p. 5) rests on averages over four sets, one of which (WPBC60) goes against it.
**Transferable techniques**:
- When a MINLP solver returns infeasible or degenerate answers on a perspective/indicator model, check whether z = 0 turns a convex constraint into one with empty interior (w² ≤ 0, Slater fails), and preprocess it to an equality or a big-M form (p. 3).
- After a mixed-integer selection step, refit the continuous model on the selected support instead of thresholding the mixed-integer solution (MI-SVM2 vs MI-SVM1, pp. 4-5).
**Quotes**:
- "We believe that the failure of the nonlinear solvers is due to a failure of a constraint qualification" (p. 3)
- "The errors that we observe from the nonlinear solvers are consistent with a failure of a constraint qualification." (p. 3)

### S063 · A complementarity constraint formulation of convex multiobjective optimization problems (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2009, doi:10.1287/ijoc.1080.0290)
**Read level**: full (all 11 file pages: §1-§4, Propositions 2.2-2.3 with the proof of 2.3, Tables 1-4). Greek symbols (η, δ) are dropped in the extraction; their meaning was recovered from context.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the MPCC-as-NLP toolkit of 2004-2006 (Fletcher-Leyffer; Fletcher-Leyffer-Ralph-Scholtes) carried into multiobjective optimization.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: How to get a good discrete picture of a Pareto set. Uniform weights do not give a uniform Pareto set (Das and Dennis, p. 2; Fig. 1 p. 3). The idea is to treat the weights or goals as variables chosen optimally (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Sayin's uniformity measure (the smallest pairwise distance among q Pareto points) is maximized in a bilevel problem whose lower level is q copies of SUM(w) or GOAL(z) (eq. 5, pp. 3-4). Under convexity the lower level is replaced by its first-order conditions, which gives an MPCC: formulations (6), (7) with one weight fixed, (8) from goal programming, and a componentwise version (9) (pp. 4-5). The MPCC is solved as an NLP with slacks (10) by filterSQP (pp. 5, 7). Feasible starts come from PATH solving the NCP with fixed weights (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A1 C² functions; A2 nonempty bounded feasible set; A3 MFCQ and SOSC at local solutions of SUM(w)/GOAL(z); A4 convexity, called "the most restrictive" (pp. 2-3). Even linear MOOPs give nonconvex MPCCs, so only local solutions are expected (pp. 4, 6).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Pareto-optimality of MPCC solutions follows as a corollary of the Miettinen-Mäkelä conditions (Cor. 2.1, Prop. 2.2, p. 5). Proposition 2.3: LICQ plus SOSC of the single-objective NLP make the active normals of its KKT complementarity system linearly independent. The proof is by contradiction on the basis matrix (pp. 5-6), and the caveat that the uniformity constraints may still be degenerate is stated (p. 6). The method's own failure is shown on a one-variable, two-objective nonconvex example (13), where the MPCC picks a maximizer, x = 0, as a "Pareto" point (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: 11 problems collected from the literature plus three random convex MOQPs (MOQP-01 to -03) from his own generator, with problems and generator published at his web page (p. 6; Table 1 p. 7); q = 10; iteration limit 1000. Failures are kept in Table 3 with codes [S] (segfault), [I] (no feasible point), [L] (local maximum picked) (p. 8). The goal formulation (8) is the only one to separate all MOLPg points (p. 8). On ex005 with q = 8, BARON runs 36,000 s and brackets the optimum between 0.3032 and 0.7055, while the MPCC solve finds 0.3580 in 0.04 s (pp. 8-9, Table 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a formulation, light theory and a computational study (abstract, p. 1; p. 9). Limits stated: users must derive KKT conditions by hand, which is "prone to error"; spurious Pareto points without convexity; q² uniformity constraints limit the method to about 10 points; an AMPL bilevel extension would need third derivatives (p. 9). Future: domain decomposition of objective space, interactive use with NIMBUS (pp. 9-10).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the MPCC-as-NLP base, filterSQP plus the 2006 strong-stationarity result, is carried into a new class, and that base is what makes the approach "practical" (p. 5).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: his own method gets the smallest breaking example (13, one variable) to show exactly where assumption A4 is needed (p. 6), and the failure gets its own code [L] in the results (p. 8).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: test problems and the random generator are published at his web page (p. 6).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: failures stay in the table with a per-instance taxonomy (p. 8); a global solver is used to bracket how far the local answers are from optimal (pp. 8-9).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: a slack formulation is chosen "to obtain a numerically favorable formulation" (p. 5), and a warm start from the NCP is used because cold MPCC solves fail to find feasible points (p. 6).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To choose parameters of a family of subproblems optimally (weights, goals), write the choice as a bilevel problem, replace the convex lower level by its KKT system, and solve the resulting MPCC as an NLP with slacks (pp. 3-5).
- Warm-start a hard MPCC from an NCP solved with the upper-level variables fixed, and retry the fixed values until the start is non-degenerate (p. 6).
- Use a global solver with a time cap only to bracket the optimum on the smallest instance, and report its bounds next to the local result (pp. 8-9, Table 4).
**Quotes**:
- "Surprisingly, the local MPCC solver finds a better solution than the global optimization approach." (p. 9)
- "We have observed examples where a lack of convexity results in spurious Pareto points being found by our approach." (p. 9)
