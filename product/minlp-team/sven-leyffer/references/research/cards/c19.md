### S159 · Using Filter Methods to Guide Convergence for ADMM, with Applications to Nonnegative Matrix Factorization Problems (Journal of Optimization Theory and Applications 209(2):66 2026, doi:10.1007/s10957-026-02983-7)
**Read level**: partial. The file is not the journal article but the ICCOPT 2022 talk slides of the same title and authors (Robert Baraldi, Sven Leyffer, Stefan Wild; "ICCOPT 2022 - Nonlinear Optimization", 7/25/22, SAND2022-10113C, p. 1). All 38 slides were read, including the two reference slides (pp. 37-38). Plots on pp. 5, 14, 22-25, 27-29, 31-34 survive only as captions, so curve shapes and comparison numbers were not read. Any claims about what the 2026 article contains: not read. Page references are slide pages ("S159 slides").
**Role**: core (read as a coauthored talk; the footer on every slide is "Robert Baraldi Sandia National Laboratories ADMM/Filters", so the voice is the presenter's) · **Coauthors**: Robert Baraldi (first author, presenter, Sandia), Stefan Wild · **Place in trajectory**: the filter line (2002 filterSQP/filter methods → augmented-Lagrangian filter, Vanaret and Leyffer 2020, cited p. 12 and p. 38) carried into block-splitting/ADMM for nonconvex, nonlinearly constrained problems. SKILL.md Method 2 already lists the ADMM extension by this DOI.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: There are two entries. One is an application: chemical spectrum analysis, where measured spectra (M ∈ R^{1750×22}) are fitted by nonnegative combinations of Gaussians (pp. 4-5). The other is a gap in the ADMM literature. "Most literature assumes c(x) = Ax − b or linear constraints, and f = f1 + f2" (p. 6). The direct multiblock extension can diverge: Chen et al. [2016] give a p = 3 example "where ADMM diverges for any ρ" (p. 7). ADMM convergence "is still “somewhat” nebulous: requires a priori knowledge of functions, ρ choice is largely open, limited choices in c(x)" (p. 9). The research questions are posed as questions. Can one solve nonconvex NLPs (1) with block structure and nonlinear constraints, and "Can filters help guide convergence?" (p. 9).
  - Transferable: enter a splitting/decomposition topic through a published divergence counterexample of the plain method (here p = 3 blocks) and the parameter the literature leaves open (ρ). Then ask whether a globalization device from your own toolkit closes both.
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Wrap block-coordinate minimization of the augmented Lagrangian in an augmented-Lagrangian filter. The filter stores pairs (η, ω) of infeasibility ‖c(x)‖ and the first-order error ‖proj_X(x − ∇L(x,y)) − x‖ of the original problem. A point is acceptable if η ≤ βη_l or ω ≤ ω_l − γη for every filter entry (Definition, p. 13). The algorithm "Monitors dual infeasibility error of the original problem while solving the augmented Lagrangian" (p. 15). ADMM-F (Algorithm 2, p. 16) works as follows. The inner loop runs block sweeps until a sufficient-decrease test is satisfied: the first sweep is a projected block steepest-descent step, later sweeps are exact block minimizations. A restoration test (13) on the upper bound U and on η_min (p. 15) triggers a feasibility restoration (minimize ‖c(x)‖² over X), and an increase of ρ (p. 16). The multiplier y is updated only at filter-acceptable points, and ρ only when restoration is entered (pp. 13, 16). What changes relative to plain ADMM (Algorithm 1, p. 8): the y update is no longer done every sweep, and ρ is adapted by the filter instead of being fixed a priori. What stays: block minimization of L_ρ. Stopping uses a relative first-order error ω₀(x_k,y_k)/ω₀(x_0,y_0) ≤ ε (14) (p. 15).
  - Transferable: decouple the multiplier and penalty updates of a splitting method from the inner sweeps, and let a two-criterion filter on (infeasibility, first-order error of the original problem) decide when to update y and when to raise ρ.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: NLP min f(x) s.t. c(x) = 0, x ∈ X with simple bounds, x split into p blocks, f, c ∈ C², "possibly separable" (p. 3). For the convergence theorem, Assumption 1 requires f, c continuously differentiable and ‖c(x)‖ → ∞ as ‖x‖ → ∞. This is "replaced with finite bounds l ≤ x ≤ u" so that iterates stay in a compact set and derivatives are bounded (p. 17). The sufficient-decrease lemma needs a Lipschitz constant L of the Lagrangian, block Lipschitz constants L_i, and constants σ_i (p. 19). The NMF and NMFC applications are bilinear: Z = XY is the coupling constraint (pp. 21, 26). The spectra model is nonlinear in (µ, σ) through X(µ, σ) (p. 30). Assumptions stated as being for the proof: not stated on the slides.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The slides state a three-outcome convergence theorem without proof (p. 18). Either there is finite termination at a KKT point, or every limit point is one of three things: (1) a KKT point, when ρ is updated finitely often; (2) a KKT point under LICQ, otherwise a Fritz-John point, when there are infinitely many restoration steps with ρ updates; (3) a minimizer of the constraint violation, from the restoration phase. The preceding slide states the same trichotomy in terms of the restoration sequence R and the successful-iterate sequence S, with LICQ failing or holding at the limit (p. 17). The inner-loop device is a generalized Cauchy point by projected gradient with step 1/L_i, after Beck and Tetruashvili [2013, Lemma 3.3]. A block-coordinate sufficient-decrease lemma (16) bounds L(x^j) − L(x^{j+1}) below by a constant times ‖d(x^j)‖². The constant depends on σ_min, p and the ratio L/L_min (p. 19). The algorithm requires "BCD satisfies a sufficient decrease condition" (p. 20). The slides do not show how the filter argument and the lemma are combined: not read.
  - Transferable: for a nested method, prove a sufficient-decrease lemma for the inner sweep (a Cauchy-point bound in the block-Lipschitz constants), and state outer convergence as an exhaustive trichotomy: KKT, FJ point where LICQ fails, or minimizer of infeasibility.
- **D5 Experiments**: Three applications. (i) Bilinear NMF (17)-(18) on a 225×225 image ("Sylvester", X ∈ R^{225×45}, first-order tolerance 1e−5), with plots of the progression of filter entries (pp. 21-23). (ii) ρ tests: trajectories of ρ and descent of L_k for different ρ initializations (pp. 24, 29). (iii) A comparison of ADMM-F with the accelerated NMF of Ang and Gillis [2019] (p. 25). (iv) NMF with completion (NMFC), 50% missing data (pp. 26-28). (v) Spectra inversion with 22 Gaussians, and the objective value per number of Gaussians on training and test data (pp. 31-34). All results are in figures whose content is not in the text layer. No timings or iteration counts are readable, and no failure cases are reported: not read.
- **D6 Positioning**: ADMM history is given from Douglas-Rachford 1956 through Gabay-Mercier and Glowinski-Marroco 1975 (p. 6). Gao et al. [2020] supply multiaffine constraints (p. 7). Wang et al. [2018] supply nonconvex settings (p. 7). Chen et al. [2016] supply the divergence counterexample (p. 7). On the home side, Vanaret and Leyffer [2020] are the "Backbone for solving nonlinear problems" (p. 12). That slide contrasts interior-point methods (large scale, no warm starts) with active-set methods (warm starts, no scaling). It notes that the quadratic AL term destroys the independence of block updates needed for scaling (p. 12). The prior work is restated, not attacked.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Lecture format. A "Lecture Goals" slide frames the work as three questions (p. 9), and the conclusions are hedged: the filter "seems able" to select ρ (p. 35). There are notational slips: the sign of the y-term differs between (5) and (9) (pp. 7, 11), and there are typos ("seuqnce", "afer", p. 18; "concensus", p. 7; "Sylverster", p. 28). The theorem is stated twice in different words (pp. 17-18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithm and theory: a filter-globalized multiblock ADMM for nonlinear equality constraints with a convergence theorem (pp. 16-18, 35), plus an application to NMF/NMFC and spectra (pp. 21-34). The stated future directions are concrete. Theory: convergence with nonsmooth regularizers ("BCD - already have drafted"), a "Filter convergence adjustment", accelerations. Practice: "Write real package that generalizes to nonlinear problems" and "Incorporate into existing code?" (p. 35). At talk time (2022) there was no software package (p. 35).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ evidence (the filter of the home toolkit, in its augmented-Lagrangian form [Vanaret and Leyffer 2020], is carried into ADMM, a field whose standard theory assumes linear constraints, fixed ρ and a priori function knowledge; S159 slides pp. 6-16). Method 3 ✅ evidence (the redesign is motivated by another group's smallest breaking example, the p = 3 divergence of direct multiblock ADMM for any ρ; S159 slides p. 7). Method 1 ⚠ variant (the outer filter test is interleaved with the inner BCD sweeps rather than wrapped around a converged subsolve, and restoration, filter and BCD are separate building blocks; but it is designed as a new algorithm rather than built inside an owned solver, and the package is still future work; S159 slides pp. 16, 35). New-pattern candidate: filter as automatic penalty-parameter selector.
**Transferable techniques**:
- Update ADMM multipliers only at filter-acceptable points and raise ρ only on entry to restoration, so that ρ is chosen by the filter rather than a priori (S159 slides pp. 13, 16).
- Measure stationarity of the original problem (ω₀ via the projected Lagrangian gradient), not of the augmented subproblem, inside the filter, and stop on its relative reduction ω₀(x_k)/ω₀(x_0) ≤ ε (S159 slides pp. 13, 15).
- Use a first sweep of projected block steepest descent with step 1/L_i to obtain a Cauchy-type sufficient-decrease bound (16) for block-coordinate descent (S159 slides pp. 16, 19).
- Reformulate bilinear NMF with a splitting variable Z = XY (and W for missing data) so that the nonlinearity sits in a constraint the filter can monitor (S159 slides pp. 21, 26).
**Quotes**:
- "Monitors dual infeasibility error of the original problem while solving the augmented Lagrangian." (p. 15)
- "Filter seems able to select correct ρ values" (p. 35)

### S160 · Optimizing Paths for Adaptive Fly-Scan Microscopy (2026 IEEE 23rd International Symposium on Biomedical Imaging (ISBI) 2026, doi:10.1109/isbi61048.2026.11515614)
**Read level**: full, arXiv version: arXiv:2509.01869v2 [eess.IV], 21 Sep 2025, "Optimizing Paths for Adaptive Fly-Scan Microscopy: An Extended Version" (pp. 1-2). All 11 file pages were read, including the cover sheet, the body §I-§IX and the references. Leyffer is fifth of five authors (p. 2). The ISBI proceedings version (pp. 1-5) was not read, so which parts were cut for it is unknown. Figure images are not in the text layer, so only their captions were read. Page references are file pages ("S160 arXiv").
**Role**: core (but the voice is the first author's; Leyffer is last author and his contribution is not stated) · **Coauthors**: Yu Lu (first author, UC Merced and Argonne), Thomas F. Lynn, Ming Du, Zichao Di (Argonne) (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: an Argonne facility collaboration (scanning x-ray microscopy data from the Advanced Photon Source, p. 7; Argonne LDRD funding, p. 9). It continues the Di-Leyffer-Wild x-ray tomography line, whose 2016 SIAM J. Imaging Sci. paper [31] supplies test image 1 (pp. 5-6, 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a measurement-efficiency problem at a light source. Raster fly-scans spend time in "uniform, uninteresting regions" (abstract, p. 2). Adaptive scanning exists only for step-scans, mostly through deep learning, which "heavily depends on the quality of the initial sampling, requires a large dataset for training, and incurs high computational costs" (abstract, p. 2). "To the best of our knowledge, these adaptive scanning approaches have not yet been extended to fly-scan" (p. 3). The problem is stated as an iteration f^{k+1} = R(F(Γ*_k)): an optimal path, then a fly-scan, then IDW reconstruction (1), with the anchor points chosen by (3) (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a data-hungry learned method dominates a measurement-design problem, propose an "analytical" alternative built from an explicit objective, a heuristic path and a cheap interpolation, and say why (dataset needs, training time) (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Four sub-problems per scan iteration (p. 3; Algorithm 1, p. 5). (a) Score function G: pick N initial anchor points with the largest normalised image-gradient magnitude (9) (p. 4). (b) Objective L = −(α log U + log ‖∇I‖) (5). It combines an "exponentially weighted uncertainty function" U (6), built from softmax-weighted normalised squared distances to the M_i nearest previous anchor points with length scale ℓ, with the gradient norm of the current reconstruction (7). This is meant to "balance exploration and exploitation", "inspired by acquisition functions typically used for Bayesian optimization" (p. 4). The anchors are moved by ADAM steps (8) (p. 4). (c) Path: instead of solving the TSP, a nearest-neighbour heuristic (10) over a candidate subset χ. The authors argue that the TSP is expensive and "the true shortest path may not be optimal for our objective function" (pp. 3, 5). (d) Completion: IDW interpolation along the scanned path (11) (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Motion blur is kept "well under control" by high-flux sources (p. 2). The images are noise-free and σ = 1 is fixed (pp. 7-8). The simulated fly-scan takes 1 pixel = 1 nm, speed 1 nm/s, exposure 0.5 s, dead time 0.02 s (p. 5). The reference image is the raster fly-scan result, "not the real ground truth" (p. 6). Hardware turning limits are ignored: the path may have sharp angles the motor cannot follow (p. 8). The loss is acknowledged to be a surrogate for the unknown ground-truth distance, and ADAM "converges to a local minimizer" (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a. There are no theorems or proofs. Design arguments are verbal: why both terms of (5) are needed (p. 4), why the score function improves the start of ADAM (p. 4), how ℓ shifts the balance between exploration and exploitation (p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**: Four images: a synthetic shapes image from [31], the Shepp-Logan phantom and Cameraman (256×256), and a WSe2 nanoflake APS image (401×81, raw data public [35]) (pp. 5, 7). The single baseline is "a uniform random selection of points instead of those obtained by optimizing using our objective function" (p. 5). Each experiment is run 10 times on a 2023 MacBook Air M3 and averaged (p. 5). The settings: 30 random initial anchors (under 7% of raster samples), N = 600 and K = 16 (N = 500, K = 12 for image 4), α = 10, ℓ = 4, S = 50 (p. 5). Metrics are PSNR and SSIM (pp. 5-6). Table I, in PSNR/SSIM for initial / random / proposed: shapes 7.3/0.62, 15.1/0.74, 23.7/0.91 at 21.4%; phantom 12.8/0.70, 17.6/0.82, 22.4/0.88 at 16.2%; Cameraman 18.6/0.63, 24.9/0.71, 30.1/0.87 at 22.4%; WSe2 16.9/0.52, 21.3/0.69, 28.3/0.85 at 23.5% (p. 6). A sensitivity study on image 4 varies one parameter with the other fixed: α ∈ {0.1,…,110}, max difference about 2 dB; ℓ ∈ {0.1,…,20}, under 2.5 dB (pp. 7-8). The parameters were found by testing "all combinations of potential parameter values to find the optimal PSNR" on image 4, which is also a reported test image (p. 8). There are no timings, no comparison with a TSP path or with learned adaptive step-scans, and no failure cases. The Total Samples column (262139 for a 256×256 image) is not defined in the text (p. 6).
  - Transferable: give a same-budget random-selection baseline for any "smart sampling" objective, so that the gain from the objective is separated from the gain of the completion step (pp. 5-6).
  - Transferable: report sensitivity one hyperparameter at a time with the others fixed, and state the maximum metric spread in absolute and relative terms ("approximately 2 dB, corresponding to less than an 8% variation") (p. 8).
- **D6 Positioning**: Claims priority without a direct competitor: "this paper is the first to investigate adaptive fly-scan path generation" (p. 8), and similarly on p. 9. It places itself between raster/space-filling scans [5], [9], [10], adaptive step-scans [6], [11], [12] and image/matrix completion [13]-[18] (p. 3). IDW is chosen over advanced completion methods because the scanned data are already "feature-dense" (p. 3). The TSP literature [22], [23] is cited and set aside on cost grounds (pp. 3, 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contribution bullets come early (p. 3). There is promotional language ("very 'smart'", p. 7; "very straightforward", p. 4) and generalisation claims from four images ("strong generalization capability", p. 6). The numbers are slightly inconsistent: the initial scan is "less than 7%" (p. 5), "approximately 6%" (p. 8), and "7.1%" in the Fig. 2 caption (p. 6). A full Discussion section lists limitations before the conclusion (pp. 8-9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithm and application: an iterative adaptive fly-scan framework using under 30% of raster measurements (pp. 3, 9). The limitations are specific: a random initial path can miss features concentrated in a small region (p. 8), sharp path angles are infeasible for motors (p. 8), and noise is not handled, with IDW possibly creating misleading gradients from noisy neighbours (pp. 8-9). The future directions are concrete: a self-adaptive initial scan with a verification layer and path-smoothness penalties, denoising (TV regularisation, a diagonal noise matrix), learned or unrolled acquisition, and 3D ptycho-tomography (p. 9).

**Method links**: Method 2 ⚠ variant (the problem arrives through a facility collaboration, APS data and LDRD funding (pp. 7, 9), as SKILL.md's Method 2 limitation describes for post-2013 extensions. But none of the home NLP/MINLP toolkit is carried in: the path problem is recognised as a TSP and then solved by a greedy nearest-neighbour heuristic, and the anchors are moved by ADAM (S160 arXiv pp. 3-5)). Method 5 ✗ contradiction (the only baseline is same-budget random selection; there is no TSP path, no learned adaptive scan, no timing and no failure cases, and the hyperparameters were tuned on a reported test image (S160 arXiv pp. 5-8). This is the first author's paper with Leyffer last, so it is weak evidence about his own practice). Method 6 ⚠ variant (a formulation argument is made, that the exact shortest path is not the right objective, but it leads to a heuristic rather than to a better formulation; S160 arXiv p. 3). New-pattern candidate: exploration-exploitation acquisition objective for adaptive measurement.
**Transferable techniques**:
- Two-term acquisition objective for where to measure next: log-uncertainty from distance to already-measured points plus log gradient magnitude of the current reconstruction, weighted by α (S160 arXiv p. 4).
- Initialise a local optimiser over measurement locations from a gradient-proportional sampling distribution, so that a local method like ADAM starts near informative regions (S160 arXiv p. 4).
- Same-budget random-selection control for adaptive-sampling claims (S160 arXiv pp. 5-6).

### S162 · Correction: McCormick envelopes in mixed-integer PDE-constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming 218:591-592 2026, doi:10.1007/s10107-026-02330-8)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-2, all of it: abstract, corrected Assumption 2.7 2., corrected Proposition 2.14, modified proof step, licence notes, the two references). Author correction to S096 (doi:10.1007/s10107-024-02181-1), confirmed on p. 1. In the text layer the symbol Ω is lost ("BV()", p. 1) and several arrows are garbled (p. 2). S096 itself is not read in this batch, so the context of the original Assumption 2.12 and the problems (McC)/(McChh) is known only from what the correction restates.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Paul Manns (TU Dortmund; corresponding author mark "B" before his name, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the MIPDECO line. SKILL.md's Signature Work Anatomy lists this correction under the McCormick paper ("Two errors corrected in print ...; who found them: not stated"), and Method 2's limitation cites the unique-solvability fix.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Self-correction. "We correct two mistakes in the paper [S. Leyffer and P. Manns, Math. Prog. 2025]" (p. 1). Mistake 1: Assumption 2.7 2. lacks the box constraints on w. Without them, unique solvability of the PDE (2) for all w ∈ BV(Ω) "is unrealistic given that (2) may become an eigenvalue problem for suitable w" (p. 1). Mistake 2: in the proof of Proposition 2.14, the lim sup-inequality was derived "with a feasibility argument for the PDE (2) instead of the PDE constraining (McChh)" (p. 1). Who found the mistakes: not stated (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: The repair restricts the assumption and adds a hypothesis. It does not re-engineer the proof. Corrected Assumption 2.7 2. requires a unique solution to (2) for all w ∈ BV(Ω) with w(x) ∈ [w_l, w_u] a.e. (p. 1). Proposition 2.14 is restated (pp. 1-2). The limit (ū, w̄, z̄) of the discretized McCormick minimizers minimizes (McC) "If, in addition", for some minimizer (u, w, z) of (McC), the projected triple (u_h, P_h w, P_h z) is feasible for (McChh) as h ↘ 0, i.e. u_h = A⁻¹(P_h z − f) (p. 2). The abstract calls this assuming "sufficient compatibility of the solutions for the PDE constraining (McChh) with the locally averaged McCormick envelopes" (p. 1). No algorithm is changed.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.12 (its parts 1. and 3. are used; their content is in S096, not read) (p. 2). The new box constraint w ∈ [w_l, w_u] a.e. in the solvability assumption (p. 1). The added compatibility hypothesis on (McChh) feasibility of the projected optimum (p. 2). The function spaces are U × W × H, with weak-* convergence in W (p. 2). The correction says outright that the original assumption was unrealistic (p. 1).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The proof is a Γ-convergence-style argument: compactness gives an accumulation point (the first part of Proposition 2.14, unchanged), then lim inf- plus lim sup-inequality, so that a limit of minimizers is a minimizer, citing "the proof of Proposition 1.18 in [1]" (Braides, Gamma-Convergence for Beginners) (p. 2). Only the lim sup step changes. Take (u, w, z) optimal for (McC), and use the assumed feasibility of (u_h, P_h w, P_h z) for (McChh). Lebesgue's differentiation theorem with Assumption 2.12 1. gives P_h z → z in H. Continuity of A⁻¹ and j with Assumption 2.12 3. gives j(u,w) + αTV(w) = lim j(u_h, P_h w) + αTV_h(P_h w) (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a lim sup (recovery-sequence) step needs feasibility for the discretized relaxation, check that the recovery sequence is feasible for the discretized constraint (here the PDE inside (McChh)), not for the continuous one. Where this cannot be proved, state it as an explicit hypothesis of the proposition.
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (a two-page correction; no computations).
- **D6 Positioning**: Cites only the original paper [2] and Braides [1] (p. 2). No other work is mentioned, and no external corrector is credited (pp. 1-2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Plain and complete. The first sentence names the count of mistakes; each mistake is followed by the corrected statement printed in full; "The proof remains the same except for the last step" limits the scope of the change (pp. 1-2). The reason for the first error is given as a mechanism (eigenvalue problem), not just as an omission (p. 1). There is no hedging.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory maintenance: a restricted solvability assumption and a weakened Proposition 2.14 (now conditional on a compatibility/feasibility hypothesis) (pp. 1-2). The cost of the repair, which is not discussed: the convergence of discretized McCormick minimizers to (McC) minimizers now holds only when the compatibility hypothesis holds, and the correction gives no sufficient condition for it (p. 2). Whether the computational example of S096, whose assumptions S096 says it certifies, also satisfies the new hypothesis is not addressed (pp. 1-2). No future work is stated.
