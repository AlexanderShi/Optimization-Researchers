# Batch s13 · Sven Leyffer · supplement

**Batch intent**: Five 2022 items from Leyffer's Argonne collaborations outside classic convex MINLP: binary optimal experimental design by policy gradient (S068), a first-order trust-region method for bound-constrained MPCCs (S092), binary PDE-constrained cloak design (S128), bilevel and robust weight tuning for high-energy-physics event generators (S136), and a SIAM News workshop report on AI for Earth system models (S171). ### S068 · Stochastic learning approach for binary optimization: Application to Bayesian optimal design of experiments (SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing 2022, doi:10.1137/21m1404363; arXiv:2101.05958)
**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract and introduction with the contributions paragraph (pp. 1-3), the stochastic formulation, Proposition 3.1 and Lemma 3.2 with proofs (pp. 5-8), Algorithm 3.1 (pp. 9-10), the statements of §3.4 and how Lemma 3.4 is used (pp. 10, 13), the start of §3.5 (p. 14), the cost discussion (pp. 16-18), the 2-D example (pp. 18-19), the advection-diffusion setup and results text (pp. 22-26), and the conclusions (pp. 27-28). Not read: the proofs of Lemmas 3.3-3.6 (pp. 10-16), the forward-model details (pp. 20-22) and the appendices. The file is the arXiv version, titled "Stochastic Learning Approach to Binary Optimization for Optimal Design of Experiments" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ahmed Attia (first author), Todd Munson (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Argonne binary-design work next to MIPDECO; the binary-control line seen from the stochastic-optimization side.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Sensor placement for Bayesian inverse problems is a binary program with an ℓ0-type penalty (pp. 1-2). The usual route relaxes to [0,1], uses gradients and then rounds, but the relaxed and binary solutions "are not guaranteed to be related" (p. 2). The relaxed gradient costs many model solves, and ℓ0 must be smoothed by ℓ1 or by continuation (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open by naming the two costs of the standard relaxation (design gradient, smoothing the penalty) and the gap it leaves (no tie between relaxed and binary optima), then make a method that removes all three.
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace min J(ζ) over {0,1}^ns by min over θ ∈ [0,1]^ns of Υ(θ) = E_{ζ∼Bernoulli(θ)} J(ζ) (eq. 3.2, p. 5). The gradient needs only values of J at sampled designs (score-function estimator, Algorithm 3.1, p. 10), so J need not be differentiable in ζ and ℓ0 can be used directly (pp. 2, 7-8). The authors note the algorithm "is equivalent to the vanilla policy gradient REINFORCE algorithm" (p. 10). Variance reduction with an optimal baseline gives Algorithm 3.2 (pp. 14, 16-17).
  - Transferable: to optimise a binary design without gradients in the design, optimise the parameters of a product-Bernoulli distribution over designs; Υ is multilinear and agrees with J at the vertices.
- **D3 Assumptions**: independent Bernoulli activation of each candidate sensor; the error covariance structure is kept (p. 5). Experiments use linear forward operators and A-optimality (p. 3). Convergence "in expectation only" with a Robbins-Monro step schedule (pp. 9, 13, 28).
- **D4 Analysis**: Proposition 3.1: [0,1]^ns is the convex hull of {0,1}^ns, the values of Υ form conv(C) of the objective values, Υ = J at vertices, and min Υ = min J (p. 6; proof via Carathéodory, p. 7). Lemma 3.2: binary optima are optima of the stochastic problem, and a unique binary optimum is the unique θ-optimum (p. 7). Lemma 3.3 bounds the gradient and Hessian; Lemma 3.4 shows the estimator is unbiased with bounded variance, which meets an assumption of a cited SGD theorem (pp. 10, 13). Proofs of 3.3-3.6 not read.
- **D5 Experiments**: a 2-D linear toy with J = 2ζ1 + 0.5ζ2 + 6.25 (p. 18) and an advection-diffusion sensor placement with 14 candidates, all 2^14 = 16,384 designs enumerated as ground truth (p. 22). Twenty iterations, batch 32, θ(0) = 0.5 (p. 22). With the optimal baseline the sampled designs fall within 1% of the global optimum (p. 24). Sparsity, fixed-budget and learning-rate studies follow (pp. 25-28).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic plus a short theory. The authors state the main open issue: choosing the learning rate, since a Robbins-Monro schedule "may require many iterations" (p. 28). Comparison with sample average approximation is left to "separate works" (p. 28). In the parts I read, the claim of "massive computational savings" over the relaxation (p. 28) rests on the model-solve counts of §3.6 (pp. 16-18), not on a relaxation solver run on the same problem; I found no such run.

**Method links**: Method 2 variant ⚠: the move re-casts a binary design problem into a class with its own machinery (stochastic optimisation and RL policy gradient), not into Leyffer's NLP or filter base (pp. 2-3, 10). Method 5 variant ⚠: the ground truth is honest (full enumeration, p. 22), but the headline saving over relaxation is argued from cost formulas, not measured (pp. 16-18, 28). Method 6 evidence ✅ (weak): the reformulation is chosen so that the nonsmooth ℓ0 penalty needs no smoothing (pp. 7-8).
**Transferable techniques**:
- For binary-design benchmarks, pick a size you can enumerate (2^14 here) and report how close the method gets to the true global optimum within a fixed small iteration budget (pp. 22-24).
- To justify a lifted formulation, prove the three facts in Proposition 3.1 (convex hull of domain, convex hull of values, agreement at vertices); together they give "same optimal value" in a few lines (pp. 6-7).
**Quotes**:
- "In general, however, the numerical solutions of the two problems are not guaranteed to be related or even similar." (p. 2)
- "We also note that Algorithm 3.1 is equivalent to the vanilla policy gradient REINFORCE algorithm [51]." (p. 10)

### S092 · Sequential linearization method for bound-constrained mathematical programs with complementarity constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2022, doi:10.1137/20m1370501; arXiv:2009.14047)
**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract, introduction and related work (pp. 1-2), the algorithm and its subproblem solution (pp. 3-6), the illustrative example (pp. 6-7), the B-stationarity test, assumption, main theorem and proof outline (pp. 7-9), the statement of Lemma 4.5 (p. 10), the second-order steps (pp. 14-16), the BQP example (pp. 16-17), the numerical results (pp. 17-20) and the conclusion and extension (pp. 20-22). The detailed proofs of Lemmas 4.3-4.5 and Theorem 4.2 (pp. 9-13) were skimmed only; the appendices were not read. The file is arXiv v4 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Christian Kirches (TU Braunschweig), Jeffrey Larson, Paul Manns (Argonne; Manns also TU Dortmund) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the MPEC line (2004-2007) restarted in the 2020s as a subproblem solver for an augmented Lagrangian method, with the Kirches-Manns mixed-integer optimal control group.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: bound-constrained MPCCs, min f(x) with bounds on x0 and 0 ≤ x1 ⊥ x2 ≥ 0 (p. 1). The motivation is downstream: they are the subproblems of an augmented Lagrangian method for general MPCCs, after slacks are added (pp. 1-2, eq. 3). The authors know of no analysis of a trust-region method that keeps complementarity feasible at every iterate (p. 2).
  - Transferable: define the new class as the subproblem your outer method (here an augmented Lagrangian) would generate, so that the solver has a clear downstream use.
- **D2 Core idea**: SLPCC: each iteration solves an LPCC (linearised objective, complementarity kept, ℓ∞ trust region) and accepts by the ratio of actual to linear predicted reduction; the inner loop halves Δ, the outer loop resets it to at least a fixed Δ (Algorithm 1, p. 4). The ℓ∞ box makes the LPCC separable into n0 one-dimensional LPs and n1 two-dimensional LPCCs, each solved by checking at most four points (Props 2.3-2.4, pp. 5-6). The authors read this as a projected-gradient method (Remark 2.5, p. 6). Optional second-order steps: a Cauchy search along a kinked path, and a bound-constrained QP on the active set the LPCC identified (pp. 14-16).
  - Transferable: choose the trust-region norm that makes the subproblem separate by coordinate, then solve each piece in closed form by listing candidate points.
- **D3 Assumptions**: f is C¹ with locally Lipschitz gradient (Assumption 4.1, p. 8); a feasible start, made by projection when needed (p. 4). Coercivity is deliberately not assumed: unboundedness is detected in the code by f(x^k) ≤ −U (p. 9). Second-order steps assume C² (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis**: Theorem 4.2: finite termination at a B-stationary point, or every accumulation point is B-stationary (pp. 8-9). Outline: Taylor reduction lemma (Lemma 4.3); finite inner loop (Lemma 4.4); near a feasible non-B-stationary point the LPCC yields an accepted step whose reduction is bounded below by a multiple of the radius (Lemma 4.5) (p. 9). Proposition 4.1 gives a constructive test for B-stationarity: a feasible descent direction for some partition of the biactive set (p. 8). The trust-region reset to at least Δ stops the radius going to zero too quickly (p. 7). The BQP step changes only a factor 1/2 in the proof (p. 16).
  - Transferable: keep acceleration steps outside the convergence proof by accepting them only if they do at least as well as the first-order step; then the global result does not depend on them (pp. 4, 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: Python code; 40 random quadratic instances in four sets and 20 nonlinear instances (pp. 17-19); compared with filterSQP, IPOPT, MINOS and SNOPT on the x1ᵀx2 ≤ 0 reformulation, by outer/major iterations, with times and objective values in the appendix tables (pp. 17-20). Failures are counted in the profile, MINOS runs with loose tolerances are kept, and four infeasible MINOS runs are excluded and said so (pp. 18-19). A MacMPEC nash1 test of the augmented Lagrangian is in the conclusion (pp. 20-21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithm plus convergence theory, with a stated motivation. Limits are written plainly: the plain method behaves like steepest descent and is "at best linear" (p. 17). The runs are slower than filterSQP, MINOS and SNOPT, and cauchy is "often the slowest solver", which they put down to Python (pp. 19-20). Degenerate instances end with a radius contracted to zero, "which we count as a failure of our algorithm" (p. 19). Extension: general mixed complementarity as in AMPL, without slacks (pp. 21-22).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅: a two-variable MPCC (eq. 8) on which exact-Hessian SQP converges to an M-stationary point that is not B-stationary; the authors give this as the reason to design with linear rather than quadratic models (pp. 6-7). Method 2 evidence ✅: the NLP trust-region toolkit and the SLQP idea (active set from an LP, then an EQP) are carried into MPCCs, with the BQP step as the stated change (pp. 14-16). Method 5 evidence ✅: failures counted, filtered runs named, per-iteration costs said to differ, and losses on time reported (pp. 17-20). Method 6 evidence ✅: the NLP baselines get the inequality form x1ᵀx2 ≤ 0, with a citation that the equality form is worse (p. 17). New-pattern candidate: **linear-model trust region on a combinatorial feasible set**: a first-order model and a trust-region norm chosen so that the subproblem separates and is solved by enumeration, with acceptance by the actual/predicted ratio (pp. 3-6). S128 uses the same construction (Hamming ball, knapsack).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When comparing an iteration-cheap method with SQP or interior-point solvers, say that iteration counts are used only to check "a reasonable number of outer iterations", and give times as reported by each solver (p. 17).
- Detect unboundedness by a large-negative objective test in the code instead of adding a coercivity assumption to the theorem (p. 9).
**Quotes**:
- "This example is a reason for developing our approach using sequential linear models rather than a sequential quadratic approach, which would suffer from the same behavior as an SQP approach." (p. 7)
- "Global convergence of Algorithm 1 to B-stationary points does not depend on—and is not hampered by—these optional steps." (p. 4)

### S128 · A mixed-integer PDE-constrained optimization formulation for electromagnetic cloaking (SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing 2022, doi:10.1137/20m1315993)
**Read level**: partial. Read: report cover (p. 1), abstract, introduction and outline (pp. 3-4), the nominal and uncertain formulations (pp. 4-5, start of §2.2), the reduced-space relaxation (p. 11), §4 with the rounding heuristic and Algorithm 4.1 (pp. 11-13), the experimental setup and nonconvexity study (pp. 13-15), the nominal results text and Table 1 (pp. 15, 18), the uncertainty results text and Tables 4-5 (p. 20), and the conclusions (pp. 21-22). Not read: the adjoint and weak gradient derivations (pp. 7-10), the FEM details (pp. 10-11) and most figures. The file is the Argonne preprint ANL/MCS-P9277-0120, January 2020 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ryan H. Vogt (North Carolina State University, first author), Todd Munson (p. 3) · **Place in trajectory**: a MIPDECO application from the period of the MIPDECO test-problem programme, using the discrete trust-region idea of the Hahn-Sager-Leyffer report it cites as [10] (pp. 12, 22).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: electromagnetic cloak design is usually done by topology optimisation; here it is posed as a MIPDECO with one binary per cell of the design region, a 2-D Helmholtz state equation with absorbing boundary conditions, and the objective of minimising the response in the cloaked region (abstract and pp. 3-4). The extension takes the incidence angle as uncertain and minimises the expected objective over a discrete set of angles (pp. 4-5).
- **D2 Core idea**: two phases. (1) Solve the continuous relaxation with a reduced-space quasi-Newton method (TAO blmvm) and round with threshold τ = 0.8 (pp. 11-12, 14). (2) Improve by a steepest-descent trust-region method on binaries: minimise the linearised objective over an ℓ1 ball, which is the Hamming distance, so the subproblem is a knapsack. Steps are accepted or rejected by the actual/predicted reduction rule "taken directly from the nonlinear trust-region method" (Algorithm 4.1, pp. 12-13). Read as rounding plus repeated local branching, but first-order, so each iteration needs only one forward and one adjoint PDE solve (p. 12).
  - Transferable: for binary design with an expensive simulator, linearise at the incumbent and restrict to a Hamming ball; the subproblem becomes a knapsack and only one forward-adjoint pair is needed per iteration.
- **D3 Assumptions**: a discretised 2-D Helmholtz problem (FEniCS, 128×128 mesh) with fixed k0 = 6π and q = 0.75 (pp. 13-14). The state equation is bilinear in (v, u), so the relaxation is nonconvex (p. 11). The method is called "a heuristic approach" (p. 12); termination at Δ < 1 means only that there is no improving point in the unit Hamming neighbourhood (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis**: no convergence theorem in the parts read. The paper checks nonconvexity numerically instead: J is evaluated along the segment between two checkerboard designs, and the curve is nonconvex, so the relaxation value is not a valid lower bound (pp. 14-15).
- **D5 Experiments**: three cloak regions × two angles, with 20×20 and 40×40 control meshes (pp. 13-14). From 25 random starts the TR method lowers the objective and its spread (p. 15). Iterations grow modestly from 400 to 1600 binaries (15-79 iterations, Table 1, p. 18). PDE and gradient solves dominate the time and the knapsack solves are cheap; this also holds for 10 or 15 angles (p. 20). No comparison with a MINLP solver or a topology-optimisation code.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a formulation plus a heuristic algorithm for one application. Stated limits: the relaxation is not a lower bound, and rounding alone can beat it (pp. 15, 18). The direct MINLP route is ruled out from experience on other MIPDECOs (p. 12). The conclusion claims cloaks "effective at inducing electromagnetic cloaking" (p. 21) and gives no future work.

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅: the nonlinear trust-region acceptance test is carried unchanged into a binary design problem (p. 13). Method 6 evidence ✅: the formulation is chosen around what solvers can do: no full-space MINLP with discretised PDEs, because that "does not result in an MINLP that can be solved by modern MINLP solvers" (p. 12); the τ = 0.8 rounding choice is reported with its effect (p. 12). Method 5 variant ⚠: there are no external baselines; the evidence is random starts, the relaxation and rounding (pp. 15, 18). Taste 3 tension ✗ (soft): no guarantee is claimed, and the paper says so ("heuristic", p. 12). New-pattern candidate: **linear-model trust region on a combinatorial feasible set** (pp. 12-13; also S092 pp. 3-6).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Test nonconvexity of a relaxation cheaply: evaluate the objective along the segment between two feasible designs and plot it; if the curve is nonconvex, say that the relaxation value is not a bound (pp. 14-15).
- Report the time split between the simulation (PDE and gradient) and the combinatorial subproblem, to show which part to optimise next (Tables 1, 4, 5, pp. 18, 20).
**Quotes**:
- "However, we found on other MIPDECOs that such an approach does not result in an MINLP that can be solved by modern MINLP solvers." (p. 12)
- "We observe that the relaxed objective function value does not always give a lower bound on the solution." (p. 18)

### S136 · BROOD: Bilevel and robust optimization and outlier detection for efficient tuning of high-energy physics event generators (SciPost Physics Core 2022, doi:10.21468/scipostphyscore.5.1.001; arXiv:2103.05751)
**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract and contents (pp. 1-3), introduction, notation and problem statement (pp. 4-8), the bilevel and robust formulations (pp. 9-13), the two filtering methods (pp. 13-16), the experimental setup and the start of the comparison metrics (pp. 16-18), the A14 metric summary (p. 23), computation times and the start of the eigentunes section (pp. 38-41), and the conclusions (p. 50). Not read: the dataset descriptions and most results (pp. 19-37), the eigentune results and physics discussion (pp. 41-49) and the online supplement (pp. 55-87). The file is arXiv v2 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Wenjing Wang, Mohan Krishnamoorthy, Juliane Müller, Stephen Mrenna, Holger Schulz, Xiangyang Ju, Zachary Marshall (p. 1; Müller and Mrenna corresponding). Leyffer is seventh of eight authors; the text does not say what he contributed. · **Place in trajectory**: a SciDAC-style physics collaboration (funding, p. 51); optimisation formulation work outside MINLP.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: tuning Monte Carlo event-generator parameters means minimising a weighted χ² over histogram bins. The weights per observable are set by hand by experts, which is slow and "inefficient and biased" (p. 4); for A14 this meant looking at hundreds of histograms (p. 7). Aim: automate the weights (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**: (i) a bilevel problem: upper level chooses weights on the simplex to minimise a merit g (portfolio mean+variance of per-observable errors, or median/mean score), lower level fits parameters on a rational surrogate (eqs. 3-9, pp. 9-11). The outer problem is treated as a black box with an RBF surrogate (p. 11). (ii) A single-level robust problem: minimise the worst bin deviation over an uncertainty interval, written with slacks t_b, plus a constraint that keeps the weights away from zero, controlled by μ (eqs. 10-11, p. 12), called "easier to solve than the bilevel problem" (p. 12). (iii) Pre-processing: drop outlier observables by a Z-score on per-observable ideal fits (p. 14), and drop bins by finding the longest run of contiguous bins that passes a χ² test. This is posed as an integer program and solved in polynomial time with a maximum-subarray algorithm (eq. 15, p. 16).
  - Transferable: when a subset-selection integer program has a contiguity constraint, check whether it reduces to a maximum-subarray-type scan before calling a MIP solver (p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions**: bins are independent (p. 7, restated as a limitation on p. 50); the surrogate fits the MC simulation (fidelity checked later, footnote p. 14); discrepancies are assumed to come from the model's domain, not from analysis mistakes (p. 13). The bilevel inner problem is multimodal, handled with 100 multistarts (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a: no theorems; the degrees of freedom of the χ² test are reduced to |B| − d because the bins share parameters (p. 15).
- **D5 Experiments**: ATLAS A14 and Sherpa datasets; methods compared against the Expert tune and all-weights-equal (Table 2, p. 17) on three metrics: weighted χ², A-optimality and log D-optimality of a linearised posterior (pp. 17-18). "No method performs the best for all metrics", and the Expert tune is best on two of three for the full data (p. 23). Bilevel runs take about 14.5 h against weeks or months by hand (p. 39).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: formulation and workflow for an application. The four contributions in the conclusion include "new metrics to easily compare different tunes" (p. 50). Stated limits: for Sherpa many optimal parameters sit on the bounds of the box, so a trust-region outer loop that moves the box is left to future work; bin correlations are ignored (p. 50).

**Method links**: Method 5 evidence ✅: the human expert is kept as a baseline and the result is reported as a split verdict by metric, not one winner (pp. 17, 23). Method 6 evidence ✅: the bilevel problem is turned into a single-level robust problem with slack variables because it is easier to solve (p. 12). Heuristic 10 evidence ✅: new comparison metrics are built because visual inspection of histograms cannot rank tunes (pp. 17, 50). All links are weak for Leyffer personally, since his share of this eight-author paper is not stated.
**Transferable techniques**:
- When an automated method replaces expert judgement, keep the expert's result as a named baseline and score both on several objective metrics; report where the expert still wins (pp. 17, 23).
- Filter data the model cannot describe before fitting: fit each observable on its own, flag outliers of the ideal per-observable errors, and give them weight zero (p. 14).
**Quotes**:
- "Based on these results we can see that no method performs the best for all metrics in all cases." (p. 23)
- "In this work, we assume that each bin is completely independent of all the other bins." (p. 50)

### S171 · Improving Earth System Predictability with Artificial Intelligence (SIAM News 55(6), July/August 2022, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (the article, pp. 3-4 of the issue, read in full). The file is the whole SIAM News issue. The front-page article is by Lai-Sang Young, and the other articles are by other authors; they were not read as Leyffer material. The title and the byline "By Sven Leyffer and Salil Mahajan" were checked (p. 3).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Salil Mahajan (Oak Ridge National Laboratory) (p. 4) · **Place in trajectory**: community service writing: the author note gives Leyffer as ICIAM secretary and SIAM president-elect (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: a report of impressions from the DOE AI4ESP workshop (October 2021; more than 450 attendees and over 150 white papers), written before the official report (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: n/a (no method). The article summarises talks: Schneider's scale-bridging (p. 3), Gentine's learned subgrid parameterisations, Shen's process-based models inside ML, and Zanna's learned closures (p. 4). The authors' own section lists applied-math challenges: knowledge-informed ML, constraints through differentiable convex-optimisation layers, and then "learning of emerging constraints, identification of erroneous constraints, and inclusion of state-of-the-art optimization methods" (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: n/a.
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a service and outreach piece. The only optimisation content is the challenge list (p. 4). The talks are reported in the speakers' voice (pp. 3-4), so they are not evidence of Leyffer's own method.

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅ (weak): from the AI4ESP material, the authors pick constrained learning and the "inclusion of state-of-the-art optimization methods" as the applied-math openings, where an optimiser's toolkit can be carried in (p. 4). Warning sign 1 / Heuristic 8: the brute-force remark is reported from Schneider's talk (p. 3), not stated by Leyffer, so it is not counted as evidence.
**Quotes**:
- "Because the use of brute-force computation to resolve such small-scale phenomena on a global scale is impractical, new approaches are necessary" (p. 3)
- "However, the challenges that accompany the interpretation of ML models persist." (p. 4)
