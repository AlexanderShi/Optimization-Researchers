### S086 · Unsupervised cell identification on multidimensional X-ray fluorescence datasets (Journal of Synchrotron Radiation 2014, doi:10.1107/s1600577514001416)
**Read level**: partial. Read pp. 1-6 (abstract, §1-§3, the §4 overview), pp. 7-12 (§4.1.3-§4.4, §5, §6, §7). Skipped: the preprocessing details §4.1.1-§4.1.2 (pp. 6-7) and the references. The title and Leyffer (third of six authors) are confirmed on p. 1.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Siwei Wang (first, corresponding), Jesse Ward, Stefan M. Wild, Chris Jacobsen, Stefan Vogt · **Place in trajectory**: Argonne MCS–APS imaging collaboration. The voice is the imaging group's, not Leyffer's.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an application: "Where are the (whole) cells?" in XFM element maps (p. 3). It differs from optical microscopy in three ways: many element channels at once, no annotated training set, and overlapping cells whose content must be split (p. 3). Existing XFM packages (MAPS, PCA_GUI) are "not yet reliable enough for unsupervised analysis" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: A four-stage pipeline in which each stage is an optimization problem whose solution feeds the next (p. 3): (i) deliberately over-segment with recursive min-cut and fit minimum-area ellipses (p. 3); (ii) merge or delete groups by a generalized likelihood-ratio test against Gaussian content models estimated from a few hand-drawn ROIs, restricted to alternatives with exactly |G_r| − 1 groups so that it stays tractable (pp. 7-8, Procedure 2); (iii) fit boundaries as Daubechies-wavelet curves by minimizing a dead-zone quadratic content penalty plus a scaled proximal term, solved by gradient sampling accelerated with Gauss-Newton because the objective is nonsmooth from pixelation (p. 9); (iv) a one-pixel add/delete local-optimality test and refinement (pp. 9-10, Procedure 3). Overlapping pixels are split in proportion to the type means, which conserves the map content (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Cells of one type have similar size and shape (modelled as ellipses), similar total content, and independent per-element contents following truncated Gaussians (pp. 3, 5, 7). The test sample was chosen with deliberately distinct cell types (red blood cells: Fe; yeast: Zn and P; algae: Mn) (p. 4), which the authors concede simplifies initialization (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no theorems; the "optimality test" is a stated local-optimality definition, p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: One APS 2-ID-E dataset (666 × 667 pixels, nine elements) is used. With no ground truth, success is judged by whether population content distributions match the ROI means (within about 20%) and literature Fe values (about 50 fg per cell against 60-80 fg) (p. 10). Robustness is checked across the four detector segments (ANOVA finds no significant difference) and across ±20% thresholds (15-20% more or fewer initial groups, but only 5-8% change in the final counts) (pp. 11-12). Runtime is about one hour on a laptop (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an application method: unsupervised identification of overlapping cells over many channels (p. 12). Limits stated concretely: runtime is exponential in the number of cells per region (p. 12), the dataset is easy (distinct contents), there is no quantitative performance metric yet, and the pipeline is one-way. Future work: feed refinement back to configuration estimation, parallelize over regions, handle arbitrary shapes and non-Gaussian contents, use full spectra (p. 12).

**Method links**: Method 2 ⚠ variant: optimization machinery (a GLR model choice, gradient sampling with Gauss-Newton, a proximal regularizer) is carried into imaging (p. 9). However, each stage is a heuristic step with no convergence claim, and the voice is the first author's. New-pattern candidate: validation without ground truth by perturbing inputs and using independent measurements (pp. 11-12).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When no labelled data exist, validate an unsupervised pipeline in two ways: re-run it on independent channels of the same measurement (here four detector segments), and perturb its one sensitive input (threshold ±20%). Then compare the output distributions with ANOVA and report initial against final count variation (pp. 11-12).
- Over-segment deliberately, then only merge or delete. This keeps a likelihood-ratio search one-directional and restricts alternatives to "one group fewer" (pp. 3, 8).
**Quotes**:
- "Currently, each procedure can be viewed as solving an optimization problem, the solution of which is then passed as an input to the next procedure." (p. 3)
- "We are aware that this test dataset contains cells with distinct elemental contents. This condition simplifies the initialization part of our cell identification." (p. 12)

### S097 · An active-set method for second-order conic-constrained quadratic programming (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2015, doi:10.1137/140958025)
**Read level**: partial, ANL preprint version (ANL/MCS-P5085-0214, 19 Feb 2014, titled "Active Set Method for Second-Order Conic-Constrained Quadratic Programming", p. 1). Read pp. 3-8 (abstract, §1, §2.1, the start of §2.2), pp. 12-13 (the start of §2.3), pp. 14-19 (§3: Example 2, the switching rule, Lemma 3, Proposition 1 with proof part (i) and the start of (ii); §4; §5). Skipped: the breakpoint and line-search derivations with Lemmas 1-2 (pp. 8-12), the EQP details, the rest of the Proposition 1 proof, and Appendix A data (p. 20). Leyffer is second of two authors (p. 1). Page references are preprint pages ("S097 preprint").
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Noam Goldberg (first author, Argonne postdoc) · **Place in trajectory**: a conic QP solver built as a warm-startable building block for MISOCP branch-and-bound.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Interior-point SOCP/SDP solvers "cannot be efficiently restarted", which hurts conic relaxations inside branch-and-bound (max-cut, MISOCP, perspective relaxations) (p. 3). General NLP solvers face the nondifferentiability of the cone constraint at the apex. Example 1 shows the NLP first-order conditions are undefined at the optimum (pp. 4-5). Table 1 runs SNOPT, MINOS, filterSQP and IPOPT on four 4-variable SOCPs and records linear convergence on SOCP-2 and IEEE exceptions on SOCP-3 (p. 5).
- **D2 Key idea**: Generalize the Moré–Toraldo two-phase active-set method for bound-constrained QP to second-order cones (pp. 3, 5). Phase I is projected gradient using the closed-form cone projection (p. 7), with breakpoints along the projected path (p. 8). Because the Cauchy point is not in closed form, the method may fall back to a projected backtracking line search on the last segment (p. 6). Phase II applies Newton-type EQP steps on the reduced subspace once the active set settles (pp. 12-13). Convergence is always checked with the projected gradient. The authors found this "more robust" than checking multiplier signs (p. 6). One change from the bound-constrained case: no change in the working set does not mean it has settled (Example 2, p. 15). So the switch also requires a step below τ_P, and τ_P is halved after each fallback to stop oscillation between phases (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Convex QP (G positive semidefinite) over a product of second-order cones, with nonnegativity treated as one-dimensional cones (p. 4). Proposition 1 assumes strict feasibility and strict complementarity at every local minimum (p. 15). Linear constraints Az = b are left to a future augmented-Lagrangian wrapper in the LANCELOT style (pp. 6-7).
- **D4 Analysis**: Proposition 1: (i) the projected-gradient phase alone converges to an optimum, and (ii) it identifies the optimal active set in finitely many steps (pp. 15, 17). The proof reuses Calamai–Moré Lemma 3.1, specialised as Lemma 3 (p. 15), and Bertsekas's stationarity result. It argues the rest "similar to [5, 15]", and reduces to Moré–Toraldo when all cones are one-dimensional (p. 17). Quadratic convergence is then inherited from the EQP phase by citation ([16, Theorem 18.4], p. 18).
- **D5 Experiments**: Random instances (N = 100-2000, 20-100 cones, density 0.1 or 1, 30 runs each) in a well-conditioned set and a set with condition numbers 90-400. Comparison with SDPT3 called through CVX (the reported time includes CVX conversion) and with filterSQP. Algorithm 3 is an order of magnitude faster, and the Newton phase never triggers on the well-conditioned set (pp. 18-19, Tables 2-3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm with finite identification of the active set. Stated limits: the advantage shrinks as the condition number grows (p. 19), and there are no linear constraints yet. Future work: an augmented-Lagrangian outer loop, structured linear constraints inside the projection, and nonconvex conic QPs (p. 19). Unstated limits visible in the text: random instances only, no MISOCP or branch-and-bound warm-start experiment (the motivating use), and no failure counts.

**Method links**: Method 3 ✅ evidence: four tiny SOCPs that break four NLP solvers, run and tabulated per solver with a comment column (p. 5, Appendix A p. 20). Example 2 is a smallest instance showing that the bound-constrained switching criterion fails for cones (p. 15). Method 6 ✅ evidence: the failure is traced to evaluation outside the square-root domain (IEEE exceptions) and to nondifferentiability at the apex, before a new method is proposed (pp. 4-5). Method 2 ✅ evidence: the bound-constrained active-set toolkit (Moré–Toraldo, Calamai–Moré) is carried into a new cone class, with the proofs transferred (pp. 15, 17). Method 1 ✅ evidence: designed "as a building block" for larger classes and for warm-started branch-and-bound (p. 3). Method 5 ⚠ variant: there are baselines, but the SDPT3 timing includes CVX, the instances are random only, and no failures are reported (pp. 18-19).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Motivate a specialised solver with a table of tiny instances (here 4 variables) run through every general-purpose solver, with one comment column per instance (superlinear, linear, IEEE exception) (p. 5).
- In a two-phase active-set method, run the termination test in the phase with global convergence. That test then also checks the identified active set (p. 6).
- When generalizing an active-set switch rule from bounds to curved constraints, look for an iterate sequence with an unchanged working set that has not settled. Add a step-size condition, and halve the threshold on every fallback (p. 15).
**Quotes**:
- "The examples illustrate that nonlinear solvers are not sufficiently robust to solve general conically constrained optimization problems and that IEEE errors may even result in failure." (p. 5)
- "Verifying termination in the gradient-projection phase also serves to determine the correctness of the identified active set, and we have found it to be a more robust alternative to evaluating the sign of the Lagrange multipliers." (p. 6)

### S111 · Optimal response to epidemics and cyber attacks in networks (Networks 2015, doi:10.1002/net.21619)
**Read level**: partial, ANL preprint version (dated 18 Jan 2012, p. 3). Read pp. 3-12 (abstract, §1, §2, §3.1 up to Lemma 4), p. 13 (Lemma 4 proof, Proposition 5 statement), pp. 17-24 (§3.2, §4, §5). Skipped: pp. 13-16 (the rest of the Proposition 5 proof, Propositions 6-7, Corollary 8, Algorithm 1 cover separation). Leyffer is second of three authors (p. 3). Page references are preprint pages ("S111 preprint").
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Noam Goldberg (first), Ilya Safro · **Place in trajectory**: follows Altunay, Leyffer, Linderoth and Xie (2011), the Open Science Grid security model, which is reference [1] (p. 24).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The authors re-read their own earlier deterministic network-response MINLP (2) in probabilistic terms. Their new probabilistic model (4) bounds each open node's infection probability through a product of neighbour terms, which gives a nonconvex multilinear INLP (pp. 6, 8). The firefighter literature is criticised as too deterministic and iterative for a one-time decision (p. 5).
- **D2 Key idea**: Replace the exact product constraint by a more conservative constraint from Hunter's bound. That bound uses the first-order terms minus joint terms along a maximum spanning tree of the neighbours, which gives a quadratic, linearizable formulation (6). Its solutions are feasible for (4) (pp. 9-10), and it does not need the independence assumption (p. 10). Cover inequalities are then extended to constraints whose left-hand side is not monotone. Lemma 4 gives a weaker-than-monotone lower bound, and Proposition 5 gives a sufficient condition for validity that can be tested in linear time (pp. 12-13). An LP-rounding knapsack heuristic finds feasible solutions (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Single time period. Infection probabilities of the previous period are independent; this is acknowledged as a simplification, which the Hunter-bound model removes (pp. 7, 10). Propagation probabilities follow Altunay et al. (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis**: NP-hardness of (2), (4) and (6) is proved by reductions from maximum independent set on a doubled graph with pendant nodes (Propositions 1-3, pp. 7-11). Validity of the cover inequalities uses the tree structure, which bounds the number of quadratic terms by |S(y)\S(x)| − 1 (Lemma 4, p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: The exact model (4) (BARON 9.3.1) is compared with the union-bound and Hunter-bound approximations (CPLEX 12.3) on the OSG-1 network. Hunter's bound has average relative error 0.01-0.07 against 0.06-0.15 for the union bound, and (4) at |V| = 60 is unsolved after 2 h (pp. 10-11, Table 1). Cuts and heuristic are tested on Erdős–Rényi graphs (50-90 nodes, densities 0.1-0.25, 10 parameter draws each) in CBC with a 2-h limit, adding features one at a time to the CBC defaults. The tables give the number of runs over the limit, and the averages exclude those runs (pp. 17-24, Tables 2-7, performance profile Fig. 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a model, valid inequalities and computation. The cuts give moderate gains in node counts and time, and "# over limit" counts drop in some rows (p. 20). The heuristic did not improve running time, which the paper reports and explains (p. 20). Limits: single period only, with multi-period use suggested through discrete-event simulation (p. 8); small random graphs; CBC preprocessing switched off for implementation reasons (p. 19, footnote).

**Method links**: Method 6 ✅ evidence: the hard multilinear INLP is replaced by a conservative quadratic, linearizable surrogate whose solutions stay feasible. It is chosen because standard MIP solvers handle it while a global MINLP solver times out (pp. 9-11). Method 5 ✅ evidence: the time-limit failures are counted in a column, averages are stated as excluding them, and the heuristic is reported as giving no gain (pp. 19-20). Method 3 ⚠ variant: the "breaking" device is a complexity reduction rather than a numerical counterexample. Monotonicity, the hypothesis that standard covers need, is checked and found to fail, and a weaker condition is substituted (p. 12). Method 2 ✅ evidence: the authors' own 2011 MINLP is re-cast into a probabilistic class (pp. 6-8). New-pattern candidate: a conservative tractable surrogate from a probability bound, which gives a feasible point and is solvable by MIP (pp. 9-11).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To make a chance-type product constraint tractable, replace it by a Hunter (spanning-tree) Bonferroni bound. The result is quadratic, conservative (so feasible for the exact model), and stays valid without independence (pp. 9-10).
- When a constraint is not monotone and standard covers do not apply, prove a one-sided increment bound (Lemma 4) and derive a cover-validity condition that can be checked on the fly before adding the cut (pp. 12-13, 24).
- Report a "# over limit" column, and say explicitly that averages exclude the runs that hit the limit (p. 19).
**Quotes**:
- "Hence, in general gi(x) is not monotone in x and consequently it is not straightforward to apply standard cover inequalities to formulation (6)." (p. 12)
- "The reason may be that CBC heuristics were sufficiently effective in finding feasible solutions and that, in fact, the tightening of the upper bounds, rather than the global lower bound, dominated the computation running times." (p. 20)

### S113 · Visualizing and improving the robustness of phase retrieval algorithms (Procedia Computer Science 51 (ICCS 2015), doi:10.1016/j.procs.2015.05.205)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-10: abstract, §1-§4, references). The title and Leyffer (second of four authors) are confirmed on p. 1.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ashish Tripathi (first), Todd Munson, Stefan M. Wild · **Place in trajectory**: Argonne ptychography/CXDI collaboration. An NLP view of an imaging workhorse algorithm.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Fienup's HIO is "the current workhorse of experimentalists" but stagnates depending on the start (p. 3). Experimentalists run many starts and pick "good" solutions by subjective criteria (p. 10).
- **D2 Key idea**: Re-cast HIO as a heuristic for a Nash equilibrium (saddle point) of a two-player game: minimize inside the support, maximize outside (p. 4). HIO is then the special case α = 1 of a two-step-length update (−αδ_s, +βδ_s̄) (p. 4). Choose (α, β) by a modified Newton step on ‖∇ψ‖² whose Hessian signs are set for min in α and max in β (eq. 10, p. 4). Replace the gradient directions with L-BFGS (complex-valued, inertia set by the sign of the initial scaling) or one of seven nonlinear CG formulas, separately inside and outside the support, with a reset to plain HIO when a direction has the wrong sign (pp. 5-7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: One synthetic 16 × 16 real exit wave with a three-pixel support, support known, one pixel value fixed. This leaves two free unknowns, so the landscape and its four minima (global, global phase shift, two twin-image minima) can be drawn (pp. 6-7).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no theorems). The derivations are a Wirtinger-calculus gradient (8) and the update algebra (9) (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: For each of the 81 inside/outside direction combinations, the method is run from 441 grid starts in [−1.5, 1.5]², each start is coloured by the minimum reached, and the success fraction is plotted. Optimal (α, β) HIO reaches the global minimum from about 75% of starts. L-BFGS inside with HS outside reaches 99%. FR and CD hurt (pp. 7-10, Figs. 4-5). No timing, no noise and no realistic size are reported.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic variants plus a visualization method. The authors state the limit to one low-dimensional synthetic problem in the Outlook. The hoped-for payoff, fewer random starts for experimentalists, is anticipated but not tested (p. 10).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ evidence: the NLP toolkit (Newton with corrected inertia, L-BFGS, nonlinear CG) is carried into phase retrieval, and the field's workhorse is re-cast as a saddle-point problem with HIO as a special case (pp. 4-6). Method 3 ⚠ variant: the smallest instance is used to see the landscape and every basin of attraction, not to produce a counterexample (pp. 6-8). New-pattern candidate: a basin-of-attraction map from a grid of starts on a reduced problem whose minima are known (pp. 7-9).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Reduce a high-dimensional nonconvex problem to two free unknowns while keeping its symmetry-induced minima. Run each algorithm variant from a grid of starts, colour each start by the minimum reached, and summarise as the fraction of starts that reach the global minimum (pp. 7-9).
- Write the field's heuristic as a special case of a parameterised optimization step (here α = 1), then optimize the parameters per iteration (p. 4).
- When mixing quasi-Newton or CG directions into a min-max iteration, check the sign of each directional derivative and reset to the plain gradient step when it is wrong (p. 7).
**Quotes**:
- "HIO with optimal (α, β) can find the global minimum mG about 75% of the time out of all 441 starting points explored." (p. 7)
- "Solutions obtained in this way are invariably different, and what are considered “good” solutions is sometimes left to more subjective, qualitative criteria." (p. 10)

### S156 · Oscillation Mitigation in Electric Power Grids via Semidefinite Programming (Tech. rep. ANL/MCS-P5345-0515, Argonne National Laboratory 2015, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-5: cover, abstract, §I-§VI, references). The title and Leyffer (second of two authors) are confirmed on pp. 1-2.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Fu Lin (first, Argonne postdoc) · **Place in trajectory**: power-grid side project. Precursor to the Lin–Leyffer SDP work (S185).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Inter-area oscillations are blackout precursors, and local damping is not enough (p. 2). The literature analyses the eigenvalues of a given system. This paper designs inertia, damping and line susceptances so that the spectrum lies in a performance region (decay band plus damping sector) (pp. 2-3). Constraints on the spectrum of a nonsymmetric matrix are nonconvex and nonsmooth (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: Assume identical generators (M_i = α, D_i = β). Then every eigenvalue of the linearized swing system is an explicit function of a Laplacian eigenvalue, γ = (−β ± √(β² − 4αλ))/(2α) (Proposition 3.1, p. 3). The region constraint becomes a band on the Laplacian spectrum, c₂ ≤ λ₂ and λ_N ≤ c_N, which is an SDP that minimizes total susceptance (Algorithm 1, p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linearized swing equation (negligible conductances, small angle differences, unit voltages) (p. 3). Identical generators, the assumption that convexifies the problem (p. 3). α and β are chosen by hand to satisfy (8) before the SDP (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis**: Proposition 3.1 is proved by eliminating v₂ from the eigenvector equation (p. 3). Proposition 4.1 (the SDP solution places the spectrum in the region) uses the fact that J = 11ᵀ/N spans the nullspace of L, so L + c₂J shifts only the zero eigenvalue (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: One 5-generator series network solved with YALMIP. The designed system halves the peak angle-difference amplitude and the oscillation length under a decaying sinusoidal disturbance (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a model and an SDP design method (p. 4). Limits visible in the text: the identical-generator assumption, a single 5-node example, and α and β not optimized jointly with L. No future work is stated (pp. 4-5).

**Method links**: Method 6 ✅ evidence: a nonconvex, nonsmooth spectral constraint is turned into a convex SDP by a structural modelling assumption, not by a new algorithm (pp. 3-4). Method 2 ⚠ variant: the "home" tools here are conic/SDP modelling, not filter or SQP, and the voice is likely the first author's. New-pattern candidate: a structural assumption that convexifies a spectral design constraint (p. 3).
**Transferable techniques**:
- For design problems on second-order network dynamics, look for an assumption (identical nodes) under which the system matrix's eigenvalues are explicit functions of the Laplacian's. Spectrum-region constraints then become linear matrix inequalities on L, with J = 11ᵀ/N used to step over the zero eigenvalue (pp. 3-4).
**Quotes**:
- "However, constraints on the spectrum of a generic A-matrix in (3) are hard constraints." (p. 3)
- "For identical generators, we obtain an explicit relation between the spectrum of the linearized dynamics and the spectrum of the interconnection matrix." (p. 2)

### S072 · A two-level approach to large mixed-integer programs with application to cogeneration in energy-efficient buildings (Computational Optimization and Applications 2016, doi:10.1007/s10589-016-9842-0; arXiv:1504.04589)
**Read level**: partial, arXiv v1 version (file pp. 1-34 of arXiv:1504.04589v1, 17 Apr 2015, p. 1). Read pp. 1-6 (abstract, §1, §1.1-§1.2, the start of §2.1), pp. 7-10 (the end of Proposition 2.1, Propositions 2.2-2.3, §2.2, Algorithm 1, the Kronecker form), pp. 10-11 (the §3 intro), pp. 12-14 (§3.4, Algorithm 2 and Proposition 3.1, partly), pp. 14-23 (§4, §5). Skipped: the semi-coarse model algebra (pp. 6-7), §3.1-§3.3 (pp. 11-12), appendices A-D (pp. 24-34). The COA version was not read. Page references are arXiv pages ("S072 arXiv").
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Fu Lin (first), Todd Munson · **Place in trajectory**: scale-driven MILP work in the Argonne energy portfolio. Multigrid thinking applied to integer programs.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A two-stage MILP that combines investment with hourly on/off operation over 10 years. That is 1.05 million second-stage binaries (3650 days × 24 h × 12 units), and the coupling constraints are as numerous as the variables. Lagrangian relaxation and scenario decomposition therefore do not apply (p. 2). Solving with short horizons gives first-stage designs that are suboptimal for long horizons (p. 2), which is shown later in Table 3 (pp. 17, 21).
- **D2 Key idea**: There are two coarsenings, named after multigrid (p. 3). "Primal" coarsening replaces hourly binaries by a choice among a few daily on/off profiles, plus convex combinations of continuous profiles. This is a restriction, so its optimum is an upper bound, and it is exact if the optimal profiles are included (Proposition 2.2, pp. 7-8). "Dual" coarsening sums constraints over each day-group. This is a relaxation of the semi-coarse model, so violated rows are added back until feasible (p. 9). An LP-relaxation warm-start phase picks the initial row set before any MILP re-solve, because MILPs "do not warm-start" (p. 9, Algorithm 1). Profiles are harvested by solving short moving-horizon MILPs, borrowed from MPC; they are valid by construction (Proposition 3.1), then selected by frequency, extremes and k-means (pp. 12-14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: m ≪ N and m ≪ M, with no sparsity assumed in B, C and D (p. 2). The data are EnergyPlus demand for five Chicago buildings, interpolated between summer and winter weeks with 2% Gaussian noise (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis**: Proposition 2.2 (restriction gives an upper bound, exact with the optimal profiles) is a feasibility-mapping argument. Finite termination of the row-addition loop follows because only finitely many rows exist (p. 9). Proposition 2.3 is immediate (p. 10). The Kronecker-product form of the restriction and aggregation operators is given (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: CPLEX 12.6.1 in AMPL, 3-h limit, 1% gap. The full model grows exponentially in time with horizon length: 84 days still has more than 18% gap after 3 h, and without the time limit the tree fills a 100 GB disk (p. 17, Table 3, footnote). The semi-coarse model solves 10 years in about 3,350 s with stable first-stage decisions (p. 19, Table 4). The first coarse iterate is within µ ≤ 0.05 of the semi-coarse optimum for all buildings (pp. 20-21). The LP warm-start cuts MILP re-solves from 6 to 2, and each is 5-20 times faster (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm and an application at a scale beyond CPLEX (pp. 4, 23). Stated open problems: how to add profiles dynamically (pricing as in column generation); how to get lower bounds, because "the coarse model is not a relaxation of the original MILP" and the LP bound gap is "sizable"; and multi-sweep fine/coarse cycles as in PDE multigrid (p. 23).

**Method links**: Method 6 ✅ evidence: the model is re-formulated (profiles, row aggregation, constraints embedded in profile generation that remove about 4.2 × 10⁶ rows) before any change of algorithm, and CPLEX logs (nodes, LP iterations, gap, disk) are read as the diagnosis (pp. 17-18). Method 5 ✅ evidence: the paper states that the method yields only an upper bound and leaves the lower bound open (p. 23). Method 2 ⚠ variant: the carried-in toolkit is multigrid/multilevel from PDE solvers, not filter or SQP (pp. 3-4, 23). New-pattern candidate: multilevel coarsening for MIP, where a restriction gives an upper bound and aggregation a relaxation that is closed by adding rows back (pp. 3, 7-9); the same pattern appears in S185.
**Transferable techniques**:
- Build two coarsenings with known directions. A variable restriction to precomputed patterns gives an upper bound, exact if the optimal pattern is present. Constraint aggregation gives a relaxation that is closed by adding violated rows. Their combination solves the restricted model exactly (pp. 7-10).
- Before re-solving MILPs in a row-generation loop, run the same loop on the LP relaxation to collect most of the needed rows, because MILPs do not warm-start (p. 9). Then report re-solve counts with and without this phase (p. 21).
- Harvest valid patterns from moving-horizon solves of short instances, which are feasible by construction. Select by frequency or extremes and cluster the continuous profiles (pp. 12-14).
- Show the scale problem with a horizon-length table (time, nodes, LP iterations and first-stage decisions against days). It shows both intractability and the bias of short horizons (p. 17).
**Quotes**:
- "Since the coarse model is a relaxation of the semi-coarse model, which itself is a tightening of the full model, the coarse model is not a relaxation of the original MILP." (p. 23)
- "It is an open problem how to generate lower bounds using appropriate coarse MILPs." (p. 23)

### S185 · A multilevel approach for a class of semidefinite programs (2016 American Control Conference (ACC) 2016, pp. 7153-7158, doi:10.1109/acc.2016.7526801)
**Read level**: full, ANL preprint version (ANL/MCS-P5402-0915, Sept 2015, titled "A Multilevel Approach to Class of Semidefinite Programs", p. 1). Read pp. 1-9 in full: abstract, §I-§VI, figures, references. Page references are preprint pages ("S185 preprint"); the ACC version was not seen.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Fu Lin (first), Zichao (Wendy) Di · **Place in trajectory**: Fu Lin's multilevel line (with S072), here for max-cut-type SDPs on graphs and power networks.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: SDPs max Tr(LX) subject to diag(X) = b and X ⪰ 0 on large sparse graphs (max-cut, phase retrieval) (pp. 2-3). Earlier coordinate-descent SDP methods work on the dense primal. Working on the dual keeps Z = Diag(y) − L as sparse as L (pp. 2-3).
- **D2 Key idea**: Use a log-barrier on the dual (3) and solve it by multilevel V-style recursion: coordinate descent at fine levels, Newton at the coarsest (n ≤ 250), restrict, solve, prolongate, refine (Algorithm 1, p. 5). Each coordinate-descent step has a closed form, Δy_i = µ/b_i − 1/S_ii, with a rank-1 Sherman–Morrison update of S = (Diag(y) − L)⁻¹, and stays positive definite (Lemma 4.1, p. 6; Algorithm 2, p. 6). Coarsening uses a binary aggregation matrix R chosen by algebraic distance and mass-weighted seeds (Ron–Safro–Brandt) (pp. 6-7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: L is a graph Laplacian and b ≥ 0. A fixed barrier parameter µ = 10⁻³ is used, not a path-following sequence (p. 7). Each non-seed node joins a single seed (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis**: Structure-preservation lemmas: RᵀLR is a Laplacian (Lemma 3.1); R satisfies (5) if and only if its columns have nonempty, disjoint, covering supports (Proposition 3.1); prolongation keeps the Laplacian property but can create negative edge weights (Lemma 3.2, Fig. 1); restriction keeps dual feasibility and prolongation keeps primal feasibility (Lemmas 3.3-3.4) (pp. 3-5). Positive definiteness of the coordinate-descent step is shown by eigenvalue interlacing plus the matrix determinant lemma (p. 6). There is no convergence result for the multilevel scheme as a whole.
- **D5 Experiments**: A Matlab prototype against SDPT3. On complete graphs n = 250-4000 (1-5 levels), the relative objective and solution differences are below 10⁻³, and time falls from 25% of SDPT3's (n = 250) to 75% (n = 4000) (p. 7). On IEEE 118-bus only single-level Newton runs (29.75% of SDPT3's time). A forced 118→60 coarsening is "not as competitive" (pp. 7-8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm with structure-preserving operators (p. 8). Stated limits: prototype "has much room for improvement" (p. 7); not competitive on the small power network (p. 8). Visible but unstated: the time advantage shrinks as n grows (pp. 7-8), although the conclusion claims competitiveness "for large problems"; the tests use dense complete graphs although sparsity motivated the dual (p. 3); there is no convergence theory.

**Method links**: Method 6 ✅ evidence: the formulation is chosen (dual rather than primal) to keep the sparsity of the graph before any algorithm is designed (pp. 2-3). Method 5 ✅ evidence (partial): it says where the method loses (118-bus coarsening) and labels the code a prototype (pp. 7-8). ⚠ The conclusion's "competitive ... for large problems" outruns a trend that worsens with n (pp. 7-8). Method 1 ⚠ variant: the solver is assembled from swappable parts (Newton at the coarsest level, coordinate descent at fine levels, an interchangeable coarsening) (p. 5), but no existing solver is modified. New-pattern candidate: multilevel coarsening carried from multigrid into optimization (with S072).
**Transferable techniques**:
- For a log-det barrier on Diag(y) − L, use the closed-form coordinate step Δy_i = µ/b_i − 1/S_ii with a Sherman–Morrison rank-1 update. Prove that it stays positive definite using interlacing plus the determinant lemma (p. 6).
- When designing restriction and prolongation operators for a structured problem class, prove which structure each operator preserves (Laplacian, dual feasibility, primal feasibility) and show with a 3-node example what is lost (a negative edge after prolongation) (pp. 3-5).
**Quotes**:
- "This allows us to exploit the sparsity structure of the underlying graphs." (p. 2)
- "(For this small example, the multilevel approach is not as competitive as SDPT3, due to the slow convergence rate of coordinate descent and the additional computation of the algebraic distance and the coarsening scheme.)" (p. 8)
