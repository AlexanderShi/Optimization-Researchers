### S146 · Concurrent optimization of capital cost and expected O&M (NREL Technical Report NREL/TP-5700-79093, 2021, doi:10.2172/1770880)
**Read level**: partial. Read: title page and contributor list (pp. 1-4), contents (pp. 5-9), Introduction and §2.1-2.3 (pp. 10-14), §3.1 optimization model (pp. 15-17), all of §3.2 optimization methods (pp. 18-25), §3.5.1 opening (p. 95), §3.5.4 overall system design (pp. 109-111) and §4 summary (p. 112). Not read: §3.3 forecasting and availability models, §3.4 dispatch MIPs, the rest of §3.5 (about 85 pages).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael J. Wagner (NREL), Alexandra Newman (Colorado School of Mines), David Morton (Northwestern) (p. 1); the acknowledgment lists 13 contributors including Jeffrey Larson (Argonne) and several PhD students (p. 4) · **Place in trajectory**: a DOE SunShot-type project report in which Argonne is one of four institutions (p. 13). The report does not say in the text which section is Leyffer's; Figure 2 (p. 13) shades roles by organization but its shading is not in the extracted text. §3.2 (mixed-integer derivative-free optimization) is the part closest to his line, but that attribution is my inference, unconfirmed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Design of a molten-salt power tower plant: choose continuous sizing variables and integer variables (heliostat panels, O&M staff, wash crews) to minimize the PPA price (Table 1, p. 15; objective (3.1), p. 16). The objective needs a black-box SAM simulation (several minutes per run, no derivatives) inside an expectation over weather and prices, giving "a mixed-integer, derivative-free optimization problem under uncertainty" (problem (3.2), p. 18). The entry point is that standard model-based MINLP methods (OA, GBD, ECP, Minotaur, Bonmin) cannot be applied to non-algebraic functions, and that DFO methods assume deterministic simulations and continuous variables (pp. 18-19).
- **D2 Core idea**: Build a piecewise-linear underestimator of the objective over integer points from secant hyperplanes through poised sets of n+1 evaluated points. A MILP master (big-M, λ, cone-membership binaries) minimizes the underestimator. Convergence is declared when underestimator and best value meet (p. 19; MILP model p. 20). Three improvements: (1) valid big-M and λ constants; (2) heuristics that keep only "useful" points and cuts, since the number of cuts grows exponentially (p. 22); (3) an ℓ∞ trust region on the integer variables, moved on improvement, with a null step otherwise, and enlarged by one only when the cutting-plane model becomes infeasible, "to avoid getting trapped in local minimizers" (pp. 22-23, Algorithm 1). Each integer point evaluation contains a continuous subproblem solved by BOBYQA/COBYLA from NLopt (pp. 24-25).
- **D3 Assumptions**: constraints are bounds on x, with simulation constraints folded into the objective (p. 16). Expectation risk measure (CVaR mentioned as an alternative, p. 18). Bounded integer domain Ω. A valid lower bound l_f of f on Ω is needed for M_i (eq. (3.3), p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis**: brief. Validity of the master depends on the constants: wrong M_i, λ or M_λ give an invalid lower bound (pp. 19-20). M_i comes from bounds on the box (eq. (3.4), p. 20). λ comes from the fact that an integer point off an integral hyperplane c^T x + b = 0 lies at distance at least 1/||c||_2 (Proposition 3.2.1, p. 21). The hyperplane with the smallest integral ||c||_1 is found by a small IP (P-hyp), and the bounds are then tightened by further optimization problems (eqs. (3.5)-(3.7), p. 21). Repeated iterates are blocked with no-good cuts or a binary-expanded valid inequality (p. 21). Global convergence of the trust-region variant is asserted, not proved, in the text I read (p. 23).
- **D5 Experiments**: a benchmark problem at n = 2, 3, 4: optimality gap and wall-clock time with and without the trust region (Fig. 6, p. 24). CPLEX on the MILP master times out at n = 3, plain enumeration at n = 4, and enumeration with heuristics and trust region solves "slightly larger problems" (Fig. 7, p. 24). The plant study varies one parameter at a time (cycle efficiency, heliostat MTTF, cost, soiling) and reports a 15-20% improvement in PPA price and LCOE (pp. 109-110).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm prototype inside an engineering software/report project. The limits are stated concretely: tightening the constants helps "but not to the level that makes it a competitive sub-problem solver" (p. 19); finding useful points is sometimes computationally difficult (p. 19); creating the piecewise-linear model remains the bottleneck (p. 19). The design study needs better constraints and convergence criteria because of scatter in the optimized values (p. 111). Future work: more market cases, data collection, release of the software (p. 112).

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅ (attribution unconfirmed): a trust region plus MILP master from the MINLP/NLP toolkit is carried into mixed-integer simulation-based DFO (pp. 18-23). Method 6 evidence ✅: big-M and λ constants are derived so that the master stays a valid lower bound before any speed-up is tried (pp. 19-21). Method 5 evidence ✅: limits are stated as plain verdicts (tightened constants still not a competitive subproblem solver; in some cases it "remains computationally difficult" to find useful points, p. 19), and the CPLEX and enumeration baselines are shown timing out (p. 24). New-pattern candidate: "trust region over integer decisions" (also in S065).
**Transferable techniques**:
- For a big-M MILP model of secant underestimators over integer points, get λ from the lattice-distance bound 1/||c||_2 for an integral hyperplane, then tighten it with a small optimization problem per hyperplane (Proposition 3.2.1, eqs. (3.5)-(3.7), p. 21).
- In a cutting-plane method over integer points, enlarge the ℓ∞ trust region only when the model inside it is infeasible, and take null steps otherwise; this keeps the work per iteration local without giving up the global stopping test (Algorithm 1, p. 23).
- Measure which cuts actually prune a "promising" point (useful cuts and useful points) before designing a cut-selection heuristic (Figs. 4-5, p. 22).
**Quotes**:
- "Traditional derivative-free optimization techniques are designed for problems with deterministic simulations and continuous variables." (p. 19)
- "Tightening these bounds improves the solution time, but not to the level that makes it a competitive sub-problem solver." (p. 19)

### S153 · Wave function analysis with a maximum flow algorithm (Molecular Physics 119(13), e1861351, 2021, doi:10.1080/00268976.2020.1861351)
**Read level**: full. All text pages read (pp. 1-19: abstract, method, implementation, C2 results, summary, appendix); figures and Table 1 (pp. 20-29) are known only from captions and the in-text references.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ron Shepard (corresponding author), Scott R. Brozell (Argonne Chemical Sciences), Jeffrey Larson, Paul Hovland (Argonne MCS) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an Argonne cross-division collaboration in quantum chemistry. Leyffer is the last of five authors, and the paper does not say what each author contributed, so the voice here is mainly the chemists'. It is not MINLP work.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In a graphically contracted function (GCF) wave function, the linear CSF expansion can have dimension N_CSF > 10^200 (p. 4). Scanning and sorting all CSF coefficients to find the dominant ones is then impractical (p. 5). The arc densities on the Shavitt graph can be computed in O(ωn) effort from the nonlinear arc factors (p. 5). The question is whether the dominant walks (CSFs) can be extracted from arc densities alone.
- **D2 Core idea**: Treat the Shavitt graph as a flow network with arc densities as capacities. A level-ordered max-min recursion d_k = max_j min(d_j, ρ_{j,k}) gives an upper bound on the largest walk density through each node, and backtracking from the head gives a candidate walk (pp. 6-7). The found walk's density is then removed from the arc densities and the procedure repeats (p. 6). When a candidate repeats, a greedy search over the fewer than 3n one-step deviations, ranked by heapsort, is used instead (pp. 8-9). Saved walks are kept in a hashed binary search tree (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions**: the arc densities of the GCF wave function are available. The candidate walk is guaranteed correct when its density exceeds half of the unsaved density d_tail (Eq. (6), p. 7). The guarantee does not hold in general (p. 7). Point-group irreps are restricted to one-dimensional irreps of D2h and its subgroups (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis**: brief. The max-min recursion yields upper bounds d_k (p. 7). The monotone bounds d_head and d_tail split the generated walks into a certified top-M′ set and an undetermined rest (p. 9). The appendix works through a four-CSF example (n = 4, N = 4) in which the first extracted walk is not the largest; the algorithm self-corrects on the next pass. A one-parameter family x(s) shows that arc densities are a many-to-one map of walk densities (pp. 18-19).
- **D5 Experiments**: one proof-of-principle application: three valence states of the C2 dissociation in the 6-31G* basis, against full-CI curves from the literature. Seven dominant CSFs are identified and interpreted (pp. 11-17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm plus a chemistry analysis. The claim is scaling: no step costs effort or storage of order N_CSF (p. 18). The ordering of extracted walks can be wrong, and the greedy step is called "ad hoc" (pp. 8-9). Future: extension to DMRG and coupled-cluster expansions (p. 18).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅ (weak; chemist-led paper, Leyffer's role unstated): the smallest example on which the method returns the wrong walk is built and run, and the failure is explained (the s-family) instead of hidden (pp. 7, 18-19). Method 2 evidence ✅ (weak): a network-flow/optimization device is carried into a chemistry object, the Shavitt graph as a flow network (pp. 5-7). New-pattern candidate: "certify part of a heuristic's output with monotone bounds" (p. 9).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When a greedy extraction cannot guarantee the right order, keep a monotone upper bound on everything not yet extracted, and report as certified only the items that beat it (p. 9).
- Give the exact sufficient condition under which the greedy step is correct (here, a majority of the remaining mass, Eq. (6)), and a small worked example where it fails (pp. 7, 18-19).
**Quotes**:
- "the dominant CSFs are generated, but not necessarily in the correct order, and in this sense the algorithm has some capacity to self-correct." (p. 8)
- "No step in this novel procedure requires effort or storage that scales as NCSF, and the algorithm is applicable to wave function expansions of practically arbitrary size." (p. 18)

### S064 · Modeling design and control problems involving neural network surrogates (Computational Optimization and Applications 2022, doi:10.1007/s10589-022-00404-9; read as arXiv:2111.10489v1)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v1). Read: abstract, introduction, contributions and related work (pp. 1-3), applications §2 (pp. 3-8), embedded formulation and stationarity §3 with Theorem 1 (pp. 8-13), MIP and MPCC formulations §4 with Proposition 4 and Theorem 2 (pp. 13-16), experiments §5 (pp. 16-21), conclusions (p. 22). Proofs of Propositions 1-3 and Lemma 1 (pp. 11-13) were skimmed only.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Dominic Yang (UCLA), Prasanna Balaprakash (Argonne) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Leyffer's entry into optimization over trained neural networks. ReLU networks are recast into his home classes: convex MINLP and MPCC solved as an NLP.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: problem (1): min f(DNN(x), x) s.t. c(DNN(x), x) ≤ 0, x ∈ X, with a trained feed-forward ReLU network inside (p. 1). The entry point is an observed failure. Embedding the network directly in an NLP through JuMP user functions makes Ipopt bounce without converging, with dual infeasibility staying high (Fig. 5, p. 9). The authors also note that results showing good behaviour of ReLU training do not transfer to optimization over trained networks (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea**: three formulations compared on three applications: engine design, adversarial MNIST attacks, oil-well flows (§2, pp. 3-8). (a) Embedded: the network as a black-box nonlinear function with its gradients (p. 8). (b) MIP: a big-M binary for each ReLU after lifting with the post-activation y and slack s; under convex f, c, X the continuous relaxation is convex, so the problem becomes a convex MINLP (Proposition 4, p. 14), with big-M constants from per-neuron bound-tightening LPs (p. 14). (c) MPCC: 0 ≤ y ⊥ s ≥ 0 written with slacks and y^T s ≤ 0, solved by an NLP solver (pp. 15-16). Two practical additions: convex-hull-of-training-data constraints (p. 4), and a warm start built from training data (Algorithm 1, pp. 5-6).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f and c are C² and convex, X is convex and compact (Assumption 1, p. 2); "The most restrictive assumption is the convexity assumption" (p. 2). The network is trained and fixed (p. 2). Theorems 1-2 assume general position of the activation hyperplanes (Proposition 2, p. 12; discussed p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis**: brief. Stationarity of the embedded problem is defined through Clarke's generalized Jacobian of a piecewise-linear map (p. 10). Under general position, the generalized Jacobian is the convex hull of products of masked weight matrices Ŵ1⋯ŴL (Propositions 2-3, pp. 12-13; Theorem 1, p. 13). Theorem 2: a point is stationary for the embedded formulation if and only if its lifted activations are strongly stationary (Scheel-Scholtes) for the MPCC, with multipliers built from scaling factors κ (Proposition 5; pp. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: CPLEX for the MIP and Ipopt for the MPCC and embedded formulations, default options, one thread on a single machine (p. 16); time limits of 3 h or 1 h or 3,000 Ipopt iterations (pp. 17-18). They report time to best solution, gap to the best known value, and counts of optimal and feasible runs (Figs. 8-9, Table 3, pp. 18-19). Bonmin is used for the oil-well MINLP (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: modelling plus theory plus evaluation. Results by formulation: the MIP gives optimality but does not scale (about 100 hidden nodes, p. 14; it times out on larger networks, pp. 17-19); the MPCC is faster but only locally optimal and sometimes up to 10% worse (p. 17); the embedded formulation scales but converges poorly with ReLU, which a smooth activation such as swish could fix (p. 22). The MIP wins on the smallest MNIST networks, by about 1-2% (p. 20). Stated limits: the size of network a standard MINLP solver can take (p. 2); the convex-hull constraint keeps solutions near the training data (pp. 4-5). The embedded formulation could not be run on the oil-well MINLP because no MINLP solver in JuMP accepted user-defined functions (p. 20).

**Method links**: Method 6 evidence ✅: the solver log (dual infeasibility, Fig. 5) is read first, a control run with swish activations rules out the solver as the cause, and the formulation is changed before any algorithm (pp. 9-10, 13). The convex-hull constraint repairs a model that predicted "negative emissions" (p. 4). Method 2 evidence ✅: ReLU networks are recast as convex MINLP and as MPCC, using his own MPCC-as-NLP slack formulation and citing Fletcher-Leyffer 2004 and FLRS 2006 (p. 15; refs. 17-18, pp. 22-23). Method 5 variant ⚠: same machine and failures kept in the counts (Table 3, p. 19), but the solvers run at default options rather than at their best settings (p. 16). Taste 3 evidence ✅: before the formulations are compared, the paper establishes what stationarity means for the nonsmooth embedded model and proves it equivalent to MPCC strong stationarity (Theorems 1-2, pp. 13, 16).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When an NLP solver fails on a nonsmooth model, rerun the same instance with a smooth surrogate of the nonsmooth piece (here, swish for ReLU). If the solver then converges, the formulation is to blame, not the solver (p. 10).
- Constrain surrogate inputs to the convex hull of the training inputs, and report what the unconstrained optimum looked like (p. 4).
- Build the warm start from training data: choose the training points that satisfy the constraints at least cost, and set every neuron variable to its activation at that input, so the start is feasible for the lifted model (Algorithm 1, pp. 5-6).
- When proposing a reformulation of a nonsmooth model, prove that its stationary points coincide with those of the original (Theorem 2, p. 16).
**Quotes**:
- "In fact, in an earlier implementation without the convex hull constraint, we observed that the optimal design was obtained for an engine that produced negative emissions." (p. 4)
- "This observation makes the use of ReLU networks questionable as embedded constraints within optimization solvers that rely on differentiable problem functions." (p. 10)

### S065 · Sequential linear integer programming for integer optimal control with total variation regularization (ESAIM: COCV 2022, doi:10.1051/cocv/2022059; read as arXiv:2106.13453v3)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v3). Read: introduction and contributions (pp. 1-3), existence and r-optimality §2 (pp. 3-4), algorithm §3 (pp. 5-7), assumptions and main statements of §4 (Assumption 4.1 p. 8; Theorem 4.14 and Remarks 4.15-4.16 p. 12; Theorem 4.23 p. 14; Corollary 4.24 and Remarks 4.26-4.27 p. 17), experiments §5 (pp. 19-25), conclusion (pp. 25-26). The proofs in §4.1-4.4 and §4.5 (pp. 9-19) and the appendix were skimmed, not checked.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Paul Manns (TU Dortmund) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the MIPDECO / mixed-integer optimal control line. A function-space trust-region method with integer subproblems is the precursor of the later Leyffer-Manns trust-region and McCormick work (SKILL anatomy 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: (P): min F(v) + α TV(v) over v(x) ∈ {ν1,…,νM} a.e. (p. 1). The entry point is a weakness of combinatorial integral approximation (CIA). CIA approximates the relaxed control in the weak-* topology, so the total variation of the integer controls goes to infinity and the controls oscillate. Its approximation arguments do not hold once a TV term is present (p. 2). Global solves of the discretized unregularized problem are said not to scale well on very fine meshes (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: TV regularization makes (P) admit minimizers (Proposition 2.3, p. 3). Local optimality is then defined in function space: v is r-optimal if it is best within an L1-ball of radius r (Definition 2.8, p. 4). SLIP is a trust-region method whose subproblem linearizes F but keeps TV exactly, with the constraint ||v − ṽ||_{L1} ≤ Δ (TR, p. 5). The outer loop resets the radius and the inner loop halves it until sufficient decrease (Algorithm 1, p. 6). On a DG0 discretization the subproblem is a linear IP (§3.3, pp. 6-7). The authors frame the method as an NLP trust-region method: "the algorithm therefore resembles a trust-region algorithm for nonlinear programming" (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions**: F is lower semicontinuous and bounded below (Assumption 2.1, p. 3). For the convergence analysis: d = 1 only, and F is C² with a Hessian bilinear form bounded in L1 × L1 (Assumption 4.1, p. 8). This excludes the L2 identity, but holds for least-squares objectives with a bounded operator K: L1 → L2 (Proposition 4.2, p. 8). Remark 4.16 says the Hessian assumption is used only to apply Taylor's theorem (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis**: brief. Existence of minimizers follows from boundedness in BV, weak-* compactness and lower semicontinuity of TV (Propositions 2.3 and 3.2, pp. 3, 5). Example 2.9 shows that, unlike in R^n, every L1-neighbourhood of an integer control contains other feasible controls (p. 4). An auxiliary problem (L) over switching locations defines L-stationarity (Theorem 4.14, p. 12). The authors say they could not construct Cauchy points for (TR), so TR-optimality is not shown to be necessary (Remark 4.15, p. 12). Theorem 4.23 gives three mutually exclusive outcomes, with weak-* accumulation points that are feasible, L-stationary and strict (p. 14). The argument hinges on TV(v^n) settling after finitely many iterations, likened to an active set settling down (Remark 4.26, p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: one 1-D convolution tracking problem with five control levels, N = 32-2048, SCIP 7.0 (pp. 19-20). Four SLIP variants are compared: reset or doubling radius, cold start or mesh sequencing (Table 1, p. 21). SLIP is compared with MIQP solved by SCIP under a 5-hour limit and with CIA+SCARP rounding (Table 2, pp. 21-22). The sensitivity study uses 25 random starts × 8 values of α, and a local MINLP check of r-optimality (pp. 23-25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory plus algorithm. Limits are reported concretely. SCIP's MIQP finds better objectives for N ≤ 128, though much more slowly (p. 22). CIA gives a better objective for N = 64 and 128 (p. 22). The method is "computationally less attractive than the combinatorial integral decomposition" (p. 26). The convergence analysis covers only d = 1 (p. 8), and the doubling-radius variant is fast but unproved (p. 26). Future work: use L-stationarity directly in the algorithm, adaptive grid refinement, curvature in the subproblems, and a rule for choosing α (pp. 19, 26).

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅: NLP trust-region globalization (ared/pred, radius control, an active-set reading of convergence) is carried into integer optimal control in function space (pp. 2, 5-6, 17). Method 5 evidence ✅: the paper reports where the method loses to both baselines (p. 22, p. 26). Relative differences below the solver's gap tolerance are clipped to the tolerance "in the interest of a fair comparison" (p. 25). Heuristic 8 evidence ✅: function space first; MIQP on fine meshes does not scale (pp. 2, 22). Taste 3 evidence ✅: a local-optimality notion that means something in infinite dimensions is defined before the convergence claim (Definition 2.8, Example 2.9, p. 4). New-pattern candidates: "trust region over integer decisions" (also in S146), and "regularize until the integer problem has minimizers, then descend on the feasible set" (pp. 2-3).
**Transferable techniques**:
- In an integer trust-region subproblem, linearize only the smooth part and keep the nonsmooth regularizer exact. The subproblem stays a linear IP after discretization (TV jumps become linear constraints over facets), and pred can be compared directly with ared (pp. 5-7).
- Before proving convergence for a discretized integer method, show by a shrinking-ball construction that the finite-dimensional notion of a local minimizer is empty in the limit, and define the neighbourhood (here, an L1 radius) that makes it meaningful (Example 2.9, Definition 2.8, p. 4).
- Check r-optimality a posteriori: re-solve the MINLP restricted to a small L1-ball around the returned point and report the relative gap, clipped at the solver tolerance (pp. 23-25).
- Report both a proven variant and a faster unproven one (reset vs doubling radius), and say which is which (pp. 21, 26).
**Quotes**:
- "The topological restrictions induced by the discreteness constraint and the TV-regularizer are strong enough such that (P) admits optimal solutions." (p. 2)
- "However, the algorithmic framework is computationally less attractive than the combinatorial integral decomposition." (p. 26)
