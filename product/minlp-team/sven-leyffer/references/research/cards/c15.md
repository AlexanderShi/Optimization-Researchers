### S134 · Switching time optimization for binary quantum optimal control (ACM Transactions on Quantum Computing 2025, doi:10.1145/3670416; read as arXiv:2308.03132v1)

**Read level**: full. Read version: arXiv v1 preprint of 6 Aug 2023 (p. 1), not the ACM TQC typeset version; cite as [S134 arXiv pp. …]. Main text pp. 1–28 read in full; appendix A proofs (pp. 32–39) read for proof structure, term-by-term algebra skimmed; appendix B tables (pp. 39–41) read; reference list skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xinyu Fei (first author, Michigan Industrial and Operations Engineering), Lucas T. Brady (KBR/NASA Ames), Jeffrey Larson (Argonne), Siqian Shen (Michigan) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: continuation of the Fei et al. binary quantum control line (ref. [18], p. 2), funded by DOE ASCR Accelerated Research for Quantum Computing (p. 28); quantum control treated as binary (bang-bang) mixed-integer optimal control.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Binary quantum control (P) with Schrödinger dynamics, controls in {0,1}^N, optionally SOS1 (exactly one controller active), objective energy ratio (2) or infidelity (3) (pp. 2). The entry point is a criticism of the standard fixed time discretization: finer grids mean more variables and cost "severely", and piecewise-constant controls limit quality (p. 3). Switching-time optimization exists in switched systems and QAOA, but assumes known analytic control forms (p. 4).
  - Transferable: enter an application field by naming the modelling convention everyone shares (here the fixed time grid) and show what it costs, before proposing any algorithm.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A four-step pipeline (Algorithm 1, p. 5; flow chart p. 6): (1) solve the continuous relaxation on a fixed grid with penalized GRAPE from [18]; (2) round to binary with a TV-norm (switch-count) penalty, by two new greedy heuristics: Algorithm 3 compares objective values (pp. 6–8), Algorithm 4 compares cumulative differences in the style of Sager's sum-up rounding (pp. 12–13); (3) merge equal consecutive intervals into a controller sequence (Algorithm 2, p. 5); (4) fix the sequence and optimize interval lengths τ by the switching-time model (STO) with SLSQP (pp. 13–14). Key idea stated as the trade-off between closeness to the relaxation and the number of switches (p. 5). Changed vs Fei et al. [18]: the time grid is no longer fixed after rounding, and the local-branching improvement step is replaced by cheap greedy rounding (pp. 4, 21–22). Not changed: the relaxation solver (GRAPE) (p. 4). Intervals may shrink to zero length in STO, which removes switches (p. 14).
  - Transferable: split a mixed-integer control problem into "which sequence" (discrete, cheap heuristic) and "when to switch" (small smooth NLP over durations with a simplex constraint), so the continuous phase repairs the error of a coarse grid.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Closed unitary dynamics, ħ = 1 (p. 2); piecewise-constant controls on equal steps Δt for the relaxation (p. 3); objectives bounded in [0,1] (p. 2); the SOS1 analysis needs either an SOS1 relaxation (Theorem 1, p. 9), a cumulative SOS1 violation ε^c(Δt) (Theorem 2, p. 9), or a quadratic SOS1 penalty plus existence of an SOS1-feasible solution of (P) (Corollary 1, p. 10). The convergence results are for α = 0 only, i.e. without the switching penalty that the method is built around (p. 8). STO is solved only to local optimality with SLSQP (p. 14); no global statement. Noise and open-system effects excluded; named as future work (p. 27).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Back-propagator precomputation turns each greedy test into one product (Proposition 1, p. 7) and cuts matrix exponentials from O(N′T²) to O(N′+T) and multiplications to O(N′T) (Proposition 2, p. 8), proved by counting. (b) Rounding-error bound F̄(u_bin) − F̄(u_con) ≤ 2C1e^{C2Δt}Δt (Theorem 1, p. 9): proof splits the change per step into O(Δt²) terms bounded via Taylor expansion of the matrix exponential and singular-value lemmas (Lemmas 1–4, pp. 8–9, 32–33), and shows by contradiction, weighting candidate controllers by the continuous control values (a convex-combination argument), that some controller has a nonpositive first-order term (pp. 33–36). (c) Quadratic-penalty argument: a penalty bounded above by F_UB forces the L1 SOS1 violation to vanish as T → ∞, by contradiction (Corollary 1, pp. 10–11). (d) Lifting trick: N non-SOS1 controllers become 2^N SOS1 controllers (all subsets, plus an idle one) with identical dynamics, by sorting control values and taking differences (Corollary 2, eqs. (27)–(28), pp. 11–12). (e) STO gradients via back propagation (pp. 14–15) and eigendecomposition of each Hermitian controller once, so each propagator costs scalar exponentials (p. 15). For Algorithm 4 the convergence is cited from SUR theory [18, 23], not re-proved (p. 13).
  - Transferable: to cover the non-SOS1 case, lift to an SOS1 system over subsets of controllers with the same dynamics, then reuse the SOS1 proof (p. 11–12).
  - Transferable: before tuning an algorithm, count matrix exponentials and products per greedy decision and precompute what does not depend on the decision (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 12 instances in four families: energy minimization on 2/4/6 qubits (5 random instances for Energy6), CNOT and NOT gate estimation at three horizons, circuit compilation for H2, LiH, BeH2 UCCSD circuits generated with Qiskit (pp. 16–18, Table 1 p. 19). Baseline: the coauthors' own best method from [18] (TR+MT+ALB), chosen because it gave the best objective/TV trade-off there (pp. 15–16). Penalties α, β tuned on a grid per instance, choosing "the best trade-off" (p. 18); tuning and comparison use the same instances. Metrics: objective (log scale), TV-norm, CPU time per phase (Fig. 4 p. 22; Table 4 p. 24); sensitivity study of α on Energy6 with the first-excited-state energy as a physical acceptance threshold (pp. 18–20). Acceleration measured against a baseline that recomputes exponentials: up to 16.3x (Table 5, p. 25). Coarser grids (T = 20 vs 100) reach the same post-STO objective at a fraction of the time (Table 6, p. 26; pp. 23–24, 26). Single macOS 8-core machine, Python 3.8, code on GitHub [52] (p. 18). Losses are visible only in the appendix table: the new rounding gives higher TV-norm than the baseline on Energy6 (20.4 vs 10.8), CircuitH2 (16 vs 8) and CircuitLiH (32 vs 18) (Table 9, p. 41). The text says gradients for STO come from finite differences (p. 15) after deriving analytic gradients (pp. 14–15); which one was used is not resolved in the text.
  - Transferable: use a physically meaningful acceptance threshold (here the first excited state energy) to pick a penalty parameter, rather than only the objective (pp. 19–20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues the authors' own [18]; credits Logsdon and Biegler for simultaneous optimization of controls and time points and justifies the decomposition by complexity (p. 3); places the cumulative-difference rounding in the Sager et al. sum-up-rounding line (p. 12); criticises time-discretized quantum control (GRAPE, Krylov, pseudospectral, RL) for cost and limited quality (p. 3) and RL-derived protocols for needing many training episodes and lacking generality (p. 4). Critique by reformulation, no direct attack.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Three-item contribution paragraph in the introduction (p. 4) with "First/Second/Third"; strong verbs in results and conclusion ("significantly improves", p. 21; "significantly outperforms", p. 27) next to a hedge that the two rounding methods are "in the same order" and both should be tried (pp. 21–22). Theorems are stated in the main text with proofs deferred to appendix A (p. 9). Figures carry objective and TV values in captions (pp. 23, 26–28).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution levels: algorithmic framework plus two rounding heuristics, asymptotic rounding-error theory for α = 0, an evaluation acceleration, and open code (pp. 4, 18, 26–27). Limits stated: dependence on penalty tuning, and CPU time growing exponentially with qubits because matrix size does (p. 22). Future work: run time evolution on quantum hardware, since only final states are needed, and robust control under noise and uncertainty (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (evidence ✅): quantum pulses re-cast into a home class, binary mixed-integer optimal control with SOS1 and a TV switching penalty, with sum-up rounding carried in from mixed-integer optimal control (pp. 2, 5, 12–13). Matches the SKILL example "quantum pulses as TV-regularized binary control".
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): the baseline is self-built (the coauthors' own [18] method at its best setting, pp. 15–16), but the switching penalties are tuned per instance on the comparison set (p. 18), the losses on TV-norm appear only in appendix Table 9 (p. 41), and the conclusion says "significantly outperforms" (p. 27). The Workflow C checkpoint (separate tuning and comparison sets) is not met.
- Method 6 (evidence ✅, weak): the time-grid formulation is identified as the source of cost and quality loss and replaced by a switching-time formulation before changing the algorithm (pp. 3, 13–14, 23–24).
- New-pattern candidate: relax, round with structure, then polish continuously (relaxation → penalized rounding → smooth optimization of the remaining continuous parameters) (pp. 4–5, 13–14).
- New-pattern candidate: price the oracle first (count expensive operations per decision and precompute) (pp. 7–8, 15, 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Fix the discrete sequence, then optimize switching times over a simplex Στ = t_f, τ ≥ 0; zero-length intervals remove switches for free (p. 14).
- Bound the error of a greedy rounding by Taylor-expanding the per-step propagator and proving by contradiction that a convex combination of candidates forces one with a nonpositive first-order term (pp. 33–36).
- Lift a non-SOS1 control system to an equivalent SOS1 system over subsets of controllers to reuse SOS1 proofs (pp. 11–12).
- Precompute back propagators and eigendecompositions so each candidate evaluation is one matrix product (pp. 7–8, 15).
- Re-solve on a coarser grid and show the post-polishing objective is unchanged to argue that grid precision is no longer needed (Table 6, p. 26).

**Quotes**:
- "Our key idea is to balance the difference between continuous and binary controls and the number of switches." (p. 5)
- "Estimating control functions by piece-wise functions also leads to limitations in obtaining better control solutions." (p. 3)

### S143 · Augmentation algorithms for total variation-regularized integer programs (arXiv e-prints 2025, arXiv:2508.05822)

**Read level**: full. Read version: arXiv:2508.05822v2 (20 Aug 2025), titled "Augmentation Algorithms for Integer Programs with Total Variation-like Regularization" (p. 1); per the work list, S151 is the same preprint under this later title. All of pp. 1–28 read; references (pp. 28–30) and appendix A (pp. 32–33) read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Dominic Yang (corresponding author, Argonne MCS), Miles Bakenhus (Illinois Institute of Technology, Applied Mathematics) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: grows out of the Leyffer–Manns sequential linear integer programming (SLIP) line for integer optimal control with TV regularization: the trust-region subproblem (5) is the subproblem of [20] (pp. 4–5); builds on Yang's specialized simplex for the same problem ([28], p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Integer programs min Σ F_v(x_v) + Σ G_uv(x_u − x_v) over {0,…,Q}^V with a separable convex budget Σ H_v(x_v) ≤ Δ on a graph (1), F, G, H univariate convex (p. 2). Unconstrained case is solved by min-cut methods (Ishikawa; Kolmogorov–Shioura via L♮-convexity) (p. 2); the budget-constrained case "has seen comparatively less study" and contains NP-hard problems (p. 3). Entry: two applications, image reconstruction with a material budget (3)+(4) and the trust-region-constrained TV subproblem (5) arising in SLIP for integer optimal control (pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable: take the subproblem your own outer method generates (here the TR subproblem of SLIP) and study it as a combinatorial problem in its own right (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Recast the problem in Graver-basis terms: with a_uv = x_u − x_v the constraint matrix [B −I] is totally unimodular, and its Graver basis is exactly ±(χ_S, χ_δ+(S) − χ_δ−(S)) for S inducing a connected subgraph (Theorem 1, p. 9). This re-derives Kolmogorov–Shioura's global optimality of up/down augmentation (Theorem 4, p. 10). Because polytope edges of a TU system are multiples of Graver elements (Remark 2, p. 8; Corollary 6, p. 10), the primal simplex method on the LP form of the augmentation subproblems (11)–(12) is a Graver augmentation method (pp. 10, 13). For the budget case, randomize the simplex (random initial basis via rooted spanning forests, random choice among tied leaving variables, budget-aware pricing w(ΔH_z, p)), stop at the first nondegenerate pivot and return it as a sampled improving feasible Graver move (Algorithm 3, pp. 15–18), alternate up/down moves (Algorithm 4, pp. 18–19), finish with a 2-opt polish (p. 18), from three initial points (minimal budget, penalized, rounded relaxation) (pp. 19–20). Changed vs Algorithm 1: exact min-cut subproblem solves replaced by randomized sampling that respects the budget; not changed: the up/down augmentation skeleton (pp. 3, 14).
  - Transferable: when the LP relaxation is TU and highly degenerate, treat simplex pivots as moves in an integer test set and randomize the degenerate choices to sample diverse improving moves (pp. 15–17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Convex univariate F_v, G_uv, H_v; integer bounds 0…Q (p. 2). The Graver certificate needs the separable convex objective and the unconstrained TV structure; with the budget, it no longer certifies optimality, and the method becomes a heuristic (p. 14). Proposition 10 needs a start that minimizes each H_v (p. 14). The complementarity of Δa⁺ and Δa⁻ in (11)–(12) is not imposed but follows from convexity of G (13) (p. 13). Proposition 12 assumes H(x^(0)) = 0 at the budget minimizer (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Max-level-set lemma: the connected component of the maximal-value set of any kernel element gives a ⊑-smaller kernel element (Lemma 2, p. 9); with a connectivity argument for minimality this proves Theorem 1 (p. 9). (b) Same pattern with multiplicity for the TV split variables (Lemma 7, Theorem 8, pp. 11–12). (c) TU → edges of the polytope are multiples of Graver/universal Gröbner elements (Remark 2, Corollary 6, pp. 8, 10). (d) Conformal (sign-compatible) decomposition of x* − x^(0) into Graver moves, ordered to be monotone in J, plus convexity of H_v around its minimizer, to show a feasible improving path to the optimum exists from the minimal-budget start (Proposition 10, pp. 14–15). (e) Counting argument for basic solutions as rooted spanning forests (Proposition 11, pp. 15–16). (f) Penalty approximation bound J(x̄) − J(x^(0)) ≤ p(J(x*) − J(x^(0))) when the penalized solution uses a fraction p of the budget, generalized from Manns–Severitt by noting their proof never uses the specific forms (Proposition 12, pp. 19–20). Local optimality notions compared: Graver-optimal implies 1-optimal but not 2-optimal (Proposition 9, p. 14). No termination or pivot-count bound for the randomized simplex (p. 18).
  - Transferable: when generalizing someone's bound, check which properties of their functions the proof actually uses; if none, state the general version (p. 20).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Two sets: image reconstruction on 6 images, N ∈ {32, 64, 96}, Q = 3, α ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 1, 2}, budget Δ = δΔ* with δ ∈ {0.25,…,1} so the budget binds, 288 instances (p. 21); TR-constrained subproblems from Manns–Severitt's public repository (pp. 21–22, 28). Six configurations (3 starts × p ∈ {0,1}) vs CPLEX default settings with 600 s limit (p. 22). Each configuration: up to 100 trials, 60 s per trial, 600 s total, best trial kept, all trials serial on one core (p. 22). Metrics: count of best solution per instance class, MIP gap against CPLEX's final lower bound before and after polishing, time (Tables 2–7, pp. 23–26). Results: CPLEX proves optimality in only two image instances (p. 22); randomized methods finish one to two orders of magnitude faster there (p. 22); CPLEX wins at N = 32 on TR instances (p. 25); for steepest-edge, 20 TR instances at N = 96 never improve on the start before timing out (p. 25); initializations give no benefit on TR instances (p. 25). Qualitative study of p: a phase transition in pivot count and objective near p = 1, sometimes with p = 1 worst (Figs. 4–5, pp. 26–27). Software available "upon request" (p. 28).
  - Transferable: report the gap of every heuristic against the exact solver's final lower bound, before and after polishing, so the effect of the polish is visible (Table 4, p. 24; Table 7, p. 26).
- **D6 Positioning**: Recovers Kolmogorov–Shioura (2009) through Graver bases rather than claiming a new unconstrained algorithm (pp. 1, 9–10); places Graver methods in the N-fold, two-stage stochastic and quadratic IP literature (p. 7); credits Manns and Severitt for the 1D and 2D TV subproblem work and generalizes their approximation result (pp. 3, 19–20); admits min-cut solves are faster than the LP form they use (p. 13) and that the connected-subgraph restriction may make subproblems harder, with a weaker iteration bound (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions given as a section-by-section outline (pp. 5–6). Hedges are explicit and frequent: "we hope this will provide a reasonable heuristic" (p. 14), "we do not have any proof" (p. 18), "merits further study" (p. 26). Some slips: the p = 0 rule is called "smallest coefficient" in the setup (p. 22) but "largest coefficient" in the results and appendix (pp. 25, 32); the appendix text says α = 1 (p. 32) while the Figure 6 caption says α = 2 (p. 33).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory (Graver basis of the TV system, local-optimality relations, existence of improving feasible paths) plus a randomized heuristic that beats CPLEX on large image instances and is comparable on TR subproblems (pp. 25, 27–28). Limits stated: no pivot bound, no approximation guarantee (p. 28); alternating up/down scheme "arbitrarily chosen" (p. 18); choice of p open (p. 27). Future: bound pivots, study p, extend to matching via alternating walks, multiple separable constraints, and embed in a trust-region method for nonlinear F (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): the authors pose the subproblems as LPs and run their own randomized primal simplex rather than the faster min-cut black box, because owning the pivot rule lets them reinterpret simplex edges as Graver moves, reject budget-violating pivots, and bias pricing (pp. 13, 15–18). This is Method 1 step 3 ("reinterpret what the inner solver already produces as what the outer algorithm needs").
- Method 5 (evidence ✅, with a variant ⚠ on baselines): claims calibrated to "competitive with and often outperforms" (p. 1); losses reported in the text (CPLEX best at N = 32; 20 timeouts without improvement, p. 25). Variant: the baseline is a commercial solver at default settings, not a self-built one; the randomized method gets best-of-100 trials within the same 600 s budget (p. 22).
- Method 4 (variant ⚠): reuses a public instance set from Manns–Severitt (INFORMS JoC repository, p. 28), but its own image instances and code are only "available from the corresponding author upon request" (p. 28).
- Method 2 (variant ⚠): the toolkit carried in is not the NLP/filter base but integer-algebra (Graver bases, TU) brought by the first author; Leyffer's line supplies the problem (SLIP subproblem, p. 5).
- Research Heuristic 4 (evidence): the subproblem created by SLIP [20] becomes its own line (pp. 4–5).
- New-pattern candidate: relax, round with structure, then polish (rounded continuous relaxation and penalized starts + augmentation + 2-opt polish) (pp. 18–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Characterize the Graver basis of a structured constraint matrix through a max-level-set connected-component lemma; this gives both an optimality certificate and an alternative proof of a known algorithm (pp. 9–10).
- Use the conformal-decomposition ordering of Graver moves plus convexity of the budget around the start to prove that a feasible monotone path to the optimum exists (pp. 14–15).
- Represent degenerate bases of a graph LP as rooted spanning forests to randomize initialization and predict each pivot's budget change (pp. 15–17).
- Weight reduced costs by predicted budget use, w = (1 + ΔH)^p, to interpolate between most-negative-reduced-cost and steepest-edge-like pricing (p. 17).
- Choose the budget as a fraction of the unconstrained optimum's budget use so that the constraint actually binds in every test instance (p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "The absence of such a move is proof of global optimality." (p. 7)
- "Like other simplex methods, we do not have any proof that this algorithm will terminate in polynomial time." (p. 18)

### S144 · A mixed-integer PDE-constrained optimization formulation for constructing electromagnetic cloaks with multiple materials (Computational Optimization and Applications 2025, doi:10.1007/s10589-024-00644-x)

**Read level**: full. Published open-access version, file pp. 1–24 (printed pp. 337–360) read in full, including declarations and references.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ryan H. Vogt (first author, affiliation NSA), Todd Munson (Argonne) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: sequel to Vogt–Leyffer–Munson's single-material MIPDECO cloaking paper (preprint [9], SIAM J. Sci. Comput. 2022 [15]) (pp. 3, 24); part of the MIPDECO line, funded by SciDAC FASTMath (p. 23).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Design a 2D Helmholtz electromagnetic cloak from p materials with an upper bound on mass (1), a topology-optimization problem posed as MIPDECO (pp. 3–4). The entry point is the generalization of their own single-material formulation in two ways, multiple materials and a mass constraint (p. 3); Haslinger's formulation supplies the physics (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: extend your own earlier formulation along exactly two named axes and state them in the introduction (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Two-phase strategy (pp. 1, 10): (1) solve the continuous relaxation in reduced space (states eliminated through the PDE, adjoint gradient) with SciPy trust-constr (pp. 7, 13), then round once with a feasibility-pump-like MILP that projects the relaxed permittivity w̃ onto the discrete permittivities in the ℓ1 norm subject to mass and SOS1 (19) (p. 11); (2) improve with a discrete steepest-descent trust-region method whose subproblem is a linear MILP in the binaries with ℓ1 trust region, SOS1 and mass constraints (Algorithm 1, p. 12). Plus mesh sequencing: solve on 20×20, warm-start 40×40 and 80×80 trust-region runs from the coarser integer solution, so only one relaxation is solved (p. 18). Changed vs [9]: SOS1 and mass constraints turn the knapsack TR subproblem into a general MILP (pp. 3, 15); not changed: reduced-space TR with adjoint gradients (pp. 3, 12). Only one MILP is solved, not the full pump (p. 11).
  - Transferable: when a PDE has a unique state for every admissible control, a feasibility-pump rounding can drop the states entirely and project only the control (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Cloak region is a box partitioned into a √N×√N control mesh (p. 4); one material or none per cell via SOS1 (6) (p. 6); mass as an inequality, argued because equality would only be feasible for C in the integer span of the densities (p. 6). Uniqueness of the PDE state for bounded nontrivial permittivity, and k0 chosen away from Helmholtz eigenfrequencies to keep uniqueness (p. 11; also p. 7). Nonconvexity from the bilinear wu term is admitted, and no valid outer approximation (hence no lower bound) is known since bounds on u are unavailable (Remark 1, p. 7). SOS1 and mass are kept out of the Lagrangian because they involve no states (p. 8). P1 finite elements and two-point Gauss quadrature, O(h²) consistent with the PDE discretization (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No theorems. Devices: formal-Lagrangian adjoint derivation of the reduced gradient (pp. 7–8, details deferred to [9]); observation that the Np-component gradient needs only N integrals, since component (i, n) is q_i times I_n, so cost is linear in N (p. 9); trust-region ratio test with radius doubling when the step hits the boundary and floor-halving on rejection (Algorithm 1, p. 12). No convergence statement for Algorithm 1 in this paper; the earlier paper is cited for the method (p. 12).
  - Transferable: check whether gradient components with respect to several binaries per cell share one integral; if so, compute N integrals and scale (p. 9).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: One geometry (D, Ω, D0 given in (20)–(21), p. 13), k0 = 6π, 2 and 4 materials, control meshes 20×20, 40×40, 80×80 on a 128×128 FEM mesh (pp. 13–14). Software: Python, FEniCS 2019.1.0, SciPy trust-constr, CPLEX 12.8 (p. 13). Reported per mesh: relaxation, rounding and final objectives with final mass (Tables 1, 3, pp. 14–15), and CPU split into relaxation, PDE solves, gradients, MIPs, iterations, state/adjoint pairs (Tables 2, 4, pp. 14, 16). The relaxation is not a lower bound for the final solution (p. 15). The relaxation dominates cost: 419,479.63 s for two materials and 1,729,093.42 s for four on the 80×80 control mesh (Tables 2, 4, pp. 14, 16). Mesh sequencing gives better objectives at 40×40 and 80×80, lighter cloaks and fewer TR iterations (Tables 5–8, pp. 18–20; p. 22). Comparisons are only internal (from-scratch vs mesh-sequencing); no external baseline. The 80×80 from-scratch runs stop at exactly 100 TR iterations (Tables 2, 4); whether this is an iteration cap is not stated. Code "is not approved for release to the general public" (p. 23).
  - Transferable: tabulate the CPU time per algorithmic component (relaxation, PDEs, gradients, MIPs) so that the bottleneck that motivates the next design choice, here mesh sequencing, is visible (Tables 2, 4; pp. 15–17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Opens with a long historical introduction from Maxwell to the 2006 metamaterial cloak (p. 2); extends Haslinger's topology optimization formulation (pp. 2–3) and the authors' own earlier MIPDECO (p. 3); cites the feasibility pump line (Fischetti–Glover–Lodi, Bonami et al., Achterberg–Berthold) as inspiration (p. 11) and SOS1 branching literature (p. 6). No critique of other methods; positioning is by extension of own work.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contribution stated as two extensions (p. 3) and a section outline (p. 4). Modelling choices are justified in place (inequality mass constraint, p. 6; ℓ1 vs ℓ2 norm, p. 11; why SOS1/mass are absent from the Lagrangian, p. 8). Some overstatements: the conclusion says the cost of "finding an optimal cloak does not depend on the number of different materials" (p. 22) while the results report effort rising "consistent with doubling the size of the optimization variable space" for four materials (p. 16), and the method finds local, not optimal, cloaks (Remark 1, p. 7). References [9] and [15] are the same work (preprint and journal) and are used interchangeably (pp. 3, 15–17, 23–24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a multi-material, mass-constrained MIPDECO formulation, a PDE-aware single-MILP rounding, a constrained discrete TR method, and mesh sequencing (pp. 3, 22). Limits acknowledged: nonconvexity and no lower bounds (pp. 7, 15); relaxation cost explodes with mesh size (pp. 14–17); mass constraint makes TR subproblems harder than the earlier O(N log N) knapsack (p. 15). Future: add a cost constraint (p. 22). Declarations note that Leyffer is an associate editor of COAP (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (evidence ✅): MINLP machinery (feasibility pump, trust-region with MILP subproblems, SOS1) carried into MIPDECO, extending the group's own home class (pp. 3, 11–12).
