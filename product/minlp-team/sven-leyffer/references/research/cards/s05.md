### S060 · Optimal response to attacks on the open science grid (Computer Networks 2011, doi:10.1016/j.comnet.2010.07.012)
**Read level**: full (preprint version: ANL/MCS-P1593-0309, 2009, titled "Optimal Security Response to Attacks on Open Science Grids"; cite as [S060 preprint p. N]). All 22 file pages read; reference list skimmed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Mine Altunay (Fermilab), Jeffrey T. Linderoth (Wisconsin), Zhen Xie (Argonne) (p. 3) · **Place in trajectory**: a DOE-security application. A network-response problem is modelled as a mixed-integer program, and the bilevel "attacker" extension is left open.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: open grids (OSG, about 8,000 users and 150 resources) cross institutional security perimeters. Incident response takes 3-14 days and there are no tools (pp. 4, 7). The entry is a new collaboration-based grid model: sites are linked by the number of common users, taken from one week of Gratia accounting data (pp. 5-7).
- **D2 Core idea**: threat levels come from a linear fixed-point system t = 1 on compromised sites and t_i = P0 Σ t_j W_ij/L_j elsewhere (p. 8). The response closes sites (binary x_i, model 4.8, p. 11) or closes and monitors links (binary y_ij, z_ij, model 4.11, p. 13), maximizing remaining utility under a cap T_max on threat levels. The bilinear products x_i x_j and t_j u_ij are replaced by exact linear constraints that use binarity and the bounds (pp. 10, 12-13). The second objective becomes a constraint, a goal-programming (ε-constraint) device, and the Pareto curve is traced by sweeping T_max (pp. 10, 19).
- **D3 Assumptions**: threat spreads in proportion to shared load, with probabilities P0 (before detection) and P1 (after), P0 + P1 ≤ 1 (pp. 8-9). Monitoring discounts threat by a constant factor 1 − D (p. 12). Initial threats T0_i are set to 0.1 for testing (p. 17). Parameters are fixed in Table 3 (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis**: one proposition. Under a contraction condition, the threat system has a solution in [0, 1]; the proof is a fixed-point iteration (Prop. 3.1, pp. 8-9). They observe that model 4.11 is a relaxation of 4.8, which explains why it gives higher utility (p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments**: GAMS + CPLEX on a desktop. Instances: a TOY network (11 sites) and OSG (23 sites, 253 edges), with 1-4 compromised sites (pp. 15, 17). The site model solves to optimality in seconds (Table 6, p. 17). The link model failed at relative MIP tolerance 1e-3 (memory and time); at 1e-2 it solves in seconds with gaps of about 11%, and at 5e-3 runs take up to 12,831 s (Table 7, pp. 17-18). They suspect symmetry (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: modelling and an application case study, called "preliminary" (p. 20). Future work: uncertain threat levels (stochastic or robust MIP), monitoring design, and the attacker's problem. The last is a bilevel program with a MIP lower level; they say it cannot be handled as an MPCC because a MIP has no closed-form optimality conditions, and call it "largely intractable at present" (pp. 20-21).

**Method links**: Method 6 (evidence ✅): bilinear terms are linearized exactly with binary-bound constraints, giving an MILP instead of a nonconvex MINLP (pp. 10, 12-13). Method 5 (evidence ✅): the failure at the tight tolerance is reported and the loose-tolerance gaps are shown openly (p. 17). Method 2 (variant ⚠): the MPCC toolkit is named as the natural route to the bilevel extension and rejected because the lower level is a MIP (p. 21), so the base cannot be carried here.
**Transferable techniques**:
- For a two-objective MIP without a multiobjective solver, move one objective into a constraint with a cap and sweep the cap. Report the curve as piecewise constant, because breakpoints are not practical to find (p. 19).
- When products of a binary and a bounded continuous variable appear, tighten the standard big-M linearization with the variable's own bound (T_j in 4.6, p. 10) rather than M = 1.
- When a variant model is a relaxation of another, say so to explain its better objective (p. 19).
**Quotes**:
- "Unfortunately, these runs failed because of the large memory demand and long execution time even for the small test problem as OSG." (p. 17)
- "Clearly, the latter approach is less intrusive and provides a higher utility for the same threat level. This observation should not come as a surprise because we can interpret (4.11) as a relaxation of (4.8)." (p. 19)

### S070 · Concrete structure design using mixed-integer nonlinear programming with complementarity constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2011, doi:10.1137/090778286)
**Read level**: full (preprint version: manuscript dated 24 November 2009; cite as [S070 preprint p. N]). Read pp. 1-25 (introduction, model description §2.3-2.4, model instances §3, results §4, conclusions §5, appendix opening). The constraint listings (pp. 6-12) were checked only for the form of the complementarity constraints. References skimmed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Andres Guerra and Alexandra M. Newman (Colorado School of Mines) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the first application where his MINLP-BB/filterSQP and MPEC-as-NLP lines meet. It names a new class, MINLP with complementarity constraints (MINLPCC).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: practice designs reinforced-concrete (RC) frames with a fixed initial stiffness. The internal forces this gives are inconsistent with the final dimensions, so designs come out overengineered (p. 2). Earlier optimization fixed some dimensions a priori, rounded continuous solutions, or used genetic algorithms (pp. 3-4). The entry is an explicit model that resolves the inconsistency (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: integer widths, depths and bar counts; binaries for bar size (SOS) and formwork reuse; finite-element equilibrium with stiffness as a function of the dimensions; inelastic steel response and ACI strength-reduction factors as complementarity constraints (pp. 2, 5-6, 14-15). The complementarity is written as nonlinear inequalities, which gives a degenerate MINLP that violates MFCQ at every feasible point. They show that MINLPBB with filterSQP still solves it (p. 2). Solver choice is argued explicitly: OA, GBD, ECP and BARON rely on convexity or underestimators, which this model lacks. Nonlinear B&B is more robust on nonconvex problems because OA/Benders cuts can cut off optima, and filterSQP is proven on MPCCs (pp. 18-19).
- **D3 Assumptions**: 2-D one-bay frames with 1-8 stories, ACI and ASCE 7 loads, shear neglected for slender elements (pp. 6, 16-17). Only local optimality is claimed, since the problem is nonconvex (p. 19). The start point is the "typical solution" built by the practitioners' iterative procedure (pp. 17-18).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no theorems). There is a modelling argument for "fixed" formwork-reuse cases: fixing the binaries removes the nonlinear equalities (3a-3f) (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: MINLPBB on Opteron servers; up to 678 variables (214 integer), 844 constraints (582 nonlinear), 120 complementarity pairs (Table 3, p. 18). Baselines: the typical practice design, the NLP root, and a rounding heuristic (pp. 20-21). Results: 13% average savings, while rounding gives no improvement (Table 4, p. 21). Convex reformulations were slower: big-M in place of SOS ran about 100× longer, and the convex hull in place of the nonlinear equalities about 375× longer (p. 20). The free case on 8 stories took 196,606 s (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: application and modelling, plus evidence that an off-the-shelf MINLP solver handles the class (p. 24). Limits stated: solutions are local, and the two largest free cases end at an inferior local optimum (p. 21). Tractability stops at eight stories. Proposed remedies: fewer integers through grouped elements, and a better branching scheme guided by a sensitivity study (p. 24).

**Method links**: Method 2 (evidence ✅): the MPEC-as-NLP result (Fletcher-Leyffer 2004) and the MINLP-BB code are carried into a structural-engineering class, and the class is named (pp. 2, 19). Method 6 (variant ⚠): the formulation is analysed for CQ failure (MFCQ violated everywhere, p. 2). However, the convex big-M and convex-hull reformulations were 100× and 375× slower than the nonconvex SOS and nonlinear-equality forms (p. 20). Under nonlinear B&B, "prefer convex" did not pay, so formulation ranking must be tested against the algorithm in use. Method 5 (evidence ✅): practice and rounding baselines, with the inferior local optima admitted (p. 21).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To justify integer variables to an applied audience, compare four numbers on the same start point: the practice design, the NLP root bound, the rounded root and the MINLP solution (Table 4, p. 21). If rounding gains nothing, the integer model has earned its place.
- Give a "fixed" variant that pins some binaries from domain knowledge (here formwork reuse), and report both cost and solution quality against the free model (pp. 16, 20-21).
- When choosing a solver for a nonconvex MINLP, write a paragraph saying which algorithm assumptions (convexity, underestimators) the model breaks (pp. 18-19).
**Quotes**:
- "This approach gives rise to a degenerate MINLP that violates the Mangasarian-Fromowitz constraint qualification at any feasible point" (p. 2)
- "However, the CPU time is about 100 times longer than that resulting from the special ordered set formulation for the three-story one-bay structure with vertical loads only." (p. 20)

### S049 · Solving mixed-integer nonlinear programs by QP-diving (Preprint ANL/MCS-P2071-0312, Argonne National Laboratory 2012, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 3-16 (abstract through conclusions) completely. Appendix tables A.1-A.3 (pp. 22-33) scanned for failures and the instances quoted in the text. References skimmed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ashutosh Mahajan (Argonne), Christian Kirches (Heidelberg) (p. 3) · **Place in trajectory**: an early Minotaur algorithm paper, and the direct descendant of his 1998/2001 early-branching SQP-B&B idea (p. 5). The extended performance profile appears here (pp. 11-12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: NLP solvers cannot be hot-started, because the Hessian and Jacobian factors are outdated after every step. LP solvers can reuse basis factors, and this hurts NLP-based B&B against LP-based methods (p. 4). The entry is measured: Table 1 compares full strong-branching sweeps by NLP, cold QP and hot QP on four instances, for example RSyn0805H at 78.7 s vs 69.8 s vs 1.94 s (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: solve one NLP at the root, then search the tree with a single QP approximation built at the root solution x̂ whose bounds change from node to node. The Hessian and Jacobian stay fixed, so BQPD can hot-start during dives (pp. 5-7). An NLP is solved only when a QP solution is integral (p. 7). The device taken from OA: replace the linear objective part by η with η ≥ f̂ + ∇f̂ᵀ(x − x̂) and η ≤ U − ε. A QP that is infeasible then also detects dominance by the incumbent, even though a QP objective can over- or under-estimate f (p. 7). Extensions: OA cuts on violated linearizations, zero multipliers raised to a fixed positive value so that the Hessian keeps constraint curvature, and convex combinations of past Hessians (pp. 9-10).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f and c convex and C², X a bounded polyhedron (pp. 3, 8). Nonnegativity of the multipliers is all that the correctness argument needs from them (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis**: finiteness and correctness (Prop. 2.1, pp. 8-9) by the standard B&B argument with two pruning cases. Case (i): an infeasible QP means that, by convexity, the linearizations are outer approximations, so the node is infeasible or dominated. Case (ii): an integral QP leads to an NLP solve and then a prune or a branch. The remark after the proof: what is solved at intermediate nodes does not matter, as long as infeasibility of the linear constraints is detected correctly (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: Minotaur QP-diving vs BONMIN B&B and Minotaur B&B, each with IPOPT and with filterSQP, all built against the same IPOPT 3.10.1. CMU-IBM library, 2-hour limit; failures coded "-1" in the tables (pp. 11, 22-26). Results, shown as extended performance profiles: with IPOPT, QP-diving is fastest on about 60% of instances and at least twice as fast on 30%. Its trees are often ten times larger, but BQPD is at least 4× faster per node on 68% of instances (pp. 13-15). It loses on some instances, for example SLay10H (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm with an implementation. Limits stated: larger trees, for three named reasons; a QP approximation that drifts as binaries switch; and BQPD reloads forced by filterSQP's shared static variables (pp. 9, 11, 15). Future work: update the QP objective or Hessian, add MILP cuts that are valid for the integer QP, redesign QP interfaces to avoid reloading, and QP versions of local-branching, diving and feasibility-pump heuristics (pp. 15-16).

**Method links**: Method 1 (evidence ✅): the algorithm is designed around what the inner solver can reuse (BQPD hot-starts), and it reinterprets the OA epigraph trick as a pruning device (pp. 5, 7). A tooling defect is reported and worked around: filterSQP changes BQPD's static variables, so BQPD is reloaded (p. 11). Heuristic 7 (evidence ✅): the downstream use (B&B strong branching and dives) chooses what the QP layer must provide (pp. 4-5). Heuristic 10 (evidence ✅): extended performance profiles are introduced because standard profiles cannot say how much faster a solver is (pp. 11-12). Method 5 (evidence ✅): same-solver builds, failure codes, and a statement of where QP-diving loses (larger trees, p. 13). Method 3 (evidence ✅, small): the one-dimensional −√x example shows that QP objectives can neither under- nor over-estimate reliably (p. 7).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Before proposing a new tree search, measure the cost of the subproblem re-solves it would replace (NLP vs cold QP vs hot QP on a strong-branching sweep, Table 1, p. 5). Let that table motivate the design.
- When a surrogate subproblem cannot bound the objective, move the objective into an epigraph variable with an incumbent cap. Then infeasibility of the surrogate certifies pruning (p. 7).
- Correctness proofs for B&B variants: list the pruning cases and show that each removes no node containing an optimum. Then state what the proof does not need, here the exact subproblem solved at intermediate nodes (pp. 8-9).
- Report a comparison as an extended performance profile (ratio to the best *other* solver), so that values below 1 read as "faster by a factor 1/τ" (pp. 11-12).
**Quotes**:
- "In fact, it is almost impossible to effectively hot-start any NLP solver." (p. 4)
- "The proof shows that in effect it does not matter what problem we solve at intermediate nodes as long as infeasibility of linear constraints is detected correctly" (p. 9)

### S053 · Heuristic static load-balancing algorithm applied to the fragment molecular orbital method (SC'12 Proceedings 2012, doi:10.1109/sc.2012.62)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1-10: introduction, the FMO load-balancing problem (§III), the HSLB algorithm (§IV A-E), results (§V) and conclusions (§VI). References skimmed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yuri Alexeev and Ashutosh Mahajan (equal contribution), Graham Fletcher (Argonne ALCF), Dmitri G. Fedorov (AIST) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an HPC application of Minotaur at petascale. MINLP is carried into processor allocation.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: dynamic load balancing (DLB) performs poorly on many thousands of cores for coarse-grained tasks of very different sizes. Static load balancing (SLB) is NP-hard but can draw on benchmarking data (p. 1). Fragment sizes are fixed by the chemistry, not by parallel efficiency (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: a four-step heuristic static load balancer (HSLB): (1) benchmark one SCC iteration at several core counts; (2) fit T_i(n) = a_i/n + b_i n^{c_i} + d_i per fragment by least squares; (3) solve an MINLP allocation of integer core counts; (4) run (pp. 5-6, 9). The min-max objective is put in epigraph form for smooth solvers (p. 7). Because a, b, c ≥ 0, the functions are convex, so Minotaur's LP/NLP-based B&B returns a global allocation (pp. 7-8). Min-max beat min-sum and max-min (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions**: the performance model has the form above. The fit parameters are constrained nonnegative so that extrapolation to large core counts behaves; c > 0 is chosen because the application is highly scalable (p. 7). Iterations are taken to be similar, so one SCC iteration is enough for the fit (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no theorems). Robustness of the fit was checked empirically: different starts give different parameters but nearly the same allocation (pp. 6-7).
- **D5 Experiments**: Blue Gene/P. On Aurora-A kinase with inhibitor (155 fragments), HSLB is at least twice as fast as DLB (p. 9). COX-1 with ibuprofen (1093 fragments) reaches 80% efficiency on 163,840 cores (p. 9). The allocation MINLP solves in under 180 s on one core (12,863 LPs, 2 NLPs for 4096 cores) (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a method and an application, with claimed generality for coarse-grained codes (p. 10). Limits stated: efficiency declines at high core counts (synchronization noise); HSLB is applied only to the monomer step; data gathering requires a separate rerun (pp. 7, 9-10). Future work: adaptive HSLB with Minotaur called directly from GAMESS (p. 10).

**Method links**: Method 2 (evidence ✅): MINLP machinery is carried into HPC scheduling, a new home class for an allocation problem (pp. 2, 7-8). Method 6 (evidence ✅): the model is shaped for the solver. The epigraph replaces the nonsmooth min-max, and sign constraints on the fitted parameters make the functions convex, so a convex-MINLP algorithm is enough (pp. 7-8). Heuristic 7 (evidence ✅): the downstream use (an allocation that GAMESS reads directly) decides the model form (p. 2).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Benchmark, fit a parametric cost model, optimize, execute. Constrain the fitted parameters' signs so that the resulting optimization is convex and extrapolates sensibly (pp. 6-7, 9).
- Check that the decision, not the fitted parameters, is stable across fitting starts. Only the allocation matters downstream (pp. 6-7).
- Compare candidate objectives (min-max, min-sum, max-min) and show the pathological allocation of the losers in a figure (p. 8, Fig. 7).
**Quotes**:
- "The MINLP approach provides great flexibility in modeling the allocation problem realistically." (p. 2)
- "More important was the observation that the differences in parameter values did not translate into significant differences in the optimal allocation of cores that we calculate in the next step." (p. 7)

### S069 · A pivoting algorithm for linear programming with linear complementarity constraints (Optimization Methods and Software 2012, doi:10.1080/10556788.2010.512956)
**Read level**: full (preprint version: ANL/MCS-P1680-1009, 2009; cite as [S069 preprint p. N]). Read pp. 3-22: introduction, stationarity, Algorithm 1 with its lemmas, the degenerate extension (Algorithm 2), anticycling (Algorithms 3-4, Prop. 5.1), the opening of the Phase I/II initialization, numerical experiments and conclusion. The worked pivoting examples (pp. 9-10, 12, 16-17) were followed only in outline. Appendix tables were not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Haw-ren Fang, Todd S. Munson (p. 3). Leyffer is marked as the corresponding author (p. 3). · **Place in trajectory**: follows the Leyffer-Munson 2007 SLPEC method. Its LPCC subproblem becomes a line of its own.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: NLP-based MPEC methods can converge to spurious C- or M-stationary points. The robust Leyffer-Munson method solves a sequence of LPCCs, which motivates a dedicated LPCC solver (p. 3). Earlier LPCC methods convert to MIP or bilevel form (cutting planes, B&B, penalty); this one works on the LPCC directly (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: generalize the simplex/active-set method. Keep at least one constraint of each complementarity pair in the working set. Drop the constraint with the most negative multiplier among the eligible ones: standard constraints, or complementarity constraints whose partner is also active (pp. 7-8). Degeneracy is handled with an extension set E (Algorithm 2, pp. 10-12). Cycling is handled by cycle detection plus an enumeration over LP pieces LP(x̂, P), P ⊆ D(x̂), each with Bland's rule; this either finds descent or certifies B-stationarity (pp. 13-16). A two-phase start: LP Phase I, then a Phase II that removes complementarity violations one at a time (pp. 17-18).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Algorithm 1 assumes nondegeneracy (n linearly independent active constraints at every vertex) and a given feasible vertex. Both are removed later: by E and anticycling (§4-5), and by the two-phase start (§6) (p. 5). The anticycling enumeration is exponential in the degree of nonstrictness |D(x̂)|, capped at 16 in practice (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis**: Lemma 3.1: termination with ŷ_q = 0 gives strong stationarity (pp. 8-9). Lemma 3.2 (cited from Scheel-Scholtes): nondegenerate B-stationarity implies strong stationarity (p. 9). Prop. 5.1: finite termination with descent or a B-stationarity certificate, since Bland's rule is finite inside each LP piece and the set U of unexamined pieces shrinks in the outer loop (p. 16). Two small examples carry the argument. The Scheel-Scholtes 3-variable LPCC shows B- without strong stationarity (p. 6). A 6-variable LPCC shows Bland's rule cycling (5.1) (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: a MATLAB implementation vs filter MPEC (SQP on the NLP reformulation with slacks). The test set is MacMPEC linearized into "MacMPEC-LPCC", 168 programs. The metric is pivots; failures are tabulated by kind (Table 1, pp. 20-21). Results: pivoting is better on performance profiles, still better after removing the 30 unbounded problems that filter MPEC handles poorly (pp. 21-22). The text says 12 LPCCs that presolve turned into LPs were removed, while Table 1 sums to 168 (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm with finite-termination theory. The conclusion calls it "an appealing alternative" (p. 22). The limit is the power-set enumeration in anticycling (p. 20). Efficiency devices (steepest edge, LU updates) are said to be applicable but are not tested (p. 4).

**Method links**: Method 2 (evidence ✅): the classical simplex method is carried into a complementarity class, rather than converting the class to MIP (pp. 3-4). Heuristic 4 (evidence ✅): the LPCC subproblem created by his own SLPEC method becomes the next paper (p. 3). Method 3 (evidence ✅): a minimal example shows that Bland's rule fails for LPCCs and motivates the anticycling scheme. The Scheel-Scholtes example is the test case for B- vs strong stationarity (pp. 6, 13). Method 4 (evidence ✅): MacMPEC is re-used by linearizing it into a new LPCC test set (p. 20). Method 5 (evidence ✅): a fair unit (pivots), failure categories for both solvers, and a re-run without the subset that biases against the competitor (pp. 20-21).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To extend an LP pivoting method to a nonconvex combinatorial class, find the invariant that keeps iterates feasible (here: one constraint of each pair stays active). Then restrict the leaving-variable rule to moves that preserve it (pp. 7-8).
- Before adapting a classical anticycling rule, try it on the smallest degenerate instance of the new class. If it cycles, publish the example and the fix (p. 13).
- Build a test set for a subproblem class by linearizing an existing public library of the parent class (MacMPEC → MacMPEC-LPCC) and say what presolve removed (p. 20).
- If a competitor is handicapped on a subset for structural reasons (here, unbounded rays), report the profile with and without that subset (p. 21).
