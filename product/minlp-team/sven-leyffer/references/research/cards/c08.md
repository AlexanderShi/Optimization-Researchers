# Paper cards · batch c08 (core)

Batch intent: See how Leyffer's two halves (MINLP modelling and filter-SQP globalization) work at the level of single papers from the late 2000s: (a) how an application that the derivative-free community had cast as a "mixed-variable" black box is re-modelled until standard MINLP machinery applies (S043), and (b) how the home filter method is redesigned so that it accepts full Newton steps without second-order corrections (S040). The batch checks Method 6 (formulation before method), Method 2 (base and extension), Method 1 (owning the instrument), Method 3 (failure-driven redesign) and Method 5 (calibrated claims), and looks for new patterns in reformulation and convergence-proof design.
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all; core batch). Extra weight on D2 (what is changed relative to the base), D3 (which assumptions the authors flag as convenient), D4 (proof skeleton of S040; facet proof of S043) and D5 (fairness of the comparison and what is not reported).

### S043 · Modeling without categorical variables: a mixed-integer nonlinear program for the optimization of thermal insulation systems (Optimization and Engineering 2010, DOI 10.1007/s11081-010-9109-z)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1–33: cover, contents, all sections 1–7, acknowledgments and reference list). The text is the 2007 preprint ANL/MCS-P1434-0607 (p. 1, p. 3), not the 2010 journal version; differences from the published version are not known (not read).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Kumar Abhishek (Lehigh ISE; the work was done largely while he visited Argonne as a student, p. 32), Jeffrey T. Linderoth (Lehigh ISE) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: Argonne period, same group and same years as FilMINT ("Abhishek et al., 2006", cited as a working paper, p. 32); uses his own MINLP-BB (Leyffer 1998, p. 24, p. 33) and FilMINT (built on filterSQP and MINTO, p. 28). It is an application/modelling paper that answers the mixed-variable pattern-search line of Audet, Dennis, Kokkolaras and Abramson (p. 3–4, p. 8).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a received formulation from another community. Load-bearing thermal insulation design had been modelled as a mixed-variable program (MVP) with categorical variables (material type, number of intercepts) and solved by filter pattern search (Kokkolaras et al. 2001; Abramson 2004) (p. 3–4, p. 8). The authors' objection is that categorical variables have no continuous relaxation, which "precludes the use of modern integer optimization techniques" (abstract p. 3; p. 4). §2.4 lists four "challenges" of the MVP model (p. 8–9):
  1. categorical variables without relaxations, and the variable n as a summation limit, so every n defines a different model;
  2. integrals with no closed form, evaluated by Simpson's rule, so derivatives are hard;
  3. a min-over-temperature stress constraint (2.5), i.e. a bilevel problem;
  4. a discontinuous objective coefficient C(t_i) (2.1).

  They add a two-variable integer counterexample (rotated, scaled quadratic) in which many points are pattern-optimal but only one is the minimum (Figure 2, p. 9). The problem statement: re-express every categorical and nonsmooth element with standard integer and nonlinear modelling, so that a modelling language and standard MINLP solvers apply (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: when a neighbouring community solves your kind of problem with a heuristic, list each feature of their model that blocks relaxation-based methods, one numbered item per feature, then remove them one at a time (p. 8–9).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key idea is a chain of reformulations, not a new algorithm (p. 10–23):
  - **Number of layers**: binary y_i for "layer i exists" with ordering y_{i+1} ≤ y_i and a cardinality equation (3.13)–(3.14); the weak variable upper bounds x_i ≤ L y_i (3.15) are replaced by the aggregated x-sums ∑_{j≥i} x_j ≤ L y_i (3.16), proved facet-defining (Theorem 3.1, p. 10–12).
  - **Material choice**: binaries z_ij with ∑_j z_ij = y_i (3.25); property data become k(t,m_i) = ∑_j z_ij k(t,m_j) (p. 12–13). Model (3.26)–(3.38) is still a "mixed-integer simulation model" (p. 14).
  - **Bilevel stress constraint**: replaced by enforcing the bound at the intercept temperatures on a finer piecewise-linear interpolation of the spline data; at both ends of the interval for the non-monotone epoxy data (4.2)–(4.5) (p. 14–15). They do not adopt Abramson's argument that the constraint is always binding: "We are not sure that this argument holds in general" (p. 14).
  - **Integrals**: more data points from the spline plus the trapezoidal rule, with a case split of [t_{i−1}, t_i] against the grid (sets A, B, D, E) and new variables v_ij, w_ij (4.6)–(4.8) (p. 15–17). Divisions are multiplied out to "avoid division by zero that can confound standard NLP software" (4.9)–(4.10) (p. 17).
  - **Discontinuous objective coefficient**: three binaries s_ki with big-M-type implications (4.15)–(4.19) (p. 18–19), giving the piecewise-smooth model (P-1).
  - **Discretized temperatures** (P-2): SOS-1 binaries d_ir select t_i from a grid (5.1). Integrals become linear (5.2)–(5.3), C becomes a constant table (5.5), the stress constraint becomes a finite big-M family (5.8), and implication cuts (5.9) are added (p. 20–22). The authors call the result a smooth MINLP (p. 22–23).

  Three models of graded smoothness, kept side by side (Table 4, p. 23): (P-0) discontinuous objective; (P-1) nonsmooth constraints; (P-2) "smooth as a whistle" but 3696 variables and 6715 constraints for N = 10.
  - Transferable technique: produce a ladder of equivalent or approximate models with graded smoothness and size, and tabulate their variable and constraint counts and smoothness class before solving any of them (Table 4, p. 23).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Property data are spline fits to look-up tables, replaced by finer piecewise-linear data "without sacrificing accuracy" (Figure 3, p. 15–16). The stress bound is only enforced at grid or intercept temperatures, so (4.2)–(4.5) and (5.8) approximate the semi-infinite constraint (p. 14, p. 21–22). Strict inequalities in C(t) are modelled with a small ε (p. 18). Discretizing t_i is justified by a stated belief: "the solution of a MINLP is typically more sensitive to the choice of the integer variables than to the choice of the continuous variables", followed by an NLP polish (p. 20). All three models are nonconvex, so the solution method "can be seen essentially as a heuristic" (p. 24, p. 28). Approximations flagged by the authors themselves: the nonsmoothness of (4.4)–(4.5) "does not appear to cause any problems for the NLP solvers" (p. 15), and the discretization assumption "may seem strong" (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: when you replace a semi-infinite or bilevel constraint by a finite sample, say where the data are non-monotone and enforce the bound at both ends there (epoxy case, p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: One polyhedral result. Theorem 3.1 (p. 10–12): for P = conv{(x,y) ∈ R_+^{N+1} × B^{N+1} : y_{i+1} ≤ y_i, x_i ≤ L y_i}, each ∑_{j≥i} x_j ≤ L y_i defines a facet. The proof is the standard "any valid inequality tight on the face is a scalar multiple" argument (Nemhauser–Wolsey Thm I.4.3.5, p. 11). It exhibits points on the face (the origin, (0, e_1 + … + e_k), (L e_i, ∑_{j≤i} e_j), and a midpoint pair (L/2 e_i, L/2 e_k)) and derives the coefficient relations (3.17)–(3.24) one family at a time (p. 11–12). Other devices are modelling devices, not proofs: big-M implications for piecewise-constant functions (p. 18–19); a partial-sum indicator p_is that is 1 exactly when T_s lies in [t_{i−1}, t_i] (p. 22); a bound B ≡ 2N for the implication cuts (5.9) (p. 22). Small typos in the reformulated constraints (summation index x_i for x_j in (3.34), p. 13; a stray "≤ a_i ≤" in (4.5), p. 15) are present in the preprint.
  - Transferable technique: when you introduce indicator variables for "item i exists", check whether the aggregated variable upper bound (sum of the tail ≤ capacity × indicator) dominates the disaggregated one, and prove it facet-defining with the scalar-multiple argument on explicit points of the face (p. 10–12).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Models in AMPL, "available ... from the authors upon request" (p. 23); property tables released as AMPL data files (p. 6). Solvers:
  - MINLP-BB (nonlinear B&B, his own) for (P-0) and (P-1), with a per-node multistart switch: R = 5 random restarts while results disagree, switched off in a subtree once all restarts agree, so a convex problem gets a multistart only at the root (p. 24);
  - branching priorities by modelling hierarchy: n, then y_i, then z_ij, with s_ki last (p. 24);
  - FilMINT (LP/NLP-based branch-and-cut) for (P-2), where MINLP-BB gave "excessive solution times" (p. 28). For nonconvexity they use the basic LP/NLP algorithm and, in some runs, linearizations as local cuts valid only in the subtree (p. 28–29).

  Runs were made without time limits on a 120-node Opteron cluster (p. 24). Results:
  - (P-0): f* = 1.039 (N = 10, 3979 nodes, 29 894 s) and 0.988 (N = 20, 13 515 nodes, 283 034 s), against Abramson's 1.0623 (Table 5, p. 24–25);
  - a sweep with n fixed at 1, …, 29 shows the objective drops then flattens, "Clearly, n = 10 is not the optimal number of intercepts" (Figure 5, p. 26);
  - (P-1): 1.02165 (N = 10) and best 0.98305 at n = 21 (Table 6, p. 27);
  - QPs per NLP stay roughly 25–47 for (P-1) but grow to about 544 for (P-0), which they attribute to the discontinuity (Table 7, p. 28);
  - (P-2) with reduced temperature windows taken from the (P-0)/(P-1) solutions and only the two epoxies: 1.281 (global cuts) and 1.212 (local cuts) (Tables 8–9, p. 29–30). After fixing the discrete choices from (P-2) and re-solving an NLP: 1.097 (Table 10, p. 31).

  The text calls the (P-2) outcome "only a little worse" than (P-0)/(P-1) (p. 29). By my arithmetic from Tables 6 and 9, 1.212 is about 19% above 1.022. The conclusion's "reduce the cooling power by 4%" for N = 10 → 20 (p. 31) is about 4.9% from Table 5. No comparison of run times with the pattern-search method is given, and no global bounds are reported (not in the text).
  - Transferable technique: when a nonconvex B&B uses a per-node multistart, attach a flag that propagates to the children and is turned off in a subtree once all restarts agree, so the overhead disappears where the relaxation behaves convexly (p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: report an inner-solver effort metric (QPs per NLP) across the model ladder, as a measure of formulation quality independent of the final objective (Table 7, p. 28).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents itself as an alternative to the mixed-variable pattern-search line (Audet and Dennis 2000, 2004; Kokkolaras et al. 2001; Abramson 2004; Abramson et al. 2004) (p. 3–4, p. 8). Criticism is direct but specific: it gives the four challenges, says optimality of a pattern-search result is "almost impossible to verify" because of the discontinuities (p. 9), and constructs a failure example (p. 9). It also declines one modelling argument of Abramson (p. 14). It places itself in standard MINLP methods: Dakin, Gupta–Ravindran, Quesada–Grossmann, and the Grossmann 2002 survey (p. 24). It claims generality as "a blueprint" for other categorical design problems (nanomaterials, sensor placement) (p. 4, p. 31). The audience includes simulation developers (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Short abstract with the thesis in the second sentence (p. 3). The introduction states the blueprint claim and a message to "application scientists who develop simulation tools" (p. 4). The paper is organised as challenge list → one section per remedy → model ladder → numbers (p. 2, p. 8–23). There are colourful phrases ("potential death-blow", p. 9; "smooth as a whistle", Table 4, p. 23). Hedging appears where approximations are made ("does not appear to cause any problems", p. 15; "This assumption may seem strong", p. 20; "essentially as a heuristic", p. 28). Engineering judgment is named as an input to the MILP ("Engineering or modeling insight is included ... by using priorities ... or by restricting temperature ranges", p. 31).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution levels: modelling (categorical → binary; facet inequality; bilevel and integral removal), application (better designs than Abramson's, cooling power 1.0623 → 0.988; mixed materials across layers possible, p. 26), software use (MINLP-BB multistart, FilMINT local cuts). Limits, some stated and some visible in the text:
  - nonconvex models are solved heuristically, with no global certificate (p. 24, p. 28);
  - the smooth model (P-2) was too large for MINLP-BB, and with FilMINT gave worse designs, even after restricting materials and temperature windows using earlier solutions (p. 28–30);
  - the stress constraint is approximated only on a grid (p. 14, p. 22);
  - very long run times (up to 283 034 s, p. 25);
  - no explicit future-work section; the blueprint claim is the forward-looking statement (p. 31).

**Method links**: Method 6 ✅ evidence: the whole paper is "fix the formulation before the method"; it is the case already cited in SKILL.md for Method 6 (thermal insulation re-modelled, [02 §3.8]). Here it is seen in detail: divisions multiplied out to keep iterates evaluable (p. 17); the negative CoolingPower found in node relaxations, traced to fractional s_ki, and repaired with CoolingPower ≥ 0 (p. 19–20); the QPs-per-NLP read-out (p. 28). · Method 2 ✅ evidence: his own MINLP toolkit (MINLP-BB, FilMINT, filterSQP) carried into a problem class owned by the derivative-free mixed-variable community (p. 24, p. 28). · Method 3 ✅ evidence: a small two-variable integer example that breaks MVP pattern search (p. 9). · Method 4 ⚠ variant: formulations are kept side by side (P-0, P-1, P-2; Table 4, p. 23) and the data tables are released (p. 6), but the models are only "available ... upon request" (p. 23), not published in a library. · Method 1 ✅ evidence: solver-level changes for nonconvexity (per-node multistart switch in MINLP-BB, p. 24; local rather than global linearization cuts in FilMINT, p. 29). · Method 5 ⚠ variant: the comparison with the baseline is by objective value only, against a published number (Abramson 2004). There is no fairness rule and no time comparison, and the conclusion "only a little worse" is softer than the tables (p. 29–30). · New-pattern candidates: "Discrete-structure-then-NLP-polish" (p. 20, p. 29–31); "coarse-model solutions restrict the fine model" (p. 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a categorical choice into "exists" binaries y_i (ordered) plus selection binaries z_ij with ∑_j z_ij = y_i, and write each property as ∑_j z_ij · data_j (p. 10–13).
- Replace a min-over-interval constraint by enforcement at grid points of a finer piecewise-linear interpolant; enforce at both interval ends where the data are non-monotone (p. 14–15).
- Model a piecewise-constant coefficient with one binary per piece and two big-M inequalities per breakpoint; then add a physical bound (here objective ≥ 0) that fractional relaxations would otherwise violate (p. 18–20).
- Discretize a continuous design variable with SOS-1 binaries to make integrals and coefficients linear; take the discrete structure and re-solve an NLP over the continuous variables (p. 20–21, p. 29–31).
- Shrink a large discretized model by restricting each variable's window using solutions of cheaper formulations (Table 8, p. 29).

**Quotes**:
- "Each of these points is a potential death-blow for standard optimization techniques." (p. 9)
- "In our view it is important to include optimization considerations in simulation tools from the start." (p. 4)

### S040 · A nonmonotone filter method for nonlinear optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2012, DOI 10.1007/s10589-011-9430-2)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1–26: cover, contents, sections 1–6, acknowledgments and reference list; Figure 3, the performance profile, is an image and its curves are not in the text). The text is the 2009 preprint ANL/MCS-P1679-0909, titled "Nonmonotone Filter Method for Nonlinear Optimization", authors Roger Fletcher, Sven Leyffer, Chungen Shen (p. 1, p. 3). The journal version lists Shen, Leyffer, Fletcher; other differences from it are not known (not read).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Roger Fletcher (Dundee), Chungen Shen (Argonne MCS at the time) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: a second-generation paper on his own 2002 filter method (Fletcher and Leyffer 2002; Fletcher, Leyffer, Toint 2002b; Fletcher, Gould, Leyffer, Toint, Wächter 2002a, cited p. 3–4, p. 25). It addresses the Maratos-effect weakness of filter SQP and comes with a new prototype solver FASTr compared with his filterSQP (p. 23).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Entry is a known weakness of the home method: "filter SQP methods may also encounter the Maratos effect" (p. 4). Three existing fixes are each found to carry a cost (p. 4):
  - Ulbrich (2004) puts the Lagrangian in the filter, which carries the risk of converging to a saddle point;
  - Wächter and Biegler (2005a) need second-order correction (SOC) steps, which are "cumbersome" to implement;
  - Gould and Toint (2003) have a nonmonotone filter framework with global convergence but no fast-local-convergence proof.

  Problem statement: a filter SQP method with both global and superlinear local convergence, using the objective (not the Lagrangian) in the filter and needing no SOC steps (abstract p. 3; p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: when your own method has a known defect and others have patched it, tabulate each patch's cost (saddle risk, implementation burden, missing proof) and aim the new design at the empty cell (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Two filters (p. 4, p. 6–10):
  - a standard **g-filter** (Chin–Fletcher acceptance (2.6), switching condition Δq > 0 (2.10), sufficient reduction Δf ≥ σΔq (2.11)) for global convergence;
  - a nonmonotone **l-filter** in which a point is acceptable if it is dominated by at most M entries (Definition 2.3, p. 7). It uses the nonmonotone actual reduction max_{0≤i≤M} f(x_{k−i}) − f(x̂) (2.4). Its switching condition is stricter: Δq(d̄) > 0 and h̃_k ≤ ζ‖d̄‖^τ with τ ∈ (2, 3] (2.8). Its sufficient reduction is weaker: Δf̃ ≥ σ min{Δq, ξ‖d̄‖²} (2.9) (p. 7–8).

  The switch from global to local is triggered when the trust-region step is interior (‖d‖∞ < ρ), read as "potentially generating Newton steps" (p. 10). The way back: on rejection the l-filter is flushed, the iterate backtracks to the last g-acceptable point x_g, and ρ = ρ_g/2 is set, to prevent oscillation between the filters (p. 7, p. 9–10). Algorithm 2.1 (p. 9). Kept: trust-region SQP with QP subproblems in the ∞-norm, the restoration phase, the objective as the filter's second measure. Changed: the acceptance test near the solution, so full SQP steps pass without SOC (p. 4, p. 24). The code also uses the main loop for both restoration and optimality (p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: when a globalization device blocks fast local steps, give the local regime its own, more permissive acceptance test, activated by an observable signal (interior trust-region step). Keep a backtrack to the last point that passed the strict test, so the global proof still applies (p. 7, p. 9–10).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Problem (1.1) with C² f and c (p. 3). Global analysis: A1 iterates in a compact set; A2 C² functions; A3 B_k uniformly bounded; A4 MFCQ at feasible accumulation points (p. 10); and Theorem 3.1 also assumes the QP subproblem is solved to global optimality (p. 13). The authors flag this last one: they call it undesirable, note it was used in Fletcher et al. 2002b, and sketch its removal through the normal–tangential decomposition and a generalized Cauchy step from one extra LP (Fletcher et al. 2002a) (p. 15). The restoration phase is not specified (p. 8). Local analysis: A5 Lipschitz Hessians, LICQ, strict complementarity, second-order sufficiency (p. 16); B_k is taken as the Hessian of the Lagrangian with λ_{k−1} (p. 16). The l-filter switching exponent τ ∈ (2, 3] is chosen so that, together with (2.9), Newton steps pass (p. 7–8, p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: when an assumption is there only for the proof (global QP solves), state it in the theorem, then say in a remark which known device removes it and which inequality that device supplies (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The global part (§3, p. 10–15):
  - **Lemma 3.1**: for an infinite sequence of l-filter entries, h(x_k) → 0 (p. 10–12). Case f unbounded above: since at most M entries can dominate, h over a window of M + 1 decreases geometrically by β relative to the previous window. Case f bounded: a neighbourhood of radius r = (h̄/4) min(1 − β, γ) around a limit (h̄, f̄) forces a point that no entry in its window can accept, a contradiction.
  - **Corollary 3.1**: the g-filter is the M = 0 case (p. 12).
  - **Lemmas 3.2–3.4**: imported from Fletcher et al. 2002b, Lemmas 3, 5, 6, "with slight modifications" (p. 12–13).
  - **Theorem 3.1**: three outcomes (restoration fails / KKT found / a KKT accumulation point) (p. 13). The proof splits by infinitely many h-type iterations (in the g-filter or in the l-filter) versus finitely many, and the latter by whether full steps d̄_k are eventually always taken (p. 13–15). A recurring device: the trust-region interval in which an f-type step is guaranteed, (3.20) and (3.25), has an upper end more than twice its lower end, so halving ρ must land in it or to its right (p. 13, p. 15).

  The local part (§4, p. 16–22):
  - **Lemma 4.1**: d̄_k → 0, by the absence of strictly feasible descent directions, with bounded and unbounded (normalized) cases (p. 16–17);
  - **Proposition 4.1**: Moré–Sorensen isolated-accumulation-point lemma (p. 17);
  - **Lemma 4.3**: rates from Facchinei–Lucidi, and ‖d̄_k‖ = Θ(‖x_k − x*‖) (p. 18);
  - **Lemma 4.4**: c_i(x_k + d̄_k) = O(‖d̄_k‖²) (p. 18);
  - **Lemma 4.5**: the exact penalty function Φ_ψ = f + ψh (ψ > ‖λ*‖∞) is used "only as a proof technique". With the quadratic-growth bound from Chamberlain et al. (watchdog paper) (4.45), it shows that two consecutive full steps reduce Φ_ψ by (γ + (1/β − 1)ψ)h (p. 19–20);
  - **Lemma 4.6**: the switching condition with τ > 2 makes the nonmonotone sufficient reduction hold (p. 20);
  - **Lemma 4.7**: a geometric acceptability lemma, so that a point whose Φ_ψ-line lies below another's by the margin is acceptable to it (Figure 2, p. 20–21);
  - **Lemma 4.8**: an induction that every full step is l-filter-acceptable from some K_3 on (p. 22);
  - **Theorem 4.1**: q-superlinear in x, q-quadratic in (x, λ) (p. 22).

  The existence of the first l-filter entry K_3 inside the region D_{K'_2}(ψ) is introduced "without loss of generality" rather than argued in detail (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: to prove a filter (or any non-merit-function) method accepts Newton steps, pick an exact penalty function as an auxiliary ruler. Show full steps decrease it by a margin proportional to h, then show any such decrease implies filter acceptability (Lemmas 4.5 and 4.7, p. 19–21).
  - Transferable technique: in trust-region global-convergence proofs, show the "good-step" radius interval is wider than a factor of two, so the halving rule cannot skip it (p. 13, p. 15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Preliminary (p. 23). Test set: all 411 CUTEr problems with fewer than 100 variables or constraints from Vanderbei's AMPL collection (p. 23). Baseline: his own filterSQP. New code: FASTr (filter active-set trust-region), with the nonmonotone g- and l-filter, M = 2 ("though we have also experimented with other values of M"), and no SOC (p. 23). Fairness: both use BQPD for the QPs, and the measure is the number of QPs solved, "roughly proportional to CPU time" (p. 23). Output: a single performance profile (Dolan–Moré) (Figure 3, p. 23). Claim: "in general, the new implementation outperforms filterSQP". The improvement is attributed, as a belief, to avoiding SOC steps far from the solution and to larger trust-region radii from nonmonotonicity (p. 23). This explanation is not isolated by an ablation, and no failure counts, M-sensitivity results or comparison with other solvers are reported ("left for later", p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: compare a new prototype with your established code using the same inner subproblem solver and an inner-solve count as the metric. That way the comparison measures the globalization, not the linear algebra (p. 23).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues Fletcher–Leyffer 2002 and the Fletcher et al. 2002a,b convergence papers, and takes its definitions from Chin and Fletcher 2003 (p. 3–5). It surveys filter variants (Gonzaga et al., Ribeiro et al., Wächter–Biegler line search, Audet–Dennis DFO, Karas et al. nonsmooth) (p. 4). It positions itself as an improvement over three named predecessors, each criticised on one specific point (Ulbrich: saddle-point risk; Wächter–Biegler: SOC steps; Gould–Toint: no fast-convergence proof, and "differs substantially ... and is easier to implement in our view") (p. 4, p. 24). There is no criticism beyond those points.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution sentence comes early in the introduction: "In this paper, we present a new filter method that combines global and fast local convergence. Our method improves on previous results for second-order filter methods." (p. 4). It is followed by a "Unlike X ... In addition ... unlike Y" contrast paragraph (p. 4). The algorithm comes with motivation paragraphs for each condition (p. 7–8) and a plain-language description of the switch and backtrack (p. 10). Imported lemmas are cited rather than reproved (p. 12–13). Weak points get candid remarks (global QP solves, p. 15; penalty function only as a device, p. 19). Hedging in the numerics ("preliminary", "We believe", p. 23). The conclusion restates the two improvements over Wächter–Biegler and Ulbrich (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution levels: algorithm (two-filter switch with backtrack), theory (global convergence under MFCQ; q-superlinear/q-quadratic local convergence without SOC), software prototype (FASTr, shorter code using one loop for restoration and optimization, p. 23). Limits stated: global QP solves assumed (removal only sketched, p. 15); restoration phase unspecified and possibly converging to a nonzero minimum of h (p. 8); numerics preliminary, against one solver, details "left for later" (p. 23). Limits visible but not stated: the local theory needs LICQ, strict complementarity and SOSC (p. 16); the choice M = 2 is not justified by reported data (p. 23). No explicit future-work list beyond "Detailed computational tests and comparisons with other solvers" (p. 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Nonmonotone acceptance through "dominated by at most M entries" generalizes a Pareto filter in one parameter, with M = 0 giving the standard filter, so the old lemma becomes a corollary (Definition 2.3, Corollary 3.1, p. 7, p. 12).
- Tighten the switching condition (h̃ ≤ ζ‖d̄‖^τ, τ > 2) in exchange for a weaker sufficient-reduction test. The trade is what lets Newton steps pass near a solution (p. 7–8, p. 20).
- Use an exact penalty function only inside the proof, never in the algorithm (p. 19).
- When a proof needs an inconvenient assumption, state the known decomposition device that removes it and the inequality that device delivers (Δq ≥ κρ when χ_k ≥ ε) (p. 15).
