### S149 · Experiments with MINLP Branching Techniques (European Workshop on Mixed Integer Nonlinear Programming 2010; Preprint ANL/MCS-P1734-0310)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1-9 was read, including Figs. 1-4 and Table 1.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, p. 3)

- **D1 Problem & entry**: He opens with new computer-science MINLP applications and announces a library for them, DIWAL (IEEE 802.11 mesh networks, data-optical networking, relay-node placement, mesh capacity fairness) (pp. 3-4). The problem then comes from his own solver: MINLPBB "is a fairly simplistic nonlinear branch-and-bound solver", with depth-first search and maximum-fractional branching, and it is outperformed by LP/NLP-based B&B (p. 4). Strong branching cuts the node count but costs too much CPU even with SQP warm starts. Pseudo-cost branching beats both (Fig. 2, p. 5). The root cause he names is that an NLP cannot be restarted from a basis the way an LP can. The factors are stale after a solve, because the solver exits once the convergence test passes and never forms new factors (pp. 4, 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Make strong branching cheap by tying it to the SQP solver (filterSQP + BQPD) (p. 6).
  1. Approximate strong branching: replace each child NLP with one SQP iteration (a single QP), warm-started from the parent's optimal active set. The fathoming rules are adapted because each solve is only approximate (p. 6).
  2. Reliability branching built on approximate strong branching, with a reliability threshold of 2 (p. 6).
  3. Hot-started QPs: after the parent NLP, re-solve the final QP so that the factors match the NLP solution, store them, and reuse them for every strong-branching QP through a hot-start feature "comparable to a dual-active-set method" (p. 7).

  What is kept: the standard MILP score with µ = 1/6 (p. 5). What changes: the oracle that evaluates the children.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: MINLP (1.1) with polyhedral X and Y, f and c twice continuously differentiable and "possibly convex" (p. 3). The NLP solver is trust-region filter SQP (p. 6). No convexity assumption is used in the branching experiments.
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a. There is no theorem. The argument is a mechanism argument: why NLP factors go stale after a Newton exit (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: Performance profiles of nodes and CPU on an unnamed set of "medium-sized problems" (Figs. 1-4, pp. 5-7). Reliability branching is best and beats BONMIN-Hybrid on CPU. The node comparison is flagged as "less relevant" because BONMIN-Hybrid counts only NLP solves and not LP nodes (pp. 6-7). Table 1 times four instances (stockcycle, RSyn0805H, SLay10H, Syn30M03H): a single QP saves a factor of 2-3, a hot-started QP up to 40 (p. 8). Test-set composition, instance count and solver versions are not given.
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: Algorithmic and preliminary ("preliminary numerical results", p. 6; "preliminary results are encouraging", p. 8). Open items: tuning the reliability parameters for MINLP (p. 7), and a QP-based tree search with only occasional NLP updates, which "may become competitive" with LP-based tree search (p. 8). The conclusion claims single-QP strong-branching heuristics are "superior to strong branching based on NLP solves" (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. Integration at the iteration level: what the inner solver already produces (final QP factors, active set) is reused for the outer branching decision. The remedy is a hot-start feature inside the QP solver, not a black-box call (pp. 6-8).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (step 2, fair unit; step 7, own method's weakness). The node metric is called less relevant because BONMIN counts differently (pp. 6-7). His own MINLPBB is called simplistic and outperformed (p. 4). ⚠ variant: the test set is unnamed and whether it was filtered is not stated, so steps 4 and 6 are absent (p. 4).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (step 1). The DIWAL library is started from collaborators' and literature applications (pp. 3-4, 8).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (weak). "In some cases, these applications require detailed reformulations to avoid or mitigate nonconvexities" (p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate: **cheap surrogate oracle for an expensive tree decision** (a single QP for a child NLP, with a stored factorization) (pp. 6-8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a MILP technique fails to transfer to MINLP on cost, find the LP property it relies on (a basis determines the iterate) and ask which part of it the NLP solver can supply (here, factors re-synchronized by one extra QP solve) (pp. 4, 6-7).
- Report a per-instance timing table at three levels of approximation (full NLP / single QP / hot QP) next to the profiles, so that the speed-up factor is visible (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Given a basis (or active set) is not sufficient to determine a starting point because the Jacobian also depends on the value of the variables, (x, y)." (p. 4)
- "These results show that the benefit obtained by solving just a single QP is only a factor of 2 or 3, whereas hot-started QPs are faster by a factor of up to 40." (p. 8)

### S100 · Oil Spill Response Planning with MINLP (SIAG/OPT Views-and-News 21(2) 2010, pp. 1-8; no DOI)
**Read level**: full. The article pp. 1-8 was read, including the model, solution approach, case study, extensions and references.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Fengqi You (first author, Argonne postdoc) (p. 1). The editor's note on p. 9 says the paper is "almost entirely the brainchild and work of one of Argonne’s talented postdocs" (see H009). The design choices below are the paper's and are not attributed to Leyffer personally.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Existing oil-spill models treat response operations and oil weathering separately, although cleanup changes slick volume and area, which feeds back into weathering and coastal protection (p. 1). The paper aims to integrate the two in one mixed-integer dynamic optimization (MIDO) model that minimizes total response cost (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Couple an ODE weathering model (spreading, evaporation, dispersion, emulsification, viscosity) with a discrete-time planning model (boom deployment with lifetimes, skimmers, in-situ burning with a big-M thickness switch, dispersant sorties with a regulatory limit) (pp. 2-5). Each planning period is made one finite element, so the continuous and discrete time grids coincide. Orthogonal collocation then turns the MIDO into an MINLP (pp. 5-6). An approximate MILP (ODE decoupled, slick thickness taken as unaffected by the response) supplies the starting point (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Known spill volume, rate and duration. Wind and current enter as constant parameters. Area thresholds area_{i,t} come from a given drift process (pp. 2, 4). A 3-D PDE slick model is set aside on purpose as "orders of magnitude beyond the current solver capabilities" (p. 2). The discretization ignores widely different rates of change, and a quasi-steady-state decoupling is named as the fallback (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (modelling paper, no theory).
- **D5 Experiments**: A Deepwater Horizon-based case: 3 staging areas, a 76-day horizon, 2,052 discrete variables, 11,482 continuous variables, 14,006 constraints. DICOPT with CPLEX 12.2 and KNITRO, 139 CPU s in total. Solved directly without the MILP initialization, the MINLP was reported "infeasible" (pp. 6-7). The input data are "available upon request" (p. 6).
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: Application and modelling. Future work: a stochastic or real-time MIDO for uncertainty, and a multi-objective version with response time (credited to a reviewer) (p. 7). No global optimality claim is made for the nonconvex MINLP.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. Formulation is chosen with the solver in mind (the PDE is dropped for solver capability, p. 2). A linear approximation initializes the nonconvex model, and without it the solve fails (pp. 6-7).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (weak; collaborator-driven). The MINLP/MIDO toolkit is carried into environmental planning, but by a postdoc (p. 1; H009 p. 9).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. The model and data are "available upon request" (p. 6), not released as a public library instance.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To couple continuous-time dynamics with discrete-time decisions, make each decision period one collocation finite element, so that a single index t serves both grids (pp. 5-6).
- For a nonconvex MINLP that fails from a cold start, build the MILP obtained by decoupling the dynamics and use its solution as the start. Report the cold-start failure as well (pp. 6-7).

**Quotes**:
- "The reformulated MINLP is highly nonlinear and nonconvex, and requires careful initializations to avoid numerical difficulties." (p. 6)
- "Our goal here is to present an approach that couples the ODE with the planning model." (p. 6)

### S054 · Proactive energy management for next-generation building systems (Fourth National Conference of IBPSA-USA (SimBuild) 2010, DOI 10.26868/30680611.2010.ts07b-01-zavala)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1-9 was read; the figures on pp. 4-6 survive only as axis residue.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Victor M. Zavala (first author), Jianhui Wang, Emil M. Constantinescu, Mihai Anitescu, Guenter Conzelmann (p. 1). This is a position/framework paper. Leyffer's share is not stated. The MINLP solution section (p. 7) cites his work (Goux-Leyffer, FilMINT).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Building energy management today is "reactive": controls use only current disturbances (EnergyPlus and TRNSYS practice). Building zones respond slowly, so storage and daily trends go unused and equipment cycles too often (p. 1). Cogeneration, storage and net metering will make operations much more complex (pp. 1-3).
- **D2 Core idea**: A proactive framework built on a scenario-based mixed-integer optimal control problem (1). Binary on/off controls w(τ) and continuous controls u(τ) act on DAE building models over a moving horizon, with Ns sampled disturbance scenarios, and the problem is solved in closed loop (p. 3). Proposed MINLP solution ideas (p. 7):
  1. Warm-starting across horizon shifts: update the integer controls via a continuous control problem to get an upper bound, reuse pseudocosts, and use local-branching heuristics.
  2. Hierarchical decomposition and linearization to a MILP, followed by fixing the binaries and solving the NLP for the continuous controls.
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Disturbances are forecast (WRF weather model with an uncertainty band, empirical models for prices and occupancy) and sampled from a distribution P (pp. 3, 5-6). Equilibrium market models are judged unsuited to buildings, so agent-based models (EMCAS) are proposed (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: Results from earlier simplified studies (continuous horizon studies, no MINLP solved here): a purely reactive strategy costs up to 24% more in the HVAC case and up to 300% more in the PV-hydrogen-battery case. A 1-day horizon reaches near-minimum cost in the HVAC case (pp. 3-4). The MINLP framework itself is not tested. Future work is "a comprehensive case study" (pp. 7-8).
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: Conceptual framework plus a solution agenda. The authors expect tree-search MINLP methods to be "unlikely to be successful" when the number of binaries or time steps is large (p. 7). They name a concrete obstacle: building simulators provide no derivatives, and they call for AD interfaces (p. 7). The claim about grid-level demand predictability is stated as a claim ("We claim", pp. 1, 7) and is not tested.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 1). The proposed fallback ranks linear first: linearize to a MILP, then fix the binaries and solve the NLP (p. 7).
- Taste, warning sign 1 ✅ evidence. Black-box simulators force logic rules, GA or DFO and repeated simulations. The remedy proposed is to expose derivatives through AD (p. 7).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (collaborator-driven). MINLP warm-start and pseudocost machinery is carried into building operation (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a moving-horizon MINLP, treat consecutive solves as related problems. Carry over the integer schedule (shifted) for an immediate upper bound and reuse pseudocosts between windows (p. 7).
- Before arguing for proactive control, run the forecast-horizon sweep (relative cost against horizon length) to show where extra forecast information stops paying, and why (storage capacity, insulation) (pp. 3-4).

**Quotes**:
- "These methods have in common that they perform a tree search to resolve the combinatorial nature of the binary decision variables." (p. 7)
- "With this, the building models can be treated only as black boxes by optimization solvers, forcing the user to rely on logic-based strategies, genetic algorithms, or derivative-free optimization." (p. 7)

### S074 · Software for nonlinearly constrained optimization (Wiley Encyclopedia of Operations Research and Management Science 2010, DOI 10.1002/9780470400531.eorms0570; read as Preprint ANL/MCS-P1768-0610)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1-17 was read; the reference list from p. 16 on was scanned. The version read is the Argonne preprint, not the encyclopedia typeset version. Cross-references to other encyclopedia chapters appear as "Chapter ??" and "(??)".
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ashutosh Mahajan (Argonne postdoc) (p. 3)

- **D1 Problem & entry**: An encyclopedia entry. The task is to categorize and survey NCO software (p. 3). The problem class is (1.1), with range constraints on nonlinear and linear functions and bounds (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Every NCO method is described by four components: approximate subproblem, global convergence strategy, global convergence mechanism, convergence test. Solvers are "differentiated by how each of these key ingredients is implemented" (p. 4). Solvers are grouped by subproblem: interior point (Ipfilter, IPOPT, KNITRO, LOQO), sequential LP/QP (CONOPT, FilterSQP, KNITRO-SLQP, LINDO, LRAMBO, NLPQLP, NPSOL, PATHNLP, SNOPT, SQPlab), and augmented Lagrangian (ALGENCAN, GALAHAD, LANCELOT, MINOS, PENNON) (pp. 4-14). Each entry has an "Algorithmic Methodology" part and a "Software and Technical Details" part. Table 1 gives the model, globalization, interfaces and language of each solver (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and c twice continuously differentiable. The survey covers solvers that handle nonconvex functions, and convex-only codes (MOSEK, CVXOPT) are set aside (pp. 3-4).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (survey). §5 classifies termination outcomes: KKT points; Fritz-John points, explained as the scaled objective-gradient weight α → 0 as multipliers diverge; local infeasibility certificates, via filter restoration or a penalty parameter driven to infinity; and unboundedness, detected via a user lower bound (pp. 14-15).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: Reference and orientation. The own solver (FilterSQP) gets the same two-paragraph treatment as the others (p. 8). Weaknesses of other codes are reported from their documentation: Ipfilter can converge to a local maximum without a fix (p. 6), MINOS has no guarantee from remote starts (p. 13), and PENNON has no convergence proof for nonconvex problems (p. 14). Web resources and test sets (NEOS, COIN-OR, CUTEr, COPS) close the chapter (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (step 4, building blocks). Every solver is decomposed into the same four interchangeable components, which is the "building blocks" view of Method 1 (p. 4).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 4, evaluability). "Remedies If Things Go Wrong" tells the reader to replace log(x1^3 + x2) by log(s) with a slack s = x1^3 + x2, s ≥ 0. The reason given is that adding the constraint x1^3 + x2 ≥ 0 does not help, since nonlinear constraints may hold only in the limit (p. 15). Large multipliers are read as a symptom of CQ failure (p. 15).
- Taste 3 ✅ evidence. Termination is analysed by what kind of point is reached (KKT vs FJ vs infeasible stationary), not just "converged" (pp. 14-15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To survey a crowded solver field, fix one component taxonomy (subproblem / strategy / mechanism / test) and give every code the same two blocks ("methodology", "software details") plus one summary table (pp. 4-5).
- If a function domain is violated at an iterate, move the nonlinear argument into a bounded slack variable rather than adding a nonlinear constraint (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "Solvers for NCO are differentiated by how each of these key ingredients is implemented." (p. 4)
- "It is often possible to reformulate the nonlinear constraints to avoid IEEE exceptions." (p. 15)

### S118 · Foundations of constrained optimization (Wiley Encyclopedia of Operations Research and Management Science 2010, DOI 10.1002/9780470400531.eorms0630; read as Preprint ANL/MCS-P1767-0610)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1-17 was read; the references from p. 15 on were scanned. The version read is the Argonne preprint.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ashutosh Mahajan (p. 3)

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The companion entry to S074. It presents NLP foundations for (1.1), min f(x) s.t. c(x) = 0, x ≥ 0, emphasising "general methods" and their "key components" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: One framework (Algorithm 1) with four components: convergence test, approximate subproblem, globalization strategy and globalization mechanism (p. 4). Each is then surveyed:
  - Tests: KKT, Fritz-John, and the infeasibility test via the feasibility problem (2.2) (pp. 4-5).
  - Subproblems: SQP, SLP, SLQP, SQQP, primal-dual IPM, with the barrier-to-primal-dual derivation (pp. 5-9).
  - Strategies: augmented Lagrangian (linearly and bound-constrained, Algorithm 3), exact penalty with steering, filter and funnel (pp. 9-13).
  - Mechanisms: line search and trust region (Algorithms 4-5) (pp. 14-15).

  The funnel is presented as a filter with the single entry (U_k, −∞) (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: C², with the CQ failure route to FJ points stated explicitly (pp. 3-5). Theory is quoted with its assumptions: SQP is quadratic under exact Hessian, full-rank active Jacobian, CQ and SOSC, and the active set is identified finitely under strict complementarity (p. 7). Bound-constrained AL is globally convergent under bounded iterates and a Jacobian rank condition (Conn et al. 1991) (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis**: Summarized, not proved. Filter acceptance with γ and β excludes infeasible accumulation, and the switching condition decides f-type and h-type iterates (pp. 12-13). The Maratos effect is remedied by second-order correction or nonmonotone acceptance (p. 13). The barrier-Hessian equivalence is shown by eliminating Δz (pp. 8-9).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: Expository. It warns that "globalization" means convergence from remote starts and should not be confused with global optimization (p. 9). It names the trust-region weakness that the subproblem can become inconsistent as Δ → 0, with three remedies: penalty, restoration, composite step (p. 15). Cross-references to other entries appear as "Sections ??–??" (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (step 4). The four-component decomposition, "implementation-oriented" (p. 4), is the building-block view that later became Uno's design (cf. SKILL.md Method 1 step 4).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The home toolkit (filter, restoration, SLQP) is presented as one of the field's standard components, with Fletcher-Leyffer filter acceptance stated in full (pp. 12-13).
- Taste 3 ✅ evidence. Convergence results are stated with their hypotheses (CQ, SOSC, strict complementarity), and the infeasible-stationary case gets its own convergence test (pp. 5, 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Teach a family of algorithms as a product of independent choices (test × subproblem × strategy × mechanism). Any new method can then be placed by naming its choice in each slot (pp. 4, 14).
- Give the infeasible case its own stopping test, from the KKT conditions of the feasibility problem (2.2) with ℓ1 multipliers in [−1, 1], instead of treating it as a failure (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "Our presentation is implementation-oriented" (p. 4)
- "Filter and funnel methods provide an alternative to penalty methods that does not rely on the use of a penalty parameter." (p. 12)

### S098 · Optimal control model of technology transition (International Journal of Global Energy Issues 2010, DOI 10.1504/ijgei.2010.036954)
**Read level**: partial. pp. 1-18 were read: the introduction, the three models, the discretization (§3; the Model II discretization on pp. 11-12 was skimmed), the results, the extensions and Appendices A and B (start). pp. 19-24 (figure residue) were not used. The version read is the journal preprint ("Vol. x, No. x").
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Donald A. Hanson (first author), Yaroslav Kryukov (corresponding author), Todd S. Munson (p. 1)

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The question is the socially optimal transition from old to low-emission energy technology under a discounted cumulative emission cap (pp. 2, 6). Historical adoption paths are S-shaped or concave with a kink (p. 3). The prior economics literature solves stylized models analytically or uses Gauss-Jacobi/Seidel iterations on optimality conditions, called "notoriously slow and unreliable" (p. 4). The methodological entry is that economists discretize annually by default, on the assumption that period length does not matter (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three optimal-control models of increasing detail: I learning-by-doing (pp. 6-7), II convex adjustment cost on the output increase (pp. 7-8), III Cobb-Douglas capital investment with a terminal capital credit e^{−rT}K(T) against horizon effects (pp. 8-10). Each is written in continuous time, discretized by explicit Euler, implicit Euler and trapezoidal collocation, and solved directly as an NLP in AMPL/KNITRO, instead of through Hamiltonian optimality conditions (pp. 10-12). Modelling moves:
  - cumulative output x(t) is written as an ODE, so that the discretization is sparse (p. 7);
  - y = max{0, q̇ⁿ} is written as two inequalities, made exact by the objective (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Constant marginal costs (I, II), the learning curve cₙ(x) = cₙ⁰(x/X̄+1)^{log₂γ}, constant-elasticity demand with growth b (p. 17), and parameters calibrated so that all models match q°(0) ≈ 60 and cₙ(T) ≈ 30 (pp. 5-7, 9; Tables 1-2). A finite horizon approximates an infinite one (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no theorems). Appendix A derives the functional forms (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: Table 3 gives CPU time for 3 models × 3 schemes × h ∈ {1, 0.5, 0.1}. Step length dominates and "no one method turns out to be the fastest across all step lengths" (pp. 12-13). Model I's apparent smooth transition at large h is a discretization artifact. Explicit Euler spreads the switch over 2-3 periods, while the implicit schemes switch in one step (p. 13). There is a sensitivity sweep over the emission reduction ζ (p. 14).
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: Economic results (learning-by-doing alone gives a bang-bang switch, adjustment costs give an S-shape, capital gives a kink plus a concave path) plus a methodological caution about discretization (p. 4). The stated goal is policy work, which needs more technologies, capital conversion and adaptive mesh refinement (pp. 14-15). The AMPL code is online (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. Formulation decides what can be solved: an ODE form for sparsity (p. 7), a smooth reformulation of max{0, ·} (p. 8), and solving the optimization directly rather than its first-order conditions, which "requires careful assumptions about curvature" (p. 4) and cannot tell maximizers from other stationary points (p. 12).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (variant on discretization). An attractive result (the smooth transition) is tested by refining h and switching schemes, and it turns out to be an artifact (p. 13).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (step 5). No winner is named among the discretization schemes; the conclusion is a rule by class, with implicit schemes preferred for stiff ODE/DAE (pp. 12-13).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak). The AMPL code is published with a run file that switches the discretization method and period length (p. 18).
- New-pattern candidate: **discretization-convergence check** (formulate in continuous time, discretize several ways, shrink h until the schemes agree before interpreting the shape) (pp. 4, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before interpreting any qualitative feature of a discretized control solution, re-solve with at least two schemes, one of them implicit, and a smaller step. Keep the feature only if it survives (pp. 4, 13).
- Write the model so that the discretization scheme is a switch in one run file, and publish that file (pp. 10, 18).
- Add a terminal value term (here e^{−rT}K(T)) to remove finite-horizon end effects, and say which correction was chosen among the alternatives (pp. 9-10).
