### S091 · Binary optimal control by trust-region steepest descent (Mathematical Programming 2023, doi:10.1007/s10107-021-01733-z)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–42 read (theory §§2–3, experiments §4, conclusions §5), with references pp. 42–44 skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Mirko Hahn (first author, Magdeburg), Sebastian Sager · **Position in trajectory**: mixed-integer optimal control line (MIPDECO/binary control), here done directly in function space, not first-discretize-then-MINLP.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: Binary optimal control is recast as optimization over measurable sets: min J(U) s.t. U ∈ Σ on a finite atomless measure space, with J = 𝒥∘ν for a vector measure ν, e.g. ν(U) = χ_U (pp. 1–2). The entry point is that the existing routes (indirect maximum-principle methods, dynamic programming, moment relaxations, combinatorial integral approximation, first-discretize-then-optimize MINLP) do not give a descent method on the set itself. The paper takes the decomposition methods' observation that "control sets form a continuum and can therefore be improved incrementally" (p. 2). Its closest relative is Céa et al. [10], and the stated difference is that the method is "stated as a generic trust-region method within a metric space" (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: (i) The derivative is a finite signed measure J′(U)(D) = 𝒥′(ν(U))(ν(D\U) − ν(D∩U)), whose Radon–Nikodym density g_U plays the role of the gradient (Thm. 1 p. 7; Cor. 1 p. 8). (ii) The trust region is the measure of the symmetric difference, d(A,B) = μ(AΔB) (p. 5), so the subproblem min ∫_D g_U dμ s.t. μ(D) ≤ Δ (eq. (8), p. 23) is solved by taking the sublevel set of g_U. (iii) Procedure 1 bisects the level η to find a near-optimal step and allows an inexact gradient and steps that only approximately match the radius on meshes (pp. 23–24). (iv) Algorithm 2 is a textbook ratio test with radius doubling and halving (p. 26). What is kept is the whole classical trust-region skeleton. What changes is the space (sets with a pseudometric) and the step (a set added by symmetric difference: "cumulatively by deriving steps that are 'added' to the current control set U rather than completely replacing it", p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1 lists the measurable space, a bounded-variation vector measure, Fréchet-differentiable 𝒥, finite μ, ‖ν(D)‖ ≤ Cμ(D), and atomlessness (p. 5). Atomlessness is "particularly important to our argument" (p. 4), since it guarantees subsets of any measure (Lemma 4, p. 10). The global error bound needs a Lipschitz derivative over conv ν(Σ). The authors say this was chosen "in the interest of brevity" over a geodesic argument that would need it only on ν(Σ) (pp. 8–9), which is an admitted convenience assumption. In the ODE case (Assumption 2) the dynamics must be C², affine in w, Lipschitz, and trajectories must stay ε away from ∂D (p. 13); affine-in-w "is always achievable for binary-valued controls by partial outer convexification" (p. 14). The PDE case (Assumption 3) needs C¹ maps, a bounded inverse of f_y, and nested mesh partitions of bounded eccentricity (pp. 20–21), contrasted with the conditions of Manns–Kirches (p. 21).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The chain rule through ν turns 𝒥′ into a signed measure, and σ-additivity is shown by tail bounds (Lemma 2, pp. 6–7). A first-order Taylor expansion o(μ(D)) is proved in set space (Thm. 1, p. 7), followed by a quadratic remainder LC²μ(D)²/2 (Lemma 3, pp. 8–9). The sublevel-set averaging lemma (Lemma 4, pp. 10–11) is the set analogue of "a steepest-descent step captures a fraction of the predicted decrease". Necessary optimality g_U ≥ 0 a.e. is proved by contradiction with Lemma 4 (Lemma 5, pp. 11–12). The instationarity measure ∫ |min{0, g_U}| dμ gives a suboptimality bound ε + LC²R²/2 within radius R (p. 12). In the ODE case, Gronwall bounds (Lemmas 6–8, pp. 14–17) and a costate/Lagrangian Taylor expansion prove Fréchet differentiability of the reduced objective in L¹ (Thm. 2, pp. 17–19). That gives g_U = (1 − 2χ_U)/m · (l_w + λᵀf_w), which the authors link to the Hamiltonian and the Competing Hamiltonian approach as a special case (p. 20). In the PDE case, Lebesgue differentiation on refining meshes gives g from DOF gradients (p. 22). Lemma 9 (pp. 25–26) gives a near-optimal step within δΔ. Theorem 3 gives finite termination at ε-stationarity with monotone objective (pp. 26–28), via the standard trust-region argument: a radius threshold Δ̄ below which steps are accepted, Δ_i ≥ min{Δ̄, Δ₀}/2, and a uniform decrease per accepted step. The Lipschitz hypothesis (10) of Theorem 3 reads ‖𝒥′(x) + 𝒥′(y)‖ in the extracted text (p. 26), while Lemma 3's (5) has a minus (p. 8). This is probably a typesetting or extraction slip; I have not checked it against another copy.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There are three proof-of-concept problems, with no competitor runs. (a) A 2D Poisson source inversion in FEniCS 2019.1.0 with local skeleton refinement (Plaza–Carey) that keeps the eccentricity bound (p. 30); D₂ is chosen by an FPTAS subset-sum (p. 31). It runs 187 iterations to objective 1.309·10⁻⁶ in 167.79 CPU s (p. 32). Table 1 gives refinement 0.851 of runtime and step-finding 0.028, while the text says refinement "accounts for 87%" (p. 32), a small inconsistency in the paper. (b) Lotka–Volterra fishing. A weight m(t) = 1 + (t_f − t) counteracts the larger impact of early switches (p. 35), and g_U is recorded through CVODES adjoint callbacks on the integrator's own grid (p. 35). It runs 90 iterations in 16.72 s to objective 1.34424 (p. 36). (c) Topology optimization, with a weighted-sum objective "because our method cannot accommodate constraints yet" (p. 39) and a radius capped to avoid disconnecting material (p. 40). It runs 26 iterations in 484.94 s (p. 41). Failure modes are reported: thinning-out from the penalty, checkerboards and escalating gradients (pp. 41–42). Plots show the objective, time per iteration, instationarity and step length or ratio ρ (Figs. 2, 4 and 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: Surveyed approaches are listed neutrally (p. 2). The method is placed against topology-optimization relatives by what it operates on: sets, not level-set functions [2]; cumulative steps, not fixed-point replacement [9, 27]; closest to [10], but generic (p. 3). The Competing Hamiltonian method is absorbed as a special case (p. 20). Leyffer's own Acta Numerica survey is cited as the MINLP route [3] (p. 2). The paper declines the measure-valued-control line (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Caveats are stated up front in the contribution paragraph: "we do not prove ultimate convergence" (p. 3). The outline promises a section that will "address some possible criticisms of the methodology" (p. 3). A concrete instance, the Lotka–Volterra problem, is given in the introduction to "aid the reader's understanding" (p. 2). Results are repeatedly labelled "proof of concept" (pp. 3, 42). A plain reason is given for not comparing, namely the difficulty of fair parameters on fixed control meshes (p. 42). Theory and intuition are interleaved: after each lemma a sentence says what it means for steps (e.g. pp. 11, 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical and algorithmic: a derivative on set space, an asymptotic stationarity guarantee under inexact gradients and inexact steps, and ODE and PDE derivations. Limits are specific: no convergence of the iterates, not even Cauchy (p. 3); no constraints (p. 39); discretization error not controlled (p. 23); "not ... a competitive topology optimization method" (p. 42); no extensive comparison (p. 42). Future work is to transfer constrained and higher-order NLP theory to measure spaces and to merge with continuous optimal control for "fast methods for mixed-integer optimal control with both ODE and PDE constraints" (p. 42).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): the home NLP toolkit (trust-region globalization and its convergence proof) is carried into binary control. The authors say: "We follow the reasoning of other trust-region convergence proofs to show that, given a small amount of groundwork, many of the steps are transferrable from conventional nonlinear optimization." (p. 3). They also "kept very closely to the theoretical framework used to validate conventional NLP methods" (p. 42). This also fits Heuristic 5 (transfer proofs lemma by lemma) and Heuristic 8 (function space first): enumerative B&B "on a fixed control mesh ... suffer[s] from an extreme disadvantage if the control mesh is too fine" (p. 42).
- Method 5 ⚠ (variant): the claims are calibrated (proof of concept, losses reported in topology optimization, pp. 41–42), but no baseline is run at all, and the stated reason is that fair comparison is hard (p. 42). This is calibration by withholding the comparison, not by building fair baselines.
- Method 1 ✅ (weak evidence): the gradient density is extracted from an off-the-shelf integrator through its adjoint machinery and callbacks (CVODES, pp. 20, 35), so the outer method is integrated at the inner solver's iteration level and inherits its adaptive grid.
- New-pattern candidate: **convex-combination-free descent on sets** (use a metric on the control set and sublevel-set steps of a gradient density instead of relaxing integrality) (pp. 5, 10, 23–24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To globalize a method on a non-vector space, find a (pseudo)metric whose ball is easy to optimize a linear model over. Here μ(AΔB) makes the trust-region subproblem a sublevel-set selection (pp. 5, 23).
- To prove a trust-region method converges under inexact gradients, set the gradient tolerance proportional to ωεΔ_i/μ(Ω) and the stationarity test at (1 − ω/3)ε. Then show (a) the test only fires near ε-stationarity, (b) a radius threshold exists below which steps are accepted, and (c) the decrease per accepted step is uniform (Algorithm 2 and Thm. 3, pp. 26–28).
- Make a bisection-based subproblem solver robust to meshes by asking for a step whose size lies in a range [Δ(1 + δ/(2η₂)), Δ], not an exact size, and refine only the candidate cells until the range can be hit (pp. 24–25).
- When a time-dependent control's effect decays toward t_f, put a weight m(t) into the measure (the trust-region metric) so that late and early switches are comparable (p. 35).
- Report per-component CPU time (init, solve, step, refine, record) to show where a new method's cost actually sits (Table 1, p. 32).

**Quotes**:
- "We present our work with the caveat that we do not prove ultimate convergence of the control sequence and that we do not even guarantee that it is a Cauchy sequence." (p. 3)
- "Outside of the field of topology optimization, where similar methods already exist, it is generally difficult to design fair comparisons to other methods." (p. 42)

### S120 · Modeling and solving cascading failures across interdependent infrastructure systems (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv:2407.16796)
**Read level**: full. pp. 1–21 read, references pp. 21–23 skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yijiang Li (first), Kibaek Kim, Matt Menickelly, Lawrence Paul Lewis, Joshua Bergerson (all Argonne) · **Position in trajectory**: bilevel/interdiction line, after Dandurand–Kim–Leyffer (SIAM J. Optim. 2021) on worst ACOPF contingencies, cited as [7] (pp. 3, 22). The application is Argonne DIS data from the Puerto Rico recovery after Hurricane Maria.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: The task is to identify the most severe contingencies in a system of interdependent infrastructure where failures cascade (p. 1). The entry is twofold. The dependency topology is not given asset by asset, only between asset classes (Table 1, p. 2), so arc weights are uncertain. And prior optimization work focuses on power-grid followers (OPF) (p. 3). Gap claim: no previous study quantified node significance "in a general system of interdependent infrastructure systems, with explicit consideration of a stochastic/nondeterministic network topology" (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: The model is a bilevel attacker–defender model. The leader disables at most N_c assets. The follower maximizes weighted service over N_p synchronized cascade stages with robust arc weights P ∈ 𝒫_f and the degradation y_{f,i} ≤ [1 − Σ P y]⁺ (eqs. (2)–(3), pp. 5–6). The robust inner minimum is dualized (LP duality, Prop. 1, pp. 6–7), producing bilinear terms λ·y (eq. (9b), p. 7). These are linearized by McCormick envelopes (eq. (10), p. 8), and exactness is proved by showing that the optimal duals λ* are binary (Lemma 1, Thm. 2, pp. 8–9). The follower then becomes a mixed-binary LP (Cor. 1, p. 9). The solution method is a Benders-type loop over leader solutions with no-good-style optimality cuts z ≥ Q(x^k) − Σ α_f(x_f − x_f^k). The coefficients α come from a one-off stage recursion over the dependency graph (Algorithm 1, p. 10). A strengthened cut adds β_f(x^k) = Q(x̃) − Q(x^k) from auxiliary subproblems that re-enable one disabled asset at a time (eqs. (17)–(18), p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The cascade is synchronized (p. 4). Service degrades linearly (p. 6). 𝒫_f is a generic polytope, invariant across stages (p. 5). In the experiments, 𝒫_f is a ±10% box around inverse-square-distance base weights, with Σ P = 1 and a distance cut-off (eqs. (24)–(25), p. 13). Importance weights are inversely proportional to asset-class frequency (Table 2, p. 13). The follower is always feasible at y = λ = v = 0 (p. 10). The model is a modelling choice and is not validated against the real cascade data; the networks are generated from frequency distributions of anonymized data (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Robust constraints are dualized into a "max over duals" form (Prop. 1 proof, eqs. (6)–(9), pp. 7). Lemma 1 (p. 8) gives integrality of the inner duals via the stationarity condition 1 − λ − π = 0 plus complementary slackness on the two cases of the [·]⁺ max. Exactness of McCormick for binary λ follows from case analysis (Thm. 2, pp. 8–9). The text also gives a negative remark that, with continuous λ, McCormick is "not exact in general", backed by observed networks (p. 9). Convergence: a repeated master solution closes the gap (Lemma 2, pp. 11–12), so the loop terminates finitely after at most Σ_{n≤N_c} C(N, n) leader solutions (Thm. 3, p. 12). Validity of the cut (15b) rests on the construction of α as a bound on the service reduction (p. 10); I found no separate lemma proving that the cut is a valid lower bound.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The setup is Julia 1.6.1, JuMP and Gurobi 9.5.1, with a 1 h limit and a 1% target gap; 10 master solutions per iteration generate cuts, and only the optimal one is strengthened (p. 14). There are two generated networks, a128 (1044 arcs) and a210 (2033 arcs) (Table 3, p. 13). Formulation comparison (Table 4, p. 14): (P_nl), solved with Gurobi's nonconvex QP, and (P_mc) give identical optima and attack sets, and (P_mc) is on average 68.17% faster. The authors read this as an empirical confirmation of Theorem 2 (p. 14). Variant comparison: bound plots show the upper bound found early while proving optimality dominates (Figs. 1–3, p. 15), and master time grows faster for Benders-S (Fig. 4, pp. 15–16). Scalability over N_p ∈ {1..4} and N_c ∈ {2..10} (Tables 5–6, pp. 17–18): N_p matters more than N_c because it enlarges the follower (p. 16), and on easy instances Benders beats Benders-S (p. 16). Instance analyses: optimal attack sets are not nested in N_c (Table 7, pp. 18–19), stage-wise worst-case arc weights differ by at most about 0.05 (Figs. 5–6, p. 19), and network service levels decay by stage (Figs. 7–8, p. 20). Against the literature, the Tang et al. [28] algorithm was run and fails even on a128 with N_p = 1, because it enumerates all leader solutions; the other methods were judged not applicable (pp. 20–21).
- **D6 Positioning**: The literature is split into non-optimization cascade studies and optimization (Stackelberg, mostly power grids) (pp. 2–3). Bilevel MIP techniques are surveyed: single-level reformulation (value function, KKT) vs decomposition (pp. 3–4). Single-level reformulation is rejected because the follower is a bilinear multistage problem and the result "could also be too large" (p. 20). Benders is acknowledged to share "some similarity" with Salmeron–Wood [22, 23], and novelty is claimed for the strengthened variant (p. 21).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Each formulation is given a name ((P_nl), (P_mc)) and the two are compared head to head (p. 9). Negative or limiting observations are written into the text: McCormick is not exact with continuous λ (p. 9), Benders-S loses on easy instances (p. 16), and a non-zero gap appears at N_p = 4 (p. 16). Practical value is argued for gap-limited solutions ("Even with the non-zero gaps, these feasible solutions have their practical uses", p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is modelling plus algorithm: the robust bilevel cascade model, the exact MILP follower and the strengthened Benders. Limits: hard instances at N_p = 4 and N_c ≥ 7–8 hit the time limit with gaps up to 55% (Table 5, p. 17). Termination is guaranteed only by enumeration (p. 12). The networks are synthetic, generated from anonymized data (p. 13). Future work: stronger cuts and valid inequalities, and symmetry of the dependency graph (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the formulation is fixed before the method. The bilinear follower is reformulated to an exact MILP via a structural integrality argument, and the gain is measured on the same algorithm (68% faster, same optima; pp. 9, 14). Both formulations are kept side by side (Method 4 step 3 style).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the same-solver rule (Gurobi for all variants), the stated gap target and limit, the variant reported to lose on easy instances, and a literature baseline actually run and reported to fail (pp. 14, 16, 20–21).
- Method 3 ✅ (weak evidence): the exactness theorem is bounded by the observation that continuous-λ McCormick fails on some networks (p. 9). The hypothesis (binary λ) is checked rather than claiming general exactness.
- New-pattern candidate: **exact relaxation via dual integrality** (prove that a McCormick/convex relaxation is exact by showing the multipliers that multiply the continuous variable are binary at optimality, then impose the binary restriction explicitly) (pp. 8–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a robust constraint y ≤ min_{P∈𝒫} [1 − Σ P x]⁺, write the inner min as an LP and dualize it into the outer max. If the bilinear dual-times-primal terms have duals that are provably 0/1 (from complementary slackness on the max with 0), linearize them exactly by McCormick with binary duals (pp. 6–9).
- Benders for interdiction with a budget: precompute a bound on the damage each attacked asset can cause by a stage recursion over the dependency graph. Strengthen each cut by re-solving the follower with one attacked asset released to obtain β coefficients, and add those auxiliary solutions' cuts too (pp. 10–11).
- Validate an exactness theorem numerically by solving both formulations with the same solver and reporting identical optima and attack sets (Table 4, p. 14).
- Report bound trajectories (upper and lower) to show whether the difficulty is finding or proving optimality (Figs. 1–3, p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "This set of results empirically confirms the exactness proof in Theorem 2." (p. 14)
- "This observation suggests that the optimal solution and objective values are likely to be found early in the execution of the algorithm, while proving optimality is a much more challenging task." (p. 15)

### S124 · Heuristic algorithms for placing geomagnetically induced current blocking devices (Electric Power Systems Research 2024, doi:10.1016/j.epsr.2024.110645; read as arXiv:2310.09409v1)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–8 of the arXiv/PSCC 2024 manuscript were read (the version is noted in the page mapping above), with references p. 7–8 skimmed. The EPSR typeset version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Minseok Ryu (first), Ahmed Attia, Arthur Barnes, Russell Bent, Adam Mate (LANL/Argonne) · **Position in trajectory**: power-grid MINLP applications with LANL. It carries the MPEC/complementarity reformulation (Fletcher–Leyffer 2004, cited [14]) into an application model.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: The problem is GIC blocking-device placement (GIC-BDP): place at most V devices to mitigate geomagnetically induced currents under geomagnetic disturbances or EMP (p. 1). The entry is computational. Spatial B&B on the MINLP is slow because of (i) the binaries, (ii) absolute-value equations for effective GIC, and (iii) nonconvex trigonometric trilinear terms in polar AC-OPF (p. 1). The paper claims that solvers fail "due to their inability to leverage the inherent problem structure" (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: First, reformulate: the rectangular power-flow form replaces the polar form "to avoid potential numerical instability" (p. 2), and |Θ_e| is replaced by the complementarity form s⁺ − s⁻ = Θ, I = s⁺ + s⁻, s⁺s⁻ ≤ 0 (eq. (10), p. 3). Second, 3ADMM-B: consensus copies separate the AC and DC networks (I^ac = I^dc) and the binaries (z^b = z with z ∈ [0,1]) (eqs. (11)–(12), p. 3). This gives three blocks (pp. 3–4): a binary block whose QP collapses, because ‖z‖² = ⟨1,z⟩ for binaries, to a unit-weight knapsack solved greedily in closed form (Remark 1 and Alg. 1, p. 4); a continuous DC-network NLP; and the AC-OPF NLP. Third, for comparison, the stochastic-learning Bernoulli method of Attia et al. [20] is adapted with budget-respecting sampling in decreasing-probability order (Alg. 2, pp. 4–5). The authors stress that "we apply ADMM to a discrete optimization problem" and that the result is "a problem-specific algorithm" (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The model (AC-rect) has operational limits, rectangular AC power flow, reactive losses from effective GIC, the DC GIC network with blocker-dependent balance (eq. (6)), a budget, and the complementarity form (eqs. (1)–(10), pp. 2–3). The E-field is fixed at 45° and magnitudes are E ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20} V/km (p. 5). No convergence assumptions are stated for 3ADMM-B; it is presented as a heuristic (pp. 1, 3).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: There are no theorems. The only analytical device is Remark 1, the binary identity ‖z‖² = ⟨1,z⟩, which turns the binary ADMM block into a sorted greedy (p. 4). The stopping rule uses normalized primal and dual residuals (Wohlberg [19]) (p. 4). No convergence or optimality-gap guarantee is given for either heuristic: n/a beyond this.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Test systems are EPRI-21 (8 substations, V = 3) and UIUC-150 (98 substations, V = 30) (p. 5). Code is Julia 1.8.1/JuMP on Argonne's Bebop cluster. Baselines SCIP and Juniper run with a 1 h limit. Every method's solution is evaluated by fixing its binaries and solving the resulting NLP, which gives a common quality meter (p. 5). The motivation runs show SCIP incumbents reduce load shedding vs "None" and "All" (Table II, Fig. 3), but gaps after 1 h are large, especially at E = 20 and on UIUC-150 (Fig. 4, pp. 5–6). In the comparison (Fig. 5, p. 6), both heuristics reach lower objective values than SCIP and Juniper, with 3ADMM-B under 100 s and SL under 1000 s. Ablations: normalized residual balancing cuts iterations from 73 to 19 (Fig. 6, p. 6), and the SL sample size N trades iterations against time per iteration (Figs. 7–8, pp. 6–7). No repeated runs are reported for the stochastic SL, and no solver settings beyond the time limit are given for SCIP or Juniper (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on its own group's prior GMD work (Ryu et al. [11, 12]) and Liang et al.'s branch-and-cut placement [8] (pp. 1–2). It departs from polar-form models [10, 12] by using the rectangular form (p. 2), and it cites the complementarity literature [13]–[16], including Fletcher–Leyffer [14], for the abs-value reformulation (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions are a numbered list (p. 1). Claims are strong: "superior performance of our methodologies in terms of both solution quality and computational speed" (abstract, p. 1), and "our heuristic approaches outperform the state-of-the-art MINLP solvers" (p. 2), based on single runs under a 1 h limit. Both heuristics are honestly labelled heuristics throughout.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a reformulation plus a heuristic algorithm, applied work. The only limit discussed is the need to tune N in SL (p. 7). Future work: line-switching as a second stage, and ML-pretrained real-time recommendations trained on heuristic outputs (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the formulation is repaired before the method. The nonsmooth abs value is replaced by smooth complementarity (citing his own MPEC-as-NLP line [14]) and the polar form by the rectangular form, both justified by solver convergence failure and numerical instability (pp. 2–3, 7).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): the home toolkit (MPEC reformulation, augmented-Lagrangian or ADMM splitting) is carried into a power-grid MINLP application through a collaborator group (pp. 3–4).
- Method 5 ✗ (contradiction): the claim of outperforming SCIP and Juniper rests on 1 h runs at default-like settings, single runs of a stochastic method, and no rule-of-thumb by class (pp. 1, 5–6). This is the opposite of "rebuild the competitors at their best settings" and of calibrated wording. The evaluation meter (fix binaries, solve NLP) is fair and shared, though (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: **consensus-copy splitting of binaries** (duplicate the integer vector into a binary block with a closed-form or greedy solution and a continuous block, linked by an ADMM consensus constraint) (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace |Θ| in a MINLP by s⁺ − s⁻ = Θ, |Θ| = s⁺ + s⁻, s± ≥ 0, s⁺s⁻ ≤ 0. This is a smooth complementarity form that NLP-based solvers handle better than the kink (eq. (10), p. 3).
- In an ADMM splitting with binaries, keep a binary copy z^b and a relaxed copy z ∈ [0,1]. With a cardinality budget, the binary block min ⟨λ,z^b⟩ + ρ/2‖z^b − z‖² becomes a sorted greedy, because ‖z^b‖² = Σ z^b (Remark 1, p. 4).
- Compare heuristics and exact solvers on one meter: fix each method's binaries and solve the remaining NLP to the same tolerance (p. 5).
- Adapt the ADMM penalty by normalized residual balancing (ρ×τ or ρ/τ when the primal and dual residuals differ by a factor β) and report the iteration savings (eq. (23), Fig. 6, p. 6).

**Quotes**:

### S135 · A bilevel optimization framework for imbalanced data classification (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv:2410.11171)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–11 of v3 (10 Jul 2025) were read, with references pp. 11–13 skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Karen Medlin (first, UNC, DOE SCGSR graduate fellow, p. 11), Krishnan Raghavan (Argonne) · **Position in trajectory**: ML-facing bilevel work. The bilevel optimization frame is applied to data selection; Leyffer is second author of a student-led paper.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: The setting is imbalanced binary classification (p. 1). Oversampling (SMOTE and its GAN derivatives) brings noise and overlap from synthetic data, and random undersampling under-fits (pp. 1–3). The entry: GANs reject synthetic points that do not reduce loss, so the paper rejects *real majority* points that do not reduce majority loss, with the accept/reject rule motivated by a bilevel problem (pp. 2–3). The gap claimed is that bilevel optimization has been used for imbalanced data, but "it has not been used to rebalance training data" (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: The bilevel model is min_{S_M ⊂ 𝒮} J(S_M; z(w*)) s.t. w* = argmin_w ℒ(S; z(w)), with S = S_m ∪ S_M (p. 6). MUBO (Alg. 1, p. 7) is a randomized accept/reject heuristic. It draws micro-samples of majority data of size ⌈m/10⌉, or ⌈m/100⌉ once the set exceeds 7m/4. It trains the network (inner loop) on all minority data plus the accepted majority data, and accepts the micro-sample only if the majority loss does not increase (pp. 6–7). The upper level is never solved as an optimization problem; the bilevel model "motivates" the accept/reject component (abstract, p. 1).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The classifier is a softmax on a scalar network output z(w) with binary cross-entropy (Defs. 1–3, pp. 4–5). The inner problem is solved approximately with Adam until a gradient tolerance or epoch cap (pp. 7, 9), so w* is not an exact argmin. Definition 5 in the extracted text sums the false-majority loss over s^F ∈ S_m (p. 5), which looks like a typo for S_M.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Lemma 1 (p. 8): for a majority point, correct classification ⇔ p(y=0|x) ≥ 1/2 ⇔ loss ≤ log 2. So the loss of a correctly classified point is strictly less than that of a misclassified one, and J^T_M < J^F_M. From this the authors argue that more correctly classified majority points imply lower majority loss, which supports loss as the acceptance proxy. They state that the converse needs "additional conditions" (p. 8). No convergence or optimality analysis of the bilevel problem is given.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Five UCI datasets: Ionosphere, Gisette, Abalone, Spambase, Connect4 (Table 2, p. 10). The setup is a 20% test split, five seeded runs, and an architecture and optimizer aligned with the GAN baselines (p. 9). Seven methods were rerun, but SMOTified-GAN's published numbers are used because its code could not be run (p. 9). Code is public (p. 9). Results (Table 1, p. 9): MUBO has the best average F1 on Gisette (0.96), Abalone (0.86) and Connect4 (0.96), and the worst of all methods on Ionosphere (0.87). The minority vs majority breakdown against GBO (Table 3, p. 10) shows MUBO has higher minority F1 and lower majority recall (pp. 10–11). There is an internal inconsistency: the text gives an imbalance ratio range "0.294% to 39.39%" (p. 9), but Table 2's smallest minority share is 3.84% (p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper contrasts data rebalancing with model rebalancing (reweighting), and focuses on data (p. 3). It reviews SMOTE variants and GAN hybrids at length (pp. 3–4). Its bilevel relatives are AutoBalance and neural architecture search, which share the lower level; the novelty is placed in the upper level (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper opens with an ATLAS particle-detector motivation, matching the Argonne context (p. 1). There are three contribution bullets (p. 3). The abstract claims F1 "up to 10% higher than state-of-the-art methods" (p. 1), while the results text says the next highest scores are "1% to 2% lower" (p. 10). The 10% figure matches only the Gisette comparison with GBO and SSG (Table 1, p. 9), so the abstract picks the most favourable pairwise gap. The Ionosphere loss is explained, not hidden (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm plus empirical results, with a light lemma. Limits are stated specifically: small datasets hinder the approach (p. 10), and "additional conditions are required" to link majority loss to F1 (p. 11). Future work: conditions for provable improvement, and good results on small and large datasets (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (weak evidence): an optimization frame (bilevel / leader–follower) is carried into ML data selection (pp. 6–7). Only the frame travels, though; none of the home NLP machinery (convergence theory, reformulation) does, so the transfer is shallow.
- Method 5 ⚠ (variant): failures are reported (Ionosphere, majority recall; pp. 10–11), runs are repeated (five seeds, p. 9) and code is public. But one baseline is taken from published numbers rather than rerun (p. 9), and the abstract's "up to 10%" is the most favourable pairwise gap (pp. 1, 9–10).
- Taste 3 (a result is only as good as the guarantee behind it) ⚠: the bilevel problem is not solved and no guarantee is offered beyond Lemma 1. The authors acknowledge the gap (pp. 8, 11). There is no Method N for this, so it is recorded here only.
- New-pattern candidate: **bilevel-as-motivation** (a bilevel model is written to justify a greedy accept/reject heuristic, not solved), also seen in the heuristic framing of S124 (pp. 1, 6–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For data subset selection, accept a random micro-batch only if a validation-type loss on the target class does not increase after retraining. Shrink the micro-batch size once the accepted set passes a threshold (Alg. 1, p. 7).
- When reporting an average F1 for imbalanced classes, also report minority and majority F1, precision and recall separately to show which class a method helps (Table 3, p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Given that our method utilizes all available minority data, Ionosphere’s experimental results indicate that a small dataset size is a hindrance to our undersampling approach." (p. 10)
- "Additional conditions are required to formally prove experimental results showing improved majority loss implies improved average F1 score." (p. 11)

### S152 · Population balance model-based dynamic multiobjective optimization of yeast cell manufacturing (IFAC-PapersOnLine 58(14) 2024, doi:10.1016/j.ifacol.2024.08.445)
**Read level**: full. All distinct content was read: §1 (pp. 2–3), §2 (pp. 3–4), §3 (pp. 4–5), §4 (pp. 5–7), §5 (pp. 7–8). The duplicated text-layer blocks were checked for differences only, and references (pp. 8–9) were skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Krystian Ganko (first, MIT, DOE CSGF), Marc D. Berliner (JuliaHub), Jinwook Rhyu, Liang Wu, Richard D. Braatz (MIT); Leyffer last author (Argonne) · **Position in trajectory**: distributed-parameter optimal control with a chemical-engineering group. There are no integer variables, so this is the continuous end of his optimal-control interests.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: Continuous yeast culture shows sustained oscillations from supercritical Hopf bifurcations (pp. 2, 4). The entry is a missing study: model-based linear MPC and input–output linearization exist, but "an optimal control study exploring the Pareto surface tradeoffs between tolerable degrees of process oscillation and economic profit has not yet been reported" (p. 2). The main technical contribution is stated as "numerical feasibility of model-based optimal control for an oscillatory distributed parameter system" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: (i) Population balance model (PBM) (1)–(15), reproduced from Inguva et al. 2023 (p. 3). (ii) Three objectives: harvest J1, feed cost J2 and oscillation J3 = −∫(dm0/dt)² (eqs. (16)–(18), p. 4). (iii) Discretize: method of lines in cell mass, choosing WENO FDM over Upwind FDM and OCoFE after accuracy and positivity tests and a mesh study (N_m = 300) (pp. 4–5). (iv) Control vector parameterization with piecewise-linear controls on K elements (eq. (22), p. 5). (v) The weighting method over a grid of 228 weight vectors (p. 6), solved with SLSQP (NLopt.jl), plus a δ-weighted input-smoothness regularizer (eq. (25), p. 5). (vi) Continuation in K: converged K-element trajectories are bisected to initialize 2K elements, from K = 2 up to 512 or 1024 (pp. 5–6), with Gaussian-perturbation multistarts at each stage (pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The reactor assumptions are free suspension, a single substrate, negligible gas-phase effects and constant volume (p. 3). Only S and m0 are measured; the filtered substrate S′ "is not observable" (footnote 1, p. 4). Variables are nondimensionalized to O(1) (p. 4). A restrictive CFL bound ν_max = 1 gives Δt_max (eqs. (20)–(21), p. 4). The stiff MOL system is integrated with CVODE BDF (pp. 4–5). The whole study is "predicated upon a near-perfect model" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: There are no theorems. The numerical-analysis devices are the CFL condition and SSP/TVD properties for the time integrator (pp. 4–5, footnote 3), and the continuity of gradients under regularization (p. 6). For verification, a few points are checked for Pareto optimality with the ε-constraint method via an L2 penalty, but the implementation "will not be discussed here due to space constraints" (p. 7). The tradeoff ratios Λ_{n,m} are computed between an oscillatory and a non-oscillatory Pareto policy (eqs. (26)–(29), p. 7).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The discretization study (Figs. 1–2, pp. 5–6) shows WENO keeps positivity better than OCoFE and smears less than Upwind. Median wall times are 304 ms (Upwind), 2278 ms (WENO), 339 ms (OCoFE) and 1191 ms (refined OCoFE), and the earlier OCoFE resolution produced non-physical results from Runge's phenomenon (footnotes 5–6, p. 5). The Pareto point cloud (Fig. 3, p. 6) has mixed-colour clusters, which the authors read as non-convergence to true Pareto points (p. 6) and confirm by inspection as locally (not globally) optimal points (p. 7). Cost per weight vector is 25,920 SLSQP runs, about 3 h on a 72-core server (footnote 8, p. 7). Two verified Pareto policies (Fig. 4, p. 7): oscillatory control is optimal only with tolerance for oscillation, S_f saturates for the whole campaign, and Λ1,2 ≈ +1.278, Λ1,3 ≈ −1.322 and Λ2,3 ≈ −1.035 (p. 7). The δ validation study is reported with "results not shown" (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: The study extends Henson's and Zhang–Henson's controller studies (pp. 2, 4) and Zhu et al.'s OCoFE simulations, which it corrects on numerical grounds (footnote 6, p. 5). The multiobjective method follows Miettinen (pp. 5, 7). The future MPC options listed are neuro-dynamic programming, hierarchical MPC, Riccati-based interior point and DDP (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Limitations are written into the results paragraph: solutions "may not be converging to the true Pareto optimal control trajectories" (p. 6), and only "rough heuristics" are used to approach global optima (p. 6). Practical implications are hedged ("One practical implication may be", p. 7; "More complete resolution of the Pareto surface will help ground these findings", p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a numerical-feasibility demonstration plus tradeoff insight. Limits: the weighting method only finds convex parts of the front (p. 5), global optimality is not guaranteed (p. 6), there is model–plant mismatch (p. 7), and MPC cost is high (p. 8). Future work is to choose an MPC scheme, which is called "an open research question" (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ (weak evidence): the numbers are read before blaming the optimizer. Discretization schemes are compared for positivity and dispersion, and a past scheme's Runge artefacts are diagnosed before optimizing (pp. 5–6). Variables are nondimensionalized for numerical precision (p. 4). The voice is mainly the Braatz group's, so this is weak evidence for Leyffer himself.
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the Pareto cloud is read critically, non-convergence is reported, the computational cost is stated and claims are hedged (pp. 6–7). But the δ study and the ε-constraint verification are not shown (pp. 6–7).
- New-pattern candidate: **coarse-to-fine continuation in the control discretization** (warm-start the 2K-element problem from the bisected K-element optimum, K = 2 → 512/1024) (pp. 5–6). It parallels the mesh-refinement-on-demand of S091 (pp. 24–25, 31).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before optimizing a PDE-constrained model, run an open-loop discretization shoot-out (accuracy, positivity, numerical diffusion and dispersion, median wall time over 100 runs) and a mesh study, and pick the cheapest scheme that keeps the state physical (pp. 5–6, footnote 5).
- Warm-start high-resolution control parameterizations by bisecting converged coarse trajectories (K → 2K). Refine each stage by multistart with Gaussian perturbations of decreasing variance around the previous local optimum (pp. 5–7).
- After a weighted-sum sweep, inspect clusters where different weights map to the same objective point as a symptom of local (non-Pareto) solutions. Verify selected points with the ε-constraint method (pp. 6–7).
- Summarize Pareto tradeoffs between two verified policies by ratios of objective differences Λ_{n,m} (eq. (26), p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "The weighting method only uncovers convex portions of the Pareto optimal set." (p. 5)
- "All discussions above are predicated upon a near-perfect model description of the yeast continuous manufacturing process." (p. 7)
