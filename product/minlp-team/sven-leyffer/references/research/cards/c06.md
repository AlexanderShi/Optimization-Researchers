### S012 · Interior methods for mathematical programs with complementarity constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2006, DOI 10.1137/040621065)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-28 of the preprint). I read the text, all theorem statements and the proofs of Theorem 3.4 (pp. 9-12) and Theorem 3.6 (pp. 13-14) line by line. The algebra of the Theorem 4.5 proof (pp. 17-19) and the Lemma 4.3/4.4 proofs (pp. 15-16) was skimmed for structure. Table 3 (problem list, p. 22) and the reference list were skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Gabriel López-Calva and Jorge Nocedal (Northwestern; López-Calva's thesis is cited for full proofs, p. 27) · **Place in trajectory**: the interior-point counterpart to Fletcher-Leyffer's "MPECs as NLPs with SQP" line. It answers the 2002 finding that LOQO and KNITRO were slower and less reliable than SQP on MacMPEC (p. 2), and it uses MacMPEC as the test bed (p. 21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The standard NLP reformulation (1.3) of an MPCC violates MFCQ at every feasible point. For interior methods this shows up as "conflicting goals of enforcing complementarity while keeping the variables x1, x2 away from their bounds" (p. 2). The entry point is a performance gap measured in earlier work: filterSQP almost always converged fast on MacMPEC, while LOQO and KNITRO "(both codes as of 2002)" were "slower and less reliable" (p. 2). The paper sorts the existing interior-point remedies into relaxation approaches (Scholtes, Liu-Sun, Raghunathan-Biegler, DeMiguel et al.) and exact-penalty approaches (Anitescu, Benson et al., Hu-Ralph) (pp. 2-3), and chooses penalization "because we view it as a general tool for handling degenerate nonlinear programs" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: enter a method paper from a documented, dated performance gap between two solver families on a public library, then classify the existing remedies into a small number of families before choosing one.
- **D2 Core idea**: Apply an interior method to the ℓ1-penalty problem (2.1), f(x) + π x1ᵀx2, with x1, x2 ≥ 0 kept by the barrier (pp. 3-4). Algorithm I (p. 5) solves each barrier problem approximately (2.7) and adds a complementarity test ‖min{x1, x2}‖ ≤ ε_comp (2.8), using the min-measure because it is scale-insensitive and independent of the number of variables (p. 4). The design contribution is the penalty update. "Classic" raises π by a factor of 10 only after a barrier problem is solved and (2.8) fails (p. 20). "Dynamic" may raise π at every inner iteration when complementarity is above ε_comp and has not fallen below η = 0.9 times its maximum over the last m = 3 iterations; the look-back allows for nonmonotone complementarity (pp. 20-21). What is kept unchanged: the interior iteration itself (barrier update, step, merit function), which is left to the base solver ipm-d (p. 20).
  - Transferable: when a penalty parameter must be adapted inside an inner loop, test progress against a fraction of the maximum over a short window rather than demanding monotone decrease, and do not increase it while the violation is already below its tolerance (bard3 shows why, p. 24).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Global theory assumes the inner algorithm always succeeds, so Algorithm I generates an infinite sequence satisfying (2.7)-(2.8); the inner algorithm's analysis is "beyond the scope of this paper" (p. 9). f, c are C¹ near the limit point, and MPCC-LICQ is assumed for stationarity (p. 9). Strong stationarity needs π_i^k x_ji^k → 0 at corner indices, the same condition Scholtes uses, or simply a bounded penalty sequence (Corollary 3.5, p. 12). The local analysis assumes a strongly stationary point with C² Lipschitz data, MPCC-LICQ, primal-dual strict complementarity and MPCC-SOSC (Assumptions 4.2, pp. 14-15), plus π > π* and rate conditions on μ, ε_pen, ε_comp (Theorem 4.5, p. 17). The authors note that Assumption 4.2(3) can be weakened (Lemma 4.4, p. 16) and contrast their assumptions with Anitescu's stronger "mixed-P property" (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Define multipliers from the barrier terms, σ1i = μ/x1i − π x2i (3.6), normalize by α = ‖(λ, σ1, σ2)‖∞ (3.7)-(3.8), show normalized multipliers of inactive constraints go to 0 via the ratio |σ1i|/|σ2i| = x2i/x1i → 0, and get boundedness by contradiction with MPCC-LICQ (pp. 10-11). (ii) Show C-stationarity by a sign argument at corner indices, σ1i > 0 ⇒ σ2i > 0 (p. 12), and strong stationarity when π x → 0 (3.12, p. 12). (iii) Allow nonmonotone penalties in the theorem on purpose, "important for deriving Theorem 3.6" (p. 12). (iv) Prove convergence of interior-relaxation methods as a corollary: map a relaxation sequence to a penalty sequence through a one-to-one KKT correspondence between (2.2) and (3.13), then apply Theorem 3.4 (pp. 13-14); the authors note the correspondence extends to general NLPs (p. 14). (v) Lemma 4.3: for π > π* the penalty problem inherits LICQ, strict complementarity and SOSC from the MPCC (p. 15). (vi) Theorem 4.5: implicit function theorem plus standard Newton analysis shows that near a regular solution one Newton step per barrier problem satisfies both stopping tests, π is never updated, and convergence is superlinear (pp. 17-19). The choice ε_comp = μ^0.4 is justified by the √μ distance to the solution when strict complementarity fails (p. 19).
  - Transferable: when two regularizations of the same degenerate problem exist (penalty vs relaxation), prove convergence for one and derive the other as a corollary through a KKT-point correspondence, and write the main theorem with a vector penalty and without monotonicity so it can absorb the mapped sequence.
  - Transferable: for a local result about an adaptive parameter, prove that near a regular solution the parameter stops changing and the inner loop needs one Newton step per outer iteration. This connects the practical algorithm to the clean local theory.
- **D5 Experiments**: 74 problems, mostly from MacMPEC, plus "a few problems to test the sensitivity of our implementations to bad scalings" (p. 21; Table 3, p. 22), restricted to fewer than 1,000 variables because the MATLAB code ipm-d cannot handle large problems (p. 21). Four methods, all in the same solver: NLP (ipm-d on (1.3)), Fixed (π = 10⁴), Classic and Dynamic (p. 21). The deliberate choice of a simple base solver without production heuristics is stated (p. 20). Measure: total inner iterations, shown as logarithmic performance profiles for two initial penalties, π⁰ = 1 and π⁰ = ‖∇f(x⁰)‖ (pp. 21, 23). Result: NLP is worst, Fixed is more robust but clearly beaten, and Classic ≈ Dynamic on MacMPEC (p. 23). Because MacMPEC is "composed almost exclusively of well-scaled problems", the authors look at ralph2, scale1, bard3 and bilin individually, with plots of complementarity and π per iteration (Figures 6-7, pp. 23-25). Failures are listed with causes: C-stationary convergence on scale4, ralph2 for Classic, no multipliers at the solution (ex9.2.2, ralphmod), other degeneracy (incid-set*, pack-rig*, pack-comp*), and a rank-deficient Jacobian (tap-09) (pp. 25-26).
  - Transferable: when the aggregate profile cannot separate two variants because the library lacks the hard case, say so and add a per-iteration trace of the controlled quantity on the few instances that do separate them (pp. 23-25).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Hu-Ralph's proof for exact subproblem solves and relates the result to Anitescu's independently derived inexact analysis, stating the difference (inner algorithm not analysed; Anitescu needs stronger assumptions, p. 12). Classic is presented as "more flexible" than solving the fixed-π penalty problem with an off-the-shelf solver, with the trade-off stated (p. 26). Predecessors are reformulated rather than attacked: the relaxation methods of Liu-Sun and Raghunathan-Biegler are given a new global proof via the correspondence (pp. 12-14).
- **D7 Writing**: Section 2 motivates the design with three numbered examples, each ending in a design lesson (warning sign, early detection, why not a huge fixed π, pp. 5-7), and Section 5 revisits them ("Example 1 (ralph2), revisited", p. 23). The paper is open about what is not done ("The analysis of the inner algorithm is beyond the scope of this paper", p. 9). Claims are dated ("both codes as of 2002", p. 2) and modest ("highly encouraging", p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory (global convergence to C-/strongly stationary points; superlinear local convergence; a relaxation corollary) plus algorithm (a dynamic penalty update), shown on a MATLAB prototype (pp. 9-26). Stated limits: no inner-loop analysis (p. 9), small problems only (p. 21), failures when no multipliers exist or degeneracy goes beyond complementarity (pp. 25-26). Stated future work: implement the method in KNITRO for large MPCCs, and develop general interior-penalty methods for degenerate NLPs (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: penalization is chosen as "a general tool for handling degenerate nonlinear programs" (p. 3), the MPCC is handled with an existing interior NLP solver (ipm-d) plus a penalty layer (p. 20), and the conclusion points back to general degenerate NLPs (p. 26).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: three tiny two-variable MPCCs (ralph2, scale1, the x1² + x2² − x1²x2² example) each break a penalty-update policy and become a design requirement (pp. 5-7). Failures are traced to violated hypotheses (no multipliers, extra degeneracy) rather than to errors in the theory (pp. 25-26).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: MacMPEC is the test bed, and bad-scaling variants are added because the library is "composed almost exclusively of well-scaled problems" (pp. 21, 23); scale4 is "a rescaling of problem scholtes3" (p. 25).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: all four methods run in one solver (same-solver rule, p. 21); the base solver is chosen so that production heuristics do not hide the effect (p. 20); competitor codes are dated "as of 2002" (p. 2); failures are reported per instance with causes (pp. 25-26).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the paper explains filterSQP's success by the formulation: (1.3) "is locally equivalent to a nonlinear program that satisfies MFCQ" (p. 2), and it reads the per-iteration complementarity and π traces as the diagnostic (pp. 6-7, 23-25).
- new-pattern candidate: **KKT-correspondence transfer between regularizations**. Prove convergence once for the penalty scheme, then transfer it to the relaxation scheme by mapping KKT points (pp. 12-14). This is close to Heuristic 5 (transfer proofs), but here the transfer is a formal corollary, not a reused proof.
- new-pattern candidate: **Windowed progress test for adaptive penalties**. Raise π only when the violation is above tolerance and has not fallen below a fraction of its recent maximum (pp. 20-21, 24-25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure complementarity by ‖min{x1, x2}‖ rather than x1ᵀx2: it is scale-insensitive and independent of the number of variables (p. 4).
- Choose ε_comp = μ^γ with γ < 1/2 (here 0.4) so that the complementarity test can still be met when strict complementarity fails and iterates are O(√μ) from the solution (p. 19).
- Prototype a new outer strategy inside a deliberately simple base solver, so that the base solver's own regularizations and scalings do not mask the effect, and say so in the paper (p. 20).
- Try two initial penalties, a naive one (π⁰ = 1) and a multiplier-norm estimate (π⁰ = ‖∇f(x⁰)‖), and report profiles for both (p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "The upward trend in complementarity should be taken as a warning sign that the penalty parameter is not large enough, since no progress is made toward satisfaction of (2.8)." (p. 6)
- "We chose to work with ipm-d because it is a simple interior solver that does not employ the regularizations, scalings, and other heuristics used in production packages that alter the MPCC, making it harder to assess the impact of the approach proposed in this paper." (p. 20)

### S018 · Leader-Follower Equilibria for Electric Power and NOx Allowances Markets (Computational Management Science 2006, DOI 10.1007/s10287-006-0020-1)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-23 of the Argonne preprint ANL/MCS-P1191-0804, including the result tables, conclusions and references).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yihsu Chen and Benjamin F. Hobbs (Johns Hopkins, Geography and Environmental Engineering), Todd S. Munson (Argonne) (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: an application of the "MPECs solved as NLPs" line (SKILL.md Signature Work 4) to a large, unstructured Stackelberg game. The economics questions and the network model come from the Hobbs group (p. 6); the solution method rests on Fletcher-Leyffer's MPEC results and Munson's PATH work (pp. 4, 14).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Can the largest producer in the PJM market manipulate both the electricity and the NOx-allowance markets (abstract, p. 2)? The entry point is a gap left by an earlier heuristic: Chen and Hobbs's Cournot analysis "failed to identify the optimal joint emissions and electricity strategy for the dominant producer, possibly underestimating the magnitude of its market power" (p. 3). A Stackelberg leader with capacity-constrained followers needs complementarity conditions, so the problem is an MPEC (p. 3). The paper takes the received difficulty head on (nonconvex feasible set; any smooth reformulation violates MFCQ) and cites recent SQP results suggesting that "an MPEC can be a numerically tractable tool to solve large-scale Stackelberg games" (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Write each follower's problem (Cournot generators, price-taking fringe, ISO with DC load flow and quadratic losses, arbitrager), show each is convex, and replace it by its KKT conditions; add market clearing and an endogenous NOx price (pp. 6-12). The leader maximizes profit over output and the number of allowances withheld nW (21), subject to the followers' equilibrium (p. 13; Table 4, p. 12). Solve it as an NLP. When that fails, use a two-phase scheme: PATH solves a square NCP, with the leader recast as a Cournot follower and nW = 0, to get an MPEC-feasible point; then NLP solvers start from there (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Followers' problems are convex: linear for price takers, concave quadratic for Cournot players when P⁰, Q⁰, B > 0 (Lemma 3.1, p. 9); the ISO's problem has a linear objective and convex constraints for nonnegative loss coefficients (Lemma 3.2, p. 10); the arbitrager's is an LP (Lemma 3.3, p. 11). Modelling assumptions flagged by the authors as likely to overstate market power: NOx trading only inside PJM (p. 6), no vertical integration or forward contracts, fixed imports, and no allowance imports or exports (pp. 19-20). The model is "developed from the bottom up" from engineering data: 14 nodes, 18 arcs, 5 periods (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Theorem 3.4: at any feasible point of the MPEC, each follower's response is globally optimal for its own problem, because convexity makes the KKT conditions sufficient (p. 13). The degeneracy of the assembled Nash-Cournot system ((7), (17), (18) imply (14)) is removed by dropping (14) and adding a hub-price equation (20), which gives a square system (p. 12). No convergence analysis of the solvers.
  - Transferable: before replacing lower-level problems by KKT conditions, prove a convexity lemma for each player type. One theorem then certifies that every MPEC-feasible point has globally optimal followers.
  - Transferable: after assembling many players' KKT systems, look for implied equations and replace one with a pinning condition until the system is square (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: One AMPL model of about 20,000 variables and 10,000 constraints, "highly nonlinear, and is relatively unstructured", released publicly (p. 13). Solver record: SNOPT and FILTER on the NLP form converge to a local minimum of the constraint violation (p. 14); even from the PATH feasible point no single NLP solver succeeds; the model is "eventually solved" by SNOPT at fixed withholding and then FILTER with variable nW (p. 14). PATH: 13.2 s, 25 major iterations (p. 15). Table 6 gives CPU time, major/minor iterations and objective values for SNOPT and FILTER for two leaders (p. 16), with the cost difference explained by linear algebra (dense reduced Hessian; FILTER's "less efficient linear algebra package", p. 16). Run times are "intended only to illustrate the level of difficulty of this model" (p. 15). Four economic scenarios (perfect competition, Nash-Cournot, Stackelberg with PECO or PSEG leading) are compared in Tables 7-11 (pp. 16-20). The leader PECO withholds 5,536 tons (7% of allowances) and gains $75.8M (pp. 17-18); PSEG as leader withholds nothing (pp. 18-19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Positions itself against simple-model analyses (Sartzetakis) and the Chen-Hobbs heuristic (p. 3), and against oligopoly models with exogenous allowance prices and without quadratic losses (p. 4). On the optimization side it cites Anitescu's and Fletcher et al.'s convergence results as the reason to try SQP (p. 14). Peers are described, not criticized.
- **D7 Writing**: The paper is written for two audiences: an economics framing (market power, HHI, cap-and-trade, pp. 2-6) and a methods section that reports the negative solver results plainly ("This negative result motivated a two-phase solution methodology", p. 14). Claims about the solution are hedged by what the numbers show: multipliers of 3.2×10⁹ and 2.9×10⁹ mean the point is "probably not strongly stationary" and "likely B-stationary", and checking this "is not practical given the size of the problem" (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an application and a solution recipe: a large, unstructured MPEC solved by an NCP-then-NLP pipeline, plus economic findings (withholding pays only for a leader long in allowances, pp. 19-20). Limits are concrete: three named assumptions that probably overestimate market power (pp. 19-20); the stationarity type of the computed solution is unverified (p. 16). Future direction: a multiyear banking model with capacity expansion leads to "a bilevel mixed integer nonlinear program whose solution is currently beyond any optimization solvers and for which no theoretical foundation exists" (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the solver failures are read (convergence to a local minimizer of infeasibility, p. 14), and the fixes are on the formulation and starting-point side: a feasibility phase from a square NCP, and grouping complementarity constraints by equation rather than lumping them into one x1ᵀx2 ≤ 0, which "improves the scaling of the model because unbounded multipliers affect fewer variables and constraints" (p. 14). The multiplier magnitudes are read as evidence about the stationarity type (p. 16).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the home toolkit (MPECs as NLPs, filterSQP) is carried into an energy-economics Stackelberg game whose received verdict was "difficult" (pp. 3-4, 14).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (weak): timings are given only "to illustrate the level of difficulty" (p. 15), solver cost differences are explained rather than ranked (p. 16), and the modelling assumptions that bias the economic conclusion are listed with their direction (pp. 19-20).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the complete AMPL model is put on a public URL (Munson's page, p. 13), but as one released application model, not as part of a maintained, corrected library.
- new-pattern candidate: **Feasibility phase by freezing the leader**. Recast the leader as one more equilibrium player with its extra decision fixed (nW = 0), solve the resulting square complementarity problem with a Newton-type NCP solver, and start the MPEC solve from that point; then chain NLP solvers (one at fixed withholding, another with withholding free) (p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a bilevel or Stackelberg model, prove a convexity lemma per follower type and conclude that every feasible point of the single-level MPEC has globally optimal followers (Lemmas 3.1-3.3, Theorem 3.4, pp. 9-13).
- When NLP solvers cannot find a feasible point of an MPEC, compute one from the square equilibrium problem obtained by freezing the leader's extra variables, then warm-start the MPEC (p. 14).
- Reformulate complementarity in groups (one product constraint per block of equations) rather than one aggregated x1ᵀx2 ≤ 0, so that unbounded multipliers touch fewer constraints (p. 14).
- Report the largest multiplier at the computed solution and say what it implies about the stationarity type (p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "No single nonlinear programming solver could solve the Stackelberg game even from the feasible starting point provided by the feasibility phase." (p. 14)
- "These large values indicate that the computed solution is probably not strongly stationary because the multipliers do not appear to be bounded." (p. 16)

### S019 · A brief history of filter methods (Preprint ANL/MCS-P1372-0906, Argonne National Laboratory 2006; published per homepage in SIAG/Optimization Views-and-News 18(1):2-12, 2007; no DOI)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-17: cover, contents, all four sections, references).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Roger Fletcher (Dundee) and Philippe Toint (Namur) (p. 3) · **Place in trajectory**: a consolidation piece ten years after Fletcher's 1996 plenary talk that introduced NLP filter methods (p. 7). It reviews the Fletcher-Leyffer-Toint filter line and its spread to other areas. SKILL.md Workflow F step 5 ("Consolidate a grown line: history, survey, shared software") is the matching habit.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Global convergence of Newton-based NLP methods from far-away starting points (p. 3). The entry point is a criticism of penalty functions: the right penalty parameter depends on the unknown optimal multipliers, and a too-large one forces monotone methods to hug the constraint manifold and take short steps (p. 4). Against this the authors set an empirical observation: the unmodified SQP method already solves many test problems, which "motivates us to find a way of inducing global convergence, which would allow the full Newton step to be taken much more often" (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: View the NLP as a biobjective problem in (h(x), f(x)), with feasibility more important, and accept a trial point if no filter entry dominates it (p. 4). Three refinements make it convergent: a sloping envelope (2.2) due to Chin, a switching condition (2.3) that requires sufficient reduction when the model predicts objective decrease (f-type versus h-type steps), and a feasibility-restoration phase when the trust-region QP becomes inconsistent (pp. 5-7; Algorithm 1, p. 6). For fast local convergence the authors prefer second-order correction steps because they keep f(x), not the Lagrangian, in the filter and need no multiplier function (p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Smooth f and c (p. 3); for the convergence outline: iterates in a compact set, C² functions, bounded Hessian approximations (p. 7). The authors call one assumption in their own SQP proof [10] "undesirable": the need for a global solution of the QP subproblem, hard to ensure unless H_k is positive semidefinite (p. 8). They note that the later trust-region SQP filter of Fletcher et al. [6] removes it (p. 9). For Ulbrich et al.'s interior filter they flag "the strong assumption that the inverse of the Jacobian is bounded" (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Convergence outline (p. 8): the filter forces all limit points to be feasible; near a feasible nonstationary point an f-type step must be generated, which is ensured by resetting the trust-region radius to at least ρ̄ at the start of each iteration; as ρ shrinks a step can only change from f-type to h-type because Δq is monotone in ρ under the global-QP assumption. The three outcomes (restoration fails, termination at a KKT point, or a feasible accumulation point that is stationary or where MFCQ fails) are called "as strong as can be expected for general NLPs" (pp. 7-8). The convergence analysis showed that features of the original method (NW/SE corner rule, unblocking) were redundant (p. 7). Other devices reviewed: Ulbrich's Lagrangian-based filter with tightened switching (pp. 8-9), Wächter-Biegler's line-search filter with SOC and a restoration trigger derived from a step-size lower bound (pp. 9, 12), the area-based nonmonotone filter (p. 10), and multidimensional filters with β ∈ (0, 1/√p) so that an acceptable point always exists (p. 13).
  - Transferable: after proving convergence, go back to the algorithm and remove every feature the proof did not need (here the NW/SE corner rule and unblocking, p. 7).
  - Transferable: in a survey of your own line, name the weakest assumption of your own theorem and point to the later work that removed it (global QP solution, pp. 8-9).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The survey reports no new numerics; it mentions LOQO's encouraging practical performance with a single-entry filter (p. 12) and the earlier observation that unmodified SQP solves many test problems (p. 4).
- **D6 Positioning**: The filter is presented as an alternative to penalty functions, not a refinement of them (pp. 3-4, 15). The authors state that NLP filter methods were developed independently of earlier similar ideas and credit Surry et al.'s multiobjective genetic algorithm and Lemaréchal-Nemirovskii-Nesterov's bundle exclusion region (p. 7). Competing filter variants (Ulbrich; Wächter-Biegler; Gonzaga-Karas-Vanti; Ribeiro et al.; Gould-Toint) are described with their trade-offs, e.g. the step acceptance in Karas et al. "seems slightly more restrictive" (p. 10), and Ulbrich et al.'s interior filter "may be more likely to converge to stationary points that are not local minimizers" (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing**: Short, first-person-plural history with a stated goal sentence ("the development of global optimization safeguards that interfere as little as possible with Newton's method", p. 4). Self-correction is told as a story: the conjecture that filters avoid the Maratos effect, its confirmation on Maratos's original example, then a counterexample that "shattered the hope" and led to SOC steps (p. 8). Uncertainty is marked about others' methods too: on LOQO's single-entry filter, "We are not sure that this device alone can guarantee convergence" (p. 12). The conclusion is a two-sentence forecast that filters "will continue to grow" (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is consolidation: one coherent account of SQP and interior filters, their convergence theory, and extensions to nonlinear equations, nonsmooth and derivative-free optimization, and augmented Lagrangians (pp. 12-15). It also shows Leyffer's own extensions of the filter (feasibility problems and multidimensional filters with Gould and Toint, bundle filter, gradient-projection AL filter with Friedlander, pp. 12-15). Limits are stated per method, not for the survey as a whole. No explicit future-work list beyond the forecast (p. 15).

**Method links**:

**Transferable techniques**:
- State the goal of a safeguard as how little it interferes with the base method, and measure success by how often the full step is accepted (p. 4).
- When you introduce an acceptance test with several pieces (envelope, switching condition, restoration), give each piece the failure it prevents: infeasible limit points, convergence to arbitrary feasible points, inconsistent QPs (pp. 5-6).
- Classify steps by what the model predicts (f-type if Δq > 0, h-type otherwise) and never add points with h = 0 to the filter; this guarantees that restoration can always find an acceptable point (p. 7).
- In a multidimensional filter, choose the envelope constant β < 1/√p so that at least one component can always improve and an acceptable point always exists (p. 13).
