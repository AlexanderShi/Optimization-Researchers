- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The report was written for the 2025 renewal of the CNRS research network GDR ROD; the project started at a scientific-council meeting on 26 June 2023 (p. 3), and the document addresses the community, its CNRS institute and neighbouring disciplines (p. 5). §B.2.2 enters the solver question empirically: a questionnaire sent on 29 April 2025 to solver designers and developers, with an eligibility gate (licensed code or executable, packages for at least one of the three major architectures, a lasting website or repository, documentation and tutorials, a development process with continuous integration and tests) and six questions: what your solver is and its type; its current limits, qualitative and quantitative; challenges specific to it; challenges common to several solvers (genericity vs efficiency, scaling, uncertainty); hybridization (machine learning, several paradigms); free remarks (pp. 31-32).
  - Transferable: gate a community survey on software-quality criteria so that the answers come from maintained tools, and fix the question order so the synthesis can follow it (pp. 31-32).
- **D2 Core idea**: A synthesis in question order (p. 32). Definitions: a solver implements an algorithm for a family of problems; abstraction between formulation and solution lets the user swap implementations or even paradigms; modelling language vs solver vs meta-solver that orchestrates several solvers (p. 32). Limits: scale (interior-point methods limited on sparse LPs above one million variables; beyond ten million variables optimization seems out of reach) (p. 33); numerics (for NLP solvers degeneracy causes instability with redundant constraints or nonsmooth functions such as max(x, y); CP handles continuous variables poorly; precision problems from three objectives on) (p. 33); modelling expertise, since one problem has formulations of different solve times (tight vs loose MILP, adequate global constraints in CP) (p. 33); openness of a solver to external components (cut generators, primal heuristics, new constraints) vs performance, which can collapse because structural properties such as dominance rules can no longer be exploited (p. 33); limited parallel gains and the GPU challenge (pp. 33, 35); weak support for uncertainty and multiple objectives; solver development poorly credited in researchers' careers and short of research engineers (p. 35). Remedies: composability and hybridization; prior model analysis to recognise structure and select algorithms, then dynamic time allocation (p. 35); machine learning: very generic deep-learning approaches rarely bring gains, whereas simple interpretable statistical models (a probabilistic model predicting branch-and-bound tree size for variable selection, a classifier for cut selection in SCIP) have succeeded; libraries MathOptAI, Gurobi Machine Learning, PySCIPOpt-ML, OMLT, Neuromancer (p. 35); combining solvers sequentially or by parts (JuMP, discrete-optimization), compact set-based formulations, SIMD grouping on GPUs, for NLP better sparse linear algebra and complementarity support to make degeneracy explicit, for CP a dedicated linear-arithmetic module (p. 36).
  - Transferable: when diagnosing a solver weakness, separate the four causes the section separates: scale, numerical degeneracy, formulation quality, and structure lost when the solver is opened to user components (p. 33).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Evidence model of §B.2.2: self-selected respondents who pass the eligibility gate (p. 31); 16 tools in Table 1: Gurobi, Hexaly, Xpress, vOptSolver, SCIP, MadNLP, Uno, ToulBar2, ACE, CP-SAT, Choco-solver, Optal-CP, tempo, marmote, JuMP, discrete-optimization (p. 34). Two are NLP-only (MadNLP, Uno; both MIT-licensed); MINLP capability is listed for Gurobi, Hexaly, Xpress and SCIP (p. 34). Question II asked for qualitative and quantitative elements (p. 32), but the synthesis gives only the two size thresholds (p. 33). Answers are not attributed to named solvers; one limit is reported as the view of some developers (pp. 33-34).

- **D6 Positioning**: §B.2.2 cross-references nonlinear programming (§B.1.1), constraint programming (§B.1.4) and metaheuristics (§B.2.1) (p. 32) and cites software URLs only, no papers (pp. 34-35). It is sceptical of generic deep learning inside solvers and positive about narrow interpretable models (p. 35). Context, not Leyffer's voice: §B.1.1 (written by Bendali, Clautiaux, Cafieri, D'Ambrosio, Fouilhoux, Grappe, Lambert, Ljubic, Messine, Pacaud, Meunier, Roupin; p. 9) recommends calling a nonlinear solver directly instead of linearizing very nonlinear problems (p. 12) and promotes complementarity and vanishing constraints, increasingly supported by default, instead of integer variables for max/min/if structure (p. 13). This is close to Leyffer's MPEC-as-NLP line (SKILL.md anatomy 4) and in tension with his slide slogan "Linearize, linearize, linearize" (SKILL.md Method 6).

- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution level: community agenda, not theory, algorithm or software. The section states no limits of its own; visible ones are a self-selected sample, no per-solver attribution, almost no quantitative data and no owner for individual claims, so Leyffer's personal positions cannot be separated (pp. 31-36). Directions listed: composability, structure detection, interpretable ML inside solvers, GPU SIMD, NLP sparse linear algebra and complementarity support, CP linear arithmetic (pp. 35-36); plus a career point: solver development is under-credited and needs research engineers (p. 35).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (collective voice): openness to external components is framed as a trade-off with performance, which can collapse when structure is lost (p. 33); the remedies ask for easier integration of specific components into generic solvers and fluent composition across solvers (pp. 35-36). This matches Method 1's building-block aim and its stated limitation ("Owning the tree can cost speed"); Leyffer's share cannot be separated.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (collective voice): formulations of the same problem differ in solve time; NLP degeneracy from redundant constraints or nonsmooth max(x, y) makes solvers unstable (p. 33).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (weak, collective voice): complementarity support in NLP solvers named as a remedy for degeneracy (p. 36), with Uno among the surveyed NLP solvers (p. 34); consistent with carrying the MPEC/complementarity toolkit into solver design.
- new-pattern candidate: solver-developer survey as a community-roadmap instrument (pp. 31-32). Single, collective occurrence; far from the three-paper threshold.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Gate a field survey on software-quality criteria (license, packages on a major architecture, lasting repository, documentation and tutorials, CI and tests) and write the synthesis in question order (pp. 31-32).
- Before proposing a fix for a failing solver, classify the failure as scale, numerical degeneracy, formulation quality, or structure lost through user extensions (p. 33).
- When putting machine learning inside a solver, target one narrow, interpretable decision (tree-size prediction for variable selection; a cut-selection classifier) rather than end-to-end deep learning (p. 35).
