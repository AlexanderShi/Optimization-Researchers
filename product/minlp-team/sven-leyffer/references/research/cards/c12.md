### H004 · Nonlinear Optimization and Beyond (INFORMS Optimization Society Newsletter 7(2):3-11 2017, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. I read all of Leyffer's article (pp. 3–9) and its reference list (pp. 9–11). I skimmed the surrounding newsletter items (Chair's column, Ben-Tal's "My Life as an Optimizer", notices) only to confirm they are not his.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (single author) · **Position in trajectory**: written in 2017 for the 2016 Farkas Prize. It is his own retrospective on three lines: filter methods, MINLP and MPECs.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The article is framed as a tribute and "a personal view of three topics in nonlinear optimization" (p. 3). MINLP and MPECs are presented as "two extensions of nonlinear optimization" of the filter/NLP base (p. 3). Each line starts from a dissatisfaction with the received tool:
  - Classical penalty parameters depend on the solution (π > ‖y*‖_D). Too large a value forces "much shortened Newton steps and slow convergence" (p. 3).
  - Fletcher "had been unhappy with the performance of classical penalty functions" (p. 3).
  - MPECs "had been assumed that these problems were too difficult for standard nonlinear solvers", and solving them via NLPs "has been commonly regarded as numerically unsafe" (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Filter: borrow domination from multiobjective optimization and accept steps not dominated in (h, f) (p. 4). Convergence then needs three refinements: an envelope (1.2), a sufficient-reduction switching condition (1.3), and feasibility restoration (p. 4).
  - MINLP: generalized OA [16]. FilMINT implements Quesada–Grossmann, interleaving NLP solves with a MINTO tree search, with cut management and cuts at non-integer points (pp. 5–6).
  - Algorithms are described through two shared building blocks, "relaxation and constraint enforcement" (p. 6). Minotaur is presented as a framework "agnostic of problem type or solvers" (p. 6).
  - MPEC: write general complementarity with slacks in the simple form (3.7) and solve the NLP (3.9) with SQP (p. 7). Where that fails (B-stationary but not strongly stationary), SLPEC with a three-dimensional filter is used (p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - NLP: f and c smooth (p. 3).
  - MINLP (2.4): f and c twice continuously differentiable and convex, X a bounded polyhedron (p. 5).
  - MPEC Theorem 2 (p. 8) assumes twice Lipschitz continuously differentiable f and c, a strongly stationary x* with MPEC-LICQ, strict complementarity for the MPEC multipliers, and that "our QP solver always chooses a linearly independent basis". The last is an assumption on the implementation, not on the problem.
  - He notes that slack reformulation preserves CQs and second-order conditions: "nothing is lost by introducing slack variables" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices** (summarised, not proved, in a newsletter):
  - Filter Lemma 1 of [8]: infinitely many filter entries plus f bounded below imply a feasible limit (p. 4).
  - The MPEC-SQP quadratic-convergence proof is split into two cases (p. 8). If x1ᵀx2 = 0 at some iterate, the SQP subproblem equals that of a well-behaved relaxed NLP (3.11), giving superlinear convergence. If x1ᵀx2 > 0, "each QP basis remains bounded away from singularity".
  - Stationarity concepts are defined through the family of relaxed NLPs (3.11) indexed by the set pairs (X1, X2) (pp. 7–8).
  - Counterexamples carry much of the argument:
    - the Gould–Toint Maratos example for filters (p. 5);
    - the one-dimensional OA example that visits all integer points, and Hijazi et al.'s exponential example (p. 5);
    - the SQPEC example min (x−1)²+y³+y², which converges quadratically to a spurious point (p. 9);
    - examples showing nonstationary or linear convergence to spurious points without slacks or with x1ᵀx2 = 0 (p. 9).
  - Transferable: for a degenerate constraint, split the local analysis on whether the degenerate product is exactly zero at the iterate. Reduce one case to a regular problem; show basis nonsingularity in the other.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none in this article. He only reports others' and his own earlier findings: 50–70% failures on some bilevel problems in early experience [3], and "our initial numerical experience with filterSQP was very positive" (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Credit and correction are explicit. The SLP filter proof by Fletcher and Toint is "the most easily understood proof", and Gould and Wächter "corrected many errors in the early proof" (p. 4).
  - "Later, Bonami et al. corrected our convergence proof [4], and Pierre developed a much nicer example" (p. 5). On the filter's Maratos immunity: "Originally, we had believed" it, until Gould and Toint's example (p. 5).
  - The filter is placed beside Gould–Toint funnels and Byrd–Nocedal–Waltz steering penalties, with no claim of superiority (p. 5).
  - QG is called "arguably the best approach to solving MINLPs" (p. 5). Hybrid single/multi-tree methods are "the most efficient class of methods for convex MINLP" (p. 6).
  - Observation: the text names "Chungen Chen" for the nonmonotone filter while reference [38] is C. Shen (p. 5, p. 11). I did not resolve this.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Each topic follows the same template (pp. 3–9): history with named people, then mechanism, then a caveat example, then a "Conclusions and Outlook" paragraph.
  - The key modelling point gets its own run-in heading, "The Importance of Being Slack" (p. 8).
  - Opinions are flagged as opinions ("At this point, I do not believe…", p. 5; "In my opinion…", p. 9).
  - Theorems are stated informally with numbered assumptions and a two-sentence proof sketch (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is synthesis and testimony, not new results. Limits are stated concretely:
  - NLP solvers fail or converge slowly on MPECs whose stationary points are B- but not strongly stationary (p. 9).
  - "Software and solvers for MPECs are far less mature than NLP or MINLP" (p. 9).
  - Future directions: MISOCP and PDE-constrained MINLP "necessitate novel approaches" (p. 7); better MPEC solvers must "build on our existing NLP solver infrastructure" (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): MINLP and MPEC are presented as extensions of the NLP base (p. 3). The MPEC outlook says to build on existing NLP infrastructure (p. 9).
- Method 3 ✅ (evidence): the argument turns on counterexamples, including others' examples against his own methods (Maratos for filters p. 5; OA worst cases p. 5; SQPEC p. 9).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): "The Importance of Being Slack". The slack form preserves CQs yet is necessary for SQP convergence, shown by two failure examples (pp. 7–9).
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): the building-blocks view of MINLP algorithms and Minotaur's solver-agnostic framework goal (p. 6).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence, partial): filter vs. modern penalty is "really a question of personal preference" (p. 5), which declines to name a winner. Variant: the uncalibrated "arguably the best approach" for QG (p. 5).
- Heuristic 9 is also supported: correctors are named for both the filter and the OA proof (pp. 4–5).
- New-pattern candidate: maturity-gap framing (p. 9; see also S021).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give the decisive modelling detail its own heading and back it with two minimal failure examples (pp. 8–9).
- Compare a family of MINLP algorithms by the two building blocks they share, relaxation and constraint enforcement, before discussing any single method (p. 6).
- List implementation assumptions (such as "QP solver chooses a linearly independent basis") among a theorem's hypotheses, not in a footnote (p. 8).
- End each topic with a short "Conclusions and Outlook" paragraph that names the maturity gap and the infrastructure to build on (pp. 7, 9).

**Quotes**:
- "At this point, I do not believe that there exists a big difference between filter methods and modern penalty function methods, and the choice of method is really a question of personal preference." (p. 5)
- "In my opinion, reliable NLP solvers are still the best approach for solving MPECs despite the potential problems with slow convergence or convergence to spurious stationary points." (p. 9)

### S021 · A survey of nonlinear robust optimization (INFOR: Information Systems and Operational Research 2020, doi:10.1080/03155986.2020.1730676)
**Read level**: full, of the ANL preprint version "Nonlinear Robust Optimization", ANL/MCS-P9040-0218 (Jan 2018; cite [S021 TR pp.]). I read pp. 1–23 in full and skimmed the references (pp. 23–27). The INFOR 2020 typeset version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Matt Menickelly, Todd Munson, Charlie Vanaret, Stefan M. Wild (all ANL MCS) · **Position in trajectory**: a survey that enters robust optimization from the NLP/MPEC side, written around the time of the Farkas-prize retrospective.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Existing reformulation methods "assume linearity of the robust constraints or uncertainty sets of favorable shape", which covers only a fraction of applications (abstract, p. 3). Prior surveys deal "mainly with linear and conic cases" (p. 3). The survey targets the NRO problem (1.1) with possibly decision-dependent sets U(x), a semi-infinite NLP (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Organize NRO around solution approaches (§4):
  - bilevel formulation turned into an MPEC via lower-level KKT (4.15) (pp. 12–13);
  - duality-based "tractable" reformulations, unified through a Wolfe-dual single-level problem (4.18) that recovers the linear robust counterpart (pp. 13–15);
  - Polak-type outer approximation by sampling (Algorithm 1) (pp. 17–18);
  - methods for implementation errors (pp. 18–19).

  New in the survey (p. 7):
  - a linearized-subproblem characterization of stationarity (Theorem 2.2): x* is stationary iff dx = 0 solves the linearized robust problem (2.4);
  - an explicit statement that (4.18) is a relaxation of (4.15) (pp. 14–15).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - All functions smooth; U(x) nonempty and compact (p. 4).
  - Single robust constraint (q = 1) "for ease of presentation" (p. 4).
  - The illustrative examples satisfy C1–C3: convex X and f; convex compact U(x); c convex in x and concave in u (p. 11).
  - The MPEC and dual routes need a lower-level CQ and convex lower level (pp. 12, 14).
  - OA needs only continuity and differentiability (O1–O2) and compact U (O3), with no convexity (p. 17). Convergence then needs global maximization over U_k or asymptotic density U_k → U (p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - Fritz-John conditions for semi-infinite programs with a finite active subset (Theorem 2.1, cited, p. 5).
  - Proof of Theorem 2.2 by comparing the stationarity systems (2.5) and (2.3) (p. 7), with the remark that CQs are "implicitly assumed in the stationarity of x*" (p. 7).
  - Information-from-infeasibility statements. A global solution of (4.15) solves (1.1) and other feasible points give upper bounds. Global infeasibility implies (1.1) is infeasible; "local infeasibility yields no information" (p. 13). For (4.18), a conclusion needs no duality gap (p. 14).
  - Small counterexamples:
    - a nominal problem that is unbounded while the robust problem is bounded ("robustness can immunize", p. 5, Fig. 1 p. 6);
    - Fig. 2c, where a robust local minimum is "practically dissatisfying" because a whole neighbourhood qualifies (p. 8);
    - Example 6.1 on u-dependent underestimators (p. 21);
    - the convex-hull trick that fails once linearity in u is relaxed to concavity (p. 22).
  - Decision-dependent sets yield a bilinear λᵀEx term, so the dual reformulation is no longer polyhedral (p. 17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none. Instead there are two running examples:
  - Example 3.1, a 2-D problem with u ∈ [1/4, 2], reformulated as an MPEC in Ex. 4.1 (p. 13), as a Wolfe dual in Ex. 4.2 (p. 15), and coded in SIPAMPL and YALMIP (Listings 1–2, pp. 19–20);
  - Example 3.2, a robust 3-SAT instance with decision-dependent U(x) (pp. 11–12).

  Software is compared in a feature table (Table 2, p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Builds on Ben-Tal–Nemirovski tractable reformulations (p. 13) and Ben-Tal–den Hertog–Vial conjugates (p. 14), restating them as a table (Table 1, p. 16).
  - Takes the bilevel route from Stein's semi-infinite work (p. 12).
  - Cites the authors' own MPEC papers [35, 36, 51–53] for the complementarity reformulation (p. 13).
  - Notes that reformulations "may be computationally more expensive than other approaches [18]" (p. 16).
  - Does not go deep into DRO: "We will not discuss either of these strategies in any further detail" (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Novelty claims are hedged ("To the best of our knowledge, the stationarity condition of Theorem 2.2 is new", p. 7) and immediately qualified: (2.4) "is not necessarily easier to solve than (1.1)" (p. 7).
  - Convexity cases are tabulated (Table 3, p. 21).
  - The conclusion diagnoses the field's maturity "through the lens of reformulation" (p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a survey plus a new stationarity characterization. Limits:
  - single-constraint presentation (p. 4);
  - DRO treated only briefly (pp. 8–10);
  - no modelling language supports generalized semi-infinite problems with decision-dependent sets (p. 20).

  Future directions: two-stage/recourse, robust MPC, integer variables (p. 22). He expects NRO to become more tractable as nonconvex methods improve, "via solutions to MPEC reformulations of bilevel problems or applications of outer approximation methods" (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): enters robust optimization by recasting the robust constraint as a bilevel problem and then an MPEC, the authors' home class, citing their own MPEC-as-NLP work. NLP solvers "can compute local solutions" despite failed CQs (pp. 12–13).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): tractability is read off the reformulation (Tables 1 and 3). A decision-dependent set is diagnosed as the source of nonconvexity (bilinear term, p. 17), and maturity is judged "through the lens of reformulation" (p. 22).
- Method 3 ✅ (evidence): minimal examples mark the limits of concepts (unbounded nominal vs. bounded robust, p. 5; dissatisfying robust local minima, p. 8; the convex-hull trick failing under concavity, p. 22).
- Taste 3 is also supported: the paper asks what a robust local minimum means for nonconvex f (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidates:
  - a running example threaded through theory, reformulations and software listings (Ex. 3.1 on pp. 11, 13, 15, 19–20);
  - maturity-gap framing (p. 22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- State stationarity for a hard problem class as "d = 0 solves a linearized subproblem" (Theorem 2.2) and say at once whether that subproblem is any easier (p. 7).
- For each reformulation, state what a global solution, a feasible point, a global infeasibility and a local infeasibility certify (pp. 13–14).
- Carry one small example through every section and code it in each surveyed tool (pp. 11–20).
- Tabulate how tractability degrades as each convexity assumption is dropped (Table 3, p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "To the best of our knowledge, the stationarity condition of Theorem 2.2 is new." (p. 7)
- "This maturity level is not surprising when viewed through the lens of reformulation." (p. 22)

### S032 · Minotaur: A mixed-integer nonlinear optimization toolkit (Mathematical Programming Computation 2021, doi:10.1007/s12532-020-00196-1)
**Read level**: full, of the ANL preprint ANL/MCS-P8010-0817 (Oct 2017; cite [S032 TR pp.]). I read pp. 1–28 in full, including the code figures and Table 6 (p. 29), and skimmed the references (pp. 28–31). The MPC 2021 published version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ashutosh Mahajan (first author), Jeff Linderoth, James Luedtke, Todd Munson · **Position in trajectory**: the framework paper for Minotaur, the software that grew out of FilMINT and MINLP-BB.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**:
  - Existing convex-MINLP solvers "are not designed to exploit the structure of nonlinear functions" (p. 4).
  - Algorithmic advances "have often exploited special problem structure". To use them, a solver must "evaluate, examine, and possibly modify the nonlinear functions" and solve several relaxation types (LP, QP, NLP, MILP) (p. 5).
  - Diversity of classes is shown as a problem-class tree (Fig. 1, p. 4).
  - The paper argues that "nonconvex forms are more challenging and diverse than integer variables" (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: A relaxation-based tree search (Algorithms 1–2, pp. 6–7) whose steps are base classes (Table 2, p. 12):
  - Brancher, Engine, NodeRelaxer, NodeProcessor, CutManager.
  - Structure lives in Handler objects, inspired by SCIP constraint handlers (p. 12), with functions relax, presolve, isFeasible, separate and branch (Table 3, p. 13).
  - A Transformer rewrites DAGs (CGraph) into simple constraint forms and reuses common subexpressions by hashing (p. 14).
  - Engines wrap filterSQP, IPOPT, BQPD, qpOASES and CLP, with warm starts (p. 15).
  - Standard solvers are rebuilt in a few lines: simple-bnb, simple-qg and simple-glob (Figs. 6–8, pp. 17–19).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - MINLP (1.1) with C² functions and a bounded polyhedral X (pp. 3–4).
  - Decidability is restricted by assuming X compact or f and c convex (p. 4).
  - "Convex" refers to the region of the nonlinear constraints excluding integrality (p. 4).
  - The QuadHandler requires finite bounds, obtained by presolve (pp. 13–14, 18).
- **D4 Analysis and devices** (no theorems; proofs n/a):
  - McCormick inequalities and secant/tangent cuts for x² and bilinear terms (p. 14).
  - Feasibility-based bound tightening by forward and reverse DAG traversal (pp. 10, 23).
  - AD: reverse-mode gradients, with Hessian columns by forward-over-reverse (p. 10).
  - Big-M coefficient tightening: if x0 = 0 forces xi = 0, then M0 can be replaced by c(0, …, 0), or x0 fixed to 1. This is "the tightest possible" (p. 23).
  - Exact linearization of products involving a binary variable (p. 24).
  - Automatic detection of perspective structure (Algorithm 3, p. 27) with the ε-approximate perspective (5.7) of Furman–Grossmann–Sawaya, which avoids the undefined gradient at z = 0 and CQ failure (p. 26).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Setup: one core, 1-hour limit, compilers and flags stated (pp. 18–19).
  - AD isolated from other MINLP routines with the LexicoBrancher on 100 NLPs (p. 19; Table 4, p. 20). There is no significant difference on about 80% of instances; CGraph is up to 8× slower on dense-Hessian QCQPs, and "CGraph never failed" (p. 20).
  - Convex MINLP: 333 MINLPLib-2 instances with integers. The comparison is against Bonmin with the same IPOPT, using extended performance profiles (p. 20; Figs. 9–10, p. 21). simple-bnb solves about 73%, like Bonmin; "On 10% of instances Bonmin is faster" (p. 20).
  - Global: 266 QCQPs against BARON-15.2, Couenne-0.5.6 and SCIP-3.2.0 at defaults. simple-glob solves over 50% versus about 70% for the others, which use heuristics and cuts that simple-glob lacks (p. 20; Fig. 11, p. 22).
  - Presolve: syn20M04M drops from >3600 s to 0.49 s for simple-qg (Table 5, p. 23). On 96 RSyn/Syn instances robustness rises by about 20% (pp. 23–24). On the convex set, presolve solves about 10% more instances for simple-bnb and 5% more for simple-qg (p. 24).
  - Perspective: six more instances solved. Nodes fall but time per NLP rises, reported per instance (pp. 26–27; Table 6, p. 29).
- **D6 Positioning**: Lists existing convex and nonconvex solvers and what they cannot do (pp. 4–5). Credits the SCIP constraint-handler idea (p. 12) and the Furman et al. perspective approximation (p. 26). Positions the paper as infrastructure ("not to develop the fastest possible solver", p. 16) rather than as a solver race.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - One running example, x1(x1+x2)+(x1+x2)², appears as a DAG (Fig. 2, p. 5), as CGraph code (Fig. 3, p. 10) and as a Transformer output (p. 14).
  - Code excerpts serve as evidence of how small an implementation is (Figs. 6–8).
  - The central claim appears at the end of the introduction: "we do not observe decreased performance for increased generality" (p. 6). It is repeated in the conclusion, "This flexibility does not come at the cost of speed and efficiency" (p. 28).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Software and an architecture, with two structure-exploiting extensions. Limits are stated:
  - only an AMPL interface exists (p. 15);
  - CGraph is slower on dense QCQPs (p. 20);
  - simple-glob lacks heuristics, convexity detection and cuts (p. 20);
  - perspective detection runs only in the initial presolve (p. 26);
  - the general tightest-M problem is nonconvex (p. 23).

  The source is on GitHub, opening "the door for future research" (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence, strong):
  - The framework decomposes MINLP algorithms into swappable components (Tables 2–3, pp. 12–13).
  - Solver-agnostic vision (p. 5).
  - Warm-started engines with callbacks for filterSQP and IPOPT (p. 15).
  - Validation by imitation: rebuilt NLPBB and QG are compared with Bonmin's versions of the same algorithms (pp. 20–21).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence, strong): before changing the algorithm, "Analysis of these models reveals" weak big-M constraints. A coefficient fix takes syn20M04M from unsolved to 0.49 s (pp. 22–23). The ε-perspective is chosen to keep CQs and gradients defined (p. 26).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence):
  - same NLP solver as the baseline (IPOPT) (p. 20);
  - competitor versions stated (p. 20);
  - losses reported (Bonmin faster on 10%; simple-glob 50% vs. 70%) (p. 20);
  - a cost that grows reported alongside a gain (time per NLP, Table 6, p. 29);
  - extended performance profiles (p. 20).

  Variant: the competitors are 2015-era versions in a paper published 2021, consistent with SKILL [03 E55].
- Heuristic 1 is also supported: AD is isolated by switching off branching effects (LexicoBrancher), and presolve and perspective are added one at a time (pp. 19, 24–27).
- New-pattern candidate: a running example threaded through the DAG figure, code and transformer (pp. 5, 10, 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To benchmark one component (derivatives), build a degenerate driver that spends nearly all time in that component (lexicographic branching over 100 bound-changed NLPs) (p. 19).
- Rebuild a known algorithm on your framework and compare against the reference implementation using the same subsolver, to show that generality costs no speed (pp. 16, 20).
- Before tuning the solver on a hard MINLP, scan for big-M switch constraints and replace M0 by c(0, …, 0) or fix the switch (p. 23).
- When a tighter formulation cuts nodes but raises per-node cost, report nodes, time per subproblem and total time side by side (Table 6, p. 29).
- Implement a structure as a handler with relax, presolve, isFeasible, separate and branch hooks, so that one class carries everything the tree needs about that structure (Table 3, p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "Our goal here is not to develop the fastest possible solver, but rather to demonstrate that our flexible implementation does not introduce additional computational overhead." (p. 16)
- "In general, nonconvex forms are more challenging and diverse than integer variables, thus motivating a more tailored approach." (p. 5)

### S087 · Learning symbolic expressions: Mixed-integer formulations, cuts, and heuristics (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2023, doi:10.1287/ijoc.2022.0050; arXiv:2102.08351)
**Read level**: full, of arXiv:2102.08351v2 (cite [S087 arXiv pp.]). I read pp. 1–20, Appendix A (the Cozad–Sahinidis formulation, pp. 20–22) and Appendix B (the proof of Lemma 2.2, pp. 22–23). The IJOC 2023 version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jongeun Kim (first author; University of Minnesota), Prasanna Balaprakash (ANL) · **Position in trajectory**: an ML-adjacent application, regression recast as a nonconvex MINLP, within his base-and-extension move into machine-learning problems.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**:
  - Symbolic regression (1.1) learns the functional form as well as the constants. With a finite operator set it is a nonconvex MINLP (p. 1).
  - Genetic programming is unstable: "it might produce highly dissimilar solutions even for the same input data" (pp. 2–3).
  - Exact MINLP approaches restrict depth, and several were "tested only on noiseless data" (p. 3).
  - AI Feynman needs unit information and smoothness (p. 3).
  - The number of trees is more than (B·d)^(2^D)/B (p. 4).
  - The paper targets a setting not studied in combination: no units, 10 points, noise (p. 16).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Tighten the Cozad–Sahinidis formulation [4] with:
    - equality tree-defining constraints (2.3) (pp. 5–6);
    - merged big-M value-defining constraints (2.4) (p. 7);
    - association-property redundancy cuts and a merged constant cut (2.8) (p. 8);
    - data-driven implication cuts for domain-restricted operators (2.12)–(2.13) (p. 10);
    - symmetry breaking rewritten for full binary trees (2.14) (p. 11).
  - Add STreCH, a local-branching heuristic [8] (p. 12). It grows the tree by searching k-neighbours, with distance δ given by one linear constraint (3.1)–(3.2) (p. 13), plus early termination, start values and fixing the top of the tree (p. 14; Algorithms 3.1–3.3, pp. 14–15).
  - Motivation: a complex formula is approximated by a simpler one with similar structure (1−x+x² over 1+x vs. 1−x over 1+x; Kepler's law with M ≫ m) (p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Bounded continuous variables with v_lo ≤ c_lo ≤ 0 ≤ c_up ≤ v_up (p. 5).
  - N is a full binary tree (p. 5).
  - The operator set is finite. In the tests it is a subset of {+, −, ∗, /, √} and the depth is at most 5 (p. 16).
  - The formulation comparison limits depth to 2 (7 nodes) so that all instances solve within 3 h (p. 17).
  - Implication cuts are untested because all test variables are positive (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - Strict-tightening lemmas of a fixed shape: same integer-feasible set, strictly smaller continuous relaxation.
    - Lemma 2.1 (p. 6) shows strict inclusion with an explicit witness tree (Fig. 2b).
    - Lemma 2.2 (p. 7; proof App. B, pp. 22–23) merges k big-M constraints on the same function into one with Σ w_i y_i + M(1−Σ y_i). It shows equality on binaries and strict inclusion on the simplex, witnessed by y_i = 1/m.
    - Lemmas 2.3 and 2.4 (pp. 8–10) are proved by reduction to Lemma 2.2.
  - Example 2.5 is a one-data-point, two-node instance whose fractional point (0.9, 0.1, 0.9, 0, −0.9) is feasible without the implication cut and cut off by it (p. 11).
  - A counting bound on the number of expression trees appears in footnote 1 (p. 4).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Test set: 71 Feynman-database formulas with operators in {+, −, ∗, /, √} (Table 3, p. 16).
  - Setup: Julia 1.5.3 with SCIP 7.0.0; code public on GitHub (p. 16).
  - Formulations: eight variants (Imp/Coz × F/R/S/N, Table 4, p. 16), compared on best-of-four and on all eight (Fig. 8, p. 17) and on B&B nodes (Fig. 9, p. 18).
    - 70% vs. 50% solved within 10 minutes; failures 2/71 vs. 4/71 (p. 17).
    - Imp-F is first on 36/71 and in the top three on 58/71 (p. 17).
  - Heuristic comparison:
    - Protocol: noisy training data, noiseless validation and test data, selection by validation error (p. 17).
    - AI Feynman is run by the authors in the new setting with a widened operator set (p. 18).
    - Discovery by depth (Table 5, p. 19): 100% for all methods at depth ≤2. At depth 3, 59.1% (STreCH) and 54.5% (MINLP) vs. 27.2% (AI Feynman). At depth ≥4, 0% vs. 10%.
    - Per-formula failure analysis: free constants vs. fixed constants; AI Feynman's trig identities (pp. 18–19).
    - A STreCH trajectory table (Table 6, p. 19) and RMSE profiles (Fig. 10, p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Constraint-by-constraint comparison with Cozad–Sahinidis [4], whose formulation is restated in their notation in Appendix A (pp. 5–11, 20–22).
  - Contradicts [4]'s advice to run formulations in parallel ("This conclusion differs from the result in [4]") and attributes the difference to the solver used, SCIP vs. BARON, with "We believe" (p. 17).

  - no formula of depth ≥4 is rediscovered (p. 19);
  - the formulation comparison stops at depth 2 (p. 17);
  - implication cuts are untested (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): an ML problem is recast as a nonconvex MINLP in the home class, solved with B&B plus a local-branching heuristic (pp. 1, 12). This parallels the ReLU recasting cited in SKILL.
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): the formulation is tightened first, with strictly smaller relaxations and fewer constraints (ndata|N| vs. ndata|N|(d+1), p. 7). The heuristic is built on the tighter formulation, and the formulation effect is measured on nodes as well as time (pp. 17–18).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence):
  - the competitor is run by the authors in the target setting (p. 18);
  - losses reported (depth ≥4, p. 19);
  - a disagreement with prior results is attributed to the solver with hedging (p. 17);
  - node limits are preferred over time limits for reproducibility (p. 15).
- Method 3 ⚠ (variant): the smallest instance (Example 2.5, p. 11) and explicit witness points (Lemma 2.1, p. 6; App. B, p. 23) are used constructively, to prove that a new constraint is strictly tighter, not to break a method.
- New-pattern candidate: strict-tightening lemma pairs, i.e. equal integer hulls and a strictly smaller relaxation shown by an explicit fractional witness (pp. 6–10, 22–23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When several big-M constraints bound the same function under mutually exclusive binaries, merge them into one constraint Σ w_i y_i + M(1−Σ y_i). This keeps the integer set and strictly tightens the relaxation (Lemma 2.2, pp. 7, 22–23).
- Derive implication cuts from the data's sign pattern (sets X_posi, X_nega, X_zero) for domain-restricted operators (p. 10).
- Define a neighbourhood of an incumbent by one linear Hamming-type constraint on assignment binaries and search it with the B&B solver, i.e. local branching on structure (pp. 12–13).
- Limit B&B nodes, not wall time, inside heuristics so that runs reproduce across machines (p. 15).
- Run the state-of-the-art competitor yourself in the new setting, then analyse per formula why each method wins or loses (pp. 18–19).
