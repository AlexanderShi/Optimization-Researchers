### S145 · Binary quantum control optimization with uncertain Hamiltonians (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2025, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2024.0560; arXiv 2401.10120)
**Read level**: full. The whole arXiv v2 text (pp. 1-30) was read, including all proofs and Remark 4. The reference list (pp. 30-38) was scanned. This is not the journal version. Any page citation from this card should be written as a version citation ("S145 arXiv").
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xinyu Fei (first author, Michigan), Lucas T. Brady (NASA Ames), Jeffrey Larson (Argonne), Siqian Shen (Michigan) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: third paper of the Fei-Brady-Larson-Leyffer-Shen binary quantum control line. It extends their deterministic model (Fei et al. 2023, Quantum, [19]) and switching-time follow-up ([20]) to uncertain Hamiltonians (pp. 2-3). The SUR theory comes from Leyffer's mixed-integer optimal control work (refs [50], [64], [65]; p. 11).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry point is a gap in the authors' own line. Their earlier models and the GRAPE/CRAB/RL literature "study only deterministic quantum optimal control problems" with fixed Hamiltonians (p. 2). Imprecise Hamiltonian estimates, time-varying noise and inhomogeneous ensembles call for robust control (p. 2). The problem is stated as a stochastic mixed-integer program SP(S). Its controls u_jk are binary with an SOS1 constraint (one controller active at a time, (4e)). The Hamiltonians are multiplicatively perturbed, (1+ξ^s_jk)H^(j) (4b), and the objective is a risk measure over S sampled scenarios (4a) (p. 5). Three contributions are listed (p. 2): the stochastic model with a sample-based reformulation, gradient methods with rounding plus derivative and gap bounds, and numerics under several variance settings.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The objective mixes risk-neutral and risk-averse terms as α·mean + (1−α)·CVaR_η (p. 6). The method is relax-then-round.
  1. Penalize SOS1 with a squared L2 term θF_L (8). Relax u to [0,1], which gives a bound-constrained problem (9) (p. 7).
  2. Remove the inner min over ζ in the CVaR. Theorem 1 gives the closed form F_C(u) = F^{s*}(u) + (1/η)Σ_{s: F^s > F^{s*}} p_s(F^s − F^{s*}), where s* is the η-tail threshold scenario (pp. 7-8). Remark 2 adds that with equal weights ζ* is the ⌈ηS⌉-th largest value (p. 8).
  3. Use the gradient (17)/(21) away from ties (Theorem 2, pp. 9-10). Per-scenario gradients come from GRAPE (p. 10).
  4. Solve with L-BFGS-B for energy problems, or with projected Adam that draws a new sample set at every iteration (Algorithm 1, p. 11).
  5. Round with sum-up rounding (SUR) on a finer grid, T_R = C_SUR·T (Algorithm 2, p. 12). The stated reason: the relaxation dominates the run time, so they "solve the continuous relaxation using fewer time steps T and round the solutions using more time steps TR" (p. 11).

  What is kept: the deterministic model and the GRAPE/SUR pipeline of [19] (p. 3). What changes: scenario copies of the dynamics (4b)-(4d) and the risk measure (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The uncertainty distribution is known and approximated by finite samples (pp. 4-5). F_X is continuous, non-negative and upper-bounded, and SP(S) is feasible (Assumptions 1-2, p. 12); "satisfied in most quantum control problems" (p. 12). The O(Δt) bound needs Assumption 3, an optimal continuous SOS1 relaxation with objective value exactly 0 (p. 14). The o(Δt)Δt bound adds Assumption 4, piecewise continuity of that optimal control (p. 16). The differentiability theorem excludes ties F^s(û) = F^{s*}(û) (Theorem 2, p. 9). In the numerics the noise is hierarchical normal: a per-scenario offset μ^s_j ~ N(0, σ^offset_j), then ξ^s_jk ~ N(μ^s_j, σ^time_j) with σ^time = 0.1σ^offset (pp. 18-19).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Theorem 1 (pp. 7-8): optimality of ζ* = F^{s*}(u) is shown by two one-sided expansions, ζ < ζ* (13) and ζ > ζ* (14). Each rewrites F_CVaR(u,ζ) as F_CVaR(u,ζ*) plus non-negative terms, using the tail-mass definition (11) of s*.
  - Theorem 2 (pp. 9-10): a local-invariance argument. With ω = min |F^s(û) − F^{s*}(û)| > 0, continuity gives a radius r on which the ordering of the scenarios, and hence s* and the tail set, does not change ((18)-(19)). F_C is then a fixed smooth combination, so it can be differentiated termwise (20).
  - Theorem 3 (pp. 12-13): the cumulative SUR error is at most (N−1)Δt/C_SUR + ((2N−1)/N)√(t_f F_L(u^con) Δt) (23). Most of the proof is deferred: "directly follows the proof of Theorem 4 and Corollary 1 in Fei et al. [19] with details being omitted here" (p. 13). The new step bounds F_L(u^con) ≤ F_UB by comparing the penalized optimum with a feasible SOS1 point (27). Convergence of the objective values is then cited from Sager-Bock-Diehl Corollary 8 (p. 13).
  - Proposition 1 (pp. 14-16): tightens the bound to O(Δt). The cell-average control u^{d,SOS1} (35) matches integrals on each cell (36). Sager-Zeile Theorem 2 gives ‖X(t_f;u*) − X(t_f;u^d)‖ ≤ C′Δt (41). With the zero-objective Assumption 3 this yields F_L(u^con) ≤ C″Δt/θ ((34), (43)-(44)).
  - Proposition 2 (p. 16): o(Δt)Δt under Assumption 4. Consequence stated: the first term dominates, so a larger C_SUR pays off (p. 16).
  - Remark 4 (pp. 16-17), a counterexample: the continuous relaxation solved on T steps is not a lower bound for the binary solution rounded on C_SUR·T steps. The example is the smallest possible: T = 1, S = 1, zero noise, a 4×4 CNOT-type target with explicit H^(0), H^(1), H^(2). It is computed: F̃(u^bin) = 0.673 < 0.678 = F̃(u^con) (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: Python 3.8 on an 8-core macOS machine. Code and results are public on GitHub ([68]) (p. 17).
  - Energy minimization: q = 6 qubits, T = 50, T_R = 200, η = 0.05, L-BFGS-B. Out-of-sample evaluation uses 10 groups × 500 scenarios (p. 19).
    - Table 1 (p. 20): the in-/out-of-sample gap shrinks with S (total gap 83.48% at S = 1, 4.08% at S = 500), and the CVaR gap exceeds the mean gap (p. 19).
    - α sweep (Fig. 3, pp. 20-21): lower α gives smaller spread. Offset heat maps compare risk-averse and risk-neutral control (Fig. 4, p. 21).
    - Table 2 (p. 23) gives the percentage change against the deterministic model in mean, CVaR and "distinguished percentage". The results are mixed and reported as such: α = 0 worsens the mean by up to 80.32% and the DP by −72.22% at σ = 0.1/0.1 (p. 23). The text explains why (p. 22).
    - Table 3 (p. 23) checks CVaR calibration: the out-of-sample share below the in-sample (1−η) percentile is within about 1% of the target (p. 23).
    - CPU time (Table 4, p. 24): q dominates (2814 s at q = 6, S = 300).
  - Circuit compilation on a gmon architecture (H2, LiH) with modified Adam (pp. 25-26).
    - The sample-size comparison is made at equal compute S × K, with the learning rates adjusted (p. 26). S = 20 wins because it gets more iterations (Table 5, p. 27).
    - The deterministic control is very poor under noise: mean 0.639 vs 8.19e-3 for SP at σ = 0.01 (Table 6, p. 28).
    - LiH runs take up to 8482 s (Table 7, p. 29).
  - Baselines: only the authors' own deterministic model. The robust-control methods reviewed on p. 2 (sample-based, minmax SQP, sequential convex programming) are not run.
  - Source issue: the Table 2 caption labels "α = 0" as optimizing the mean and "α = 1" as optimizing the CVaR (p. 23). The text uses the opposite (p. 22), and so does the definition on p. 6.
- **D6 Positioning**: The literature review sorts deterministic quantum control (GRAPE, Krylov GRAPE, CRAB, evolutionary, RL) from robust and ensemble control (Fourier, pseudospectral, sampling, minmax, SCP) (pp. 1-2). The paper presents itself as the binary/mixed-integer stochastic variant, building on the authors' own [19], [20] (pp. 2-3). Mixed-integer optimal control sources (Sager et al., Sager-Zeile, Leyffer-Manns-Winckler, Manns-Kirches) are cited for SUR. The paper notes that SUR has so far been used to round either continuous-time controls or relaxations on the same grid (p. 11). Pang and Leyffer 2004 is cited for VaR (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contributions are enumerated "First/Second/Third" (p. 2). Theorems come with full proofs where the argument is new and are deferred to citations where it is reused (p. 13). Assumptions are numbered and justified in one clause each (p. 12). The negative remark about the lower bound is stated openly as "We provide a counterexample in the following remark" (p. 16). The conclusion gives a usage rule by problem class: L-BFGS-B for energy problems, modified Adam for infidelity (p. 30; also p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: Contributions are model-level (a risk-measure SP for binary quantum control), algorithmic (closed-form CVaR plus gradients, coarse-relax/fine-round SUR) and theoretical (rounding-gap bounds). Limits are stated concretely: CPU time grows exponentially with qubits, and scenario count adds cost, which could be parallelized (pp. 24, 29). Assumption 3 (zero optimal objective) is strong; it is labelled an assumption but its realism is not discussed (p. 14). Future work: run the time evolution on quantum hardware, and use model-free/RL methods for more complex uncertainty (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The home class (mixed-integer optimal control with SUR and its rounding-error theory, refs [50], [64], [65]) is carried into quantum control. The quantum pulse problem is recast as a binary SOS1 control problem (pp. 3-5, 11-16).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. Remark 4 builds the smallest instance (T = 1, S = 1, 4×4) that breaks the natural belief that the relaxation bounds the rounded solution, and runs it to get numbers (pp. 16-17).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Out-of-sample testing, an equal-compute rule (S × K, p. 26), a rule of thumb by class (p. 30) and unfavourable Table 2 entries (p. 23) all fit the method. But the only baseline is the authors' own deterministic model, and the published robust-control competitors cited on p. 2 are not run.
- New-pattern candidate: **coarse-to-fine discretization**. Solve the expensive relaxation on a coarse time grid and round on a C_SUR-times finer grid; the bound then shows that C_SUR controls the dominant error term (pp. 11, 16).
- New-pattern candidate: **collapse an inner optimization in closed form before applying gradient methods**. Here the CVaR ζ-minimization is replaced by a tail-sum formula and differentiated away from ties (pp. 7-10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To make CVaR usable inside a gradient method, write min_ζ CVaR in closed form at the η-tail threshold scenario. Prove optimality by two one-sided expansions, and get differentiability away from ties by a local-invariance argument on the scenario ordering (pp. 7-10).
- Separate the solve grid from the rounding grid. Relax on T steps and apply SUR on C_SUR·T steps. Then state a bound whose first term (N−1)Δt/C_SUR shows the payoff of refining only the rounding (pp. 11-13, 16).
- When you suspect a bound does not hold, write the smallest numerical counterexample (one time step, one scenario, explicit 4×4 matrices) and print both objective values (p. 17).
- Compare sample sizes for a stochastic method at a fixed product of scenarios × iterations, not at a fixed iteration count (p. 26).
- Check a risk-averse model's calibration out of sample: the share of out-of-sample scenarios below the in-sample (1−η) percentile, against the target 1−η (Table 3, p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, we solve the continuous relaxation using fewer time steps T and round the solutions using more time steps TR to achieve a balance between computational time and the difference between continuous and binary solutions." (p. 11)
- "In numerical studies we empirically show that L-BFGS-B is better for quantum problems aiming to minimize the energy of a quantum state, while Adam performs better on problems minimizing the infidelity compared with a target quantum operator." (p. 10)

### S150 · Optimal Experimental Design of a Moving Sensor for Linear Bayesian Inverse Problems (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2509.15961)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1-31 were read, including the proofs in Appendix A (pp. 28-31). The reference list (pp. 31-34) was scanned.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Nicole Aretz (first author, Oden Institute), Thomas Lynn (Johns Hopkins APL), Karen Willcox (Oden Institute) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Leyffer's PDE-constrained and optimal-control line moved into Bayesian OED with Willcox's group. The code is under his GitHub account (github.com/leyffer/m2dtIceSheet, p. 28).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The application is a mobile sensor that measures a PDE state continuously, used to infer the state's parameters. The posterior uncertainty depends on the path, so the path should be designed beforehand (p. 1). The gap is stated against three lines of work (p. 2): waypoint and real-time steering, stationary sensor selection (convex relaxation, greedy, combinatorial integral approximation), and continuous-path optimization for deterministic inverse problems or filtering. The problem is the OED program (22): minimize Ψ(Σ_post(p)) + R(α,p) over controls α and path p. The constraints are the path ODE, the initial position and the obstacle/path constraints c(p,t) ≥ 0 (p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Three design choices make this a smooth NLP that IPOPT can solve.
  1. Linearity removes the nesting. For a linear PDE with Gaussian prior and noise, Σ_post(p) = (G(p)*C_noise^{-1}G(p) + Σ_pr^{-1})^{-1} does not depend on the data. "As such, we can solve the OED problem efficiently, which would otherwise be a bilevel optimization problem" (pp. 6-7).
  2. The path is parameterized through a control ODE (unicycle model, Example 1, p. 4). This guarantees realistic paths and turns path and obstacle constraints into explicit algebraic constraints (pp. 2, 8, 12-13).
  3. Time is discretized consistently. The measurements are linearly interpolated in time, and the noise is an operator in L²([0,T]), so Σ_noise^{-1} is a FE-type matrix (26)-(27). The cost then converges as Δt → 0 instead of collapsing as it would for uncorrelated measurements (pp. 9-11, 22).

  Solver: IPOPT with a quasi-Newton Hessian, because second derivatives of the implicit OED term would be "prohibitive" (p. 13). Cost reduction comes from three devices: precomputing the M PDE solves, starting with pointwise measurements, and warm-starting from coarse grids (pp. 11-12, 15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Linear PDE solution operator F, well-defined and continuous (p. 3). Kernel bound (6) (p. 5). Gaussian prior, non-degenerate without loss of generality (p. 5). Additive Gaussian noise with a positive-definite covariance operator (p. 6). Obstacles are enclosed in ellipsoids or rectangles (19)-(20). The ℓ∞ rectangle constraint is not differentiable at its centre but is smooth near the feasible set (p. 8). The gradient results need Φ(x,·) Lipschitz, plus either a C¹ kernel (Cor. 2) or an H¹ state with a ball-truncated kernel (Cor. 3) (pp. 13-15). The authors call global differentiability of Φ "a strong assumption" and relax it in Cor. 3 (p. 14). Nonlinear PDEs are deferred to a Laplace approximation (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Proposition 1 and Corollary 1 (pp. 9, 11, 28-29): G(p^h) and Σ_post are continuous in each p_k. The proof uses Lipschitz continuity of Φ, the L¹ ≤ √|Ω|·L² bound on a bounded domain, and eigenvalues bounded below by those of Σ_pr^{-1}.
  - Proposition 2 (pp. 13-14, 29-30): gradients of the A- and D-criteria, −2 tr(Σ_post Gᵀ Σ_noise^{-1} dG/dp_{k,i} Σ_post) and −2Ψ_D tr(Gᵀ Σ_noise^{-1} dG/dp_{k,i} Σ_post). The proof chains three matrix-calculus identities (scalar-by-matrix, the derivative of an inverse, and the trace symmetry that merges the two terms). The point made is that eigenvalues are not differentiable, but trace and determinant are (p. 13).
  - Corollaries 2-3 (pp. 14-15, 31): sufficient conditions for differentiability of G by Leibniz's rule, or by a change of variables onto B_r(0).
  - No convergence theorem for the optimizer: "we expect the nonlinear solvers to converge to a stationary point" (p. 13). Consistency as Δt → 0 is shown numerically, not proved (pp. 21-22).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Setup (pp. 15-17): a pollutant spreads in an urban domain, with a convection-diffusion equation driven by a steady Navier-Stokes velocity field. Cubic FE with 32,096 dofs, Crank-Nicolson time stepping, about 70 s per forward solve (p. 16). Two sources with unknown amplitudes m ~ N((1,1), I) (pp. 16-17). Signal-to-noise ratio 12-16 dB (p. 17).
  - Initialization (Fig. 4, p. 18): a grid search over constant controls (θ0, ω0) shows that the A/D criteria are nonconvex with many local minima, and three local minima are taken as starts.
  - Results (Table 1, p. 20):
    - Start 1: A-OED ×0.624, 286 iterations, 49.8 s.
    - Start 3: ×0.635, but 886 iterations and termination only at "acceptable" level, explained by the path hugging the boundary of the admissible region (pp. 19-20).
    - Start 2 fails on the design objective: A-OED rises (×1.027). Only the regularization term fell, which the authors interpret as the start already being near a local minimum (p. 19).
    - Each run reports constraint violation, NLP error and termination code.
  - Refinement (pp. 21-23): under Δt refinement both Ψ_A and ∇Ψ_A converge linearly, with one outlier that the authors discuss (p. 21). A cost extrapolation to Δt = 10⁻⁴ gives more than 29 h, which motivates warm starts (p. 22). Warm-starting with interpolated controls and multipliers over six halvings drops the iteration count from 142 to 1 (Table 2, p. 23).
  - Averaged measurements (Fig. 8, p. 24): with ball-averaged sensing the utility changes by at most ×1.0162. Footnote 4 lists four slow cases.
  - M = 25 PCA-reduced field (pp. 25-26): A-OED ×0.859 (Table 3, p. 26).
  - Baselines: none. No comparison with waypoint, greedy or stationary designs.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself between optimal control of mobile platforms and PDE-constrained OED (p. 2). Combinatorial integral approximation (Sager, Kirches-Manns-Ulbrich) appears only as a stationary-sensor alternative (p. 2). It notes that the Bayesian and deterministic settings are "structural analogues" here (p. 3). It builds on the infinite-dimensional Bayesian framework (Stuart; Bui-Thanh-Ghattas et al.) and on likelihood-informed reduction (Cui-Marzouk-Willcox) (pp. 6, 25). Prior work is not criticized.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Fig. 1 maps the nested structure of OED → inverse problem → forward model (p. 3). Running examples (unicycle, Gaussian-blurred and ball-averaged sensors) are introduced before they are used (pp. 4-5). The authors give a reason for each numerical anomaly: path 2 (p. 19), path 3 (p. 20), the Δt outlier (p. 21), the slow radii (p. 24 fn. 4). Hedging is moderate ("we expect", p. 13; "indicates", p. 22).
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: Contributions are a formulation, a discretization consistent under time refinement, gradient formulas, and an IPOPT-based workflow with warm starts. The limits are named concretely: linear PDEs only, a single sensor, parameter fields only via PCA reduction (pp. 25, 27-28). Future work (pp. 27-28): expected information gain for nonlinear maps, infinite-dimensional parameters, multifidelity reduction, robustness to aleatoric uncertainty, cheaper initial guesses. An extension to several sensors and to continuous stationary placement is called "straightforward", without a demonstration (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The home NLP toolkit (interior-point NLP, quasi-Newton, warm starts with multipliers) is carried into Bayesian OED. The path-design object is recast as a smooth control-parameterized NLP (pp. 2, 12-13, 22).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. The formulation is fixed before the method. Linearity removes the bilevel structure (p. 7). The ODE parameterization makes the constraints explicit (pp. 4, 13). Obstacles are written as constraints that are smooth near the feasible set (p. 8). The noise operator is chosen so the discretization stays consistent (pp. 10-11, 22).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Failures are kept in the statistics: start 2 makes the design worse, start 3 terminates only at "acceptable", and per-run NLP error and constraint violation are reported (Table 1, p. 20). But no competing design method is run.
- Heuristic 8 ✅ evidence. The brute-force cost extrapolation (more than 29 h at Δt = 10⁻⁴, p. 22) is used to justify the warm-start continuation.
- New-pattern candidate: **coarse-to-fine continuation** (grid halving with warm-started controls and multipliers; pointwise → averaged measurements) used both to save cost and to check consistency (pp. 11-12, 21-24).
- New-pattern candidate: **collapse an inner problem in closed form**. Here the linear-Gaussian posterior covariance replaces the bilevel inner inverse problem (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For linear-Gaussian inverse problems, the posterior covariance does not depend on the data. Write the design problem directly on Σ_post(p) and avoid a bilevel formulation (pp. 6-7).
- Parameterize a moving device's path through a control ODE, so physical limits become bounds on the controls and obstacles become explicit path constraints that an interior-point solver handles (pp. 4, 8, 12-13).
- Model the measurement noise as an operator on L²([0,T]), for example the solution operator of −Δv + 100v = f. The discretized information then converges under time refinement and does not blow up as it would with i.i.d. samples. Test this by refining Δt (pp. 10-11, 21-22).
- Differentiate trace and determinant criteria rather than eigenvalues. Use the derivative-of-inverse identity, and reduce each gradient entry to a single trace (pp. 13, 29-30).
- Before the NLP, run a cheap grid search over a two-parameter reduced control family. It maps the nonconvexity and supplies several distinct starts (pp. 17-18).
- Warm-start each refinement level with interpolated controls and Lagrange multipliers, and report the falling iteration count and initial NLP error as evidence of convergence to a continuous-time minimizer (Table 2, pp. 22-23).

**Quotes**:
- "The fact that the forward model is linear implies that the posterior covariance matrix does not depend on observational data, or on the posterior mean. As such, we can solve the OED problem efficiently, which would otherwise be a bilevel optimization problem." (p. 7)
- "Because all functions (explicit and implicit) are smooth, we expect the nonlinear solvers to converge to a stationary point of (30)." (p. 13)

### S164 · An Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers for Topology Optimization (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2509.19888)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1-9 were read, including the appendix with the convergence argument (pp. 6-9).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Harsh Choudhary (first and corresponding author, Czech Technical University), Dominic Yang (Argonne) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a short extended-abstract-style paper in the mixed-integer PDE-constrained optimization (MIPDECO) line. It combines Yang's TV/budget subproblem algorithm ([4]) with the funnel strategy from Kiessling-Leyffer-Vanaret's funnel SQP ([5]) (pp. 3-4).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The application is heat-sink design with two conductive materials. A binary design field w(x) is regularized by total variation, because TO problems are ill-posed without it (p. 1). The constraints are an elliptic PDE, the boundary conditions and a volume budget (1) (p. 2). After discretization (2) the problem is a MINLP. Three difficulties are listed: integer variables in a nonconvex feasible set, a nonsmooth TV term, and compliance that is nonconvex in w (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Variable splitting: a continuous copy v ∈ [0,1] carries the PDE with SIMP conductivity k(v) = δ + (1−δ)v^p, chosen "because it promotes the integrality of v". The binary w carries the TV term. Both copies carry the volume constraint (3) (p. 3).
  - Dualizing v = w gives an augmented Lagrangian (4). ADMM alternates between two subproblems (pp. 3-4):
    - Cont, a reduced-space PDE-constrained NLP in v, solved with IPOPT (5);
    - Disc, a binary TV + quadratic + budget problem, solved with a randomized heuristic that moves on connected regions, after Yang [4] (6).
  - Change from standard ADMM: the fixed penalty is replaced by a funnel. A step is accepted if ‖w−v‖² ≤ βτ^(j), and the funnel then shrinks, τ ← (1−ζ)τ + ζ‖w−v‖². Otherwise the step is rejected and ρ ← cρ (Algorithm 1, p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**:
  - Discretization (p. 2): piecewise-constant controls on a triangular mesh, and TV with edge weights s_ee′ ∈ {1, √2} so that it does not depend on the mesh type.
  - Convergence argument: block-Lipschitz continuity of the augmented Lagrangian in v (Assumption 1, p. 7). Theorem 1 also assumes L^(j) bounded below and that the iterates stay in the funnel for all j, n, i.e. ‖w^(j+n) − v^(j+n)‖² ≤ βτ^(j) (p. 7). Claim 1 adds a Hessian lower bound ∇²F_w ≥ mI (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: The appendix (pp. 6-9) gives a two-case argument.
  - Case (i), finitely many penalty updates: the funnel recursion gives τ^(j) → 0 (p. 7).
  - Case (ii), ρ → ∞: Claim 1 is a Taylor expansion of F_w(v) about w together with Cauchy-Schwarz, giving ‖v* − w‖ ≤ ‖∇F_w(w)‖/(ρ/2 + c) (p. 8). Claim 2 uses the finiteness of the binary feasible set F to bound G(w*) − G(w), so that for large ρ the Disc solution minimizes ‖v* − w‖² over F (pp. 8-9).
  - Rigor:
    - The two claims are labelled "Claim" and "Argument" rather than lemmas (p. 8).
    - In case (i) the displayed chain ends with "≤ −ζ(1 − β)τ^(j)" and sets θ = ζ(1−β) (p. 7). As printed this is an algebra slip: (1−ζ)τ + ζβτ = (1 − ζ(1−β))τ, so the contraction factor should be 1 − ζ(1−β).
    - The funnel hypothesis of Theorem 1 is close to the conclusion.
    - The main text asserts that "by definition τ(j) is a decreasing function which ensures convergence of iterates" (p. 4).
  - Also inconsistent: (4) has +λ inside the quadratic, while (5)-(6) use −λ (pp. 3-4).
- **D5 Experiments**: One model problem: the unit square, h = 1/32 (2048 elements), mixed Dirichlet/Neumann boundary, α = 5·10⁻⁵ (pp. 4-5).
  - Fig. 1 shows the evolution of v and w, and Fig. 2 shows ‖w−v‖² and the objective (p. 5).
  - A scatter over initial ρ: for ρ ≳ 2.4×10⁻², the iterates always stay in the funnel and converge. Smaller ρ triggers penalty updates until that value is reached (pp. 4-5).
  - Run times over a range of meshes on a Xeon SLURM cluster, 40 ADMM iterations per mesh, medians with quartile bands (Fig. 3, pp. 5-6).
  - The text claims that the randomized connected-region solver "outperforms the deterministic ones like Gurobi which scales exponentially with the dimension of control space" (p. 5). The evidence is Fig. 3 only. No time limits, gaps or solution-quality comparison are given, and Gurobi's settings are not stated.
- **D6 Positioning**: Five references in all (pp. 5-6). The paper positions itself as ADMM for integer, TV-regularized, PDE-constrained TO. The discrete subproblem is tied to the class in Yang [4] (image denoising, TO). The funnel comes from Leyffer's own funnel SQP [5]. There is no discussion of other MIPDECO approaches (trust-region TV methods, SUR/CIA, branch-and-bound) or of nonconvex ADMM convergence theory.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Short and declarative: problem statement, method, numerical results. The contribution is never stated as a list. The abstract hedges ("facilitating convergence under appropriate conditions", p. 1). Theory is in an appendix and labelled "asymptotic convergence bounds" (p. 6).
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: An algorithm (funnel-ADMM with an IPOPT subproblem and a randomized discrete subproblem) with a preliminary convergence argument and one test problem. There is no limitations or future-work section. The observed sensitivity to the initial penalty (p. 4) is the only weakness the paper reports.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. Home globalization (the funnel from funnel SQP, [5]) is carried into ADMM as a penalty-update rule (p. 4). The continuous subproblem is handed to an IPOPT-class NLP solver (p. 3).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The algorithm is decomposed into a continuous NLP block and a discrete combinatorial block. This is decomposition along the integer/continuous line, not integration at the iteration level that Taste 1 prefers. Each block uses a solver built for its own structure (pp. 3-4).
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction. The comparison with Gurobi is a one-line claim backed by a run-time figure, with no stated fairness rule, solver settings or solution-quality comparison (p. 5).
- Taste 3 (guarantees) ⚠ variant. The paper does offer a convergence argument, but at "Claim/Argument" level, with a contraction-factor slip and a hypothesis that is close to the conclusion (pp. 7-9).
- New-pattern candidate: **NLP globalization acceptance tests transplanted to non-NLP outer loops**. Here a funnel accept/reject test drives the ADMM penalty (p. 4). Compare S167, p. 5.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split a mixed-integer PDE design variable into a continuous copy that carries the PDE (solved with a gradient NLP solver, with SIMP to push toward 0/1) and a binary copy that carries the combinatorial regularizer and the budget. Duplicate the budget constraint in both blocks (p. 3).
- Replace ADMM's fixed or heuristically tuned penalty with a funnel. Accept a step only if the coupling residual is within βτ, shrink τ by a convex combination after an accepted step, and raise ρ after a rejected one (p. 4).
- Report sensitivity to the initial penalty with a scatter of runs coloured by the final-to-initial penalty ratio, which shows where the funnel stops rejecting steps (Fig. 2, pp. 4-5).

**Quotes**:
- "Unlike traditional ADMM methods that use a fixed penalty parameter, we adopt the funnel-based strategy in [5] to adaptively increase the penalty when constraint infeasibility is high." (p. 4)
- "The choice of initial penalty parameter is instrumental in the convergence" (p. 4)

### S165 · Optimizing Paths for Adaptive Fly-Scan Microscopy: An Extended Version (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2509.01869)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1-9 were read (p. 1 is the license cover sheet). The references (pp. 10-11) were scanned.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yu Lu (first author, UC Merced), Thomas F. Lynn, Ming Du, Zichao Di (Argonne); Leyffer is last author (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: an x-ray microscopy application from the Argonne facility side (the WSe2 data come from the Advanced Photon Source, p. 7). It reuses a synthetic test image from Di-Leyffer-Wild 2016 ([31], p. 6). It is the extended version of ISBI paper S160, per the batch note.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Raster fly-scans waste time in uniform regions, and no adaptive fly-scan method is known to the authors (p. 2). Adaptive step-scan methods exist but rely on deep learning, which "heavily depends on the quality of the initial sampling, requires a large dataset for training, and incurs high computational costs" (p. 2). The problem is iterative (pp. 3-4): choose anchor points Ω*_k by minimizing a surrogate loss L(Ω_k, f^k), connect them with a short path, fly-scan along it, and complete the image by inverse-distance weighting (IDW). The surrogate is needed because the true target ‖R(F(H(Ω))) − f*‖_F needs the unknown ground truth f*.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: An analytic loop with four parts (pp. 4-5):
  1. the loss (5) is −(α log U(Ω) + log ‖∇I‖), where U is an "exponentially weighted uncertainty function" over nearest scanned neighbours (6) and ∇I is the image gradient (7). The two terms trade exploration against exploitation, "inspired by acquisition functions typically used for Bayesian optimization";
  2. anchor points are moved by ADAM (8);
  3. a gradient-proportional score function (9) supplies the initial anchors, because ADAM finds only local minima;
  4. the path is built greedily by nearest neighbour over a candidate subset χ (10) instead of an exact TSP, which is called computationally expensive and possibly not optimal for the objective anyway (pp. 3, 4-5). Completion uses IDW along the path (11).

  The whole loop is Algorithm 1 (p. 5). The authors deliberately chose "an analytical adaptive fly-scan framework rather than employing deep learning-based methods" (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Noise-free images, with σ = 1 fixed (pp. 7-8). Simulated fly-scan physics: 1 nm per pixel, 1 nm/s, 0.5 s exposure, 0.02 s dead time (p. 5). The initial scan uses 30 random anchors, under 7% of the raster samples (p. 5). Ground truth is the raster fly-scan result, not the real object (p. 6). The motion hardware can follow sharp turns, an assumption the authors flag as a limitation (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: n/a. There are no theorems. The design arguments are qualitative: exploration vs exploitation (p. 4), and the case for a score-function initialization given ADAM's local convergence (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: Four images: synthetic shapes, Shepp-Logan phantom, Cameraman, and a real WSe2 nanoflake from APS step-scan data (p. 5). Parameters: N = 600, K = 16 (N = 500, K = 12 for the WSe2 image), α = 10, ℓ = 4, S = 50. Each setting runs 10 times on an M3 MacBook Air, and averages are reported (p. 5). Metrics are PSNR and SSIM (pp. 5-6).
  - Baselines: the initial scan, and uniform random anchors with the same number of samples (p. 5). No existing adaptive scanning method is run.
  - Table I (p. 6): the proposed method beats random on every image, for example 23.7 vs 15.1 dB on the synthetic image using 21.4% of samples, and 28.3 vs 21.3 dB on WSe2 using 23.5%.
  - Parameter analysis on WSe2 (pp. 7-8): the spread across α is about 2 dB and across ℓ under 2.5 dB, read as stability.
  - Source issues:
    - "Total Samples" is 262139 for 256 × 256 images (Table I, p. 6).
    - The text discusses the sharp angles of "the final scan path in Fig. 5(c)" (p. 8), but Fig. 5(c) is captioned as the gradient map, and the path is 5(b) (p. 7).
    - The conclusion says "three experiments on four images" (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: Scanning methods are sorted on two axes, raster vs adaptive and fly vs step (p. 3). The paper claims priority: "the first work in the adaptive path reseach for the fly-scan technique" (p. 9; also p. 8). It positions against deep-learning adaptive step-scan ([11], [12]) on cost and data grounds (pp. 2-3). Matrix completion methods are set aside as slower and as designed for arbitrary missing data (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contributions come as three bullets (p. 3). The experiment objectives are numbered (p. 5). The limitations section is concrete, with a mechanism for each limitation (pp. 8-9):
  - a random initial path may miss small feature regions;
  - sharp turns may exceed what the motor can follow;
  - IDW can turn noisy samples into false gradients.

  Future work is a list (p. 9). Some evaluative wording is promotional: the proposed scan path is called very smart (p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: An application framework (surrogate acquisition loss, score initialization, greedy routing, IDW completion). Fewer than 30% of raster measurements reach PSNR 22-30 dB (pp. 6, 9). The limits are concrete (pp. 8-9). Future work: self-adaptive initial scans, hardware-aware smoothing penalties, denoising, deep-learning feature extractors and 3D ptycho-tomography (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. The project enters a new area through an Argonne facility and its collaborators (APS data, p. 7), but none of the home NLP/MINLP toolkit is carried in. The optimizer is ADAM, and routing uses a greedy heuristic in place of TSP (pp. 4-5). This is the "extensions came through collaborators and laboratory facilities" pattern without the base.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The equal-sample random baseline isolates the value of the loss (pp. 5-6), and the limitations are concrete (pp. 8-9). But no published adaptive-scanning method is compared, and the reference is a raster fly-scan rather than the true object (p. 6).
- Heuristic 8 ✗ contradiction (mild). The exact TSP is dismissed as "computationally expensive" with no size estimate or timing, although the path is built over hundreds of anchors (N = 500-600) (pp. 3, 4-5).
- New-pattern candidate: **a surrogate acquisition objective when the true error cannot be computed**. Combine an uncertainty term (exploration) with a feature-gradient term (exploitation) in place of the unavailable ‖reconstruction − truth‖ (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the quantity you want to minimize needs unknown ground truth, write a log-sum surrogate of an uncertainty term and a signal-gradient term, and expose the exploration/exploitation weight α as the single knob to study (pp. 4, 7-8).
- Give a local optimizer a data-driven start: sample initial points from a distribution proportional to the current reconstruction's gradient magnitude (p. 4).
- Isolate the contribution of a selection rule by comparing against uniform random selection with exactly the same sample budget (Table I, p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "However, considering the dataset requirements and training time, we propose an analytical adaptive fly-scan framework rather than employing deep learning-based methods." (p. 3)
- "This issue arises because the proposed framework focuses solely on path efficiency." (p. 8)

### S167 · Sampling Imbalanced Data with Multi-objective Bilevel Optimization (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2506.11315)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1-16 were read, including Technical Appendix A (pp. 13-16, figure captions). The references (pp. 10-13) were scanned.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Karen Medlin (first author, UNC Chapel Hill, DOE SCGSR graduate student), Krishnan Raghavan (Argonne) (pp. 1, 10) · **Place in trajectory**: the sequel to the same authors' MUBO (Majority Undersampling with Bilevel Optimization, arXiv 2410.11171, ref [34]). The bilevel/leader-follower modelling from Leyffer's MPEC line is applied to ML training-set selection (pp. 3, 5).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Imbalanced two-class problems. Reweighting the loss overfits the minority class, and naive resampling only changes counts without adding diversity: generating more upright zeros in MNIST does not prepare a model for rotated zeros (p. 1). New samples should "(i) enhance minority diversity and (ii) avoid overlapping with majority data" (p. 1). A second gap: no metric measures how a sampling method's diversity and overlap affect the model (abstract, p. 1; p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: MOODS is a bilevel program (1) (p. 4). The upper level chooses the training subset S ⊂ S^Tr to minimize (1 − F1_minority, 1 − F1_overall) on validation data. The lower level trains the network weights w* = argmin L(S; z_w).
  - Algorithm 1 (p. 5):
    - start roughly balanced;
    - each outer step adds random majority points and SVM-SMOTE synthetic minority points;
    - fully retrain;
    - accept only if both validation objectives improve;
    - on rejection, revert, halve the sampling probabilities of the rejected synthetic points and shrink the batch;
    - repeat.
  - "We note that the two loops do not alternate. At every iteration of the outer loop, we completely solve the training problem" (p. 5).
  - Differences from their own MUBO (p. 3): two objectives instead of majority loss alone, the outer loop aims at a single Pareto point, and a small amount of synthetic data is added.
  - The ε/δ metric (pp. 6-7): the network output z_w* serves as a 1-D proxy for the inputs, with the architecture held fixed. Overlap is the share of minority points on the wrong side of z = 1/2 (Def. 4). Diversity is the order-of-magnitude increase in z-variance (Def. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Two-class problems, binary cross-entropy (pp. 3-4). Train/validation/test splits are disjoint, and validation drives the upper level to prevent overfitting (pp. 3, 5). The z-proxy is valid only with the architecture held constant (p. 6). No convergence assumptions are stated. The authors say they have "not yet confirmed convergence" (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: n/a for proofs. The analytic content is definitional: Defs. 1-6 set up loss, classification, F1, overlap, variance increase and S_{ε/δ} (pp. 4-7). Convergence is open: an attempt that combined both objectives with standard descent did not find a Pareto front (p. 5). Fig. 3 shows Pareto points of five runs as empirical evidence of balanced descent (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**: Seven UCI datasets with 335 to 376,640 points, 7-57 features and minority shares of 0.29%-39.39% (Table 1, p. 8).
  - Fairness rule: the same architecture for all methods (256-128 hidden units, Adam, learning rate 1e-4, batch size 32), aligned with the competitors' papers (p. 7). Five seeded runs per dataset (p. 7).
  - Baselines: no sampling, SMOTE, SVM-SMOTE, GBO, SSG, MUBO, rerun by the authors. SMOTified-GAN could not be run, so its published numbers are used (p. 8). Hyperparameters were not tuned (p. 8).
  - Code is anonymized on 4open.science (p. 8).
  - Results (Table 2, p. 8): MOODS is best or tied on six datasets. The ties are Ecoli 0.92 with SMOTified-GAN, Winequality 0.62 with GBO and Connect4 0.96 with MUBO, while the text says MOODS's scores "surpass those of comparable sampling techniques on six imbalanced datasets" (p. 8). It loses on Spambase, explained as the least imbalanced dataset with persistent overlap (pp. 8, 10).
  - Per-class results (Table 3, p. 9) are mixed. For example, on Winequality MOODS has F1m 0.31 against MUBO's 0.76 (pp. 8-9).
  - Internal inconsistencies:
    - Spambase's overlap decrease is 3 in Table 2 (p. 8) and in the text on p. 10, "from 13% to 10%" on p. 8, and "10 = 13 − 3" in Table 4 (p. 9).
    - Connect4's size is 376,640 in Table 1 but "176,640" on p. 10.
    - ΔO is defined as a floor in ℕ (p. 6) but reported as non-integers such as 2.1 (pp. 6, 8).
- **D6 Positioning**: Sampling methods are sorted into SMOTE variants, overlap-reducing SMOTEs, diversity methods and GAN hybrids, and the GANs are criticized for instability and mode collapse (pp. 2-3). The authors note that none of these measures diversity reliably (p. 3). MOODS is claimed to be, with the authors' own MUBO, the only bilevel method that rebalances the training data itself (p. 3). It is separated from AutoBalance and architecture-search bilevel methods because its upper level selects data, not hyperparameters (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Two contributions are stated in the abstract ("First... Second..."). The headline claim is "state-of-the-art performance" with "a 1 − 15% increase in F1 scores" (p. 1). The paper is candid about the method's weakest point (p. 5): the Pareto point found "is somewhat unsatisfactory", and convergence is not confirmed. The new metric is introduced "To verify our findings" (p. 5), so a metric the authors defined themselves serves as the explanation for the F1 gains (Table 2 caption, p. 8).
- **D8 Contributions & limits**: A formulation and an algorithm (multi-objective bilevel sampling), plus an evaluation metric. Limits named: no convergence confirmation, no hyperparameter tuning (p. 8), and Spambase may need a richer model (p. 10). Future work: the multi-class case and alternative convergence criteria for the upper level (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. The home modelling class (bilevel, leader-follower programs from the MPEC line) is carried into ML data selection, and the newcomer's object (the training set) is recast as an upper-level decision (pp. 4-5).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Several elements fit: a fairness rule (identical architecture, p. 7), reruns of most baselines, transparency about the one non-rerun baseline (p. 8) and reporting the losing dataset (pp. 8, 10). Against it: ties are worded as wins (scores that "surpass those of comparable sampling techniques on six imbalanced datasets", p. 8), there is no date or version stamping, and the numbers across Tables 2 and 4 are inconsistent.
- Workflow B checkpoint ✅ evidence. An open gap is written down rather than argued away: "have not yet confirmed convergence" (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: **NLP globalization acceptance tests transplanted to non-NLP outer loops**. Here a dominance test (accept only if both objectives improve) with reject-and-shrink acts like a filter acceptance test on training subsets (p. 5). Compare S164, p. 4.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Make a resampling heuristic into a bilevel algorithm. The outer loop proposes a small change to the training set, the inner loop retrains fully, and the change is accepted only if every validation objective improves. On rejection, down-weight the proposal distribution of the rejected points (Algorithm 1, p. 5).
- Measure a data-sampling method's effect through the model. With a fixed architecture, read the 1-D logit z_w* as a proxy for the inputs, and report minority overlap across the decision threshold and the log10 variance gain (Defs. 4-6, pp. 6-7).
- When one competitor cannot be rerun, use its published numbers and say so in the experimental section (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Despite strong empirical findings, we did not find a Pareto front so have not yet confirmed convergence nor that the objective is being achieved." (p. 5)
- "Note also that there was no attempt made in this paper to optimize the hyper-parameters of the model." (p. 8)
