### S168 · Remark on Algorithm 1012: Computing Projections with Large Datasets (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 50(2) 2024, doi:10.1145/3656581)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-8, all of it: abstract, §1-§3, references). Accepted-manuscript text; the title, and Leyffer as third author, are confirmed on p. 1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Tyler H. Chang (first author, Argonne), Layne T. Watson, Thomas C. H. Lux, Hussain M. J. Almohri · **Place in trajectory**: Fletcher's QP solver BQPD (refs [6], [7], p. 7), which SKILL.md says Leyffer keeps beside its C++ port (Method 1 step 6), is lent to a computational-geometry/ML-interpolation code.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a failure in shipped software. DELAUNAYSPARSE (TOMS Alg. 1012) extrapolates by projecting onto the convex hull of the training set, which is an equality-constrained NNLS (2). It calls SLATEC DWNNLS, which turns the equality into a weighted penalty row with μ² = 10⁻⁴η/‖[W; eᵀ]‖∞ (pp. 2-3). Because ‖·‖∞ = n after normalisation, μ goes to numerical zero as the number of training points n grows, and μW becomes row-rank deficient. Skewed data dimensions make the same happen at smaller n (p. 3). The problem statement is "solvers built for few variables meet ML-sized n" (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable: before replacing a solver, write down the exact mechanism by which it fails (here the penalty-weight formula combined with the normalisation), so that the remedy targets that mechanism.
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Pose (2) as a general QP (4) and solve it with the primal active-set solver BQPD, using its structure (p. 3). Four things make this work. (i) The Hessian WᵀW is never formed; BQPD takes a Hessian-vector product routine, O(nd) (p. 3). (ii) The active-constraint matrix [e : I_A : I_I] is all unit columns except one, so its LU factors cost O(n) and updates O(1) (p. 3). (iii) The solution is sparse (each facet has at most d vertices, d ≪ n), so the reduced Hessian ZᵀWᵀWZ is d×d (p. 3). (iv) The equality eᵀx = 1 is kept exactly instead of being penalised. What did not change: the Delaunay interpolation algorithm and its interface. The only addition is a public PROJECT routine plus a private GDOTX for WᵀWx (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when a dense-looking QP has a sparse optimum, count the per-iteration cost of an active-set method through the reduced-Hessian dimension (here d, not n) before reaching for a first-order or conic solver.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Data are normalised to the unit sphere (p. 2). The solver is assumed to need only matrix-vector products. The tolerance is tightened from the user's ε to ε^1.5 for BQPD, because ill-conditioned boundary simplices can misjudge points that lie less than ε outside the hull (p. 4). Stated engineering limits: BQPD passes data through Fortran common blocks, is not threadsafe, and must be called inside an OpenMP lock. Its O(n) workspace exceeds the O(d²) of the original code (pp. 3-4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: There are no theorems. The analysis is a complexity accounting. Storage is O(n) + O(d²) and flops are O(nd + d³) per iteration. This is compared with DELAUNAYSPARSE's O(nd²) iteration cost, to argue that the change does not increase time complexity (pp. 3-4). The error of (3) is proportional to μ, a cited fact (p. 2).
  - Transferable: a complexity argument of the form "new workspace ≤ input size and new per-iteration flops ≤ host per-iteration flops" is enough to justify swapping a component inside published software.
- **D5 Experiments**: (a) Synthetic: a full factorial design over d ∈ {2, 8, 16}, n ∈ {d+1, 2⁹, 2¹⁴}, three geometries (lattice, ball, box), three scale factors, three data modifiers and three skews. That gives 405 designs × 10 trials = 4050 experiments with 1000 extrapolation points each (pp. 4-5, Table 1). Outcome: 276 error-71 failures with DWNNLS against 0 with BQPD. Only n matters (0 / 15 / 261 failures by n level), which "supports the hypothesis" that the number of variables is the core problem (p. 5). (b) Real data: KDDCUP99, reduced by rescaling, PCA to 26 dimensions and merging of near-duplicates to 77,802 training points (p. 6). Subsamples n_k from 1,000 to 20,000 with 10 seeds are used. BQPD is compared with DWNNLS and with OSQP, ECOS and SCS through CVXPY 1.3.1, all at the same accuracy (~1.8×10⁻¹²) with an iteration limit of 10,000 (p. 6). The table reports success rate, the number of extrapolation points and the condition number. BQPD succeeds at 1.0 throughout. DWNNLS falls from 0.30 to 0.17 and ECOS from 1.0 to 0.90, while SCS is at 0.0 (p. 7, Table 3). Only success rates are reported, with no timing.
  - Transferable: test a failure hypothesis with a full factorial synthetic design, then report failure counts broken down by factor level. The factor that explains the failures shows up directly.
  - Transferable: when comparing against solvers reached through a modelling layer, state the layer's version, the common accuracy target and the iteration cap (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: It positions itself as a "Remark" that updates the authors' own Algorithm 1012 (refs [1], [2]). DWNNLS (Hanson-Haskell 1982) is not criticised as wrong. It "were not intended for usage with many variable problems" (p. 1). Off-the-shelf conic and QP solvers (OSQP, ECOS, SCS) are shown to fail too, to argue that the problem is harder than thought and that PROJECT "may be of external interest" (p. 3). Interpolation accuracy is explicitly out of scope and deferred to [9] (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Short and mechanism-first. Section 1 ends with the plan and the claim of external interest (p. 3), Section 2 gives the algebra and complexity, Section 3 is "Demonstration of solutions". Hedged causal language: "supports the hypothesis" (p. 5), "again suggesting" (p. 7). Limitations sit in the body text (thread safety, storage), not in a closing section (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is software: a new PROJECT subroutine and the replacement of DWNNLS by BQPD in DELAUNAYSPARSE (pp. 3-4). Concrete limits: not threadsafe; more workspace (p. 4); no timing data; real data only from KDDCUP99 (pp. 5-7). No future work is stated.

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence (the BQPD-centred toolkit is used as a swappable building block. It is driven through a user Hessian-vector routine, the code change is one routine, and the common-block limitation matches the "shared static state in BQPD" diagnosis in SKILL.md Workflow D; pp. 3-4). Method 6 ✅ evidence (the failure is traced to the penalty reformulation of the equality constraint (3) and to how μ scales with n, and the fix keeps the constraint exact; pp. 2-3). Method 5 ✅ evidence (failures counted per solver and size, with a common accuracy target, iteration cap and modelling-layer version stated; pp. 5-7), with a gap: no timing comparison and no tuning of competitors. Method 2 ⚠ variant (here the home NLP toolkit is carried into someone else's code as a component, not into a new problem class). New-pattern candidate: factorial failure-hypothesis benchmark.
**Transferable techniques**:
- Matrix-free active-set QP for projections onto a convex hull: Hessian-vector product O(nd), unit-column LU, d×d reduced Hessian (p. 3).
- Full factorial synthetic stress design, with failure counts by factor level, to isolate the cause of a failure (pp. 4-5).
- Real-data benchmark built from repeated random subsamples of growing size n_k, with the condition number reported next to each success rate (pp. 6-7).
**Quotes**:
- "The difficulty of this problem is greater than originally thought, as observed through the failure of other well-known open source QP solvers on the failure cases of DWNNLS." (p. 3)
- "Given the robustness of the original Delaunay interpolation code and algorithm, only BQPD gives performance on these extrapolation tasks that is in keeping with the spirit of DELAUNAYSPARSE." (p. 7)

### S123 · Robust A-optimal experimental design for sensor placement in Bayesian linear inverse problems (SIAM/ASA Journal on Uncertainty Quantification 2025, doi:10.1137/24m1667543; arXiv:2305.03855)
**Read level**: full, arXiv version (pp. 1-25 of arXiv:2305.03855v1, "Robust A-Optimal Experimental Design for Bayesian Inverse Problems", submitted 9 May 2023, p. 1). All of the body was read, including Appendix A (pp. 22-23). The reference list was skimmed. Per the work-list note this is the same paper; the JUQ version (title narrowed to "sensor placement ... linear") was not read, so any changes in the journal version are unknown. Page references below are arXiv pages ("S123 arXiv").
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ahmed Attia (first author), Todd Munson (all Argonne MCS, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a follow-up to the same trio's stochastic-learning approach to binary OED (ref [11], SISC 2022, p. 4). Binary (integer) design is carried into Bayesian inverse problems.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The state of the art "generally overlook[s] misspecification" of the prior and the noise covariances (abstract, p. 1). The paper adds that the max-min design "has been given little attention in model-constrained Bayesian OED" (p. 2). The problem is binary sensor placement ζ ∈ {0,1}^ns maximising a penalised A-optimal utility Tr(FIM) − αΦ(ζ) against the worst case over an admissible set Λ of covariance parameters (3.1) (pp. 3, 7). The motivation is made concrete by a one-dimensional binary toy with five λ values (Fig. 1, p. 7). There the design that is optimal at a nominal λ (ζ = 1) differs from the robust one (ζ = 0), and the lower envelope is not attained by any single λ (p. 7).
  - Transferable: open a robust-optimisation paper with a two-point design space and a handful of scenarios, which is enough to show that a nominal-value solution is wrong.
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: The paper puts the inner min inside the expectation of a Bernoulli relaxation: max_p E_{ζ∼P(ζ|p)}[min_λ J(ζ,λ)] (3.3)-(3.4) (p. 10). This builds a smooth surface through the worst-case values at the binary corners. The paper first shows that the two "intuitive" alternatives are not equivalent to each other or to (3.1): relaxing ζ to [0,1] (3.2a), and putting min_λ outside the expectation (3.2b). Their envelopes are non-differentiable and their optima are nonbinary (Fig. 2, pp. 9-10). The outer loop is a Levitin-Polyak-type exchange scheme: maximise over a finite sample Λ̂, then add the worst λ for the current design (Alg. 3.1, p. 8, ref [29]). The outer step is a projected stochastic gradient in p using the score-function gradient (3.5)-(3.6) with an optimal baseline for variance reduction (3.9) (pp. 11-12). The inner step is bound-constrained L-BFGS on λ, minimising E_ζ[J(ζ,λ)] as a stochastic stand-in for enumerating designs (pp. 12, 16). The final design is chosen as the best of m samples from the final policy (Alg. 3.2, p. 13). Kept from [11]: the Bernoulli policy and its gradient estimator. New: the robust objective and the λ-exchange loop.
  - Transferable: when a min-max over binary variables is relaxed, check on a toy that the relaxation of the robust problem is not confused with the robust version of the relaxation. Order matters (Figs. 2-3, pp. 9-10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linear(ised) Gaussian Bayesian inverse problem, posterior covariance (2.3) (p. 3), with A-optimality as Tr(FIM) (2.12) (p. 6). The covariances are parameterised as SPD Γ(λ) with λ ∈ Λ (p. 6). Uncertainty in the prior is removed for A-optimality, because the trace splits and Tr(Γ_pr(λ)⁻¹) does not depend on ζ (pp. 7-8). The paper notes this fails for D-optimality (p. 8). J must be differentiable in λ but not in ζ, and Φ may be nonsmooth (pp. 10-11). Experiments use a diagonal noise covariance diag(λ²) with λ_i ∈ [0.02, 0.04] (pp. 15-16). The authors claim, without demonstrating it, that extension to other utilities and uncertain parameters is "easily" done (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Linearity of the trace to decouple the prior term from the design, a simplification argument that changes the model (pp. 7-8). (ii) Lemma A.1, the derivative of the pseudo-inverse-weighted precision ∂W_Γ = −W_Γ ∂(W⊙Γ) W_Γ. It is proved by two cases: interior designs, via the matrix-inverse derivative, and boundary designs, via a block partition in which the zero-weight block drops out (pp. 22-23). (iii) The gradient with respect to the noise parameter (A.7)-(A.8), giving (3.8) (pp. 11, 23). (iv) The log-derivative ("kernel trick") gradient of a Bernoulli expectation (3.5)-(3.6) and the optimal-baseline formula (3.9b), both from [11] (pp. 11-12). There is no convergence theorem for Algorithm 3.2. The conclusion states that "there are no global optimality guarantees" (p. 22).
  - Transferable: for a derivative through a pseudo-inverse that becomes singular at the boundary of a box, permute the zero-weight indices into one block and show that the interior formula still holds blockwise (p. 22).
- **D5 Experiments**: A 2-D advection-diffusion contaminant model with two buildings. The velocity comes from steady Navier-Stokes. There are 16 observation times and a Laplacian-squared prior (pp. 14-15). Everything runs in PyOED, with the scripts released publicly (p. 14). Validation is against brute force over the sampled Λ̂: ns = 2 (4 designs; the global optimum (1,1) is found after 1 iteration, p. 17); ns = 5 (32 designs, ℓ0 penalty with α = 10; the recommended design matches the global optimum, pp. 17-18); ns = 10 with budget τ = 3 imposed as the penalty |‖ζ‖₀ − 3| with α = 50 (results "almost identical to the global optimum", pp. 19-20). Efficiency is tracked up to ns = 150 through the number of new function evaluations per iteration and a redundancy ratio. Memoisation is done by hashing (ζ, λ) pairs (pp. 20-21, Fig. 11). Settings are reported in full: p⁰ = 0.5, 100 outer iterations, γ₁ = 1e-4 with 5 inner steps (chosen deliberately to avoid early collapse to a degenerate policy), N_ens = N_b = 32 and m = 5 (p. 16). There are no competing robust-OED baselines and no nominal-design comparison on the PDE problem. The brute-force optimum is taken over the algorithm's own sample Λ̂, not over Λ (p. 16).
  - Transferable: validate a heuristic for a binary problem on instance sizes where enumeration is possible, and plot every design's objective under every scenario with the returned design starred (Figs. 6-7, 10).
  - Transferable: memoise expensive objective calls keyed on a hash of the (design, scenario) vector, and report a redundancy ratio to show how much reuse the sampling gives (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in robust OED (Pronzato-Walter max-min, Wald's maximin; refs [33-35, 44-46], pp. 2, 7-8). It notes that the max-min approach "has not been widely used, despite its importance, because of the computational overhead" (p. 8). It extends the authors' own [11] rather than criticising others, and the only critique of others is the general one about misspecification being overlooked (p. 1). Relaxation-plus-rounding approaches (sum-up rounding, ref [47]) are described as giving "little attention" to the effect of relaxation (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: There is a sequence of three diagrams on one toy example: nominal vs robust (Fig. 1), the two naive formulations (Fig. 2), the proposed formulation (Fig. 3) (pp. 7-10). Formal derivations go to an appendix. The scope claims are generous ("can be easily extended", p. 2), but the limits in the conclusion are concrete (p. 21-22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm and a formulation: a stochastic formulation for robust binary optimisation applied to robust A-OED (p. 21). The three limits are stated specifically (pp. 21-22): (1) the learning rate is tuned empirically, which is "not practical for large-dimensional problems"; (2) the method can be trapped by early degeneration of the policy or by nonconvexity of the expectation surface, which is mitigated by small steps and random perturbation (also p. 12); (3) there is no global optimality guarantee. Future work: automatic tuning of the step size, and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo over the activation probabilities for global optimisation (p. 22).

**Method links**: Method 3 ✅ evidence (the smallest breaking example comes first. A one-dimensional binary design with five scenarios refutes nominal design and then both naive formulations, before the proposed one is introduced; pp. 7-10). Method 6 ✅ evidence (the formulation is fixed before the method. The order of min and expectation decides differentiability and binarity, and trace linearity removes the prior uncertainty for A-optimality; pp. 7-10). Method 2 ⚠ variant (integer-design thinking is carried into Bayesian OED, but the vehicle is the coauthors' stochastic-learning gradient estimator, not his NLP/filter toolkit; pp. 4, 11). Method 5 ⚠ variant (the claims are calibrated against brute-force optima on small instances, and failure modes and the lack of guarantees are stated. But there are no external baselines, and the "global optimum" is relative to the self-generated Λ̂; pp. 16-22). Tension with SKILL.md Research Taste 3 ("a result is only as good as the guarantee behind it"): this paper ships with no convergence or optimality theory (p. 22). New-pattern candidate: exchange (scenario-growing) outer approximation for robust binary problems.
**Transferable techniques**:
- Put the worst case inside the expectation of a Bernoulli policy, max_p E[min_λ J], to get a smooth surrogate that interpolates the robust values at binary corners (pp. 10-11).
- Use a scenario-exchange loop: optimise against a finite sample Λ̂, add the adversary's best response, and repeat (Alg. 3.1-3.2, pp. 8, 13).
- Use few, small outer steps per exchange round so that the policy does not collapse to a corner before the adversary has explored (pp. 12, 16).
- Use the optimal-baseline score-function gradient for binary policies (3.9) (p. 12).
**Quotes**:
- "While these attempts to formulate the robust binary OED optimization problem are rather intuitive, the two formulations (3.2a) and (3.2b) are neither equivalent to each other nor equivalent to the original robust binary optimization problem (3.1)." (p. 9)
- "Third, while the numerical results indicate good performance of the optimization approach, there are no global optimality guarantees." (p. 22)

### S133 · Basic research needs for inverse methods for complex systems under uncertainty (US DOE Office of Science ASCR workshop report 2025, doi:10.2172/2583339)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-37 read: front matter, executive summary, §1-§3, Appendices A-C, acknowledgments p. 56). The references (pp. 38-55) were skimmed by search for Leyffer's papers and for optimisation entries. Title and DOI are confirmed on p. 3, and Leyffer is listed as one of nine "Other Contributors" on p. 3 and as a participant on p. 36.
**Role**: core (by plan) · **Voice**: committee and community. Co-chairs Donatelli, Jakeman and Shields, plus an organising committee (p. 3). Which passages Leyffer wrote is not stated anywhere in the report, so nothing below is attributed to him personally. · **Coauthors**: the 21 named co-chairs, organising-committee members and contributors (p. 3) · **Place in trajectory**: his presence in DOE agenda-setting for inverse problems, where his own papers are cited for joint multimodal inversion.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The report is agenda-setting, not a research paper. Inverse problems are central to DOE missions and are becoming more nonlinear, high-dimensional, multiphysics and data-heterogeneous, while AI and HPC open new options (pp. 5, 8). The entry point is a charge from DOE ASCR. The workshop runs for 2.5 days with breakouts, and a report is due within three months (pp. 31-32). Science drivers are given as eight vignettes: 3D reconstruction, fusion plasma, seismic digital twins, high-energy physics, materials design, nuclear structure, wildfire, quantum (pp. 11-14).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Four priority research directions, each with two thrusts in the form "Challenges and Gaps / Why Now? / Research Opportunities" (pp. 15-29). PRD 1 is about structure: exploit, discover, preserve. PRD 2 is about model misspecification: correct or calibrate during inversion, and robust solvers. PRD 3 is multimodal and dynamic data: joint registration and inversion, assimilation. PRD 4 is goal-oriented inverse problems for downstream tasks such as control, design, OED and risk. A recurring design move across PRDs is to fold a step that is usually done before or after the inversion into the inversion itself. This applies to calibration (p. 20), registration and fusion (p. 24) and the downstream task (pp. 26-28).
  - Transferable: when writing a research agenda, use the fixed three-part template "Challenges and Gaps / Why Now? / Research Opportunities" for every thrust (pp. 16-29).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: n/a as a research model. It gives working definitions of forward model, inverse problem, ill-posed, complex system, and aleatoric vs epistemic uncertainty (pp. 8-10). It sets the scale as "O(10⁶)" variables and up to "O(10⁶) CPU hours" per forward solve (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a (no theorems). Calls for "provable convergence rates" (p. 17), "convergence guarantees" (p. 19) and "strong theoretical guarantees extending beyond linear and Gaussian settings" (p. 22) appear only as research needs.
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no experiments). The evidence consists of cited literature and vignettes (pp. 11-14, 38-55).
- **D6 Positioning**: The report builds on ASCR's own past investments: exascale, SciDAC, randomized linear algebra, SciML (p. 15). Leyffer's papers are cited twice in PRD 3 Thrust A. One is ref [159] (Di, Leyffer, Wild 2016, joint X-ray fluorescence/transmission inversion), cited for combining experimental measurements with physical models (p. 23). The other is ref [190] (Di et al. 2019, simultaneous alignment and reconstruction), cited for the claim that registration inside the inversion beats preprocessing (p. 24; refs pp. 50, 52). Optimisation-method needs appear as "nonsmooth and potentially derivative-free optimization algorithms, inexact gradient methods, higher-order methods" (p. 17). Mixed-integer optimisation appears once, as a compounding difficulty in goal-oriented inverse problems (p. 27).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Programme-document register: "transformative", "unprecedented" and "Why Now?" sections (passim, e.g. pp. 8, 15). The executive summary repeats each PRD in a boxed paragraph (pp. 5-6). There is no hedging of feasibility, and the opportunities are not prioritised within PRDs.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a community roadmap: four PRDs (pp. 5-7, 30). The limits are not discussed by the report itself. Visible ones: no prioritisation across PRDs; no costed milestones; the authorship of individual sections is not recorded (pp. 3, 31-32). Future directions are the content of the report.

**Method links**: Method 1 ⚠ variant, committee voice (the report repeatedly argues for integrating calibration, registration and downstream tasks into the inversion instead of a sequential pipeline, citing Leyffer's joint-inversion papers for it; pp. 20, 23-24, 26-28. This matches SKILL.md Taste 1, "integration, not decomposition along software lines", but cannot be attributed to him). No new-pattern candidates: the voice is not attributable.
