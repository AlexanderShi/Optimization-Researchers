### S119 · Implementing a unified solver for nonlinearly constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming Computation 2026, doi:10.1007/s12532-026-00310-9; arXiv 2406.13454)
**Read level**: full. Read: all of the body, pp. 1-24 (§§1-8), Table 6 (p. 35), the UML diagram (Fig. 5, p. 36) and Algorithms 2-3 (pp. 37-38). Skimmed: the per-instance appendix Tables 3-5 (pp. 26-34), read for structure and spot-checked rows only. References (pp. 24-26) skimmed. The file is the arXiv v2 text, i.e. the "first revision" recorded as E53 in 03-process-evidence. Version wording below refers to this v2.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Charlie Vanaret (Argonne MCS and ZIB; first author, p. 1). The acknowledgements thank eight open-source contributors to Uno (p. 2). · **Place in trajectory**: the "rebuild the base" step of Method 2 (step 6). The filterSQP-era NLP toolkit (filter, trust region, restoration, BQPD) is re-cast as one modular C++ library. The aim is to carry it to equilibrium constraints and robust optimization (p. 3). It started as "Argonot" at ISMP 2018 (footnote 1, p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an observation about software, not a failing method. SQP and interior-point methods "typically share key algorithmic components", yet each is built as a separate monolithic solver (abstract, p. 1; p. 3). The problem is stated in a general form with separated nonlinear, linear and bound constraints (eq. (1), p. 2). The exposition uses the reduced form min f(x) s.t. c(x) = 0, x ≥ 0 "without loss of generality" (p. 2). The paper names four aims: a coherent hierarchy of strategies, a range of methods that can be compared on one instance, a test bed for new strategies, and lower development and maintenance cost (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable: open a methods-software paper with the redundancy across existing solvers, not with a new algorithm. Then list the aims the abstraction is meant to serve (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Lagrange-Newton methods are assembled from eight building blocks in four layers (p. 5). The reformulation layer has constraint relaxation and inequality handling. The subproblem layer has the Hessian model and inertia correction. The subproblem-solver layer has the subproblem solver. The globalization layer has the globalization strategy and the globalization mechanism. One abstract algorithm with an inner "repeat" loop (globalization mechanism) and an outer "while" loop covers them all (Algorithm 1, pp. 5-6). The "wheel of strategies" lists the concrete options under each ingredient (Fig. 1, p. 6). Each ingredient is an abstract C++ class, implemented once (§5.1, p. 14; UML Fig. 5, p. 36). New solvers are elements of the Cartesian product of the ingredients (p. 15). Two such combinations rebuild existing solvers as "presets": filtersqp (trust-region restoration filter SQP) and ipopt (line-search restoration filter IPM). The paper lists what each preset leaves out, such as second-order corrections and IPOPT's scaling, least-squares multipliers and soft restoration (p. 15). The pieces fit together because the progress measures (infeasibility η, objective ω_π, auxiliary ξ) and their predicted reductions are generated by the reformulations, not hand-coded per method (§4.1, p. 7; §5.3.3, pp. 16-17). The design keeps the classical methods unchanged. What changes is that they become parameter settings of one code.
  - Transferable: before writing a new solver, describe your class of methods as an abstract loop plus a small list of interchangeable ingredients. Implement each ingredient once behind an interface (pp. 5-6, 14).
  - Transferable: let the problem reformulation define the progress measures (η, ω_π, ξ) and their models. Then merit functions and filters are just two acceptance tests on the same quantities, and new combinations stay consistent (pp. 7-9, 16-17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f and c may be nonconvex (p. 2). An objective multiplier π ≥ 0 in a scaled (Fritz John) Lagrangian handles infeasibility and CQ failure "in a consistent way" (p. 4). The Fritz John conditions (2a)-(2d) give KKT points when π* > 0 and Fritz John points when π* = 0 (p. 4). Combinations are meaningful only if (i) subproblem solutions achieve sufficient decrease (e.g. a fraction of Cauchy decrease) or are descent directions for the KKT residual, and (ii) the reformulations are asymptotically equivalent to the original problem (p. 17). The authors admit that "all combinations do not necessarily result in sensible algorithms, or even convergent approaches" (p. 15). IPM with trust region is prohibited for now (p. 15). The inverse penalty parameter ρ on f (not on ‖c‖₁) is chosen for three stated reasons: it is easier to drive to 0, it enters the Hessian as the objective multiplier, and barrier/proximal terms stay enforced as ρ → 0 (p. 10).
- **D4 Proof devices**: n/a. The paper proves no theorems and relies on the cited convergence theory: filter SQP [19, 22], line-search filter [47, 48] (p. 19); ℓ1 exactness via Fletcher's book Thm 14.3.1 (p. 10). Its analytical device is a common notation, not a proof. The three progress measures and their predicted reductions (p. 7) let a merit function (sufficient decrease (4), p. 8) and a filter (envelope with β, γ, switching condition, pp. 8-9) be written as two acceptance rules on the same quantities. Termination is a classification of outcomes, not an optimality test: feasible KKT (π* > 0), feasible FJ (π* = 0, CQ fails), infeasible stationary point, small trust region with feasibility, or a loose tolerance met for many consecutive iterations (§5.3.7, pp. 18-19).
  - Transferable: write the termination test as a taxonomy of outcomes (KKT / Fritz John / infeasible stationary / stalled), using an objective multiplier π, so the solver's output itself says whether a CQ failed (pp. 4, 18-19).
- **D5 Experiments**: 429 small CUTE problems in AMPL translations from Neumaier's COCONUT library (p. 21, footnote 3), dimensions in Table 1 (p. 22; n at most 90, m at most 91). Baselines are named with versions: filterSQP 20010817 with BQPD, IPOPT 3.14.20 with MA57 3.10.0, SNOPT 7.5-1.2, MINOS 5.51, LANCELOT, LOQO 7.03, CONOPT 3.17A (p. 21). The unit is objective evaluations, not CPU time (p. 21). Dolan-Moré performance profiles compare each preset with its original and then all solvers together (Fig. 4, p. 23). A shifted geometric mean (s = 10) counts a failure as 10⁶ evaluations (p. 24). Table 2: filtersqp preset 18.08 vs filterSQP 18.73; ipopt preset 48.45 vs IPOPT 34.63; CONOPT 43.15, SNOPT 61.02, LOQO 71.44, LANCELOT 92.27, MINOS 159.78 (p. 24). Failures are defined: IPOPT's "too few degrees of freedom" counts as failure (p. 21). The paper names 12 instances solved by IPOPT but not by the ipopt preset and attributes them, "most likely", to the unimplemented features (p. 23). Per-instance counts are split into LPs, QPs and NLPs (Tables 3-5, pp. 26-34). All log files are public in a repository (p. 21). Missing: CPU times, large-scale instances, and any test of a new combination (the v1 §7.2 combination is absent from v2, consistent with E53).
  - Transferable: validate a framework by imitation. Rebuild two reference solvers as presets, list what each preset omits, compare each against its original on the same instances in a per-solver profile, and attribute every residual failure to an omitted feature (pp. 15, 21-23).
  - Transferable: when solvers differ in linear algebra and language, compare them on function evaluations, charge failures a fixed large count in a shifted geometric mean, and publish the logs (pp. 21, 24).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continuity, not criticism. Fifteen solvers are placed in one table along the framework's axes (constraint relaxation, inequality handling, globalization strategy and mechanism, Hessian model, inertia correction) (§4.7, p. 13; Table 6, p. 35). The table exposes a gap: "FICO XSLP is the only solver that does not implement a proper globalization strategy" (p. 14). The SQP classification follows Nocedal-Wright [39] (p. 11). The inverse-penalty notation follows Byrd-Curtis-Nocedal [9] (p. 10). The filter material cites the authors' own line (Fletcher-Leyffer manuals and convergence papers [19-22]) together with Wächter-Biegler [47-49] (pp. 8, 19). The authors' own earlier survey work (Gould-Leyffer 2003; Leyffer-Mahajan 2010) is cited as the common ground (p. 3).
  - Transferable: place the field's existing solvers in one table along the axes of your framework. The empty or odd cells become findings (Table 6, p. 35; p. 14).
- **D7 Writing**: The contribution is stated as a framework ("eight building blocks") plus software (abstract, p. 1). Two figures carry the idea: the colour-coded "wheel of strategies" (Fig. 1, p. 6) and multicolour pseudocode in which each line is tagged by its ingredient (Algorithms 2-3, pp. 37-38). The text says the complete pseudocode is there to "highlight the complexity of algorithm design" in contrast with the "basic (simplistic)" Algorithm 1 (p. 19). Hedging is moderate: "We believe that Uno has the potential" (pp. 3, 24); "preliminary numerical results" (p. 3). The abstract says Uno is "highly competitive" (p. 1), while §7 says the presets "mimic" their originals and "perform well with respect to all the state-of-the-art solvers" (p. 23). So even in v2 the abstract claims more than the body. Limitations are listed in the text: IPM with trust region is prohibited (p. 15), presets omit features (p. 15), there are no quasi-Newton Hessians yet (p. 18), and the planned features are named (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a framework/taxonomy and software (Uno 2.2.0, about 10,000 lines, MIT licence; C, Julia, Python, Fortran, AMPL interfaces, p. 14). The convergence theory is cited, not new. Limits are specific, and several are stated by the authors. Arbitrary combinations may not converge (p. 15). The ipopt preset is weaker than IPOPT (SGM 48.45 vs 34.63, p. 24; 12 failures, p. 23). Tests use small problems only (Table 1, p. 22). The unit is evaluations only (p. 21). The filter must be flushed when μ changes, with a planned alternative (p. 21). Future work: L-BFGS/SR1, MINRES, SLP-EQP, SQuID's Sℓ1QP, a parallel line search, more subproblem-solver interfaces (p. 24), and a GALAHAD TRS interface (p. 15).

### S126 · A globally convergent method for computing B-stationary points of mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints (Computational Optimization and Applications 2026, doi:10.1007/s10589-026-00832-x; arXiv 2501.13835)
**Read level**: full. Read: all of the body, pp. 1-38 (§§1-6, with every proof). Appendix A (proofs of Lemmas 3.1-3.2, pp. 39-40), Appendix B (B-stationarity check of competitors' points, p. 40), Appendix C (LPEC-solver comparison, pp. 40-41) and Appendix D (random MPEC generator, pp. 41-43) were all read. References (pp. 44-47) skimmed. The text is arXiv v5 (8 Sep 2026).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Armin Nurkanović (Freiburg, Systems Control and Optimization Laboratory; first author, p. 1). The implementation lives in the nosnoc GitHub organisation, in CasADi/Matlab (pp. 5, 32). Anton Pozharskiy is thanked for translating MacMPEC to CasADi (p. 38). · **Place in trajectory**: the MPEC line (Fletcher-Leyffer 2004 MPECs as NLPs; Leyffer-Munson 2007 SLPEC filter [46]; Kirches-Larson-Leyffer-Manns 2022 [44]; Kim-Leyffer-Munson 2020 [43]) carried to a certified B-stationary method. MINLP enters only as a tool (MILP for LPECs) and as a baseline (Bonmin).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap between what methods compute and what is necessary for optimality. The NLP form (2) of an MPEC is "numerically and theoretically degenerate": MFCQ fails at every feasible point, so multiplier sets are unbounded (pp. 2-3). Weaker stationarity concepts "allow first-order descent directions, making them too weak as practical stopping criteria" (p. 3). Regularization and penalty methods may converge to non-B-stationary points (p. 3). Active-set methods need MPEC-LICQ (p. 3). LPEC-based methods certify B-stationarity but are complex to implement (p. 4). The problem (1) has twice continuously differentiable f, c, complementarity on variable pairs (general g ⊥ h via slacks), and equality constraints omitted for light notation (p. 2). Remark 2.1 sets the bar: without MPEC-LICQ, B-stationarity cannot be verified by multipliers, so an LPEC certificate is needed (p. 9).
  - Transferable: enter a crowded method area through the stopping criterion. Ask which stationarity concept the existing methods can certify and whether it still allows descent directions (pp. 3, 9).
- **D2 Core idea**: MPECopt is a two-phase hybrid active-set method (pp. 4, 10). Phase I (Algorithm 2, p. 20) runs Scholtes' regularization Reg(τ). At a nearly feasible x*(τ) it builds an LPEC with trust region ρ_I. Any feasible LPEC point defines an active set whose branch NLP (BNLP) is feasible; τ shrinks only if this fails. Phase II (Algorithm 3, p. 22) works from a feasible iterate. It solves LPEC(x^k, ρ) (eq. (15), trust region ‖d‖∞ ≤ ρ). If d = 0, a B-stationary point is certified. Otherwise the step's partition picks a BNLP, and its stationary point is accepted only if f decreases; else ρ shrinks. The design choice: "the LPECs steps never update the iterates, but only provide better active sets or certify B-stationarity" (p. 4). This keeps all Phase II iterates feasible and needs no globalization of LPEC steps (p. 11). LPECs are solved as big-M MILPs (18). M = max(x₁, x₂) + ρ suffices because the trust region dominates when ρ < M/2, so the relaxation is tight (pp. 15-16). Integer preprocessing fixes y_i for non-biactive indices (p. 15), and a feasible binary vector from the previous BNLP gives an incumbent (p. 16). The LPEC needs to be solved to optimality only to certify the final point. Otherwise its first feasible (Phase I) or first improving (Phase II) point suffices (pp. 4, 15). Differences from the closest work are spelled out: unlike Lin-Fukushima it uses LPECs instead of multipliers, so MPEC-LICQ is not needed; unlike Kazi et al. it never applies LPEC steps directly (p. 4).
  - Transferable: let the cheap combinatorial model choose the branch and let the NLP solver choose the point. Never step to the model's point, so feasibility is kept and no globalization of the model step is needed (pp. 4, 11).
  - Transferable: in a big-M MILP inside a trust-region method, set M from the trust-region radius and the current point. The trust region then makes the relaxation tight (pp. 15-16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 4.1: a compact X contains the feasible set; unboundedness is detected by an unbounded BNLP (p. 23). Assumption 4.2: MPEC-MFCQ at every feasible point (p. 23); it implies MPEC-ACQ, which Example 3.1 shows cannot be dropped (pp. 11-12, 23). Assumption 4.3 is an explicit assumption on the NLP solver (p. 24): from a feasible nonstationary start near x̄, it returns a stationary point with decrease at least α_x̄ (28), or a stationary point of the feasibility problem. It is justified by the global convergence theorems of Chin-Fletcher, Fletcher-Leyffer-Toint and Wächter-Biegler, and by named solvers, including filterSQP and Uno (pp. 24-25). A gap is admitted: a restoration phase could return a worse objective. "We did not observe this in any of our numerical experiments and therefore do not treat it further"; a remedy constraint f(x) ≤ f(x^k) + ε is suggested (p. 25). Finite termination additionally needs isolated BNLP stationary points (p. 30), argued to be mild under second-order sufficiency (p. 31). BNLPs are assumed solved to full convergence (p. 38). The LPEC can always be solved or certified infeasible by B&B MILP solvers (p. 25).
- **D4 Proof devices**:
  - Radius threshold ρ̄ (16): below it, the reduced LPEC (12) and the full LPEC (15) have the same local minimizers. Inactive linearizations stay inactive by Cauchy-Schwarz, and branches of strictly active pairs become unreachable (Lemma 3.1, p. 13; proof in App. A, p. 39).
  - Feasibility of the predicted BNLP: the MPEC-linearized cone decomposes into the BNLPs' linearized cones (41) under MPEC-ACQ, and P(x+d) ⊆ P(x) for small ρ (Lemma 3.2, p. 14; App. A, pp. 39-40).
  - Tight LP relaxation at S-stationary points. The LP relaxation of the MILP coincides with the LP of the relaxed NLP's tangent cone, so d = 0 is optimal for both, and certification costs one LP (Proposition 3.1, pp. 16-17).
  - Strict interior descent direction under MFCQ: s(t) = s̄ + t·λd with λ large, normalised to ‖s‖∞ = 1 with margin ε (Proposition 4.2, pp. 25-26).
  - Perturbation from a feasible point: an LPEC at a nearby infeasible x̃ is feasible for ρ in [max{h_c/ε, h⊥}, κ]. The ball inclusion B(x̃, ρ) ⊂ B(x*, ρ̄) transfers Lemma 3.2 (Theorem 4.1, pp. 26-28, Fig. 4). For Phase I, δ = √τ̄ comes from x₁x₂ ≤ τ (Corollary 4.1, p. 29).
  - Finite inner loop: geometric trust-region shrinkage reaches ρ < ρ̄, where any nonzero LPEC solution is a common descent direction of all reachable BNLPs (Proposition 4.3, p. 29). Cone scaling d = t·d^k then lets the MILP stop at any nonzero feasible point (Corollary 4.2, p. 30).
  - Three exclusive outcomes (Theorem 4.2, pp. 30-31): the objective is monotone and bounded below, and the solver's sufficient decrease (28) near a non-B-stationary accumulation point gives a contradiction. The proof "follows similar lines" to Chin-Fletcher Thm 1 and Fletcher-Leyffer-Toint Thm 7 case (C) (p. 30). Finite termination follows from finitely many BNLPs times isolated stationary points (p. 31).
  - Small worked examples, each with a figure: Example 3.1 (2-D; LPEC cannot verify geometric B-stationarity when MPEC-ACQ fails, pp. 11-12); Example 3.2 (two S-stationary points; a larger trust region finds the better branch, pp. 13-14); Example 3.3 (a = 1.1; the LPEC predicts an infeasible branch unless τ is small, pp. 19-20).
  - Transferable: prove that an inexact solve of the combinatorial subproblem is enough (first feasible point in Phase I, first descent point in Phase II), and solve exactly only for the final certificate. Then measure the predicted saving (Cor. 4.1-4.2, pp. 29-30; Figs. 6, 8).
  - Transferable: state the inner NLP solver's behaviour as a named assumption (outcomes plus a sufficient-decrease constant), cite the theorems and solvers that deliver it, and say which case you do not treat (Assumption 4.3, pp. 24-25).
- **D5 Experiments**: Two benchmarks. MacMPEC: 191 problems from the AMPL library on Leyffer's wiki (p. 33). Synthetic: 75 random nonlinear MPECs with 200 to 4,000 complementarity pairs and 500 to 10,000 variables, built from random LPECs (Hu et al.) plus CUTEst unconstrained objectives, polynomial-ised constraints and duplicated inequalities "specifically designed to violate the MPEC-LICQ" (pp. 35-36; generator App. D, pp. 41-43). Six methods are compared: two MPECopt variants, Scholtes regularization, the ℓ∞ penalty, the plain NLP with IPOPT, and the big-M MINLP in Bonmin (p. 34). Same-solver rule: IPOPT with MA27 and identical non-default options for all methods and all NLPs (p. 32). "Since all methods use IPOPT as the NLP solver, we can directly compare the computation times." (p. 34). The success criterion is a B-stationary point. Other methods' outputs are checked post hoc by a stated procedure (App. B, p. 40), and the cost of that check is not charged to them (p. 34). Absolute timing profiles (wall seconds, no per-problem scaling) are shown beside Dolan-Moré profiles (p. 33; Figs. 5, 7). MacMPEC success rates: MPECopt-Reg-Gurobi 94.24%, Reg 93.2%, ℓ∞ 92.14%, NLP 90.00%, MINLP 72.52%. The MINLP stopped at feasible non-B-stationary points in 39 cases (p. 34). Synthetic set: MPECopt-Reg-Gurobi 96.16%, MPECopt-ℓ∞ and Pen-ℓ∞ and Reg 94.67%, NLP 16.00%, mostly 45-minute timeouts (pp. 36-37). The MINLP is excluded there "as it was already not competitive" (p. 36). Failure causes are reported: timeouts, failed restoration, iteration limits (pp. 34, 37). Theory is checked by histograms of MILP node counts, exact vs early-terminated: with early termination only LPs were needed on MacMPEC, and in Phase I a single LP always sufficed on the synthetic set (Figs. 6, 8; pp. 34-37). App. C: MILP-based LPEC solves are up to two orders of magnitude faster than regularization-based LPEC solves, and HiGHS fails on siouxfls and siouxfls1 (pp. 40-41). Omitted variants (ℓ1 penalty, HiGHS) are named, with their outcomes, and left out of the plots only (p. 34). Code and data are public (pp. 33, 38).
  - Transferable: when your method returns a certificate and the baselines do not, grade every method by the same certificate after the run. Describe the checking procedure in an appendix, and do not charge its cost to the baselines (p. 34; App. B, p. 40).
  - Transferable: when the standard library is too easy, write a public generator for large instances that violate the assumption your method is designed to survive (here MPEC-LICQ) (pp. 35-36; App. D).
  - Transferable: test a theoretical complexity claim directly by histogramming the subproblem work (MILP nodes) with and without the theory-licensed early stop (Figs. 6, 8).
- **D6 Positioning**: The survey divides the field into regularization/penalty, active-set/combinatorial and hybrid methods (pp. 3-4). Criticism is phrased as assumptions and stationarity strength, not as numerical failure. Regularization "may converge to points that are not B-stationary" unless restrictive assumptions hold (p. 3). Multiplier-based active-set methods need MPEC-LICQ (p. 3). Guo-Deng prove M-stationarity, which "may still admit descent directions" (p. 4). For LPEC methods, including the authors' own [44, 46]: "A main drawback of these methods is their implementation complexity" (p. 4). Remark 3.1 separates the method from MINLP (pp. 17-18). The big-M MINLP converges globally when the relaxation is convex, but B&B gap termination and OA's multi-point MILPs do not map to B-stationarity. Example 3.2's MINLP relaxation is a convex QP on which MPECopt finds only a B-stationary point. The Acta Numerica survey [6] is the MINLP reference (p. 18). Hu et al. [30, 31] and Jara-Moroni et al. [35] are credited for the MILP view of LPECs and the random-LPEC recipe (pp. 4, 15, 41).
  - Transferable: when your method uses MINLP machinery, write a remark on why it is not an MINLP algorithm: what its termination certifies, what the MINLP's termination certifies, and a small example where they differ (Remark 3.1, pp. 17-18).
- **D7 Writing**: A contributions paragraph comes at the end of the introduction (pp. 4-5). Every key claim is shown on a two-variable example with a figure before it is proved (Examples 3.1-3.3). Surprise is marked: "Interestingly" (pp. 5, 38), "Remarkably" (p. 29), "Somewhat surprisingly" (p. 41). Claims are calibrated: the results section concludes that "MPECopt is competitive" (p. 37), and the conclusion says "generally faster and more reliable" (p. 37). An unexplained empirical regularity is stated as open: regularization limits are usually B-stationary even when not S-stationary, and "current convergence results for these methods do not explain this phenomenon" (p. 38). The text admits that "all methods – sometimes with minor tuning – can successfully compute B-stationary points on most MacMPEC problems" (p. 35).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithm, convergence theory, open-source code and a new public benchmark generator (pp. 4-5, 33, 38, 41). Limits stated by the authors: MPEC-MFCQ everywhere (p. 23); a worse-objective restoration case not treated (p. 25); BNLPs solved exactly (p. 38); only B-stationary, not global, points, even when the MINLP relaxation is convex (p. 18); worst-case exponential LPECs (p. 15); HiGHS LPEC failures on two problems (p. 40). Future: inexact BNLP solves "while maintaining robust and fast convergence" (p. 38). An open question for theory: why regularization methods end at B-stationary points (p. 38).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Make the algorithm's termination a certificate (d = 0 solves the LPEC), and grade every competitor by the same certificate post hoc, with the checking protocol in an appendix (pp. 9, 34, 40).
- Use the combinatorial model only to choose an active set; accept only points produced by the NLP solver with a strict objective decrease; shrink a trust region on rejection (Algorithm 3, p. 22; p. 11).
- Bound big-M by the trust region (M = max(x₁, x₂) + ρ, ρ < M/2), fix integers that the trust region makes unreachable, and seed an incumbent from the last subproblem (pp. 15-16).
- Prove which subproblem solves can stop early, then show the saving with node-count histograms for exact vs early-stopped solves (pp. 29-30, 34-37).
- State the inner solver's required behaviour as a numbered assumption with a sufficient-decrease constant, back it with cited theorems and named solvers, and say which case you do not treat (pp. 24-25).
- Build a public generator for large instances that violate your method's competitors' key assumption, and keep the old library as the easy set (pp. 35-36, 41-43).
- Show absolute wall-time profiles beside relative performance profiles, and run every method on the same NLP solver with the same options (pp. 32-34).
