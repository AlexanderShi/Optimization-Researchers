### S078 · Computing sparse Hessian and Jacobian approximations with optimal hereditary properties (Large-Scale Optimization with Applications, Part II, IMA Vol. 1997, doi:10.1007/978-1-4612-1960-6_3)
**Read level**: full. All 14 pages read; I followed the matrix displays of the tridiagonal example (p. 6) only as far as the extraction allows.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Roger Fletcher, Andreas Grothey (p. 1; preprint dated 16 Oct 1996) · **Place in trajectory**: early Dundee NLP-side work on quasi-Newton approximations for large sparse problems, before filterSQP. It is not MINLP work.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For large sparse problems, Hessians are costly (AD gives gradients cheaply, not Hessians, p. 2). Dense quasi-Newton updates fill in, limited-memory updates ignore sparsity, and Toint's and Fletcher's sparse updates assume a non-sparse diagonal (pp. 2, 7). The aim is a sparse B that satisfies all m recent quasi-Newton conditions BΓ = Δ at once (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when existing updates satisfy only one secant condition, ask whether the whole bundle of recent difference pairs can be fitted at once, in the least-squares sense.
- **D2 Core idea**: Pose the estimate as a constrained Procrustes problem: minimize ||BΓ − Δ||_F² subject to symmetry and the sparsity pattern (CPP, p. 3). Its first-order conditions give a Lyapunov-type equation. Vectorized, this becomes a Kronecker-sum system, which is reduced to the lower-triangular non-sparse entries, PᵀKPb′ = w′ (pp. 4-5). The reduced system is solved by CG with matrix-free products in under 7mL flops, warm-started from the previous approximation and solved inexactly early on (pp. 11-12).
  - Transferable: if a matrix-estimation problem is a convex QP in the matrix entries, write its KKT system in vec/Kronecker form, then project onto the free entries to get a sparse SPD-or-PSD system.
- **D3 Assumptions**: f ∈ C², r ∈ C¹, sparsity pattern S symmetric (p. 2). Uniqueness needs the row-independence condition of Theorem 1 (p. 7). Theorem 2's near-converse assumes a non-sparse diagonal and an ordering with no LDLᵀ fill-in (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis**: Theorem 1 (sufficient condition: an ordering in which the rows of Γ indexed by each column's non-zeros are linearly independent) is proved by contradiction, one column at a time (p. 7). Theorem 2 (almost necessary) builds a null vector from the largest dependent index j0, with the no-fill-in assumption keeping it in the sparse space (pp. 7-8). The Jacobian case is an iff, Theorem 3 (p. 10). Quadratic termination in at most max_j |T*_j| steps (p. 9). A worked tridiagonal example shows that linear independence of Γ's columns alone is not enough (p. 6).
  - Transferable: pair a sufficient condition with a near-necessary one under a stated structural assumption (here, no fill-in), and add a three-variable example that shows the weaker condition fails.
- **D5 Experiments**: Matlab trust-region code; chained Rosenbrock and a boundary value problem at n = 10 and 100; compared with Nocedal's L-BFGS (m = 2, 5) and with Newton's method; iterations, gradient calls and function calls reported (pp. 12-13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic plus theoretical. The limits are stated plainly: no gain over Nocedal's method despite using sparsity, Hessian cost about 70mL flops per iteration, an order of magnitude more than L-BFGS, and iteration counts called only "a rough idea" because trust-region and line-search iterations differ in cost (p. 13). No obvious sparsity in PᵀKP, and Theorem 1's condition is hard to verify (p. 11). Future work: make it efficient, and embed it in a trust-region SQP (p. 13).

**Method links**: Method 5 evidence ✅: reports that the method loses, and states the weakness of the iteration meter (p. 13). Method 3 evidence ✅ (small form): a three-variable example marks the edge of a sufficient condition (pp. 6, 9). Method 2 evidence ✅ (weak): the stated next step is to carry the update into SQP (p. 13).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When the cost per iteration differs between methods, say that iteration counts give only a rough comparison, and report the flop cost of each iteration beside them (p. 13).
- Warm-start the inner iterative solve from the previous outer iteration's answer, and tighten its tolerance as the outer method converges (p. 11).
**Quotes**:
- "Overall, we have been rather disappointed by these preliminary experiments" (p. 13)
- "It should not come as a surprise that Newton's method is the best of the three methods since it utilizes the most problem information (exact Hessian) of the three methods." (p. 13)

### S075 · Comparison of certain MINLP algorithms when applied to a model structure determination and parameter estimation problem (Computers & Chemical Engineering 1998, doi:10.1016/s0098-1354(98)00238-5)
**Read level**: full. All 13 pages read; the figures are known only from captions and axis labels.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Hans Skrifvars and Tapio Westerlund (Åbo Akademi; Westerlund is the corresponding author, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an early applied comparison with the Åbo ECP group. Leyffer's contribution is the QP-based MIQP B&B code (Fletcher and Leyffer NA/151), pp. 6, 11.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Choosing the structure of a model by minimizing Akaike's information criterion (AIC) is an MINLP. The application is FTIR spectroscopy: which wave numbers enter an inverse calibration model c = Pa (pp. 1-2, 6-7). The full problem would have 16,800 variables; averaging gives 600- and 1,200-variable instances with 2^600 ≈ 10^180 possible structures (pp. 7-8).
- **D2 Core idea**: Binary variables switch each parameter on, with bounds tied to them (pp. 3-4). For a known covariance the objective J1 is convex (an MIQP). For an unknown covariance J2 is nonconvex, so they solve J1 repeatedly and re-estimate the covariance by ML after each solve, a relaxation procedure (p. 4). OA, ECP and the QP-based B&B (pp. 5-6) are then compared on these MIQPs.
  - Transferable: when the exact formulation is nonconvex only through a nuisance parameter, fix that parameter, solve the convex problem to global optimality, update the parameter in an outer loop, and say that this outer loop has no convergence proof.
- **D3 Assumptions**: linear model; normal i.i.d. residuals; convexity for OA and ECP global optimality (p. 5); R̂0 = I to start (p. 4). The relaxation is admitted to be non-rigorous: "no proof for convergence has been given so far" (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no new theorems; the convergence properties of OA and ECP are cited, p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: two FTIR instances, bound curves against both CPU time and number of function evaluations (Figs 2-6, pp. 8-11). The two meters reverse the ranking. 100 absorbances: B&B about 15 s, OA 60 s, ECP 480 s (p. 8), but ECP needs 65 evaluations against OA's about 2000 and B&B's about 6000 (p. 9). 200 absorbances: B&B 130 s and 24,000 evaluations, OA 4700 s and 7700, ECP 4400 s and 72 (p. 11). For B&B, Hessian-vector products are counted in place of evaluations, which also counts tableau pivots, and the paper says so (p. 9). DECstation; the CPLEX MILP solver for OA and ECP (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a comparison on one application. The conclusion is a profile of bottlenecks, not a winner: ECP's time goes into the MILPs, B&B's into function evaluations, and "all methods can be recommended" (p. 12). Limits: one problem class; J2 is not treated (p. 4); the relaxation is unproved (p. 4).

**Method links**: Method 5 evidence ✅: two meters, a caveat on what the B&B counter actually counts, and a conclusion by bottleneck, not a winner (pp. 9, 12). Method 6 evidence ✅: the convex J1 is chosen over the nonconvex J2, with the nonconvexity pushed into an outer loop (pp. 4, 7). Method 4 evidence ✅ (indirect): the same model is listed in S039 as a "large convex MIQP" application (S039 p. 2).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Plot bounds against two meters (CPU time and function evaluations), and say which bottleneck each meter shows (pp. 8-9, 12).
- When a solver cannot report the shared meter, name the proxy you counted and what else it includes (p. 9).
**Quotes**:
- "In the case of the QPbased B&B method it is not possible to count function evaluations separately." (p. 9)
- "The ECP spends most of the time in solving the MILP, while the B&B again spends most time in solving the non-linear functions." (p. 12)

### S026 · A bundle filter method for nonsmooth nonlinear optimization (Dundee report NA/195, 1999, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 16 pages read: statement, algorithm, and all the lemmas and proofs.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Roger Fletcher (p. 1, dated 22 Dec 1999) · **Place in trajectory**: the filter (NA/171, 1997) carried into nonsmooth convex optimization, motivated by decomposition master problems. It is a companion to S055.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: minimize a convex nonsmooth f subject to a convex nonsmooth c(x) ≤ 0 over a polyhedron (p. 1). The entry is an application plus a known defect. Such problems are Benders master problems, for example in stochastic NLP (p. 2). Kiwiel's approach, the nearest relative, uses an exact penalty, which "introduces numerical difficulties" because the bundle sees subgradients of widely varying size (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: An SLP bundle method with a trust region (LP(x(k), j, ρ), p. 5). The penalty is replaced by a filter with a tilted envelope (β, γ) (p. 7). A sufficient-reduction test splits steps into f-type and h-type (p. 7). Null steps add cuts when the trial point is rejected (p. 8). A restoration phase handles inconsistent LPs (p. 9). Changed from Kiwiel: the globalization. Unchanged: the cutting-plane model (pp. 2, 4).
  - Transferable: to carry a globalization device into a new class, keep the class's standard model (here the bundle LP) intact and swap only the acceptance test.
- **D3 Assumptions**: A1 bounded polyhedral X; A2 convex, locally Lipschitz f and c; A3 an oracle returning one arbitrary subgradient at each point (pp. 1-2). A single constraint, with more handled by a max or by one bundle per constraint (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis**: nonsmooth Fritz-John conditions by contradiction, using upper semicontinuity of the subdifferential and the mean-value theorem (Lemma 2.1, p. 3), extended to ε-subgradients (Cor. 2.2, p. 4). The inner iteration is finite: dividing the LP by ρ gives a ρ-independent LP over the tangent cone (Lemma 4.1, pp. 9-10). Null steps change the LP by at least σ2Δl or βτ (Remark 4.2, pp. 10-11). Case split A-D (p. 11). Limit points are feasible by citing Chin and Fletcher's report (Lemma 4.3, p. 11). The LP is consistent for ρ in [h(k)/ε, κ] (Lemma 4.4, pp. 12-13). Lemma 4.5 and Theorem 4.6 follow (pp. 13-15).
  - Transferable: to show an inner loop is finite as the trust region shrinks, rescale the subproblem by the radius, so that its solution no longer depends on ρ and the predicted reduction becomes linear in ρ (pp. 9-10).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory and algorithm only; no numerics. The key feasibility lemma rests on an unpublished report, listed as "A really cool paper on filter methods", NA/XXX (pp. 11, 15). The extension to several constraints is asserted, not analysed (p. 2). The restoration phase is only sketched ("a number of traditional techniques", p. 9).

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅: the home device (the filter) is carried into nonsmooth NLP, with the claim that it avoids penalty estimation and allows useful nonmonotonicity (p. 2). Taste 3 evidence ✅: the paper's substance is a global convergence proof (pp. 9-15). Heuristic 3 contradiction ✗ (mild): theory comes before any numbers here (pp. 1-15; no experiments).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Use a case split (restoration fails / optimum found / infinitely many serious steps / infinitely many null steps), and prove one lemma per case (p. 11).
**Quotes**:
- "Instead the novel concept of a filter [6] is used to force global convergence." (p. 2)
- "Filter methods have several advantages over penalty function methods." (p. 2)

### S055 · A note on feasibility in Benders decomposition (Dundee report NA/188, 1999, rev. 2000, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 13 pages read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Andreas Grothey, Ken McKinnon (Edinburgh), p. 1; dated 15 Jan 1999, revised 24 Mar 1999 and 1 Jun 2000 · **Place in trajectory**: Benders for large NLPs, from the Edinburgh decomposition collaboration. It is the counterexample half of what S026 supplies the bundle machinery for.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Benders decomposition used to split large NLPs. The convergence theorems of Geoffrion and of Flippo-Rinnooy Kan assume Y finite, all subproblems feasible, or finite dual sets, which "appears to rule Benders Decomposition out" for general NLPs (p. 3). The entry is a counterexample: a convex NLP in three variables (P1), two circles of radius √ln(y) (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Feasibility cuts from infeasible subproblems carry no objective information, so the iterates creep toward feasibility and never reach it (p. 6). Fix: when S(y) is infeasible, also solve a relaxed NLP with the constraints loosened to m1·ĉ⁺ (m1 > 1), which satisfies Slater. Its multipliers give an extra optimality cut (pp. 6-7). The algorithm differs from Geoffrion's only in that step (p. 7).
  - Transferable: when a decomposition stalls on infeasible subproblems, enlarge the subproblem until it has an interior, and take objective cuts from it as well as feasibility cuts.
- **D3 Assumptions**: A1 f and c convex and C²; A2 X and Y compact polyhedra; A3 Slater (p. 2). Convexity is said to be relaxable by heuristics that shift cuts down (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis**: closed-form recursion: from 1 ≤ y(k) < e, Benders gives y(k+1) = (2 − ln y(k)) y(k) < e. Proved by the substitution y = eγ and monotonicity of (1 − ln γ)γ (Lemma 2.1, p. 5). The limit is e, feasible but non-stationary (p. 5). Hypothesis check: the example violates Geoffrion's all-feasible assumption and Flippo-Rinnooy Kan's finiteness, so the published theorems are not wrong (§2.1, p. 5). New cuts underestimate v(y) by aggregating OA linearizations with KKT multipliers (Lemma 3.4, p. 9). Theorem 3.1 (pp. 9-10).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (the analytic example only).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: counterexample plus repair. The gap is admitted in print: Theorem 3.1 only excludes f̂ > f* + ε1·m, and "it is possible for ε1m to be of order 1" (Remark 3.3, p. 10; Remark 3.1, p. 8). §3.2 reads like working notes left in the report: "This is Roger's approach", "I am not sure that adding a constraint ... helps us much", and an open question whether this argues against decomposition at the NLP level (pp. 11-12). Exact-penalty alternative rejected: unknown parameter, and no l1QP solvers (p. 7).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅ (the canonical case, already [03 E44]): a smallest convex example, a hypothesis check before claiming an error (p. 5), then a redesign (p. 6). Workflow B checkpoint evidence ✅: an unclosed gap is written down (p. 10), not argued away. Method 6 evidence ✅ (partial): the failure is traced to how infeasible subproblems are formulated, not to Benders as such (pp. 3, 6). New-pattern candidate: "admit the residual gap of your own theorem in print" (Remark 3.3, p. 10; working notes, pp. 11-12).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the counterexample so that the iteration has a closed-form recursion, then prove the limit with a one-variable monotonicity argument (pp. 4-5).
- After the counterexample, add a paragraph that checks it against each cited theorem's hypotheses, and say which hypothesis it violates (p. 5).
- State what your convergence theorem does not exclude, with the size of the residual gap (Remark 3.3, p. 10).
**Quotes**:
- "At a first glance, the example seems to indicate that there are errors in the various convergence results in the literature." (p. 5)
- "Is this maybe an argument against decomposition (at least at the NLP level)?" (p. 12)

### S039 · Solving large MINLPs on computational grids (Optimization and Engineering 2002, doi:10.1023/a:1021047328089)
**Read level**: full. All 22 pages read: the Dundee NA/200 preprint version dated 24 May 2001 (p. 1), not the journal typesetting. The text layer drops every "c" and the "fi/ff" ligatures; it is still readable throughout.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jean-Pierre Goux (Artelys), p. 1 · **Place in trajectory**: the first parallel version of his MINLP_BB-style nonlinear B&B with filterSQP. Condor/MW infrastructure from the Argonne-Wisconsin metaNEOS circle (acknowledgements, p. 17); it predates FilMINT.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: large MINLPs whose B&B trees run to 10^9 NLPs (pp. 13-14). The platform is a computational grid of idle, heterogeneous, unreliable workstations (p. 3). The issues "unique to MINLP" are said to make it worth doing after parallel MILP work (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: master-worker B&B in which each task is an MINLP subtree, not a single NLP. The first one-NLP-per-task version failed because warm-started NLPs solved too fast for the master (pp. 8, 14). Workers search depth-first and stop at fathoming, at an integer solution or at a time limit (10 or 100 s). They then get a recourse period to fathom nodes with g_rel ≤ 0.5 (p. 9). The master runs lazy best-first with pool-size hysteresis (pp. 9-10, 15). B&B was chosen over OA and Benders because the tasks are independent, need less synchronization and are all of one type (p. 6).
  - Transferable: size the parallel task from the inner solver's measured cost (here 1-2 QPs per warm-started node, p. 14), not from the algorithm's natural unit.
- **D3 Assumptions**: compact X and Y, f and g C². No convexity is assumed; global optimality is guaranteed only when each NLP is solved globally (pp. 2, 4).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no theorems). Design arguments only: the NLP solver needs robustness under degeneracy, fast and reliable infeasibility detection by a feasibility problem, and warm starts (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: 146 Condor machines; 10 problems from MacMINLP (core reload, cyclic scheduling, space truss, stockcycle, trim loss), AMPL models online (pp. 12-13). The meter is parallel efficiency, not speed-up, with the reason stated (pp. 12-13). Efficiency reaches 80% and exceeds 40% for most problems (p. 14). trimlon7 needs about 10^9 NLPs, a 10^-43 fraction of the tree (pp. 13-14). The non-monotone lower bound on nonconvex trimlon7 is reported (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: software plus computational evidence. Claimed: "up to two orders of magnitude" faster than serial (pp. 14, 16). My own check: the work-time/job-time ratios in Table 3 are at most about 66 (trimloss4: 321.52/4.88) and about 49 (trimlon7) (p. 13), so the claim rounds up. The paper also says speed-up cannot be measured on a grid (p. 12). Limits: B&B ignores convexity guarantees on nonconvex instances (pp. 4, 16). Future work: early branching (2-3x, less reliable on nonconvex problems) and parallel cut generation (p. 17).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: their own C++ B&B with a pluggable NLP solver, designed around filterSQP's warm-start and infeasibility-detection properties (pp. 5, 8). Heuristic 7 evidence ✅: the requirements on the NLP solver are derived from what the B&B tree needs (p. 5). Method 4 evidence ✅: the test set is his MacMINLP collection with models online (pp. 13, 21). Method 5 variant ⚠: the choice of meter is justified (pp. 12-13), but the "two orders of magnitude" headline is not backed by any ratio in Table 3 (pp. 13-14). New-pattern candidate: "size parallel tasks from the inner solver's measured cost" (pp. 8-9, 14).
**Transferable techniques**:
- On a platform where speed-up is not measurable, report parallel efficiency = work-time / (average workers × wall time), and say why speed-up is not used (pp. 12-13).
- Give a worker a "recourse period" to finish small subtrees (relative gap ≤ 0.5) before it returns, so that the tasks sent back to the master are larger (p. 9).
- Normalize heterogeneous workers with a benchmark task run on joining (p. 11).
**Quotes**:
- "Nevertheless, we have found branh-and-bound to work well in pratie even on nononvex examples." (p. 4)
- "In a rst implementation, eah worker was assigned a single NLP node." (p. 8)

### S067 · The penalty interior point method fails to converge for mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints (Dundee report NA/208, 2002, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 7 pages read. This is the Feb 2002 report version; the journal version is D001 (OMS 2005), not read here. The text layer drops "c".
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, p. 1); Pang thanked for "going over an earlier version" (p. 7) · **Place in trajectory**: the start of his MPEC line (with Fletcher's NA/209 and NA/210 the same year).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: PIPA (Luo-Pang-Ralph) has a published convergence theorem under strict complementarity [SC] and nonsingularity [NS] (Theorem 1.1, p. 2). The entry is a counterexample to it.
- **D2 Core idea**: a three-variable MPEC (2.1): objective x + w, a box on x, one linear equation in x and y, and one complementarity pair y ⊥ w. Its solution satisfies [SC] (y + w = 2 > 0) and [NS] (p. 3). The text layer drops minus signs, so I do not restate the exact data. From (0, 1, 0.02), PIPA's trust-region-like bound ||dx||² ≤ ρ(||F|| + yᵀw) shrinks with infeasibility. Complementarity halves at every step, so x moves only by a convergent geometric sum and stalls (pp. 4-6).
  - Transferable: to break a method, look for a radius or step bound tied to the iterates (here to feasibility) rather than controlled by the algorithm; then make feasibility converge faster than optimality.
- **D3 Assumptions**: those of the theorem under test, [SC] and [NS], both checked at the solution (p. 3) and at the spurious limit (p. 6). A simplified PIPA with fixed parameters is stated as such (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis**: closed-form LP basis and multipliers for the direction-finding problem (pp. 3-4). Step-size bound 5/9 ≤ α ≤ 1 via a Maple series expansion in w (p. 4). An induction on y_{k+1}w_{k+1} ≤ ½ y_k w_k (p. 4). Armijo acceptance, and the penalty parameter never increased (p. 5). Geometric-sum bounds confine x to within 0.4828 of x0 = 0 and give y ≤ 1.4828 (pp. 5-6). Compactness gives a limit point that satisfies [SC] and [NS] but is not stationary (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: PIPA coded with AMPL's looping extension; Table 1 lists 10 iterates with actual and predicted reductions, confirming the bounds (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: counterexample plus diagnosis. The cause is the radius tied to feasibility. The symptom is detectable: the multiplier of the radius constraint tends to 1 while actual and predicted reductions agree (p. 7). This version is hedged: "This example seems to ontradit" the theorem (p. 7), after "contradicting Theorem 1.1" (pp. 2, 6). It does not locate a faulty proof step. The later preprint does ("part (i) of the proof ... is wrong", [03 E45]; I did not read that version).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅: every step in one paper: the smallest instance (p. 3), hypotheses checked as satisfied (pp. 3, 6), the run in AMPL (p. 6), a symptom and a cause (p. 7). Method 3 variant ⚠ (version path): the 2002 report stops at "seems to contradict" (p. 7), and pointing to the faulty proof step (Method 3 step 3) appears only in the later version [03 E45]. This is after Pang read an earlier draft (p. 7); what prompted the change is not documented.
**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove the counterexample analytically, then run it in a modelling language and tabulate iterates with actual and predicted reduction, so a reader can check both (p. 6).
- End with a detectable symptom (a multiplier of the offending constraint that does not vanish), not only the failure (p. 7).
**Quotes**:
- "This paper presents a small example for whih the Penalty Interior Point Method onverges to a non-stationary point." (p. 1)
- "Linking the trust-region radius to the feasibility of the problem is ounter intuitive." (p. 7)

### H013 · Large Scale Nonconvex Optimization (SIAG/Optimization Views-and-News 14(1), 2003, guest editors Leyffer and Nocedal; no DOI)
**Read level**: partial. Read: the contents (p. 1), the guest editors' introduction (pp. 1-2), Leyffer's own article "Mathematical Programs with Complementarity Constraints" (pp. 15-18; it has its own work entry, S057, so it is summarized here only for how it fits the issue), and the chair's and editor's columns (pp. 24-25), which are the voices of Wolkowicz and Sturm. Not read: the articles by Gould (pp. 2-7), Sachs (pp. 7-10), Biegler and Wächter (pp. 10-15), Bussieck and Pruessner (pp. 19-22) and the Bulletin (other authors).
**Role**: supplement (editorial work) · **Coauthors**: Jorge Nocedal (co-guest editor; the introduction is signed by both, 10 Mar 2003, p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: the first year at Argonne (his article's affiliation, p. 15). The issue puts MPCC and MINLP side by side as the frontier for NLP methods.

- **D1 Problem and entry point (editors)**: the issue is framed as NLP algorithms being extended to new classes: SQP/AL against interior methods "has revitalized the area" (p. 1). DAE/PDE constraints, MPCC and MINLP follow (pp. 1-2). MINLP, through Bussieck and Pruessner, is "a wide open field" (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea (editors)**: a curated tour from the NLP core outward, five papers that "encourage researchers working in nonlinear optimization to branch out" (p. 2).
