### S016 · Deterministic methods for mixed integer nonlinear programming (PhD thesis, University of Dundee 1993, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. Chapters 1-9 read completely (pp. 1-105). Chapter 2 (NLP review, pp. 18-26) and the LCP tableau algebra of §3.3 (pp. 32-38) were read for content, not checked line by line. Appendix A (test problems, pp. 106-113) skimmed for structure and reported values; Bibliography (pp. 114-118) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, p. 1). The file has no acknowledgements page; the supervisor is not named in the text (team.json gives Roger Fletcher). The coauthored Fletcher-Leyffer report NA/141 on OA is in the bibliography as [19] (p. 115), but grep finds no in-text citation of [19]. · **Place in trajectory**: the starting point. Chapters 4-5 are the material of the 1994 Fletcher-Leyffer OA paper; the MIQP chapter (Ch. 3) is the seed of his 1998 MIQP work (Heuristic 4); the NLP solver is still a packaged NAG routine (p. 77), before filterSQP.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a received result that he tests: Duran and Grossmann's OA, shown superior to branch and bound and Benders, but on problems linear in the integer variables y, with a master program that is not exactly equivalent to P (pp. 11, 46, 63). He states P as a general convex MINLP with y allowed to enter f and g nonlinearly, and lists his assumptions A1-A4 (convexity, C¹, a CQ at every NLP subproblem solution, exact NLP solves), claimed to be "considerably weaker than the assumptions used by Duran and Grossmann" (pp. 14-15). He says each one is needed and where it is later relaxed: A2 in Ch. 7, A1 and A4 in Ch. 8 (p. 15). He also checks a published empirical claim against his own runs: Gupta and Ravindran's "linear" growth of B&B time in the number of integer variables is "very surprising", and his Chapter 6 results "point to an increase in computing time which is exponential" (p. 9).
  - Transferable: open a thesis by restating the leading method's class in your more general class, list the assumptions it really needs, and mark which later chapter relaxes each one.
- **D2 Core idea**: OA is rebuilt from a projection onto y plus the linearization of each NLP subproblem at its solution (pp. 47-49), with infeasible y cut off by linearizations taken at the solution of a phase I feasibility problem (pp. 49-53). This yields a master program M equivalent to P (Theorem 4.2.1, p. 53). On top of M he builds five algorithms: linear OA (Algorithm 1, p. 61); quadratic OA, which adds the Lagrangian Hessian term to the master objective and leaves the constraints, and hence finiteness, unchanged (Algorithm 2, pp. 64-65); LP/NLP-based B&B generalized to nonlinear y (Algorithm 3, p. 68); a new QP/NLP-based B&B (Algorithm 4, p. 70); and an OA-flavoured Lagrangean decomposition (Algorithm 5, p. 74). Two extensions follow: nonsmooth OA for composite and exact-penalty MINLPs (Algorithm 6, p. 89), and an OA framework with linear underestimators and tolerances for nonconvex and inexact cases (Algorithm 7, p. 96). Kept from Duran-Grossmann: the NLP/MILP alternation and supporting hyperplanes. Changed: the treatment of infeasibility, y allowed to enter nonlinearly, a curvature term, a simpler proof (p. 58). Quadratic OA is read as "a Sequential Quadratic Programming method generalized to integer programming" (p. 66). The MIQP masters then call for an MIQP B&B, whose child lower bounds come from one parametric step of the dual active-set method on the parent's final LCP tableau (§3.4, pp. 38-41).
  - Transferable: when a linear outer model fails on curved problems, put curvature into the objective of the master and leave its feasible region alone; the finiteness proof then carries over unchanged (p. 65).
  - Transferable: reuse the parent node's final factorization or tableau to price both children before solving them (pp. 38-42).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A1 convex f, g with X a compact polyhedron; A2 C¹; A3 a CQ at every NLP(y) solution; A4 exact NLP solves (p. 15). A1 is "the most serious restriction" (p. 15). A3 is shown to be "more important than has previously been recognised": without it OA can regenerate an integer assignment, shown by an explicit cycling example (pp. 15, 62). Finiteness needs |Y| < ∞ (Theorem 5.2.1, p. 61); Lagrangean decomposition is also handled for bounded infinite Y (pp. 75-76). Nonsmooth OA needs no CQ for finiteness, only for the equivalence of the penalty problem with P (pp. 86, 90). Ch. 8 replaces A1 by A1' (g convex, plus linear underestimators of f with multipliers matching the x-part of the gradient), p. 95, and drops A4 by tolerances ε1 (NLP accuracy), ε2 (underestimator gap) and ε3 (MILP accuracy), pp. 95-96. The mixed tolerance η ≤ UBD − ε(1+|UBD|) is the one used in practice (p. 64).
- **D4 Proof devices**:
  - Finiteness by non-repetition: assume a visited y^l is feasible in the master, then combine the strict bound η < UBD ≤ f^l with "no linearized feasible descent direction" under the CQ to reach a contradiction (Theorem 5.2.1, pp. 61-62). He calls this "much more simple" than Duran and Grossmann's proof via integer polyhedra (p. 63).
  - Multiplier aggregation: multiply the linearized constraints by the KKT weights of the feasibility problem and add the normal-cone inequality; KKT turns the sum into 0 ≥ Σ w_i g_i^+, which contradicts infeasibility (Lemma 4.2.1, p. 51). The same proof shows that only strongly active constraints need to be linearized (p. 51).
  - Dominance of cut families by aggregation: weighting the OA cuts by λ and substituting the x-part of KKT yields exactly the Benders cut, so the OA cuts are at least as strong (pp. 56-57; repeated for the nonsmooth case on p. 90).
  - Bolzano-Weierstrass plus Cauchy-Schwarz on a convergent subsequence of master solutions, for bounded but infinite Y (Theorem 5.5.1, pp. 75-76).
  - Subdifferential replaces gradient; convexity plus monotonicity of h keep the linearizations as supports (Theorem 7.2.1, pp. 85-86). Penalty-parameter bounds A5 make the exact-penalty MINLP equivalent to P (Theorem 7.2.2, p. 87).
  - Tolerance chain ε0 > ε1 + ε2 + ε3 with ε0 non-decreasing, giving finiteness at an ε0-optimal point (Lemma 8.3.1, Theorem 8.3.1, pp. 97-98). Lower bounds on ε2 and ε1 come from the solver tolerance, the multipliers and the gradient norms ((8.12), (8.15), pp. 102-103). Integer cuts exclude perturbed binary points if ε < 1/(p+1) (pp. 101-102).
  - Pardalos-Rosen LP for a best linear underestimator of a concave f over conv(R); the dual LP shows at least p+1 active constraints (pp. 99-100).
  - Operation counts before experiments: the child bounds cost 5n² + mn + k² + O(n) flops, less than a QP solve but an order more than most-fractional branching (pp. 41-42).
  - Counterexamples, each small and computed: the Duran-Grossmann master that admits an infeasible y (one variable, y ∈ {−1, 1}, p. 49); a CQ failure that makes OA cycle (p. 62); y ∈ {0, ε, …, 1/2, 1} on which linear OA, Benders and LP/NLP-BB visit every point while NLP-BB needs one branch (pp. 63-64, 69); exp(−y) on which quadratic OA visits every point while linear OA needs one iteration (p. 66); −ln(x+1), showing that η in the quadratic master is no lower bound (p. 66); the Viswanathan-Grossmann stopping rule stopping early on a convex MINLP (p. 95); a nonconvexity that passes the Kocis-Grossmann local and global tests (Fig. 8.1, pp. 93-94).
  - Transferable: to show that one cut family dominates another, derive the weaker cut as a multiplier-weighted sum of the stronger cuts plus KKT (pp. 56-57).
  - Transferable: when you propose a remedy for method A's worst case, also build the worst case of the remedy (pp. 63-66); together the two examples mark out the class where each method fits.
- **D5 Experiments**: Two studies. (1) MIQP B&B (§3.5): branching rules BR1 (most fractional, no bounds), BR2 (branch on max min of the new bounds) and BR3 (BR1 plus bounds on stacked nodes) on Beale, HS76, AVGAS and AFIRO variants (LP problems made MIQP by adding a Hessian and integrality), a 6×4 QAP and random MIQPs averaged over 5 runs. Reported: QPs generated and solved, CPU on a SUN SPARC SLC (pp. 43-45). The failure is kept in the tables ("AFIRO2 (c) not solved with stacksize 10,000", p. 44). The conclusion is by class: BR3 is the best overall, BR2 when the integrality gap is large (pp. 44-45). (2) Five MINLP codes (nonlinear B&B, linear OA, quadratic OA, LP/NLP-BB, QP/NLP-BB), all FORTRAN 77, NLPs solved by NAG E04UCF, master programs solved by his own MILP/MIQP B&B on bqpd (pp. 77-78). 22 problems: Duran-Grossmann TP1-3, BATCH and BATCH-int, Asaadi NLPs with added integrality, 2DEx, gear-train GTD and chains, AVGAS/AFIRO MIQPs (Table 6.1, p. 78). Fairness: all codes start from the same NLP relaxation and stop at ε-optimality (p. 78). Meters: CPU (accurate to "about 5%"), the number of LP/QP solves, and the number of NLP solves, each with its weakness stated (p. 78). The LP/QP count is preferred because the two QP solvers differ in degeneracy handling and reliability (p. 80). Missing runs are explained: no quadratic-OA results for BATCH, GTD, GTD chain(3) because of round-off growth in bqpd's reduced Hessian (BATCH aborted after an hour; the GTD runs "would otherwise distort the results", p. 79). Handicaps of his own setup are stated: NAG has no phase I output, so OA needs an extra feasibility NLP (p. 80); commercial MILP solvers might speed up linear and quadratic OA (pp. 80-81). His nonlinear B&B is checked against Duran-Grossmann's B&B counts on TP1-3 (5/13/20 NLPs against 8/25/43, Table 6.4, p. 81). A formulation test: the nonconvex BATCH-int is solved by all five codes in about 20% of the time of the binary, convexified BATCH (p. 81). Result: a three-row "Rule of Thumb" (p. 83). No experiments for Chapters 7-8.
  - Transferable: give every code the same starting point and the same termination rule. Report the counter that is not distorted by differing subsolvers (here LP/QP solves) next to CPU, and state each meter's weakness (pp. 78-80).
  - Transferable: rebuild the competitor's baseline well and re-run their published test problems; if your baseline beats their reported numbers, say what that does to their headline comparison (Table 6.4, p. 81).
- **D6 Positioning**: He continues Duran-Grossmann OA ("owes much to the innovative work", p. 58), Yuan et al., Quesada-Grossmann LP/NLP-BB, Geoffrion and Flippo et al. on Benders ("elegant discussion", p. 10), and Michelon-Maculan on Lagrangean decomposition (pp. 10-11, 71). Corrections are made directly and name the step: the master programs of [15] and [79] "are not correctly equivalent to P, with the serious consequence that their algorithms might cycle" (p. 46); the inaccuracy in [60] is corrected (p. 67); Yuan et al. "erroneously" call their OA Benders (p. 11); Lazimy's MIQP Benders makes an "unnecessary assumption" (p. 10) and is "inadequate" because it linearizes (p. 27). The heuristic literature is judged on guarantees: Amir-Hasegawa give no convergence result and ignore barrier ill-conditioning (pp. 12-13); the Davydov-Sigal and Li penalty reformulations turn a convex MINLP into a nonconvex NLP (pp. 13-14); the Kocis-Grossmann and Viswanathan-Grossmann OA heuristics give up finite convergence (pp. 93-95). Fletcher's book and bqpd report are the NLP/QP base (refs [17], [18], pp. 22-23, 31, 77). Jeroslow's non-computability result frames what nonconvex MINLP cannot do (p. 93).
  - Transferable: criticise a heuristic by the guarantee it gives up (finiteness, recovery of the convex case as a special case), and show the failure on the smallest instance (p. 95).
- **D7 Writing**: Each chapter introduction says what is new and against whom ("improves on this work in a number of ways", p. 58; "correcting an inaccuracy", p. 46). Every algorithm is followed by a computed small example, often with a figure (Figs. 3.4, 4.1, 5.2, 8.1). Personal judgements are marked in the first person and kept apart from theorems: "It seems to me" (p. 13); "I do not regard Lagrangean Decomposition as a valuable alternative to outer approximation" (p. 75), which drops his own Algorithm 5 in the same chapter that proves it finite; "in my view" (p. 105). Chapter 8 is hedged as pointing "towards interesting new developments" (p. 92). The empirical result is condensed into a three-row table, the "Rule of Thumb" (p. 83). A small inconsistency: Ch. 6 speaks of "two broad classes" (p. 79), while the conclusion speaks of "three broad classes" (p. 105).
  - Transferable: end an empirical chapter with a class → method table rather than a winner (p. 83).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are mostly theory and algorithms: a corrected and simpler OA master and finiteness proof, OA cuts shown to dominate Benders cuts, four new or generalized algorithms, nonsmooth OA, and a tolerance framework. There is also an MIQP B&B code and a first five-code comparison with a rule of thumb (pp. 104-105). The limits are stated concretely: convexity (p. 15); MIQP software scarce (p. 66); quadratic OA can mislead on monotone functions (pp. 66, 82); round-off in bqpd (p. 79); NAG gives no phase I solution (p. 80); his home-made MILP solver may handicap OA (p. 80); the Ch. 8 nonconvex method is rigorous only for concave pure-integer objectives over convex sets, otherwise a heuristic (pp. 99-100). Future directions: adapt ε0 from solver accuracies in a "rigorous implementation" and report the final ε0 (p. 103); globally convergent algorithms for nonconvex classes through the framework (p. 105); a fixed bqpd release (p. 79).

- New-pattern candidates: "solver-agnostic theory" (write the infeasibility theory against a generic weighted phase I problem F that covers l1, l∞ and sequential phase I methods, pp. 49-51); "tolerance budget from inner solvers" (outer termination tolerance derived from the NLP and MILP tolerances, multipliers and gradient norms, and reported with the solution, pp. 95-103); "paired worst cases mark out the class" (pp. 63-66 → p. 83); "reinterpret one method as another to compare cuts" (Benders = aggregated OA, p. 57; quadratic OA = SQP for integers, p. 66; B&B = "clever rounding", p. 28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Test an equivalence claim on the smallest instance before building on it: a one-continuous-variable, two-point y example exposed the Duran-Grossmann master (p. 49).
- Write theory against an abstract class of solver outputs (any phase I yields some weights w ≥ 0 and a feasibility problem F), so that the result holds whatever NLP code is used (pp. 49-51).
- For every remedy, construct its own worst case; turn the pair of examples into a class-based recommendation (pp. 63-66, 83).
- Prove one cut family dominates another by aggregating the stronger cuts with KKT multipliers to recover the weaker (pp. 56-57).
- Price child nodes from the parent's final factorization with one dual active-set step, and count the flops before the experiment (pp. 38-42).
- Make the termination tolerance a computed quantity, ε0 > ε1 + ε2 + ε3, bounded below by the inner solvers' tolerances times multiplier and gradient norms, and report it with the answer (pp. 96-103).
- In a solver comparison, use the same start and the same ε-termination, prefer a counter immune to differing subsolver quality, explain every missing table entry, and benchmark your baseline against the competitor's published counts (pp. 78-81).
- Publish each test instance with its relaxation value and its integer optimum (pp. 106-113).
