### S040 · Separation and relaxation for cones of quadratic forms (Mathematical Programming 2013, DOI 10.1007/s10107-011-0495-6)
**Read level**: partial (abstract, §1 and §2 pp. 3–9, §3.1–3.3 pp. 9–15, start of §3.4 pp. 15–16, §4.1–4.2 pp. 18–20, §5.3 and §6 pp. 26–28; §3.4 hierarchy details pp. 16–18, Examples 1–4 pp. 20–23 and §5.1–5.2 pp. 23–26 not read). First-page check: title and authors "Samuel Burer & Hongbo Dong" match (p. 1, p. 3).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Hongbo Dong (Burer's Iowa student when most of the work was done; then at Wisconsin, p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: theory follow-up to the 2009 copositive representation: studies the cone C(P) = cone{xxᵀ : x ∈ P} directly and gets the first separation procedures for 5×5 CP matrices and the 4- and 5-dimensional box cones.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: (1) is a nonconvex QP over a pointed polyhedral cone P; it equals a linear program over C(P) (Theorem 1, p. 6). C is NP-hard in general, but "even partial information about C can help" (p. 4); the standard relaxation D(P) (PSD plus pairwise products of the constraints, (3)) is strong yet inexact (p. 4). Known exactness thresholds are small: C = D iff n ≤ 4 (CP) and n ≤ 3 (box) (p. 5; Theorem 4, p. 19; Theorem 5, p. 24).
- **D2 Core idea**: Separate over C by optimizing over the dual of C_bd, the cone generated only by boundary points of P (Algorithm 1, pp. 9–10); since bd(P) is a union of faces Pᵢ, C*_bd = ∩ C*ᵢ, so separation in dimension n needs only tractable cones in dimension n−1 (p. 9). Duality gives a nonlinear representation X = λ(XBᵀe)(XBᵀe)ᵀ + Z, Z ∈ C_bd (Proposition 2, p. 12); replacing the rank-1 nonlinear term by a rank-3 linear term along a "step direction" d gives relaxations C(d) with C = ∩_d C(d) (Propositions 4 and 6, pp. 13–14), and the tractable D(d) with C ⊆ C(d) ⊆ D(d) ⊆ D (Theorem 3, p. 15); applied recursively this gives a finite hierarchy for each choice of directions (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: to separate over a hard cone generated by a set, separate first over the cone generated by its boundary, which splits into lower-dimensional copies of the same object; the induction then rides on the largest dimension where the object is known to be tractable (pp. 9, 19–20, 27).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Three assumptions on P: nonempty slice x₁ = 1, pointedness, relint>(P) nonempty; the third is shown to be without loss of generality (Assumptions 1–3, p. 7). Optimization over C_bd is assumed possible ("provided one can optimize", abstract).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Correctness of Algorithm 1 by a Schur-complement argument (Lemma 2, pp. 8–9) and a contradiction using a shift Q + t(AᵀA + BᵀeeᵀB) ⪰ 0 (Theorem 2, p. 11); conic strong duality to read off the nonlinear representation (p. 12); extreme-ray argument with the smallest step α to the boundary for C ⊆ C(d) (Proposition 4, p. 13); exactness of ∩_d C(d) by choosing d as a global minimizer of xᵀQx on a bounded slice (Proposition 6, p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: Illustrative examples only: the Burer–Letchford box-QP instance with a 9.29% gap closed after 20 separation cuts and by D₄(d) exactly with Yalmip-CSDP (Examples 5–6, pp. 27–28); 1,000 random Z ∈ D₄ ∩ TRI \ C₄ with mean normalized distance 0.0022 to C₄ (Example 7, p. 28).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: First separation algorithm for C(ℝ⁵₊) (Corollary 1, p. 20) and for the 4- and 5-dimensional box cones (Corollaries 3–4, p. 27). Left open: whether ∩_d D(d) = C ("We were unable to prove or disprove", p. 15; repeated p. 28), the link with Parrilo's hierarchy, choosing d adaptively, and computation for large n, where the authors suggest working on small groups (triples) of variables (p. 28).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence: the whole paper works on one cone C(P) whose linear optimization is exactly the QP (Theorem 1, p. 6) and attacks the cone by separation and relaxation (pp. 9–16). Method 2 ✅ evidence: results are stated by dimension (n = 5 for CP, n = 4, 5 for the box) and the future-work section proposes applying the small-dimensional separators to triples of variables inside large QPs (pp. 20, 27–28). Method 4 ✅ evidence (step 1): the first test is a literature instance where the standard relaxation leaves a 9.29% gap (Example 5, p. 27). Method 3 ✅ evidence (claim levels): the open equality ∩_d D(d) = C is printed as open rather than conjectured (p. 15); the "good approximation" view of D₄ ∩ TRI is backed only by 1,000 random samples and called "some evidence" (p. 28).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Boundary decomposition for separation: dimension-n separation reduces to optimization over n (or 2(n−1)) embedded copies of the dimension-(n−1) dual cone (pp. 19–20, 26–27).
- Linearize a rank-1 nonlinear representation by a rank-3 term along a step direction, then show the intersection over all directions is exact (pp. 13–14).
**Quotes**:
- "This is the first separation algorithm for C5 and successfully answers the open question as to whether or not C5 is tractable." (p. 20)
- "Random numerical experiments suggest that encountering an X in the difference" (p. 28)

### S037 · Unbounded convex sets for non-convex mixed-integer quadratic programming (Mathematical Programming 2014, DOI 10.1007/s10107-012-0609-9)
**Read level**: partial (abstract, §1–§2 pp. 1–5, §3.1–3.2 pp. 5–7, §3.4 start p. 8, §3.6 p. 11, §5.2 pp. 14–15, §5.3 Theorem 7 p. 17, §7 pp. 21–23, §8 pp. 24–25; §3.3, §3.5, §4, §5.1, §5.4, §6 and most proofs not read). First-page check: title and authors "Samuel Burer · Adam N. Letchford" match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Adam N. Letchford (Lancaster) · **Position in trajectory**: polyhedral-plus-convex-analysis study of the hull of {(x, xxᵀ)} with integer and continuous x, following their 2009 box-QP paper; full version of an IPCO extended abstract (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Lift an MIQP to (x, y = xxᵀ) and study the convex hull of feasible pairs, so that valid inequalities can feed LP/convex bounding (p. 2). The authors restrict to the unconstrained case and justify it: the constrained hull is a face of the unconstrained set (Proposition 7, p. 11; announced p. 2). Two complications: the hull is not closed (Proposition 1, p. 5) and its closure is never polyhedral (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Define MIQ⁺ and MIQ as closures of the convex hulls (pp. 5–6); combine polyhedral theory with convex analysis (p. 2). Read MILP and BQP structure into the lifted set: split disjunctions (vᵀx ≤ s) ∨ (vᵀx ≥ s+1) give "split" inequalities (Proposition 12, p. 14) that dominate the psd inequalities (Theorem 4, p. 14) and induce facets when v's nonzero components are relatively prime (Theorem 5, pp. 14–15); the Boolean quadric polytope is a face (Theorem 7, p. 17).
  - Transferable: when your lifted set is unbounded and non-closed, characterize extreme points and rays first and show the constrained problem is a face of the unconstrained set; then every inequality you derive for the simple set is valid for the constrained one (pp. 8, 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linear equality constraints only, nonnegative or free integer/continuous variables; Q symmetric without loss of generality (pp. 1–2). The inequalities are also stated to be valid with quadratic constraints (p. 2).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Non-closedness by an explicit sequence of convex combinations of (t, t²) and (0, 0) (Proposition 1, p. 5); strong NP-hardness via the closest vector problem (Proposition 2, p. 6); split inequalities by expanding (vᵀx + s)(vᵀx + s + 1) ≥ 0 (p. 14); facet proof via a unimodular matrix with v as first row (pp. 14–15); complete description of MIQ₂,₀ by a rank case split on the 2×2 matrix A using Lemmas 4–5 (pp. 22–23).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Fundamental properties (dimension, complexity, extreme points/rays, affine symmetries) and several facet classes (abstract; p. 2). Complete linear descriptions only for small cases: MIQ₀,ₙ by psd inequalities, MIQ⁺₀,ₙ iff n ≤ 3, MIQ₂,₀ by split inequalities (Theorem 8, pp. 22–23). Open: polynomial-time optimization over MIQ⁺ₙ,₀ for fixed n, non-standard splits, complete descriptions for other small (n₁, n₂), and split-separation complexity, which they conjecture strongly NP-hard (pp. 24–25); a mixed-case facet statement is also left as a conjecture (p. 20).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence: one convex object (closure of the lifted hull) carries every linearly constrained MIQP, via a face (Proposition 7, p. 11). Method 2 ✅ evidence: complete descriptions are pursued only in the smallest dimensions where they hold (n ≤ 3 continuous, (2,0) integer), with the boundary stated (pp. 21–23). Method 3 ✅ evidence (claim levels): open separation complexity printed as a conjecture with a suggested heuristic route via the psd eigenvector (p. 25).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Validity of a disjunction-derived quadratic inequality by expanding the product of the two disjunct slacks and linearizing (split inequalities, p. 14), then a dominance theorem against the psd family (p. 14).
- Use a known polytope (BQP) as a face of the new set to lift its facets (Theorem 7 and Corollary 5, p. 17).
**Quotes**:
- "It is our hope that the valid inequalities that we have derived will be used as cutting planes within exact algorithms for non-convex MIQPs, whether constrained or not." (p. 24)
- "Unfortunately, we conjecture that this problem is strongly NP-hard." (p. 25)

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nayoung Cho, Ann Melissa Campbell (Iowa Management Sciences, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an applied robust-routing side paper; no conic machinery.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: TSP with interval travel times [lₑ, uₑ] (iTSP, p. 2). The goal: control conservatism at the cost of one deterministic TSP (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: "Modified-Soyster": a single TSP with costs (1−γ)l + γu, γ ∈ [0, 1] (model (8), pp. 12–13). The authors present γ as a manager's degree of pessimism (pp. 3–4) and contrast its "balanced" conservatism with Bertsimas–Sim's "focused" one (p. 13).

- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A cheap, interpretable robust model with provable monotone comparative statics; proposed as a quick heuristic for the other models (p. 23). Future work: asymmetric TSP and edge-specific γₑ (p. 25). Limits are stated only lightly: γ and Γ are "not directly comparable" (p. 20).

**Method links**: Method 4 ⚠ variant: the third test set is constructed where the authors expect their model to win and is labelled ad hoc (pp. 23–24): the filter is disclosed, but the pool is built in the method's favour, not the baseline's failure region. Method 5 ⚠ variant (weak): a simple modification benchmarked against the published models, with computation times reported first (p. 21). New-pattern candidate: "comparative statics by 2-D projection": prove parameter monotonicity by projecting the combinatorial feasible set onto the two objective components (pp. 14–17).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Project a hard combinatorial feasible set onto the two objective components (L, R) and use the two optimality inequalities at γ₁ < γ₂ to get monotone, ratio-bounded trade-offs without solving anything (pp. 14–17).
- Cross-evaluation table: evaluate each model's optimal solution under every other model's objective and report percentage gaps (pp. 21–22).
**Quotes**:
- "In this sense, increased conservatism is guaranteed to mitigate the worst possible outcome." (p. 16)
- "Although we admit the new instances are somewhat ad hoc" (p. 23)

### S060 · A first-order smoothing technique for a class of large-scale linear programs (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2014, DOI 10.1137/110854400)
**Read level**: partial (abstract, §1 pp. 1–3, Proposition 3.2 and its comment p. 8, §4 pp. 12–15, §5 pp. 15–21, §6 p. 22; §2 review, §3.1–3.3 derivations pp. 3–12 mostly not read). First-page check: title and authors "Jieqiu Chen and Samuel Burer" match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jieqiu Chen (Burer's former student, then postdoc at Argonne, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a convex first-order algorithm paper for machine-learning LPs, outside the nonconvex QP line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: LP (P) min cᵀα + wᵀξ with Aα − b ≤ ξ, from LP-based ranking and 1-norm SVMs, where A is large and fully dense (kernel matrices), so simplex and interior-point methods run out of memory (pp. 1–2). The subgradient method is memory-light but O(1/ε²) (p. 2); Mangasarian's generalized Newton "may not be sufficiently robust" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Rewrite (P) as the nonsmooth (NS0) and apply Nesterov's primal-dual excessive-gap technique (EGT), O(1/ε) (p. 2). The obstacle is the unbounded primal set: bound it with cᵀα ≤ θ for any upper bound θ on the optimal value plus Assumption 1.1 (pp. 2–3); complexity then depends on θ (Proposition 3.2, p. 8), so θ is updated dynamically while the excessive-gap condition is kept, by a bisection step (Lemma 4.1, Proposition 4.2, Algorithm 5, pp. 12–13).
  - Transferable: when a first-order method needs a compact domain, bound the domain by an upper bound on the optimal value, make the complexity explicit in that bound, then tighten the bound online without restarting by checking the method's invariant (pp. 3, 12–13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: c, w ≥ 0; Assumption 1.1: a known bound h on the α-components with zero cost (pp. 2–3), verified for the 1-norm SVM by a derived bound on γ (p. 18).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Nesterov's EGT theorem applied with chosen prox-functions and norms; the chosen dual-space prox gives the lowest complexity among those considered, the ℓ₁ alternative being O(√m) worse (p. 8); intermediate-value argument for the existence of θ⁺ (Lemma 4.1, p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: 14 UCI ranking datasets against the incremental subgradient method of [2], protocol: run EGT to ε, then run subgradient until it matches EGT's objective or hits 18,000 s (p. 16); subgradient matches in 5 of 14 and is faster in 3 of them (p. 16); 1-norm SVM (linear and kernel, C = 1 and 0.01) against Mangasarian's Newton, which is faster when it converges but often fails (Tables 4–9, pp. 20–21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic (EGT specialization plus dynamic θ) and computational. Limits printed: subgradient is the better choice if a good solution quickly matters (p. 16); EGT solves only 1 of 6 instances of the hardest kernel set at C = 1 (p. 21). Future: the θ-update idea for other nonsmooth problems with unbounded feasible sets (p. 22).

**Method links**: Method 5 ✅ evidence: a known algorithm (Nesterov EGT) plus one trick (bound by θ, update θ dynamically), benchmarked against the published method of each application, with losses printed (pp. 2, 16, 21). Method 4 ⚠ variant: test sets are the applications' standard datasets, not a filtered hard pool (pp. 15–16, 19).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Comparison protocol for methods without a common stopping rule: run yours to tolerance, then run the baseline until it matches your objective value or a time limit (p. 16).
- Ablation of a parameter update against an oracle best case (θ = θ* from a presolved LP) and the no-update case on one instance (Figure 1, pp. 13–14).
**Quotes**:
- "This idea could be extended to other convex nonsmooth problems with unbounded feasible sets." (p. 22)
- "This observation suggests that the subgradient method might be a better choice if obtaining a good solution in a short time is important." (p. 16)

### S033 · A two-variable approach to the two-trust-region subproblem (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2016, DOI 10.1137/130945880)
**Read level**: partial (abstract, §1 pp. 1–3, §2 pp. 3–8, start of §3.1 pp. 8–9, §4 pp. 13–15, §5 pp. 15–20; the break technicalities §3.1–3.3 pp. 9–13 and §5.1 details pp. 15–17 skimmed only). First-page check: title and authors "Boshi Yang and Samuel Burer" match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Boshi Yang (Burer's PhD student, Iowa Mathematics, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: follow-up to Burer–Anstreicher 2013 (SOC-RLT for extended TRS): characterizes all remaining valid quadratics for TTRS when n = 2 and reuses the 2013 unsolved instances.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: TTRS (CDT problem) is polynomial-time (Bienstock, via Barvinok), but not by convex relaxation (p. 2); the basic SDP plus SOCRLT still leaves a gap (pp. 3–4). The authors keep the convex-relaxation question anyway, arguing that it helps when TTRS is a substructure (p. 2). - **D2 Core idea**: For n = 2, find a subcone G containing every extreme ray of K by showing each valid f is minorized by some g ∈ G (Proposition 2.2, p. 5).   - Transferable: to prove a relaxation exact, show that every valid inequality is minorized by one from your family; then the family contains all extreme rays of the valid cone (Proposition 2.2, p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: n = 2 for the theory (p. 6); F full-dimensional, neither ellipsoid contains the other, independent constraint gradients at vertices; the latter is a simplifying assumption whose removal is said to need "a slightly more careful presentation" (p. 3).
- **D4 Proof devices**: The "break" of a quadratic at a boundary zero (order of the first nonzero derivative along the boundary), shown independent of the parameterization via Faà di Bruno (Proposition 3.1, pp. 8–9); perturbation f + λg with λ < 0 when zeros and breaks of f are dominated by those of g (Lemma 4.1, pp. 13–14); nonvertex zeros have even break (Lemma 4.2, p. 14); case analysis on the maximum breaks (Theorem 4.3, pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Experiments**: An n = 2 instance with a 2.74% SOCRLT gap closed by one lifted-RLT cut, with λ_min in closed form (p. 19); the 2013 example closed after six cuts (p. 19); on the 1,000 random instances per n = 5, 10, 20 from [8], the 215 instances unsolved by SOCRLT (relative gap < 10⁻⁴ as the solved criterion) yield 82 solved (Table 3, p. 19), at more than ten times the CPU time (p. 20); a footnote says one column differs from [8] because a different SDP solver was used (p. 19).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Full specification of the needed inequalities for n = 2 (p. 2); the general-n question is left open ("We are unsure whether this class of lifted-RLT constraints closes the relaxation gap for general n", p. 18); 133 of the 215 hard instances remain unsolved and the cuts are expensive (pp. 19–20).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ evidence: small substructure (two ellipsoids), kitchen sink (SDP + SOCRLT, which captures all RLT) as the reference, a strictly stronger family, proof only in n = 2 and a heuristic beyond (pp. 4–8, 17–18). Method 4 ✅ evidence: the hard pool is the earlier paper's unsolved instances (41 + 70 + 104 = 215), with the solved criterion restated and the solver change disclosed (p. 19). Method 3 ✅ evidence (claim levels): the n > 2 extension is labelled a heuristic and its exactness left open (p. 18). Signature Work link: this is the "solved by the 2016 method" count of 82 that SKILL.md Method 4 lists among inconsistent counts; here it is 82 of 215, about 38% (p. 19).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Minorization argument: exactness ⇐ every valid quadratic dominates one in your generating family (pp. 5, 14–15).
- Local order-of-contact ("break") bookkeeping at the zeros of a valid quadratic to decide when it can be perturbed by another valid quadratic (pp. 8, 13–14).
- Seed the separation of a new cut family with the support points returned by an existing, cheaper separation routine (p. 17).
**Quotes**:
- "We believe it is still interesting to seek the convex relaxation that solves TTRS exactly." (p. 2)
- "Applying our heuristic lifted-RLT constraints to the 215 previously unsolved instances, we can solve 82—about 38%—of them; see Table 3." (p. 19)

### S045 · Robust sensitivity analysis of the optimal value of linear programming (Optimization Methods and Software 2017, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2016.1256400; read as arXiv 1509.04682v2)
**Read level**: partial (arXiv v2: abstract, §1 pp. 1–6, §2.1 p. 6, §3.1–3.3 pp. 12–16, §4 setup p. 16, §4.3–4.5 results pp. 21–24, §5 pp. 24–25; §2.2–2.3 and §4.1–4.2 details not read). First-page check: title and authors "Guanglin Xu, Samuel Burer" match (p. 1). The file is the arXiv preprint, not the published version.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Guanglin Xu (Burer's PhD student, Iowa Management Sciences, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: carries the copositive reformulation plus SDP/RLT/SOC-RLT relaxations into LP sensitivity analysis, a question from outside nonconvex QP.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Classical sensitivity analysis handles single changes, the 100-percent rule, tolerance ranges or intervals (p. 2); no one had studied simultaneous perturbations of (b̂, ĉ) in a general region, because they lead to NP-hard nonconvex QPs (p. 2). Closest work: Peng and Zhu's worst-case LP with b̂ in an ellipsoid (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Define best- and worst-case optimal values p⁻, p⁺ over a compact convex uncertainty set U, write them as bilinear QPs, replace them with finite variants q⁻, q⁺ (p. 4), reformulate exactly as generalized copositive programs over hom(U) × cones (Theorem 1, p. 13, via corollary 8.3 of Burer 2012), then relax to SDPs with RLT and SOC-RLT constraints built from U and a redundant complementarity x ∘ s = 0 (pp. 12–14).
  - Transferable: add a redundant nonconvex constraint (here complementary slackness) to the exact formulation because its linearization strengthens the relaxation; then measure its effect separately (pp. 12, 23–24).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: U compact, convex, containing (0, 0) (Assumption 1, p. 6); for finite variants, nominal primal and dual feasible sets nonempty and one bounded (Assumption 2, p. 10). Only (b, c) are perturbed; the constraint matrix is not, and only the optimal value is studied, not the solution (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Direct embedding of the bilinear QPs into the general form (12) so Lemma 1 (copositive equivalence) applies (Theorem 1, p. 13); validity of product constraints by multiplying pairs of linear constraints of R₊ × K (pp. 13–14).
- **D5 Experiments**: Six literature examples plus one network flow problem, Python with Mosek at defaults (p. 16); quality is certified from the other side by feasible solutions: SDP-solution translation with alternating bilinear local improvement and a 10,000-point extreme-point sampler (Algorithm 1, pp. 15–16); the systemic-risk table shows only the 10 instances on which Mosek had no numerical problems (footnote, p. 21); gaps of 0.2% remain in two SOC network cases (Table 4, p. 24), although the text says the relaxations capture the values "in all cases" (p. 23); dropping the linearized complementarity changes q⁺ by up to 1671% (Table 5, p. 24).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: New problem (RSA) plus copositive/SDP machinery; future work: speed of the largest relaxations, and perturbations of the constraint matrix (p. 25). The authors note the lack of competitors: "we are aware of no existing methods" (p. 23).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence: a new question is first packaged as an exact copositive program, then attacked by tractable relaxations (pp. 3, 12–14). Method 3 ⚠ variant: "tight" is checked against independent feasible-solution bounds (a two-sided signal, pp. 15–16), but the summary "no gap … for all tested examples" (p. 4) sits beside 0.2% gaps in Table 4 (p. 24). Method 4 ⚠ variant: the filter is disclosed (only instances without solver numerical trouble, p. 21), but it removes instances that are hard for the method rather than for a baseline. New-pattern candidate: "redundant constraint for relaxation strength": a redundant nonconvex constraint (complementary slackness) is added because its linearization tightens the relaxation, and its effect is measured by ablation (pp. 12, 23–24).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Two-sided certification of a relaxation bound: translate the relaxation's solution into a feasible point, improve it by alternating convex solves on a bilinear objective, and add a random-extreme-point sampler; report the better of the two as the gap reference (pp. 15–16).
- Show the value of each added constraint class by an "without it" ablation table (Table 5, p. 24).
**Quotes**:
- "First, we focus only on how the optimal value is affected by uncertainty, not the optimal solution." (p. 4)
- "As ours is the first approach to study general perturbations in the literature, we are aware of no existing methods for this problem with which to compare our results." (p. 23)
