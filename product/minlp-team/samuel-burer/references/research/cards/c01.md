**Batch intent**: See how Burer worked in his thesis-era papers with Monteiro and Zhang (2000-2002, Georgia Tech / Rice / first Iowa year), before the low-rank SDPLR paper (2003) and long before the MINLP relaxation work. ### S014 · A projected gradient algorithm for solving the maxcut SDP relaxation (Optimization Methods and Software 2001, DOI 10.1080/10556780108805818)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-21, including references; the preprint has no appendices).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Renato D.C. Monteiro (both at Georgia Tech, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the first paper in the factorization line. It takes X = LLᵀ for one SDP (maxcut), and S017 (p. 2) and SDPLR (2003, SKILL.md Signature Work) build on it.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In Goemans-Williamson the expensive step is solving the maxcut SDP. A general SDP code "drastically limits the size" (p. 2). Interior-point methods (Benson et al.) take few expensive Newton iterations. Homer and Peinado's first-order method takes many cheap ones and reaches 0.2% relative error quickly but converges slowly in the limit (p. 2). The entry point is an accuracy regime: first-order methods for "only moderate accuracy" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open an algorithm paper by naming the accuracy regime in which the method should win (here 0.2% relative error), and make that the stopping rule for every competitor.
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep Homer-Peinado's change of variables but restrict the factor to be lower triangular, X = LLᵀ with L ∈ Lⁿ (negative diagonal allowed, so there are no inequality constraints, p. 5). Solve (LP) by a projected gradient method: gradient 2·low(CL), row-wise projection onto the tangent space, then row normalization U(·) after an Armijo step (pp. 6-8). Section 3.1 shows that steepest ascent on the normalized unconstrained objective and the projected gradient give the same directions, so the only advantage over Homer-Peinado is the triangular structure (fewer flops, less memory, pp. 9-10). A greedy vertex reordering (highest degree first) reduces the per-iteration cost m(G) (pp. 12-13).
  - Transferable: when a new method is "a variant of X", prove the two search directions coincide. Any speed-up can then be credited cleanly to the one structural change (here the triangular factor).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Nonnegative edge weights for the 0.87856 guarantee (pp. 1, 4). Choosing L ∈ Lⁿ rather than Lⁿ₊ rests on "limited computational testing" (p. 5). There is no global-convergence theorem. Accumulation points are stationary, and convergence to a non-global point is called "unlikely" because (LP) is equivalent to the convex SDP; it was always observed experimentally (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Proposition 3.2 is a global-optimality certificate for the nonconvex factored problem. If S = Σλᵢeᵢeᵢᵀ − C ⪰ 0 at a stationary point, then low(SL) = 0 makes SX strictly upper triangular, so X•S = 0 and (X, λ, S) is primal-dual optimal for the SDP (p. 8). Flop counts are written as τ·m(G) + O(·), with m(G) = Σ_{ij∈E} min{i, j} as a graph-ordering measure (pp. 10-12). The Armijo search reuses the expansion of ⟨ℓ̃ᵢ, ℓ̃ⱼ⟩ as a quadratic in α (eq. 7), so trial steps cost O(|E|) (pp. 11-12).
  - Transferable: for a factored (Burer-Monteiro-type) reformulation, derive the dual slack from the stationarity multipliers and test S ⪰ 0. This gives an a-posteriori global certificate for a nonconvex method.
  - Transferable: express per-iteration cost through a structure-dependent quantity (here m(G)). The quantity then suggests a cheap preprocessing step (reordering) that lowers it.
- **D5 Experiments**: Code in ANSI C on a Sparc 20, with L⁰ = I and a stated step-size scheme chosen "experimentally" (p. 13). 21 rudy G-set graphs, n = 800-7000 (Table 2, p. 14). Baselines are Benson-Ye-Zhang (byz) and Homer-Peinado (hp), with code supplied by the authors (acknowledgements, p. 20). Reference optima come from byz run to a 10⁻⁶ duality gap (p. 14). Iterations, times and best of n random cuts are reported at 0.2% and 0.02% accuracy (Tables 3-4, pp. 15-16). Larger graphs were run on a different IBM machine after the preliminary version (Table 5, p. 17). There is a memory table (p. 18) and a practical stopping rule (8) on 27 graphs (Tables 7-8, pp. 18-19). Losses are printed: iterations grow "drastically" from 0.2% to 0.02%, especially with negative weights (p. 17), and cut quality is worse than byz with no explanation (p. 17).
  - Transferable: benchmark at two accuracy levels. The jump between them shows where a first-order method stops paying, and it should be reported rather than hidden.
- **D6 Positioning**: Presented as an improvement over two named predecessors: the second-order Benson et al. and the first-order Homer-Peinado "from which our method was derived" (p. 20). The comparison with Helmberg-Rendl's spectral bundle method is explicitly left open as "currently unclear" (p. 20).
- **D7 Writing**: States a "running theme" in the introduction (p. 2) and gives a three-point "main conclusions" list before Section 2 (p. 3). The paper argues that the projected-gradient reading has "a more intuitive appeal" even though the method can be derived like Homer-Peinado's (p. 3). Hedging is moderate and specific ("we believe that the presence of negative edge weights increases the ill conditioning", p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and computational (a faster, leaner first-order code), plus a broader claim: first-order methods often reach moderate accuracy "much more quickly than interior-point methods", and "the same results are apt to hold elsewhere" (p. 20). Future directions are concrete: parallelize, add second-order information against slow asymptotic convergence, and extend to other SDPs (p. 20). The last of these is the SDPLR programme.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: a variant of an existing code, benchmarked against the best public codes the authors could obtain, with the parameter choices and the stopping rule justified experimentally ("the motivation for this particular stopping criterion is experimental rather than theoretical", p. 18) and the global-convergence gap stated (p. 8).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the claim that the method never stalls at a non-global point is labelled as an observation ("unlikely", "we have observed", p. 8), not a theorem. Labelling a claim by its level is already there in 2001, but there is no two-signal or exact certification.
- new-pattern candidate: **dual certificate for a factored nonconvex reformulation** (Prop. 3.2, p. 8; recurs in S017 pp. 19-24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive an a-posteriori global-optimality certificate (S = Diag(λ) − C ⪰ 0) from the stationarity multipliers of the factored problem (p. 8).
- Report per-iteration complexity through a graph quantity, then reorder the input to reduce it, and give the measured ratio m(G′)/m(G) (pp. 10-13, Table 1).
- Run every competitor to the same relative-error target computed from a high-accuracy reference solve (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "a running theme of this paper is that the speed of first-order methods make them attractive choices for solving the maxcut SDP relaxation, particularly when one desires a solution of only moderate accuracy." (p. 2)
- "We currently are unable to give a reasonable explanation as to why this occurs" (p. 17)

### S006 · Rank-two relaxation heuristics for max-cut and other binary quadratic programs (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2002, DOI 10.1137/s1052623400382467)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-19 = printed pp. 503-521, including references).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Renato D.C. Monteiro (Georgia Tech), Yin Zhang (Rice) · **Place in trajectory**: the step from solving the SDP to restricting its rank on purpose. It produced the code CirCut and is reused as the template for S025 (S025 pp. 2-3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A question: can Goemans-Williamson-type SDP approaches "ever become competitive in attacking large-scale problems?" (p. 2). The obstacle named is scalability: the SDP has an order of magnitude more variables and constraints than the original problem (p. 2). The paper's answer, for lower bounds (cuts), is "negative, at least for some problem classes" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: frame a computational paper as a yes/no question about an established method's practical value, and say in advance which half (here lower bounds only) the evidence will address.
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace the unit vectors vᵢ ∈ ℝⁿ of the GW relaxation by unit vectors in ℝ², i.e. rank(X) ≤ 2. In polar coordinates this is the unconstrained problem min f(θ) = ½ W•cos(T(θ)) with n variables (pp. 4-5). Cuts are generated deterministically: Procedure-CUT sweeps all half-circle partitions of the sorted angles (pp. 7-8). Algorithm 1 minimizes, rounds to the best cut, perturbs that cut (most likely a saddle point) and restarts, with N non-improving perturbations allowed and M random starts (pp. 8-9). What is kept is the GW rounding geometry; the convexity, and with it the upper bound, is given up (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when an SDP is used only for its primal rounding, try the lowest rank at which the rounding still makes sense, and replace the random hyperplane by an exhaustive deterministic sweep that becomes cheap at that rank.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The relaxation is nonconvex with "multiple local, nonglobal minima" (p. 5). The GW analysis still gives γ(x*) ≥ 0.878·ψ(θ), but ψ(θ) is not an upper bound, so "there is no performance guarantee" (p. 8). The trade-off is stated up front: computational efficiency against a theoretical guarantee (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Every cut is a stationary point of f (Prop. 3.1, p. 6). A new notion, the "nonnegatively summable" matrix, characterizes maximum cuts: q(x) − q(x̄) = 2δᵀM(x̄)δ over 0/1 vectors δ (Lemma 3.3, p. 6). The Hessian at a cut equals M(x̄), so a cut that is a local minimum of f must be a maximum cut, and every other cut is a saddle point (Theorem 3.4, p. 7). The converse fails, shown by the K3 counterexample (p. 7). A bipartite-graph special case completes the picture (Prop. 3.5, p. 7).
  - Transferable: compute the Hessian of the relaxation at the images of discrete solutions and match it to a combinatorial optimality condition. This shows which discrete points can be local minima and which must be saddles, which justifies a "perturb and restart" heuristic.
- **D5 Experiments**: CirCut in Fortran90 uses a gradient method with Armijo backtracking, stopped at 10⁻⁴ relative change because "the accuracy of the minimization is not crucial" (p. 9). Three comparisons follow. (i) SBmethod (Helmberg-Rendl) at default parameters on 4 DIMACS torus instances, with an older SBmethod version that still had GW rounding (p. 10). (ii) DSDP (Choi-Ye) on 27 G-set graphs up to n = 20000, using DSDP times as reported on a different HP machine; three reasons are given for not rerunning it (p. 11, Table 4 p. 12). (iii) Hartmann's spin-glass heuristic on 20 cubic lattices, both compiled with the same options (pp. 12-15). The MAX-BISECTION extension uses the unconstrained relaxation because numerically it produced better bisections (pp. 15-17). Losses are printed: DSDP wins on G50 (p. 11); H2 gives the best cuts, and CirCut cannot match it even with long runs (p. 15).
  - Transferable: when you cannot rerun a competitor, say why in numbered reasons and compare only where the gap is large enough (orders of magnitude) that machine differences cannot reverse it (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: Built on Goemans-Williamson and challenges the practical use of SDP relaxations for lower bounds: "the SDP relaxation does not seem to hold much promise" for high-quality approximate solutions (p. 2), repeated in the conclusion (p. 18). Also compared with a physics heuristic (Hartmann) "for effectively solving a particular MAX-CUT problem arising in physics" (abstract, p. 1). The caveat on unequal outputs is repeated (pp. 9, 11).
- **D7 Writing**: Rhetorical question plus a hedged answer ("we hope to provide a partial answer", p. 2). There is an explicit "geometric interpretation" paragraph for the algorithm (p. 9). The conclusion lists three numbered factors behind the method's performance (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A heuristic and its code, plus a structural theory of stationary points. Limits are concrete: no upper bound (abstract p. 1, p. 8); the minimization version (MIN-BISECTION) breaks when all weights are positive, so "Further investigation is clearly needed" (p. 17); whether rank-two works for another problem "must be determined by an individual investigation and a careful evaluation" (p. 15). MAX-CLIQUE is named as the next candidate (p. 18). S025 takes this up through the stable-set problem.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: a known relaxation combined with a rank restriction, implemented and benchmarked against the best public SDP codes at their defaults ("The default parameter settings were used for SBmethod.", p. 10), with the missing guarantee stated (p. 8).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (direction reversed): here the move is away from the convex lifted object, to a nonconvex low-rank formulation in the original dimension, because the goal is primal solutions, not a bound (pp. 2, 4). This conflicts with the later taste "a single strong bound" (SKILL.md Research Taste 2): in 2001-02 he published a method that explicitly gives no bound.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: no engineered hard pool. Standard libraries (DIMACS torus, G-set) plus a domain-specific external family (spin glasses) are used, and the losing family is kept in the table (pp. 10-15).
- new-pattern candidate: **classify the local optima of a continuous formulation in combinatorial terms, then escape from saddle points** (Theorem 3.4 p. 7, Algorithm 1 pp. 8-9; recurs in S025 Theorem 2, Prop. 1, pp. 5-6, 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Exhaustive deterministic rounding at rank two (sort the angles, sweep every half-circle in O(n) moves) instead of random hyperplanes (pp. 7-8).
- Restart from a slight perturbation of the rounded discrete point, because theory says that point is a saddle unless it is optimal (pp. 7-9).
- Name in the text which outputs differ between the compared codes (upper bound vs none), so readers do not take times "at face value" (pp. 9, 11).

**Quotes**:
- "Since these codes produce both an upper bound and a lower bound, while our code only gives the latter, the comparisons should not be taken at face value." (p. 9)
- "CirCut does not seem to be able to achieve the same cut values as H2 even if CirCut is allowed to search for a very long time." (p. 15)

### S017 · Solving a class of semidefinite programs via nonlinear programming (Mathematical Programming 2002, DOI 10.1007/s101070100279)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1-26 = printed pp. 97-122, including references).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Renato D.C. Monteiro (Georgia Tech), Yin Zhang (Rice); Burer's affiliation is already Iowa (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the theory paper of the 2000-2002 factorization line. It generalizes S014's X = LLᵀ to a class of linear and nonlinear SDPs and gives a first-order log-barrier method with a global-convergence proof. Numerics are in companion papers ([3], [4], p. 25).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Primal-dual interior-point methods are limited at large scale by Newton systems that need "large, dense linear systems in each iteration" (p. 2), and even dual scaling still stores and factors a dense matrix (p. 2). The aim is twofold: "to reduce the number of constraints" and to allow gradient methods "free from the need for second-order information and linear system solving" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two steps (p. 6). (1) A square-slack substitution Z_I + H(y) = LLᵀ. (2) An elimination: given the "free" entries L_I (in the main case the diagonal), the remaining entries L_J are fixed uniquely by a Cholesky-like recursion (Lemma 1, eq. 10, pp. 7-8). The SDP becomes the minimization of f(L_I, y) over the "orthant" ℝⁿ₊₊ × ℝᴺ, whose dimension equals that of the SDP's feasible set (pp. 2, 9). Unlike S014, which kept the factor's rows as constrained variables, this version eliminates all equality constraints and turns the matrix cone into a positivity constraint on a vector (p. 2).
  - Transferable: if a square-slack substitution makes a problem nonconvex, follow it immediately with an elimination that leaves a simple (orthant) domain, and prove a bijection with the interior of the original feasible set.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The class (NLSDP) with D ⊆ I ⊂ K, covering maxcut-type relaxations, two Lovász theta formulations and PSD matrix completion (Examples 1-5, pp. 4-5). For the algorithm: I = D, linear g, affine H, and Assumptions 1-2 (a strictly feasible dual X and linear independence, p. 18). The transformation lives only on the interior, so the NLP "in general does not have an optimal solution" when optima lie on the boundary (p. 18).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Recursive uniqueness, "just like in a Cholesky factorization procedure" (Lemma 1, pp. 7-8). A homeomorphism Φ between L_I × ℝᵐ and int F_sdp gives equal optimal values (Lemma 2, Theorem 1, pp. 8-9). A "dual estimate" X, defined by a square linear system solved in reverse order (Lemma 3, pp. 9-10), gives the gradient as 2π(XL) (Theorem 2, pp. 10-11). Lemma 5 and Theorem 5 read dual feasibility and the duality gap directly off the gradient: X ⪰ 0 iff ∇_w f ≥ 0, and X•S = ½wᵀ∇_w f (p. 19). A compactness result (Proposition 8, a variant of Monteiro-Pang) bounds level sets (pp. 20-21). Theorem 6 shows that accumulation points are optimal (p. 22). Theorem 7 shows that the transformed log-barrier problem has a unique stationary point, the image of the central-path point, so the nonlinear change of variables creates no spurious stationary points (pp. 22-24). The barrier algorithm then converges by Theorem 6 (p. 24). Flop and space counts are given for the dense and sparse cases, with symbolic factorization for the sparsity pattern (pp. 12-16).
  - Transferable: after a nonconvex change of variables of a convex problem, prove that the transformed barrier problem has exactly one stationary point (via XS = νI). Any convergent gradient method then suffices for each barrier subproblem.
  - Transferable: define a dual estimate as the unique solution of a triangular system attached to the current factor. Both the gradient and the dual-feasibility test then come from it.
- **D5 Experiments**: none in this paper. The authors report that they "have recently implemented variants" and found the approach viable, with the numbers in companion reports [3], [4] (p. 25).
- **D6 Positioning**: Placed after Helmberg-Rendl (bundle), Homer-Peinado (X = VVᵀ) and Burer-Monteiro [2] (X = LLᵀ), whose substitutions are read as "a matrix analogy to the square-slack variable substitution" (p. 2). The paper acknowledges that the square slack "has not been regarded as practically useful" and turns that weakness into its first step (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing**: Says openly that "This paper is heavy in notation" and introduces notation gradually (p. 3). Readers are told to assume the simplest case (I = diagonal) to follow the main ideas (p. 6). Main ideas (§3.2) come before the formal statements (§3.3), and a five-example section shows the class is broad (pp. 4-5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical and algorithmic: a general transformation plus a convergent first-order barrier method for linear SDPs. Limits are stated: convergence for nonlinear SDPs "might be a much more complicated issue"; "very high accuracy should not be expected" from gradient methods (pp. 24-25); and a generic NLP solver is unlikely to be effective without exploiting structure (p. 25). Extensions (general linear SDPs, a second-order potential-reduction method) are in [4] (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: an exact reformulation of the whole problem, with equal optimal values (Theorem 1) and a bijection (Lemma 2), but into a nonconvex NLP over an orthant rather than into one convex cone (pp. 8-9). The "package the difficulty" move is there; the target space is computational, not convex.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: here the theory paper comes first and the computation is split off into companion reports (p. 25). The order is the reverse of "prototype first, theory later" within the paper, though S014 had already supplied the prototype for maxcut (p. 2).
- new-pattern candidate: **dual certificate for a factored nonconvex reformulation** (dual estimate X(w, y), Lemma 5 and Theorem 5, p. 19; Theorem 7, pp. 23-24; also S014 p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Elimination by recursion in Cholesky order to turn a matrix equality into an explicit function of a few free entries (pp. 7-8).
- Show the transformed barrier problem has no spurious stationary points by mapping stationarity back to the central-path condition XS = νI (pp. 23-24).
- Give complexity tables split into sparse, mixed and dense regimes in terms of nz(L) (Tables 1-2, p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "This paper is heavy in notation." (p. 3)
- "The square slack substitution is an old idea in nonlinear programming that has not been regarded as practically useful in general, in part because it can turn a convex feasibility set into a nonconvex one." (p. 6)

### S025 · Maximum stable set formulations and heuristics based on continuous optimization (Mathematical Programming 2002, DOI 10.1007/s10107-002-0356-4)
**Read level**: full for the main text (file pp. 1-21 = printed pp. 137-157). Appendix A (complete proof of Theorem 3(c)-(d), pp. 21-28) was read for its structure (Lemmas 6-8, the first-order conditions and the path construction), with the formula-heavy steps on pp. 26-28 skimmed. Appendix B (example, pp. 28-29) and the references (pp. 29-30) were read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Renato D.C. Monteiro (Georgia Tech), Yin Zhang (Rice) · **Place in trajectory**: the second application of the rank-restriction strategy from S006 (pp. 2-3), now to the Lovász theta SDP. Code "Max-AO" (p. 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Can the Lovász theta SDP, an upper bound on α(G), also give large stable sets (lower bounds) without solving an SDP (p. 2)? Interior-point methods are "excessively time-consuming" from |V| ≥ 500 and |E| ≥ 1000 (p. 2). The question is carried over from S006: "it is natural to ask whether or not a similar strategy would also work well for the MSS problem" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: once a strategy works on one problem, port it to the closest problem with a well-studied SDP and test which of its structural properties carry over.
- **D2 Core idea**: Restrict X in the theta SDP to rank one (X = xxᵀ) and to rank two (X = xxᵀ + yyᵀ). Both restrictions have optimal value exactly α(G) (Theorems 1 and 4, pp. 4, 10), and every feasible point induces a stable set at least as large as its objective value (pp. 4, 9). The rank-two problem is better for algorithms. Its canonical solutions at a non-maximum stable set are not local maxima (Proposition 1, p. 12), so a local search can "escape" to larger stable sets, while rank-one local maxima are exactly the maximal stable sets, where the search gets stuck (Theorem 2, p. 5; discussion p. 12). Rank three is not exact: the pentagon gives value 2 1/9 > 2 (§3.2, p. 13).
  - Transferable: when restricting a lifted relaxation to low rank, find the largest rank at which the restriction is still exact, and certify that the next rank fails with the smallest counterexample.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Simple undirected graphs (p. 1). Both low-rank NLPs are NP-hard, so only stationary points or local maximizers can be expected (p. 13). Augmented Lagrangian convergence holds "under some mild conditions" to stationary points, and to local maxima in practice (p. 15). Escapes have no guarantee (p. 18).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Cauchy-Schwarz on the support gives (eᵀx)² ≤ |S| with equality only at ±x^S (Lemma 1, p. 4). Local maximizers are classified through an auxiliary problem restricted to a support set (Lemma 3, Theorem 2, pp. 5-6). For rank two, the support of a feasible point induces a bipartite subgraph (Lemma 4, pp. 9-10). An auxiliary problem on a component bipartition is bounded by a chain of triangle, Pythagoras and Cauchy-Schwarz inequalities ((9)-(13), pp. 8-9). Lemma 5 builds a feasible path between two points whose supports use orthogonal directions w̃ ⊥ ŵ, with a monotone objective (pp. 11-12); it shows canonical points are not local maxima (Proposition 1, p. 12). Proposition 2 proves the threshold set Bᴵ_z (cut-off ε_z^{1/2}) is always bipartite, so stable sets can be extracted from infeasible iterates (p. 16). Appendix A uses first-order conditions and a rotated-path argument by contradiction (pp. 22-28). Appendix B gives an 8-node example of a local maximizer whose induced stable set is not maximal (pp. 28-29).
  - Transferable: to show a point is not a local maximizer, build an explicit feasible path along orthogonal directions on which the objective is strictly monotone (Lemma 5, p. 11).
  - Transferable: to round an approximately feasible iterate, define a support threshold from the largest constraint violation (ε^{1/2}) and prove that the thresholded support keeps the combinatorial property you need (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: Max-AO in ANSI C on one processor of an SGI Origin2000 (p. 18). The augmented Lagrangian uses limited-memory BFGS, a strong Wolfe-Powell line search and spherical projection (p. 15), and a stopping rule tailored to the problem: stop when f is within 0.01 of the size of the extracted stable set (Algorithm AL', p. 17). Three heuristics (h1, h21, h25), ten randomized runs each, on 64 DIMACS clique complements (p. 18). Max/average size and average time are reported (Tables 1-2, pp. 19-20). Success rates are 42%, 65% and 75% on the 55 graphs with known α (p. 19). Timings are calibrated with the DIMACS benchmark program, since direct time comparison is "not readily available" (Table 3, p. 20). Quality is compared with Balas-Niehaus and Benson-Ye on the 12 graphs they report (pp. 20-21). Weak families are named: "much room for improvement on some graphs — for example, the "brock" instances" (p. 19).
  - Transferable: when heuristics cannot be timed on the same machine, report the community's machine-calibration benchmark on yours (DIMACS r100.5-r500.5) next to the times (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends "the path laid in [5]" (= S006, p. 21). Credits Gruber and Rendl for noting α₁ = α(G) independently (p. 4). Places the heuristics in the line of continuous Motzkin-Straus-type approaches (p. 3). The value of the theta bound itself is explicitly kept: "the upper bound ϑ(G) that (1) provides on α(G) can be highly valuable in its own right" (p. 21).
- **D7 Writing**: Announces a "surprising result" (p. 6). A section heading is a question ("Extension to higher ranks?", p. 13). The paper turns an apparent defect into an advantage ("On the surface, the above properties seem to be disadvantages ... However, Proposition 1 actually shows a significant advantage", p. 12). The overview gives three numbered claims (i)-(iii) (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory (two exact nonconvex formulations with a classification of local optima), a heuristic and its code. Limits are stated concretely: the local maximizers of the rank-two problem are only partly characterized (p. 11); escapes sometimes fail (p. 18); brock instances are weak (p. 19). Future work: the weighted stable-set extension, with the canonical solution given explicitly; a trust-region local solver; combination with discrete heuristics; more reliable escapes (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (stop rule): generalization stops at the first rank where it fails, and the boundary is published with an explicit small counterexample (pentagon at rank three, p. 13). This is the same move as "stop generalizing at the first dimension with a certified counterexample", 11 years before the TRS work.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (claims at their level): theory and observation are kept apart ("We stress that there is no theory guaranteeing", p. 18; and the statement that in practice the algorithm was always observed to terminate, p. 17), and the incomplete classification of rank-two local maxima is stated as such, with a counterexample in Appendix B (pp. 11, 28-29).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: a strategy that worked on max-cut is carried to a new problem, prototyped with off-the-shelf NLP components, and the solver adaptations are called a first attempt ("there may be opportunities for further performance improvements", p. 15).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: an exact reformulation of the whole problem (optimal value α(G)), but by restricting rank, not by convexifying; as in S006, the lift is reversed.
- new-pattern candidate: **classify the local optima of a continuous formulation in combinatorial terms, then escape from saddle points** (Theorems 2 and 5, Proposition 1, pp. 5-6, 10-12; the rank-two heuristic, pp. 17-18; also S006 p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Rank-restrict a lifted SDP and prove the restriction is exact by showing that every feasible point induces a combinatorial object whose size bounds the objective (Lemmas 2 and 4, pp. 4, 9-10).
- Restart a local solver from a slightly perturbed "canonical" point that theory shows is not a local maximizer (the rank-two heuristic, pp. 17-18).
- A problem-tailored stopping rule: stop when the continuous objective agrees with the extracted discrete value to 0.01 (p. 17).
