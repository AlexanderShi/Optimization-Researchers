# Batch c05 · Samuel Burer · core

**Batch intent**: See how Burer used the 2009 copositive (CP) representation of nonconvex QP in 2012-2014. He extended the theory to general cones (S009) and to all of QCQP (S022). He turned it into a global solver (S008). He used the same lifted (x, X) machinery on small trust-region substructures (S012). And he deliberately weakened SDP relaxations to make them faster (S043). I read for Method 1 (one convex object), Method 2 (small substructure, report by dimension), Method 3 (conjecture vs theorem), Method 4 (test pools) and Method 5 (prototype, theory later), and for reusable proof devices.

### S008 · Globally solving nonconvex quadratic programming problems via completely positive programming (Mathematical Programming Computation 2012, DOI 10.1007/s12532-011-0033-9)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-20; reference list skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jieqiu Chen (Argonne MCS; first author) · **Place in trajectory**: this is the solver step of the CP line. The 2009 CP representation and the 2010 DNN algorithm (refs [3], [4], p. 2) are merged with the 2008 finite KKT branch-and-bound of Burer & Vandenbussche (p. 2). The code is the public QuadprogBB (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is a general nonconvex QP with linear constraints and bounds, (QP), assumed bounded, with an interior point and full-row-rank A_eq (p. 2). QP is also framed as "an important substructure occurring in other optimization problems", such as trust-region variants (p. 2). The paper starts from two existing ingredients, finite KKT branching [6] and CP/DNN relaxations [3,4], and states that the main goal is to show their merger is "an attractive method" (p. 3). A secondary goal is a practitioner tool with quadprog syntax that needs only an LP solver (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The finite branch-and-bound of [6] is kept: branching on KKT complementarities makes the tree finite, unlike spatial branching (p. 4). Two things change. The node relaxation is now the DNN relaxation (DNP) of the CP program for (NQP), which lifts the dual variables and the complementarities into the DNN matrix (pp. 5, 13). It is solved by the specialised decomposition algorithm of [4], which needs finite upper bounds on all variables (p. 5). The reformulation into the (NQP) standard form is built so that the number of complementarities is "exactly the same as the number naturally occurring in the KKT system". Free variables are therefore split after forming the KKT system, not before (p. 6). The implied complementarity λ_B∘ρ_B = 0 is enforced at children but never branched on (pp. 7, 12). Branching uses maximum violation on [0,1]-scaled variables, node selection is best-bound, and the fathoming tolerance is relative 10⁻⁶ (p. 12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: H is not PSD. The feasible set is bounded, (QP) has an interior point, and A_eq has full row rank (p. 2). These assumptions are checked in preprocessing (p. 11) and used in the dual-boundedness proof (p. 10). The DNN algorithm needs explicit finite bounds, so the dual multipliers must be bounded (pp. 5, 8). Finiteness and correctness for the new relaxation are asserted, not proved: "the same types of results can be shown" (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (i) An RLT-linearized version of the identity x^T(KKT stationarity) = 0, eq. (13)→(16), adds the constraint that makes the dual variables bounded (pp. 8-9). (ii) Boundedness is proved through recession cones. The primal-side recession cone is trivial because of the RLT bounds (17)-(18) (p. 9). The remaining cone R is shown to be {0} by recognizing it as the dual optimal face of the LP max{0 : x̂ ∈ P} and applying complementary slackness at an interior point plus full row rank (Prop. 3.1, p. 10). (iii) The proof of existence (Prop. 3.1) becomes a computation: bounds come from min/max LPs (19) over the same valid constraints (p. 10), warm-started in CPLEX (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: 243 instances in four families: BoxQP 90 (from Burer's earlier papers), Globallib 83, CUTEr 6 (3 of 9 excluded "either because of size or numerical difficulties"), and RandQP 64 from Sven Leyffer's generator (pp. 12-13, Table 1). Baselines are BurVan, the co-author's earlier code, run with its simpler relaxation "to have the best overall times for their method" (fn. 2, p. 13), and Couenne 0.3 (p. 14). The protocol (p. 14) is a relative tolerance of 10⁻⁶ for all codes, a 10 h limit, 1 GB memory, and a Pentium D 3.2 GHz. Two disclosures: BurVan runs on CPLEX 9.1 because conversion to 12.2 failed with run-time errors (p. 13), and BurVan cannot handle equality constraints, so RandQP has only two solvers (p. 14). Results are shown as log-log CPU scatter plots (Figs. 1-2, p. 15), average optimality gaps on failed instances (Table 2, p. 16) and performance profiles (Fig. 3, p. 17). "Nontrivial" means more than 10 s for at least one method, and trivial instances are excluded from the plots (pp. 14, 16). Losses are printed: "For most Globallib, RandQP and CUTEr instances, Couenne outperforms Quadprogbb" (p. 16). The extension (pp. 17-18) solves a box- and ball-constrained QP by Lagrangian bisection with QuadprogBB as subroutine on 33 instances: 31/33 solved within 1 h against 0 for Couenne. Full run details are posted as an online appendix (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds directly on the authors' own line ([3], [4], [6]); BARON and Couenne are named as "excellent off-the-shelf" global solvers (p. 2), but only Couenne is benchmarked (p. 14; BARON is not run—not explained). Differences from Couenne are explained structurally (general MINLP, spatial B&B possibly infinite, LP vs SDP node relaxations, p. 14), and the BoxQP win is attributed to relaxation strength (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing**: Main and secondary goal stated separately (p. 3). The reformulation section opens with a warning: "We caution the reader that, in order to simplify the notation, we sometimes abuse it" (p. 6). Findings are bulleted interpretations per family, losses included (p. 16). A failed design variant is recorded in a footnote (fn. 1, p. 11): bounding sums of variables gave looser bounds and "much longer time" in B&B.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions are algorithmic and software. The public QuadprogBB uses quadprog syntax and needs only an LP solver (pp. 3, 11). Stated limits and future work are brief: smarter branching-variable choice, and a better DNN subroutine, which the paper "treated as a black box" (p. 19). Limits that are only visible in the text: BurVan's CPLEX version could not be matched (p. 13), BARON is not compared (pp. 2, 14), and finiteness is not proved for the new relaxation (p. 4).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: the exact CP reformulation → DNN relaxation → solver chain (pp. 2-3, 5), with the solver as Method 1 step 6. Method 5 evidence ✅: theory is asserted rather than proved ("the same types of results can be shown", p. 4), and a failed variant is recorded with its reason (fn. 1, p. 11). The earlier code is the first baseline at its best configuration, and the version mismatch is disclosed (pp. 13-14); this matches H5. Method 4 variant ⚠: the BoxQP ladder rung (90 instances, p. 13) is reused, but the filter here removes *trivial* instances (< 10 s for every method) rather than keeping only instances the baseline fails on (pp. 14, 16). New-pattern candidate: "finite KKT-complementarity branching instead of spatial branching" (p. 4). New-pattern candidate: "ship the global solver behind a familiar local-solver interface and demonstrate it as a subroutine" (pp. 3, 17-18).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To feed a relaxation algorithm that needs bounded variables, prove the dual multipliers are bounded through recession cones. Add the RLT-linearized identity x^T·(stationarity) = 0, show the residual recession cone is the dual optimal face of a trivial LP, and kill it with an interior point plus full row rank (pp. 8-10). Then compute the bounds with min/max LPs (p. 10).
- When reformulating to a standard form, count the complementarities: create them from the original KKT system and only then split or shift variables, so that the finite B&B tree does not grow (p. 6).
- Keep implied complementarities at the children without branching on them (pp. 7, 12).
- Report "out of resources" cases as average optimality gap at termination for each method and family, next to scatter plots and performance profiles restricted to nontrivial instances (pp. 14-17).
**Quotes**:
- "The BurVan code is not publicly available, but since the second author was a co-author of the BurVan code, the code was available to us." (p. 13)
- "We attribute the performance differences mainly to the relaxation differences: we use stronger SDP-based relaxations, while Couenne uses weaker LP relaxations." (p. 16)

### S009 · Copositive programming (Handbook on Semidefinite, Conic and Polynomial Optimization 2012, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4614-0769-0_8)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-18; reference list skimmed).
**Role**: core (handbook chapter, sole author; partly a survey, partly new results) · **Coauthors**: none (Dür and Povh thanked for discussions, p. 16) · **Place in trajectory**: generalises the 2009 CP representation from the nonnegative orthant to "completely positive over ℜ₊ × K" for an arbitrary closed convex cone K (p. 2). S022 then uses this extension as its main tool (S022 p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The chapter starts from the linear conic program (8.1) and the claim that "understanding K is key" (p. 2). It turns this into a modelling rule: ask whether a new problem is a linear conic program over a well-understood K (p. 2). The concrete target is the nonconvex quadratic conic program (8.2), min x^TQx + 2c^Tx subject to Ax = b and x ∈ K. It is to be modelled exactly over the cone of matrices completely positive over ℜ₊ × K (p. 2). The text notes that this cone "may be harder to understand than K" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The optimization result follows from a convex hull result. R = clconv(L₁) (Theorem 8.1, p. 5), where R is the CP-over-ℜ₊×K set cut out by Ax = b and diag(AXA^T) = b∘b. This gives the equivalence (8.11) (Corollary 8.3, p. 10). The result is then extended in three directions. (a) Under (8.9), the x-variable is eliminated, giving a smaller cone C₀ and an object that may have interior (Theorem 8.2, pp. 6-7). (b) A quadratic constraint fixed at its min (or max) value over L is captured exactly (Theorem 8.3, p. 8): complementarity x_i x_j = 0 becomes X_ij = 0 and binarity becomes X_ii = x_i (p. 9). (c) Several such constraints can be added (p. 9). Duality is treated next (§8.3.2, pp. 11-12), then what is known about the cone (§8.4, pp. 12-14), then applications in chronological order (§8.5, pp. 15-16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: K closed and convex; L nonempty (p. 3). Theorem 8.3 needs φ_* and φ^* finite and an x̄ ∈ L with d^T(Fx̄ + f) = 0 for every recession direction d. This holds when L is bounded or the variables in the quadratic are bounded (pp. 8-9). Proposition 8.4 needs full row rank of A (p. 11). For semidefinite K, the cone must be vectorised (fn. 2, p. 2).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (i) Prop. 8.1 is a perturbation argument. Each recession term (0;d)(0;d)^T is absorbed into a feasible rank-one term (1; x̄ + d/√ε) with weight ε, taking ε → 0 (p. 4). (ii) Prop. 8.2 uses the equality case of Cauchy-Schwarz applied to b = Σζ^k(Az^k) and b∘b = Σ(Az^k)∘(Az^k), which forces Az^k = ζ^k δ (pp. 4-5). (iii) Prop. 8.3 shows, through a Gram factorisation, that Ax = b and diag(AXA^T) = b∘b ⇔ MYM^T = 0 ⇔ MY = 0. Hence R never has interior (pp. 5-6). (iv) The x-variable is eliminated with α = A^Ty ∈ K* (pp. 6-7). (v) An averaging argument shows that if the weighted sum of quadratic values equals the extreme value φ_*, every atom attains it (Theorem 8.3, p. 9). (vi) Linear independence of the 2m+1 constraint matrices is checked block by block (Prop. 8.4, p. 11). (vii) A checkable Slater-type condition gives dual interior and primal attainment (Prop. 8.5, p. 12). The proofs "have been inspired by [5, 9]" (p. 5).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no computation). The chapter reports others' computational ranges: interior-point methods struggle at n ≥ 100 even for r = 0, decomposition methods for DNN have reached n ≈ 1,000, and an adaptive method has solved standard QPs with n = 10,000 (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: The chapter is explicitly partial: it is "not meant to be a complete survey", and the reader is sent to Dür's survey [17] (p. 3). A concurrent similar result [20] (Eichfelder-Povh) and the set-copositivity work [19] are acknowledged in a footnote (p. 2). The chapter credits the first copositive representation of an NP-hard problem to [7] (p. 15). It notes that the derivation of the QAP result in [34] "is not subsumed" by its own techniques (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing**: Theorem-proof style with a short motivating paragraph per section. A "two important remarks" paragraph sets scope and admits bias (p. 3). §8.4 lists explicitly what is unknown for each cone: "Nothing is known when K is a semidefinite cone", and mixed products "ha[ve] not been studied" (p. 13). The chapter ends with applications rather than conclusions (pp. 15-16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical and expository: the generalisation from ℜⁿ₊ to ℜ₊×K (pp. 2-9), the elimination of x and the interior question (pp. 6-8), and basic duality (pp. 11-12). Limits are stated concretely. C and C* are intractable, and checking copositivity is co-NP-complete (p. 12). General conditions for R₀ to have interior "seem difficult" (p. 8). The approximation hierarchies grow exponentially, and there are O(n⁵) 5×5 submatrices (p. 14). The future direction is the cones for products of orthant, SOC and PSD cones (pp. 3, 13).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: the stated rule "When faced with a new optimization problem…" (p. 2), and the exactness hypothesis written down and then widened, from ℜⁿ₊ to general K (pp. 2-9, Theorem 8.3 hypotheses p. 8). Method 1 step 4 evidence ✅: special cases are collected (Theorems 8.4-8.9, pp. 15-16). Method 2 evidence ✅: small-dimension exactness, DNN = CP iff n ≤ 3 in the bordered notation (Prop. 8.6, p. 13), is used for explicit low-dimensional hulls, e.g. the 2-D box via slacks with n = 4 (Theorem 8.10, p. 16). The 5×5 boundary is noted (p. 14). New-pattern candidate: "a quadratic constraint held at its extreme value over L is captured exactly by its linearisation" (Theorem 8.3, pp. 8-9). The same device carries S022 (S022 pp. 2-3).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove that a lifted convex set equals a closed convex hull, use two inclusions. Absorb the recession directions by ε-perturbation (p. 4). Recover feasible atoms from any CP decomposition through the equality case of Cauchy-Schwarz (pp. 4-5).
- To add a nonconvex quadratic equation to an exact conic model, write it as "quadratic = its maximum (or minimum) over the linear slice". The linearised version is then exact because every atom of the decomposition must attain the extreme value (p. 9).
- Check whether a lifted model can have interior (here it cannot, since MY = 0, p. 6). If not, look for a variable elimination that restores it (Theorem 8.2, pp. 7-8). The answer controls strong duality and attainment (p. 12).
- In a handbook chapter, state what is unknown cone by cone (p. 13). This tells readers where the open problems are.
**Quotes**:
- "When faced with a new optimization problem, it thus seems prudent to determine if that problem can be modeled as a linear conic program over some (hopefully) well understood K." (p. 2)
- "Instead, we intend only to give an introduction to some of the main results in the area, tainted with our own biases." (p. 3)

### S022 · Representing quadratically constrained quadratic programs as generalized copositive programs (Operations Research Letters 2012, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2012.02.001)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-4).
**Role**: core (4-page letter) · **Coauthors**: Hongbo Dong (corresponding author; Wisconsin Institutes for Discovery at the time, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: completes the representation side of Method 1 for QCQP. It uses the handbook chapter's Theorem 3 (= S009 Theorem 8.3) as its "main tool" (p. 2), and in its closing remarks it reuses an idea from the S012 manuscript ([9], p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: General QCQP (1) with arbitrary symmetric Q, Q^j (p. 1). The entry point is that solving (1) is "in a certain sense" equivalent to characterising C′(F) = cl conv{(x, xx^T)} (p. 1). The CP route had covered QP with special quadratic constraints only [7], and K-constrained QP [8, 13]. The question is whether *every* bounded QCQP has a generalized-CP representation (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two constructions turn the feasible set F into the form L ∩ Q required by Theorem 1, where L is a bounded slice of a cone and Q fixes quadratics at their maximum over L (p. 2). (a) SOC route (§2, pp. 2-3). Scale F into the unit ball and lift by one coordinate so that the ball becomes the sphere ⟨w,w⟩ = 1, the maximum of ⟨w,w⟩ over L. Shift each Q^j by λ_j I so that the constraints become convex (Prop. 1, p. 2). Write each convex quadratic as an SOC constraint (Alizadeh-Goldfarb, Prop. 2, p. 3). The result is a GCP over ℜ₊ × (r+1 SOCs), with exact dimension and equation counts (Corollary 1, p. 3). (b) PSD route (§3, p. 3). Use [1 x^T; x X] ⪰ 0 with trace(X) ≤ 1 and x_i² − X_ii = 0, which forces X = xx^T. Since x_i² − X_ii ≤ 0 is implied by the PSD constraint, the equations hold at their maximum value 0. The result is a GCP over ℜ₊ × S₊^{n+1} × ℜ₊^{r+1} (Corollary 2, p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: F nonempty and bounded, scaled into the unit ball (p. 2). λ_j are chosen large enough that P^j ⪰ 0 (p. 2). Theorem 1 is a deliberately simplified variant of [8, Thm 3]: it assumes a bounded L, allows several quadratic constraints, and uses the "maximum" orientation (p. 2). K is encoded as vectors (p. 2).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (i) Lifting one dimension turns an inequality into a sphere equality, with w_{n+1}² as the slack (Prop. 1, p. 2). (ii) A convex quadratic is written as an SOC (Prop. 2, p. 3). (iii) The device of a quadratic held at its extreme value is reused twice: max ⟨w,w⟩ = 1 (p. 3), and max (x_i² − X_ii) = 0 because of PSD (p. 3). (iv) The equations are counted exactly as 2m + m′ + 1 (Corollaries 1-2, p. 3). (v) The proposed cone D(K) = {X ⪰ 0 : Xs ∈ K for all s ∈ Ext(K*)} generalises the DNN matrices. C(K) ⊆ D(K) is proved through the extreme rays xx^T (Prop. 3, p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a (no computation).
- **D6 Positioning**: Results "complement" Peña et al.'s higher-order CP approach for polynomial problems, which uses "a different generalization" (p. 2). Eichfelder-Jahn's set-semidefinite cones are acknowledged as the same object up to symmetry (p. 2). Zuluaga et al.'s hierarchies are offered as an alternative (p. 4). The D(K) idea is credited to the Burer-Anstreicher TRS manuscript (S012) "[b]orrowing some ideas" (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing**: Very compact. The key theorem is stated up front, with an honest paragraph on how it differs from the original (p. 2). Each construction ends in a corollary with an exact size count (p. 3). The remarks section states unproved claims as facts. Tractability of D(K) for products of orthants and SOCs, and C(K) = D(K) iff n₁ = 1 or n₁ = n₂ = 2, come with "For the sake of space, we do not prove these claims here" (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical: every bounded QCQP has two explicit generalized-CP representations (Corollaries 1-2, p. 3). The approximation of the new cones is the open part: "Relatively little is known" (p. 3). For PSD factors, tractability of D(K) and equality with C(K) are unknown (p. 4). The paper closes by saying the results "motivate further study of the GCP cones" (p. 4).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: the "package" view is restated ("focuses the difficulty of nonconvex QPs completely on the CP matrices", p. 1), and all of QCQP is now one linear conic program (pp. 2-3). Method 1 practice ✅: the "slight lift" by one coordinate (w_{n+1}, p. 2) appears here in 2012, a decade before the 2023 slight-lift paper cited in the SKILL. Method 2 evidence ✅: results are reported by dimension (C(K) = D(K) exactly for n₁ = 1 or n₁ = n₂ = 2, p. 4). Method 3 variant ⚠: claims are printed as established facts without proof and without the "Conjecture" label (p. 4). This departs from Method 3 step 5 (write each claim at its level). New-pattern candidate: "quadratic held at its extreme value" (pp. 2-3; cf. S009 p. 9). New-pattern candidate: "generalize DNN through the extreme rays of K*" (D(K), p. 4).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To make a nonconvex inequality-constrained set fit an exactness theorem that wants equations at extreme values, scale into the unit ball. Then add one coordinate so that the ball becomes the sphere ⟨w,w⟩ = 1, which is the maximum of ⟨w,w⟩ over the lifted convex set (pp. 2-3).
- Force X = xx^T exactly with PSD plus the diagonal equations x_i² = X_ii. Then observe that these equations sit at the maximum of a function bounded above by 0 (p. 3).
- Close every representation result with an exact count of variables and equality constraints, so that readers can judge practicality (Corollaries 1-2, p. 3).
- To approximate a new cone C(K), propose the analogue of DNN, D(K) = {X ⪰ 0 : Xs ∈ K for every extreme ray s of K*}, and prove containment through rank-one extreme rays (p. 4).
**Quotes**:
- "This approach focuses the difficulty of nonconvex QPs completely on the CP matrices." (p. 1)
- "For the sake of space, we do not prove these claims here." (p. 4)

### S012 · Second-order-cone constraints for extended trust-region subproblems (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2013, DOI 10.1137/110826862)
**Read level**: full, author preprint version ("March 4, 2011 / Revised: July 7, 2012 / Revised: November 16, 2012", p. 1). All pp. 1-27 were read, including the algebraic appendix (pp. 24-27) in full. Cite as [S012 preprint p. N].
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Kurt M. Anstreicher (Iowa colleague) · **Place in trajectory**: the first TRS-substructure paper. It introduces the term "SOC-RLT" (p. 2) and the unsolved TTRS instance family (pp. 17-23) that the SKILL says later papers reuse.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: TRS is the nonconvex problem that SDP solves exactly (eq. (1), p. 2). The question is what happens when constraints are added. The entry has two parts. Sturm-Zhang's SOC constraint for TRS1 "appears to be unique in the literature" (p. 2). Ye-Zhang had asked whether exact convex formulations exist for TRS2p and TTRS, and the paper says Ye-Zhang "motivated our study here" (p. 3). Four problems are ordered "by the complexity of the geometry of their feasible regions": TRS1, TRS2p, TRS2 and TTRS (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: An SOC-RLT constraint linearizes the product of a valid linear factor and a valid SOC constraint: (u − a^Tx)‖x‖ ≤ u − a^Tx becomes ‖ux − Xa‖ ≤ u − a^Tx (p. 2). For TRS2p, SDP + 1 RLT + 2 SOC-RLT constraints is exact (relaxation (5), p. 8; Corollary 2, p. 12). For TRS2 the analogous relaxation (8) is not exact when the cuts intersect (p. 13). For TTRS, SOC-RLT cuts are generated from *every* supporting hyperplane of the ball. This gives the semi-infinite set H, which is equivalent to all RLT products between the two ellipsoids (pp. 14-15). The cuts are separated by solving a TRS, and the relaxation r⁺(TTRS) is computed this way (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: An orthogonal transformation puts the cut(s) on x₁ (Prop. 1 shows that this does not change the relaxation, pp. 4-5). l < u, since otherwise the problem reduces to TRS in dimension n−1 (p. 8). In TTRS, H ≻ 0 and the second ellipsoid is full-dimensional (p. 14). Tractability claims are to ε tolerance through the ellipsoid method (p. 15).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (i) Pataki's rank bound for extreme points of block SDP systems, Σ r_j(r_j+1) ≤ 2m (Prop. 2, p. 5). This gives a one-line proof of TRS exactness (Lemma 1, p. 5). (ii) A 3×3 principal-minor determinant (4) shows that X₁₁ = x₁² forces X_{·1} = x₁x. A linear embedding of a lower-dimensional TRS then gives rank one (Lemma 2, p. 6). (iii) The proof splits into cases by which constraints are active (Theorems 1-2, pp. 6-7, 11). (iv) In a "structured convex combination", a rank-one shift Y − ε(u−x₁)²yy^T with y ∈ Range(Y) keeps PSD and feasibility, which contradicts extremality (Theorem 1, p. 7). (v) An explicit decomposition Y = (1−λ)Y¹ + λY² with a PSD-diagonal remainder block W writes Y through two TRS2p-feasible points (Lemma 4, pp. 8-10). The algebra is in the appendix (pp. 24-27). (vi) A tilted combination Y¹(ε) is used with the derivative of f(ε) (Theorem 2, p. 12). (vii) Separation over a ∈ bd(B) is an equality-constrained TRS, so it can be solved in polynomial time to ε, and optimization follows by the separation-optimization equivalence (Prop. 3, Cor. 3, p. 15). (viii) A counterexample is certified numerically: an n = 3 intersecting TRS2 instance has a gap of about 7%, with v from Couenne and Y̅ of numerical rank 3 (p. 13). (ix) The value of a concentric TTRS instance is verified by writing the feasible set as the union of two TRS1 instances, using the paper's own result (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: The tests run in three stages. (a) Literature failure instances: Yuan's n = 2 instance is solved by one SOC-RLT cut. The three Ye-Zhang instances give r⁺ = v with rank-one solutions (p. 16). (b) A hand-built n = 2 instance with a positive gap: r⁺ ≈ −4.0360 vs v = −4, a gap of 0.9% (pp. 16-17, Fig. 1). (c) A generator built from Martínez's local-nonglobal minimizer theorem (Theorem 3, p. 18): take the TRS global minimizer, cut it off with a second ellipsoid, and leave the local-nonglobal minimizer feasible. The idea is credited to Wolkowicz (fn. 1, p. 18). There are 1000 instances for each n = 5, 10, 20 (p. 19). Cut separation is capped at 25 "because we found that some instances would keep generating non-productive cuts due to numerical issues in the solver" (p. 19). The setup is SeDuMi 1.3, Matlab R2012a, Core 2 Quad 2.4 GHz (p. 19). v(TTRS) is *not* computed. The gap is estimated with the upper bound from the x* of the relaxation itself (p. 19). A second signal, the rank measure λ_n/λ_{n−1}, is tracked and found to correlate with gap and cut count (pp. 19-21, Figs. 2-3). "Solved" means a relative gap below 10⁻⁴ (p. 21). Table 1 reports the outcomes (p. 23). The share of instances solved by the basic SDP falls from 92.2% (n = 5) to 4.1% (n = 20). The share unsolved rises from 4.1% to 10.4%. Average times are 2.5, 5.9 and 14.3 s (p. 21). The prose groups for n = 5, 4.0% and 3.8% split by cut count (p. 21), are not the same split as Table 1's 3.7% and 4.1% (p. 23). The groups are described only as "mostly", so this is not an error.
- **D6 Positioning**: Re-proves Sturm-Zhang's TRS1 result in order to build machinery ("Our proof of this result introduces important machinery that will be required later", p. 3), and offers it as "an alternative proof … of independent interest" (p. 4). Answers Ye-Zhang's question affirmatively for TRS2p and partially for TTRS (p. 3). Contrasts its approach with Peng-Yuan, Beck-Eldar and Ai-Zhang, who give conditions for when SDP is tight: "we … strengthen the SDP relaxation generally" (p. 4). Names [21] as "the only paper in the literature to study TRS2p formally" (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**: The problems are ordered from easy to hard by geometry (p. 3). The complexity takeaway is stated explicitly (quote below). The reader is warned that "the structure of the convex combination is not particularly intuitive" (p. 6). The non-intersecting TRS2 claim is labelled a conjecture, backed by random tests at n ≤ 10 (p. 13). The paper says openly where the new relaxation is still not exact (§5.2, p. 16). The experiments are introduced as illustrating "both the computational value of our approach and the extent to which more work is needed" (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are theoretical and computational: an exact SOCP/SDP formulation of TRS2p (polynomial-time solvable), a new polynomial-time-separable SOC-RLT family for TTRS that applies to "any QCQP with two or more convex quadratic constraints" (p. 3), and its computational evaluation. Open problems are stated concretely: the non-intersecting TRS2 conjecture, closing the gap in the intersecting case (p. 13), and the complexity of TTRS (p. 3). In Table 1, up to 10.4% of instances stay unsolved (p. 23). The gap estimate depends on the relaxation's own x*, which can overstate gaps; the paper does not discuss this (p. 19).

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅: the small known case is re-derived to build machinery (pp. 3-4). Exactness is pushed to two parallel cuts, and generalization stops at the first certified counterexample, intersecting cuts with n = 3 (p. 13). The SOC × linear factor recombination is coined here (p. 2). Method 3 evidence ✅: the regularity seen in random low-dimensional tests is printed as a conjecture, not a theorem (p. 13). The rank measure is used as a second, independent signal next to the gap (pp. 19-21), an early form of the 2023 two-signal rule. Method 4 evidence ✅: literature failure instances come first (p. 16), then a generator built from a theorem about where the baseline breaks, credited to Wolkowicz (p. 18), with a disclosed cut cap (p. 19). Method 4 variant ⚠: the 2013 pool is *not* filtered to exclude instances the basic SDP solves. Instead Table 1 reports the basic SDP's share (92.2% at n = 5, p. 23). Method 1 evidence ✅: TRS2p becomes an exact linear program over one convex set in (x, X) (pp. 8-12).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove exactness of an SDP-based relaxation by showing every extreme point has rank-one Y. Use Pataki's bound for the inactive-cone cases and an explicit rank-one shift along a vector in Range(Y) for the active cases (pp. 5-7, 11-12).
- Build valid conic cuts by multiplying a valid SOC constraint by a valid nonnegative linear factor and linearizing ("SOC-RLT", p. 2). Index the semi-infinite family by supporting hyperplanes, and separate it by solving a TRS (pp. 14-15).
- Build hard test instances from a theorem about local-nonglobal minimizers: cut off the global minimizer with an extra constraint that leaves the local one feasible (p. 18).
- Track a second certificate of tightness (the eigenvalue ratio λ_n/λ_{n−1} of the lifted matrix) next to the gap, and show they agree (pp. 19-21).
- Order the problem variants by the geometry of the feasible set, and state where tractability flips (p. 3).

### S043 · Faster, but weaker, relaxations for quadratically constrained quadratic programs (Computational Optimization and Applications 2014, DOI 10.1007/s10589-013-9618-8)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1-19; reference list skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Sunyoung Kim (Ewha), Masakazu Kojima (Chuo/JST CREST) · **Place in trajectory**: the "cheaper intermediate" direction. Instead of strengthening the SDP, it gives up bound quality for speed, combining the Kim-Kojima d.c./SOCP line with SDP block structure (pp. 2-4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: SDP relaxations of QCQP give tight bounds but are expensive for interior-point methods (p. 2). SOCP relaxations in x are faster but never tighter than their SDP counterparts (Prop. 1, p. 3, via Fujie-Kojima). The entry is the gap between the two: "there should exist relaxations between the two extremes" (p. 4). The paper calls this a "middle way" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Split each A_i = B_i − (B_i − A_i), with B_i ⪰ 0 and B_i − A_i C-block diagonal for a partition C of the variables. Keep x^TB_ix as a convex (SOC) term, and linearize only the block-diagonal part through X (relaxation (10), p. 7). Grone et al.'s chordal completion (Prop. 3, p. 7) then removes all off-block entries of X, so only the blocks X_{CkCk} ⪰ x_{Ck}x_{Ck}^T remain (eq. (9), p. 7). The partition controls speed. Bound quality is controlled by choosing B_i "minimally PSD". Prop. 4 shows that a smaller B_i in the Loewner order cannot worsen the bound (p. 8). Algorithm 1 computes minimal elements by projecting a factor onto a subspace (Algorithm 1, Prop. 6, pp. 10-11). Algorithm 2 sweeps the blocks and returns a minimal element of F(A) without solving an SDP (Theorem 1, pp. 11-12). Two shifts start the sweep: the first is ρ(A_i)I, and the second is the ρ-shift of the off-block part only (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: QCQP (1) is taken as already containing every constraint of interest (p. 3). The variables are bounded, x ∈ [0,1]ⁿ, with X_jj ≤ x_j added for boundedness (pp. 5, 7). Without bounds, diagonal entries of X could grow without limit (p. 5). Optimizing over the choice of B_i is nonconvex and "likely to require as much time to solve as the SDP", so it is replaced by the minimality heuristic (p. 8).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (i) Chordal (arrow-structure) matrix completion reduces the full PSD constraint to block constraints (Props. 2-3, pp. 5, 7). (ii) A monotonicity argument: (B_i − B′_i)•(X − xx^T) ≥ 0 shows that a Loewner-smaller B gives a no-worse bound (Prop. 4, p. 8). (iii) Minimal elements can always be obtained by an SDP with any positive-definite objective C, a route that is rejected as too slow (p. 9). (iv) Algorithm 1 is proved to give the minimum element through a range-inclusion argument and an orthonormal-complement lemma (Lemma 1, Prop. 6, pp. 10-11). (v) Algorithm 2 is proved correct by induction with a contradiction on minimality (Lemma 2, Theorem 1, pp. 11-12).
- **D5 Experiments**: 400 instances: 199 MaxCut (21 Gset + 178 BiqMac), 64 BiqMac BinQP and 36 bounded GlobalLib, with n from 16 to 800 (pp. 13-14, Fig. 1). (The text says "three groups", which sum to 299 of the 400; the rest of the composition is not explained in the text.) Each instance gets 4 nested partitions, r = 1, 2, 4, 8 blocks, by repeated halving. r = 1 is the SDP base case (pp. 14-15). The design is a 2×2 ablation: first or second shift × stop after the shift (N) or run the full Algorithm 2 (Y). This gives variants 1N, 1Y, 2N and 2Y (p. 15). The solver is default SeDuMi on one core of a Core 2 Quad 2.4 GHz (p. 15). The metrics are the bound ratio β_r = b_r/b₁ and the time ratio τ_r = t_r/t₁, with preprocessing included in t_r. Instances need b₁ ≤ −0.01 so that the ratios are stable (p. 16). Results are box plots, with a paragraph explaining how to read Matlab's box plots and outlier rules (p. 16, Figs. 2-3). Findings (pp. 17-18): the second shift keeps or improves the bound as r grows, the full Algorithm 2 always helps the bound, and 2Y is best overall. Median time ratios are below 1, but the speed-up is at most about 2×, with "many individual ratios above 1". No SOCP baseline is run, even though the motivation promises "better bounds than SOCPs" (p. 4). The comparison is to SDP only (pp. 15-18).
- **D6 Positioning**: Places itself among first-order and other alternatives to interior-point SDP methods, including Burer-Monteiro [1] (p. 2), and among LP/SOCP relaxations (p. 2). Closest relatives are Kim-Kojima, Saxena-Bonami-Lee and Zheng-Sun-Li, summarized neutrally (pp. 3-4). It claims to be unique in that it "ensures a favorable block-diagonal structure … while simultaneously improving the bound quality" (p. 2). Bao-Sahinidis-Tawarmalani is not cited in this paper (reference list, p. 19).
- **D7 Writing**: Gentle introduction first: §3.1 gives the diagonal case before the general block construction (pp. 4-6). Drawbacks are stated in the introduction: choices whose impact "we do not know how to predict effectively" (p. 2). The title itself says the bound is weaker. Plot-reading instructions are given (p. 16). There is a typo: "the fourth approximately halves the fourth" (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a framework with an algorithm and computation: a tunable mixed SOCP-SDP and a fast minimal-element algorithm (pp. 4-12). The limits are concrete. There is no order-of-magnitude speed-up (p. 18). The partitions are "simplistic" and do not use the data (pp. 15, 18). Ratios vary widely with outliers (p. 18). The future directions are data-driven partition heuristics and overlapping blocks (coverings) through chordal extensions (p. 18).

**Method links**: Method 1 stop-rule evidence ✅: it builds a cheaper intermediate between SOCP and SDP (pp. 2, 4) and states what it costs. Method 1 stop-rule variant ⚠: the SKILL frames this paper as Burer's answer to Bao-Sahinidis-Tawarmalani's DNN-cost finding. The paper itself motivates by interior-point SDP cost (p. 2), does not mention DNN, and does not cite Bao et al. (pp. 2, 19). That link is the SKILL's inference, not the paper's statement. Method 5 evidence ✅: the framework is shown working before any theory predicts the effect of its choices ("we simply illustrate the general behavior", p. 2), and the losses are printed (p. 18). Method 4 variant ⚠: the tests use public libraries as given, with no hard-pool filter, because the question here is a speed/bound trade-off, not the marginal value of the new inequalities (pp. 13-14). The H4 grid is in evidence: the relaxation is sized by auxiliary variables (O(n) vs O(n²)) and by the cone (p. 5). New-pattern candidate: "2×2 factorial ablation of design choices, reported as ratios to the base case" (pp. 15-16). New-pattern candidate: "turn an intractable optimal-parameter choice into a monotone order and pick a minimal element" (Prop. 4, pp. 8-9).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To trade bound quality for speed in an SDP relaxation, choose a variable partition and keep only the blocks that the objective and constraints touch. Chordal completion (Grone et al.) shows the off-block entries can be dropped exactly (pp. 5, 7).
- When the best splitting parameter is intractable to optimize, prove a monotonicity (smaller in the Loewner order ⇒ no worse bound). Then compute a *minimal* element with a cheap combinatorial or linear-algebra sweep instead of an SDP (pp. 8-12).
- Isolate the effect of each design choice with a 2×2 factorial of variants (1N/1Y/2N/2Y) on nested partitions r = 1, 2, 4, 8. Report bound and time as ratios to the exact base case, include preprocessing in time, and screen out near-zero denominators (pp. 15-16).
- When a box plot carries the result, explain in the text how to read it, including the outlier rule (p. 16).
**Quotes**:
- "In this paper, we describe a “middle way” between SOCP and SDP relaxations." (p. 2)
- "In particular, we do not see a full order-of-magnitude speed up." (p. 18)
