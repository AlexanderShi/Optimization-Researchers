### H001 · COAP 2009 best paper award (Computational Optimization and Applications 2010, DOI 10.1007/s10589-010-9368-9)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–4, including the reference list and the two bios).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: S. Burer, D. Vandenbussche (the batch list names both as authors) · **Voice**: an unsigned award note that speaks of "the authors" in the third person. The Axioma paragraph switches to the first person ("we obtain better primal solutions", p. 3), so the note reads as the awardees' own retrospective and not as an editor's citation. Its claims are therefore stated-joint. **Place in trajectory**: looks back on 2002–2007 (p. 1). It covers the move from 0-1 IP SDP relaxations to nonconvex QP, which led to the finite SDP branch-and-bound papers and to BoxQP as a standard test case.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Each author wanted "to gain a greater understanding of the other's field of expertise" (SDP for Burer, IP techniques for continuous nonconvex problems for Vandenbussche, p. 1). The entry point was a gap between theory and computation: the Lovász–Schrijver SDP-relaxation literature was "mainly ... theory", and for realistic IPs "the large size of the proposed SDP relaxations posed a serious computational challenge" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea / design**: (i) An augmented Lagrangian algorithm with block coordinate descent: penalize some constraints, solve the subproblem loosely with a few BCD cycles (independent convex QPs plus one PSD projection by spectral decomposition, pp. 1–2). (ii) When SDP bounds proved too slow or too weak against a dual-simplex IP solver, the authors went looking for "problem classes for which these SDPs relaxations would provide uniformly good bounds in a short amount of time" (p. 2) and moved to nonconvex QP. (iii) They combined the Vandenbussche–Nemhauser KKT/complementarity finite branch-and-cut with strong SDP bounds. The "hard part" was that the LP and SDP relaxations were unbounded, and "combining features of both relaxation types" fixed it (p. 2). (iv) For the COAP paper they tailored the general method to box constraints and compared it head-to-head with the LP-based branch-and-cut (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions / model**: Nonconvex QP with a nonconvex quadratic objective and linear constraints. The COAP paper covers box constraints only, up to 100 variables (p. 3). No formal assumptions are stated in the note.
- **D4 Analysis / proof devices**: n/a (no proofs). Named structural devices: reformulation through KKT conditions as an LP with complementarity constraints, which gives finite branching (p. 2), and LP+SDP combination to make relaxations bounded (p. 2).
- **D5 Experiments**: Summarized only. Loose subproblem solves still gave convergent multipliers and "best-known bounds for several unsolved problems" in QAPLIB (p. 2). The SDP-based B&B "far outperformed" the LP-based branch-and-cut on BoxQP up to n = 100 (p. 3). No tables.
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits Monteiro's survey as precedent for first-order large-scale SDP (p. 1). Claims priority with a hedge: "To the authors knowledge, this is one of the first clear examples in which an SDP-based branch-and-bound algorithm outperforms an LP-based one" (p. 3). Lists later uses of the augmented-Lagrangian + BCD idea (Povh et al., Wen et al., Zhao et al., Burer [5]) and BoxQP becoming "a standard test case in mixed integer nonlinear programming" (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**: A narrative arc in three moves: motivation (cross-field curiosity), obstacle (SDP too slow for IP), pivot ("So the authors shifted their focus away from IPs to continuous problems", p. 2). Then a moral stated as a general lesson (p. 3). Hedges are rare and placed on the priority claim.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The lessons drawn are practical power of SDP relaxations for continuous nonconvex problems, paying for an expensive but strong relaxation, and classic methods (augmented Lagrangian, BCD) still mattering (p. 3). Industrial echo: Axioma uses stronger SOCP relaxations to get better primal solutions "and better bounds to convince clients" (p. 3). Limits are not discussed. No future directions beyond the list of follow-on work (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: loose inner solves were found by experiment ("Somewhat surprisingly ...", p. 2). This is the H7 pattern, and the note gives the origin story of the loose-subproblem habit.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak): BoxQP grew from their test bed into "a standard test case" used by others (p. 3), which is the ladder that M4 describes.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the "strong but expensive relaxation pays" credo (p. 3) is the computational side of M1. Here the object is an SDP+LP relaxation inside finite B&B, not an exact convex reformulation.
- New-pattern candidate: **cross-field pairing to learn the other's field**. The collaboration was "mostly motivated by the desire of each author to gain a greater understanding of the other's field of expertise" (p. 1). The SDP × IP pairing produced the first SDP-beats-LP B&B (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: **look for the problem class where the strong relaxation wins uniformly** (p. 2). This is H1 (let the tool choose the problem class), already in SKILL.md, and the note confirms it.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When your relaxation is strong but slow on class A, do not keep optimizing it there. Ask which class gives "uniformly good bounds in a short amount of time" (p. 2) and move to it.
- To get a finite B&B for a continuous nonconvex QP, reformulate through KKT conditions as an LP with complementarity constraints and branch on complementarity (p. 2).
- In an augmented-Lagrangian SDP solver, try one or a few BCD cycles per outer iteration before solving subproblems accurately. The multipliers can still converge (p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "Somewhat surprisingly, computational experiments showed that the dual multipliers converged reasonably well even when the subproblems were solved very loosely by only a few cycles of block coordinate descent." (p. 2)
- "More generally, the paper also illustrates that it can be worthwhile to pay for a computationally expensive, but strong, relaxation" (p. 3)

### S010 · Computable representations for convex hulls of low-dimensional quadratic forms (Mathematical Programming Ser. B 2010, DOI 10.1007/s10107-010-0355-9)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 2–12; file p. 1 is the publisher cover sheet; references skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Kurt M. Anstreicher (corresponding author) · **Place in trajectory**: received 27 May 2008 (p. 2). The bibliography of S016 dates the manuscript to February 2007, revised June 2009 (S016 preprint p. 31). This is the first exact-hull-in-low-dimension paper in the Anstreicher–Burer line, built directly on the 2009 copositive representation [5] (p. 3). It is the ancestor of the 2013 TRS/SOC-RLT work and of the later "kitchen sink" program.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Represent C = Conv{[1 x; x xxᵀ] : x ∈ F}, which is "a fundamental problem for global optimization methods based on convex relaxations of products of variables, for example the popular BARON algorithm" (p. 3). F is usually simple because it comes "via a partitioning of the underlying feasible set", so the paper takes the "two most common choices for F, a simplex and a box" in low dimension (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea / design**: Read known completely-positive (CP) matrix theory as convex-hull theorems. (i) Simplex, n ≤ 4: C = D_S (DNN matrices with E•X = 1). Proof: CP = DNN in that dimension (Kogan–Berman graph criterion), and the extreme points of {X ∈ CP : E•X = 1} are rank one (Theorem 1, pp. 4–5). An affine map carries this to triangles and tetrahedra (Corollary 1, p. 5). (ii) Box, n = 2: C = PSD ∩ RLT (Theorem 2, p. 7). The device is the bordered transform Y⁺ = TYTᵀ, which turns PSD ∩ RLT into a DNN condition on (x, X, s, Z, S) (p. 6), followed by Burer's 2009 CP representation with slacks (Prop. 3, p. 7). (iii) n ≤ 3: any triangulated polytope gives a disjunctive representation D_P (Theorem 3, p. 9), and the 3-cube is handled with 6 tetrahedra. Changed relative to the classics: Linderoth's triangle and Al-Khayyal–Falk's box results are recovered as special cases. Unchanged: the lifted (x, X) space.
- **D3 Assumptions / model**: F is a regular simplex, a box [0, e] (a hyper-rectangle [l, u] by affine transformation with general RLT constraints, p. 8), or a triangulated polytope of low dimension. The parallelepiped is omitted "since this case does not commonly occur in practice" (p. 8). The dimension limits n ≤ 4 and n = 2 are sharp, not a convenience: known counterexample for the simplex at n > 4 (p. 5); explicit counterexample for the box at n = 3 (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis / proof devices**:
  - Extreme-point argument. Every extreme point of the DNN set is CP by the graph criterion (Prop. 2), then rank one (Prop. 1), so it lies in C. Compactness closes the proof (Theorem 1, p. 5).
  - Slack-variable lifting and an explicit CP factorization. Build the 2×2-block CP factor of [X Z; Zᵀ S], then rescale the columns with λᵢ = ½eᵀ(xⁱ + sⁱ) to get a CP factor of Y⁺ (Theorem 2 proof, p. 7). An alternative proof via Pataki's extreme-point rank theory is mentioned, but the authors "prefer the proof given since it is both simpler and more closely related to the analysis for the case F = S" (p. 7).
  - Explicit counterexample. Q = (1/3)eeᵀ − I at n = 3 has true value −2/3 against a relaxation value of −3/4 (p. 8). Q was found "by detailed examination of the sets D_Tijk" of the triangulation (p. 10).
  - Symmetry propagation of a cut. Complementing variables (x_j → 1 − x_j) takes the box to itself, and the 2³ = 8 combinations give 4 distinct cuts P₀…P₃ (p. 10).
  - Slice-and-plot. Fix x = diag(X) = e/2, the "worst possible" point, and plot the 3-D projection onto (X12, X13, X23). The cut set R̄₂ is a tetrahedron whose vertices lie in C, so on that slice the four cuts give the hull (pp. 10–11).
  - Numerical non-membership certificate. Ŷ ∈ (D_B ∩ cuts) \ C "has been established numerically using the disjunctive formulation for C given by Theorem 3" (p. 11).
- **D5 Experiments**: Light and exploratory. On random n = 3 problems the exact QPB value "is almost always given by" PSD ∩ RLT (p. 9). On 15 Vandenbussche–Nemhauser instances of size n = 30, the gap has a maximum of 3.06%, averages 0.41%, and is 0.00% on 8 instances. These figures are cited from Anstreicher [1] and are not new runs (p. 9). Fig. 1 boundaries are computed numerically. The authors warn that "one cannot be sure from the figure alone that R̄2 is a tetrahedron, but this can be easily verified" (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: "This result is a straightforward consequence of existing theory for completely positive matrices, but to our knowledge does not appear in the literature" (p. 3). The paper generalizes and simplifies Linderoth [8], which extends Sherali–Alameddine (p. 3), and extends the known fact that RLT gives the hull for {(x1, x2, x1x2)} on a box (p. 3). It compares even-handedly with Jach–Michaels–Weismantel's explicit bivariate envelopes: their advantage is explicit formulas, their disadvantage is case analysis "depend[ing] on the particular quadratic form" (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing**: The introduction announces every negative result next to the positive one: the n > 4 counterexample, the "example to show that the given representation for C does not hold when n > 2", and that the derived cuts "are still not sufficient" (p. 3). The exploratory section is labelled as such: "While we have not been able to answer this question fully, we now describe some exploratory results" (p. 9). The paper is short (11 printed pages), and the theorems have one-paragraph proofs.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: exact computable hull representations (simplex n ≤ 4, box n = 2, polytope n ≤ 3 by disjunction). Exploratory: four new cuts for the 3-cube. The limit is stated concretely: the cuts do not give C, and "Obtaining constraints on Y that fully characterize C" for the 3-cube "remains an interesting open problem" (pp. 11–12). The disjunctive form needs extra variables. The paper asks for a description "without adding additional variables" (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (strong, earliest instance in the corpus read so far). Small substructures (n ≤ 4 simplex, n = 2 box) that recur inside B&B partitions (p. 3). The best known relaxation (PSD ∩ RLT) is shown to be exact at n = 2 and to fail at n = 3 by a certified counterexample (p. 8). A disjunctive representation is used as a proof/certification device, and a direct description is sought (pp. 9, 11). The paper stops and publishes the boundary as an open problem (p. 12). This matches M2 steps 6–7 and its stop rule.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the 2009 CP representation (Prop. 3) is the engine of the box proof (p. 7). The low-dimensional hulls are read off from properties of the CP/DNN cones.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: computation proposes. Random n = 3 instances are "almost always" exact (p. 9), and the counterexample Q came from inspecting the triangulation (p. 10). But the non-membership Ŷ ∉ C is certified only "numerically" through the disjunctive SDP (p. 11), not in exact arithmetic. This fits the 2009–2013 practice that SKILL.md says predates the exact certificates of 2025–26.
- New-pattern candidate: **reuse your own exact representation as a lemma for small hulls**. The 2009 CP theorem becomes the proof device for a low-dimensional hull result one year later (p. 7). See also S016 Theorem 2.4.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To test whether a relaxation R equals a convex hull C in dimension n, write a disjunctive exact representation of C (e.g., a triangulation into simplices, each with a DNN representation) and solve over it. This gives a computable membership test for candidate points and objective vectors, even when no explicit description is known (pp. 9, 11).
- To find a separating objective, inspect the pieces of the disjunction (here the six tetrahedra D_Tijk) for where R is weakest (p. 10). Then multiply cuts by symmetry: apply the box's complementation maps and keep the distinct images (p. 10).
- To visualize a hull gap, fix the "worst" values of x and diag(X) and plot the remaining three off-diagonal entries in 3-D. Then check combinatorially that vertices are in C and facets are your cuts (pp. 10–11).
- Turn PSD ∩ RLT into a DNN condition with the bordered transform Y⁺ = TYTᵀ, T = [1 0; 0 I; e −I]. Then any CP/DNN theorem applies to box-constrained problems (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "This result is a straightforward consequence of existing theory for completely positive matrices, but to our knowledge does not appear in the literature." (p. 3)
- "While we have not been able to answer this question fully, we now describe some exploratory results." (p. 9)

### S020 · Optimizing a polyhedral-semidefinite relaxation of completely positive programs (Mathematical Programming Computation 2010, DOI 10.1007/s12532-010-0010-8)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 2–20; file p. 1 is the cover sheet; references skimmed; the online appendix with per-instance QAP results, p. 15 footnote, was not read).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: sole author · **Place in trajectory**: received 25 Dec 2008 (p. 2). The first computational payoff of the 2009 CP representation: theory (2009), then a DNN solver (this paper, the decomposition later used in QuadProgBB), then B&B. It replaces his own Burer–Vandenbussche augmented Lagrangian as the BoxQP state of the art (p. 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: (NQP) has nonnegative and binary variables, linear equations and complementarities (p. 3). By Burer [6] it is equivalent to a completely positive program (CPP), which is convex but NP-hard (pp. 3–4). The tractable DNN relaxation is polynomial but expensive. The entry point is a timing of the bottleneck: SeDuMi on standard QPs took "1, 30, 385, and 1787" seconds for n = 25, 50, 75, 100, because the Newton system is O(n² × n²) (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea / design**: An augmented-Lagrangian decomposition that splits DNN constraints into two easy groups: {Ax = b, diag(AXAᵀ) = b², Y ⪰ 0} and {x_B = diag(X_BB), X_E = 0, Y ≥ 0} (p. 4). Through the reformulation MYMᵀ = 0 ⇔ MY = 0 (Prop. 1, p. 6), the linear equations are moved into the cone J = {Z ⪰ 0 : MZMᵀ = 0} = {NPNᵀ : P ⪰ 0} (Lemma 1, p. 7). Projection onto J costs one small spectral decomposition, and the Y-subproblem separates into O(n²) scalar box QPs (p. 9). A valid lower bound is available at any time from the Lagrangian with σ = 0 (pp. 9–10). Changed relative to Burer–Vandenbussche [7]: symmetry of Y is enforced and the column QP subproblems solved by CPLEX disappear. Unchanged: AL framework, one BCD loop, bound every 25 iterations (pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions / model**: Standard form Ax = b, x ≥ 0 is "critical for our paper" (p. 3). Known (possibly infinite) upper bounds x ≤ u give U = (1, uᵀ; u, uuᵀ) and Y ≤ U. Finite bounds ensure a finite lower bound (pp. 5, 10). N is an orthonormal null-space basis of M = [b, −A] (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis / proof devices**:
  - Gram-factor argument for MYMᵀ = 0 ⇒ MY = 0: trace(MVVᵀMᵀ) = ‖MV‖² = 0 (Prop. 1, p. 6).
  - Null-space parametrization of a face of the PSD cone (Lemma 1, p. 7).
  - Projection formula proj_J(R) = N proj⪰0(NᵀRN) Nᵀ, by completing the square in the inner product. proj_J* follows from Moreau's decomposition (Prop. 2, pp. 7–8). Cost O(n²k) (p. 8).
  - Valid bound from Lagrangian relaxation: with S ∈ J* the Z-part has value 0, leaving a separable O(n²) problem (pp. 9–10).
  - No convergence proof of the overall method is given or claimed. The σ rules are experimental (pp. 11, 16).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Setup: Matlab + C/MEX kernels, single thread forced by maxNumCompThreads(1) (p. 11).
  - σ update: σ ← (1 + (v − v̄)/(1 + |v̄|))σ, with a safeguard of more BCD loops if the ratio is nonpositive (pp. 11–12). σ₀ is calibrated per class because no universal rule was found (p. 12). Stop at 10⁻⁵ average relative bound change or 6,000 iterations (p. 12).
  - Fair-time protocol: "run time" is the time to first reach v̂ = min of the two methods' best bounds (p. 12).
  - BoxQP: 99 instances = 54 (VN) + 36 (BV) + 9 new at p = 125, generated the same way (p. 12). Log-log scatter with y = x and y = 10x reference lines. Algorithm 1 is 10.21× faster on average and about 7× per iteration, which "explains Fig. 1 to a large degree" (p. 13).
  - QAP: all 92 QAPLIB instances with p ≤ 36. 4.83× vs Burer–Vandenbussche, 2.94× vs Zhao–Sun–Toh, whose code was augmented with Jansson et al.'s rigorous bounds to make bounds valid (pp. 14–15). Time-per-iteration ratio vs p trends upward, so the advantage shrinks with size because both methods share the O(p⁶) PSD projection (pp. 14–15). Best known lower bound on tai35b, while ZST gets tai30b (p. 15).
  - B&B for BoxQP: replicates the exact BV tree (branching, node selection, fathoming, heuristic) and swaps only the node solver. Warm start pads S and sets σ₀ = √σ_parent, with the square root found by experiment. 1000-iteration limit per node for both methods (p. 16). Results: 11.28× faster on average, 20.50× at p = 125 (p. 17).
  - Quadratic (multiple) knapsack: extended form with complementarities (p. 18). "Not competitive" with Pisinger et al. on the single knapsack (p. 18). Table 1 reports mean (sd) times over 50 instances per (p, q) cell, p ≤ 50, q ∈ {1, 5, p/2} (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Places itself in a line of AL decomposition SDP solvers that "began with the work of [7]", his own (p. 4). Baselines are his own previous method and the strongest recent general method (ZST) (p. 4), whose authors shared their code (p. 19). The state-of-the-art claim for BoxQP is by transitivity: BARON < VN branch-and-cut < BV < Algorithm 1 (p. 17). This is exactly the "BARON beaten by transitivity" gap that SKILL.md H5 records. Gives credit where others win: de Klerk–Sotirov preprocessing obtains best bounds such as esc32h (p. 14), and ZST obtains tai30b (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing**: The introduction ends with numbered "main conclusions" (i)–(iv) (p. 5). Comparison caveat: "we caution the reader that many of the details of [7] are overly simplified for the sake of brevity" (p. 10). Hedged equivalence claim: "we believe, but have not rigorously proved, that the two are equivalent" (pp. 13–14). Candid failure statements: "Despite many attempts, we have been unable to establish a single rule or strategy for setting σ0" (p. 12). Each implementation rule is given with its reason ("The reason for the square root is experimental", p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and software contribution: a state-of-the-art BoxQP global solver at the time (p. 17) and a general DNN relaxation solver (p. 5). Specific limits: σ₀ sensitivity (p. 12), the O(p⁶) projection bottleneck (p. 15), loss to specialized knapsack codes (p. 18). Future: generalize to arbitrary linear conic programs over the DNN cone. The open design question is which constraints go to the Y and which to the Z subproblem (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the 2009 exact CP reformulation, then its tractable outer approximation (DNN), then a specialized solver, then B&B (pp. 3–5). This is the "2009 theory → OptDNN 2010" path of M1 step 6. The M1 stop rule is visible too: the paper exists because IPM solution of the DNN was too slow (p. 4).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the bottleneck is timed first (SeDuMi 1/30/385/1787 s, p. 4). Inner problems are solved loosely ("it pays only to solve these problems very loosely", p. 11). σ rules come from experiment (pp. 11, 16). There is no convergence theory.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the BoxQP ladder 54 → 90 → 99, adding 9 instances at p = 125 "generated in the same way" (p. 12). The 54/36 old rungs are kept.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: claim levels are graded ("we believe, but have not rigorously proved", pp. 13–14). Here it concerns relaxation equivalence, not tightness.
- New-pattern candidate: **swap one component, freeze the rest**. To compare node solvers in B&B, he replicates his earlier code's tree exactly and changes only the relaxation solver, so that run-time differences can be attributed (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: **equal-bound timing**. Time each method to the common best bound v̂ instead of to its own termination (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before designing a first-order method, time an IPM on a ladder of sizes and name the operation that blows up (here the O(n² × n²) Newton system, p. 4).
- Put linear equality constraints into the cone by squaring them (MYMᵀ = 0 ⇔ MY = 0 for Y ⪰ 0). The cone {Z ⪰ 0 : MZMᵀ = 0} = N·PSD·Nᵀ then projects at the cost of one small eigendecomposition (pp. 6–8).
- Keep a valid lower bound at every outer iteration with a dual-feasible multiplier (S ∈ J*) so that the Lagrangian evaluates in closed form. This makes an inexact SDP solver usable inside B&B (pp. 4, 9–10).
- Fair comparison of two bounding algorithms: define each method's run time as the time to first reach the common bound v̂ = min(best_A, best_B). Plot log-log with y = x and y = 10x reference lines (pp. 12–13). Also decompose the speed-up into per-iteration cost × iteration count (p. 13).
- To compare relaxation solvers inside B&B, freeze the branching rule, node selection, fathoming tolerance and heuristic of the incumbent code and swap only the solver (p. 16). Warm-start child nodes from parent multipliers and damp the penalty (σ₀ = √σ) (p. 16).
- When adding a new baseline that lacks valid bounds, attach a rigorous-bound post-processor (Jansson et al.) and report that it did not change its run times much (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "Consistent with the experience of Burer–Vandenbussche [7] and other augmented-Lagrangian methods for SDP, we find that it pays only to solve these problems very loosely." (p. 11)
- "Though our method was successful for p ≈ 50, it was not competitive with the specialized method of [20]." (p. 18)

### S016 · The MILP road to MIQCP (Mixed Integer Nonlinear Programming, IMA Vol. 154, 2011/12, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4614-1927-3_13)
**Read level**: full, read in the authors' **preprint** version (manuscript pp. 1–33, references skimmed). The printed chapter's page numbers were not available, so page references are preprint pages: `[S016 preprint p. N]`.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Anureet Saxena (Axioma) · **Place in trajectory**: a survey-with-new-results written about 2009–2010 (it cites S020 and S010 as "To appear", p. 31). It sets the road map that SKILL.md H2 names. Sections 4.1–4.3 review Saxena–Bonami–Lee [52, 53]. Sections 2.5.3 and 2.5.5 contain new theorems (pp. 9, 11). The paper does not say who wrote which part.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: MIQCP. Entry by analogy: track "the influence of MILP in solving" MIQCP, both to catalog results for an MILP-trained reader and to define "a roadmap for further research" (p. 1). The stance: view MIQCP "as a natural progression from MILP rather than, say, a special case of MINLP" (p. 2). Explicit scope: "We are also not specifically concerned with the global optimization of (MIQCP)", but with strong convex relaxations and valid inequalities (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea / design**: A single organizing object, clconv F̂ in the lifted space (x, X = xxᵀ). The "hard" quadratic constraints become "easy" linear ones, and all nonconvexity sits in X = xxᵀ (p. 4). Linear objective implies min over clconv F̂ (Prop. 2.2, p. 5). The chapter then (i) builds the static chain clconv F̂ ⊆ L̂ ∩ RLT ∩ R2 ∩ S2 ∩ PSD from products of valid inequalities (Prop. 2.3, p. 7). (ii) It catalogs when equality holds: box n ≤ 2, binary grid n ≤ 2 (Padberg), orthant n ≤ 3 (new), simplex n ≤ 4, half-ellipsoid any n (Sturm–Zhang), bounded quadratic form up to a recession cone (new) (pp. 7–12). (iii) It covers dynamic cuts: X − xxᵀ ⪯ 0 is rewritten as univariate constraints ⟨X, vvᵀ⟩ ≤ (vᵀx)² along eigenvectors of X̂ − x̂x̂ᵀ. A secant-split disjunction gives a CGLP (pp. 18–20), the analogue of split cuts (p. 30). (iv) It covers projected relaxations in the x-space (pp. 26–27).
- **D3 Assumptions / model**: The (MIQCP) data and bounds include possibly infinite l, u. Integer variables have integer bounds (p. 2). Constraints are partitioned into LQ / CQ / NQ (p. 3). Theorem 3.3 needs boundedness only of the binary and complementarity variables in {x ≥ 0 : Ax = b}: "it is only important that certain variables are bounded" (p. 16). Theorem 3.5 (sequential convexification) needs bounded F and no integers (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis / proof devices**:
  - Product-of-valid-inequalities linearization (RLT, rank-2 linear, and the new "rank-2 second-order" SOC × linear inequalities S2, with a worked example) (pp. 5–6).
  - Recession-cone decomposition. Show L̂ ∩ RLT ∩ PSD = conv F̂ + rcone, using a CP factorization of the DNN matrix (DNN = CP for size ≤ 4). Split factor columns by whether ζ_j > 0 (Lemma 2.2, p. 9). Then a separating-hyperplane contradiction via a negative recession direction d ≥ 0 extracted from a CP factor (Theorem 2.4, p. 10).
  - Pataki rank bound r̄(r̄+1)/2 + s̄ ≤ p + q, with case analysis on whether b_l and b_u are finite, gives rank ≤ 1 at extreme points (Theorem 2.7, pp. 11–12).
  - Binary-shift identity (x_i − l_i)² = x_i − l_i, linearized (Prop. 2.4, p. 8).
  - Worked CGLP example on GLOBALLib st_ph11. A disjunction from a secant split at θ = 2 gives an explicit cut. The authors show that even full convexification with respect to bounds does not cut off x̂, because the general linear constraint must enter "in a more aggressive fashion" (pp. 21–22).
  - Open question stated: rcone(clconv F̂) vs D̂ "is unclear" (p. 12). One-variable step of Theorem 3.5 "likely to be NP-hard" (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Reproduced study (Saxena et al. [52]): 129 GLOBALLib instances, filtered to "Only instances having vRLT > v* were selected" (p. 23). Metric: % duality gap closed relative to the RLT relaxation (p. 23). Ablation ladder V1 (PSD), V2 (PSD + disjunctive), V2-SI (secant instead of disjunctive), V2-Dsj (no PSD). Average gap closed: 24.80 / 76.49 / 44.40 / 41.54% (p. 24). Explanation: global CGLP view vs local 2-D secant. Removing PSD has a "debilitating effect" (p. 24). Eigenvalue-sum trajectories are shown on three instances chosen by stated selection criteria (Table 2, p. 25).
  - New case study: relaxation (5.1) for BoxQP on 9 spar100 instances. Solvers: ipm (black-box IPM), cp (S020's solver), proj (Saxena et al. projected cuts). Times: ipm 5,355–10,296 s, cp 54–101 s, proj 538–1,510 s preprocessing + 0.8–2.0 s solve (p. 28). Gap closed: cp 98.17–99.39% vs proj 92.16–96.47% (p. 29). The amortization argument: proj's preprocessing pays off over a B&B tree because cuts are mainly generated at the root (p. 29).

Computational comments come as numbered "First ... Fifth" remarks (pp. 23–24, 28–29). The paper closes with a comparative table, not prose (p. 30).

Field limits stated: relaxations are "currently solved by interior point methods that lack efficient re-optimization capabilities making them bottlenecks" in B&B (p. 30). Exact descriptions are "unknown for most classes of problems except for some very small problem instances" (p. 30). Future: LP outer approximations of the convex quadratic cuts (Ben-Tal–Nemirovski) to get warm-startable LPs and reuse MILP machinery (p. 29). The "breakthrough" results are "yet to come" (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To derive valid inequalities in the lifted space, multiply every pair of known valid constraints, including linear × SOC, and linearize with X = xxᵀ. Name each family by its source (RLT, R2, S2) and then ask, family by family, whether it adds anything to the hull (pp. 5–7).
- Exactness proof pattern for unbounded sets: show relaxation = conv F̂ + recession cone. Then argue by contradiction that a separating objective would have a negative direction of recession, hence an unbounded true problem (Theorem 2.4, pp. 9–10).
- For a feasibility system "PSD + p equalities + q inequalities", apply Pataki's rank bound case by case on which bounds are finite to force rank-one extreme points (pp. 11–12).
- Ablation design for a cut family: compare (base + new cuts) vs (base + cheap surrogate cuts on the same nonconvexities) vs (weaker base + new cuts). This separates the value of the cut machinery, of the information source and of the base relaxation (pp. 23–24).
- To show why a stronger relaxation is needed, give a 3-variable worked example where even complete convexification with respect to bounds fails to cut off the point (pp. 21–22).

### S002 · Non-convex mixed-integer nonlinear programming: A survey (Surveys in Operations Research and Management Science 2012, DOI 10.1016/j.sorms.2012.08.001)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 2–9 text; file p. 1 is the Elsevier cover sheet; reference list pp. 9–11 skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Adam N. Letchford (Lancaster) · **Place in trajectory**: received 9 July 2012, accepted 8 August 2012 (p. 2). This is his most-cited survey (859 cites in the batch list) and the field-level entry point for the non-convex MINLP case. Its quadratic section places his own CP/BoxQP work (Chen & Burer, Anstreicher & Burer, Burer & Letchford, Burer & Saxena) in context (pp. 7–8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The convex/non-convex split. For non-convex MINLP "even the continuous relaxation is a global optimization problem" (abstract, p. 2). Some simple cases are "even undecidable", though all such proofs use unbounded feasible regions, and in practice regions are bounded (p. 3). "Some relatively small non-convex MINLPs, with just tens of variables, can cause existing methods to run into serious difficulties" (p. 3). Existing surveys cover the convex case, so this one concentrates on the non-convex case and on the quadratic case, which is "also of interest to ourselves" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea / design**: An organization by ingredients: (§3) under/over-estimators, separable functions, factorization, standard vs spatial branching (pp. 3–5); then algorithms built from them: spatial B&B, branch-and-reduce, αBB, MILP conversion, others, heuristics (pp. 5–6); then the quadratic case: binary QP, RLT, SDP/CP, polyhedral theory and convex analysis, extra techniques, polynomial extensions (pp. 6–8); then software (p. 8). Each concept gets one concrete example: McCormick envelopes (p. 3), αBB under-estimator f + xᵀDiag(α)x − αᵀx (p. 4), factorization of exp(√(y1y2 + y3)) (p. 4), spatial-branching tightening on z = y² with Figs. 1–2 (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions / model**: General MINLP min{f⁰ : fʲ ≤ 0, x ∈ ℤ₊^{n1}, y ∈ ℝ₊^{n2}} (p. 2). Polynomials can be reduced to quadratics with auxiliary variables, with "substantial overhead" possible (pp. 3, 6).
- **D4 Analysis / proof devices**: n/a (no proofs). The expository devices are listed under D2.
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits the chemical-engineering community for "some of the first and most influential research in MINLPs" (p. 3) and for extending spatial B&B to integer variables (pp. 4–5). Branch-and-reduce is called "a major step forward" (p. 5). On αBB's advantage (no auxiliary variables) and cost (needs a convex NLP solver), the tone is even-handed (p. 5). Leyffer et al.'s warning is noted: naive PLF approximation can make a feasible MINLP infeasible (p. 6). Lists exactly five global solvers (BARON, αBB, LINDO-Global, Couenne, GloMIQO) and heuristic packages (p. 8). Self-citation is moderate and confined to §§5.3–5.5 (pp. 7–8).
- **D7 Writing**: A table of contents and a structure paragraph up front (pp. 2–3). Each method gets a short paragraph with attribution. The conclusion uses first-person advocacy: "We believe", "We also advocate" (pp. 8–9). There is little hedging in the body. The survey states its own aim modestly: "we have tried to give a fair and up-to-date introduction" (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An expository contribution (no new results). Future directions (pp. 8–9): (i) MINLP-specific insight beyond MILP and global optimization, especially convex hulls needing "both polyhedral theory and convex analysis"; (ii) algorithms for special cases, which "allow the development of newer techniques (e.g., semidefinite relaxations), which may then progress to more general techniques"; (iii) "an increasing place for heuristics and approximation algorithms". The survey's own limits are not discussed. Coverage is restricted by design to the non-convex case (p. 3).
