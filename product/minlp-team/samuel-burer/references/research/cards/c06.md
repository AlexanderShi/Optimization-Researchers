### S021 · The trust region subproblem with non-intersecting linear constraints (Mathematical Programming 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10107-014-0749-1)
Read level: full (pp. 1–12, including the references).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Boshi Yang (Dept. of Mathematics, Univ. of Iowa, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: proves the 2013 Burer–Anstreicher open case. It extends the m = 2 parallel-cut exactness to any number m of non-intersecting cuts (p. 3), and is the paper later cited as [12] in S024 (S024 p. 21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The question is when the convex relaxation (Rm), built from the SDP plus all SOC-RLT and RLT constraints, is exact for the TRS with m linear cuts. Exactness was already known for m = 0, m = 1 and m = 2 with parallel cuts, and Burer–Anstreicher had a counterexample for two cuts that intersect inside the ball (pp. 1–3). That counterexample "leaves open" the non-intersecting case, which is where the paper enters (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: use a published counterexample's distinguishing geometric feature (here, cuts meeting inside the ball) as the hypothesis to negate, then aim for a two-sided demarcation: exact precisely when the feature is absent (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Prove that every extreme point of the compact F(Rm) has X = xxᵀ, so v(Rm) = v(Tm) and the problem is polynomial-time solvable (p. 3). The relaxation itself is not new: it is the "kitchen sink" with every SOC-RLT and pairwise RLT constraint (p. 3). What is new is an extreme-point argument by induction on m that works for general m (p. 7). Section 2 first shows that Ye–Zhang's multi-step m = 2 method already extends to non-intersecting cuts, so the novelty is the general-m approach (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: before claiming novelty, show how far the rival's method already reaches, then say exactly where it stops ("we could not see how to extend…", p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: F(Tm) ≠ ∅. Assumption 1: no two hyperplanes aᵢᵀx = bᵢ meet within F(Tm); it "can be verified in polynomial time by checking a polynomial number of convex feasibility problems" (p. 6). Assumption 3 relaxes this so that cuts may meet on the sphere (p. 10). Case 1 (a redundant cut) and Case 2 (no redundant cut) split the proof (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Lemma 2: from (x, X) ∈ F(Rm) with aᵢᵀx < bᵢ, the point zᵢ = (bᵢ − aᵢᵀx)⁻¹(bᵢx − Xaᵢ) is feasible for (Tm), using the SOC-RLT and RLT rows (p. 6). (ii) Lemma 3: each extreme point of (compact convex set ∩ half-space) is a convex combination of at most two extreme points of the set, by a basis-size-2 polytope argument (pp. 6–7). (iii) Induction on m with base case Lemma 1, stated in the paper as m = 0 (p. 7). S024 p. 21 later says the correct base case is m = 1. (iv) Proposition 1: an active SOC-RLT row forces each summand of a rank-1 decomposition onto a boundary ray of the SOC (p. 8). (v) Proposition 2: a tight RLT row gives sᵀYt = 0, rank(Y) ≤ 2 via Lemma 3, and a Schur complement on W = [sᵀ; tᵀ; I] Y [s t I] gives Y = sum of two rank-1 terms, which contradicts extremality (pp. 9–10). (vi) Proposition 3: shrink the ball to 1 − ε, apply Corollary 1, and pass to the limit by compactness (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: to prove "relaxation exact", prove "every extreme point is rank-1" by induction on the number of added constraints. Lemma 3 (at most two extreme points after one cut) caps the rank at 2, and a Schur complement argument then kills rank 2 (pp. 6–10).
  - Transferable technique: when a hypothesis fails only on the boundary, tighten the set by ε, apply the strict result, and take the limit using compactness (p. 11).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none as a study. Two numerical illustrations: the Burer–Anstreicher counterexample (v(T2) ≈ −12.9419 vs v(R2) ≈ −13.8410, rank of X̄ = 3) examined "from the viewpoint of our proof" (p. 10), and a pentagon inscribed in the disk where the RLT-only relaxation gives −17.7984 at rank 2 and (Rm) gives −17.4873 at numerical rank 1 (pp. 11–12).
  - Transferable technique: rerun the known counterexample and say which step of your proof it violates (here Proposition 1 via Lemma 4) (p. 10).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper builds directly on Sturm–Zhang [12] and Burer–Anstreicher [2] for m = 1, and on Ye–Zhang [14] and [2] for m = 2 (pp. 2–3). It treats Ye–Zhang generously: it extends their analysis to the non-intersecting case and concedes "at least for m = 2, our result may not be surprising" (p. 4). It cites trust-region classics (Moré–Sorensen, Conn–Gould–Toint) for motivation only (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A 12-page paper. The main goal is stated at the end of §1 ("This is our main goal", p. 3). The order is motivation by revisiting a rival analysis (§2), then three lemmas, then the result as Theorem 1 + Theorem 2 → Corollary 1 (pp. 7–10). The counterexample is re-examined after the proof, followed by a short extension section with one worked application (pp. 10–12). Hedging is light. The acknowledgments credit Anstreicher for "suggesting first that the case m = 2 might be extensible to arbitrary m" (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theoretical. It gives exactness and polynomial-time solvability for any m under Assumption 1, and with [2] a "very clear demarcation" of when (Rm) is exact (p. 3). The limits are specific: the proof leans heavily on Assumption 1 through Lemma 4 (p. 10), and the extension covers only intersections on the boundary (p. 10). No future directions are listed. The base-case misstatement was corrected by Burer in S024 (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: a small substructure (TRS + cuts), the full SOC-RLT + RLT intersection as the relaxation, and a result reported with its exact boundary (non-intersecting vs the [2] counterexample) (pp. 3, 10).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the 2013 conjecture is proved with a student (pp. 3, 12). The counterexample is re-read through the proof (p. 10). The induction base case was later publicly corrected (S024 p. 21).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: exactness via "every extreme point is rank-1" of one lifted convex set (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: perturb or tighten, prove, pass to the limit (p. 11; also in S019 and S023).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Extreme-point rank-1 induction on the number of constraints, using Lemma 3 (convex set ∩ half-space → at most two extreme points) plus a Schur-complement rank-2 contradiction (pp. 6–10).
- Map any relaxation point with slack in cut i to a feasible original point zᵢ, read off from the SOC-RLT row (Lemma 2, p. 6).
- Close the paper by re-examining the known counterexample against the step of the proof it breaks (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "This paper shows that the convex relaxation has no gap for arbitrary m as long as the linear constraints are non-intersecting." (p. 1)
- "On the other hand, we could not see how to extend the approach of Ye and Zhang to general m, and so to the best of our knowledge, our general approach in this paper contributes something new." (p. 4)

### S024 · A gentle, geometric introduction to copositive optimization (Mathematical Programming 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10107-015-0888-z)
Read level: full (author preprint revised 17 Jan 2015, pp. 1–34, including the references).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the tutorial "entry point" written after the 2009 CP representation and the 2010–2014 low-dimensional hull papers. It recapitulates [9] (Burer 2009) in §10 (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is by the geometry of the feasible set, with few variables or few constraints. For each shape the paper asks for an explicit convex description of G = conv{(x, xxᵀ) : x ∈ F} (pp. 2, 5). The motivation is cut characterization: "much can be learned by trying to characterize all the cuts" (p. 2). Copositive cuts depend only on x ≥ 0, "not … on the objective or the data" (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: introduce a hard cone through a sequence of shapes that grow in difficulty (interval → triangle → quadrilateral → ball → ball minus caps → inscribed polygon → two ellipsoids → general polygon → polytope), each with the same G and K roles (pp. 4–31).
- **D2 Core idea**: The same three steps in every section. Homogenize to a cone and characterize K = conv{yyᵀ : y in cone} by PSD plus linear/SOC constraints. Then dehomogenize, using that every νₖ > 0, to obtain G (Theorem 1, p. 8; Theorem 3, p. 14; Theorem 8, p. 31). The constraints have a geometric reading: the RLT rows are relaxed products (bᵢ − aᵢᵀx)(bⱼ − aⱼᵀx) ≥ 0, one per pair of facets (p. 9), and SOC-RLT is ‖x‖ ≤ 1 multiplied by bᵢ − aᵢᵀx ≥ 0 (p. 17). The one new result is §9: for general polygons, quadratics qλ that "partially inscribe" F, with λmax computed from the remaining vertices (pp. 27–28).
  - Transferable technique: generate valid quadratic cuts by taking the product of two supporting inequalities minus λ(line through the two support vertices)², then raise λ to the first vertex contact (λmax formula, p. 27).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: proper triangle or quadrilateral, i.e. bounded with interior (pp. 5, 10). For tetrahedra the result relies on Lemma 2 (DNN = CP for d ≤ 4) and "cannot be extended verbatim to simplices in R⁴ or higher" (p. 8). Non-intersecting, non-redundant caps (Condition 1, p. 18). Nonempty bounded polytope in §10 (p. 30). §8 is summarized without proof from [24] (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Maxfield–Minc (DNN = CP for d ≤ 4) combined with an invertible C = (b, −A): factor CYCᵀ, then map back with C⁻¹ (Proposition 1, p. 7). (ii) For the quadrilateral, augment A by a column α so that Ĉ is invertible, impose Ŷ₄₄ = 0, and project (p. 11). (iii) Extreme-ray rank-1 by subtracting εzzᵀ with z a boundary combination of two spectral factors (Proposition 3, pp. 13–14). (iv) A three-case extreme-ray argument for one cap (Proposition 4, p. 16). (v) Lemma 5 (an extreme ray of P ∩ half-space is a sum of at most two extreme rays of P) plus the Schur complement on W, giving induction over caps (pp. 19–21). This is the S021 machinery in cone form. (vi) Every proof dehomogenizes by showing νₖ > 0 from boundedness (pp. 8, 14, 31).
  - Transferable technique: prove hull results as "K ⊆ R is easy; show every extreme ray of R is rank-1", and then reuse a single dehomogenization proof across all shapes ("The proof matches exactly the proof of Theorem 3", p. 17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: §9 only. 6,000 random polygon instances, m ∈ {5,…,10} with 1,000 each and nothing repeated. Instances are solved with the PSD + CYCᵀ ≥ 0 relaxation; "globally solved" means a numerically rank-1 optimal Y; 4,835 remained unsolved. Those were re-solved while separating the §9 quadratics, and all 4,835 were solved, 85% with 1–2 cuts and one with 10 cuts (pp. 28–29). Solver, tolerances and code are not reported in the text read. Figure 8 shows one m = 5 instance (p. 29).
  - Transferable technique: screen random instances with the baseline relaxation, keep only the unsolved ones, and report how many cuts each needed (pp. 28–29).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper presents itself as "a unified presentation" of results already in the literature (Anstreicher–Burer [1], Sturm–Zhang [23], Burer–Anstreicher [11], Burer–Yang [12], Yang–Burer [24]), with simplified proofs (p. 3). It points to nine surveys (p. 3) and credits Bienstock and Bienstock–Michalka for polynomial solvability without convex relaxation (pp. 15, 23). It explicitly does not compare with branch-and-bound, sparsity or higher-order lifting (p. 3). It corrects his own [12]: the base case should be m = 1, not m = 0 (p. 21).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Scope disclaimers come up front ("we do not claim to have identified the best or only way…", p. 3). The order runs from "easiest to hardest" (p. 3). A §1.1 "Reuse of notation" note warns that G has a different definition in each section but the same role (p. 3). Every section has a figure (Figs. 1–8). The conclusion is short and points to practice: apply the cut classes when the geometry appears as a substructure, e.g. projections onto pairs (xᵢ, xⱼ) (pp. 31–32).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Expository, with one new conjecture and supporting computation (§9). Limits are stated plainly: no proof that the §9 quadratics suffice ("we conjecture that they are", p. 28), and the separation is not claimed efficient in theory (pp. 28–29). The two-ellipsoid hull needs a semi-infinite family (p. 26). Future work is to test the cut classes in practice (pp. 31–32).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: each geometry is packaged as one convex set G = {Y(x, X) ∈ K}. "This is the promise and the goal of copositive optimization." (p. 2). §10 restates the CP representation (pp. 29–31).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: small substructures (dimension 2–3, few constraints), explicit non-disjunctive descriptions, and the boundary stated by dimension (d ≥ 5 breaks Lemma 2, p. 8). Cuts are meant for "anytime the corresponding geometry appears as a substructure" (p. 32).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: §9 regularity printed as a conjecture backed by 6,000 instances (p. 28). The published base-case error in [12] is narrowed and fixed in print (p. 21). ⚠ variant: "solved" rests on a single signal, numerical rank 1 (p. 28), not the later two-signal rule.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the hard pool is only the instances the baseline relaxation fails on (4,835 of 6,000), and the filter is disclosed (p. 28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Homogenize → characterize conv{yyᵀ} over the cone → dehomogenize via νₖ > 0; one template proof reused across shapes (pp. 7–8, 14, 31).
- Partially-inscribed quadratic cuts with the closed-form λmax over the remaining vertices (p. 27).
- A notation-reuse warning paragraph that turns repeated symbols into a teaching device (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "Hence, our main contribution is a unified presentation of the various results, and we do so from “easiest to hardest” in hopes of gently introducing the reader to the insights provided by copositive optimization." (p. 3)
- "At this time, we do not have a proof that all valid quadratics of the type described are enough to capture G, but we conjecture that they are." (p. 28)

### S023 · A copositive approach for two-stage adjustable robust optimization with uncertain right-hand sides (Computational Optimization and Applications 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10589-017-9974-x, arXiv 1609.07402)
Read level: full (arXiv v2, 20 May 2017, pp. 1–30, including Appendix §7 proofs, pp. 25–30).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Guanglin Xu (Univ. of Iowa, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: carries the copositive representation of nonconvex QP into robust optimization. It applies Burer 2009 and the 2012 generalized CP representation [17, 18, 20] to the bilinear inner max of ARO (p. 6).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two-stage adjustable robust LP with uncertain right-hand sides is NP-hard. The affine policy is the tractable incumbent but generally suboptimal (pp. 1–2). The entry is that after LP duality, the worst-case recourse π(x) is a bilinear program over U × W, which already has a copositive representation (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: when a new problem's inner subproblem becomes a bilinear or nonconvex QP after duality, plug in an existing exact conic representation of that QP rather than designing a policy (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Copositive representation of π(x). Add a redundant ball I•Z ≤ r so that Slater holds and the dual is affine in x, then collapse the problem to a single copositive minimization, (RLP) exact (Theorem 1, p. 8). The affine policy is re-derived in the same language: (Aff) is equivalent to a tractable inner approximation IA of COP (Theorem 2, p. 11), and IA is a restriction of (RLP) with Λ₂ = 0, ρ = 0 (Proposition 3, p. 12). Any cone IB "in between" gives v*RLP ≤ v*IB ≤ v*Aff (Theorem 3, p. 12). The concrete IB adds a PSD term and an inner approximation of COP(Û): exact for the SOC, RLT-type for polyhedral Û (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: express the incumbent tractable method as one specific inner approximation of your exact conic reformulation; any richer approximation is then guaranteed at least as good (Theorems 2–3, pp. 11–12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1: X and the cone Û are tractable and Û is full-dimensional. Assumption 2: feasible. Assumption 3: bounded. Assumption 4: relatively complete recourse (p. 3). Assumption 4 gives strong duality (Proposition 1, p. 8); without it the copositive program "still yields a valid upper bound" (pp. 4, 8). A "small but critical detail" in the derivation keeps y(u) rather than substituting Yu (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) LP duality turns the max–min into a bilinear max over U × W (p. 6). (ii) Homogenize via u₁ = 1, then use the copositive representation of the bilinear program with ZEᵀ = 0 in place of diag(EZEᵀ) = 0 (pp. 6–7). (iii) A redundant norm bound from polynomially bounded vertices of W ([35] cor. 3.2d) makes ρI strictly copositive, so Slater holds (pp. 7–8). (iv) Lemma 1: IA ⊆ COP, shown by a term-by-term sign check (pp. 10–11). (v) Lemma 2: an analytic robust value ½(√s + s) for the 2-norm temporal network (pp. 14, 25–26). (vi) Proposition 6: an explicit feasible dual certificate; Lemma 3 (PSD on Null(E) ⇒ ρI + V + μEᵀE ≻ 0) and a sum-of-squares completion show V is PSD on Null(E) (pp. 26–29). (vii) Theorem 4: take ρ → 0 (p. 16). (viii) Proposition 7: an explicit optimal primal Z (pp. 17, 29). (ix) Proposition 8: monotonicity in the uncertainty set plus feasibility of the same Z (pp. 29–30).
  - Transferable technique: to prove a relaxation is exact on a family of size s, write down explicit primal and dual certificates in closed form as functions of s (Propositions 6–7, pp. 26–29).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Mosek 8.0.0.28 beta via YALMIP in MATLAB R2014a on an Intel Core i3 machine with 4 GB RAM (p. 13). Results:
  - Temporal network: treated analytically. For the 2-norm set the method is exact, while the affine policy has a gap that grows with s (pp. 14–17). For the 1-norm set it improves on affine without closing the gap (p. 17).
  - Newsvendor instance from [4]: v*RLP = −825.83, v*Aff = −41.83, v*IB = −411.08, the same bound as [4] (p. 18).
  - Lot-sizing, N = 8 with a ball uncertainty set: LB 1573.8 by Monte Carlo sampling, Aff 1950.8, IB 1794.0. With the budget uncertainty set the method "did not perform better than the affine policy" (pp. 19–20).
  - 1,000 random instances with (k, m, n₁, n₂) = (17, 16, 3, 5) from the generator of [30]: in 971 the bound equals the affine value, in 29 it closes 20.2% of the gap on average (pp. 20–21).
  - Table 2 lists problem sizes and times, from 0.23 s to 35 s (p. 21).
  - Seeds are not reported. The realized lot-sizing costs are printed in Table 1 (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: publish the realized random data (here the cost table) when an instance comes from a random draw (p. 19). Report a gap-closed ratio against a sampling lower bound when the true optimum is unknown (p. 21).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Covers the affine policy (Ben-Tal et al.), extended affine (Chen–Zhang), polynomial policies (Bertsimas–Iancu–Parrilo), MILP/SDP approximations (Ardestani-Jaafari–Delage), and exact methods (column-and-constraint generation, Benders) (p. 2). The closest work is Chang et al. (LP duality + RLT) and Ardestani-Jaafari–Delage [4]. Burer and Xu prefer their own approach "because it clearly separates the use of conic duality from the choice of approximation" (p. 4). They disclose that Hanasusanto–Kuhn's Corollary 1, posted the same day, is equivalent to their Theorem 1, and list the differences (pp. 4–5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: §1 gives a roadmap with the two "extremes", the copositive program vs the affine policy, and the approximations interpolating between them (p. 4). Theorems are short and mostly proved by citing structure. Long certificates go to the Appendix (pp. 25–30). Claims are hedged precisely: "it appears that the specification of our cone … corresponds directly to … their approach [6], but we have not proved this formally" (p. 18). Losses are printed (pp. 20–21). One textual inconsistency: v*RLP,1 is ½(s + 1) on p. 14 and ½(√s + 1) on p. 17.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory (an exact reformulation and an ordering of bounds) plus an analytic exactness class and modest numerics. Limits are specific: no improvement on the budget set or on 971/1,000 random instances (pp. 20–21), and Assumption 4 is unverified for the random generator (p. 20). Open questions: tighter IB, other tractable classes, and above all what policy class the copositive approach enforces and how to extract y(·) (p. 21). Acknowledgments credit Ardestani-Jaafari and Delage for "pointing out an error in one of our codes" (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the whole adjustable problem is packaged as one linear program over COP(Û × R₊ᵐ), then tractable inner approximations are built (pp. 8, 12–13).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (partial): starts with a literature family where the incumbent (affine policy) has an arbitrarily large gap (p. 14). ⚠ variant: the random test set is not filtered; the unconditional rate (29/1,000 improved) is reported instead (p. 21).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the claim level is labelled ("we have not proved this formally", p. 18), and a code error found by others is credited (p. 22).
- New-pattern candidate: incumbent-as-restriction sandwich (pp. 11–12).
- New-pattern candidate: perturb, prove, pass to the limit (ρ → 0, p. 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Add a provably redundant norm ball to get Slater and strong conic duality, then dualize the inner problem so the outer problem collapses to one minimization (pp. 7–8).
- Show the incumbent policy equals a named restriction of your conic program, so every "in-between" cone gives a certified ordering of bounds (pp. 11–12).
- Disclose concurrent equivalent results by date and list how the papers still differ (pp. 4–5).

**Quotes**:
- "Although all three approaches are closely related, we prefer our approach because it clearly separates the use of conic duality from the choice of approximation." (p. 4)
- "We found that, in this case, our method did not perform better than the affine policy." (p. 20)

### S019 · Exact semidefinite formulations for a class of (random and non-random) nonconvex quadratic programs (Mathematical Programming 2020, DOI 10.1007/s10107-019-01367-2, arXiv 1802.02688)
Read level: full (arXiv v2, 7 Nov 2018, pp. 1–20, including the references). The published version and the 2021 correction (DOI 10.1007/s10107-021-01684-5) were not read in this batch.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yinyu Ye (Stanford, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the "diagonal QCQP" exactness paper. SKILL.md (Method 3) records that its Theorem 3 was corrected in 2021 and that the arXiv version still carries the original statement. The text read here is that arXiv v2.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper asks for a priori bounds on the minimum rank r* of Shor SDP optima. The known bounds are the data-free Pataki/Barvinok bound and Laurent–Varvitsiotis's tree-width bound, which gives r* ≤ 2 for diagonal QCQPs (pp. 2–3). The entry is data-dependent: a "feasibility number" f computed from n LPs (p. 4). A second motivation is average-case analysis of random data, following the LP simplex/IPM literature and SDP recovery problems (pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable technique: when a structural bound (graph or tree-width) stalls at 2, look for a data-dependent certificate that can reach 1 on specific classes (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: By complementarity, rank(Y*) + rank(Z*) ≤ n + 1 (p. 6). For diagonal data the dual condition S(y)jj = s(y)j = 0 is polyhedral, so Farkas turns it into n LP feasibility systems (5). Each feasible system forces one positive diagonal entry of S, so rank(Z) ≥ f and rank(Y*) ≤ n − f + 1 (Lemma 1, Theorem 1, p. 7). General QCQPs are lifted to diagonal form through yᵢ = Qᵢᵀx plus a redundant ball (p. 13), giving eigenvalue-only conditions (Theorem 2, p. 13). The random case: if C ⪰ 0 and the eigenvalues of Aᵢ are Gaussian with m ≤ n^q, then Prob(r* = 1) → 1 (Theorem 3, p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: turn a rank bound on the primal into a count of dual diagonal entries that must be positive, each checked by an LP via Farkas (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1: feasible. Assumption 2: some ȳ ≤ 0 with Σ ȳᵢAᵢ ≺ 0, which bounds the feasible set. Assumption 3: an interior point (p. 6). The diagonal case is equivalent to commuting data matrices with a shared eigenbasis, and is NP-hard (it contains 0-1 IPs) (p. 3). f does not depend on b (pp. 4, 7). Theorem 3 needs C ⪰ 0, independent Gaussian eigenvalues, and an added ball xᵀx ≤ ρ² (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) A Schur complement argument: Sjj = 0 forces sj = 0 (Lemma 1, p. 7). (ii) Complementary rank bound (p. 6). (iii) Perturb and pass to the limit, used repeatedly: C + D → C (Proposition 1, p. 9), c + εd (Proposition 2, p. 10), objective perturbation (Proposition 3, p. 12), the ε Σ yᵢᵀyᵢ term (pp. 14), and random Bᵢ with ε → 0 (pp. 15, 17). (iv) The diagonalizing lift with a "technically redundant" ball kept so the lifted problem satisfies the assumptions (p. 13). (v) Probabilistic counting: the equality system (13) has C(n−1, 2) bases with feasibility probability α > 0, exhibited by an explicit open set of realizations, giving θ = 1 − (1 − α)^C(n−1,2) and a product θ^{nm} "exploiting independence" (pp. 16–17). Lemma 3 is a l'Hôpital limit (p. 15). The proof of Theorem 3 is the text that SKILL.md reports was later corrected; this batch did not check which step fails.
  - Transferable technique: show a probability is positive by exhibiting one explicit realization in the interior of the good set, then cross-check its size by Monte Carlo (α ≈ 1/6, pp. 16–17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none, apart from one Monte Carlo estimate α ≈ 1/6 (p. 17). Worked analytic examples: the binary knapsack, where the bound is worse (f = 0, p. 8); the intersection of 2-norm and ∞-norm balls (p. 10); and C arbitrary with Aᵢ ∈ {±I, 0} (pp. 11–12).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper complements and refines Sojoudi–Lavaei [27]. It reproves their sign-definite result and gives a case their theory does not cover, because f uses the data Cjj, [Aᵢ]jj (pp. 4, 11). It reports that the lift gives a "simplified" derivation of Madani et al.'s r* ≤ 2 for polynomial optimization (pp. 4, 13). It separates its random-data model from smoothed analysis (p. 5), and uses Burer–Anstreicher's counterexample to show the diagonal assumption is needed (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract claims priority ("To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result…", p. 1). The paper compares honestly against the incumbent bound, with "an example for which our bound is worse" (p. 8). There is a framing sentence about where "easy" SDP relaxations end (p. 13). It closes with an explicit conjecture and a suggested work-around (p. 17). Acknowledgments credit Gang Luo for "an error in the knapsack example" (pp. 17–18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: a polynomially checkable sufficient condition, an eigenvalue-only condition for general QCQPs, and a high-probability exactness theorem. Limits as written: the bound can be weaker than Laurent–Varvitsiotis (p. 8), Theorem 2 ignores linear terms (p. 14), and Theorem 3 needs C ⪰ 0. The conjecture is that it holds for random C, with the proof breaking down "for analyzing the relevant feasibility system for Xjj" (p. 17). Per SKILL.md, Theorem 3 itself was later corrected (correction not read here).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the hypothesis that makes the convex object exact is written down and made checkable in polynomial time (f = n), and the general problem is lifted to the structured class (diagonal) where the hypothesis applies (pp. 7, 13).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: a high-probability result printed as a theorem rests on a probabilistic counting step (pp. 16–17), backed by a Monte Carlo check of α only (p. 17). SKILL.md records the 2021 correction of this theorem. On the other side, this version credits the finder of the knapsack error (pp. 17–18) and labels the random-C extension as a conjecture (p. 17), which is ✅ evidence.
