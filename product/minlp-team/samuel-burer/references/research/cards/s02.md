### S035 · On handling free variables in interior-point methods for conic linear optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2008, DOI 10.1137/06066847X)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. F. Anjos · **Position in trajectory**: algorithmic SDP numerics from his interior-point/SDPLR period, before the QP-relaxation focus. The issue is vol. 18 no. 4, dated 2007 on p. 1. **Read**: full text (pp. 1–16, including references).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Free variables make the reduced Newton system (7) indefinite, so the Cholesky factorization that IPM codes rely on cannot be used. Splitting z = z⁺ − z⁻ gives an unbounded primal optimal set and a dual feasible set without interior. The authors stress that the issue is computational, not theoretical (p. 2). No method is a consensus choice (p. 3). The entry point is Mészáros's LP regularization, whose δ update is "somewhat ad hoc" and comes without a global convergence argument (pp. 3, 9).
- **D2 Core idea**: Treat the regularized direction (15) as an inexact Newton direction. Accept it only if δₖ‖Δzₖ‖ ≤ βσμₖ/2 (18). Otherwise halve δ and re-solve (Algorithm 2, p. 10). The Schur complement AXS⁻¹Aᵀ + δ⁻¹EEᵀ (17) is positive definite (p. 9). Unchanged: the Kojima–Megiddo–Mizuno infeasible path-following framework (Algorithm 1, p. 6). The deliberate choice: global convergence without a polynomial bound, because the inexact-IPM alternatives were "significantly more difficult to implement" and the Freund–Jarre–Mizuno variant they implemented "did not work as well" (p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: The analysis is stated for LP only; extension to SOCP/SDP is asserted as "well known" (p. 4). A has full row rank, E has full column rank, both problems are interior feasible, and the initial point satisfies r_p = r_d1 = 0 "for the sake of brevity" (pp. 4, 6). The experiments deliberately include rank-deficient E, against the full-column-rank assumption (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The proof repairs one proposition. Inexactness breaks r_α = (1 − α)r (21), so Proposition 3.4 fails. Bounding the inexactness by half the centring term restores it (Proposition 5.2, p. 11), with α* changed from σε/η to σε/2η. Theorem 5.1 (p. 10) then follows the steps of the proof of Theorem 3.1 (p. 7).
- **D5 Experiments**: SDPT3 3.02 with four free-variable treatments (regularize, explicit indefinite, split, quadratic cone) on 135 instances in 5 sets: 9 DIMACS, 2×27 modified SDPLIB, 12 quantum chemistry, 60 moment relaxations (Table 1, p. 13). The metric is log₁₀ of the DIMACS error, best value over the run. Regularization matches the best on DIMACS/SDPLIB and wins clearly on the other three sets. It needs more iterations (Table 4) but less time per iteration (Table 5, pp. 13–14).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and numerical. Limits stated: not claimed best "in all situations" (p. 3); SDPT3 only, and in SeDuMi split variables already work well (pp. 3–4); no polynomial bound; extending to SeDuMi's self-dual embedding is left open (p. 14). A failed first design is reported: nonincreasing δ was "(ultimately flawed)" because δ went to 0 too fast (p. 11).

**Method links**: Method 5 ✅ evidence: the simple variant is chosen because it worked in code and the tried alternative is reported as worse (p. 9); the failed nonincreasing-δ variant is recorded with its reason (p. 11). New-pattern candidate: *inexactness budget tied to the duality gap*. Make a cheap approximate step admissible by bounding its error against the centring term σμ, then reuse a classical convergence proof with one proposition repaired (pp. 10–11).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To make a cheap approximate step provably safe, find the single inequality in a classical proof that the approximation breaks, and add an acceptance test that restores it, with a while-loop that shrinks the parameter (p. 10).
- Let a step-control parameter increase again ("our best guess given current information") instead of forcing monotonicity, and report the monotone design that failed (p. 11).
- Report accuracy as log₁₀ of the worst DIMACS error over the run so that methods with different stopping behaviour compare fairly (p. 13).
**Quotes**: "We emphasize that free variables are a computational issue and not a theoretical one." (p. 2) · "Ultimately, we believe that our analysis allows us to identify the essence of a good regularization strategy. It should (i) be convergent, (ii) be easy to implement, and (iii) work well in practice." (p. 9)

### H002 · SDPLR 1.03-beta User's Guide (short version) (author-hosted software documentation 2009, no DOI)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: R. D. C. Monteiro, C. Choi (listed as developers, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: maintenance release of the Burer–Monteiro low-rank SDP code six years after the 2003 paper. Choi, his former student, joins as developer. **Read**: full text (pp. 1–5).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The guide documents a C package "for solving large-scale semidefinite programming problems", with source, binaries and a Matlab interface (p. 1). This is software documentation, not a research question.
- **D2 Core idea**: The input format extends sparse SDPA so that low-rank data blocks can be given as a factorization BDBᵀ, with B dense nⱼ×r and D diagonal (p. 4). A reserved line will hold the trace of the primal matrix for ±1 combinatorial SDPs, "which can be used to provide dual bounds during the execution of SDPLR", a feature in development (p. 4). The worked example is the Lovász theta SDP of the 5-cycle (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: n/a (software). Building it needs BLAS/LAPACK and the bundled GSL (pp. 1–2).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The install test is `sdplr vibra1.dat-s`, compared with the supplied output file (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is software. Dual bounds for ±1 SDPs are announced as future work (p. 4). Parameters are explained interactively through `sdplr gen params` (p. 3).

**Method links**: Method 5 ✅ evidence: the 2000 prototype became maintained public software, with Matlab and command-line entry points (pp. 1–3). Method 1 ✅ weak evidence: the input format exposes low-rank data structure, the same structure the method exploits (p. 4). Also bears on H10 (release code).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Ship a solver with a structure-aware input format (here low-rank BDBᵀ blocks) as a small extension of the community standard format, and include a classic toy instance (θ of C₅) as the format example (pp. 3–5).
- Provide a parameter-file generator with inline explanations instead of documenting every parameter in prose (p. 3).
**Quotes**: "SDPLR is a C software package for solving large-scale semidefinite programming problems." (p. 1) · "The SDPLR format, which SDPLR is also capable of handling, is more or less an extension of sparse SDPA format which also allows low-rank data matrices to be specified easily." (p. 3)

### S028 · The difference between 5×5 doubly nonnegative and completely positive matrices (Linear Algebra and its Applications 2009, DOI 10.1016/j.laa.2009.05.021)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: K. M. Anstreicher (corresponding author), M. Dür · **Position in trajectory**: follow-up to his 2009 copositive representation (cited as [5], p. 2). It studies the gap between the tractable DNN cone and the CP cone, in the first dimension (n = 5) where the gap exists. The work began during Dür's NSF-funded visit to Iowa (p. 15). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–15).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Dₙ = Cₙ only for n ≤ 4, so n = 5 is the first open case (p. 3). From the optimization side, Cₙ is "the true domain of interest" and Dₙ only a tractable approximation (p. 3). The goal is to understand the "bad" set D₅ \ C₅ (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Parameterization: the extremely bad matrices (rank-3, cyclic extreme rays of D₅) are written as ΛRRᵀΛ with a 2-parameter R, using 12 representative permutations instead of 120 (Theorem 3, pp. 4–6).
  - CP-reduction: a finite procedure peels rank-one CP pieces εffᵀ off X via LP (6) over index partitions (Algorithm 1, Theorem 5, pp. 7–9).
  - Main result: for n = 5, CP-irreducible ⇔ extremely bad, so every bad matrix is a CP matrix plus one extremely bad matrix (Corollary 2, p. 12).
  - Separation: a Horn-matrix-based copositive cut separates any extremely bad matrix, and the cut is sharp (Theorem 8, p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: n ≥ 5 in general and n = 5 for the main results (p. 3). Extreme rays of D₅ are taken from Hamilton-Jester & Li (Theorems 1–2, p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - A checkable characterization of CP-reducibility via a range condition (Theorem 4, p. 7).
  - Termination by a potential: rank + number of nonzeros strictly decreases (Theorem 5, pp. 8–9).
  - Case analysis over permutation classes, with Lemma 3 scaling invariance (Theorems 6–7, pp. 9–12).
  - Farkas alternatives reduced to sign contradictions between two index sets, I = {1,2} and I = {1,5} (pp. 11–12).
  - The authors note that Algorithm 1 is finite but not polynomial, since the number of partitions can be exponential (p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. There are worked examples: one iteration of Algorithm 1 on a CP matrix fails to recover a CP factorization (p. 8), and the single cut closes the gap between the ϑ′ (DNN) bound and α for the 5-cycle stable-set problem (pp. 13–14).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory (matrix geometry) with one optimization application. Limits: no separation for an arbitrary bad matrix, which is stated as open and tied to a full outer description of C₅ (pp. 12–13). Algorithm 1 need not return a CP factorization even for CP input (p. 8).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence: once the difficulty sits in one cone (the CP representation [5]), the next paper attacks that cone, here its gap to the DNN approximation and a separating cut (pp. 2–3, 12–13). Method 2 ✅ evidence: smallest dimension where the question is nontrivial (n = 5), an explicit description of what goes wrong, and publication of the boundary (no general separation) as an open problem (pp. 3, 12–13). New-pattern candidate: *decompose the relaxation's excess into "good + one irreducible bad piece"*. The DNN relaxation's failure is characterized by a canonical residual and then cut off (pp. 9, 12–13).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To understand where a tractable cone K̃ ⊃ K fails, study the extreme rays of K̃ that are not in K. Parameterize them explicitly, then show every point of K̃ \ K is in K plus one such ray (pp. 4–6, 12).
- Prove finiteness of a peeling algorithm by a potential such as rank + nonzero count that must strictly drop at each line-search stop (pp. 8–9).
- Check a new cut on the canonical gap instance (stable set on C₅, where ϑ′ = √5 > α = 2), and relate it to cuts known from other derivations (p. 14).
**Quotes**: "This terminology reinforces the perspective from optimization applications involving CP matrices [5], where Cn is the true domain of interest and Dn serves only as a (tractable) approximation of Cn." (p. 3) · "We have not been able to derive a separating inequality for an arbitrary bad matrix, but we show below how to separate an extremely bad matrix" (p. 12)

### S041 · A p-cone sequential relaxation procedure for 0-1 integer programs (Optimization Methods & Software 2009, DOI 10.1080/10556780903057341)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Chen (his PhD student) · **Position in trajectory**: the last of his 0-1 IP lift-and-project work, before the "shift … from IPs to continuous problems" (H1). Received February 2008 (p. 2). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–26 up to the acknowledgements; reference list not read).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: All earlier sequential lift-and-project procedures (Lovász–Schrijver, Balas–Ceria–Cornuéjols, Kojima–Tunçel, Sherali–Adams, Lasserre) use LP or SDP in the lifted space. The question is whether p-order-cone programming, now well understood and SOCP-representable, gives new procedures with "their own theoretical and computational advantages" (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: The 0-1 points lie in the p-ball ‖x_J − e/2‖_p ≤ |J|^{1/p}/2 (Proposition 2.1, p. 5). Multiply each linear constraint bᵢ − aᵢᵀx ≥ 0 by this ball constraint, linearize X = xxᵀ, keep only diag(X) = x, and project (pp. 5–6). This yields a family N_(p,J). It reduces to LP-based Lovász–Schrijver when p = ∞ and J = [n] (Theorem 2.5), to the SDP-based version when PSD is added (Theorem 2.6), and to Balas–Ceria–Cornuéjols when |J| = 1 (Theorem 2.8) (pp. 9–11). The family is a product of constraints, "not derived from semi-definiteness" (p. 26).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: P ⊆ [0,1]ⁿ compact convex, possibly given by a semi-infinite description (p. 5). Symmetry and PSD of X are deliberately not imposed "because they are not necessary for the theoretical convergence" (p. 8). The complexity result assumes P is a polytope (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Two monotonicity properties: in the set P (Proposition 3.7) and in p (Proposition 3.9, via the convexity Lemma 3.8) (pp. 15–16).
  - The iteration bound O(√|I|) for p = 2 versus O(√(|J||I|)) otherwise comes from barrier parameters (Corollary 3.6, p. 14).
  - Main lemma (Theorem 3.15, p. 20), proved in three steps of increasing geometry: polytope (two proofs, pp. 16–17); ball, via n nearly-active perturbed constraints, Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury and a θ → 0 Taylor argument (pp. 17–19); general compact convex P, via a supporting ball containing P (Proposition 3.14, p. 20).
  - Asymptotic convergence, assuming every index recurs infinitely often (Theorem 4.1, pp. 21–22).
  - An explicit polytope family P(a) shows that convergence can need infinitely many rounds (Proposition 4.2, pp. 22–23).
  - A convergence rate is judged hard because the proof gives cuts "with no quantitative knowledge of their strength" (p. 21).
- **D5 Experiments**: p = 2 (MOSEK 5.0) against p = ∞ (CPLEX 9.0 dual simplex and MOSEK IPM). The instances are 8 DIMACS stable-set graphs (presolve off, single 2.8 GHz Opteron) and 7 small MIPLIB 2003 instances (presolve on to avoid running out of memory). The p = ∞ LP is faster and gives better bounds, the opposite of the stated hypothesis (pp. 23–25, Tables 2 and 4).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theoretical (a unifying family plus convergence). Limits stated: no finite convergence and no rate (pp. 21–23); the SOCP is not competitive computationally, with the hope that future SOCP software will change that (pp. 25–26).

**Method links**: Method 1 ⚠ variant: the lift-then-one-cone move here makes a parameterized family that contains the classical procedures as special cases (taste item 4, unification). It is not an exact reformulation (pp. 9–11). Method 5 ✗ contradiction: the paper reverses the M5 order. Theory carries it; the experiment is run after the theory and refutes the computational hypothesis, and the authors print that (pp. 23, 25). This is consistent with H8 (print losses with a hypothesis). New-pattern candidate: *escalating-geometry proof (polytope → ball → general convex set)*: prove a cut-off property for polytopes, extend it to balls by perturbed nearly-active constraints, then to any compact convex set through a supporting ball and monotonicity (pp. 16–20).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a relaxation family by multiplying each valid linear constraint by a norm-ball constraint that circumscribes {0,1}^J. Varying the norm p then gives a monotone strength/complexity dial (pp. 5, 14–16).
- To justify a new lift-and-project operator, show that the classical ones are parameter settings of it (p = ∞, J = [n]; |J| = 1) (pp. 9–11).
- Extend a polytope argument to general convex sets by enclosing P in a ball that supports P at the extreme point, then use monotonicity of the operator (pp. 18–20).
- State the computational hypothesis before the experiment ("We had hypothesized …") so that a negative result reads as a test, not a failure (p. 23).
**Quotes**: "Our hope is that, by introducing POCP relaxations, we might discover new lift-and-project procedures that have their own theoretical and computational advantages." (p. 3) · "These results are a testament to the high quality of current LP software." (p. 25)

### S047 · Relaxing the optimality conditions of box QP (Computational Optimization and Applications 2011, DOI 10.1007/s10589-009-9273-2)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Chen (his PhD student; corresponding author) · **Position in trajectory**: sequel to Burer & Vandenbussche's first-order KKT-based SDP branch-and-bound for box QP (cited [2], p. 2). It adds second-order conditions. Received October 2007, online 2009 (p. 1). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–20; reference list not read).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Global box QP (1) needs strong cheap convex relaxations (p. 2). First-order conditions had been put into LP/SDP relaxations before; the open step is the second-order condition Q_{I(x)I(x)} ⪰ 0, which depends on the unknown inactive set (pp. 2–4). Nesterov's p-norm-box analogue relies on smoothness, which fails for p = ∞ (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Remove the index set: with w = x ∘ (e − x), local convexity becomes Q ∘ wwᵀ ⪰ 0 (Proposition 2.1, pp. 4–5).
  - Relax this, together with complementarity, through one big lifted PSD matrix (SDP12, p. 6), or through a slimmer version that keeps only the second-order information (SDP2, pp. 6–7). Table 1 gives the size of each (p. 7).
  - Node version: the box-specialized w for [l, u] (Proposition 5.1, pp. 14–15).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Q is indefinite, not PSD (p. 1). The bounded Shor relaxation includes diag(X) ≤ x, because without it the SDP is unbounded when Q ⋡ 0 (Proposition 3.1, p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The main theorem is negative: SDP0 = SDP2 = SDP12 in optimal value (Section 4). Devices:
  - Lemma 4.1: extend an optimal solution of the weaker problem (p. 7).
  - Dual complementary slackness gives Q_JJ ⪰ 0 on J = {i: X_ii < x_i} (Lemma 4.2, p. 8).
  - Feasible paths with directional derivatives show SDP0 optima satisfy the first-order inequalities (10) (Propositions 4.4–4.6, Theorem 4.8, pp. 9–11).
  - An explicit lifted point with a large constant Λ, justified by a Schur complement and a null-space lemma (pp. 11–13).
- **D5 Experiments**: B&B in MATLAB/YALMIP/SeDuMi 1.1 on 100 random instances, n = 20, entries U[−1,1], tol 10⁻³ (pp. 15–17).
  - The new "advanced" branching (most fractional × longest edge) cuts nodes and time by more than 44% (p. 18).
  - SDP2 needs 21% fewer nodes but more total CPU, "So (SDP0) is the overall winner" (p. 18).
  - A switching scheme (SDP0 → SDP2 when a W-block test fails) helps but is "not fully successful as hoped" (p. 19).
  - SDP12 needs 65% fewer nodes than SDP0, at the longest times (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a theoretical device (index-free second-order condition) and a branching rule. Limits stated: root equivalence; the CPU-time loss; only n = 20 random instances. The B&B is not meant to beat other codes (p. 13). Future work: extend to QP over the simplex; faster SDP solvers (p. 20).

**Method links**: Method 2 ⚠ variant: the paper strengthens the incumbent Shor relaxation with more valid structure (optimality conditions) and checks whether it goes strictly beyond, but the answer at the root is "no" (equivalence theorem), and the gain appears only on subdivided boxes (pp. 2, 13–20). Method 4 ⚠ variant: the testbed is 100 uniform random instances with no filtering for hardness, before his later "hard pool" practice (p. 17). Consistent with H8: the CPU loss and the partly failed switching scheme are printed (pp. 18–19). New-pattern candidate: *relax the optimality conditions, not only the feasible set*. It continues the first-order version of Burer & Vandenbussche and is proposed for other QP classes (pp. 2, 20). Second candidate: *root-equivalent relaxations can differ inside B&B, so compare them at the nodes* (pp. 13, 18).
**Transferable techniques**:
- To put a condition that depends on an unknown active set into a relaxation, find a smooth weight vector (here w = x ∘ (e − x)) that vanishes exactly on the active indices. The condition then becomes a single matrix inequality, Q ∘ wwᵀ ⪰ 0 (pp. 4–5).
- Prove equivalence of a strengthened relaxation by building, from an optimum of the weak one, a feasible point of the strong one. Fill the extra blocks via a Schur complement with a large scalar (pp. 11–13).
- Branch where the relaxation is weakest: SDP enforces X = xxᵀ poorly in the interior, so split at x̄ᵢ, weighted with edge length to keep convergence (p. 16).
- Present B&B comparisons as log–log scatter plots of per-instance nodes and times against y = x (pp. 17–19).
**Quotes**: "In Sect. 4, we prove the surprising, somewhat negative result that all three achieve the same optimal value." (p. 2) · "Relaxing the standard second-order necessary optimality conditions is one of the main theoretical ideas of this paper. This task is non-trivial since it implicitly involves knowledge of the active/inactive constraint set at a general point." (p. 20)

### S063 · A semidefinite programming approach to the hypergraph minimum bisection problem (Optimization 2011, DOI 10.1080/02331930903233744)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: C. Choi (first and corresponding author; Burer's former student, then at UC Denver) · **Position in trajectory**: student-led application of his SDP/SDPLR toolkit to a combinatorial problem. Received October 2008, online April 2010 (pp. 1–2). **Read**: full text (pp. 1–15; reference list not read). Some mathematical symbols are lost in the extracted text; the prose is readable.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: HMB is NP-hard, served mostly by heuristics such as METIS, and "there has been no known mathematical program that is equivalent to HMB" (p. 2). The authors work on the complement problem CHMB because only the complement has approximation results in the graph case (Ye–Zhang 0.602) (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: An exact QCQP (Q-CHMB). Vertices are xⱼ ∈ {±1}, and each hyperedge gets yᵢ ∈ {−1,0,1} with yᵢ² − yᵢxⱼ = 0 for all j ∈ Eᵢ, so yᵢ ≠ 0 only if the edge is uncut (p. 4; equivalence in Proposition 2.1, p. 5). Lifting gives the SDP (S-CHMB) (p. 5). Rounding uses a balanced Goemans–Williamson variant (n + m random hyperplanes, sort and split), then FM local search (p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: n even, nonnegative weights (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Gram-vector geometry: all x̃ⱼ in a hyperedge make the same angle arccos‖ỹᵢ‖ with ỹᵢ, so they lie on a disc D(ỹᵢ) (Proposition 4.1, Corollary 4.2, pp. 10–11). A Lagrangian lemma decides when a hyperplane misses a disc (Lemma 4.3, pp. 11–12). Together these give the uncut-probability lower bound (Proposition 4.4, p. 13). An F-distribution and one-tailed Chebyshev argument then shows this bound tends to 0 as O((n+m)⁻²) (Proposition 4.6, pp. 14–15). So the approximation programme is reported as unfinished.
- **D5 Experiments**: 15 ISPD98 circuit benchmarks, solved with SDPLR 1.02 (infeasibility tolerance loosened to 10⁻³) and compared with the best of 100 METIS runs. The average SDP gap is 1.97% (worst 7.99% on t6) (Table 2, pp. 7–8). Rounded solutions are competitive with METIS at 45–55% balance (Table 3, p. 8). Caveats: METIS cannot give an exact 50/50 split; the older benchmarks are used because newer ones are too large for SDPLR; s15850 took about 12 h against under 6 s for METIS (pp. 7–9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is the first exact model plus computation. Limits stated: no approximation guarantee, and the theory is "somewhat disappointing" against the computation; SDPLR running time needs improvement (p. 15).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence: the first move is to write the problem exactly as one QCQP so that an SDP relaxation exists (pp. 3–5). Method 5 ✅ evidence: computation on real benchmarks carries the paper, and the missing theory is stated (pp. 13, 15). Method 3 ✅ evidence (writing side): the partial result is labelled "Towards an approximation guarantee", and its own limit (the bound → 0) is proved and set against the experiments (pp. 9, 15). Method 4 ⚠ variant: the benchmark filter is disclosed (older, SDPLR-sized instances) and the baseline's balance limitation is stated, but the instance pool is not engineered (pp. 7–8).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode "hyperedge uncut" with a ternary indicator yᵢ and the products yᵢ(yᵢ − xⱼ) = 0 for all j ∈ Eᵢ. Maximizing Σwᵢyᵢ² then counts uncut edges exactly (pp. 4–5).
- For an SDP relaxation, read off the geometry of the Gram vectors (equal angles to a hub vector, discs) before trying a rounding analysis (pp. 10–11).
- When an analysis falls short, prove where it falls short (the bound → 0) and say so beside the positive computational evidence (pp. 14–15).
**Quotes**: "We believe that having a mathematical model is a good starting point for studying the hypergraph minimum-bisection problem." (p. 15) · "Although Proposition 4.6 makes sense from a theoretical point of view, it is inconsistent with our computational results in which our method seems to work well on large-scale circuit partitioning problems." (p. 15)

### S042 · Robust rankings for college football (Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports 2012, DOI 10.1515/1559-0410.1405)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: an applied side project that uses robust optimization and an MINLP solved by constraint generation. **Read**: full text (pp. 1–20; reference list not read).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The trigger was a 2010 BCS incident. One FCS game missing from Colley's data swapped LSU and Boise State in the BCS ranking (pp. 4–5). This raises the question whether the Colley Matrix (CM) top 25 is sensitive to "inconsequential" games between teams with win rate below 30% (pp. 5, 8). The CM method is chosen because it is the only BCS computer ranking published in full (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: CM+ ratings solve min_r max_{Δ∈D(Γ)} ‖Cr − b′‖₂ (4). Γ switched inconsequential games change only b, linearly, while C is unchanged (pp. 12–13). The problem is reformulated as a convex QP with one linear cut per scenario (6) and solved by constraint generation: CPLEX solves the integer separation subproblem over Δ (p. 13). Γ = 0 gives back CM (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Only win/loss outcomes are used. Robustness is defined only against switches of games among bottom teams (t = 25, ω = 0.3), with no stochastic model (pp. 5–6, 8). Omitted games are excluded from scope (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Algebraic invariance C′ = C, so the uncertainty sits in the right-hand side (p. 12). For Γ ≥ N, the rankings provably cannot change under any switches (p. 16). The text calls problem (4) a mixed-integer nonlinear program that appears to be NP-hard (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Experiments**: All 1- and 2-game switches for 2006–2011 are enumerated, and the switch measure δ (1-norm of rank changes in the top 25) is recorded. For CM, L = 1 gives mean 5.1 and max 20 (Fig. 1, pp. 10–11). For CM+ with Γ = 5 the mean is 1.8 and the max 10 (Fig. 4, p. 18). Per-instance bubble plots (Fig. 5, p. 19). Solves take under a minute with CPLEX 12.2 (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are a robustness concept and a method. Limits stated: no best a priori Γ (p. 6); larger N and Γ may take hours (p. 14); the robust rankings are hard to judge objectively for any one Γ (p. 14). Extensions: other sports, other definitions of the inconsequential set (p. 20).

**Method links**: Method 4 ⚠ variant: the incumbent is first stress-tested exactly where it is fragile (exhaustive enumeration of all L ≤ 2 switches of inconsequential games), and the fix is judged on the same perturbation set. The pool is defined by a rule but not filtered for hardness (pp. 8–11, 16–19). Method 3 ✅ evidence (credit and revision): footnote 1 says the first version used p = ∞, which had many alternative optima, and thanks the referees for the switch to p = 2 (pp. 12–13). New-pattern candidate: *diagnose the incumbent's sensitivity, then robustify with a budget Γ that nests the incumbent at Γ = 0* (pp. 6, 10–11).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Before proposing a robust variant, quantify the base method's fragility with a single scalar (the switch measure) over all small perturbations, and plot both methods on the same scale (pp. 9–11, 18–19).
- Solve min-max over a combinatorial uncertainty set by constraint generation: a convex master problem over finitely many scenario cuts, plus an integer separation subproblem (p. 13).
- Pick the uncertainty budget so that Γ = 0 gives back the incumbent and a large Γ gives provable invariance, then study intermediate Γ (pp. 6, 16).

### S052 · Newsvendor games: convex optimization of centralized inventory operations (TOP 2012, DOI 10.1007/s11750-010-0160-x)
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. Dror · **Position in trajectory**: an applied SDP paper in operations management. The CV and Crossref date it 2012 (print, TOP 20(3)); the text read is the 2010 OnlineFirst version (accepted September 2010, p. 1). **Read**: partial. Read: pp. 1–13 (abstract, introduction, model, assumptions, Theorems 3.1–3.2 with proofs, n = 3 case) and pp. 15–17 (managerial comments, fair allocation, Theorem 4.1 statement, summary). Not read: the three numerical examples (pp. 13–15) and the appendix proof of Theorem 4.1 (p. 17 onward).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Hartman & Dror (2003) showed that choosing the outlets' demand correlations to minimize the centralized newsvendor cost is an SDP (p. 3). The paper asks for its structure and for a fair (core, nucleolus) allocation of the optimized cost (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: The problem min σᵀRσ over correlation matrices (2) is the max-cut SDP feasible set (p. 8). Shapiro's (1982) minimum-trace-factor-analysis theorem classifies the cases by the sign of vᵀσ (Theorem 3.1, pp. 8–9). For vᵀσ < 0, an explicit rank-2 optimum is built for n = 3 (Proposition 3.1, pp. 9–11). It is lifted to any n through a balanced bi- or tri-partition of the outlets (Proposition 3.2, Theorem 3.2, pp. 11–12). Under the optimal R*, the nucleolus is in closed form: outlet 1 pays everything when vᵀσ > 0 (Theorem 4.1, p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Jointly normal demand, identical holding and penalty costs, and correlations that can be set freely while the means and standard deviations stay fixed. The last is presented as analogous to a perfect-information assumption, one that lets them "gauge the value of modifying correlations" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Factor R = VVᵀ with unit rows and σᵀV = 0 (p. 9). Uniqueness for n = 3 is shown by rotating V so that V₁· = (1, 0) (pp. 10–11). The aggregation proof is σᵀV* = σ̄ᵀV̄* = 0 (p. 12). The rank bound (1 or 2) is compared with Pataki's √(2n) bound, which holds for any objective (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (three 6-outlet worked examples on pp. 13–15, not read).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory with managerial reading: target R*, group outlets, correlate only between groups (pp. 15–16). Limits stated: normality is key; general holding and penalty costs admit no fair allocation (p. 7); the low rank has no "satisfactory a priori explanation" (p. 15); constrained correlation changes would need a general SDP solver (p. 17). Theorem 3.1 is credited to Shapiro in the acknowledgements (p. 17).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence: the operational question is packaged as one convex object (an SDP over the elliptope), and the analysis is done on that object (pp. 8–9). Method 2 ✅ evidence: the smallest nontrivial case (n = 3) is solved exactly first, then the general case is reduced to it by aggregation (pp. 9–12). Also ties to the low-rank theme of Burer–Monteiro: a closed-form rank-1 or rank-2 optimum, versus the high-rank interior points that off-the-shelf SDP solvers return (pp. 12–13). New-pattern candidate: *closed-form low-rank optimum as structural insight*. A solver's output would hide the structure; the explicit low-rank solution gives a managerial rule (pp. 12–16).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Solve the n = 3 case of a structured SDP explicitly, then reduce general n to it by grouping variables into a balanced partition whose aggregated data satisfy the n = 3 condition (pp. 11–12).
- Search for a low-rank optimum through a factor V with unit rows and a linear condition σᵀV = 0, instead of through R itself (p. 9).
- Look up adjacent literatures (here statistics: minimum-trace factor analysis) for theorems on the same SDP feasible set, and credit the person who pointed you to them (pp. 8, 17).
**Quotes**: "In this sense, solving (2) via a standard SDP solver does not reveal the structure of Theorem 3.2." (p. 13) · "We unfortunately do not have a satisfactory a priori explanation, but it is interesting to understand the implications of this result." (p. 15)
