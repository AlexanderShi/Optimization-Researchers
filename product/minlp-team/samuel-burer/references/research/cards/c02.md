# Batch c02 · Samuel Burer · core

**Batch intent**: See how Burer's first-order SDP programme of 2003–2006 was built and then carried to new problem classes. Four questions: (a) how the low-rank factorization algorithm (S001) was assembled from known pieces and evaluated before any convergence proof; (b) how the follow-up theory paper (S003) closed part of that gap and fed back into the code; (c) how the augmented-Lagrangian machinery was moved to Lovász–Schrijver lift-and-project relaxations of 0-1 programs (S013), the IP work just before his move to continuous nonconvex QP; (d) what happens when the SDP tool goes into a machine-learning application (S005). I read against Method 5 (prototype first, theory follows) and Method 1 (one lifted convex object), and look for the roots of H6–H8 (bound alarms, loose inner solves, printed losses).

**Material roles**: S001 core · S003 core · S005 core (second author of three; ML venue) · S013 core.

### S001 · A nonlinear programming algorithm for solving semidefinite programs via low-rank factorization (Mathematical Programming, Ser. B 2003, DOI 10.1007/s10107-002-0352-8)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–29: text, all tables and the reference list).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Renato D. C. Monteiro (Georgia Tech) · **Place in trajectory**: Burer had just moved to Iowa (affiliation p. 1, with a Georgia Tech e-mail). The paper follows his earlier papers with Monteiro and Zhang on nonlinear reformulations of SDP ([4]–[6], p. 2). It is the SDPLR paper that S005 and S013 later use as a tool.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a practical gap. Interior-point SDP methods are "quite time- and memory-intensive", especially for relaxations of large combinatorial problems, and a trend toward "practically efficient algorithms that are less likely to have strong theoretical guarantees" had begun; the paper places itself in that trend as "experimental" (p. 1). The direct predecessors are Homer–Peinado's X = VVᵀ for maxcut and the authors' Cholesky variant (p. 2). The problem is stated as three questions about the nonconvex reformulation (2): can n² variables be managed (Q1), which method fits and can it exploit sparsity (Q2), can a global solution be expected despite nonconvexity (Q3) (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: state the open risks of a new reformulation as numbered questions in the introduction, then answer each one with a named tool (theorem, algorithm, experiment), as pp. 2–3 do.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Replace X ⪰ 0 by X = RRᵀ with R ∈ ℝⁿˣʳ. Choose r by the Barvinok–Pataki bound r(r+1)/2 ≤ m, so the restricted problem (N_r) still contains an SDP optimum while having nr instead of n² variables (pp. 3, 5). Solve (N_r) by an augmented Lagrangian with limited-memory BFGS (three updates) and a strong Wolfe–Powell line search (pp. 8–10). Function and gradient cost scale with the nonzeros of the data (Props 5–7, pp. 11–12). What changed relative to Homer–Peinado: general standard-form SDP instead of maxcut only, and a rectangular factor sized by a rank theorem. What stayed: a first-order method on a factorized variable. For maxcut the norm-one row constraints are eliminated, so the problem becomes unconstrained (18) (p. 19). A dynamic-rank scheme (raise r until the injected point is a local minimum) is derived from Prop. 4 (p. 8). It is tested as LR5 on maxcut and bisection and deliberately not used on Lovász theta (pp. 14, 20).
  - Transferable technique: when a convex problem has a known bound on the rank (or support) of some optimal solution, restrict the variable to that size and accept nonconvexity in exchange for size.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The constraint matrices are linearly independent; primal and dual optimal sets are nonempty with zero duality gap, and the authors flag the Vandenberghe–Boyd counterexample with a positive gap (p. 4). Theorem 1 needs an extreme point, and "all of the specific SDPs that we consider in this paper satisfy the hypothesis" (p. 5). Prop. 4 needs regularity (linearly independent constraint gradients), which they cannot guarantee for theta. That is the stated reason the dynamic rank is not used there (p. 14). For maxcut and bisection they check the regularity explicitly (pp. 20, 24).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - First- and second-order conditions for (N_r) through S = C − Σ yᵢAᵢ, giving ∇L = 2SR (Prop. 2, p. 6).
  - Orthogonal invariance R → RQ shows there are no strict local minima, so standard sufficiency conditions are useless (pp. 6–7).
  - Rank-independent sufficient condition: a stationary R with S ⪰ 0 is SDP-optimal (Prop. 3, p. 7).
  - The rank-escalation lemma (Prop. 4, p. 7): append a zero column; if the injected point is still a local minimum of (N_{r+1}), test directions D that are nonzero only in the new column; second-order necessity then forces dᵀSd ≥ 0 for every d, so S ⪰ 0.
  - Flop and space counts per data structure (sparse vs rank-one) as propositions (Props 5–8, pp. 11–12, 15).
  - No convergence proof; the authors say so on p. 27.
  - Transferable technique: to certify optimality of a factorized local minimum, embed it one dimension higher and read second-order conditions along the new coordinate only. This turns a nonconvex local condition into a convex certificate (S ⪰ 0).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Setup: ANSI C on an SGI Origin2000, one processor per code (p. 13). Baselines are the spectral bundle code SB and the dual-scaling interior-point code DSDP, "run with their default parameters" (p. 16).
  - Instances: Lovász theta on DIMACS hamming graphs, Gset and second-DIMACS graphs (Tables 1–7, pp. 16–20); maxcut on torus and Gset graphs up to G81 with 20,000 vertices (Tables 8–14, pp. 21–24); minimum bisection (Tables 15–18, p. 25). There is a ten-hour limit per instance (pp. 18, 25).
  - Accuracy is not assumed from status. Each table reports the final infeasibility and the relative difference between the primal value (LR) and a dual value (SB, DSDP-d) (Tables 3, 10, 17).
  - The 10⁻⁵ infeasibility target is argued from experiment. They report that no primal value ever passed below a dual lower bound (p. 14).
  - Losses are printed. On the hamming theta graphs SB wins, and the authors explain that SB needs an "atypical" single iteration there (Table 4, p. 17). Industry2 accuracy is affected by SB's time limit (p. 25).
  - A time-matched comparison (§4.4, Tables 19–20, p. 26) gives SB's objective at the moment LR stopped. SB is within about 10⁻⁴ on theta but only about 10⁻² on maxcut.
  - Headline: G81, where "LR5 is almost 500 times faster than SB" (p. 22).
  - Transferable technique: when your method is primal-only and the baseline is dual, put a "relative primal–dual difference" column in every table and check for bound crossing. It certifies both codes at once.
  - Transferable technique: report the baseline's objective at the moment your method stopped (time-matched truncation, §4.4). It separates "slow to converge" from "slow to get close".
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues Homer–Peinado [15] and the authors' own [4]–[6] (p. 2). It separates itself from Bentler and from Lu–Klein, whose aims are "quite different" and whose method is "theoretical in nature and no computational results are presented" (p. 3). Rivals are named with care: dual methods are "much more valuable than our primal method" when a valid bound is needed, because the primal must be solved exactly to guarantee a bound (p. 13). Speed "may not be the most important characteristic" (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The Q1–Q3 frame opens the paper and each answer is mapped to a section (pp. 2–3). Hedges are dense and explicit: "experimental" (p. 1), "largely experimental" (p. 3), "we feel" (pp. 14, 27). Claims are strong only where measured ("the best progress made on solving the Lovász SDP to date", p. 27). A self-contained warning subsection (§3.2.1, pp. 12–13) explains a failure mode (gradient-preserved zero columns) and its fix.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions are algorithmic (low-rank AL method), theoretical (optimality conditions, Props 2–4) and computational (p. 27). The limits are specific:
  - slow augmented-Lagrangian subproblems for theta and bisection;
  - no formal convergence proof;
  - only equality-form SDPs;
  - possible dual low-rank analogue via Pataki's bound on rank(S*) (p. 27).
  The S003 card shows the convergence gap was taken up two years later.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. Two known pieces combined (Homer–Peinado factorization plus the Barvinok–Pataki rank bound, pp. 2–3), the practical claim carried by experiment with the theory gap printed (pp. 3, 27), baselines at defaults (p. 16), and a variant dropped for weak theory and poor convergence (dynamic rank on theta, pp. 14–15).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The "one object" move runs the other way: a convex lifted object (X ⪰ 0) is collapsed back to a nonconvex factor whose size is fixed by a theorem (pp. 5, 27). Here the reformulation serves computation, not exactness of a hull.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (early form). Accuracy rests on two independent signals, primal infeasibility and primal–dual agreement with another code, plus a bound-crossing check (pp. 14, 17).
- New-pattern candidate: **valid bound from an inexact first-order solver**. Here it appears as the acknowledged weakness (a primal method gives no bound, p. 13), which S003 and S013 then address.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Size a factorized variable by a rank theorem (r(r+1)/2 ≤ m), not by trial (p. 5).
- Rank escalation with a zero-column injection test as a certificate of SDP optimality (Prop. 4, pp. 7–8).
- Initialize away from structured zero patterns: perturb a structured feasible start by a small dense matrix, because gradients preserve zero columns and silently solve a smaller problem (§3.2.1, pp. 12–13, 15).
- In the AL update, de-emphasize the penalty (γ = √10 instead of 10) and favour multiplier progress when ill-conditioning appears (p. 15).
- Time-matched baseline truncation (§4.4, p. 26).

**Quotes**:
- "Nonetheless, after much experimentation, we feel that 10−5 is a reasonable amount of infeasibility to require." (p. 14)
- "Although we have some theoretical justification that explains the observed practical convergence, we do not currently have a formal convergence proof." (p. 27)

### S003 · Local minima and convergence in low-rank semidefinite programming (Mathematical Programming, Ser. A 2005, DOI 10.1007/s10107-004-0564-1)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–18: text, Tables 1–4 and the reference list).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Renato D. C. Monteiro · **Place in trajectory**: The theory sequel to S001, received September 2003 (p. 1). The abstract calls S001's algorithm "successful, yet experimental" (p. 1). Kurt Anstreicher is thanked for QAP discussions (p. 18), the first sign in this batch of the later collaborator.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is the unexplained practical behaviour of S001: although NSDP_r is nonconvex, an optimal solution was always reached in the experiments, and "a formal convergence proof for their method is not established" (p. 3). A second, practical entry is that S001 gave no lower bounds during the run (p. 3). The problem is stated as: classify the local minima of LRSDP_r, and prove convergence for a slight variant of the S001 algorithm (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: write the theory paper on your own experimental algorithm. Name the observed behaviour (here "(ii)", p. 3) and set out to "shed some theoretical light" on exactly that (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: There are three moves.
  - Show that the change of variables X = RRᵀ introduces no extraneous local minima (Prop. 2.3, p. 4).
  - Classify local minima of the rank-constrained problem: for r ≥ r_{m+1}, a local minimum is optimal unless it lies in the relative interior of a positive-dimensional face on which the objective is constant (Thm 3.4, pp. 6–7).
  - Perturb the augmented Lagrangian by μ_k det(RᵀR) with μ_k → 0. Local minima of the perturbed subproblem are rank-deficient (Lemma 5.2, p. 11), which supplies hypothesis (d) of Thm 4.1 and gives primal and dual optimality of accumulation points (Thm 5.3, p. 11).
  What changed: only the det term. What did not change: everything else in S001 (p. 13). The det term is not implemented, because its gradient needs a cofactor matrix the authors cannot compute stably (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: when a proof needs a property that the algorithm does not guarantee (here rank deficiency), add a vanishing perturbation that forces it. Then argue that the perturbed and practical algorithms differ only in the limit μ_k → 0 (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: SDP has an interior point (the dual need not), and both problems attain their values with zero gap (pp. 1–2). r is large enough that LRSDP_r ≡ SDP (p. 2). Thm 3.4 needs r ≥ r_{m+1}, one level above Pataki's bound (pp. 6–7). Thm 5.3 needs r > r_{m+1} and a bounded {R^k} (p. 11). Thm 5.4 drops boundedness through a Frobenius ball, at the price of r > r_{m+2} (p. 12). The authors state which hypotheses they cannot verify. Feasibility of the limit and boundedness of {S^k} are equivalent under the theorem's assumptions, but "we do not know whether one of these two conditions will always hold" (p. 12). Boundedness is argued for the test problems through their implied trace bound (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - Lemma 2.1: RRᵀ = SSᵀ ⇒ S = RQ with Q orthogonal, via range equality and the extension of orthonormal rows (p. 4).
  - Prop. 2.3 by a subsequence argument (p. 4).
  - Pataki's rank-reduction step applied to a whole sequence (Lemma 3.2, pp. 5–6): move each X^k along ±R^kΔ^k(R^k)ᵀ inside the iso-cost slice. The extreme-point property of the limit forces the moves to vanish.
  - Counting unknowns against equations in system φ(R) gives the r_{m+1} threshold (p. 5).
  - Thm 4.1's key step builds test directions H^k = [0,…,0,h](Q^k)ᵀ from a zero column, which yields lim inf S^k • hhᵀ ≥ 0, i.e. asymptotic dual feasibility (p. 8). Boundedness of S^k is by contradiction with a recession direction that would violate the primal interior assumption (pp. 8–9).
  - Lemma 5.1: δ = 0 is not a local minimum of det(I + δΔ) (pp. 10–11). This is used with a Cholesky reparametrization R_δ = R·chol(I + δΔ) that keeps all inner products fixed (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: carry over a single-point structural lemma (rank reduction) to sequences by building the perturbation sequence and using extremality of the limit to show it vanishes (Lemma 3.2).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Setup: same code as S001, on a Pentium 2.4 GHz (pp. 13–14).
  - New certificates, computed during the run: primal feasibility, complementarity ‖S^kR^k‖ and λ_min(S^k) (p. 13). Lower bounds come from shifting S̄ by its minimum eigenvalue (LASO Lanczos) when the identity lies in the span of the A_i (p. 14). An easily computable scaling extends this to 0-1 QPs (p. 15).
  - Results: five maxcut and five stable-set relaxations (Table 1, p. 14). The stable-set bounds are weaker, and Table 2 shows the cost of tightening ρ_c (p. 15).
  - QAP: QAPR1 and QAPR2 for 17 QAPLIB instances with 30 ≤ ℓ ≤ 40. The largest run takes about 6.4 days (ste36b) (Table 4, pp. 16–17).
  - A cross-paper timing is made on a different machine. On nug30 a bound of 5629 takes about 36 minutes, against Rendl–Sotirov's 5651 in about 10 hours on an Athlon XP 1.8 GHz, and the machine is named (p. 17).
  - Exclusions are explained: QAPR3 is not tested because inequalities do not yet fit the method and choosing them is itself hard (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: if the problem class implies a constant trace, turn any approximate dual slack into a valid bound by shifting by λ_min. Report that bound beside the primal value (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: Three families of SDP methods are set out and placed by accuracy and size: second-order interior point, iterative-solver interior point and first-order NLP (p. 2). The paper's own ancestor is S001. The theory corrects the practice in print: the paper notes that the S001 implementation required only r ≥ r_m, and that, given Theorem 3.4, the new implementation uses r ≥ r_{m+1} (p. 7). QAP results are placed against Lin–Saigal, Zhao et al. and Rendl–Sotirov (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The results are labelled by distance from practice. The convergent variant is "slight", and the paper says plainly that the numerical results use the unperturbed S001 algorithm (p. 13). Each theorem is followed by a "roughly speaking" reading (p. 7: how large r must be). Drawbacks are named right after the theorems ("A disadvantage of Theorem 4.1…", p. 9; "one drawback of the above theorem…", p. 12). One proof is "left to the reader" (Thm 4.2, p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions are theory (no spurious minima; classification; convergence of a variant) and practice (in-run lower bounds; the largest QAP SDPs to date, p. 3). Limits are stated specifically:
  - "flat" faces can harbour non-global local minima (p. 7);
  - the det term is not implemented (p. 13);
  - feasibility and boundedness are not guaranteed (p. 12);
  - stable-set duals are less accurate (p. 15);
  - inequality constraints are open (p. 16);
  - QAP times are long (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (step 6, "publish with the gap stated, then prove what you can"). This is the very paper the SKILL cites for that step. It confirms that the proved algorithm is a variant that is not run (p. 13).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. The claim is narrowed in print. The 2003 code's r ≥ r_m is shown insufficient for the local-minimum guarantee and the implementation changed (p. 7). Unverified conditions are flagged as "always observed in our practical experiments" rather than proved (p. 12).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The object studied is the face structure of the SDP feasible set, and the hardness of the nonconvex reformulation is located in "flat" faces (Thm 3.4, pp. 6–7). The aim is algorithm convergence, not an exact reformulation of a hard problem.
- New-pattern candidate: **valid bound from an inexact first-order solver** (λ_min shift, pp. 14–15; links S001 p. 13 and S013 p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Vanishing determinant perturbation μ_k det(RᵀR) to force rank deficiency in factorized subproblems (Lemmas 5.1–5.2, pp. 10–11).
- Zero-column test directions to extract dual feasibility from second-order conditions (Thm 4.1 proof, p. 8).
- Replace an assumed bounded iterate sequence by a Frobenius-ball constraint and pay one rank level (r > r_{m+2}, Thm 5.4, p. 12).
- In-run certificate triple: primal infeasibility, ‖SR‖, λ_min(S) (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "In particular, we show that the change of variables does not introduce any extraneous local minima." (p. 3)
- "While it seems that the extra term is necessary for theoretical convergence, it does not appear to be necessary for practical convergence." (p. 13)

### S005 · Ensemble pruning via semi-definite programming (Journal of Machine Learning Research 2006, no DOI; JMLR 7:1315–1338)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–24: text, Tables 1–5, Figures 1–3 and the reference list).
**Role**: core (applied ML paper; Burer is second of three authors) · **Coauthors**: Yi Zhang, W. Nick Street (all three at Iowa Management Sciences, p. 1). The batch metadata also lists K. P. Bennett and E. Parrado-Hernández as authors, but p. 1 names them as the issue's editors · **Place in trajectory**: An outside application of S001's SDPLR code (cited as the solver, p. 8). The acknowledgment cites only Burer's NSF grants, including CCF-0545514 (p. 22). Which author did which part is not stated.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry comes from an application. Ensembles grow large (memory, prediction time, distributed and streaming settings), and bigger is not always better (boosting overfits) (pp. 1–2). Existing pruning heuristics "all resort to greedy search, which is on the lower end of optimization techniques and usually without either theoretical or empirical quality guarantees" (p. 2). The problem is stated as choosing k classifiers to minimise a quadratic proxy of ensemble error, a 0-1 QP with a cardinality constraint (3) (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: when an applied field solves a subset-selection problem greedily, write the objective explicitly as a 0-1 QP and ask which known relaxation fits it.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The error matrix P gives G = PᵀP. It is normalized to G̃, with error rates on the diagonal and averaged conditional co-error rates off it (pp. 4–5). The pruning QP is then mapped to max-cut-with-size-k (MC-k): substitute x = (v+1)/2, homogenize with v₀ = 1 into matrices H and D, relax v₀ ∈ {−1, 1}, lift to V = vvᵀ and drop rank one (pp. 6–8). The SDP is solved with SDPLR and rounded with the Han–Ye–Zhang randomization (p. 8). What changed relative to MC-k practice: a minimization objective and a 0/1 origin. What did not: the Goemans–Williamson-type relaxation and rounding (pp. 7–8).
  - Transferable technique: the homogenization trick. Prepend v₀ = 1, absorb linear and constant terms into a bordered matrix (H, D in (10)–(11)), then relax v₀ ∈ {−1, 1} by sign symmetry (pp. 6–8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The ensemble error is approximated by a linear combination of strength and pairwise-diversity terms, by analogy with the continuous-output decompositions (Krogh–Vedelsby, Zhou et al.) (pp. 4–5). The authors call the diversity term "ad hoc" and report that other diversity measures made no significant difference (p. 5). The cardinality constraint is needed because without it the optimum is empty (p. 5). They state that the equivalence with MC-k is between optimal solution sets, not values, so MC-k's approximation guarantee does not transfer (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No theorem is proved; this is n/a in the theorem sense. The derivation devices are the variable transformation and homogenization (pp. 6–7), the sign-symmetry argument for relaxing v₀ (p. 8) and rank-one dropping (p. 8). The authors explain why a guarantee is hard to get: SDP approximation results favour maximization form, and theirs is a minimization (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: when you borrow an approximation algorithm through a reformulation, state explicitly whether the reformulation preserves optimal sets or optimal values, and so whether the guarantee survives (p. 7).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - UCI study: 24 data sets from 16 UCI sources (multi-class sets split into class pairs), AdaBoost with 100 unpruned C4.5 trees, pruned to 25. Five ten-fold cross-validations, with win–loss–tie by mean and by a 95% t test (pp. 9–10). Baselines are diversity-based and Kappa pruning, chosen because they also prune to a preset size (p. 9).
  - UCI results: SDP-25 beats both baselines on most sets and is statistically tied with the unpruned ensemble (Table 1, p. 10). Its errors also vary less (p. 10).
  - Timing: log-log growth, about a day for 3,000 classifiers, with a MATLAB caveat (Table 2, Fig. 1, pp. 11–12).
  - Case study: DMEF catalog data with 19 skewed categories; bagged trees with balanced bootstrap; selective sharing of 475 pooled trees down to 25 per category. AUC with paired t tests (Table 4, p. 16), a class-balanced Ĝ (p. 15), and Herfindahl-index analysis of the sharing structure (pp. 17–21).
  - Transferable technique: pick baselines that can be forced to the same output size as your method, so the comparison is fair at equal cost (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against greedy pruning heuristics (Margineantu–Dietterich, Prodromidis et al.) and evolutionary approaches (pp. 2–3). It is set apart from weight-based ensemble optimization, which has "no control over the final size" (p. 3). SDP in ML is placed beside sparse PCA and kernel learning (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Applied-ML style, with motivation through examples (WalMart, peer-to-peer spam filters, p. 2). Hedges appear in the formulation ("should be a good approximation", "ad hoc", p. 5) and in the scaling claim ("we may cautiously conclude", p. 11). Table cross-references in the text are wrong: the UCI results table is captioned Table 1 but cited as "Table 3" (pp. 9–11), and the sharing-statistics table is captioned Table 5 but cited as "Table 4" (p. 17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: An application and method contribution, without theory. The stated limits:
  - no approximation guarantee (p. 7);
  - ad hoc normalization and weights to tune (p. 21);
  - the cardinality constraint is needed to avoid a trivial solution (p. 21);
  - about a day for 3,000 classifiers (p. 11).
  Future directions: tune the normalization and the diagonal/off-diagonal weighting; find an objective whose optimal subset size is endogenous (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. One lifted convex object (V ⪰ 0 with diag V = e) packages the combinatorial difficulty (pp. 7–8). Here it serves as a heuristic relaxation for rounding, not an exact reformulation, and the paper says the guarantee is lost (p. 7).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (weak). The authors' own public code (SDPLR) is used as an off-the-shelf solver in a new domain, with a timing study before any theory (pp. 8, 11).
- New-pattern candidate: **export the SDP tool into an applied collaborator's problem** (relax an applied 0-1 QP and use one's own solver). Only this paper in the batch shows it.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Homogenize a 0/1 quadratic with a cardinality constraint into a ±1 problem with bordered matrices, then relax to an SDP (pp. 6–8).
- Split the data used to build the selection matrix by class and recombine convexly (Ĝ = λG̃_pos + (1−λ)G̃_neg) when classes are highly skewed (p. 15).
- Report a concentration index (Herfindahl) of where the selected items come from, to read structure out of a selection (pp. 18–21).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, if we are able to transform the ensemble pruning formulation so that it fits into the framework of MC-k, we may obtain a good solution for the ensemble pruning problem." (p. 6)
- "In fact, it seems extremely difficult to derive directly any approximation guarantees for our problem." (p. 7)

### S013 · Solving lift-and-project relaxations of binary integer programs (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2006, DOI 10.1137/040609574)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–25: text, proofs in §3.4, Tables 1–9 and the reference list).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Dieter Vandenbussche (UIUC, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Received June 2004 (p. 1). This is the 0-1 IP work of the Burer–Vandenbussche partnership that later produced the finite SDP branch-and-bound for nonconvex QP. The conclusion already names "problems with a mix of binary and continuous variables and of continuous nonconvex QP's" as next targets (p. 24).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Lift-and-project relaxations are "quite powerful theoretically" but "present great computational challenges" because one must optimize in the lifted space (p. 1). Only one computational study of the first-level Lovász–Schrijver relaxations existed (Dash), "due to the dramatic size of these relaxations", with one SDP of "well over 1.7 million constraints" (pp. 1–2). The problem is stated as computing the bounds min{cᵀx : x ∈ N(K)} and min{cᵀx : x ∈ N₊(K)} for general 0-1 programs (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: choose a relaxation that is known to be strong in theory but has almost no computational record. The first method that computes it at scale is the contribution, and its numbers also become the first evidence of the relaxation's strength.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The lifted LP is restructured by introducing Z = YQ (columns Y(e₀ − eᵢ)). The constraints then separate over the columns of Y and Z, and only the linking equations h(Y, Z) = 0 couple them (pp. 4–5). These are dualized, and instead of the subgradient method the augmented Lagrangian is used: each subproblem is a nonseparable convex QP solved by one cycle of block coordinate descent over columns (pp. 11–12, 14). For N₊(K), an "excess" copy U = Y with U ⪰ 0 is introduced, so the PSD constraint is handled by a projection of the symmetrized Y − σ⁻¹S onto the PSD cone (pp. 12–13). §3.3 shows the AL step is a bundle step without the bundle approximation and without a serious-step test (pp. 8–9). Initial experiments with subgradient methods were slow, and the authors say this motivated the AL choice (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: variable splitting by copies (Z = YQ, U = Y) to make a monolithic lifted relaxation separable. Put the coupling and the cone in separate blocks and dualize only the copy equations.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Generic 0-1 IP with Ax ≤ b. K is replaced by K̂ = K ∩ [0, 1]^{1+n} without changing the optimal sets (p. 4). Theorem 3.1 needs A of full row rank and Slater points for both (18) and (20) (p. 7). The dual of (2) has an interior point, so there is no gap (p. 5). For QAP, constraints (7) are implied by (6), so Z is dropped and 2n+1 subproblems become n+1 (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - Theorem 3.1: for linear conic problems, the AL method converges with a constant penalty σ. This extends Poljak–Tret'jakov from ℝ^q₊ to a general cone (p. 7, proof §3.4, pp. 9–11).
  - The proof is a monotonicity inequality for the dual iterates (Lemma 3.2), an exactness lemma at a dual optimum (Lemma 3.3), and a telescoping sum ‖λ^{k+1} − λ*‖² ≤ ‖λ^k − λ*‖² − σ²‖b − Ay^k‖², giving Ay^k → b (Prop. 3.4, pp. 10–11). Each iterate is then the exact primal–dual solution of a perturbed problem with right-hand side Ay^k (Lemma 3.5, p. 11).
  - Also used: a symmetric/skew-symmetric split showing that projecting a nonsymmetric matrix equals projecting its symmetric part (p. 13); a duality argument that the multiplier S may be kept PSD by projection (p. 13); and the AL–bundle correspondence (25) (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: to prove convergence with a fixed parameter, show each iterate solves exactly a problem whose data are perturbed by the current residual, then prove the residual is square-summable by telescoping (Lemma 3.5 and Prop. 3.4).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Setup: ANSI C on a Pentium 4 at 2.4 GHz; CPLEX 8.1 pivoting QP for subproblems, chosen for warm starts; LAPACK for eigendecompositions (pp. 13–14).
  - Theory and practice part ways openly: theory says σ need not grow, but practice raises it 10× every 500 iterations (p. 14). Stop at violation < 10⁻⁶, with an alternate stop at σ > 10⁸ found on "a handful of instances" (p. 14).
  - Four classes: 26 complemented DIMACS clique graphs, Erdős–Turán with n = 60–150, two generated markshare-type 0-1 instances, and 91 QAPLIB instances (pp. 14–16).
  - Baselines: CPLEX dual simplex with default and steepest-edge pricing, 15,000 s limit (Table 2, p. 17); the volume subgradient algorithm with starting duals tuned for it (all −1) and with "surpass time" columns (Table 3, pp. 17–18); CSDP failed for memory (p. 17).
  - Quality yardsticks: α, ϑ, ϑ⁺ and CPLEX IP bounds (pp. 19–20). For QAP: GLB, KCCEB and RSB (pp. 16, 21–22).
  - Printed losses:
    - subgradient beats AL on Erdős–Turán (p. 17);
    - the computed N₊ bound is above ϑ on most stable-set instances, i.e. not optimized fully (p. 19);
    - CPLEX's IP upper bound after 15,000 s beats both relaxations on Erdős–Turán (p. 20);
    - markshare gets no improvement over the LP bound of 0 (p. 20);
    - Hahn's level-2 RLT bounds, received after first submission, beat N₊ on several QAPs (Table 8, p. 23);
    - computing N is slower than KCCEB's QAP-specific code (p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: give a "surpass time" column, the time at which each method first beats the other's final bound (Table 3). It reports speed and quality in one number.
  - Transferable technique: run the baseline with its own best tuning (here the volume algorithm's starting duals) and say that you tuned it (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Placed in the lift-and-project lineage (Balas; Sherali–Adams; Lovász–Schrijver; Lasserre; Bienstock–Zuckerberg) and Laurent's comparisons (p. 1). It claims to fill a gap: the AL method for LPs was "not new" (Poljak–Tret'jakov; Sherali et al.), but "little work has been done on the computational aspects", and the AL method for SDPs is presented "for the first time" (p. 2). Rivals are treated fairly: KCCEB is shown equivalent in theory to N(K) (p. 16), and its structure-exploiting code "does not appear to be generalizable", while the AL method "can be applied to any 0-1 problem" (p. 23). The headline is that N₊ is tighter than RSB, previously the strongest known QAPLIB bounds (p. 23).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper says early on that applying an NLP algorithm to linear conic problems "may seem counterintuitive" (p. 6). Each implementation choice is tied to a theory statement and its practical override (σ growth, one BCD cycle, pp. 14–15). The authors keep the claim at "can be (approximately) solved for general 0-1 integer programs" (p. 19), not "solved". A late result (Hahn's bounds) is added as its own table with provenance ("After the initial appearance of this paper", p. 23).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions are algorithmic (decomposition plus AL for LS relaxations), theoretical (Thm 3.1 for general cones) and computational (strongest known QAPLIB bounds at the time; first large N₊ computations) (p. 24). The limits are specific:
  - incomplete N₊ optimization (p. 19);
  - slow AL on Erdős–Turán (pp. 17–18);
  - no gain on markshare (p. 20);
  - O(n³) eigendecompositions dominate for QAP (pp. 23–24).
  Future directions: special-purpose QP subproblem solvers; mixed binary–continuous and nonconvex QP relaxations; other block-angular conic problems (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. One lifted object Y = [1 xᵀ; x xxᵀ] carries the strength, and the work goes into "attacking the object" with a tractable outer approximation and a solver (pp. 3–5). The conclusion points this machinery at mixed binary–continuous and nonconvex QP (p. 24), the scope of the later copositive and B&B work.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. Subgradient experiments first, then a method change "motivated" by them (p. 5); practice overrides theory with the override printed (σ growth, one BCD cycle, pp. 14–15); strongest public codes as baselines (p. 17).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (early form). The authors report that the computed N₊ value lies above ϑ, which theory says it cannot exceed at optimality. They read this as incomplete optimization, not as a property of the relaxation (p. 19). Theory–practice discrepancies with KCCEB are attributed to numerics (pp. 22–23).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. Instances include a generator for small, hard 0-1 programs (markshare via Cornuéjols–Dawande, p. 15), but the test pool is mainly public libraries (DIMACS, QAPLIB) and not filtered to where the baseline fails.
- New-pattern candidates: **valid bound from an inexact first-order solver** (AL "yields both primal and dual solutions, as well as dual bounds", p. 6; links S001 p. 13, S003 p. 14); **variable splitting by copies to decompose a lifted relaxation** (pp. 4–5, 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Copy-variable decomposition (Z = YQ, U = Y) plus dualizing only the copy equations (pp. 4–5, 12).
- Solve AL subproblems with a single block-coordinate cycle and a warm-startable pivoting QP solver, because the subproblem data change little between iterations (pp. 13–14).
- Project a nonsymmetric matrix onto the PSD cone by projecting its symmetric part (p. 13).
- Constant-penalty convergence for linear conic AL by the telescoping residual argument (pp. 10–11).
- "Surpass time" columns in head-to-head bound tables (Table 3, p. 18).
