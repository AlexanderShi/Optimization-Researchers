### SW1. The achievable-region method: stochastic control written as mathematical programs (1993–2000) · SD, LO, IO

Bertsimas, Paschalidis & Tsitsiklis, *Ann. Appl. Probab.* 4(1):43–75, 1994, DOI 10.1214/aoap/1177005200 (scanned copy, no text layer: not read); Bertsimas & Niño-Mora, "Conservation laws, extended polymatroids and multiarmed bandit problems; a polyhedral approach to indexable systems", *Math. Oper. Res.* 21(2):257–306, 1996, DOI 10.1287/moor.21.2.257 (IPCO 1993 version; first page read from the scan); Bertsimas, "The achievable region method in the optimal control of queueing systems; formulations, bounds and policies", *Queueing Syst.* 21:337–389, 1995, DOI 10.1007/BF01149167 (solo survey; read in part); Bertsimas & Niño-Mora, restless bandits, *Oper. Res.* 48(1):80–90, 2000, DOI 10.1287/opre.48.1.80.12444 (not read).

- **Origin.** [stated, primary] The survey presents a new approach that "the author and his co-workers have developed" to formulate stochastic control problems, mainly queueing systems, as mathematical programs (p. 337). It credits the idea of describing a queue's performance space as a polyhedron to Coffman and Mitrani and to Gelenbe and Mitrani, with extensions by Federgruen and Groenevelt, Shanthikumar and Yao, and Tsoucas (pp. 339–340). How he personally arrived at it is not in any source I read. [inferred] The thesis-era work on probabilistic analysis of combinatorial problems, an MIT environment strong in LP and polyhedral combinatorics, and the collaboration with Tsitsiklis (the 1994 AAP paper) are the likely roots.
- **Why then.** [stated] He frames it as a parallel to two decades of mathematical-programming work on sharper formulations, polyhedral and, more recently, nonlinear (p. 337). [practice] His NSF Presidential Young Investigator award (1991–1996, with Draper Laboratory matching funds) carried a cohort of nine PhD students whom the survey thanks (p. 387).
- **Key insight** (from the papers). Define performance vectors, find constraints that every policy satisfies, and optimize over that region. When the region is exactly a polyhedron (an extended polymatroid, for indexable systems) the problem is solved by an adaptive greedy algorithm, and Gittins indices appear as dual variables (MOR 1996, p. 257). Where it is not exact, the relaxations give bounds and suggest heuristics. The survey states the thesis in one line: "better formulations lead to deeper understanding and better solution methods" (p. 337). [stated, primary]
- **Minimum evidence.** The exact characterization of indexable systems (multiclass single-station queues and multiarmed bandits). The MOR abstract lists ten consequences, among them an LP proof of the decomposability of Gittins indices and an algorithm as fast as the fastest known (p. 257). For networks, computational results summarized in the survey: bounds good in imbalanced traffic, asymptotically exact in some routing examples, very tight in closed networks (pp. 381–382). [practice, primary]
- **Abandoned paths and limits.** [stated, primary] Section 7 of the survey admits that turning relaxations into policies was not solved: "the heuristics were somewhat ad hoc", and "For queueing networks we did not propose policies." (p. 385). It also reports negative complexity results that limit any full characterization of the region. Its open problems (systematic policies from relaxations; the power of semidefinite relaxations for multiclass networks) were followed only in part: the SDP direction reappears in Bertsimas & Natarajan 2007 (DOI 10.1007/s11134-007-9028-7). The survey cites a Bertsimas–Xu paper on polling systems and dynamic vehicle routing as submitted; it appears in neither CV nor DBLP (§3). After 2000 the line gives way to fluid relaxations (2002–2003) and robust queueing (2011–2018). [practice; "gives way" inferred]
- **Reception.** MOR 1996: 29 months from submission to the last revision. Scholar: 288 citations (MOR 1996), 234 (AAP 1994), 321 (restless bandits 2000). The CV lists the Erlang Prize and the SIAM Optimization Prize in 1996; which works those citations named was not verified. In 2024–2026 he returned to restless bandits with Niño-Mora, now with ML-trained policies for fluid models (*Mach. Learn.* 2026). [practice; prize link unverified]
- **Method shown.** Formulation first: put a problem owned by another tradition (here stochastic control, normally handled by dynamic programming) into an LP, conic or integer program, then read bounds, indices and heuristics off the formulation and its dual. Cross-reference: §4 P1.
- **Prologue: the thesis and "A priori optimization"** (*Oper. Res.* 38(6):1019–1033, 1990, DOI 10.1287/opre.38.6.1019; read). The 1990 paper names two motives; the second is the robustness, with respect to optimality, of optimal solutions to deterministic problems when instances change (p. 1019). It says that Jaillet (1985, 1988) introduced the probabilistic TSP and that the problem was first formulated by the third author, Odoni, prompted by daily mail delivery: "it was this last application that led to the initial formulation of the PTSP" (p. 1023). Its conclusion lists stochastic demands and dynamic arrivals as next steps; both became his next papers (*Oper. Res.* 1992, stochastic demand; Bertsimas & van Ryzin 1991, dynamic routing). [stated and practice, primary] In 2014 he described the thesis as an extension of deterministic vehicle routing to stochastic analysis, with a UPS-style delivery example, combining stochastic and optimization ideas (oral history 2014, pp. 1–2). [stated, primary] The robustness motive appears 14 years before SW3. [inferred link]

### SW2. Moment problems and optimal probability inequalities through semidefinite optimization (1998–2005) · CP, SD, AP (finance)

Bertsimas & Popescu, "On the relation between option and stock prices: a convex optimization approach", *Oper. Res.* 50(2):358–374, 2002, DOI 10.1287/opre.50.2.358.424 (not read); Bertsimas & Sethuraman, "Moment problems and semidefinite optimization", in *Handbook of Semidefinite Programming*, 2000, pp. 469–509, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4615-4381-7_16 (author copy of April 2000 read in part); Bertsimas & Popescu, "Optimal inequalities in probability theory: a convex optimization approach", *SIAM J. Optim.* 15(3):780–804, 2005, DOI 10.1137/S1052623401399903 (read in part); Bertsimas, Natarajan & Teo, *SIAM J. Optim.* 15(1):185–209, 2004, DOI 10.1137/S1052623403430610 (not read).

- **Origin.** [stated, primary] Both texts open with the classical bounds of Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff and ask whether they are best possible, whether they extend to several variables, and whether there is "a general theory based on optimization methods" for them (SIOPT p. 780). The 2000 chapter adds the finance question: bound derivative prices from moments without assuming any model of price dynamics, set against the Black–Scholes framework and the 1997 Nobel prize. [inferred] The personal route runs through I. Popescu's thesis (1999, "Applications of optimization in probability, finance and revenue management", CV) and the SDP relaxations already announced in the 1995 survey.
- **Why then.** [inferred] Semidefinite optimization had become a solvable, standard model class by the late 1990s (the 2000 chapter appears in a handbook devoted to it). [practice] Funding: NSF DMI-9610486 and the Singapore-MIT Alliance (both acknowledged, 2000 and 2005).
- **Key insight** (from the papers). A tight moment bound is the optimal value of a convex, often semidefinite, program; its dual gives the worst-case distribution. In one dimension with k moments the optimal bound is an SDP in k + 1 dimensions; for polynomial sets in n dimensions a sequence of SDPs of polynomial size gives improving bounds (SIOPT abstract, p. 780).
- **Minimum evidence.** The univariate SDP characterization, together with complexity results that mark where tractability ends: NP-hard for k ≥ 4 on R^n (and k ≥ 2 on the nonnegative orthant), with polynomial algorithms for k = 1 and k = 2 under convexity conditions (SIOPT abstract). [stated, primary]
- **Abandoned paths and limits.** The NP-hardness results are the stated limits. The April 2000 author copy lists I. Popescu as a third author, while the published chapter (Crossref), Scholar and the CV list only Bertsimas and Sethuraman; why is unknown (Contradictions). Review was slow: 30 months from submission to acceptance. [practice]
- **Reception.** Scholar 1,858 (chapter, merged versions), 703 (SIOPT 2005), 292 (OR 2002). The line continues in his own minimax and distributionally robust work (Bertsimas, Doan, Natarajan & Teo, *Math. Oper. Res.* 35(3):580–602, 2010, CV no. 116; adaptive DRO, *Manag. Sci.* 2019). [practice] How others received it was not read.
- **Method shown.** Take a probability question, write it as an optimization problem, and map where it stops being tractable. Same family as SW1. Cross-reference: §4 P1, P2.

### SW3. "The Price of Robustness" and robust discrete optimization (2001–2004) · RO, IO, LO

Bertsimas & Sim, *Oper. Res.* 52(1):35–53, 2004, DOI 10.1287/opre.1030.0065 (author copy read in part); Bertsimas & Sim, "Robust discrete optimization and network flows", *Math. Program.* 98:49–71, 2003, DOI 10.1007/s10107-003-0396-4 (read in part); talk abstract "The Price of Robustness for Conic Optimization" (Bertsimas & Sim, Waterloo workshop page, 2004 by its folder name; read).

- **Origin.** [stated, primary] The 2004 abstract states the target directly: robust approaches since the early 1970s raise the worry that a solution "might be too conservative" (p. 35). The paper names Soyster (*Oper. Res.* 21(5):1154–1157, 1973, DOI 10.1287/opre.21.5.1154) as too conservative and the ellipsoidal sets of Ben-Tal–Nemirovski and El Ghaoui as giving nonlinear, conic counterparts (pp. 35–36). The 2003 paper adds that such conic counterparts cannot be applied directly to discrete problems, and that the earlier scenario-based robust discrete framework (Kouvelis and Yu) makes many polynomial problems NP-hard (pp. 49–50). [inferred] The personal origin: M. Sim's PhD (thesis "Robust optimization", 2004, two Nicholson second prizes, CV), funded by a National University of Singapore scholarship, with Bertsimas supported by the Singapore-MIT Alliance (acknowledged in 2003). No first-person account was found (one WebSearch, Gaps). The robustness motive of 1990 (SW1 prologue) and the 1996 title "robust algorithms" for vehicle routing (DOI 10.1287/opre.44.2.286) suggest a long incubation. The moment line (from 1998) and the RO line (from 2001) also begin just after the change in how he chose topics that he dates to his mid-thirties (§1.1). [inferred]
- **Why then.** [stated in the co-authored survey; author copy dated 2007] The late-1990s work of Ben-Tal and Nemirovski and El Ghaoui, together with advances in computing and fast interior-point methods for convex and semidefinite optimization, set off the surge of interest; the survey names computational tractability as a primary motivation and goal. [practice] MIP solvers were good enough by then: the 2004 experiments solve a 200-item robust knapsack to optimality with CPLEX 6.0 (p. 44).
- **Key insight** (from the papers). Protect each constraint against at most Γ of its uncertain coefficients deviating; the robust counterpart stays a linear (or mixed-integer) program of moderately larger size and comes with probabilistic bounds on violation that do not depend on the solution. With cost uncertainty in a 0–1 problem on n variables, solving at most n + 1 nominal problems suffices, so polynomial solvability and α-approximability carry over (2003 abstract). The 2004 talk abstract states the conic version of the principle: robust conic problems "retain their original structure". [stated, primary]
- **Minimum evidence.** Robust knapsack: with a violation-probability bound of 0.57%, "we only reduce the objective by 1.54%" (p. 44). A portfolio example taken from Ben-Tal and Nemirovski. [practice, primary]
- **Abandoned paths and limits.** [stated, primary] On the NetLib problem PILOT4 the method helped little: relaxing full protection still raised cost sharply "unless one is willing to accept unrealistically high probabilities for constraint violation" (p. 48). The authors attribute this to having few uncertain coefficients per constraint. A homepage-only manuscript, "Robust Discrete Optimization under Ellipsoidal Uncertainty Sets", has no published version in the CV or DBLP (§3). The data-free assumptions (symmetric, bounded, independent deviations) were later replaced by sets built from risk measures (2009) and from data with hypothesis tests (2018). [practice; "replaced" inferred]
- **Reception.** Fast review: about 15 months (2004) and 9 months (2003). Rank 1 of all his work (7,185 Scholar, 4,091 Crossref citations). The CV lists the INFORMS Optimization Society Farkas Prize in 2008; the citation text was not read. MIT News (2025) credits him with developing "the concepts and tools of 'robust optimization'" [observed, secondary]. RO became his largest program (38 archive PDFs; textbook 2022; retrospective 2026).
- **Method shown.** Choose the model, here the uncertainty set, so that the robust problem keeps the complexity class of the nominal one; tune conservatism with one interpretable parameter; report the price paid in objective value. Cross-reference: §4 P2, P3.

### SW4. Best subset selection and optimal classification trees under a "modern optimization lens" (2014–2017) · ML, IO, HS

Bertsimas, King & Mazumder, "Best subset selection via a modern optimization lens", *Ann. Statist.* 44(2):813–852, 2016, DOI 10.1214/15-AOS1388, arXiv:1507.03133 (read in part); the 2014–2015 Philip McCord Morse Lecture paper (INFORMS, 11 Nov 2014; read in part); Bertsimas & Dunn, "Optimal classification trees", *Mach. Learn.* 106(7):1039–1082, 2017, DOI 10.1007/s10994-017-5633-9 (read in part). Precursors: CRIO (*Oper. Res.* 55(2):252–271, 2007, DOI 10.1287/opre.1060.0360; read in part); Bertsimas & Shioda, "Algorithm for cardinality-constrained quadratic optimization", *Comput. Optim. Appl.* 43(1):1–22, 2009, DOI 10.1007/s10589-007-9126-9 (not read).

- **Origin.** [stated, primary] His research page explains why statistics ignored integer optimization and adds "While such beliefs were accurate two decades ago" discrete optimization has since progressed a great deal. The 2016 paper says best subset selection had been "widely dismissed as being intractable by the greater statistical community" (p. 814). The OCT paper blames the lack of optimal-tree algorithms on "a failure to address correctly the underlying nature of the decision tree problem" (p. 1041). [practice] The idea is older than the program: CRIO, submitted in November 2002, opens with "Motivated by the significant advances in integer optimization in the past decade" (p. 252). Student line: R. Shioda (PhD 2003, integer programming for data mining), A. Chang (2012), A. King (2015, regression under a modern optimization lens), J. Dunn (2018, optimal trees); R. Mazumder was his postdoc in 2012–2013.
- **Why then.** [stated, primary] Solver and hardware speedups, quantified in each text, with numbers that change between them: "an astonishing 200 billion factor speedup" for 1990–2014 (Morse lecture, 2014); "an astonishing 450 billion factor speedup in solving MIO problems" for 1991–2015 (*Ann. Statist.*, p. 813); "an astonishing 800 billion factor speedup" over the last 25 years (OCT, p. 1039). The 2016 paper builds its number from Bixby's version-on-version CPLEX tests, Gurobi releases and Top500 hardware data (pp. 819–820). [practice] The 2014 Morse lecture was the platform; a doctoral class on statistics under this lens was announced for spring 2016 (research page).
- **Key insight** (from the papers). Pose the estimator exactly (cardinality-constrained least squares; a whole tree as one MIO), find good solutions with a discrete extension of first-order methods (or with CART) and feed them to the MIO solver as warm starts, so that the solver proves optimality or reports a gap even when stopped early. Tractability is redefined as the ability to "solve problems of practical interest in times that are appropriate for the application" (p. 817). [stated, primary]
- **Minimum evidence.** Best subset: provable optimality in minutes for n in the thousands and p in the hundreds; better sparsity and prediction than Lasso. OCT: a warm-started depth-2 tree on the Wine data (n = 178, p = 13) solved faster than without warm start; then 53 UCI data sets with 1–2% (axis-aligned) and 3–5% (hyperplane) average out-of-sample gains over CART. [practice, primary]
- **Abandoned paths and limits.** [stated, primary] For p in the thousands the method finds near-optimal solutions in minutes but "takes hours to provide certificates on the quality of the solution" (p. 818). The 2016 paper calls the 2009 tailored branch and bound with Shioda "less scalable" than the new method (p. 817): the problem-specific B&B was dropped for a generic MIO solver plus warm starts. CRIO's partition-into-polyhedral-regions method was not continued [inferred]. The OCT acknowledgments thank a colleague for "comments on our earlier MIO formulation": the formulation was revised before publication [stated]. A 2018 CV item claiming that trees with hyperplanes are as powerful as neural networks (with Mazumder and Sobiesk) is still listed as submitted in December 2025 (§3). [practice]
- **Reception.** *Ann. Statist.*: received June 2014, revised August 2015, published 2016 (two reviewers and an associate editor thanked). OCT: received September 2015, accepted March 2017 (three reviewers). Scholar 1,284 and 1,223. INFORMS gave him the Morse Lectureship (2013, lecture delivered 2014) and the 2021 Lanchester Prize for the 2019 book with Dunn [observed]. A 2019 company press release says the von Neumann Prize recognized his "optimal classification (OCT) and regression (ORT) trees" (Contradictions). The method was commercialized through Interpretable AI LLC (co-founded 2018, CV). *Statistical Science* 35(4) printed Bertsimas, Pauphilet & Van Parys (pp. 555–578, DOI 10.1214/19-STS701) next to Hastie, Tibshirani & Tibshirani, "Best Subset, Forward Stepwise or Lasso?" (pp. 579–592, DOI 10.1214/19-STS733); I did not read the latter, and the critique belongs to agent 05. [practice]
- **Method shown.** Exact formulation, heuristic warm start, certificate, out-of-sample benchmark against the incumbent heuristic, backed by a quantified "why now" (solver speedups). Cross-reference: §4 P1, P4.

### SW5. "From Predictive to Prescriptive Analytics" (2014–2020) · DA, ML, AP

Bertsimas & Kallus, *Manag. Sci.* 66(3):1025–1044, 2020, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2018.3253; arXiv:1402.5481 v1 (22 Feb 2014), v2 (26 Jun 2014, withdrawn), v3 (9 Feb 2015), v4 (19 Jul 2018). Abstract pages of all four versions read; the full text was not read.

- **Origin.** [stated, primary] The v1 abstract (titled "From Predictions to Data-Driven Decisions Using Machine Learning") says it is unclear how to turn expected-value predictions into decisions with high profit and low risk, and cites portfolio allocation and inventory management. The v3 abstract adds that the setting reflects "our practical experience with the data available in applications of OR/MS": the data are mainly auxiliary observations (covariates), not the demand or returns themselves. [practice] N. Kallus, PhD 2015 ("From data to decisions through new interfaces between optimization and statistics"), submitted v1.
- **Why then.** [stated] v1 points to the recent growth in the availability of data and to flexible ML methods. [practice] He gave plenaries on predictive to prescriptive analytics in 2015 and 2016 (CV; UNC video, 2016).
- **Key insight** (v3–v4 abstracts). Treat the decision as a conditional stochastic optimization problem and weight past observations with ML methods (local regression, CART, random forests); the resulting prescriptions are tractable and asymptotically optimal, also for non-iid and censored data, and v4 extends them to decisions that affect the uncertainty (pricing). A coefficient of prescriptiveness P plays the role of R² for decisions.
- **Minimum evidence.** v1: a portfolio example. From v3: inventory for the distribution arm of a media company (about one billion units a year), using internal data and data harvested from IMDb, Rotten Tomatoes and Google; the data account for an 88% improvement measured by P. [stated, primary]
- **Abandoned paths and retracted claims.** [stated, primary] v2 carries the comment "Withdrawn due to crucial error in Theorem 2.2". v1 had claimed that the prescriptions "converge to the omniscient optimum for almost all realizations of data", even for non-iid data; from v3 the claim reads asymptotic optimality "under mild conditions". The title, the portfolio framing and the general ML-refitting pitch of v1 were dropped; covariates, censoring, the P metric and a real case were added. [practice; the narrowing is my reading of the wording]
- **Reception.** About six years from first preprint to journal (2014 → 2020). Scholar 1,626 citations, his most-cited paper among those first posted in 2014 or later. It seeded a line of his own: prescriptive trees (2019), observational data (NeurIPS 2018), the voice of optimization (2021), bootstrap robust prescriptive analytics (2022), side information (2023), prescribe-then-select (2025). Applied in the COVID-19 work that won the 2020 Pierskalla Award ("From predictions to prescriptions: a data-driven response to COVID-19", DOI 10.1007/s10729-020-09542-0). [practice; award observed]
- **Method shown.** Decisions, not predictions, are the object; borrow the ML estimator but evaluate it by decision quality; validate on a real partner's data. Cross-reference: §4 P1, P6.
