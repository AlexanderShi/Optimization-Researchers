### S058 · Data-driven estimation in equilibrium using inverse optimization (Mathematical Programming 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10107-014-0819-4; arXiv 1308.3397)
Read level: **full** (pp. 1–36 of the arXiv v2 preprint: body pp. 1–29 and Appendices A–C pp. 31–36 read; reference list pp. 29–31 skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vishal Gupta (MIT Operations Research Center), Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis (Boston University) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: data-driven estimation from his robust-optimization years, written with two members of his student lineages (Gupta in RO, Paschalidis in SD, per SKILL.md's Academic Lineage). Its core device is his own Aghassi–Bertsimas–Perakis 2006 reformulation of variational inequalities (VIs) as convex programs (ref. [2], p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + CP + ML; AP (traffic, pricing) as testbeds}: The entry is a modelling gap. Equilibrium models (Nash, Wardrop, market) need primitives such as utilities or congestion functions, which cannot be observed, while the equilibria themselves can be. Small errors in the primitives matter most when the model is used to redesign a system (Braess's paradox, p. 2). The problem is posed as an inverse variational inequality: given observations believed to be approximate equilibria, find the function f that defines the VI (problem (11), p. 9). The authors claim to be the first to pose it (p. 3). The incumbent is structural estimation: a parametric model with probabilistic assumptions, structural equations and a GMM fit that is often non-convex with flat regions and many local optima (p. 3). BLP-type demand estimation also assumes interior equilibria (p. 11).
  - Transferable: when a field estimates primitives from data it assumes optimal or in equilibrium, check whether its estimators need interior or unique solutions. A dual characterization that needs neither is a way in (Remarks 1–2, p. 10).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Theorem 2, taken from ref. [2], says x̂ is an ε-approximate equilibrium of VI(f, A, b, C) if and only if some y satisfies dual feasibility Aᵀy ≤_C f(x̂) and the duality-gap bound f(x̂)ᵀx̂ − bᵀy ≤ ε (pp. 8–9). Imposing this for every observation, with one common f, gives a single minimization over (θ, y, ε) (Theorem 3, p. 10). Because the observations enter as data, the problem class depends only on how f depends on θ. With f linear in θ over arbitrary nonlinear basis functions, the problem is an LP for the nonnegative orthant and an SOCP for the second-order cone (Remark 3, p. 10). The nonparametric version picks the minimum-RKHS-norm f among the functions that approximately reconcile the data (p. 15). A representer theorem, extended to the constrained multivariate case, reduces this to a convex QP whose size grows with N, not with the dimension of the RKHS (Theorems 4–5, pp. 15–16). From inverse optimization the paper keeps the duality device (pp. 2–3). It changes the target from optima to equilibria, and the goal from a claim about the true mechanism to predictive power (p. 3). The paper states the trade-off itself: the parametric form gives control over f at a possible cost in tractability, while the nonparametric form is always convex (p. 17).
  - Transferable: make the unknown enter linearly (θ over basis functions), so the cone fixes the inverse problem's class whatever the nonlinearity in x (p. 10).
- **D3 Model and data**: VIs over conic-representable sets F = {x : Ax = b, x ∈ C}, with a Slater condition for each observation (Assumptions 1–2, pp. 8–9); Θ compact and f continuous in θ (p. 10). The guarantees add four assumptions: i.i.d. observations (A3), Slater almost surely (A4), x̃ ∈ F̃ almost surely (A5) and F̃ inside a ball of radius R (A6) (pp. 20, 22). The prediction bounds also need a strongly monotone fitted f (Theorem 8, p. 23). Admitted limits: the method covers continuous problems only (p. 4), and the representer argument fails when the VI involves partial derivatives of the unknown, as in the demand example (Remark 4, p. 19). All data are synthetic. The first test case is a Bertrand–Nash duopoly with known demand: N = 250 with full information (p. 24), then N = 40 with unobserved firm-specific shocks, a noisy proxy and a misspecified class (nine exponential terms in each price plus linear terms, Eq. 33) (p. 26). The second is the Sioux Falls network (24 nodes, 76 arcs), with demands perturbed by up to 10%, equilibrium flows from the BPR function, and those flows perturbed by up to 10% (N = 40, pp. 27–28). In §8.1 the fitted functions are normalized to equal the true function at one point, to make the visual comparison easier (App. C.1, p. 35).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: (i) Strong conic duality gives the characterization; a complementary-slackness variant (Eq. 10) is used in §8.1 (p. 9). (ii) The observations are data, so the parametrization in θ alone sets the complexity (Remark 3, p. 10). (iii) The paper prints the boundary: linear demand gives an LP (Eq. 15), multinomial-logit demand a non-convex problem (pp. 11–12). (iv) The representer theorem is proved by orthogonal projection onto span{k_xj}, one coordinate at a time (Theorem 4; App. A.1, p. 31). It holds for any problem that depends only on norms and point evaluations and has an objective nondecreasing in the norms (§6, p. 18). (v) The norm constraint on ε is dualized into a penalty λ‖ε‖ to remove infeasibility (Eq. 25, p. 16). (vi) Ambiguity-set envelopes are computed by LPs in (α, y) (§6.2, p. 19). (vii) Generalization comes in two forms. The ℓ∞ parametric fit is a randomized uncertain convex program, which gives a binomial-tail confidence β(α) that decays exponentially in N (Theorem 6, p. 20; Remark 6, p. 21; App. A.3, p. 32). A Rademacher bound adds an O(1/√N) term for both parametric and kernel fits, using a duality-based uniform bound B on the residual (Theorem 7, p. 22; Lemmas 1–3, pp. 32–33). (viii) Prediction error follows from Pang's error bound under strong monotonicity (Theorem 1, p. 8; Theorem 8, p. 23). (ix) For scale, the kernel matrix K is approximately low-rank, and for fixed α the network problem splits into shortest-path subproblems (pp. 17–18). Appendix B recasts structural estimation as an inverse VI and shows that it carries bilinear non-convex terms, which problem (12) avoids (pp. 34–35).
  - Transferable: to prove generalization for a fitted optimization model, write the fit as a randomized uncertain convex program for ℓ∞ residuals, or bound the residual uniformly by duality and apply Rademacher complexity (pp. 20–22, 32–33).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: No solver, version, machine or run time is reported anywhere in the file (checked by search). §8.1: the correctly specified parametric fit recovers the truth exactly, which the authors call unsurprising (p. 24). A Gaussian-kernel fit tuned by 10-fold cross-validation (CV) also reconciles all the data exactly yet differs from the truth, and the ambiguity set shows how wide the band of such functions is (Fig. 1, p. 25). §8.2: ℓ∞ residuals with ℓ1 regularization. λ is tuned by CV on one half of the data, and θ is refit on the other half because CV voids Theorem 6 (p. 26). The text gives λ = .01 for the tuned value and λ = .1 for the refit, then shows the λ = .01 fit in Figure 2 (p. 26). On 200 new points the theorem allows a violation probability of at most .21 (at 90% confidence); .025 is observed, and the mean prediction error is (−.002, 0.02) (p. 26). The kernel fit is not monotone, so its equilibria are not unique and no prediction error is reported for it (p. 27). §8.3: polynomial kernels up to degree 6 with 5-fold CV. The degree-3 fit tracks the truth (BPR is degree 4) and is stable across degrees (Fig. 4, p. 28). On 500 new points the mean relative approximation error is 6.5% and the relative prediction error about 5.5% (p. 28). For the same quantity the paper reports the theorem's bound (9.73×10⁵), a CV estimate (6.24×10⁵) and a held-out estimate (7.78×10⁵) (pp. 28–29). No competing estimator is run; structural estimation is compared only analytically (App. B, pp. 34–35).
  - Transferable: report the theorem's bound, a cross-validation estimate and a held-out estimate side by side for the same quantity (pp. 26, 28–29).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper extends inverse optimization from optima to equilibria (Heuberger's survey for combinatorial problems, Ahuja–Orlin for LP, Iyengar–Kang for conic programs; p. 3) and claims priority for inverse VIs (p. 3). Structural estimation (Rust; Bajari–Benkard–Levin; Allon–Federgruen–Pierson; Nevo) is called complementary, not wrong: where its assumptions hold it may support stronger claims (p. 4). Appendix B shows that, with the same orthogonality constraints and interior equilibria, the inverse-VI form reproduces the traditional estimators (pp. 34–35). The paper frames the choice with Breiman's two cultures and sides with algorithmic modelling (p. 3). The core device is a self-citation (Aghassi–Bertsimas–Perakis 2006, p. 8). The learning theory is borrowed from Campi–Garatti, Campi–Carè, Bartlett–Mendelson, Wahba and Girosi et al. (pp. 12, 20–21). Keshavarz–Wang–Boyd, *Imputing a convex objective function*, is in the reference list (ref. 29, p. 30) but is not cited in the text of this version.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The priority claim and the predictive philosophy come before the contributions (p. 3). Five numbered contributions follow (pp. 4–5). Numbered remarks advertise strengths: multiple equilibria, boundary equilibria and complexity (p. 10). The experimental insights are listed before the experiments (pp. 23–24). The experiments are reported candidly: the recovery is called unsurprising (p. 24), CV is admitted to void Theorem 6 (p. 26), and non-monotonicity is called a potential weakness (p. 27). The theorems carry intuitive remarks (Remarks 5 and 8; pp. 20, 22). The conclusion is one paragraph with no limits or future work (p. 29). Slips in this version: the running head misspells the first author as Berstimas (even pages from p. 2); the text calls the nonparametric fit a red line with circles, while Fig. 1's caption gives it a black line with square markers (pp. 25–26); and the λ values above (p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions at four levels: a new problem class (the inverse VI), tractable reformulations (conic programs and QPs), statistical guarantees, and synthetic demonstrations (pp. 4–5, 29). Limits stated: continuous problems only (p. 4); Theorem 6's proof does not extend to other norms (p. 21); Rademacher bounds are loose in small samples, so CV or the bootstrap is recommended (p. 23); the non-identifiability persists as N grows (p. 25); kernel fits can be non-monotone (p. 27); the partial-derivative case is left as an open area of research (Remark 4, p. 19), the only future direction given. Not stated: no real data, no run times, no estimator baseline.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): a problem owned by econometrics, estimating equilibrium primitives, is written exactly as a conic program through a duality device. The class is kept: LP or SOCP when f is linear in θ, a convex QP for kernels (pp. 8–10, 15–16). The boundary is printed: logit demand is non-convex (p. 12), partial derivatives break the representer theorem (p. 19), and the structural formulation has bilinear terms (App. B, pp. 34–35). The method's dualization step drives the whole paper: the dual variables y certify approximate equilibrium.
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): data-driven, and motivated by downstream design decisions (p. 2), but judged only by approximation and prediction error on synthetic data. There is no decision owner, current rule or decision metric (pp. 23–29).
- Tension T3 (evidence; a tension, not a method): the paper is sold on minimal probabilistic assumptions (p. 3), yet every guarantee rests on i.i.d. sampling (Assumption 3, p. 20) and probabilistic learning theory (pp. 20–23).
- New-pattern candidate: *duality-gap characterization turns an inverse problem into one convex program*, with the observations as data (pp. 8–10).
- New-pattern candidate: *Rashomon envelopes*: compute the upper and lower envelopes of all models that fit the data equally well, to block unwarranted claims (§6.2, p. 19; p. 25).
- New-pattern candidate: *predict, do not identify*: fit for predictive power with no claim to the true mechanism, and call the structural school complementary (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Characterize approximate optimality or approximate equilibrium by dual feasibility plus a duality gap ε_j per observation, then minimize ‖ε‖ over the unknown parameters (Theorems 2–3, pp. 8–10).
- Parametrize the unknown map linearly in θ over nonlinear basis functions, so the inverse problem is an LP or SOCP (Remark 3, p. 10).
- For the nonparametric version, fit by minimum RKHS norm with a normalization constraint and reduce it by a representer theorem to a convex QP in n·N coefficients. Dualize the residual-norm constraint into a penalty to avoid infeasibility (pp. 15–16).
- Before trusting a fitted parametric form, compute the envelope of all kernel functions within κ of the best fit (one LP per evaluation point). Set κ from a generalization bound or as two CV standard deviations (pp. 19, 22–23, 27, 29).
- Tune hyperparameters by CV on half the sample and refit on the other half, so a theorem that forbids data snooping still applies (p. 26).
- On networks, exploit the approximately low-rank kernel matrix and the split into shortest-path subproblems for fixed coefficients (pp. 17–18).

**Quotes**:
- "We make no claim that this fitted model accurately reflects “reality,” merely that it has good predictive power." (p. 3)
- "Calculating nonparametric sets of plausible alternatives helps guard against these types of unwarranted claims." (p. 25)

### S119 · Robust queueing theory (Operations Research 2015, DOI 10.1287/opre.2015.1367)
Read level: **full** (pp. 1–56 of the submitted manuscript: body pp. 1–53 read in full, including all proofs; reference list pp. 53–56 skimmed). The appendix cited on pp. 14 and 25 for the tightness details is not in the file and was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Chaithanya Bandi, Nataly Youssef (both MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a return to his 1985–1995 queueing roots with the 2000s robust-optimization toolkit; it cites his own 1990 G/G/s analysis as evidence of what the probabilistic route costs (p. 2). Part of a Bandi–Bertsimas programme to analyse stochastic systems (market design, information theory, finance) by robust optimization (p. 4), following Bertsimas–Gamarnik–Rikun 2011 (p. 4). A companion paper on transient analysis was submitted in 2014 (pp. 53–54). This is the robust-queueing anchor of the SKILL's SD row.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + RO}: The entry is a long-standing intractability. Beyond exponential assumptions the GI/GI/m queue is, in the authors' words, by and large intractable (p. 2). Pollaczek's method works in a multi-dimensional complex plane; Bertsimas's own 1990 method needs a linear system of dimension 4.5 million for p = 5 phases (p. 2). Networks lose Burke's theorem once a queue is not M/M/m. What remains is simulation, which is slow for heavy tails in heavy traffic, and approximations such as QNA and QNET, which do not model heavy tails (pp. 2–3). Kingman's centennial question, why insist on treating arrivals as random variables, is the authority for dropping distributions (p. 3). The problem is stated as: model inter-arrival and service times by uncertainty sets, analyse the worst-case system time, and build a network calculus on it (pp. 3–4, 6).
  - Transferable: frame a paradigm switch with the incumbent field's most senior statement of doubt, then show the incumbent's cost with your own earlier work (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Make the limit laws the primitives. The CLT (light tails) and the generalized CLT (tail index 1 < α ≤ 2) suggest bounding partial sums from below for inter-arrival times and from above for service times, by (n − k)/λ − Γ(n − k)^{1/α} and (n − k)/µ + Γ(n − k)^{1/α}, with Γ a variability parameter chosen like a quantile (pp. 7–9). Assumption 1 turns this into polyhedral sets U^a and U^s, and for m servers into U^s_m over jobs drawn from different blocks of m (pp. 9–10). The worst case of Lindley's recursion (one server) or Krivulin's recursion (m servers) then reduces to a one-dimensional problem with a closed-form upper bound (Theorems 2–3, pp. 13, 25). For networks, three closure results let each queue be analysed alone. With adversarial servers the departures lie in the arrival set, which the paper calls akin to Burke's theorem (Theorem 4, p. 28). Superposition and thinning stay in the family, with explicit maps for (λ, Γ, α) (Theorems 5–6, pp. 36–39). The effective network parameters solve a linear system (Theorem 7, pp. 40–42). Finally, Γ is calibrated by regression so that the worst case approximates the simulated mean (RQNA, pp. 45–49). Kept from the incumbents: the classical recursions, Kingman's functional form and QNA's decomposition (p. 45). Changed: distributions are replaced by sets, and expectations by worst cases.
  - Transferable: when a performance measure is a maximum of partial sums, put the uncertainty on the partial sums with the CLT scaling, not on the individual variables (pp. 7–10).
- **D3 Model and data**: Single-class FCFS queues with m servers and ρ = λ/(mµ) < 1 (p. 11). The data primitives are (λ, Γa, αa) and (µ, Γs, αs), with symmetric tails in §§3–5 and asymmetric tails in §6 (pp. 11, 42). The sets do not imply independence, and they imply bounded service times (large but bounded with the right Γs) (p. 10). The departure analysis assumes adversarial servers that maximize the system time (pp. 26, 36). Thinning assumes a rational fraction f realized by a deterministic round-robin split, which the authors say approximates probabilistic routing (pp. 38–39). The routing matrix is sub-stochastic (p. 42). The data come from simulated queues with normal, truncated Pareto, Erlang and exponential distributions, with heavy tails truncated to get finite moments and n = 30,000 arrivals per run (pp. 45, 47, 51). The networks are Kuehn's nine-queue network (pp. 49–50) and randomly generated networks of 10–30 nodes with 0–70% feedback, ρ from 0.5 to 0.95 and one to four types of arrival distribution (pp. 50–53).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: (i) Exchanging the order of maximization gives an upper bound, and a feasible worst-case path with nondecreasing service times, built by backward substitution, shows that it is attained (Proposition 1, p. 12). (ii) The worst case becomes a one-dimensional maximization over the index k (Eqs. 12 and 45; pp. 13, 24). (iii) The closed form comes from relaxing the index to a real number and solving max βx^{1/α} − δx (Eq. 15, p. 13). The tightness discussion: the error Δ → 0 as ρ → 1, and the unconstrained maximizer is inside the range for large n (p. 14); the details are deferred to the missing appendix. (iv) Overtaking in multi-server queues is handled by a restricted no-overtaking policy class, an explicit recursion, and an induction showing that the FCFS worst case equals the restricted one (Propositions 2–4, pp. 15–24). (v) Departures are bounded by a two-case argument on a concave function g (one server) or h (m servers) (pp. 28–33). (vi) Superposition reduces to a one-dimensional concave maximization over the share x of arrivals, plus induction (Eq. 75, pp. 37–38). Thinning splits a stream into q round-robin streams and recombines p of them (pp. 39–40). (vii) A power substitution x_j = (λ̄_j Γa,j)^{α/(α−1)} makes the network fixed point linear (pp. 41–42). The §6 proofs are omitted as straightforward generalizations (p. 42), and the Theorem 8 bound is called not tight (p. 42).
  - Transferable: when the exact analysis of a policy is hard (overtaking), solve a restricted policy class exactly and prove that the worst case over the full class equals it (pp. 15–24).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: First, a calibration step: Γa = σa and Γs = f(ρ, σa, σs, α), a Kingman-motivated form whose θ are fitted by linear regression on simulated single queues, in a service-dependent and a service-independent version (Table 1, pp. 45–46). This is done before any network instance is seen (pp. 45–46). Single queues: errors within about 9.5% of simulation (Tables 2–3, p. 47). On Kuehn's network with single servers, QNA's errors range from 5.84% to 23.71% in absolute value, against 6.74–9.28% for RQNA with service-independent calibration and 0.68–3.88% with service-dependent calibration (Table 4, p. 50). The text speaks of improvements of generally one order of magnitude (p. 49). In one cell QNA beats service-independent RQNA (normal service, (c²a, c²s) = (1, 1): 5.84% against 7.12%), and the text does not remark on it (Table 4, p. 50). QNA appears only in Table 4. Multi-server networks: up to 10.91% with service-independent calibration (10 servers, Pareto) and up to 6.13% with service-dependent calibration (Tables 5–6, p. 51). Random networks: 2.86–6.19% by size and feedback, and 2.72–8.64% by traffic intensity and diversity of arrival distributions (Tables 7–10, pp. 52–53). The metric is the percent error of the expected system time against simulation. Not reported: run times of RQNA or of the simulations (although slow simulation is part of the motivation, pp. 2–3), simulation confidence intervals, the number of random networks per cell, and QNA's implementation. The headline numbers drift: errors in single digits for all experiments (abstract p. 1; conclusion p. 53), often 4–6% and at most 8% (p. 5), within 8.7% (p. 43), below 8.5% (p. 52). Meanwhile Table 5 contains 10.91% (p. 51) and Table 10 contains 8.64% (p. 53).
  - Transferable: calibrate a worst-case model's parameters on isolated components (single queues) and validate it on systems that were never used in the calibration (networks) (pp. 45–53).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper opens with Erlang 1909 and the renewal-process tradition (p. 1), then Pollaczek, Burke and Jackson (p. 2), QNA (Whitt 1983) and QNET (Dai–Harrison) (p. 3). Its closest relatives are distinguished on three counts (assumptions, tighter single-server bounds, generality to multi-server queues and feedback): deterministic network calculus (Cruz), stochastic network calculus (whose primitives are probabilistic), and adversarial queueing theory, which addresses stability only and so overlaps only philosophically (pp. 4–6). Critics of network calculus (Ciucu–Hohlfeld; Jiang) are quoted to make the case (p. 5). Self-citations place the programme: Bertsimas 1990 (p. 2), Bertsimas–Gamarnik–Rikun 2011 (p. 4), four Bandi–Bertsimas papers (p. 4), the RO surveys by Ben-Tal et al. and Bertsimas–Brown–Caramanis (p. 3), and Bertsimas–Gupta–Kallus for building sets from data (p. 48). The benchmark chosen is QNA, the incumbent approximation (p. 49).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract opens with the proposal as an alternative approach and closes with error and insensitivity claims (p. 1). There is a long historical introduction, an authority quote from Kingman (p. 3), and "in our opinion" on RO as the natural framework (p. 3). The contributions are listed (a)–(f) by section (p. 6). Sections 3 and 4 end with "Implications and Insights", and Theorems 7 and 10 are followed by lists of insights (pp. 25, 33, 41, 44). The introduction says the framework allows "exact performance analysis of the underlying stochastic system" (p. 4), yet the stochastic networks are matched only after regression calibration (pp. 45–47). The headline numbers vary across abstract, introduction, body and conclusion (see D5). Hedges sit in the body: somewhat sensitive to the diversity of arrival distributions (p. 1); a bound that is not tight (p. 42).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions at five levels: a modelling paradigm (sets from limit laws), closed-form theory (single- and multi-server queues, heavy tails), a network calculus (the Burke-like departure theorem, superposition, thinning), an algorithm (RQNA) and a computational validation (pp. 6, 52–53). Limits stated: the closed forms are nearly tight only in heavy traffic (p. 14); the asymmetric-tail bound is not tight (p. 42) and the superposition bound may not be tight (p. 43); departures need adversarial servers (p. 36); the method is sensitive to diverse arrival distributions (pp. 1, 53); heavy tails are truncated in the simulations (p. 45). Future work: transient analysis (companion paper) and priority disciplines (pp. 6, 53). Not stated: every numerical result depends on the simulation-fitted θ, and there is no run-time comparison.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): a problem owned by probabilistic queueing theory is rewritten as worst-case optimization over polyhedral sets (pp. 9–11), and tractability is kept. But it is kept as closed forms, one-dimensional problems and a linear system rather than a solver class (pp. 13, 25, 41–42). There is no dual reading, and the boundary is printed only in part (bounds that are not tight, pp. 42–43).
- Method 3 (variant ⚠): Γa and Γs are the knobs that limit the adversary and set the degree of robustness (p. 11). They are not priced for a decision-maker; they are fitted by regression so that the worst case matches simulated means (pp. 45–47). This is the knob set from data, as in the SKILL's correction.
- Method 2 (variant ⚠): the "why now" is qualitative. Commercial codes solve large structured LP, MIP and QP problems, and RO is a fast-growing area (p. 3). There is no speed-up figure, and the results need no solver at all except for Fig. 1 (p. 34).
- Method 4 (variant ⚠): the incumbent approximation (QNA) is the opponent and is beaten on the one network where it is run (Table 4, p. 50). There is no exact solve or certificate, and QNA is absent from the multi-server and random-network tables (pp. 51–53).
- Tension T3 (evidence; a tension, not a method): probability is replaced as the primitive, yet it supplies the sets (CLT and generalized CLT, pp. 7–9) and, through simulation regression, the parameters (pp. 45–47).
- Tension T6 (evidence): a single-digit headline (pp. 1, 53) against a 10.91% table entry (p. 51).
- New-pattern candidate: *limit laws as uncertainty sets* (pp. 7–10).
- New-pattern candidate: *closure calculus*: prove that the system's operations (departure, superposition, thinning) map the set family to itself, then decompose the network (pp. 26–42).
- New-pattern candidate: *worst-case-to-mean calibration* by regression on simulated components (pp. 45–47).
- New-pattern candidate: *recover the incumbent theory's qualitative law first* (Kingman's bound) as validation of the new paradigm (pp. 25–26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Bound partial sums, not individual variables, with the CLT or generalized-CLT scaling (n − k)^{1/α} and a quantile-like Γ (pp. 7–10).
- Exchange max and sup to get an upper bound, then construct a feasible extremal path (here nondecreasing service times by backward substitution) to show that the bound is attained (p. 12).
- Relax a discrete index to a continuous one to get closed forms, and state where the relaxation is tight (heavy traffic, large n) (pp. 13–14).
- Solve a restricted policy class exactly (no overtaking) and prove that the worst case over all FCFS behaviour equals it (pp. 15–24).
- Reduce coupled nonlinear network equations to a linear system by a power substitution (pp. 41–42).
- Check a new model first against the incumbent theory's qualitative law: the same dependence on λ/(1 − ρ), with Γ²/m in place of σ²/m (pp. 25–26).
- Report errors along each axis a sceptic would vary: number of servers, network size, feedback, traffic intensity, diversity of distributions (pp. 50–53).

**Quotes**:
- "The key idea of our approach is to make the limit laws of probability theory the primitive assumptions" (p. 3)

### S124 · The power of optimization over randomization in designing experiments involving small samples (Operations Research 2015, DOI 10.1287/opre.2015.1361)
Read level: **full** (file pp. 1–10 = the INFORMS cover sheet plus Articles-in-Advance pp. 1–9, including endnotes, references and author biographies). The text layer garbles decimal points, Greek letters and Figure 6; Figure 6's values are not readable.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Mac Johnson (Vertex Pharmaceuticals; the paper grew from his EMBA independent study at MIT Sloan) and Nathan Kallus (PhD candidate, MIT Operations Research Center) (pp. 2, 10) · **Position in trajectory**: an early piece of his statistics-under-optimization line: received November 2013, accepted January 2015 (p. 2). For the general theory it points to Kallus's single-author arXiv paper on optimal a priori balance (pp. 5, 10). The SKILL already cites it by DOI for Research Taste mark 3 and the anti-pattern "Randomization in small samples"; this card supplies the full-text evidence.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + ML (statistics: design of experiments); AP (preclinical oncology)}: The entry is a practitioner's problem. Mouse cancer models use groups of 8–10 (typically 40–60 mice in four to six groups), and the coefficient of variation of pre-treatment tumour size is often 50% or more (p. 2). Randomization has been the standard for close to a century. Yet with four groups of ten it leaves an average maximal discrepancy of 0.66 SD between some two groups, and still 0.47 SD with twenty per group. Getting below 0.1 SD needs more than 400 subjects per group, and below 0.01 SD more than 40,000 (p. 3). The stated problem: compose the groups so as to minimize the maximum pairwise difference in the mean and variance of baseline covariates (pp. 2, 4). Block designs do not handle continuous covariates, and pair matching finds few good pairs in small samples (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: open with the incumbent's failure in the user's own units (SD of discrepancy; subjects needed per group) on the practitioner's typical experiment (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Random assignment is replaced by an exact MILP that minimizes the worst pairwise discrepancy in normalized means plus ρ times the discrepancy in second moments, with four linear constraints per pair. Which group receives which treatment is then randomized (p. 4). Randomizing the labels keeps the treatment-effect estimate unbiased, with no omitted-variable bias (p. 7). It also restores the symmetry removed by the constraint x_ip = 0 for i < p (p. 4). The method extends to r covariates by whitening with the inverse square root of the sample covariance (pp. 4–5). An optimized assignment is not random enough for randomization tests, so the paper proposes a bootstrap test that re-solves the assignment on each resample (p. 8). Kept: unbiasedness through randomization (of the labels only) and the potential-outcomes frame (endnote 3, p. 9). Changed: optimization, not chance, decides the partition.
  - Transferable: optimize the structure, randomize the labels. Keep the guarantee the old method gave (unbiasedness) by randomizing only the part of the decision the objective is symmetric in (pp. 4, 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: n = mk subjects with pre-treatment covariates normalized to zero mean and unit variance (p. 4). ρ is chosen by the researcher (p. 4) and fixed arbitrarily for all later examples (p. 5). Higher moments are not in the objective (p. 6). Potential outcomes under the stable-unit-treatment-value assumption (endnote 3, p. 9). All data are simulated. The cases are normal covariates (Figs. 1, 3, 4; Table 2), uniform covariates for the pair-matching argument (p. 6), and three-dimensional standard normal covariates (Table 3, p. 7). The last case is a murine tumour study: initial weights normal (mean 200 mg, SD 300 mg, truncated at zero), Gomp-ex growth over one day (Wheldon 1988) with chosen rate parameters, and treatment effects of 0, 50 and 250 mg (p. 8). No real experiment is reported.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: (i) After symmetry breaking the MILP has m(1 + 2n − m)/2 binaries and one continuous variable. Further reduction by orbitopal fixing is noted, and Gurobi's symmetry detection is used (p. 4). (ii) The multivariate MILP uses matrices M and V of pairwise moment differences (pp. 4–5). (iii) The theory is borrowed from the probabilistic analysis of combinatorial optimization. With ρ = 0 and m = 2 the problem is balanced number partitioning, whose optimum is exponentially small in k (Karmarkar–Karp; Karmarkar et al. 1986; Mertens's spin-glass prediction). Randomization's expected discrepancy decays like 1/√k (by Jensen's inequality; exactly so for normal covariates) (p. 6). (iv) Pair matching on uniform covariates decays only like k^{−3/2} or k^{−1} (p. 6). (v) Unbiasedness follows from randomizing the treatment labels (p. 7). (vi) Under Morgan–Rubin's additive model (their Theorem 3.2) the variance reduction is exponentially better than re-randomization's, but the paper calls that model very restrictive (p. 8). (vii) The bootstrap test, steps 1–5 (p. 8). No new theorem is proved.
  - Transferable: to argue for an exact method against randomization, map a special case onto a combinatorial problem with a known probabilistic analysis and compare the asymptotic orders (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi v5.6 on a personal computer with 8 cores. The instance with n = 40, m = 4 is solved to full optimality in under twenty seconds, and the bound and incumbent are plotted (Fig. 2, p. 4). Larger instances reach near-optimal solutions within a few minutes, but proving optimality takes longer (p. 4). No gaps are reported for the simulation instances. The opponents are complete randomization, Mahalanobis pairwise matching, re-randomization (Morgan–Rubin, 5% acceptance) and Morris's finite selection model (FSM) (p. 6). At δ = 0.1 SD: 3 for optimization, 128 for randomization, 9 for pairwise matching, 4 for re-randomization. At δ = 0.0001: 8 for optimization against about 4.4 thousand (pairwise), 0.82 million (re-randomization) and 1.27×10⁸ (randomization) (p. 6).   - Transferable: score a design by downstream inference, not only by balance: the spread of effect estimates and the rejection rates at zero, small and large effects under a mechanistic simulator (p. 8).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper argues against the statistical canon on its own ground: Fisher's block and orthogonal designs (categorical covariates only), pairwise matching (Rosenbaum–Rubin; Greevy et al.), re-randomization (Morgan–Rubin 2012) and Morris's FSM (pp. 2–3). Re-randomization is recast as a principled version of the informal practice of cherry-picking among several randomizations (endnote 1, p. 9), and Morgan–Rubin's additive model is called very restrictive (p. 8). The theory comes from the probabilistic analysis of combinatorial optimization (Karmarkar–Karp; Karmarkar et al.; Mertens) (p. 6), and the symmetry handling from orbitopal fixing (Kaibel et al.) (p. 4). The general theory is left to Kallus (2014), where optimization, randomization and pair matching are all read as minimizing worst-case variance (p. 5). The 17-item reference list (p. 10) contains no work on covariate-adaptive allocation (minimization) in clinical trials.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The title states the thesis as a slogan, and the abstract claims orders of magnitude and exponentially lower discrepancy (p. 2). One running example, four groups of ten mice, shows the problem (p. 3) and the fix (pp. 5–6). Strong verdicts (optimization starkly superior in matching means in all circumstances tested, p. 5; the cost-saving benefits called clear, p. 8) sit next to admitted arbitrary choices of ρ and of the growth parameters (pp. 5, 8). The caveats live in endnotes (endnotes 2 and 5, p. 9). The closing section, Practical Significance, extends the claim to fields from neurobiology to investment analysis and market research, and says the method can be implemented even in Microsoft Excel (p. 9). The paper has nine printed pages and no theorem-proof structure.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions at three levels: a formulation (an MILP for balanced group assignment), evidence (borrowed asymptotic theory plus simulation) and an inference procedure (the bootstrap test) (pp. 4–9). Limits stated: optimality proofs are slow for larger instances (p. 4); higher moments and interactions are not optimized, and treatment effects may depend on them (pp. 6, 8); the gains depend on the nature of the treatment effect (p. 8); the test may be conservative (endnote 5, p. 9). Not stated: there is no real experiment or field use, no guidance on choosing ρ, and no certification status for the solutions behind the tables. There is no future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): a design problem owned by statistics, settled by randomization since Fisher, is written exactly as an MILP and extended to vectors of covariates (pp. 4–5). The boundary is printed only qualitatively: optimality proofs are slow for larger instances (p. 4).
- Method 4 (evidence ✅, with ⚠): one joint MIO replaces an unoptimized practice. Solver, version, machine and time are stated (Gurobi v5.6, 8 cores, under 20 s for n = 40, m = 4; p. 4), and the incumbents are dueled on synthetic data with known truth (pp. 5–9). Deviations from the method: no warm start, and no gaps reported for larger instances (p. 4). Where the method wins is means and variances, not higher moments (Tables 2–3, p. 7; compare the summary on p. 9).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅): the owner and the problem come from practice (a Vertex scientist, p. 10; mouse oncology, p. 2). The current rule (randomization) is the baseline, and the score is the decision-relevant one: power to detect a drug effect, with missed rejections framed as squandered experiments (p. 8). There is no field test (p. 8).
- Method 3 (variant ⚠): ρ is the knob between first- and second-moment balance. The achievable range is shown as ρ varies (Fig. 4, p. 5), and then ρ is fixed arbitrarily (p. 5). This matches the SKILL's correction that knobs get fixed arbitrarily.
- Heuristic 1 (pointer): the cited Kallus (2014) reads randomization, pair matching and optimization as solutions of one worst-case-variance problem (p. 5).
- Tension T3 (evidence; a tension, not a method): the case for optimization rests on probabilistic analysis and a bootstrap test (pp. 6, 8).
- New-pattern candidate: *optimize the partition, randomize the labels* (pp. 4, 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *re-solve the optimization inside the bootstrap* to test an optimized design (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: *verifiability as an argument for optimization*: a reproducible optimum can be checked, a random draw cannot (p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: *price the gap with the probabilistic analysis of the underlying combinatorial problem* (number partitioning against 1/√k) (p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- MILP for balanced assignment: minimize d subject to d ≥ ±(μ_p − μ_q) ± ρ(σ²_p − σ²_q) for every pair of groups, assignment and group-size constraints, and x_ip = 0 for i < p to break symmetry (p. 4).
- Whiten multivariate covariates with the inverse square root of the sample covariance before balancing (p. 4).
- Randomize the treatment labels after optimizing the groups, to keep the effect estimate unbiased (pp. 4, 7).
- Bootstrap test for an optimized design: resample the covariates, re-solve the assignment, recompute the effect statistic, and take p = (1 + number of resampled statistics at least as large)/(1 + B) (p. 8).
- Report, for every method, the number of subjects per group needed to reach a target discrepancy (Table 1, p. 6).
- Check balance on moments outside the objective (higher, log and cross moments) against every competitor (Tables 2–3, p. 7).
