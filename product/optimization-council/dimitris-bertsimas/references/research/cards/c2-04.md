### S046 · Online vehicle routing: The edge of optimization in large-scale applications (Operations Research 2019, DOI 10.1287/opre.2018.1763)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–46, the accepted manuscript dated March 2018 ("Accepted for publication in Operations Research", p. 1), including Appendix A (pp. 40–43) and the references (pp. 43–46). Figures 3 and 5–7 survive only as axis labels and captions, so what they show is taken from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Patrick Jaillet and Sébastien Martin, MIT Operations Research Center (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a return to routing about 30 years after the thesis papers (round-1 cards S038, S016), now as a data-rich, centrally controlled problem. The network-flow formulation follows co-author Jaillet's truckload work [34] (pp. 6, 46). Martin wrote the simulator (TaxiSimulation, [20], p. 45) and went on to Boston's school buses (S137). Funded by ONR (p. 40).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + AP; SD for the online setting; HS for the backbone and local search}: The entry is an industry change. Ride-sharing firms now control fleets centrally, customers book ahead or accept pick-up windows, and the demand data are massive; this raises "new challenges" for real-time routing (pp. 1–2).
  - The literature had judged that taxi routing leaves "limited space for optimization", because problems are large and requests come late ([27], p. 3).
  - Exact algorithms "rarely scale past a few dozens" or hundreds of customers and vehicles (pp. 3–4).
  - The problem: online taxi routing as dial-a-ride with hard pick-up windows and one customer per taxi. The objective is profit, and each request must be accepted or rejected by its confirmation time (pp. 7–9).
  - The stated thesis: some "seemingly intractable optimization formulations in vehicle routing" can be scaled to the largest problem sizes (pp. 4, 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Re-optimize every 30 seconds, giving the offline problem 15 seconds (p. 10). The offline problem is a network-flow MIO whose arc variables are not indexed by taxi, so its size is O(E + N) (pp. 11–12). With pick-up times fixed, the MIO is integral and a max-flow LP solves it (Theorem 1, p. 13). Scaling rests on three moves:
  - *K-neighborhood* keeps, for each node, the K arcs with the least "lost time" (pp. 20–22).
  - *Backbone* solves the max-flow LP many times with random fixed pick-up times. The MIO then runs only on the union of their optimal arcs (pp. 22–23).
  - *Local backbone* samples pick-up times only inside windows that keep the current solution feasible. It warm-starts the MIO with that solution and repeats (pp. 24–25).

  What changes from the classical rolling horizon ([5, 31], p. 3) is the support: the exact MIO stays at the core, but runs on a learned sparse support. Re-optimization and the network-flow model of [34] stay.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions:
    - The graph is assumed acyclic. This holds when each pick-up window is shorter than the rides, as with windows of about 5 minutes; with larger windows "the algorithms of this paper would have to be adapted" (pp. 7–8).
    - Taxis are identical (p. 8). Windows are hard, though the approach "can be extended to the soft time window case" (p. 2).
    - Travel times are deterministic once revealed, and destinations are known at request (p. 9).
    - Travel times are stationary over the experiment, and the vehicles are under full control (p. 38). Idle taxis wait where they are (p. 33).
  - Synthetic city: 192 nodes and 640 arcs, 20 taxis, and Poisson demand of 40, 70 or 140 customers per hour. Fares are $80 and driving costs $5 per hour of driving; waiting costs $1 per hour (p. 16).
  - Real case: Manhattan's OpenStreetMap network, with 4,324 intersections and 9,518 arcs (p. 26). The demand is the 26,109 yellow-cab rides of Friday 15 April 2016, 12:00–1:30 pm. The date was "chosen purely arbitrarily, though we selected a time of high demand" (p. 28).
  - Travel times are estimated from that day's rides with the group's own method [6]. Windows, request times and confirmation times are set per simulation (p. 28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - The MIO (5)–(14) handles the time windows with "two strengthened Big M sets of constraints" (p. 12). Acyclicity removes the cycle-breaking constraints, and variables exist only for arcs compatible with the windows (p. 12).
  - Theorem 1 is proved by substitution. Once the times are fixed, each time constraint either forces its arc to zero or is inactive, which leaves the network-flow polytope (p. 13).
  - K-neighborhood (Algorithm 1) cuts the model to O(|C| + |K|) variables and constraints, from O(|C|² + |C||K|) (pp. 21–22).
  - Backbone (Algorithm 2, p. 23) and local backbone (Algorithm 3, p. 25). Each customer's feasible pick-up interval comes from forward and backward recursions, (16)–(19). They are linear in route length and akin to the Lazy and Eager Scheduling of [4] (pp. 24–25).
  - Remark 1: adding non-local random pick-up times makes the local backbone converge to the optimum. Each such sample has positive probability of being compatible with the optimal solution (p. 26). This is an existence argument with no rate.
  - Online, a confirmed customer gets p_c = 1, which "does not break the network-flow structure" (p. 32).
  - Engineering, the "formulation warm-start": the pruned graph is updated in place, with heaps keeping the K best arcs. Building it takes 1–2 seconds instead of 10–40 (pp. 31–32).
  - There is no complexity, approximation or probabilistic bound anywhere in the paper (pp. 1–46).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Synthetic study* (pp. 14–19): 20 random simulations per setting, with windows of 1–6 minutes at three demand levels. Gurobi computes MIOoptimal (p. 16).
    - Gains over greedy (Table 1, p. 17): MIOoptimal 1.42–19.93%, 2-OPT 0.96–11.64%, maxflow −2.60% to 12.82%.
    - MIO time grows exponentially with demand and window length (Figure 3, p. 18). The regime reading follows (p. 19).
  - *The boundary is printed.* Proving optimality within an hour on a typical laptop stops at about 150 customers with 5-minute windows (p. 20). A NYC re-optimization has more than 10 million binary variables (p. 20).
  - *Offline NYC* (pp. 29–30): 2,700 taxis, 6,000–6,500 customers, five random problems, 5 minutes per algorithm. That is longer than the 15 seconds available in practice, given "to compensate for the fact that we do not provide a warm-start" (p. 30).
    - Gains over greedy (Table 2, p. 29): local-backbone 9.13% at K = 20; MIOoptimal at best 5.41% (K = 4); 2-OPT 4.03%.
    - MIOoptimal proved optimality only at K = 2, where it is 5.05% worse than greedy (pp. 29–30).
  - *Online NYC* (pp. 33–38): 1.5-hour simulations on identical machines with 2 CPUs and 8 GB (p. 34). The fleet is 4,000 taxis, serving 80% of demand at best (p. 35), or varied from 2,000 to 10,000 (p. 36). The baselines are pure-online (nearest taxi), no-reopt (insertion planning) and 2-OPT re-optimization with warm starts (pp. 33–34). "Their parameters were all optimized to get the best results" (p. 34). Results:
    - backbone beats 2-OPT by 1–3.5% (p. 35);
    - asking for requests 10 minutes ahead adds 18% profit (p. 36);
    - performance dips near 70% served demand, the hardest case, where time ran short (p. 37);
    - with 10-minute windows, backbone earns 10% more than pure-online (p. 38).
  - *Limits reported*:
    - Metaheuristics such as tabu search could not be made to work within the time budget (pp. 4, 14).
    - MIOoptimal is intractable at scale for K ≥ 4 (p. 22).
    - The backbone grows too many arcs for windows over about 2 minutes (p. 23).
    - In large cities, 2-OPT runs out of time before it reaches a local optimum (p. 43).
  - *Not reported*: solver versions, and any gap for local-backbone. Code and data are promised with the published version (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in dynamic vehicle routing.
  - From the re-optimization and rolling-horizon line ([5, 31]) it takes the finding that online quality depends on the quality of the offline solutions ([34], p. 3).
  - Large ride-sharing systems are said to focus on "managing large-scale decision systems" more than on "optimizing vehicle actions" (p. 3).
  - Competitive-ratio analysis ([17], Jaillet and Wagner) is the worst-case alternative (pp. 3, 44).
  - Column generation and custom branching do not scale (pp. 3–4). Special-purpose heuristics must be re-adapted to every new problem (p. 4).
  - 2-OPT* [28] is chosen as the benchmark, as "a good trade-off" (p. 4).
  - The backbone idea is credited to Schneider et al. [30] for the TSP, "though formulated as a greedy heuristic" (p. 39).
  - No large-scale benchmark exists ([27], p. 4), so the paper builds one from public NYC data (pp. 4, 28).
- **D7 Writing**: The thesis sits in the title and in the section names: "the edge of optimization" or "of optimality" (title, p. 1; §3, p. 9; §3.4, p. 19). Contributions are stated as results (p. 6).
  - Recommendations are given by regime, including a concession: at low demand, "we recommend using greedy" (p. 19).
  - Advice to industry closes the experiments: reward early requests and wider windows (p. 38).
  - Hedges cover facts ("we could not find any official statistics", p. 19) and tuning ("empirically found", pp. 22, 26).
  - Headline against body: the abstract's "more than 25,000 customers per hour" (p. 1) does not match the 26,109 customers over 1.5 hours actually simulated (pp. 27–28, 34–35). That is about 17,400 an hour (my arithmetic).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: algorithms (K-neighborhood, backbone, local backbone, formulation warm-start), an integrality result (Theorem 1), open simulation software, and a finding on the value of prior information (pp. 6, 39).
  - Stated limits:
    - acyclicity (p. 8) and hard windows (p. 2);
    - stationary travel times and full control (p. 38);
    - the idle-taxi policy is left unstudied (p. 33);
    - the offline comparison covers a single re-optimization iteration (p. 30), which the online study then runs in full (pp. 33–38).
  - Unstated limits:
    - all demand comes from one 90-minute window on one date (p. 28);
    - no gap or certificate at scale, and no solver version (pp. 16, 28–30);
    - Remark 1 is only an existence argument (p. 26).
  - Future work (pp. 38–39): moving idle taxis by demand forecasts; re-estimating travel times online; a mode that recommends customers to drivers; other uses, from cargo ships and private jets to electric, self-driving and pooled vehicles.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅: a network-flow MIO with a class-keeping device. Fixed pick-up times make it an integral LP (Theorem 1, p. 13), and confirming a customer keeps the flow structure (p. 32). The boundary is printed: about 150 customers for a proof within an hour (p. 20), and intractability for K ≥ 4 at scale (p. 22). The scaling device is a sparser support, not a reformulation (pp. 20–25). The big-M constraints are explicitly strengthened (p. 12), which counts against the skill's blind spot on *formulation strength*.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠: a "seemingly intractable" belief is reopened (pp. 4, 6). But the *why now* is a change in the industry (central control, prior requests, data; pp. 1–2), with no solver × hardware figure.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠:
  - The incumbents (greedy insertion, 2-OPT*) are the opponents (pp. 14, 33–34), and the previous solution warm-starts the MIO (pp. 25, 31). Time limits are stated: 15 seconds and 5 minutes (pp. 10, 30).
  - At scale there is no certificate. MIOoptimal proved optimality only at K = 2 (p. 30), and local-backbone reports no gap.
  - This fits Heuristic 3 (local search over the structural variables, with MIO kept to small problems) and Tension T1.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: decisions are scored in profit against an emulated current rule. Nearest-taxi dispatch is "not too different from real taxi behavior" (p. 33), and pure-online is "representative of typical taxi system greedy strategies" (p. 38). There is no owner, no partner data and no field test, only public data and a simulator (p. 28).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: the value of information is drawn as curves. Extra profit is plotted against the mean prior request time (Figure 5, pp. 35–36) and against window length (Figure 7, pp. 37–38), and the curves become advice to operators (p. 38). There is no bound and no knob inside the model.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅, lineage only: Martin's simulator line (p. 45) continues in S137.
- New-pattern candidate: *backbone*. Pool the supports of many cheap randomized relaxations (here integral LPs), then solve the exact MIO on their union (pp. 22–23, 39). S137's scenario generation followed by joint selection is a sibling (S137 p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *MIO as the move of a local search* (pp. 24–26). Siblings: S137 p. 4 and S096 p. 6.
- New-pattern candidate: *regime map of where optimization pays*: greedy at low demand; maxflow for short windows; MIO for medium or high demand with wide windows (p. 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) If fixing one continuous decision makes an MIO integral, use the fixed problem twice. Use it as a heuristic, and, with random fixings, as a generator of candidate variables for the exact MIO (pp. 13, 22–23).
- (D4) Prune by a locality cost (here the K arcs per node with the least lost time). Calibrate K on the regime (supply against demand), not on instance size (pp. 21–22).
- (D2) Run a local backbone. Compute, for each variable, the slack interval that keeps the incumbent feasible, and sample inside it. Re-solve the MIO on the sampled support, warm-started by the incumbent. Add a few non-local samples so the search can escape (pp. 24–26).
- (D5) In rolling-horizon systems, warm-start the formulation as well as the solution: update the sparse model incrementally with heaps (pp. 31–32).
- (D5) Before scaling up, map the regimes on synthetic instances small enough to solve to optimality. Recommend the simple method where it is near-optimal (pp. 14–19).
- (D5) For online policies, vary one information parameter (mean prior request time, window length) and plot the gain against the incumbent rule (pp. 34–38).

**Quotes**:
- "In this situation, optimization is not extremely useful and we recommend using greedy, which is the fastest and the most interpretable." (p. 19)
- "The core idea of a “backbone” is that some decision variables do not vary too much across almost all near-optimal solutions, and that identifying them can significantly accelerate the optimization process." (p. 39)

### S093 · Exact first-choice product line optimization (Operations Research 2019, DOI 10.1287/opre.2018.1825)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–21, with the references and author notes. File p. 1 is the INFORMS download cover; pp. 2–21 are the journal print. The e-companion is not in the file and was not read. It holds every proof (EC.1.1–EC.1.9), the constraint-generation algorithms (EC.2) and the extra experiments (EC.3.1–EC.3.2). Displayed formulas lose their "=" signs and some symbols, so statements rest on the prose and the legible parts.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Velibor V. Mišić, then at UCLA Anderson (p. 2). His MIT thesis (Mišić 2016) is cited (p. 21). · **Position in trajectory**: Mišić's line on product lines, after the robust product line paper (Bertsimas and Mišić 2017, cited p. 3). The paper was received in August 2017 and accepted in September 2018, in the review area "Machine Learning and Data Science" (p. 2). The author note calls it "part of his long-term interest in large-scale optimization" (p. 21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + AP (marketing: product line design)}: The problem is a firm's: which candidate products to offer, when customers choose by preference and products cannibalize one another (p. 2).
  - It is "notoriously difficult" (p. 2). It is also NP-hard (Kohli and Krishnamurti 1989) and hard to approximate within O(n^(1−ε)) (Aouad et al. 2017) (p. 5).
  - The entry is a gap in method: "many papers consider heuristics; few consider directly formulating and solving the problem as an integer optimization (IO) problem" (p. 3).
  - The foil is the two closest IO papers, McBride–Zufryden (1988) and Belloni et al. (2008). The latter needed a week of computation on a real instance (pp. 2–3).
  - The stated problem: choose a product line from the permissible ones to maximize expected profit per customer, under a ranking-based first-choice model (eq. (1), p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Aggregate the preference constraints. For each option i, a single constraint forces to zero every option ranked below i when i is offered ((2d)–(2e)). The choice variables y then need not be binary, and the model has only n binary variables (p. 6). Belloni et al.'s model (3) has the same variables and only disaggregates these constraints (p. 7).
  - The structure, global x and local y for each customer type, invites Benders decomposition, which had not been used for product line design (pp. 4, 10).
  - At binary x the cuts come in closed form (Propositions 6–7, pp. 10–11). At fractional x, a greedy primal phase and a dual reconstruction give them, "without using an LO solver" (pp. 3, 12–13).
  - The full method first generates cuts on the LO relaxation, then uses them to warm-start a lazy-constraint MIO phase (p. 14).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Customers follow K rankings with probabilities λ; neither is restricted (p. 5). The ranking model covers utility maximization from conjoint data, lexicographic rules, and nonparametric models estimated from transactions (p. 5).
  - Assumption 1: the permissible product lines are {x : Cx ≤ d}, called "relatively mild" (p. 6). Profits are fixed and known; they are not decisions (p. 6). Assumptions 2–3 (nonnegative utilities consistent with the rankings) serve only the utility-based rival formulations (p. 7).
  - Synthetic data: rankings drawn uniformly from all rankings, profits from {1, …, 100}, λ uniform on the simplex, 20 instances per (n, K, b) (p. 16).
  - For the rivals, utilities are set to u = n − σ (eq. (15), p. 17). The paper admits these "may not be representative" of conjoint utilities, and reports a check with conjoint utilities in EC.3.1 (pp. 17–18).
  - Real data: the Toubia et al. (2003) conjoint study of laptop bags for Timbuk2, with 330 respondents and 3,584 candidate products, as used by Belloni et al. (2008) (p. 19).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Strength is measured by containment of LO relaxations, in four results (pp. 8–9; proofs in the e-companion, not read):
    - Theorem 1: the new formulation is contained in each of the three others.
    - Propositions 1–2: orderings among the rivals.
    - Proposition 3: formulation (3) and the utility formulation (4) are incomparable, shown on a one-segment instance.
    - Together: (2) is strongest and (5) weakest; (3) and (4) lie between and are incomparable (p. 9).
  - Corollary 1 orders the LO bounds the same way (p. 9).
  - Proposition 4: for one segment and no side constraints the formulation is integral, because its constraint matrix is totally unimodular. Proposition 5: the rivals are not, by explicit instances (p. 9).
  - Benders decomposition:
    - LO duality eliminates y (pp. 9–10).
    - Proposition 6: at binary x, the first-choice vector is the only feasible y (p. 10).
    - Proposition 7: the dual solution in closed form (p. 11).
    - Cuts are added lazily inside one branch-and-bound tree, instead of by re-solving the master (p. 11).
  - Theorem 2: the greedy primal phase and the dual phase give optimal primal and dual solutions, by complementary slackness (pp. 12–13). The primal phase takes the options in decreasing order of profit and records which constraint binds (A, B or C events).
  - Example 1 traces both phases in two tables and three figures; both objectives equal 51.05 (pp. 13–14).
  - The reformulation and constraint generation are exact. They terminate finitely because the subproblem algorithms return extreme points (pp. 14–15; proofs in EC.2).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Setup*: a late-2013 MacBook Pro (quad-core 2.6 GHz i7, 16 GB), Julia 0.6.0 with JuMP, and Gurobi 7.5. All code is public on GitHub (vvmisic/optimalPLD) (p. 16).
  - *Tractability* (Table 3, pp. 16–17): n up to 500 and K up to 1,000, averaged over 20 instances. The hardest cell (500, 500, b = 5) takes 1,510.80 s on average, and constrained instances take longer.
  - *Beyond that* (Table 4, p. 17): n = 500 and K = 1,000, with a one-hour limit. Final gaps are 3.33% (b = 5), 1.34% (b = 10) and 0.11% (no constraint); 1, 1 and 14 of the 20 instances are solved.
  - *Bounds* (Table 5, p. 17): the new formulation's integrality gaps are "below 2%" on average, with cell averages up to 2.67%. The rivals reach about 6%, 11% and 20% at n = 100.
  - *Solve times* (Table 6, p. 18), with a one-hour limit on the rivals:
    - At (100, 500, unconstrained), under 30 s against about 7, 20 and 50 minutes.
    - The new formulation solves every instance; (3) misses one, (5) is said to miss 52, and (4) misses almost all at (100, 1,000).
    - Two slips between text and table (my arithmetic). The speed-ups quoted for b = 5 (10×, 100×, 60×) match the b = 10 row; the b = 5 row gives about 4×, 36× and 66×. And the table's misses for (5) sum to 62.
  - *A reported surprise*: (4) is tighter than (5) yet much slower (p. 19).
  - *Real data* (pp. 19–20): product-line widths t ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 10, 20, 50}.
    - The incumbent D&C heuristic (Green and Krieger 1993), run from 10 random starts, warm-starts the MIO. The LO phase has no limit; the MIO phase stops at 6 hours or a 0.01% gap (p. 19).
    - Widths up to 10 are proved optimal; widths 20 and 50 end at gaps of 1.74% and 3.09% (Table 7, p. 19).
    - Gurobi cannot solve the direct formulation at this scale, for lack of memory (p. 20).
    - Belloni et al.'s instance (t = 5) takes 606.22 s against their week, with the caveat about hardware and solvers in the same paragraph (p. 20). Runs without the warm start are in EC.3.2 (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper continues two IO lines: McBride–Zufryden (utility-based) and Belloni et al. (ranking-based, solved by Lagrangean relaxation). It proves both weaker (pp. 3, 8–9).
  - It sets product line design beside assortment optimization (MNL, nested logit, latent-class MNL, Markov chain) and ranking-based assortment work (Aouad et al.; Honhon et al.) (p. 4). The stated differences (p. 4):
    - the model works for any first-choice model;
    - MIO gives provably optimal solutions rather than approximation guarantees;
    - it can ride solver progress and Benders decomposition.
  - Belloni et al.'s method is contrasted on implementation effort, with a hedge: "Although it is subjective, we believe these components require a higher level of expertise and effort to develop" (p. 16).
  - Belloni, the area editor, is thanked (p. 20).
  - His own textbooks anchor the background: on formulation strength (Bertsimas and Weismantel 2005, p. 8) and on Benders decomposition (Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis 1997, p. 9).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract runs in order (p. 2): the firm's question; a "new" formulation, proved "stronger"; a "unified understanding"; the "surprising fact" about Benders cuts; and the headline, a week down to about 10 minutes.
  - The contributions form a numbered list (p. 3). Surprise is flagged three times ("Surprisingly", "unexpected and surprising"; pp. 3–4, 11).
  - The results are paired with a worked example (pp. 13–14).
  - The caveat about hardware and solvers sits in §5.3 (p. 20). The abstract and conclusion repeat the week-to-10-minutes contrast without it; the conclusion adds "on a consumer laptop" (p. 20).
  - The conclusion invites "further exploration of linear and mixed-integer optimization modeling" in marketing (p. 20).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: theory (the strength ordering, integrality for one segment); an algorithm (Benders decomposition with closed-form and greedy cuts); computation; released code (pp. 3, 16).
  - Stated limits:
    - the formulation is not integral for K ≥ 2 (p. 9);
    - the largest instances end with gaps (pp. 17, 20);
    - the rivals' utilities may be unrepresentative (p. 17);
    - part of the headline speed-up is hardware and solvers (p. 20).
  - Unstated limits: profits are fixed, so prices are not decided (p. 6); each segment chooses deterministically (p. 2).
  - Future work: more LO and MIO modeling in product design and in marketing science (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅: a problem owned by marketing is written exactly as an MIO with n binary variables (p. 6). It comes with a proved strength ordering and integrality for one segment (pp. 8–9), and an exact Benders reformulation (pp. 14–15). This also counts against the skill's Tension T4 and its blind spot on *formulation strength*. In 2019, relaxation strength is the main theorem, and the experiments test strength against speed (Tables 5–6, pp. 17–18), printing where the link fails: (4) is tighter than (5) but slower (p. 19).
- Method 2 · variant ⚠: the old belief is one named instance, which needed a week in 2008 and is re-solved in 606.22 s (p. 20); there is no solver × hardware figure. The confound is conceded in the body (p. 20), not in the abstract (p. 2). NP-hardness (p. 5) does not deter the exact attempt.
- Method 4 · evidence ✅:
  - The incumbent heuristic (D&C, 10 random starts) warm-starts the MIO (p. 19).
  - Limits (1 hour, 6 hours, 0.01%), solver version and machine are stated (pp. 16–17, 19).
  - Final gaps are printed, and nothing unproved is called optimal (Tables 4 and 7, pp. 17, 19).
  - The incumbent formulations are duelled under the same solver and limit (Table 6, p. 18). The code is released (p. 16).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅, lineage only: Mišić's thesis line (Mišić 2016, cited p. 21), after the robust product line paper (Bertsimas and Mišić 2017, p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *formulation-strength tournament*. Order every published formulation by containment of LO relaxations. Test that order against integrality gaps and solve times, and report where it breaks (pp. 8–9, 17–19). S046's strengthened big-M constraints are a lighter instance (S046 p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: *cuts without an LP solver*. Exploit the subproblem structure so that Benders cuts come in closed form, or from a greedy primal–dual pair, even at fractional masters (pp. 10–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Aggregate pairwise ordering constraints into one constraint per option: the y's of all options ranked below i sum to at most 1 − x_i. The continuous y then take binary values on their own (pp. 6–7).

### S096 · Optimal prescriptive trees (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2019, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2018.0005)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–21, including the references. File p. 1 is the cover; pp. 2–21 are the journal print. Figures 1–12 exist only as captions, so results shown only in figures are taken from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jack Dunn and Nishanth Mundru, MIT (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: extends Dunn's optimal trees (thesis 2018; Bertsimas and Dunn 2017, 2019) from prediction to prescription (p. 5). It follows the Bertsimas–Kallus prescriptive analytics line and the group's 2017 diabetes kNN study (p. 3). The paper was accepted for the IJOO Special Issue on Machine Learning and Optimization, and "Andrea Lodi served as the editor-in-chief for this paper" (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML; AP (personalized medicine, advertising)}: Granular data let decision-makers personalize, in online advertising and in medicine (p. 2).
  - The problem: prescribe one of m treatments as a function of features, learning from observational data (x_i, y_i, z_i) (p. 2).
  - Three challenges frame it (pp. 2–3): counterfactual outcomes are unobserved; treatment assignment in observational data is biased; and the method must be interpretable, since "interpretability is highly desirable always and a necessity in many contexts".
  - The objective is a convex combination of the prescription error (1) and the prediction error (2), weighted by the prescription factor µ (eq. (3), p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Let the tree's leaves be the neighbourhoods that impute counterfactuals, so that the partition and the imputation are learned together (pp. 6–7).
  - Keep a prediction term. The pure prescription objective (µ = 1, Kallus's personalization-tree objective) gives a high predictive error and "an optimistic estimate of the prescriptive error that is not supported in out-of-sample experiments" (p. 7).
  - In one synthetic experiment, the lowest prescription error comes at µ between 0.5 and 0.8 (Figure 2, p. 7).
  - Against Kallus (2017), two things change: the prediction term, and near-optimal training in place of greedy splitting (p. 4). Axis-parallel splits and leaf-level estimates stay.
  - A linear variant fits an ℓ1-regularized regression for each treatment in each leaf (pp. 8–9).
  - Training is the coordinate descent of optimal trees, not MIO. An earlier MIO attempt by Bertsimas and Dunn (2017) did not scale, so they had fallen back on a greedy CART-like heuristic (p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Observational triples, m treatments, and smaller outcomes are better (pp. 2–3).
  - Counterfactuals are leaf means (eq. (5), p. 7) or per-leaf linear predictions (eq. (9), p. 9).
  - Hyperparameters (p. 8): the minimum leaf size n_min, the depth D_max, the complexity α, the minimum number of samples of a treatment before a leaf may prescribe it (n_treatment), and µ. Defaults: n_min = 1, n_treatment = 10, µ = 0.5. D_max and α are tuned as in Dunn (2018).
  - No identification assumption (unconfoundedness, overlap) is stated. The paper names "the bias inherent in observational data" as a challenge (p. 2), but its method does not model the assignment (pp. 6–9).
  - Synthetic data follow Powers et al. (2017): n = 1,000, d = 20, a test set of 60,000, treatment assigned by a logistic function of y0, and noise σ of 0.1, 0.2 and 0.5 (pp. 9–10).
  - Real data (pp. 15–19):
    - warfarin: 5,528 patients; doses binned into three groups; treatments reassigned by a BMI-based model, as in Kallus (2017) (pp. 15–16);
    - diabetes: Boston Medical Center records, with more than 100,000 visits and 13 regimens (p. 17);
    - jobs: LaLonde's data, with 297 controls, 425 treated and 7 covariates (p. 18);
    - infant health: IHDP, with 139 treated, 608 controls, 25 covariates and Hill's setting B (p. 19).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for proofs: the paper has no theorem. Its devices:
  - the loss blend (eqs. (3), (6), (10); pp. 3, 7, 9);
  - leaf-based counterfactual estimates (eqs. (5), (7)–(9); pp. 7–9);
  - coordinate descent from random starting trees. Nodes are visited in random order; at each, the move deletes a split, re-optimizes it or adds one, and improvements are kept until a local minimum is reached (p. 6);
  - fast elastic-net updates inside the leaves (p. 8).

  The unbiasedness and consistency of the µ = 1 objective rests on a citation to Kallus (2017) (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Synthetic* (pp. 9–14): three two-treatment experiments and one with three treatments, 100 simulations each.
    - Metrics: treatment accuracy and effect R² (p. 10); outcome R² with three treatments (p. 14).
    - Baselines: Kallus's greedy PT; OPT(1), OPT(0.5) and OPT(0.5)-L; regress-and-compare (RC) with ORT, LASSO or RF; and causal forests at default parameters. Causal MARS is dropped as similar to causal forests (p. 10).
    - Winners: OPT(0.5)-L in Experiments 1–2 (p. 11). RC–RF and causal forests in Experiment 3, with OPT(0.5)-L close (p. 13). OPT(0.5) and OPT(0.5)-L with three treatments (p. 14).
    - Sample trees recover the true boundary: treatment and effect accuracies of 0.99 and 0.90, then 0.925 and 0.82, with no spurious variables (pp. 11–12).
    - The cost of a wrong prescription is similar across methods (p. 14).
  - *Warfarin* (pp. 15–17): 100 replications; training n from 500 to 2,500; test set 2,500.
    - Classification with the known optimal doses bounds the attainable error: random forests reach about 32% at n = 2,500 (p. 16).
    - Greedy PT leads at small n. At n = 2,500, OPT(1) beats it by about 0.6%, and OPT(0.5) adds a further 0.6% (p. 17).
    - The RC methods beat every tree. The paper reports this against Kallus (2017) and attributes Kallus's opposite finding to weaker RC regressors (p. 17).
  - *Diabetes* (pp. 17–18): the protocol of the 2017 study.
    - Data are split 50:50. Counterfactuals are imputed by kNN; results are insensitive to imputing by LASSO or RF instead. Metrics are relative to the standard of care.
    - A baseline continuing current care and an oracle bracket the methods.
    - Training n runs from 1,000 to 50,000, over 100 splits.
    - OPT(0.5) is best at every size, and RC–kNN performs similarly at large n (pp. 17–18).
  - *Jobs* (pp. 18–19): 70:30 splits, repeated 100 times, with kNN-imputed counterfactuals.
    - A threshold δ is chosen for each instance to maximize the predicted average test income (p. 18).
    - Average income (Table 1, p. 19): OPT(0.5)-L $6,000.02; RC–LASSO $5,990.85, with standard errors of about $18–19; the baseline $5,467.09; the oracle $7,717.96.
    - The lead is smaller than one standard error (my arithmetic).
  - *Infant health* (pp. 19–20): 90:10 splits, repeated 100 times. Effect R² is 0.759 for OPT(0.5)-L against 0.704 for RC–RF (Table 2, p. 20).
  - *Not reported*: run times, software, machine and code. The scalability claim (seconds for n in the hundreds of thousands, p. 5) has no timing table behind it.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Regress-and-compare is recast as a depth-1 tree; the family includes the group's own 2017 diabetes kNN study (p. 3). Its stated weaknesses: nonlinear outcome functions hurt interpretability, and each treatment's model sees only part of the data (pp. 3–4).
  - Propensity weighting needs known scores, and inverse weighting is unstable in small samples (p. 4).
  - Kallus's personalization trees are the nearest work. The two named differences are the prediction term and near-optimal training (p. 4).
  - Causal trees and forests estimate effects, not prescriptions, and handle two treatments only (p. 4).
  - Breiman is invoked for interpretability: "On interpretability Trees rate an A+" (p. 5).
  - Where its warfarin result disagrees with Kallus's, the paper explains the disagreement rather than dismissing it (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract hedges ("either outperform or are comparable", p. 2), and so do the contributions ("it is our belief", p. 5).
  - The contributions are four labelled claims: interpretability, scalability, multiple treatments, effective prescriptions (p. 5).
  - The one-paragraph conclusion claims "superior prescriptive power" (p. 20). The body is weaker: RC wins on warfarin (p. 17), and RC–RF and causal forests win Experiment 3 (p. 13).
  - The title says "optimal", but training is coordinate descent "toward global optimality", with no certificate (p. 6).
  - Drawn trees are read against the ground truth as evidence of interpretability (pp. 11–13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a method (the blended objective, trained jointly) and broad evidence from four synthetic and four real settings (pp. 5, 9–20).
  - Stated limits: µ may need tuning (p. 8). RC gains flexibility "at a complete loss of interpretability" (p. 17).
  - Unstated limits:
    - no theory and no identification assumption (pp. 2–21);
    - no run times and no code (pp. 2–21);
    - for diabetes and jobs, the evaluation rests on imputed counterfactuals (pp. 17–18), and δ is set on the predicted test income (p. 18).
  - The conclusion states no limitation and no future work (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: decisions are scored against the current rule: HbA1c change relative to the standard of care (p. 17), and income against the baseline (p. 19). An oracle serves as the ceiling (pp. 17, 19). Prescriptive trees serve where people must see why (pp. 3, 18). This is evidence for steps 2 and 4–5. ### S137 · Optimizing schools' start time and bus routes (PNAS 2019, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1811462116)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–6, the PNAS "Latest Articles" print ("1 of 6" to "6 of 6"), including the references. The SI Appendix (formulations, benchmark details, the afternoon problem, the preference score) is not in the file and was not read. Figures exist only as captions and axis labels.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Arthur Delarue and Sébastien Martin, MIT Operations Research Center. The three contributed equally, and Martin is the corresponding author (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: Martin's routing line after S046. It also became part of Delarue's 2021 thesis (note 05 §5.1; the thesis was not read here). By the stated author contributions, all three designed the research; Delarue and Martin "performed research", analyzed the data and wrote the paper (p. 1). The partner is Boston Public Schools (pp. 2–3, 6). The paper is a PNAS Direct Submission, handled by a guest editor (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + IO; DA as decision support for policy}: The owner is Boston Public Schools (BPS).
  - Transport takes over 10% of a $1 billion budget (p. 1).
  - Routes for 125 public and over 80 private and charter schools were drawn by hand (p. 3).
  - A 1990 policy to rotate start times was never enforced (p. 5).
  - Early high-school starts are linked to harms to health and achievement and, in Boston, to inequity (pp. 1, 4–5).

  The entry is a gap. Algorithms for the subproblems exist, but combining them is less studied (p. 2), and general-purpose methods "lack portability" (p. 2). The paper states that "no existing algorithms address bell time selection in conjunction with bus routing" (p. 2). The problem it poses, the school time selection problem (STSP), is to choose each school's start time, pricing transport through routing and weighing community objectives (pp. 2, 4–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: BiRD (biobjective routing decomposition) splits routing into four steps (pp. 2–3):
  - stop assignment, by IO;
  - single-school routing: an insertion heuristic builds trips, and a set-cover IO combines them with the fewest buses;
  - scenario selection: generate "not one but several" routing scenarios per school, on the Pareto frontier of buses against ride time. One scenario per school is then chosen jointly, by an IO with network-flow structure that maximizes bus reuse;
  - bus scheduling, by IO, for morning and afternoon jointly.

  The scenario step bridges single-school and multi-school routing (Fig. 2, p. 3).
  - Start times: a trip-compatibility cost, summed over pairs of scenarios, gives pairwise costs for each pair of schools and times. These form a tractable proxy for transport cost and make the STSP a generalized quadratic assignment problem (GQAP) (p. 4).
  - The GQAP is solved by local improvement: the start times of a random subset of schools are re-optimized by MIO, with the others fixed (p. 4).
  - Further objectives enter as weighted "GQAP representable" terms (p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Data: anonymized BPS student data for 2017–2018 (p. 2), and Google Maps travel times (p. 6).
  - Constraints: four bus types, one of them wheelchair-accessible (p. 3); maximum walking distance and riding time; the available fleet (Fig. 2, p. 3).
  - Tractability choices: the routing proxy, with trade-off parameters set by cross-validation (p. 4), and local search on small random subsets (p. 4). The main text treats the morning; the afternoon is in the SI (p. 2).
  - Community inputs:
    - a BPS preference survey, whose response rate is lower at schools with more disadvantaged students (Fig. 4C, p. 4; Fig. 5, p. 5);
    - four objectives from a 2016 engagement process (p. 5).
  - The objectives are combined as a weighted average, with weights for policy-makers' priorities (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: The IO models are detailed only in the SI (not read):
  - assignment with walking limits, minimizing the number of stops (pp. 2–3);
  - set cover "reminiscent of crew scheduling" (p. 3);
  - a network-flow IO for scenario selection (p. 3);
  - the GQAP with pairwise affinity costs (p. 4).

  The local improvement carries one stated guarantee, which the paper calls interpretable. With subset size n, a converged solution "can only be improved by changing the bell times of at least n + 1 schools" (p. 4). There is no theorem, bound or gap. Earlier GQAP formulations of the STSP (ref. 25) are noted as intractable even for small instances (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Routing benchmarks* (p. 3): 32 published instances (ref. 23) and 20 synthetic ones.
    - BiRD beats the existing methods (refs 20, 22) on all but one, by 4% on average on the published set and 12% on the synthetic set.
    - Ablation: computing two scenarios per school and selecting system-wide improves BiRD by 20% over taking each school's best scenario.
  - *Boston routing* (p. 3): 530 buses against 650 for the manual routes, an 18% cut worth an estimated $10–15 million. BPS removed 50 buses in the first year, saving about $5 million. Average ride time stayed at about 23 minutes.
  - *Start times, synthetic* (p. 4): optimized three-tier times cost 11% less than balancing routes across tiers. Freeing the middle-tier times gains another 1–2%.
  - *Start times, Boston* (p. 4): 450 buses, 15% fewer than with current start times and 31% fewer than in 2016–2017.
  - *Equity* (Fig. 4, p. 4): a 0.78 correlation between neighbourhood income and elementary start time. High-school start times skew early for disadvantaged students (χ² P < 10⁻⁵).
  - *Trade-offs*: each additional point of community satisfaction can cost "dozens of additional buses and tens of millions" (p. 5). The team explored "tens of thousands of tradeoffs" for BPS (p. 6).
  - *Outcome*: the new policy was approved unanimously in December 2017 (p. 6).
    - The proposed plan would have cut the share of high-school students starting before 8:00 AM from 74% to 6%, and of elementary students dismissed after 4:00 PM from 33% to 15%. Up to $18 million would have been reinvested (p. 6).
    - The start times "have not been implemented yet" (p. 2). After protests, BPS "delayed the plan's implementation" (p. 6).
  - *Not in the main text*: run times, solver, machine, gaps. Julia and JuMP are named (pp. 2, 6), and the code is in a public repository (ref. 28, p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper cites reviews and subproblem algorithms of school bus routing (refs 15–18) and metaheuristics (refs 19–22) (p. 2). It also cites variants such as mixed loads, transfers and time windows (refs 18–24) (p. 2).
  - General-purpose algorithms are faulted for ignoring practical constraints and so lacking portability (p. 2).
  - An earlier STSP framework (ref. 25, Wenzel 2016) is credited, but its GQAP formulations were intractable (pp. 2, 4).
  - The paper claims the first joint model of start times and routing (p. 2). It stays modular: any state-of-the-art single-school router could replace its own (p. 3).
  - The health and equity literature supplies the motivation (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing**:

  - The headings are plain and playful: "A BiRD's Eye View", "Gridlock", "The Greater Good" (pp. 2, 5). Synthetic findings are hedged ("suggest", p. 4).
