Read level: **full**: pp. 1–14, all text including the declarations and references (pp. 13–14).
- Figures 1–3 are captions only. The tree is known from the text's walk-through (p. 9) and Table 4 (p. 11).
- First-page check: title as listed. Bertsimas is seventh of eight authors, co-senior with Kaafarani (p. 1).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors** (p. 1):
- Mary Bokenkamp (MGH; co-first author) and Yu Ma (MIT ORC; co-first and corresponding author);
- Ander Dorken-Gallastegi, Jefferson A. Proaño-Zamudio, Anthony Gebran, George C. Velmahos and Haytham M. A. Kaafarani (MGH trauma and emergency surgery);
- Bertsimas and Kaafarani "contributed equally to this work as senior authors".
- Contributions (p. 13):
  - methodology: Y.M. and D.B.;
  - software: Y.M.;
  - supervision, resources and project administration: D.B. and H.M.A.K.

**Position in trajectory**:
- A 2025 paper of the health programme (SKILL.md trajectory row 2019–2026).
- The MGH "surgical-engineering collaborative group" moves from risk prediction to treatment prescription (p. 2; refs. 13–16, p. 14):
  - before: Optimal Classification Trees (POTTER 2018; the trauma tool TOP 2021), whose smartphone apps are "in use by thousands of surgeons worldwide";
  - now: Optimal Policy Trees (OPT).
- The tools are the group's own: OPT (Bertsimas–Dunn–Mundru 2019; Amram–Dunn–Zhuo 2022) and Optimal Impute (Bertsimas–Pawlowski–Zhuo 2018) (pp. 4–5, 14).
- works.json records a medRxiv preprint (DOI 10.1101/2024.06.06.24308557). The journal received the paper on 22 May 2025 and accepted it on 17 September 2025 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML; AP (trauma surgery)}:
  - The entry is a clinical controversy without trial evidence:
    - REBOA mortality data conflict: higher mortality in refs. [1,2], lower in [3–5] (pp. 1–2);
    - complications are reported inconsistently (p. 2);
    - randomized trials are hard to design in an emergency (p. 2);
    - a joint professional statement finds no evidence that REBOA improves survival over standard care (p. 11).
  - The aim: OPTs "to improve the appropriate use and decrease the misuse of REBOA in hemodynamically unstable blunt trauma patients" (p. 2). The policy uses trauma-bay information and targets 24 h mortality (pp. 4–5).
  - The step from OCT to OPT is framed as "the progression from outcome-estimation-based methods to decision-based methods" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Learn a single-tree prescription policy from registry data (p. 5):
    - rewards come from random-forest propensity and outcome models, combined by doubly robust estimation;
    - an OPT is then trained on those rewards.
  - Relative to the predictive line (OCT):
    - kept: one interpretable tree;
    - changed: the leaves prescribe a treatment (REBOA or No REBOA) instead of a risk (p. 2).
  - Why one tree: ensembles (Regress and Compare, causal forests) are called "black box" methods built from "multiple weak decisions" that physicians cannot follow, whereas one tree shows the decision path (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Data: ACS-TQIP Participant Use Files 2010–2019 (p. 2).
    - Included: patients older than 16 with blunt trauma in hemorrhagic shock (SBP ≤ 90, or ≥ 4 RBC units within 4 h).
    - Excluded: transfers in the first 24 h; missing age or length of stay.
    - REBOA within 4 h is identified by ICD-10 procedure codes.
    - Cohort: 4.5 million records, then 121,465, then 100,615 patients, of whom 803 (0.8%) had REBOA (p. 6).
  - Decision-time features only (pp. 3–4):
    - age, sex, admission physiology, oxygen, intubation in the emergency department (ED), body size, signs of life, hospital teaching status and ACS verification level;
    - injuries diagnosable in the trauma bay;
    - procedures within one hour of arrival.
    - Blood-product volumes and the injury severity score (ISS) are excluded on purpose, because the surgeon would not know them at the decision (p. 3).
  - Admitted gaps (p. 12):
    - some variables are collected at the 4 h cutoff and "could be unavailable at the time of REBOA decision";
    - there are no data on the device, sheath size, timing, occlusion level or duration, and no FAST exam.
  - [my reading] The inclusion criterion itself (≥ 4 RBC units within 4 h, p. 2) uses information from after the decision. The limitations do not raise this.
  - Outcome: 24 h mortality only. Secondary outcomes are reported descriptively (p. 4).
  - Missing values are imputed by Optimal Impute (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a; no new formulation, the paper is empirical. Analysis devices:
  - OPT "leverages the power of mixed-integer programming"; it is cited, not formulated (p. 4).
  - Rewards: random-forest propensity and outcome models give doubly robust reward matrices (p. 5).
  - Hyperparameters (minimum leaf size, maximum depth, complexity parameter) are chosen by grid search on training reward (p. 5).
  - Policy evaluation:
    - rewards are re-estimated on the test half only, so that nothing leaks from the training set (p. 5);
    - expected mortality under the tree's prescriptions is compared with that under the observed assignments, as an absolute risk reduction (ARR);
    - the ARR is reported overall, by observed group and per leaf (p. 6).
  - A clinical-logic loop:
    - the best tree is evaluated "for clinical integrity and logic". If a variable unknowable at REBOA time has slipped in, for example, the dataset is adjusted and the steps repeated (p. 5);
    - the final tree was "selected through validation both computationally and through physician feedback" (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - The 50:50 split keeps enough data for test-set reward estimation. The root node has N = 50,308 (pp. 5, 9).
  - Descriptives (Tables 1–3, pp. 7–9), with chi-squared and Kruskal–Wallis tests in STATA 17 (p. 6):
    - 24 h mortality: 46.9% with REBOA vs 20.9% without;
    - in-hospital mortality: 61.8% vs 30.8% (p. 9).
  - Headline (p. 10; Table 4, p. 11):
    - ARR 0.9% overall and 0.8% for No-REBOA patients;
    - ARR 18% for REBOA patients (47% → 29%);
    - the largest leaf gains are at leaves 5 (7.98%) and 17 (5.44%).
  - The tree:
    - it splits on pulse, SBP (obtainable; < 68), GCS (< 5; < 6) and pneumothorax (p. 9);
    - it uses BMI and pulse oximetry, which the authors explain as possible imputation or database artefacts (p. 12);
    - it does not use pelvic fracture (p. 12).
  - Not reported:
    - the AUCs of the propensity and outcome models, announced as the metric (p. 6) [not found in pp. 1–13];
    - confidence intervals for any ARR;
    - how many test patients the tree moves to each treatment;
    - any comparator policy (CART, causal forest, treat-none). OPT's superiority is asserted by citation only (p. 4).
  - Data and example code are said to be on GitHub (p. 13; not checked).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Clinical: the REBOA literature is split both ways (pp. 1–2, 11). Moore et al.'s prospective study of the ER-REBOA catheter is called encouraging (p. 11).
  - Methodological:
    - OPT "has been shown to achieve superior solutions to traditional machine-learning single-tree methods such as Classification and Regression Trees (CART)" [15,16] (p. 4);
    - Regress and Compare and Causal Forest are presented as black boxes (p. 4);
    - graph analytics (MEGA, knowledge graphs, graph neural networks) is offered as a complement for future work (pp. 4–5, 12).
  - Priority claims (p. 11): "the first study to our knowledge that employs AI to elucidate indications for REBOA"; the first to use OPTs in trauma.
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Structured MDPI abstract with a strong close: their "groundbreaking use will be in prescribing interventions and changing outcomes" (p. 1).
  - The abstract's "18% reduction" is an ARR in percentage points (p. 10).
  - Heavy hedging later: "proof-of-concept" (pp. 1, 11, 13), "prototype" (p. 11), "not ready for bedside use" (p. 13). Tension T6 within one paper.
  - Slips:
    - Tables 1 and 2 are captioned as showing very similar cohorts, while nearly every row has p < 0.001 (pp. 7–8);
    - "Regress and Compare [17]" cites a paper on doubly robust estimation (pp. 4, 14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution level: an application and proof of concept of the group's prescriptive tool in a new clinical area.
  - The limits are specific (p. 12):
    - a registry not designed for REBOA, without device or FAST data;

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅: decisions are scored, not predictions.
  - Expected 24 h mortality under the policy is compared with that under the treatments actually given, so observed practice is the baseline (pp. 6, 10–11).
  - The tree is learned on decision rewards (p. 5).
  - The evaluation limits are stated, and deployment is deferred explicitly (pp. 11–13), as in step 6 (propose, then field-test).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the *current rule* (SKILL.md's term) is observed practice, not a stated rule. The ARR rests on model-imputed counterfactuals (pp. 2, 5–6, 10):
  - with no confidence interval;
  - with no overlap check, although only 0.8% were treated;
  - with no reported accuracy for the nuisance models.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: there is no duel.
  - OPT's advantage over CART and over black-box ensembles is asserted by citation (p. 4); no rival policy is run.
  - No solver, time limit or gap is reported for the MIO-based tree.
  - Hyperparameters are chosen on training reward (p. 5).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅: one programme carried forward with the same partner (pp. 2, 5, 13–14):
  - OCT risk calculators (2018, 2021), then OPT prescriptions, with the same MGH surgeons;
  - run by an ORC student (Yu Ma) with the group's tools.
- Also:
  - Taste mark 5 ✅: one tree is chosen so that physicians can follow it (p. 4).
  - Heuristic 4 ✅: specific limits are printed (p. 12).
  - Tension T6: the headline "18%" and "groundbreaking" (p. 1) vs "not ready for bedside use" (p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate (existing name): *Counterfactual replay against the historical decision-maker*. The tree's prescriptions and the observed treatments are both scored by test-half reward models (pp. 5–6). Compare c33's S457 and c38's S615.
- New-pattern candidate (existing name): *analytics partner inside a clinical team's study*. There are co-first and co-senior pairs across MGH and MIT, and methodology and software come from the ORC side (pp. 1, 13). Compare s19's S707.
- New-pattern candidate: *decision-time feature gate*. Only information available at the moment of decision enters the model, with explicit time windows and a review that removes violators (pp. 3–5). Also S390 (p. 2) and S439 (p. 6) in this batch.
- New-pattern candidate: *clinical-logic audit loop on the final tree*: refit until physicians accept the tree's logic (pp. 5, 10). Compare c38's S615 (final pick by clinical consistency) and c34's S621.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) For off-policy evaluation of a learned prescription, fit fresh propensity and outcome models on the test half only. Score both the policy and the observed assignments with them (p. 5).
- (D3) Write down what the decision-maker knows at decision time and exclude everything else. Here: trauma-bay vitals, injuries visible on primary and secondary survey, and procedures within one hour. Then audit the final model for violations (pp. 3–5).
- (D5) Report the risk reduction overall, separately for each observed-treatment group, and per leaf, so that readers see where the policy changes outcomes (pp. 6, 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "To avoid any information from the training set leaking through to the out-of-sample evaluation, instead of directly using the rewards from our existing reward estimator trained on the training set, we estimate a new set of reward estimators using only the test set and evaluate the policy against these rewards." (p. 5)
- "Our model is not ready for bedside use, and further studies with more granular data can improve its performance further for clinical practice." (p. 13)

### S390 · Holistic AI in medicine; improved performance and explainability (NPJ Digital Medicine 2026, DOI 10.1038/s41746-025-02298-3)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–8, all text including methods, data and code availability, references and declarations.
- Figure 1 survives as box labels and caption, Figure 2 as a caption.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Periklis Petridis, Georgios Margaritis and Vasiliki Stoumpou (MIT ORC) (p. 1). Contributions (p. 8):
- "D.B. directed the overall research";
- P.P. and G.M. developed the methodology;
- P.P. and V.S. performed the explanation annotations.

**Version**: the journal version of S524 (arXiv 2507.00205; card c33, read as v1). Received 30 June 2025, accepted 17 December 2025 (p. 7). Compared with the v1 digest in c33, the journal version:
- adds a fine-tuned HAIM baseline (HAIM-FT) and a zero-shot Gemini-2.5-Flash baseline (p. 3);
- adds two summarizers, MedGemma 4B and Gemini-2.5-Flash (pp. 3, 6);
- adds a manual error analysis on pleural effusion, and a note that the pathology tasks are partly information extraction (p. 3);
- moves the headline from 90.3 (fine-tuned Llama in v1, per c33) to 91.3 (fine-tuned Gemini) (p. 3).

The text does not say whether reviewers asked for these. The acknowledgements thank only the MIMIC and HAIM authors (p. 8).
**Position in trajectory**: a sequel to the group's HAIM framework (Soenksen et al. 2022, ref. 22). The abstract says "we previously introduced HAIM" (p. 1). The paper belongs to the 2019–2026 period of health care and LLMs.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; AP (ICU medicine); no optimization model}:
  - The entry is the group's own predecessor:
    - HAIM "uses data in a task-agnostic manner and lacks explainability" (p. 1);
    - its ClinicalBERT pipeline must chunk long notes into 512 tokens and average the embeddings (pp. 3, 6).
  - The framing:
    - only 5% of health-care organizations have deployed AI in clinical practice (p. 1);
    - deployment is limited by weak predictive performance and by lack of explainability (p. 1);
    - generative models can explain, but predict worse than discriminative ones (pp. 1–2).
  - The tasks: five ICU predictions (pleural effusion, cardiomegaly, pneumonia, 48 h mortality, length of stay), each with an explanation (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - GenAI is used "auxiliarily" (pp. 2, 6):
    - retrieve task-relevant chunks per modality;
    - summarize them with an LLM;
    - fine-tune ClinicalBERT on the summaries;
    - concatenate the per-modality embeddings as HAIM does, and predict with a discriminative model;
    - generate explanations that cite the patient's documents.
  - Relative to HAIM (p. 6):
    - kept: modalities processed separately and concatenated;
    - changed: inputs curated and summarized per task; encoders fine-tuned instead of frozen.
  - The claimed reason for the gain: one summary per ICU stay carries the stay's label, so fine-tuning works, whereas HAIM's chunks have no labels of their own (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Data: HAIM-MIMIC-MM, derived from MIMIC-IV: 34,537 samples, 7,279 hospitalizations and 6,485 patients, in four modalities (p. 2).
    - Task cohorts range from 3,512 to 16,888 (Table 1, p. 3).
  - Decision time: one random scan per ICU stay; only earlier information is used (p. 2).
  - Splits: 80/20, repeated over five splits, separated by patient (pp. 2–3).
  - Admitted: the pathology tasks are often stated in the notes, so for some patients they are information extraction (p. 3).
  - Pipeline settings (p. 6):
    - images and their reports are described by Qwen2.5-VL-72B; time series become summary statistics;
    - retrieval uses a hybrid BM25–SBERT score with α = 0.5, keeping the top-k chunks (k is not given in the main text);
    - summarizers: MedGemma 4B, Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen3-32B and Gemini-2.5-Flash;
    - ClinicalBERT is fine-tuned per summary type; the downstream model is "e.g., XGBoost".
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a; there is no optimization model and no proof. Devices:
  - the hybrid relevance score, eq. (1) (p. 6);
  - the concatenated feature vector, eq. (2) (p. 6);
  - a two-stage LLM judge (p. 4). An adversarial "Clinical Documentation Quality Analyst" is followed by an "Expert Medical Evaluation Analyst" scoring on 1–5 rubrics; the model is GPT-4.1 mini at temperature 0.2;
  - Wasserstein distances between score distributions (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Average AUC, with standard errors over 5 runs (Table 2, p. 3):
    - HAIM 79.9; HAIM-FT 79.7; zero-shot Gemini 79.6;
    - xHAIM 72.3–85.1, depending on the summarizer;
    - xHAIM-FT 77.7 (MedGemma), 89.8 (Qwen3), 90.2 (Llama) and 91.3 (Gemini).
  - Per task, fine-tuned Gemini vs HAIM (p. 3): +13.8, +17.1 and +20.4 on the three pathologies; +3.1 on mortality; +2.2 on length of stay.
  - The fine-tuning confound is tested: HAIM-FT gains nothing (79.7 vs 79.9) (p. 3).
  - Zero-shot Gemini beats HAIM on the pathologies but scores 44.7 on length of stay (p. 3).
  - With MedGemma, xHAIM-FT falls below HAIM on all five tasks. This is printed in Table 2 (p. 3), but the text says only that MedGemma "underperforms relative to the others" (p. 3).
  - [my check] The text's "AUC improvement of 13.4%" (p. 3) matches neither gain computed from Table 2: the absolute gain is 11.4 points (91.3 − 79.9), the relative gain 14.3%.
  - "Statistically meaningful" gains are claimed without a test (p. 3).
  - Explanations (pp. 4–5; Tables 3–5):
    - only two tasks are rated (pleural effusion, mortality);
    - two annotators rate 50 explanations per task, then the judge rates 1,000;
    - scores lie between about 3.4 and 4.3;
    - Wasserstein distances are at most 0.40, and the LLM–human distances are comparable to the human–human ones, except for citation and factuality on mortality.
    - The text says mortality "consistently receives lower scores" (p. 4). [my check] This holds for citation and factuality only: overall quality is not lower for mortality (Table 3, p. 4; Table 4, p. 5).
    - The annotators are two of the authors (p. 8), described as "human expert annotations" (p. 4).
  - Not reported:
    - calibration metrics, though the predictions are called "well-calibrated" (p. 5);
    - compute cost;
    - clinician raters;
    - external validation (its absence is admitted, p. 5).
  - The code "will be made available upon publication" (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - HAIM is "a state-of-the-art multimodal framework" and the main baseline (p. 1). ClinicalBERT is the black box (p. 1).
  - LLM-based clinical prediction is cited as approaches that "often underperform compared to traditional, albeit black-box, ML models" (p. 2).
  - Novelty claim: "among the first works in healthcare that combines" RAG-like pipelines with prediction and fine-tuning (p. 2).
  - No OR or interpretable-ML literature (for example the group's OCT) is cited.
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Nature format, with Results before Methods.
  - Strong framing:
    - xHAIM "transforms AI from a black-box predictor into an explainable decision support system" (p. 1);
    - "a significant advancement" (p. 5);
    - the gains "challenge the prevailing assumption that more data is always better" (p. 5).
  - Operational claims are left untested: reduced chart-review time and robustness to deployment in other hospitals (pp. 5–6).
  - The example explanation for mortality talks about the model conditionally: "the model likely weighed" recent trends (p. 5). [my reading] It rationalizes a prediction rather than tracing the discriminative model.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: a framework, an empirical gain on one public dataset, and a protocol for evaluating explanations.
  - Stated limits (p. 5): the currency of knowledge sources, LLM compute overhead, and validation beyond MIMIC-IV.
  - Unstated:
    - decisions are never modelled;
    - the explanations are rated by the authors and an LLM, not by clinicians;
    - the share of the pathology gains that is information extraction is not quantified in the main text (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · contradiction ✗: the paper claims "decision support" (pp. 1, 5) but scores only AUC and explanation ratings (pp. 3–5).
  - The baselines are the group's own earlier system and a zero-shot LLM.
  - There is no decision, no owner and no current clinical rule.
  - Same verdict as c33's S524.

- Also: Taste mark 5 · variant ⚠. Interpretability is delivered as post-hoc LLM narratives over a black-box predictor, and the paper concedes that generative models are not inherently interpretable (pp. 1, 5). Contrast S686 in this batch, where one tree is the explanation.
- New-pattern candidates (existing names). S390 and S524 are two versions of one work, so the first three count once with c33's S524:

- New-pattern candidate: *decision-time feature gate*: a random scan sets the decision time, and only prior information is used (p. 2). See S686.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) When your method adds a training step (here fine-tuning), give the baseline the same step and report it. Then the gain cannot be credited to the step alone (p. 3, Table 2).
- (D5) Add a zero-shot baseline from a frontier model. Show where it beats the old baseline and where it fails: pathologies vs length of stay at 44.7 (p. 3).
- (D5) Calibrate an automated judge on a small double-annotated sample (50 items per task) before scaling it to 1,000 items (pp. 4–5):
  - compare its score distribution with each annotator's by a Wasserstein distance on the rubric scale;
  - report the annotator–annotator distance beside it.
- (D5) On a task with few residual errors, inspect the errors by hand to separate mislabeling from model confusion (p. 3).
