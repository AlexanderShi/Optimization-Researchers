### S193 · From predictions to prescriptions in multistage optimization problems (arXiv preprint 2019, arXiv 1904.11637)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:1904.11637v1): main text pp. 1–32 read in full, with every proof followed (Theorems 1–4, Lemmas 1–10, pp. 9–27) but the constants C1–C5 in (7) (p. 27) not re-derived; Appendix A (Theorem 5, Lemma 11, pp. 34–35) and Appendix B (the SDDP-type algorithm and its cuts, pp. 35–38) read in full; references (pp. 32–33) skimmed. First-page check: title as listed; authors "Dimitris Bertsimas · Christopher McCord" (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Christopher McCord (MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the multistage sequel of SW5 (Bertsimas–Kallus, S008, cited here as arXiv:1402.5481, p. 32), whose framework it extends (contribution 1, p. 5); Lemmas 1, 3 and 4 are "refined versions" of Bertsimas–Kallus results (p. 10). Its test problems come from his adaptive-MIO paper with Georghiou (S082, pp. 28–29). The research notes place McCord in the DA thesis lineage (McCord 2019; 01-publications.md §1.5) and list this paper as arXiv-only, v1 (03-process-evidence.md §C.2, #348).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + SD; ML as the weight learners; LO and IO as engines in the lot-sizing example and Appendix B}: The entry is a gap between two communities. Multistage optimization under uncertainty has mostly been studied with the distribution known a priori (Bellman onward, p. 2); data-driven methods use i.i.d. samples of the uncertainty but leave out auxiliary covariates (p. 2). Two naive repairs are named and rejected: solving a deterministic problem with point predictions ignores the uncertainty (p. 2); fitting a generative parametric model and running SAA on its conditional samples "does not necessarily lead to asymptotically optimal decisions" (p. 3). The nearest multistage competitor, Ban et al. (2018), builds one scenario tree per covariate realization by (sparse) linear regression and so assumes uncertainty linear in the covariates with additive noise; this paper assumes no parametric form (p. 4). The problem is stated as the conditional dynamic program (2): minimize expected cost given X0 = x0, conditioning at every stage on the latest covariates (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Replace each conditional expectation in (2) by a weighted sum of next-stage value functions evaluated at the training observations, the weights w^t_{N,i}(x_{t−1}) coming from kNN, CART or random forests (Definitions 1–3 and (3), pp. 6–7). SAA is the special case w = 1/N (p. 6). Because the weights are nonnegative and sum to one, (3) is a DP whose uncertainty has a known finite distribution, so exact and approximate DP algorithms apply unchanged (p. 7). Changed relative to Bertsimas–Kallus: stages, with the weights re-conditioned on the newest covariates at each stage; for the theory, CART and Breiman's forests are replaced by Wager–Athey's honest, random-split, regular trees and by forests of subsampled trees whose minimum leaf size grows like N^δ, because "greedy decision trees have proven difficult to analyze theoretically" (pp. 14–15, 20–21). Unchanged: the weight functions and the regularity, existence and continuity assumptions, now required for the value function at each stage (p. 10). Computation: an SDDP-type decomposition whose cuts are averaged with the ML weights (Appendix B, Algorithm 1, line 22, p. 37) and, for lot sizing, a basestock policy with one target level per training covariate and period, NT parameters in all (p. 31).
- **D3 Model and data**: Finite horizon of T + 1 stages; known transitions s_{t+1} = f_t(z_t) and costs g_t(z_t); the uncertainty is revealed before each decision and its distribution "does not depend on the current state or past decisions" (p. 2), the restriction the conclusion names as future work (p. 31). Covariates follow a Markov process and Y_t is conditionally independent of the past given X_{t−1} (p. 5; Assumption 4, p. 9). Assumptions 1–4: compact feasible sets, finite expectations, equicontinuous stage value functions, compact covariate support, uniformly exponential tails of the noise, continuity of conditional expectations in x (p. 9); integer decisions are allowed, since a finite Z_t satisfies equicontinuity automatically (p. 9). For trees and forests, X uniform on [0,1]^d, "assumed for simplicity" (Assumption 5 and footnote 1, p. 15), and Lipschitz value functions (Assumption 6, p. 15). For the finite-sample bound, a small-ball lower bound on covariate mass (Assumption 7) and uniformly subgaussian noise (Assumption 8) (p. 22). Data are synthetic only. Twelve-stage inventory control with advance orders (c1 = 5), immediate orders (c2 = 10), backlog (cb = −10), holding (ch = 5), zero initial inventory and a cumulative advance-order budget 50(t + 1); covariates a three-dimensional AR(1) with coefficient 0.7 and N(0, I3) innovations; demand a factor model in the lagged covariates, truncated at zero (p. 28). Lot sizing replaces the immediate order by M binary fixed-quantity options with unit costs uniform on (5, 10), forbids backorders, and caps demand at 200 "to ensure feasibility" (pp. 29–30).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Strong asymptotic optimality with kNN weights, k = min{⌈CN^δ⌉, N − 1} (Theorem 1, p. 9). Skeleton: the stage-0 estimation error is bounded by a single-stage error plus the uniform error of the next stage, using |min h − min g| ≤ sup|h − g| (Lemma 2, p. 11) and the weights as a probability measure; repeating for t = 1, …, T − 1 bounds it by a sum of T single-stage uniform conditional-estimation errors (pp. 13–14). Each term vanishes by uniform consistency of kNN regression (Lemma 5, quoting Biau–Devroye Theorem 12.1, p. 12), then simultaneously for all z through a countable dense subset (Lemma 4, p. 12), then uniformly through equicontinuity and compactness (Lemma 1, pp. 10–11); equicontinuity passes to the weighted sums by Jensen (p. 14). Argmin limit points follow from Lemma 3 (p. 11).
  - Weak asymptotic optimality with CART and forest weights (Theorems 2–3, pp. 15, 20). Leaf diameters shrink in probability: per-coordinate split counts dominate a binomial, Hoeffding and a union bound over at most N/k leaves (Lemma 6, pp. 16–17); uniform consistency by a bias–variance split with Biau–Devroye's Lemma 12.1 and the same union bound (Lemma 7, pp. 17–18); a ν-net covering argument turns fixed-z bounds into sup-over-z bounds for Lipschitz functions (Lemma 8, p. 18); the weighted error is shown 2L_{t+1}-Lipschitz in z before covering (pp. 19–20); forests follow by Jensen over a fixed number B of trees (Lemma 9, p. 21).
  - Finite-sample regret for kNN (Theorem 4, pp. 22–23): with δ = 2/(2 + d), Õ(T/N^{1/3}) for d = 1 and Õ(T/N^{1/(2d)}) for d ≥ 2, against Õ(T/√N) for multistage SAA without covariates (p. 23). Lemma 10 (p. 23; proof pp. 24–25): the bias is bounded by the distance to the k-th neighbour, controlled by an ε/2-net and a binomial Hoeffding bound; the variance by Biau–Devroye's count of at most (25^d/d)N^{2d} neighbour orderings and Hoeffding for subgaussian sums. Then covering in z, a union bound over stages and a system of three inequalities give the explicit constants (7) (pp. 25–27).
  - Appendix A: strong asymptotic optimality for single-stage forests with fixed leaf size (Theorem 5), via Wager–Athey's bias theorem, McDiarmid with bounded differences 2s_N/N, and Borel–Cantelli (Lemma 11, pp. 34–35); the proof "does not extend to the multistage problem we consider here" (p. 21).
  - Appendix B: with linear f, g and polyhedral Z the deterministic equivalent of (3) is an LP, but with O(N^T) variables (p. 35). Hence SDDP: forward passes sample paths from the weights, a CLT-based statistical upper bound, backward passes average cut coefficients with the weights, and stopping when the upper bound is within ε of the lower bound gives an ε-optimal solution with probability at least 1 − α (pp. 35–37). Cut classes: Benders (valid; tight under strong duality), integer optimality cuts (valid, tight and finite for binary states, but "very ineffective in practice"), Lagrangian cuts of Zou et al. for binary states, and binary expansion for mixed-integer states (p. 38).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Two synthetic twelve-stage problems, inventory control (Fig. 1, p. 29) and lot sizing (Fig. 2, p. 30), scored by out-of-sample expected cost against the training size N on a log axis from 10 to 1000, averaged over one hundred training sets (p. 28). Curves: SAA (covariates ignored), kNN, RF and an "OPT" curve that appears in both legends but is not defined in the text (pp. 29–30); for lot sizing also static-kNN and static-RF, which use only the time-0 covariates (p. 31). Reported: SAA is suboptimal (p. 29) and "clearly suboptimal" (p. 31); kNN and RF approach OPT, which the text reads as asymptotic optimality (p. 29); using covariates cuts cost by "over 15%" (p. 29) and "nearly 15%" (p. 31); covariates that arrive over time beat time-0 covariates (p. 31). Not reported in the text: CART weights (covered by Theorem 2 but not run), k, the number of trees, whether the honest and random-split variants were used, the algorithm for the inventory example, how the NT basestock levels were optimized, solver, hardware, run times, optimality gaps, and any variability measure (pp. 28–31). The claimed "practical tractability" (p. 6) and "very little additional computational cost" (p. 31) are asserted, not measured. At the experiments' d = 3, Theorem 4's rate would be Õ(T/N^{1/6}) [my arithmetic]; the paper does not connect its rates to Figures 1–2. No real data, partner or current rule; adaptive-RO decision rules, the method of the paper the test problems come from, are not run (pp. 4, 28–31).
- **D6 Positioning**: Presented as an extension of Bertsimas–Kallus, cited as the 2014 arXiv preprint (pp. 4–5, 32); the novelty claims for CART and forest weights in the single-stage setting are made against that preprint (p. 5) and were not checked against the 2020 journal version. Neighbours surveyed without criticism: DP and ADP (Bellman, Bertsekas, Powell), SAA (Shapiro), robust DP (Iyengar), DRO (Goh–Sim), adaptive RO including his own survey (Bertsimas, Brown and Caramanis 2011) (pp. 3–4). RL is credited for unknown dynamics but said to "struggle to incorporate complex constraints that are common in OR problems"; contextual bandits are credited in advertising (p. 4). Single-stage covariate papers (Rudin–Vahn, Cohen et al., Ferreira et al., Bertsimas–Kallus) are listed; only Ban et al. (2018) is criticized, for its parametric assumption, and it is not compared empirically (pp. 4, 28–31). Statistical decision theory and ML are said to care about estimation and prediction rather than prescription (p. 4). The probabilistic tools are borrowed and named: Wager–Athey, Biau–Devroye, Shapiro's SDDP analysis, Zou et al.'s cuts (pp. 12, 14–17, 23–25, 35–38).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract runs: framework; unknown distribution with noisy observations and covariates; the three named learners; asymptotic optimality "under mild conditions"; finite-sample guarantees for kNN; computational examples showing a "significant decrease in cost" (p. 1). It carries no number. Contributions are listed by section, each with a novelty claim per learner and setting (pp. 5–6); the finite-sample bound is called, to the authors' knowledge, the first with auxiliary data (p. 21). Each theorem is followed by a comparison paragraph that tells the reader what the result costs or buys: against Bertsimas–Kallus (p. 10), against the SAA rate, with the dimension penalty explained as the price of conditioning (pp. 21–23). "Mild" sits beside assumptions and learners the authors themselves strengthen or modify (pp. 15, 21). The conclusion says the problem "is often tractable in practice" (p. 31) without times. Preprint slips: the lot-sizing balance constraint repeats the objective's cost terms (c2j qj z2tj + ch It) and omits demand (p. 30); the regret R_N counts g0 twice (p. 22); Definition 4 writes Q0 for Q1 and v*(x) for v*(x0) (p. 8); M names both the binary order options (p. 29) and the SDDP sample paths (p. 36); "auxilliary" (p. 5), "axuiliary" (p. 31).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Levels: framework (multistage weighted SAA), theory (consistency for three learners, the first finite-sample rate for kNN), algorithm (weight-averaged SDDP cuts, offered "for the reader's convenience", p. 35), illustration (synthetic). Stated limits: decisions that change the distribution of the uncertainty, as in pricing, are left for future work (p. 31), as are "efficient variants" for specific applications (pp. 31–32); fixed-leaf forests are proved only for one stage (p. 21); the rate pays a penalty that grows with the covariate dimension (pp. 22–23); the deterministic equivalent is impractical at O(N^T) (p. 35); basestock is not optimal for lot sizing because the value function is nonconvex (p. 31); integer optimality cuts are ineffective (p. 38). Unstated: the experiments do not say whether they used the learners the theorems cover (pp. 15, 21 vs 28–31); no run times, gaps or variability; synthetic data only; no comparison with Ban et al. or with decision rules.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) If a learner yields nonnegative weights summing to one, present the method as a reweighted SAA: every solver for the finite-scenario problem then applies unchanged, and SAA becomes both your special case and your baseline (pp. 6–7, 35).
- (D4) Telescope a multistage regret into a sum of single-stage uniform estimation errors with |min h − min g| ≤ sup|h − g| and Jensen over the weights; the single-stage consistency result then carries over stage by stage (pp. 11, 13–14).
- (D4) Upgrade pointwise almost-sure convergence to uniform convergence: a countable dense subset plus equicontinuity, then compactness (Lemmas 1 and 4, pp. 10–12); for Lipschitz objectives, a ν-net covering with ν = ε/(2L) turns a fixed-z tail bound into a sup-over-z bound (Lemma 8, p. 18).
- (D4) When the practical learner resists analysis, prove the result for a named analyzable variant (honest, random-split, regular trees; subsampled forests), state what differs from the standard implementation, and add a separate theorem for the closest practical variant where it holds (pp. 14–15, 21, 34–35).
- (D4) Report a finite-sample rate next to the no-covariate SAA rate and explain the dimension penalty as the price of conditioning against a better baseline (pp. 21–23).
- (D5) Build a value-of-information ladder: no covariates (SAA), time-0 covariates only (static), covariates over time (dynamic), and a full-information reference, all plotted against training size and averaged over many training sets (pp. 28–31).
- (D4) For integer stages, parametrize the policy by covariate-indexed basestock levels (NT parameters) to make the weighted DP computable (p. 31); in SDDP, average cut coefficients with the learned weights, and switch from Benders to Lagrangian cuts when states are binary (pp. 37–38).

### S199 · The price of interpretability (arXiv preprint 2019, arXiv 1907.03419)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:1907.03419v1): main text pp. 1–14 and Figure 9 with its table (p. 15) read in full; appendix proofs A.1–A.3 (pp. 17–19) followed line by line; references (pp. 15–16) skimmed. Figures are only partly legible in the text layer (axis ticks and legends; in Figs. 7–8 the minus and inequality signs of the λ ranges are lost, pp. 11, 14). First-page check: the title matches; the author line misspells the name as "Dimitris Bersimas", with the Sloan School affiliation and an MIT address, so the work is his (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Arthur Delarue (MIT ORC), Patrick Jaillet (MIT EECS), Sebastien Martin (MIT ORC) (p. 1); funded in part by an ONR grant (p. 14) · **Position in trajectory**: a "price of X" manuscript after robustness (S001, card in c07) and fairness (S012, card in c13). SKILL.md lists it under Method 3 as unpublished and cites it for Taste mark 5; the research notes record it as an INFORMS Data Mining student-paper finalist in 2019 and still unpublished (01-publications.md §3; not checked here). It builds directly on OCT [3] (S011) and best subset [5] (S010) (pp. 2, 5, 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO; DA in the human-in-the-loop uses of §5.3 and §6.2.1}: The entry is a property stakeholders demand but nobody has defined. Stakes: bail decisions and personalized medicine (citing his own diabetes paper [4]), a GDPR "right to explanation", algorithm aversion, and black-box random forests and neural networks (p. 1). Gap: interpretability "is hard to define and even harder to quantify", with a "know it when you see it" aspect that impedes quantitative analysis (p. 2); post-hoc local explanations and global surrogates can misrepresent the model (p. 2). Stated problem: give interpretability a mathematical definition from which its trade-off with accuracy, the "price", can be computed (abstract and §1.2, pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A model is the endpoint of an interpretable path m0 → m1 → … → mK, each step taken inside a modeller-chosen neighbourhood S(m): change one coefficient, split one leaf, split one cluster (pp. 3–4, Fig. 1). A model's interpretability loss is the loss of the best path that reaches it (eq. 1, p. 5). Path length recovers the field's proxies (sparsity, number of splits, number of clusters, p. 5) but cannot separate two depth-2 iris trees with equal error, t_good and t_bad (pp. 3–6), so the costs of the intermediate models must count (incrementality, Table 1, p. 6). Coherence axioms (Definition 2, p. 7) lead to the weighted-sum family L_α and to the one-parameter L_γ with α_k = γ^k (Definition 3, p. 8); γ → ∞ recovers complexity (sparsity), γ → 0 recovers greedy construction (Theorem 1, p. 8). The price is the Pareto front of cost against L_α, computed by weighted sums over path lengths (Propositions 1–2, p. 9). Changed: interpretability becomes a property of how a model is built and read, not only of the final model. Unchanged: the model classes and their MIO formulations (OCT for trees; best subset as the γ → ∞ case).
- **D3 Model and data**: A model space M with a positive cost c(m), possibly regularized (p. 2); S(m) nonempty for every m (p. 3); bounded costs for Theorem 1 and 0 < c_min ≤ c ≤ c_max with γ ≥ 1 for Proposition 2 (pp. 8–9). The step function, the base model m0 and γ are declared modelling choices (pp. 3, 12); γ = 1 in the regression study, "meaning the costs of all steps matter equally" (p. 12). Data: a 50-point, two-feature, two-class subsample of iris (p. 3); a toy OLS that predicts a child's age from height and weight with correlation 0.9 (p. 6); a job-prestige survey data set of 100 rows and 6 features after one-hot encoding (p. 13); California test scores 1998–1999, standardized, starting from an existing model that uses only the subsidized-lunch percentage (MSE 0.122 against an optimal 0.095) (p. 14). No train/test split is described; the reported MSEs read as in-sample (pp. 6, 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Lift from models to paths: L(m) is the minimum path loss over paths reaching m (eq. 1, p. 5). Problem (2), min c(m) subject to L(m) ≤ ℓ, gives the Pareto front; with path complexity it becomes the best K-step path (3), which generalizes best subset selection, the best tree of a given size and K-means (p. 5).
  - Axioms (Definition 2, p. 7): (a) the loss depends only on the cost sequence; (b) weak Pareto dominance. Corollary: deleting the last step cannot make a path less interpretable; path complexity is coherent (Remark, p. 7).
  - Theorem 1 (p. 8; proof pp. 17–18): for γ → ∞, split the weighted sums at the shorter path's length, bound the prefix by c_max times a geometric sum, and let the extra γ^k terms dominate (eqs. 19–29); for γ → 0, factor out γ^K at the first differing cost in lexicographic order (eqs. 30–34).
  - Proposition 1 (p. 9; proof p. 18): weighted-sum scalarization c(m_K) + λ Σ α_k c(m_k) yields Pareto-optimal models; the inner problem is (10) with coefficients (λα_1, …, λα_{K−1}, (1 + λ)α_K). Proposition 2 (p. 9; proof p. 19): for γ ≥ 1, paths longer than K_max = c_max/(λ c_min) (γ = 1) or a logarithmic expression (γ > 1) are never optimal, because the empty model's value, at most c_max, beats λ c_min Σ γ^k.
  - Trees (14) (p. 11): K copies of an OCT-type tree of depth at most D; tree k has exactly k active splits (14c); active nodes and split variables are monotone in k (14d–14e), and split thresholds of shared nodes are held equal by big-M-type constraints (14f).
  - Linear regression (17) (pp. 12–13): an SOS-1 constraint on each step β_{k+1} − β_k gives a convex integer program, said to be solvable for d ≤ 10, K ≤ 10 and any n; the reformulation (18) separates an outer choice of the coordinate sequence (combinatorial) from an inner convex QP in the step values; local improvement re-optimizes q randomly chosen steps over all d^q index choices (Algorithm 1, p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Toy OLS: Pareto fronts for γ = 1 (Fig. 5) and for γ = 0.5 and 10 (Fig. 6), showing the two limits of Theorem 1; Pareto points the weighted sum cannot reach are drawn dashed (pp. 9–10).
  - Iris trees of depth at most 2, by MIO (14): a price curve (Fig. 7); the most interpretable tree with two errors is t_good, with L_γ = 9 for γ = 1 (p. 11), consistent with its step costs 4, 3 and 2 (Fig. 3, p. 5); t_bad's 7, 4 and 2 would give 13 (Fig. 2, p. 4) [my arithmetic].
  - Job prestige, K = 10: Gurobi certifies the optimum in 5.078 s; local improvement takes 0.004 s with a 0.02% gap (q = 1) and 0.019 s with a 0.00% gap (q = 2) (Table 2, p. 14), "two orders of magnitude faster" (p. 13).
  - California: a price curve over λ (Fig. 8); log λ ≈ −1.65 is chosen to avoid "diminishing interprtability returns", giving a four-step update from the old model to MSE 0.097, called near-optimal (p. 14). The first step, adding district income, leaves the MSE at 0.122 (Fig. 9b, p. 15). Compared along the path with forward stagewise (greedy) and a "direct" path that adds the final least-squares coefficients one by one (Fig. 9c, p. 15).
  - Not reported: Gurobi version, hardware, repeated runs of the randomized heuristic, its starting sequence, out-of-sample error, any user study, other interpretability measures as baselines. Observation not verifiable in the text layer (signs lost): the Fig. 8 legend lists λ bands for K = 0, 1 and 3–7, with no K = 2, and appears to put log λ ≈ −1.65 in the K = 3 band, while the chosen path has four steps (pp. 14–15).
- **D6 Positioning**: Framed by Breiman: the two goals of prediction and information (p. 1), and the central problem of the best model at a given interpretability (p. 5). Prior work sorted into interpretable model classes (trees including OCT [3], rule lists, decision sets, case-based reasoning, GAMs, scoring systems), sparse regression, where LASSO, stagewise and least-angle regression are called "heuristics" and his own MIO work "scalable mixed-integer approaches" [5, 6], post-hoc local explanations (LIME), feature importance, and global surrogates that may mislead (pp. 1–2). Lipton and Doshi-Velez–Kim are used to show the definitional gap (p. 2). Generality is claimed: "our model generalizes many existing approaches" (p. 5) and the framework offers "a principled way to answer a central question" of the literature (p. 9). **Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To define a vague property, fix a step neighbourhood S and a base model m0, define the loss on paths, and give each model the loss of its best path (pp. 3–5).
- (D4) Write coherence axioms first, then a weighted family that satisfies them, then a one-parameter member (α_k = γ^k), and prove that its limits recover the field's proxies (pp. 7–8, 17–18).
- (D4) Compute a "price" as a Pareto front by weighted-sum scalarization, bound the number of subproblems (Proposition 2), and draw the non-convex parts the scalarization cannot reach (pp. 9–10, 18–19).
- (D4) Encode a nested sequence of models as K coupled copies with monotone activation and equal-split linking constraints (14c–14f) (p. 11).
- (D4) Split a path problem into an outer combinatorial choice of indices and an inner convex QP; improve by re-optimizing q randomly chosen steps over all index choices (Algorithm 1, p. 13).
- (D5) Calibrate the heuristic against the exact MIO on a small real instance and print time and gap side by side (Table 2, p. 14).
- (D2) Set the step weights to the probabilities that a decision maker picks complexity k (α_k = p_k): the solution is a nested family that minimizes expected cost over the choice of k (§5.3, p. 12).
- (D5) Start the path at the deployed model, so that an update is shown as a short sequence of readable coefficient changes with the MSE after each (p. 14; Fig. 9b, p. 15).
