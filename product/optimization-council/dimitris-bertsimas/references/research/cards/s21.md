# Batch s21 · Dimitris Bertsimas · supplement (round 1)

**Batch intent**: Three 2024 preprints from the 2019–2026 ML and health-care period, each carried by an MIT ORC student. Together they show what his "modern optimization lens" looks like at the edge of his ML output:
- S478 (with Vasiliki Stoumpou, arXiv 2410.19200; ML) extends Random Forests with adaptive sample weights and per-sample tree weights to ask whether boosting is stronger than bagging. It contains no optimization model.
- S626 (with Matthew Peroni, arXiv 2405.20486, a JMLR submission; DA+ML) turns the group's Optimal Policy Trees on machine-learning practice itself: which model to use for which input, and when to abstain.
- S696 (with Catherine Ning, the surgeon G. A. Margonis and seven clinical coauthors, Research Square, December 2024; ML+AP) rebalances a survival-model training set by matching integer programs within risk strata.

Questions asked of each work:
- Is there an exact formulation, and does it keep a solver class (Method 1)?
- How is the incumbent dueled, and can the headline numbers be traced to the tables (Method 4, Tension T6)?
- Is a decision scored, or only a prediction (Method 5)?
- Is a property priced by a knob (Method 3)?
- What do the author lists and contribution statements say about who did the work (Method 6, Heuristic 8)?

Two moves recur across the batch and are tested as candidates: per-sample model choice (S478, S626) and optimizing the training sample instead of the model (S478, S696, and S620 in c39).

### S478 · Binary Classification: Is Boosting stronger than Bagging? (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2410.19200)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–26. Read: the abstract, §1 (contributions, related work), §2 (Algorithms 1–2, eqs. 1–8), §3 (Tables 1–7), §4, the declarations, the references and appendix Tables 8–10. Figures 1–6 (trees and bar charts) have no text layer. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Stoumpou match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Vasiliki Stoumpou (MIT ORC, p. 1). The contribution statement says that Bertsimas "motivated, supported and supervised the presented work and edited the paper". Stoumpou "developed the presented algorithms, implemented the pipeline and the code, ran the experiments and wrote the paper" (p. 21) · **Position in trajectory**: ML period. A student-led line with no optimization formulation and no group self-citations (references pp. 21–23). The same student leads S682 (card in c34).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; AP only as test material (two hospital data sets)}:
  - The common view: boosting, XGBoost above all, is "generally considered more performant" than bagging on tabular data, and neither is interpretable (abstract p. 1; p. 2).
  - Entry: two "simplistic assumptions" of Random Forests, namely that all samples and all trees are equally important (abstract p. 1; p. 5).
  - Problem: extend RF so that it matches boosting in AUC and partially recovers interpretability. Binary classification only (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: the "Enhanced Random Forest" (ERF) (pp. 5–10).
  - Iterate RF fits (Algorithm 1, pp. 6–7). Each round:
    - sets the class threshold t* by Youden's J (eq. 1);
    - adds λ times each sample's misclassification margin to its weight, and clips negative weights at zero;
    - uses the weights both in the split criterion and as bootstrap selection probabilities.

    It stops after a fixed number of rounds or by validation AUC.
  - Options:
    - sample features per tree instead of per split (p. 7);
    - prune the lowest bucket of sample weights, and the features below 10% of the top MDI importance, until validation AUC drops (Algorithm 2, pp. 7–8).
  - Per-sample tree weights:
    - a new point is predicted only by the L best trees of each of its M nearest training neighbours (eqs. 4–5, p. 9);
    - two scores (count, and negative rank sum; eqs. 6–8) rank the trees for that point, and the top few are shown as its explanation (pp. 9–10).
  - What changes: AdaBoost's hard-sample weighting moves into bagging. Each tree stays a full predictor that a user can read. Residual-fitted boosting trees are not (pp. 3, 9–10).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Binary labels; a train/validation/test split (p. 6).
  - Fifteen data sets of 4,909–51,476 rows and 11–211 one-hot features. Thirteen are public; two (diabetes, spleen) come from collaborating hospitals (Table 1, p. 11; p. 21).
  - All ensembles have 200 trees. RF and CART are capped at depth 5–6. XGBoost is tuned over depth 3–6 and learning rate 0.01–0.4, and AdaBoost over depth 3–6 and learning rate 0.01–1.0 (p. 11).
  - Neighbours are found by Euclidean distance on the encoded features (p. 9).
  - The weight update rests on nothing beyond the AdaBoost analogy (pp. 3, 6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a. There is no optimization model, theorem or convergence argument; D2 gives the algorithm. [my reading] The tree weights (eq. 5, p. 9) are vote counts over the neighbours' top-L lists, not fitted to any objective.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol: tuning by 5-fold CV, a 15% hold-out test set, AUC, and Wilcoxon tests with "adjusted" p-values (the correction is not named) (pp. 11–12).
  - Tuned (Table 2, p. 12):

    | Method | Mean AUC | p vs ERF |
    |---|---|---|
    | ERF | 0.8494 | – |
    | XGBoost | 0.8500 | 0.3028 |
    | AdaBoost | 0.8431 | 0.0738 |
    | RF | 0.8298 | 0.0012 |
    | CART | 0.7967 | 0.0002 |

    XGBoost is ahead on 10 of 15 sets (my count), which the text concedes.
  - At defaults (Table 3, p. 13): ERF 0.8483, XGBoost 0.8355 (p = 0.0256).
  - Also reported:
    - a factorial ablation: sample weights plus sample probabilities are best on 11 of 15 sets (pp. 13–14);
    - cleaning removes 26.55% of features and 11.01% of samples on average, for less than 0.2% AUC loss (Table 5, p. 15);
    - tree weights add a gain on 6 of 15 sets inside ERF, and on 13 of 15 on a vanilla RF (pp. 18–19).
  - Not reported: package names and versions, run times, seeds. The code is "available upon request" (p. 21).
  - [my reading]
    - ERF's default learning rate of 0.2 was chosen on the same 15 sets (p. 12).
    - Tuning budgets were unequal: ERF was tuned over several switches and a learning rate, RF over depth 5–6 only (p. 11).
    - Three sets (credit card, deposit, gamma) show identical ERF AUCs in the tuned and default tables, although their tuned settings differ from the stated default (Tables 2, 3, 8; pp. 12–13, 24).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: adaptive sample weights, per-sample tree weights for partial interpretability, and data cleaning. The abstract claims "the equivalence of bagging and boosting methods in performance, and the edge of bagging in interpretability" (abstract p. 1; pp. 2–3, 20).
  - Stated limits: binary only, with multi-class as the next step (p. 20); the tree scores "are a proxy" (p. 16).
  - Not stated [my reading]: one test split per set; no theory; no run times. The claim of beating XGBoost rests on the default regime (pp. 1, 12–13).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11–13): only the duel step survives, outside MIO.
  - Present: four incumbents in a tuned and a default regime, with tests. The tuned loss to XGBoost is printed (p. 12).
  - Absent: an exact model, a warm start, a certificate and a time limit. Step 5's equal budgets are not met (unequal tuning, p. 11).
- Method 6 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 21–23): a student-carried line that he sponsored and edited (p. 21), with no optimization and no group work cited. As in c28 S132 and c34 S682, he sponsors this programme rather than shapes it. Methods 1 and 5 are not engaged; given his stated role, this is not counted as counter-evidence. *Not counted*: Taste warning signs 3 and 4 apply (greedy CART trees kept as the explanation; accuracy without decision value).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 9–10, 18): interpretability is named as the property, but no price is computed.
  - Tree weighting is said to come "without sacrificing performance" (p. 18).
  - The knobs M and L are set by validation (p. 9).
  - No curve of AUC against the number of trees shown is given.
- New-pattern candidate: **sponsor-and-edit senior authorship on student-led lines outside optimization** (p. 21), the name used in c28, c34 and c38.
- New-pattern candidate: **per-sample model choice**. Each point gets its own tree weights (pp. 8–10); S626 learns the same choice as a policy tree.
- New-pattern candidate: **optimize the training sample, not the model** (c39's name), in a heuristic variant. Hard samples are up-weighted and low-weight samples pruned, while CART is unchanged. "Cleaning the dataset is actually beneficial" on 8 of 15 sets (pp. 6–8, 14–15, 20). Compare S696.
- New-pattern candidate: **default-parameter duel beside the tuned duel**. Robustness is claimed from the small loss between the tuned and the default runs (pp. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Run every method twice: tuned by CV, and at one default fixed across all data sets. Report both tables; a small loss from tuned to default is evidence of robustness (pp. 11–13). [my reading] Fix your own method's default on data outside the benchmark.
- To explain an ensemble's prediction for one point:
  - weight each tree by how often it is among the L best trees of the point's M nearest training neighbours;
  - break ties by summed rank;
  - show the top three trees (eqs. 4–8, pp. 9–10; Fig. 1, p. 17).
- Ablate a multi-part extension factorially in one table: per-tree features, sample weights, sample probabilities and their combinations (Table 4, p. 14).
- Report data cleaning as three numbers per data set (AUC change, feature reduction, sample reduction). State the tolerance and the stopping rule: 0.2% AUC, 70 rounds, a 1% early stop (pp. 14–15; Tables 9–10, p. 25).

- "with high probability, XGBoost outperforming our method is due to randomness, although it performs slightly better in most datasets" (p. 12)

### S626 · Policy Trees for Prediction: Interpretable and Adaptive Model Selection for Machine Learning (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2405.20486)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–45. Read: the abstract, §§1–6, Appendices A–D with all proofs, and the references. Figures 1–16 (trees and reward plots) have only captions. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Peroni match (p. 1). The file is arXiv v1 of 30 May 2024 in the JMLR template ("Submitted 5/30/24", with placeholders for volume and URL, p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Matthew Peroni (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the Optimal Trees → Optimal Policy Trees line (SW4, SW5; Bertsimas–Dunn 2017 and 2019; Amram–Dunn–Zhuo 2022, pp. 4–6, 42), turned on machine-learning practice itself. The motivating cases are the group's multimodal papers on hurricane forecasting and health care (Boussioux et al. 2022; Soenksen et al. 2022, p. 3). MIT SuperCloud is acknowledged (p. 31).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA+ML; IO as the tree engine; AP and physics as test material}:
  - Setting: many capable models are available through open-source packages and APIs (p. 1).
  - The practitioner's questions: which model to use, and when? Ensemble or not? When are the models error-prone? Black box or interpretable (pp. 1–2)?
  - Gaps (pp. 2–3):
    - mixture-of-experts models are not interpretable and need differentiable, retrainable experts (IME 2023);
    - hierarchical mixtures of experts use soft splits;
    - rejection learning trains the predictor and the rejector jointly, or handles a single predictor.
  - Entry: models that "cannot practically, or in some cases legally, be fine-tuned" (p. 3).
  - Problem, stated as a prescription: choose a model or an ensemble for each input, or reject it, with query access only (pp. 2, 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: model selection becomes policy learning (pp. 4–10).
  - The treatments are the constituent models, fixed ensemble weight vectors and a dummy rejection model.
  - Per-sample rewards are computed on a validation set kept apart from model training: cross-entropy or misclassification, and squared error in regression. They form a complete reward matrix, so "no reward estimation will be necessary" (pp. 4, 8–10).
  - An Optimal Policy Tree maximizes the total reward, with a split penalty λ, a depth D and a leaf minimum c_min (eq. 6, p. 5).
    - It is trained by the Optimal Trees coordinate-descent method (Bertsimas–Dunn 2019, §8.4), not greedily (pp. 5–6).
    - It may split on the models' own output scores, used as confidence features (p. 7).
  - What changes against the natural alternative: the Meta-Tree fits CART to the label of each sample's best model (eq. 16). Both learn trees of the same class, but OP2T sees relative rewards (pp. 11, 14).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions:
    - query access only (p. 4);
    - a finite set of weights W, fixed in advance, that always contains every single model (pp. 6, 8–9);
    - parallel splits; hyperplane splits were implemented and gave no benefit (pp. 6, 15);
    - the rejection model is assumed "perfectly calibrated", with α_k < 1/K (p. 9);
    - smooth predictions motivate the cross-entropy reward (p. 8).
  - Data (pp. 17–28):
    - a 1-D toy (n = 500);
    - Concrete (n = 1080, p = 8);
    - a synthetic projectile with drag (n = 20k);
    - IMDb reviews (50k; BERT, Word2Vec and TF-IDF logistic regression);
    - MIMIC-IV mortality (n = 11,306, 14 features, missing values set to −1).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Leaf separability: for a given tree, each leaf's prescription is found by enumeration (eqs. 3–5, p. 5).
  - Proposition 3: two disjoint dominated subspaces with different best models suffice for an "informative" policy, one that beats the best single model. The proof is by construction and linearity of expectation (p. 13; App. C.2, pp. 37–38). Two intervals on the real line need only a depth-3 tree (pp. 13–14).
  - Proposition 4: the reward gap between OP2T and a Meta-Tree (or Meta-XGB) can be arbitrarily large for unbounded rewards. On fixed data it is at most ((m−1)/m)·R_max. Proof: a non-separable subset whose majority-best model has a far worse total reward, and a contradiction with a depth-0 tree (p. 14; App. C.3, pp. 38–39).
  - Proposition 6: raising the rejection reward toward the reward bound removes the maximal dominated subspaces in the limit, and raises the expected reward of those that remain (p. 16; App. C.4, pp. 39–40).
  - Proposition 7: a closed-form critical rejection threshold α* for each leaf under cross-entropy (App. C.1, pp. 36–37).
  - App. B writes the single-model rejection interval as an MIO "purely to demonstrate" that it can be solved, then notes a polynomial enumeration (pp. 33–34). [my reading] As printed, (24) contains the bilinear term (h(x_i) − a)(b − h(x_i)), so it is not a linear MIO.
  - The trees carry no gap or certificate (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Benchmarks: Meta-Tree (CART) and Meta-XGB, both tuned. OP2T is tuned over λ, D ≤ 10 and c_min by 3- or 5-fold CV (p. 16).
  - Results:
    - Concrete: MSE 36.13, against 39.2 for the best single model and 39.69 and 39.57 for the two meta-learners; 25.22 with 37.43% rejected (Table 1, pp. 19–20).
    - Projectile: Meta-XGB is best at every rejection level (140.85 against OP2T's 141.74 without rejection). At depth 2, OP2T's MSE is 183.26 against the Meta-Tree's 79,344.09 (Tables 3–4, p. 23; Fig. 8, p. 24).
    - IMDb: 93.53% accuracy, against BERT's 92.8 and Meta-XGB's 93.44 (Table 5, pp. 25–26).
    - MIMIC-IV: AUC 0.9235, against 0.9224 and 0.9209 for Meta-XGB on the two feature spaces (Tables 6–7, p. 28).
  - Not reported: only single splits are used (e.g., 60/30/10, p. 28). The tree software is not named, and there are no run times, seeds, intervals or tests.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Four listed contributions (p. 4):
    - interpretable adaptive selection with query access only;
    - regression and classification;
    - parameterized rejection as regularization;
    - theory.
  - Stated limits:
    - the quality of a depth-bounded policy depends on the number and shape of the maximal dominated subspaces (p. 15);
    - Meta-XGB wins on the projectile data (p. 23).
  - Future work: other rewards, dynamic ensemble weights, and the theory of expert selection (p. 31).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 4, 11, 14, 23–25): the prescription-over-prediction thesis, applied to ML practice.
  - The decision is which model, ensemble or abstention to use.
  - The Meta-Tree, which predicts the best model's label, plays the predict-then-act incumbent.
  - Proposition 4 and the depth-2 projectile trees show the gap when the best-model labels are not separable.
  - There is no owner, partner rule or field test. The "best model in hindsight" stands in for the current rule (p. 25).
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 10–11, 20, 23, 26, 28, 36): one knob, the rejection reward α, prices abstention.
  - Its meaning in each leaf does not depend on the solution:
    - in regression, a model is prescribed only if its leaf MSE is below α (pp. 10–11);
    - in classification with the misclassification reward, rejection is prescribed if and only if the models' leaf accuracy is below the rejection model's (p. 10).
  - α* has a closed form (p. 36).
  - Every experiment prints the error against the share rejected, for two or three values of α.
  - The curves are thin, and no bad case is named for the knob.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 5–6, 16, 23, 27): the abstract's "globally optimized" trees are trained by coordinate descent, with no gap or time limit (Tension T1).
  - The duel steps are present: the greedy Meta-Tree at matched shallow depth (Table 4, p. 23), and the black-box Meta-XGB.
  - The loss to Meta-XGB is printed and explained by Proposition 4 (p. 23).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 4–6, 42): a new ORC student extends the prescriptive-tree programme to model selection, motivated by the group's own multimodal papers.
- New-pattern candidate: **per-sample model choice** (pp. 4–9). S478 is a heuristic version.
- New-pattern candidate: **practitioner questions as the paper's spine**. Four questions open the paper. They are answered under their own headings after the illustrative example and after the Concrete and IMDb experiments (pp. 2, 7, 20–21, 27). Compare "numbered open questions answered in order" (c04).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the actions are models you can run on every validation sample, build the full reward matrix and fit a prescriptive tree to it directly. No counterfactual estimation is needed (pp. 4, 8–9, 35).
- Add a dummy "reject" action with a constant reward α. In each leaf it wins exactly when every model's leaf loss is worse than α, which gives the knob a stated meaning (pp. 9–11; App. C.1, p. 36).
- To show why learning from relative rewards beats classifying the best model:
  - construct a non-separable subset whose majority-best model has a far worse total reward;
  - bound the gain by ((m−1)/m)·R_max (p. 14; App. C.3, pp. 38–39).
- Compare against the greedy rival at matched shallow depths, where trees stay interpretable, not only at the tuned depth (Table 4, p. 23; Fig. 8, p. 24).
- Let the policy split on the constituent models' output scores. On IMDb and MIMIC-IV, the best trees split on those scores alone (pp. 7, 26, 28–29).

**Quotes**:
- "This demonstrates the benefit of prescription over prediction in this setting." (p. 24)
- "This work takes a first step toward using prescriptive analytics to enhance predictive models, and we hope it encourages further research in this direction." (p. 31)

### S696 · Improving survival models in healthcare: a novel matching approach (Research Square preprint 2024, DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-5467577/v1)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–21. Read: the cover sheet and declarations (pp. 1–2), the abstract, §§1–4, Algorithm 1, Problems 1–3, Tables 1–6, the references and Tables S1–S2. Figures 1–4 have only captions and box labels. First-page check: the title and the authors match, with Bertsimas first of ten (pp. 1, 3). Posted 12 December 2024 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors** (p. 3): Catherine Ning (MIT ORC); the clinicians Per Eystein Lønning (Bergen), Hideo Baba (Kumamoto), Itaru Endo (Yokohama City), Richard Burkhart (Johns Hopkins), Federico N. Aucejo (Cleveland Clinic), Felix Balzer and Martin E. Kreis (Charité); and, last, Georgios Antonios Margonis (MIT ORC and Memorial Sloan Kettering) · **Position in trajectory**:
- The health programme of 2019–2026, within his partnership with Margonis on colorectal liver metastases (CRLM); their joint 2023 paper is cited (ref. [15], p. 19).
- The Jameel Clinic funds Bertsimas (p. 18).
- This is the first public version of the line published in 2026 as S620 (card in c39). S620 is retitled, has Ning as first and corresponding author, and uses eight strata and two external cohorts.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+AP (oncology prognosis); IO as the matching engine; DA as motivation}:
  - Practice: prognostic Cox models are trained on the whole observational cohort. A 2024 review found that all 71 CRLM models were trained this way (p. 4).
  - Treatment propensities unbalance treated and untreated patients, so a model "may disproportionately focus on densely populated areas of the data" (p. 4).
  - Entry, a transfer: matching is standard for estimating treatment effects, but its use for prognostication "has not been previously explored" (p. 4).
  - Problem, stated as two hypotheses (p. 4):
    - matching within prognostic strata;
    - a smaller, balanced cohort that improves accuracy.
- **D2 Core idea**: two stages (pp. 5–8).
  - Stage 1 (Algorithm 1, p. 6; pp. 7–8):
    - fit a Cox model on the untreated patients, estimate everyone's 5-year risk of death, and stratify both arms into risk deciles;
    - in each stratum, select patients by a minimum-distance matching integer program. Problem 1 is 1-1 matching with at least α pairs, which equalizes counts across strata. Problem 2 is an exact cover, used where the minority side has fewer than α patients. Problem 3 is a relaxed version with up to two matches per patient.
  - Stage 2: train separate Cox models on the matched untreated (3A) and treated (3B) patients, optionally duplicating or tripling the matched data (pp. 5–6).
  - What changes: matching, a tool of causal inference, now shapes a training set. The Cox learner is unchanged (pp. 4–5, 16).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Cohort: 1,799 of 2,375 patients with resected CRLM (1,197 with adjuvant chemotherapy, 602 without), 10 covariates chosen from the literature, and 5-year overall survival as the endpoint (pp. 3, 11).
  - Multiple imputation under missing-at-random (p. 10).
  - α = 23, the smallest stratum size of at least 20. Strata 3 and 10 use Problem 2, because stratum 3 holds a single untreated patient (p. 13).
  - The matched training sets have 124–156 patients (Table S2, p. 21), about one-sixth of the cohort (p. 16).
  - Admitted: only observed confounding is addressed (p. 17), although hypothesis 1 had claimed matching on "latent confounding factors" (p. 4).
  - [my reading] Age stays imbalanced after matching (p < 0.001, Table 2, p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Problems 1–3 minimize the summed standardized Euclidean distance. Each patient has at most one match (two in Problem 3), and each stratum a floor of α pairs (pp. 7–8).
  - They are solved by Gurobi 11.0.0 from Julia 1.9.4 on a MacBook M2 Pro. No solve times are given (p. 10).
  - [my reading, as in card S620] The problems are bipartite b-matchings with a cardinality floor, that is, min-cost flows with integral LP relaxations. The paper does not use this structure.
  - There is no theorem. The mechanism (equal attention across risk strata, rather than removal of confounding) is argued from the failure of PSM, not tested (p. 17).
  - Validation uses Efron's bias-corrected bootstrap, as set out by Harrell, with 100 resamples (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Baselines (pp. 5, 10):
    - Model 1: the whole cohort, with treatment added as a covariate "for fair comparison";
    - Models 2A/2B: one model per arm, unmatched;
    - PSM models: MatchIt, with a random-forest or GLM propensity score and a caliper of 0.2.
  - Metrics: Harrell's and Uno's C, the Brier score, the O/E ratio, ICI, E50, E90, Emax and the calibration slope (pp. 9–10).
  - Bias-corrected Harrell's C (Table 5, p. 15):

    | Model | Harrell's C |
    |---|---|
    | Model 1 | 0.6115 |
    | 3A 1-1 | 0.6535 |
    | 3B relaxed | 0.6747 |
    | 3B relaxed, tripled data | 0.6901 |

    The PSM models score about the same as the unmatched per-arm models.
  - Failures printed in the body: 3B relaxed calibrates worse than its competitors, and the text reports 3A 1-1's calibration as the best except for the calibration slope (p. 14).
  - [my arithmetic] Against Model 1, the largest bias-corrected gains are +12.9% (Harrell) and +15.1% (Uno), both with tripled data. Gains near 20% appear only against Model 2B, or in-sample against the row 1B (Tables 3 and 5, p. 15).
    - So the abstract's "up to 20% in bootstrapped C-indices compared to model 1" (p. 3) cannot be traced to any one cell.
    - Model 1 keeps the calibration slope closest to 1 (0.9226, Table 6, p. 16).
    - Some bias-corrected absolute-error indices are negative (3A 1-1 tripled, p. 16).
  - [my reading] The text does not say whether the matching was repeated inside the bootstrap, nor on which population each model was scored. The in-sample tables use "each model's respective training set" (p. 14).
    - If 3A/3B are scored on their risk-equalized 124–156 patients and Model 1 on all 1,799, the C comparison mixes model quality with case-mix spread.
    - Duplicated patients may also shrink the optimism estimate: the intervals for the duplicated and tripled data in Table 5 are narrower (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Claimed contribution: "the first methodological study" to improve the prognostic power of Cox models "through optimized data selection" (p. 3). It is summed up as "less is more" and offered for any observational data (p. 17).
  - Stated limits:
    - unobserved confounding is out of scope (p. 17);
    - the small matched sets hurt 3B's calibration, which oversampling remedies (p. 14);
    - future studies must validate the method (pp. 17–18).
  - Not stated [my reading]:
    - validation is internal only, by choice (p. 17);
    - one disease and one learner;
    - no decision analysis, although the choice of chemotherapy is the motivation (p. 17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To rebalance a training set across risk levels:
  - fit a baseline risk model on the control arm, and stratify both arms by its deciles;
  - within each stratum, select pairs by a minimum-distance matching IP with a floor α;
  - fall back to exact cover where the minority side has fewer than α patients (Algorithm 1, p. 6; pp. 7–8, 13).
- Keep isolating baselines that use the same learner: the whole-cohort model with treatment as a covariate, the unmatched per-arm models and the PSM-matched models (pp. 5, 14).
- Validate the development process with Efron's optimism bootstrap (100 resamples) instead of a single split. Report discrimination and calibration separately, including ICI, E50, E90 and the calibration slope (pp. 9–10, 17).
- [my reading] When the training subsets differ in case-mix, score every compared model on one common population, or per risk stratum, before comparing C-indices (Tables 3–6, pp. 14–16). S620 later reports per-stratum external results.
