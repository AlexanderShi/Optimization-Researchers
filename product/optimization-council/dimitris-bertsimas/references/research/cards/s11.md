### S200 · Data-driven learning in dynamic pricing using adaptive optimization (Optimization Online 2017, no DOI or arXiv id)
Read level: **partial**. Read:
- the abstract and §1 (pp. 1–8);
- §2 model and assumptions (pp. 9–12);
- §3 formulation (pp. 12–18);
- §4 Propositions 1–2 (pp. 18–20);
- §5 approximation scheme, statements and prose (pp. 20–30; formula blocks garbled);
- §7 baselines (pp. 34–35) and §8 experiments (pp. 35–42);
- e-companion EC.1 and the start of EC.2 (p. 46), and the duality and big-M steps of EC.3 (pp. 49–50).

§6 extensions (pp. 30–34) were skimmed by headings; the references (pp. 42–45) were not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Phebe Vayanos (MIT Sloan affiliation, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO entering revenue management with learning. The piecewise-constant decision rules follow Vayanos et al. (2011) (p. 25). Finite adaptability (Bertsimas–Caramanis) is set aside as unsuited to decision-dependent sets (Remark 9, p. 25).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + DA; AP: revenue management}: A retailer with finite inventory sells over a finite horizon to price-sensitive customers. The demand-curve parameters are fixed but unknown, and only a history of sales data is available (p. 1).
  - The entry: most studies assume a known demand curve, an assumption the paper calls unrealistic in most practical settings (p. 3). Bayesian formulations are intractable at realistic size and restrict priors to conjugate classes (p. 5). Robust pricing papers assume a known demand model, so there is "no opportunity for learning" (p. 6).
  - Two questions follow: how to model demand uncertainty from data, and how to price so as to balance exploitation and exploration (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Make learning a property of the uncertainty set.
  - The prior set contains every (α, β) not falsified by the history under a norm-bounded residual (eq. (4), p. 13). For p = 2 it is the least-squares confidence ellipsoid (p. 14). It is the 1960s set-membership estimator of system identification, extended into the future (Remark 4, p. 16).
  - The future price–demand paths form a policy-dependent set U(π) (eq. (5), p. 15). Exploration means prices that prune this set; exploitation means prices that are functions of the observed history (pp. 12, 16).
  - The problem DP maximizes worst-case profit over pricing policies (p. 18).
  - What changes from classical adaptive RO: there, decisions depend on primitive uncertainties that are observed or can be inferred. Here the residual cannot be inferred, because α and β are unknown (Remark 3, p. 13).
- **D3 Model and data**: Linear demand d_t = α + β p_t + ε_t with ‖ε‖_p ≤ η, p ∈ {1, 2, ∞}, and no distribution (p. 10).
  - Assumption (A1): the model is valid over the price range, with p and η known. Assumption (A2): the data-consistent set is bounded with a nonempty interior (p. 10).
  - The authors defend linearity over a moderate price interval (pp. 10–11) and recommend the 2-norm for residuals that look like a truncated normal. They admit that η is hard to set with little data: a large η is always valid but slows learning (p. 11).
  - Profit includes holding and backlog costs (eq. (1), p. 9). Discrete price sets are treated as the practical case (Remark 8, p. 24).
  - Data: two synthetic instances, each with four historical price–demand pairs (pp. 35–36).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Propositions 1–2 (p. 20). With zero holding and backlog costs and β ≤ 0, a static robust policy is optimal, by the minimax theorem (EC.1, p. 46). Otherwise exploration can pay, shown by a two-period example (EC.2, p. 46).
  - DP is called "severely computationally intractable" (p. 20). A hierarchy follows:
    - aggregate stages into M macro-periods with a reduced information vector, such as the least-squares estimates and cumulative demand (pp. 21–22);
    - choose among K candidate strategies, constant or randomized (pp. 23–24);
    - use binary rules that are piecewise constant on a preselected box partition (pp. 25–26).
  - Proposition 3 (pp. 28–29) turns this into a mixed-binary conic program. It is conservative for p ∈ [1, ∞] and exact for p ∈ {1, ∞}, by RO duality plus big-M linearization (EC.3, pp. 49–50). Its size is polynomial for a fixed number of partition cells |S| and exponential in M otherwise (p. 29). Remedies: breakpoints as binary-encoded decisions, and lifted, non-axis-aligned partitions (pp. 29–30).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: CPLEX 12.6 on a 2.66 GHz Core i7-920 with 24 GB (p. 35).
  - Learning gain over the static robust policy:
    - above 73% in under 3 s, with every frontier problem solved in under 10 s (pp. 37–39);
    - about −10% when |S| = 1 and M ≤ 2 (p. 37).
  - Out of sample, against the myopic policy: worst case +31.5% and mean +9.2%, but up to $223 less on some draws (p. 39).
  - Folding horizon, 500 draws, against the certainty-equivalent and myopic rules: worst-case loss down 76.6% and 92.8%, mean up 1.28% and 4.52% (pp. 40–41). The tail matches the perfect-information bound (p. 42).
  - No real data.
- **D6 Positioning**: Three literatures are reviewed and set aside: Bayesian learning, learning with classical estimation, and robust pricing without learning (pp. 4–6). The paper calls its own approach a data-driven, distribution-free paradigm (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing**: Numbered contributions (pp. 7–8). Four "to the best of our knowledge" priority claims (pp. 8, 15, 16, 29). No conclusion section: the paper ends with conclusions from the numerical experiments (p. 42).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions: a policy-dependent uncertainty set (modelling), a mixed-binary conic inner approximation (solution), and extensions to inventory and multi-product pricing (pp. 7–8, 30–34).
  - Stated limits: the approximation is conservative, and its size explodes in M when |S| is free (p. 29); η and the norm must be known (p. 10). Future work: finite adaptability with decision-dependent sets (Remark 9, p. 25).
  - Not shown: real data, or the gap to DP itself. Only static and perfect-information comparators are used (pp. 36–42).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 variant ⚠: A problem owned by Bayesian learning and dual control (pp. 4–5) is rewritten as adaptive RO and brought into the mixed-binary conic class. It gets there by a conservative approximation hierarchy, not by an exact class-keeping device. The paper prints where the approximation is exact (p ∈ {1, ∞}) and where its size explodes (pp. 28–29).
- Method 3 variant ⚠: The value of learning is priced as a "learning gain" and drawn as an efficient frontier against solver time over the design knobs M and |S|. The bad case (about −10%) is printed (pp. 37–38). The natural conservatism knob, η, is left to the seller (p. 11).
- Method 4 variant ⚠: The |S| = 1 solution warm-starts finer partitions, and times are reported cumulatively (p. 29; Figure 4 caption, p. 38). Solver, version and machine are stated (p. 35). There is no incumbent heuristic as warm start and no gap.
- Method 5 variant ⚠: Decisions are scored by profit distributions (mean, VaR, CVaR) against the two rules "commonly used in practice" and a perfect-information bound (pp. 34, 40–42). The instances are synthetic, with no owner.
- Heuristic 4 evidence ✅: the negative learning gain at small |S| and M, and the possible $223 loss against myopic pricing, are printed in the body (pp. 37, 39).
- New-pattern candidates:
  - *learning as a decision-dependent uncertainty set* (pp. 7–8, 15–16);
  - *approximation-knob frontier (price of tractability)* (pp. 37–39; also S117).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the uncertainty set as all parameters not falsified by the data under a norm-bounded residual. Prior and posterior sets then have the same conic form and always contain the true parameters, so updating keeps the class (pp. 7, 13–16).
- Before building adaptivity, prove when a static robust policy is already optimal (minimax theorem), and give a two-period counterexample for the other case (Propositions 1–2, p. 20; EC.1–EC.2, p. 46).
- Make an intractable adaptive problem solvable in steps:
  - aggregate stages, and summarize history by a few statistics;
  - choose among K candidate strategies;
  - warm-start finer partitions from coarser ones;
  - plot the gain–time efficient frontier (pp. 21–29, 38).
- Judge robust policies on the downside (worst case, VaR, CVaR) in a folding-horizon simulation. Compare against certainty-equivalent and myopic rules and against a perfect-information bound (pp. 40–42).

**Quotes**:
- "This construction enables us to unify optimization and (dynamic) estimation." (p. 7)
- "while the adaptive policy may yield up to $223 less than the myopic policy, it yields higher profits with probability greater than 81.6%" (p. 39)

### S225 · The trimmed lasso: Sparsity and robustness (arXiv preprint 2017, arXiv 1708.04527)
Read level: **partial**. Read:
- the abstract and §1 (pp. 1–7);
- §2.1–2.2 and §2.5 (pp. 8–10, 13);
- §3 (pp. 14–17);
- §4.1–4.2, and the §4.4 statements with Example 4.4 (pp. 17–23);
- §5 and §6 (pp. 25–32);
- Appendix C.3–C.4 (pp. 43–44).

§2.3–2.4, §4.3 and §4.5 were skimmed; the proofs in Appendices A–C (pp. 32–43) were not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Martin S. Copenhaver, Rahul Mazumder (Sloan School and Operations Research Center, MIT, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the robustness reading of sparse regression (Heuristic 1). It comes one year after best subset selection with Mazumder (SW4). Certification by MIO follows the group's earlier statistics papers [14, 16] (pp. 6, 28).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO; RO as the lens}: Separable nonconvex penalties (ℓq, MCP, SCAD) bridge the Lasso and best subset selection but give no direct control of sparsity (pp. 2–3). The trimmed Lasso T_k(β), the sum of the p − k smallest |β_i|, was introduced earlier, mainly as a reformulation tool (p. 3). This paper asks instead about its structural properties and its relation to existing penalties, from the viewpoints of sparsity and robustness (pp. 3, 7).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - T_k is an exact penalty. For λ large enough it solves constrained best subset selection, and k gives direct control of sparsity (Theorem 2.3, p. 9).
  - Robustness from one uncertainty set (at most k perturbed columns, each with norm at most λ):
    - min-max over this set gives SLOPE (Proposition 1.2, p. 6);
    - min-min over it gives the trimmed Lasso plus an ℓ1 term (Theorem 3.1 and Corollary 3.2, p. 15).
  - The Lasso is the min-max case with every column perturbed (Proposition 1.1, p. 5). The trimmed Lasso is that Lasso with k corruptions discarded, in the spirit of least trimmed squares (p. 17).
- **D3 Model and data**: Linear regression with least-squares loss, plus a small ηℓ1 term that makes the problems well-posed (p. 9, fn. 2). Section 4 compares bounded penalties of the form μ min{g(|β|), 1} (pp. 17–18) and treats unbounded ones (ℓq, log) as limits (pp. 23–24). Computational instance: n = 100, p = 20, k = 2, SNR = 10, Σ_ij = 0.8^|i−j|, ‖β_true‖0 = 10 (pp. 29, 43–44).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Formulations:
    - T_k has a binary representation (Lemma 2.1, p. 8). It gives a big-M linear MIO, but without a tuned M an SOS formulation is "vastly superior" at proving optimality (pp. 28–29).
    - A Scholtes-type MPCC relaxation leads to the same penalty (pp. 9–10).
  - Algorithms:
    - Algorithm 1 uses the DC split f = f1 − f2, which Theorem 3.1 makes natural. It converges in finitely many steps to a local minimum, at a linear rate (a result of [39]; pp. 25–26).
    - ADMM (Algorithm 2) has no convergence guarantee; its γ-step is solved in closed form by sorting (pp. 27–28; App. C.3, p. 43).
  - Convex envelope: the envelope of T_k is (‖β‖1 − k)+ (Lemma 5.3, p. 27). For large M, the LP relaxation of the big-M MIO is the ordinary Lasso (p. 29).
  - Comparison with separable penalties:
    - every clipped-Lasso optimum is optimal for some trimmed problem (Theorem 4.1, p. 19);
    - the converse holds if and only if a discrete convexity condition on optimal values holds (Theorem 4.2, p. 21);
    - a 2 × 2 example shows the containment is strict (Example 4.4, p. 23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - A "limited demonstration" (p. 29): alternating minimization, ADMM and the convex envelope against MIO (Gurobi, version not given, p. 44), by relative optimality gap, on 100 seeds at p = 20.
  - Result: the heuristics do well for small λ and break down as sparsity bites; ADMM is best and the envelope worst (pp. 29–31).
  - Not done: a runtime study, and out-of-sample or recovery tests (p. 31). Code is on GitHub (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends the earlier trimmed-Lasso work [39, 43, 69, 72] from computation to structure (p. 3). Separable penalties are shown to be subsumed, not attacked (p. 7). Robust statistics (Huber, LTS, total least squares) is set beside RO (pp. 3–5).
- **D7 Writing**: The robustness discussion opens with Huber's remark that "robust" is a loaded word (p. 4). Three numbered contributions (p. 7). Computational claims are hedged (p. 29).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Structural and algorithmic results (p. 7; §6, pp. 31–32). Stated limits: no statistical properties and no rigorous computational comparison (pp. 29, 31). Future directions: algorithms and statistical properties (p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅: Best subset selection is rewritten as an exact penalty with a binary representation and a certifying MIO (pp. 8–9, 28–29). The authors print the weakness of their own big-M model: its LP relaxation collapses to the Lasso, and SOS proves optimality faster (p. 29). This is Tension T4's formulation-strength point, stated by the authors themselves.
- Heuristic 1 evidence ✅ (full text): the robust problems behind the Lasso and SLOPE are identified (Propositions 1.1–1.2), and the trimmed Lasso is derived as their min-min analogue (pp. 5–6, 14–17).
- Method 4 variant ⚠: Heuristic upper bounds are paired with an MIO that "maintains a suboptimality gap" (pp. 28, 31). The duel is on the objective gap only, at p = 20, with no runtime or out-of-sample comparison (p. 31).
- New-pattern candidate: *min-max/min-min pairing*. From one uncertainty set, derive the pessimistic estimator (RO) and the optimistic one (robust statistics) as a complementary pair (pp. 7, 14–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To interpret a new penalty, find the uncertainty set whose min-max counterpart is a known convex penalty, then take the min-min counterpart of the same set (pp. 5–6, 14–15).
- To show that a penalty family is strictly richer:
  - rewrite separable bounded penalties as a minimum over trimmed problems (eq. (29), p. 18);
  - characterize the converse by a discrete convexity condition (Theorem 4.2, p. 21);
  - close with a tiny explicit counterexample (Example 4.4, p. 23).
- Compute the convex envelope to show what the LP relaxation of a big-M formulation keeps, and what it loses (Lemma 5.3, p. 27; p. 29).
- Report heuristic quality as geometric-mean relative gaps to MIO optima, across 100 seeds and a λ path, with every seed and parameter listed (pp. 29–31, 43–44).

**Quotes**:
- "This gives an interpretation of the SLOPE and trimmed Lasso as a complementary pair of penalties, one under a pessimistic (min-max) model and the other under an optimistic (min-min) model." (p. 7)
- "However, by design, the MIO approach maintains a suboptimality gap throughout computation and can therefore be terminated, before optimality is certified, with a certificate of suboptimality." (p. 31)

### S117 · Binary decision rules for multistage adaptive mixed-integer optimization (Mathematical Programming 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10107-017-1135-6)
Read level: **partial**. Read:
- the abstract and §1 (pp. 1–5);
- §2 statements, constructions and prose (pp. 5–20), with the proof of Proposition 4 (p. 13);
- §3–4 statements (pp. 20–27);
- §5 computations (pp. 27–33);
- §6 conclusions (p. 34).

The appendix proofs (pp. 34–39) were read only for structure: Lemma 1 (p. 34), and the conclusion drawn from Lemma 3 (p. 38).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Angelos Georghiou (Desautels Faculty of Management, McGill, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received 2 October 2014, accepted 16 March 2017 (p. 1). It is the scalable sequel to the authors' own near-optimal but slow binary rule (1.2) from [10] (pp. 3–4). It builds on Bertsimas–Caramanis [8] and on the lifting technique of Georghiou et al. [20] (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + IO; AP as testbeds}: Multistage adaptive problems with real and binary recourse (1.1) are #P-hard or otherwise intractable. The paper quotes Shapiro–Nemirovski on posing a modest, achievable goal (p. 2).
  - Real-valued decision rules are plentiful and scale (Table 1, p. 3). Binary rules are scarce, and each existing kind falls short: near-optimal but unscalable (1.2); randomized, with probabilistic guarantees only; or K-adaptable, limited to two stages (p. 3).
  - The goal is scalable binary rules that work alongside the real-valued ones (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Set y(ξ) = Y G(ξ), where G is a vector of indicators of hyperplanes and Y is integer, with 0 ≤ YG ≤ e (p. 6).
  - Lift the uncertainty to (ξ, G(ξ)), so the constraints become linear in the lifted vector (pp. 8–10).
  - Build the exact convex hull of the nonconvex lifted support from one-sided limit points at the partition vertices (Proposition 3, pp. 11–12).
  - Dualize (Proposition 4, p. 13). The resulting MILP (2.20) yields the best binary rule for the chosen G (p. 13).
  - What changes from (1.2): the structure is fixed in advance and only the shape is optimized, which buys scalability (p. 34).
- **D3 Model and data**: An expected-cost objective, with constraints enforced for every ξ in a polyhedral support with ξ1 = 1 (pp. 2, 5). The second-moment matrix comes analytically or by offline Monte Carlo (p. 8).
  - Cases: fixed recourse (§2); random recourse linear in ξ (§3, p. 20); multistage with non-anticipativity (§4, pp. 23–24). The tractable case is a box set with axis-aligned breakpoints (pp. 14–17); general polyhedra are handled conservatively (pp. 18–20).
  - Data: 30 random instances per setting. One problem is inventory control (worst-case cost, discrete lots), the other a knapsack (Beta-distributed weights, expected objective) (pp. 27–28, 31–32).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Theorem 1: the problem is NP-hard even with Y relaxed to real values (p. 10). The proof goes through an epsilon integer feasibility problem (Lemmas 1–3, pp. 34–38).
  - Size: the exact hull can grow exponentially (p. 13). For boxes it needs 2 + k(4r + 4) constraints (p. 17), and restricting Y to {−1, 0, 1} loses nothing (Proposition 6, pp. 17–18).
  - Random recourse has the same complexity (Proposition 7, pp. 22–23). In the multistage case the exponential growth in T is implicit (p. 26).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: CPLEX 12.5 on a 3.40 GHz Core i5-3570 with 8 GB (pp. 4, 27).
  - Inventory:
    - The rules beat static binary decisions by 15–20%, reported at global, 1% and 5% gaps. Solving to global optimality is impractical for T > 20 and r > 9 (Table 2, pp. 28–29).
    - Against the flexible rule (1.2), the new rules are 19–60% worse at r = 0 and 1–30% worse at r = 9. They solve in seconds (at most 5 s), while (1.2) takes up to 16,613 s (Table 3, p. 29).
    - Gains saturate for r > 3. Average times reach 19,298 s at N = 4, r = 5, T = 50 (Table 5, p. 31).
  - Knapsack: gains of 26–36%. Partial-information rules reach T = 25 with 1,300 binary rules, at up to 11,349 s (Table 6, p. 33).
- **D6 Positioning**: Presented as a complement to real-valued rules, and as the scalable alternative to the group's own rule (1.2) (pp. 3–4). The conclusion weighs the two at length (p. 34).
- **D7 Writing**: Theorem 1 is called "a rather disappointing result" (p. 10). The loss against (1.2) is stated in a table caption (p. 29).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions: an exact MILP reformulation, a complexity result, polynomial structures, random recourse, and computations (pp. 4, 34).
  - Stated limits: NP-hard in general; the exact size can be exponential (p. 13); only conservative for general polyhedra (pp. 19–20); weaker than (1.2) (p. 29); the enriched basis needs expensive solves (p. 30).
  - Headline against table: the conclusion's "horizons ≤ 50, ∼5 min" (p. 34) holds for r ≤ 3 (357 s at N = 4, r = 3, T = 50). It does not hold for r = 5, at 19,298 s (Table 5, p. 31).
  - No future-work list.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅: A class-keeping device (lifting, exact convex hull, LP duality) keeps discontinuous binary decision rules in the MILP class. The boundary is printed: NP-hard in general; exponential exact size; polynomial for boxes; conservative for general polyhedra (pp. 9–20).
- Method 3 variant ⚠: Optimality is traded for scalability through three knobs: the breakpoints r, the information a rule may use, and the termination gap. Tables report the gain against time, the saturation point (r > 3) and the bad case against (1.2) (pp. 28–33). The priced property is tractability, not a property the decision-maker wants.
- Method 4 variant ⚠: Results at 1% and 5% gaps are shown next to global optima (p. 28). The duel is with the group's own earlier rule, which wins on quality and loses on time (Table 3, p. 29). There is no out-of-sample test.
- Heuristic 4 evidence ✅: the loss against (1.2) and the impracticality thresholds are printed in the body (p. 29).
- Tension T6 evidence: the conclusion's "∼5 min" for horizons up to 50 (p. 34) does not hold for r = 5 in Table 5 (p. 31).
- New-pattern candidate: *approximation-knob frontier (price of tractability)* (Tables 2–3 and 5–6, pp. 28–33; also S200).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Lift and hull. To keep indicator-based decision rules linear:
  - lift ξ to (ξ, G(ξ));
  - take the convex hull of the lifted support from one-sided limits at the partition vertices;
  - dualize (pp. 8–13).
- With box sets and axis-aligned breakpoints, use separability. The hull is a product of marginal hulls, and the integer coefficients can be restricted to {−1, 0, 1}. In the tests this gives the same optima much faster (pp. 14–18, 29).
- Solve the flexible but slow rule on a short horizon and read off its hyperplane directions. Use them to enrich the scalable rule's basis on longer horizons (pp. 29–30).
- Restrict what a rule may observe to partial information (ξ_s, ξ_t). This cuts integer variables from s(r + 1) to 2(r + 1); report the quality cost in a table (p. 33).

**Quotes**:
- "Theorem 1 provides a rather disappointing result on the complexity of Problems (2.10) and (2.14)." (p. 10)
- "The proposed decision rule problems have great scalability properties but do not perform as well compared to binary decision rules of type (1.2)" (p. 29)

### S260 · Optimization over continuous and multi-dimensional decisions with observational data (NeurIPS 2018, arXiv 1807.04183)
Read level: **full** for the main text (pp. 1–9). Appendices read: the proof of Theorem 1 (pp. 10–12), App. B (pp. 14–15) and App. D (p. 16). Not read: the proof of Example 1 (pp. 12–14) and App. C, data generation (pp. 15–16).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Christopher McCord (Operations Research Center, MIT, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a step in the prescriptive-analytics line, after Bertsimas–Kallus [4, 5] and optimal prescriptive trees [3] (pp. 2–3). McCord is listed in the skill's DA lineage.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML; AP: dosing, pricing}: Minimize a known cost of an outcome whose distribution depends on the decision, given only observational data (X_i, Z_i, Y_i). The data are incomplete (no counterfactuals) and confounded (pp. 1–2).
  - Gaps named in earlier work:
    - earlier prescriptive methods assume the decision does not affect the outcome (p. 2);
    - the earlier kernel method does not scale with the dimension of the decision (p. 2);
    - the MIO for tree ensembles ignores uncertainty (p. 2);
    - policy learning mostly treats finite treatments (p. 3).
  - The entry is a flaw of predict-then-optimize. It "tries to learn too much", and can pick a decision with a small but highly uncertain predicted cost (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Choose z to minimize μ̂(x, z) + λ1√V(x, z) + λ2 B(x, z) (eq. (2), p. 4).
  - μ̂ is a weight-based prediction (trees, forests, linear models), V its conditional variance, and B a bound on its bias under a Lipschitz assumption (pp. 3–4).
  - It is a lower-confidence-bound rule for offline exploitation, the reverse of UCB (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions:
    - ignorability (Assumption 1, p. 2);
    - honest weights (Assumption 2, p. 3);
    - weights in [0, 1/γ_n], Lipschitz within each of Γ_n regions (Assumption 3, p. 4);
    - a bounded domain and a bounded, Lipschitz cost (Assumptions 4–5, p. 5).
  - Homoscedasticity is assumed when the variances are unknown (p. 4).
  - Data:
    - synthetic pricing, with z ∈ R^5 (p. 7);
    - semi-synthetic Warfarin data: 5,410 patients with known optimal stable doses, 99 covariates, and a simulated physician policy based on BMI (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Theorem 1, a uniform generalization bound (p. 5). The proof uses Bernstein's inequality conditional on the design (honesty), then Jensen's inequality plus the Lipschitz assumption for the bias, then a ν-net in each region and a union bound (pp. 11–12).
  - Theorem 2: regret depends only on the variance and bias at the optimal action (p. 5). Corollary 1 gives asymptotic optimality (p. 6). In Example 1, the penalized method's regret is asymptotically strictly below that of predicted-cost minimization (p. 6).
  - Tractability:
    - trees: one convex problem per leaf, solved in parallel;
    - linear models: an SOCP, which Gurobi handles even with a mixed-integer decision set (pp. 6, 15);
    - ensembles: a coordinate-descent heuristic (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Pricing: 100 training sets per sample size n. At n = 2000, UP-RF raises expected revenue by $270 over RF (Wilcoxon p = 4.4 × 10^−18); CART and Lasso fall below the plotted range (p. 7).
  - Warfarin: a fixed 4,000/1,410 split, 100 randomizations, and dose MSE against the true optimal dose. Penalization improves CART, RF and Lasso by 2.2%, 8.6% and 0.5% (Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon) (pp. 8–9).
    - Comparators: a constant 35 mg/week rule; CRM given the true propensities, which "always performed poorly"; and an unattainable lower bound (p. 8).
  - λ sensitivity is checked in App. D (p. 16). No run times appear in the parts read.
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Bertsimas–Kallus, Kao et al., Elmachtoub–Grigas and Mišić (p. 2). Against continuous-treatment policy learning (Kallus–Zhou), it prefers regression, which the authors believe scales better and avoids density estimation (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**: Theory motivates the penalties (pp. 4–5). The conclusion restates the contributions and names no limits or future work (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: a framework, finite-sample bounds, asymptotic optimality and tractability (p. 9). Simplifications admitted: homoscedasticity (p. 4), and λ2 = 0 for regularized linear models "for computational reasons" (p. 15). Assumed but not validated: ignorability, and the simulated physician policy (pp. 2, 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 evidence ✅: Learning and deciding happen in one model, and decisions are scored on decision metrics: revenue, and dose error against the true optimal dose. The baselines are unpenalized predict-then-optimize, a constant-dose rule and an unattainable bound (pp. 7–8). The variance and bias penalties (p. 4) anticipate the skill's Method 5 checkpoint, where ML weights turn optimistic and a robust layer is added. Variant aspect: there is no owner's rule, and the historical policy is simulated (p. 8).
- Method 1 evidence ✅: The learner class is chosen so that the prescriptive problem stays tractable: per-leaf convex problems for trees, an SOCP for linear models (pp. 6, 15).
- Method 6 evidence ✅ (lineage only): the DA programme continues with a new student after Bertsimas–Kallus (pp. 2–3).
- New-pattern candidate: *pessimistic prescription*. Penalize the variance and bias of a learned cost, so that regret depends only on the uncertainty at the optimal action (pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the learner's prediction as Σ_i w_i(x, z) c(z; Y_i). This gives closed-form variance and bias proxies to penalize (pp. 3–4).
- Pair a regret bound with a toy example in which the naive method's regret is asymptotically worse (Theorem 2 and Example 1, pp. 5–6).
- Build a semi-synthetic observational benchmark from data with known optimal decisions:
  - simulate a historical policy;
  - fix the split in advance "to prevent cheating";
  - test across 100 randomizations with Bonferroni correction (pp. 8–9).
- Tune the penalties without counterfactuals by imputing outcomes on a validation set with a random forest (pp. 4–5).

**Quotes**:
- "A key issue with the direct approach is that it tries to learn too much." (p. 2)
- "Because the predicted cost is penalized by V (x, z) and B(x, z), it does not matter how poor the prediction of cost is at suboptimal actions." (p. 5)

### S268 · Robust transient analysis of multi-server queueing systems and feed-forward networks (Queueing Systems 2018, DOI 10.1007/s11134-017-9566-6)
Read level: **partial**, of the manuscript version headed "Submitted to Operations Research" and titled "Robust Transient Multi-Server Queues and Feedforward Networks" (p. 1). It has the same authors and the same work. The Queueing Systems print was not read; all pages below are manuscript pages. Read:
- the abstract and §1 (pp. 1–5);
- §2 (pp. 5–10);
- §3 statements, and §3.2 averaging with its results (pp. 10–21);
- §4 statements, and §4.2 results (pp. 21–32);
- §5 (pp. 32–36);
- §6 statements and algorithm (pp. 36–40), and §7 (p. 40).

The appendix proofs (pp. 45–51) were read only for method (induction, p. 45).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chaithanya Bandi (Kellogg, Northwestern), Nataly Youssef (Operations Research Center, MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the transient sequel to the authors' steady-state robust queueing (Bandi et al. 2012) (p. 4). It presents itself as part of "a broader investigation" of stochastic systems via RO (Bandi and Bertsimas 2013) (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + RO; AP: call centres, data centres, cloud computing}: Applications with heavy tails and long transients never reach steady state (p. 2). Yet probabilistic transient analysis is "by and large analytically intractable" (p. 2), and a tractable transient theory of G/G/m queues is lacking (Odoni–Roth, p. 3). Earlier SDP bounds miss heavy tails and initial buffers (p. 3). The entry: carry the authors' robust steady-state framework to transient, multi-server, tandem and feed-forward settings (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace distributions by polyhedral sets on normalized partial sums of interarrival and service times, shaped by the generalized CLT. The parameters (γ, Γ) set conservatism, and α ∈ (1, 2] sets the tail (pp. 5–7).
  - The worst-case system time then follows in closed form (Theorems 2–4, pp. 9–14).
  - (γ, Γ) are then treated as random variables (normal or stable), with Γ = θγ, and the worst case is averaged by a double integral (pp. 9–10, 15–18).
  - The averaging is presented as breaking new ground (pp. 1, 4).
- **D3 Model and data**: FCFS queues, ρ < 1, an initial buffer n0 ≥ 0 (p. 10). The Assumption 1 sets build multi-server service sets on blocks of m jobs (p. 7). For Γ_m < 0 the paper gives only an upper bound on the worst case (pp. 11–12).
  - Tractability choices admitted:
    - Γ is made linear in γ, because its exact distribution is hard to characterize (p. 17);
    - θ is tuned to match Kingman's bound for light tails, and heavy tails get an extension of that formula (pp. 17–18);
    - tandems use a GEV fit to the aggregated service parameter (Table 3, p. 28).
  - The worst-case steady state under heavy tails comes from the truncated tails that the sets imply (pp. 34–35).
  - Data: simulation only.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Recursions: Lindley's for one server (p. 5), Krivulin's for multi-server queues (p. 11).
  - Proposition 1: the worst case equals the queue without overtaking (p. 11; App. A3, p. 47). Closed forms come from a one-dimensional optimization (p. 12).
  - Tandem results are proved by induction (App. A1, p. 45). Feed-forward networks are combinations of tandem paths, with a three-step algorithm (Propositions 2–3, pp. 36–40).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Measure: average percent error against simulated expected system times, with normal and Pareto primitives, ρ = 0.95–0.99, 1, 10 and 20 servers, and several n0 (Tables 1–2, pp. 18–20).
  - Single queues: errors are within 10% for light tails. For heavy tails they grow with the number of servers, up to 15% and 30% (p. 21).
  - Tandems of J = 10, 25 and 50: errors mostly within 10% "with occasional outliers" (Tables 4–5, pp. 30–32). Multi-server Pareto tandems are omitted because simulating them is costly (p. 32).
  - Speed: milliseconds per evaluation at a discretization of a thousand (p. 35).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against transform and numerical traditions that yield no general insight (pp. 2–3). Results are checked against Kingman (p. 9) and Morse (1955) (p. 34), and agree with probabilistic conclusions for heavy tails (p. 35). The reference list (pp. 41–44) contains no Whitt–You or Goldberg–Li robust-queueing paper.
- **D7 Writing**: "Break new ground(s)" claims (pp. 1, 4, 6). A [stated-joint] rationale: RO is a natural framework for stochastic systems, tied to the rise of commercial solvers (p. 4). A practitioner-facing "robust relaxation time" (pp. 32–34).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions: closed forms with insights, an averaging algorithm, and tandem and feed-forward extensions (pp. 4, 40).
  - Limits as printed: heavy-tailed multi-server errors up to 30% (p. 21); an omitted case (p. 32); θ for heavy tails set by an ad hoc extension, not by matching (pp. 17–18).
  - No future-work list (p. 40).
  - Context: critics later named choosing the sets, and tying them to the real queue, as open challenges [05 §4.5]. Here that tie is the θ calibration.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 variant ⚠: A problem owned by probabilistic queueing theory is rewritten as RO over polyhedral sets. The output is closed forms, not an LP or MIO. The boundary is partly printed: only an upper bound when Γ_m < 0 (p. 11), and a heavy-tailed steady state that is an artefact of the sets (pp. 34–35).
- Method 3 variant ⚠: Γ is the familiar one-knob conservatism (pp. 1, 7). Here it is integrated out under a distribution taken from limit laws and calibrated to Kingman (pp. 15–18), instead of being handed over as a price curve. This is one answer to the correction "set the knob from data", here from theory.
- Method 6 evidence ✅: A programme step with the same two co-authors (p. 4). The manuscript is headed for Operations Research (p. 1); the index lists Queueing Systems 2018.
- Tension T3 evidence: RO is claimed as the natural framework for stochastic systems (p. 4). Yet the sets come from the generalized CLT (p. 6), the parameters get probability distributions (pp. 15–17), and the results are reconciled with probabilistic theory (p. 35).
- Heuristic 4 evidence ✅: the 30% errors and the omitted case are printed in the body (pp. 21, 32).
- New-pattern candidate: *average the worst case over the conservatism knob* (pp. 9–10, 15–18, 40).

**Transferable techniques**:
- CLT-shaped polyhedral sets: bound the normalized partial sums, (Σ_{i=k+1}^{n} T_i − (n−k)/λ)/(n−k)^{1/α} ≥ −Γ, for all k, with α < 2 for heavy tails (pp. 6–7).
- Calibrate the knob's distribution so that the averaged worst case matches a known asymptotic, here Kingman's heavy-traffic bound (p. 17).
- Collapse a (J + 1)-dimensional integral to a double integral by fitting an extreme-value distribution to the sampled aggregate parameter (p. 28).
- Report accuracy against simulation in grids over ρ, the number of servers, n0 and the tail index, including where the method degrades (pp. 19–21, 30–32).
