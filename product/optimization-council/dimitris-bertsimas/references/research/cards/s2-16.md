### D020 · Tensor completion with noisy side information (Mach. Learn. 2023, DOI 10.1007/s10994-023-06338-5)
Read level: **full** (preprint version): pp. 1–31 (abstract, §§1–6, Appendices A–C, references). The file is the 2019 preprint; cite it as [D020 preprint p. N]. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Pawlowski (both MIT Sloan and ORC) match. The page carries a JMLR template header dated 2019 with "Editor: TBD" (p. 1), so this is a preprint, not the Machine Learning print.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Colin Pawlowski (MIT Sloan and ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: health machine learning from the ML-lens period. It cites the group's interpretable matrix completion (Bertsimas and Li, arXiv 2018, p. 3; carded as S383) and extends Farias and Li's slice-rank tensor model (p. 4). Four years separate the 2019 preprint from the 2023 print.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; AP (health) as testbed}: The task is to fill in a 3-way tensor (patients × drugs × doses) when noisy features of rows and columns are also available (pp. 1–2). Two gaps are named:
  - tensor methods that use side information are "computationally intensive and difficult to implement" (Narita et al.; Rai et al.'s Bayesian model with many tuning parameters, p. 4);
  - drug-response studies first reduce each (patient, drug) pair to a sensitivity value from a fitted model and only then complete a matrix. This paper models the raw dose-level data instead (pp. 4–5).

  The stated aim is to "prescribe the best anti-cancer treatment for each individual" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Each slice is modelled additively (Table 1, p. 8; eqs. 10, 15–16, pp. 12, 16):
  - a low slice-rank part U S^k V^T shared across slices;
  - a ridge-regularized regression part, X W^k (one-sided) or X W^k Y^T (two-sided).

  The model nests both baselines: W = 0 gives the tensor model, U, S, V = 0 the regression, and γ → 0 problem (8) (pp. 13–14). The algorithm alternates three steps and repeats them each pass (Algorithms 1–5, pp. 9–10, 13, 15, 27):
  - Farias–Li Slice Learning on the residual tensor (truncated SVDs of the mode-1 and mode-2 unfoldings, then S^k = U^T R^k V);
  - Nesterov-accelerated gradient steps on the regression weights;
  - refilling the missing entries.
- **D3 Model and data**: The model assumes slice rank at most r, with latent features constant across slices (pp. 11, 25). The authors "avoid making any strong assumptions" about how X, Y and Z relate (p. 2). They admit one tractability choice: a Frobenius penalty instead of a nuclear norm or a low-rank W, because it reuses the same solver (p. 16). Data:
  - synthetic 200 × 200 × 10 tensors of slice rank 20, with X = U + noise, Y = V + noise and 80% of entries missing completely at random (p. 17);
  - GDSC: 955 "patients" (cell lines, p. 2), 265 drugs, 12 doses and 2,004 genomic features;
  - CCLE: 461 × 24 × 8, with 2,036 features (p. 19).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Problem (8) is nonconvex and its global optimum is not computed (p. 12). The algorithms return "locally optimal" solutions (pp. 13, 15). Convergence rests on one sentence: each step decreases the objective, so a stationary point is reached (p. 14). My reading: the truncated-SVD step is not shown to minimize (12), so the descent claim is asserted, not proved. There is no theorem and no recovery guarantee.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Protocol: out-of-sample R² averaged over slices, against a slice-mean baseline, over 5 seeds. The rank r is tuned first with W = 0 held fixed, then γ (pp. 18, 20–21).
  - Simulated: at σ = 1, R² is 0.957 for TensorTwoSided, 0.933 for Tensor and 0.298 for TwoSided; at σ = 0 it is 0.997, 0.933 and 0.988 (p. 18).
  - Real data, with 20–80% of the observed values hidden. Baselines: piecewise-linear interpolation (also the warm start), the NLME dose–response model of the original GDSC paper, per-dose SoftImpute, Tensor and Genomic (p. 20).
  - GDSC: Tensor and TensorGenomic tie at 20–60% missing, and TensorGenomic wins at 80%. CCLE: TensorGenomic beats Tensor at every level and ties Genomic at 80%. NLME's R² turns negative at high missingness (p. 21).
  - Not reported: run times (despite "scales to large data sets", p. 1), software, and error bars in the text.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The paper offers two models, two alternating-minimization algorithms, and simulated and real evaluations (p. 5). The abstract says TensorGenomic "outperforms all state-of-the-art methods" (p. 1). The body calls it "equally the best" with Tensor at 20–60% missing on GDSC (p. 21). The conclusion states no limitations and no future work (pp. 23–24). The discussion suggests that earlier chemotherapy rounds may be the best predictor of response (p. 23). That remains a hypothesis: the study scores imputation, not treatment choices.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 21, 23): the motivation is a decision (drug and dose per patient, p. 2), and the practice model is a baseline (NLME, "used in practice", p. 23). But the score is imputation R², not a decision metric. This matches the SKILL's warning sign 4, "prediction accuracy with no decision value", softened by the clinical discussion (p. 23).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 12, 20–21): the warm start (piecewise-linear imputation) and the duel with the practice model (NLME) are present. There is no exact solve or certificate, and the paper says so (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: *isolate the gain by matching everything but the new ingredient* (pp. 13–14, 18, 20–21): both baselines are special cases of the model, and the rank is tuned with W = 0 first. Tensor and TensorGenomic therefore share r (p. 21), and the difference measures the side information alone.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the new model additively so that each baseline is a special case (W = 0; U, S, V = 0; γ → 0). The results table then isolates the new ingredient (pp. 13–14).
- Tune sequentially. First fix the shared hyperparameter (rank) with the new block switched off, then tune the new block's regularization, so baseline and proposal share one backbone (pp. 18, 20).
- Put the field's working model in the comparison (here the NLME dose–response fit of the original data paper) and show where it breaks: R² < 0 at high missingness (p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "In this work, we avoid the dependence upon intermediate models by casting this as a tensor completion problem instead of a matrix completion problem." (p. 5)
- "Unlike the previous one-sided and two-sided regression formulations, problem (8) is nonconvex, so we cannot compute the global optimal solution." (p. 12)

### H003 · Interpretable Prescriptive Neural Networks (submitted to Machine Learning 2023, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–38 (abstract, §§1–6, declarations, references, Appendices A–B). The appendix tree figures on pp. 31–38 survive only as captions in the text layer. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Everest and Stoumpou (all MIT ORC) match (p. 1). The work-list note records a homepage entry, "submitted to Machine Learning, 2023". The file is that manuscript, without journal header or date.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Lisa Everest, Vasiliki Stoumpou (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Voice**: per the contribution statement, Bertsimas "inspired, supported, and supervised the presented work and edited the paper"; the two students wrote the code, ran the experiments and co-wrote the text (p. 28) · **Position in trajectory**: the prescriptive-analytics programme (SW5) after optimal prescriptive trees and optimal policy trees, which serve here as baselines (pp. 3–4, 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML; AP (health, pricing) as testbeds}: Most ML work targets prediction accuracy; the paper wants to move "from prediction to prescription" (p. 2). It names a shortcoming in each alternative (pp. 4–5):
  - Regress & Compare trains one model per treatment, which thins the data, and it is a black box;
  - optimal prescriptive trees cannot learn complex outcome functions beyond small depth;
  - fully optimal policy trees scale poorly;
  - causal methods estimate effects, not explicit policies.
- **D2 Core idea**: Three steps (p. 5):
  1. Estimate a counterfactual reward matrix Γ by the direct method, propensity scores or the doubly robust estimator (eq. 1, p. 6). Continuous doses are discretized (p. 7).
  2. Train a feedforward network with one output per treatment to minimize Σ_i Σ_t P[τ(x_i) = t] Γ_{i,t}. This is a softmax relaxation of the indicator objective, trained with Adam (eqs. 2–4, pp. 7–8).
  3. Fit an Optimal Classification Tree to the network's prescriptions (the OCT-PNN) to recover interpretability (p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: The data are observational, {(x_i, y_i, t_i)}, with unknown counterfactuals (p. 2). No identification assumptions (unconfoundedness, overlap) are stated. Counterfactuals are estimated separately on the training and test halves of a 50/50 split (p. 10).
  - Synthetic (after Amram, Dunn and Zhuo): d = 10, 25,000 training and 50,000 test points, 5 data sets × 5 models, outcome noise N(0, 2.0) (pp. 11–13).
  - Real: diabetes records of 58,200 patients at Boston Medical Center; 97,295 strawberry purchases from dunnhumby's "The Complete Journey"; 35,954 splenic-injury patients; 9,998 REBOA patients (pp. 20–23).
  - Three of the four real data sets are confidential, and the code is "available upon request" (pp. 27–28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a beyond the softmax relaxation of a piecewise-constant policy objective (p. 8). There is no theorem, and the OCT step is ordinary supervised learning on the network's labels (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Synthetic scores are accuracy and mean regret against the true optimal treatment, mean (± s.d.) over 25 models (eq. 8, p. 13). PNN and OCT-PNN do not always win:
  - optimal policy trees and causal forests lead binary experiment A (98.29% and 97.96%, against 95.76% for PNN; Table 3, p. 14);
  - Regress & Compare leads both multiple-discrete experiments (Table 5, p. 15);
  - optimal policy trees lead multiple-continuous experiment A (Table 10, p. 18).

  On real data, the best of 5 PNNs per split is chosen on a 15% validation set (p. 19). Each method is scored by its mean estimated outcome under Γ and by its improvement over the actual outcomes (eqs. 16–17, pp. 19–20). Improvement over actual outcomes, PNN against the best non-PNN method:
  - diabetes: 2.52% against 1.94% for Regress & Compare (Table 11, p. 21);
  - groceries: 113.77% against 104.17% (Table 12, p. 22);
  - spleen: 20.15% against 17.09% (Table 13, p. 23);
  - REBOA: 9.67% against 7.07% (Table 14, p. 24).

  By my arithmetic these gaps average 3.96 points, which is the abstract's "4% on average". OCT-PNN falls 0.83 points short of PNN on average (p. 1). The group's own optimal prescriptive trees raise mortality in REBOA (−61.10%, p. 24). My readings:
  - Eq. (17) puts an absolute value in the numerator, yet the tables report negative improvements (pp. 20–24).
  - The diabetes text claims PNNs win "by roughly 1% or more" (p. 20). Table 11 shows a 0.58-point gap over Regress & Compare, with overlapping s.d.
  - PNN is trained on, and scored by, rewards estimated with the same method, so the real-data gains are never checked against observed outcomes.

  No software, run times or hardware are reported.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The paper calls PNN and OCT-PNN "tractable, flexible, and strongly performing", with interpretability recovered (pp. 1, 26–27). The conclusion adds two claims that no experiment measures: no "large computational resources" are needed, and performance is "unaffected by dataset size" (p. 27). No limitations or future work are stated (pp. 26–27).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 24–26): the price of interpretability is one number (0.83 points, OCT-PNN against PNN). No depth–performance curve is drawn, and the depth-4/5 trees shown for reading are single runs (pp. 24–26).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13, 19–20): decisions are the object, and the observed ("Actual") outcome is the real-data baseline. But the prescriptions are scored on estimated counterfactuals, without a field test, and the paper does not say what this evaluation cannot show (step 6).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13–24): there is a duel with the incumbents (causal forests, Regress & Compare and the group's own two tree methods), and the negative synthetic cases are printed. There is no certificate, solver or time limit for the OCT step, and no statistical test behind "significantly" (p. 20).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3–4, 13, 28): new students extend the programme line ([BK20] → [BDM19] → [ADZ22] → PNN), with the group's earlier methods as baselines and Bertsimas as supervisor and editor.
- New-pattern candidate: *distil the black box into an optimal tree* (pp. 8, 19–26): train the flexible model on the decision objective, fit an OCT to its outputs as class labels, and report the tree's fidelity (93–97% training accuracy on the PNN prescriptions, pp. 20, 22–24) together with its price.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Relax the indicator objective Σ 1{τ(x) = t} Γ_{i,t} with a softmax over the treatment outputs. Any classifier architecture can then train directly on decision cost (pp. 7–8).
- Report the student tree's fidelity to its teacher (training accuracy on the teacher's labels) next to its decision performance (pp. 20, 22–24).
- Re-estimate counterfactuals separately on the test half to avoid leakage, with a split that leaves each half enough data for the estimator (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Furthermore, OCT-PNNs perform almost just as strongly as their PNN counterparts on which they are trained, reducing improvement by only 0.83% on average compared to the PNNs." (p. 1)
- "Dimitris Bertsimas inspired, supported, and supervised the presented work and edited the paper." (p. 28)

### H004 · Probabilistic Robust Optimization (submitted to Operations Research 2023, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–27 (abstract, §§1–7, Theorems 1–4 with proofs, Appendix A formulation; the Appendix B figures survive only as axis labels). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas (Sloan) and Benjamin Boucher (ORC) match (p. 1). The file has no journal header or date. The work-list note records the homepage entry "submitted to Operations Research, 2023".
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Benjamin Boucher (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the data-driven uncertainty-set line of the RO programme. It builds on Bandi and Bertsimas's entropy-based typical-set sets (p. 4) and Bertsimas, Gupta and Kallus's data-driven RO (p. 2), and cites the 2022 RO book with den Hertog (pp. 1, 3, 9). The case is Hartford Hospital's Bone and Joint Institute (p. 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO; DA (data-driven sets); AP (health) as testbed}: Norm-based sets standardize by mean and variance and "were proposed at a time when data was not necessarily plentiful" (p. 1). They misjudge uniform, long-tailed, moment-free (Cauchy), same-moment and discrete uncertainties (pp. 3–4). DRO protects in expectation, so single outcomes can be badly infeasible (p. 1). Data-driven sets that bound the probability of violation leave its size uncontrolled (p. 2). The entry point is applications that tolerate frequent small violations but not large ones: a robustified objective, or a hospital's maximum bed census (pp. 2, 17).
- **D2 Core idea**: Budget the information content of tail events: U_PRO = {z : −(1/m) Σ log P(z̃_i ≥ z_i) ≤ Γ} (eq. 1, pp. 2, 4).
  - The set uses reliability functions rather than densities, after cumulative residual entropy (pp. 4–5).
  - Under independence it equals {z : P(z̃ ≥ z) ≥ e^{−mΓ}} (p. 5).
  - Weibull marginals recover the classical sets: exponential gives ℓ1, Rayleigh the ball, Dirac the box (p. 5).

  The constraint function g must be non-decreasing in z; transformations cover other cases (pp. 1, 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: Assumptions:
  - independent uncertain parameters for Theorems 1–2 (pp. 6, 8), each with a density (p. 6);
  - log-concave reliability functions, which make the set convex and, the authors argue citing Bagnoli and Bergstrom, cover most distributions (p. 9);
  - correlations only sketched, through joint reliability functions (p. 5).

  Synthetic data: n = 10 parameters, seven families plus Cauchy, N_train ∈ {30, 100, 300, 1,000}, N_test = 10,000, 100 trials (pp. 15–16). Hospital data: the BJI from January 2020 to April 2022, with 10 operating blocks, 28 providers and 9 resource types. Rest-time distributions come from the 12 prior months. Of 16 monthly timetables, the first 10 tune the parameter and the last 6 test it (pp. 20–21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 1: P(z̃ ∈ U_PRO) equals the Erlang(m) CDF at mΓ, whatever the distribution. The device is the change of variables t_i = −log S_i(z_i), which makes each coordinate Exp(1) (pp. 6–7). The bound is tight for exponential data and a linear constraint (pp. 7–8).
  - Theorem 2: a mean-violation bound for linear constraints, under independence, nonnegative support and log-concavity. The proof combines the scaling inclusion {−Σ log S_i ≤ nΓu} ⊂ u·U_PRO with Theorem 1's tail (pp. 8–9).
  - Theorem 3: a convex robust counterpart by Fenchel duality, with m + 1 extra variables (one in the linear case) (p. 10).
  - Cutting planes, with a binary subproblem for discrete distributions (p. 11).
  - Theorem 4: the data-driven optimum converges at rate O(N^{−1/2}). The proof uses Glivenko–Cantelli, a multivariate DKW inequality and the monotonicity of the optimal value in Γ (pp. 12–13).
  - The data-driven set is a binary θ-assignment (8) with an LP relaxation whose optimum has at most m + 1 nonzeros and two fractional entries. Linear constraints keep a linear counterpart, with one constraint per sample (pp. 13–14).

  My readings: §3.3 announces a bound on the mean of the constraint but states no result (p. 8). The inclusion chain in Theorem 4's proof runs in reverse order of set size (p. 12); since the argument is symmetric, the conclusion is unaffected.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Synthetic setup: min b s.t. z^T x ≤ b ∀z ∈ U over the simplex, a robust-portfolio form. Sets are compared on the attained objective b, not on their radius Γ (p. 14). Opponents are ℓ1, ℓ2, box, nominal, naive and DRO with total variation (p. 15). Software: Python 3.9.12, Gurobi 9.5.1, CVXPY 1.3.0 (p. 16). The text discusses one trial (normal, N_train = 1,000) and calls the others similar (p. 16). Results (p. 17):
  - PRO's probability of violation is worse than ℓ1, ball and DRO for b ∈ [≈0.7, 1];
  - its maximum violation is clearly lower;
  - its mean violation is far lower for b < 0.75 and equal above;
  - it reaches b < 0.7, which the centred sets cannot;
  - relaxed, binary and exact PRO coincide.

  Hospital: θ variables make the census linear in the uncertainty (p. 19). Julia 1.6.3 and Gurobi run "with a timer of 5 minutes", and no gaps are reported (p. 21). Each method's parameter is chosen from a grid of 10 (p. 21). Average maximum census, elective surgeries, test months: Real 31.67, deterministic 29.83, ℓ1 29.17, ℓ2 30.17, ℓ∞ 29.83, DRO 30.00, PRO 27.33 (Table 2, p. 21). Over all surgeries the paper reports 56.56 → 54.44 and calls it 3.9% (p. 22); my arithmetic gives 3.7% of 56.56. The census is recomputed from realized rest times, assuming they do not depend on the weekday of surgery (p. 21). The evaluation is retrospective, not a deployment.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Five contributions are listed (p. 2). Printed limits: a higher probability of violation (pp. 2, 17), no exact method for the non-log-concave Cauchy (p. 16), and subsampling when there are too many data points (p. 14). Future work: feed feature-based ML predictions of each parameter's distribution into the set (p. 22). The abstract's "up to 8.16 %" (p. 1) is the best month; the average is 3.9% (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 6–9, 14–17, 21): one knob Γ comes with a solution-independent coverage law (Theorem 1) and a mean-violation bound (Theorem 2). The trade-off is drawn against the attained objective, with its bad case printed (probability of violation, p. 17). Γ is set from data on training months (p. 21), the correction the skill asks for. The property priced is the size of violations rather than their frequency.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 10, 13–14, 16, 19): class-keeping devices: a Fenchel counterpart, and θ-lifting that keeps a linear constraint linear and the census MIO linear. The boundary is printed (Cauchy; subsampling).
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (p. 1): the old belief is conceded as right when formed ("at a time when data was not necessarily plentiful"). The change is data, not solver speed, and no figure quantifies it.
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 21–22): the hospital's realized ("Real") census is the baseline, with training and test months. There is no field test, and the headline uses the best month.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (p. 21): MIO timetables are solved under a 5-minute limit with no gap or certificate reported.
- New-pattern candidate: *compare on the attained objective, not on the set's parameter* (p. 14): radii of different sets mean different things, so each set's violation statistics are traced against the objective it reaches.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Change variables, t_i = −log S_i(z_i), to turn any continuous marginal into Exp(1). Set coverage then becomes an Erlang tail, whatever the distribution (pp. 6–7).
- Benchmark uncertainty sets on the objective–violation frontier, with the objective b on the x-axis, and report probability, maximum and mean violation separately (pp. 14–17).
- Lift a nonconvex dependence on a discrete uncertain quantity (days of rest) with assignment variables θ_{i,k}. The robust constraint then stays linear in θ and in the decisions (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "However, the remaining violations, even if infrequent, can be very large and can hence be prohibitive in real-world applications." (p. 2)
- "Overall, the PRO approach manages to reduce the average maximum census by 3.9% from 56.56 to 54.44, with up to 8.16% improvement over the historical census observed." (p. 22)

### S271 · An interpretable AI model for recurrence prediction after surgery in gastrointestinal stromal tumour: an observational cohort study (EClinicalMedicine 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.eclinm.2023.102200)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–11 (summary, research in context, introduction, methods, results, discussion, contributors, declarations, references). The supplementary appendix (eMethods, eResults, eFigures) is not in the file and was not read. First-page check: the title matches. Bertsimas is first author jointly with Margonis: "Authors had equal contribution and share first-authorship" (p. 1). The corresponding author is Samuel Singer (MSKCC, p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: G. A. Margonis (co-first) and 16 others from MSKCC, GEIS, the Polish registry and other centres (p. 1) · **Voice**: the clinical team's ("we collaborated with a group from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology", p. 2; also p. 3). Bertsimas's printed roles: conception and design, drafting, statistical analysis and data verification (p. 10) · **Position in trajectory**: one of a series of OCT models in surgical oncology and acute care, cited as refs. 13–22 (p. 4), published in a clinical journal (Workflow E).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP (health); IO as the tree engine}: Selecting patients for adjuvant imatinib after GIST resection needs calibrated recurrence probabilities. The existing tools give qualitative strata (NIH, AFIP) or ranges (Joensuu maps), and the MSK nomogram, the only tool with patient-level probabilities, overestimates risk (pp. 2–3). Entry points: ranking is not calibration (p. 3), and Joensuu's evidence shows non-linear effects of size and mitotic count (p. 3). More data cannot help, since only pre-imatinib-era patients keep the disease's natural history (p. 2). Hence "a different methodological approach" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Train an Optimal Classification Tree on the same three predictors every existing tool uses (tumour size, mitotic count, site), so that any gain can be attributed to the method (pp. 4, 6, 8). Validate externally on calibration and decision curves, not only on discrimination (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Model and data**: The internal cohort has 395 patients (MSKCC 1982–2001; the Spanish GEIS group, 80 sites, 1987–2011) and 147 recurrences. The external Polish registry has 556 patients and 173 recurrences (pp. 1, 5–6). The feature choice is checked by five selection analyses: optimal feature selection, SHAP on XGBoost, LASSO, MRMR and RFE (pp. 4, 6). Hyperparameters are tuned by 5-fold CV over minbucket {5, 10, 15%}, depth 3–5 and gini or entropy (p. 4). ### S308 · A prescriptive machine learning approach to mixed-integer convex optimization (INFORMS J. Comput. 2023, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2022.0188)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–18 (cover page, abstract, §§1–6, Tables 1–13, references). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Cheol Woo Kim match (pp. 1–2). The file is the Articles in Advance version of 2 August 2023 (p. 2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Cheol Woo Kim (MIT ORC, p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: it merges the learning-to-optimize line with the prescriptive-trees line:
- the learning-to-optimize papers of Bertsimas and Stellato, "The voice of optimization" (2021) and "Online mixed-integer optimization in milliseconds" (2022) (pp. 2–3);
- Optimal Policy Trees (Amram, Dunn and Zhuo 2022, p. 3).

It runs on Interpretable AI software (p. 9), thanks Jack Dunn (p. 17), and was funded in part by OCP (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + IO; ML}: Similar MICO instances are solved again and again. Bertsimas and Stellato predict each instance's optimal strategy (its tight constraints and binary values) with a classifier (pp. 2–3, 6). The entry point: classification treats every wrong strategy as equally bad, although some are near-optimal and others infeasible. Earlier work also chose OCT for interpretability, "rather than performance" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace the classifier by a prescriptive learner. The evaluator is the optimizer itself, so counterfactuals need not be estimated (Algorithm 1, p. 7; p. 16):
  - apply every training strategy to every training instance;
  - record its cost, or a penalty M if it is infeasible, as an exact reward matrix;
  - train an Optimal Policy Tree on that matrix;
  - at test time, apply the k best strategies and keep the best result.

  OPT(k, Q) fills Q of the k slots with the most frequent training strategies to cut suboptimality (Algorithm 2, pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**: The setting is parametric MICO with convex objective and constraints, solved to optimality offline (p. 6). Parameters are sampled uniformly from a ball B(ū, r) (p. 7), and problem size and parameter dimension stay fixed between training and deployment (p. 17). Instances: transportation (LO), portfolio (QO), facility location (MILO) and hybrid-vehicle control (MIQO) (pp. 10–11), plus six MIPLIB problems whose key parameters are chosen by rules the authors call "slightly arbitrary" (pp. 14–15). Each experiment uses 7,000 training and 3,000 test instances (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The analysis is a learning-objective argument, not a theorem. OPT minimizes the empirical E[v(u, h(u))]; classification minimizes a 0-1 surrogate, E[1{h(u) ≠ argmin v}] (pp. 6–7). Both search the same space of trees, so any gap is attributed to the objective (p. 7). A theoretical explanation is left open (p. 17).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Metrics: accurate (feasible with suboptimality < 0.001), suboptimal, feasible, infeasible, and maximum suboptimality on the test set (pp. 9–10). Both learners use the depth grid {5, 10}; the software is IAI with Gurobi, no version or hardware stated; the code is on GitHub (p. 9). Results:
  - OPT(1) finds feasible solutions more often, e.g., transportation 20 × 10: 3,000 against 2,299 for OCT(1) (Table 4, p. 11);
  - when feasible, OCT(1) has slightly fewer suboptimal solutions (p. 12);
  - the penalty M barely matters (Tables 8–9, pp. 13–14);
  - OPT trains faster for small strategy sets but slower for large ones (|S| = 127: 1,704 s against 460 s; Table 10, p. 14);
  - online solves run "hundreds of times faster" than Gurobi from scratch (Figure 4, p. 13);
  - MIPLIB binkar10_1 with r = 4 and |S| = 1,023: OPT(1) leaves 106 instances infeasible against 652 for OCT(1), but is accurate on only 276 against 2,179 (Table 12, p. 16).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are prescriptive OPT(k) in place of classification, the OPT(k, Q) extension, and evidence that OPT avoids infeasible outputs and is therefore "safer" to deploy (pp. 3, 16). Printed limits: relative speed-up is not the focus (p. 3), training is slower for large strategy sets (p. 13), and problem size is fixed (p. 17). Future work: a theory of why prescriptive methods avoid infeasibility, varying problem sizes, and other learning-augmented methods (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 6–7, 9–12): the paper's thesis is to train on the cost of the decision, not on prediction labels. Strategies are scored by feasibility and suboptimality against the group's own incumbent approach, OCT(k).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 7, 9, 11–16): the incumbent is dueled with the same hypothesis class and depth grid, and the negative cases are printed (more suboptimal solutions, slower training at large |S|). The solver version and machine are not stated.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 9, 17): a new student merges two programme lines, using the group's company software.
- Heuristic 5 (not a method) · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 6): one optimization solved repeatedly online, with the optimizer's strategy learned offline.
- New-pattern candidate: *isolate the gain by matching everything but the new ingredient* (p. 7): OPT(k) and OCT(k) share the hypothesis space and the tuning grid, so "any performance gap can be attributed to the difference between the learning objectives".

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the downstream evaluator is an optimizer, compute counterfactual rewards exactly. Apply every candidate strategy to every training instance, with a large penalty for infeasibility, instead of estimating them (pp. 7, 16).
- Test how sensitive the results are to the infeasibility penalty M over several orders of magnitude before reporting (Tables 8–9, pp. 13–14).
- Report accurate, suboptimal and infeasible counts separately, together with the maximum suboptimality, so that average accuracy cannot hide infeasibility (pp. 9–10).

**Quotes**:
- "In a sense, classification algorithms optimize a surrogate objective, instead of optimizing the actual objective." (p. 7)
- "Hence, any performance gap can be attributed to the difference between the learning objectives that prescriptive and predictive algorithms try to optimize." (p. 7)
