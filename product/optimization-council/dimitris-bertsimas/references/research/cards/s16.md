### S294 · National and subnational short-term forecasting of COVID-19 in Germany and Poland during early 2021 (Communications Medicine 2022, DOI 10.1038/s43856-022-00191-8)
Read level: **partial**. Read: the medRxiv preprint's main text, pp. 1–18; acknowledgements, contributions and team list, pp. 22–24; Table 2 (p. 12); supplement Table S1 (p. 39). Figure pages were read for captions only. The rest of the supplement (pp. 25–47) was not read.
**Role**: supplement (consortium paper; the voice is the hub coordinators') · **Coauthors**: 42 authors (p. 1). The manuscript was written by J. Bracher, T. Gneiting and M. Schienle (p. 23). Bertsimas's team was CovidAnalytics-DELPHI: Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC; his PhD student, PhD 2022 per 04-mentorship.md), Bertsimas and Saksham Soni (MIT Sloan) (p. 23). · **Position in trajectory**: the COVID-19 analytics line of 2020–2022 {AP}. It is a second external scoreboard for DELPHI, after the US Forecast Hub evaluation the SKILL already cites (Method 5 checkpoint).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP; no optimization model}: Short-term forecasts inform public-health decisions. Evaluating them requires prospective, pre-registered studies that prevent "selective reporting and hindsight bias" (p. 2). This is the second and final part of the German and Polish COVID-19 Forecast Hub study, pre-registered on 8 Oct 2020. It covers forecasts made from 11 Jan to 29 Mar 2021 (p. 2). Bertsimas enters only as a member of a contributing team (p. 23).
- **D2 Core idea**: One submission format collects real-time quantile forecasts: 15 models from 14 teams (p. 5). They are scored on the weighted interval score (WIS) against three pre-specified naive baselines (pp. 4–6) and combined into median, mean and inverse-WIS quantile ensembles; the median ensemble was the pre-specified primary (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: The truth data are RKI and Polish Ministry of Health counts (primary) or JHU CSSE (pp. 3–4). Each target gets a point forecast plus 23 quantiles, weekly, at horizons of 1–4 weeks, with the focus on 1–2 (pp. 3–4). Missing weeks are imputed with the worst score of any model (p. 5). Table 1 lists DELPHI as a compartmental model using JHU truth data (p. 5). No model used mobility or social-media data (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for his contribution. The paper's own devices are the WIS and its decomposition into spread, overprediction and underprediction (pp. 4–5), and quantile averaging ("Vincentization"); the inverse-WIS weights are heuristic, not estimated (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: There were 12 weekly forecasts per target, nationally and for 16 states and 16 voivodeships (Tables 2–3, pp. 12–13). The median ensemble was "consistently among the best-performing methods" (p. 9).
  - DELPHI's relative WIS against the last-observation baseline was above 1 (worse) in 9 of the 16 national target × horizon cells (Table S1, p. 39):
    - Germany: 1-week cases 1.04 and 1-week deaths 1.77.
    - Poland: cases 1.33–1.54 at all four horizons, and deaths 1.46, 1.08 and 1.09 at 1, 2 and 4 weeks.
  - DELPHI was below 1 for German cases at 2–4 weeks (0.92, 0.84, 0.85), for German deaths at 2–4 weeks (0.86, 0.55, 0.43) and for Polish deaths at 3 weeks (0.97). The median ensemble was below 1 in all 16 cells (p. 39).
  - DELPHI's Polish entries and its German case entries include imputed worst scores for missing weeks, a "pessimistic assessment" (Table 2, p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Ensembles performed best; many but not all models beat the naive baseline; trend changes in cases were hard to predict (pp. 2, 9, 16). The limits are named specifically:
  - turning points and the "forecaster's dilemma" (p. 17);
  - the choice of baseline (p. 16);
  - heuristic ensemble weights (p. 17);
  - changing case ascertainment (p. 17).
  The next step is hospitalization forecasts in the European hub (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 12, 39). DELPHI is judged here on predictions, scored on the field's metric against naive rules, not on decisions. Most cells are worse than the naive rule, some because of imputed weeks. This supports the critic-informed checkpoint already in Method 5 ("score forecasts on the field's metric"), from a second hub.
- Heuristic 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 23). His team submitted to a pre-registered, prospective external scoreboard that printed its weaker cells.
- New-pattern candidate: none.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Pre-register targets, horizons, baselines and the rule for missing forecasts before any forecast is scored (pp. 2, 5).
- Impute a missing forecast week with the worst score any model achieved that week, so selective submission cannot help (p. 5).
- Report scores relative to several pre-specified naive baselines (last value carried forward, trend extrapolation, exponential smoothing), because any one baseline may suit one period by luck (pp. 5–6, 16).
- Decompose WIS into spread, over- and underprediction to tell a team which way to fix its intervals (pp. 4–5, 9).

**Quotes**:
- "JB, TG and MSe wrote the manuscript." (p. 23)
- "The pre-specified median ensemble was consistently among the best-performing methods" (p. 9)

### S299 · Tabtext: A flexible and contextual approach to tabular data representation (arXiv preprint 2022, arXiv 2206.10381)
Read level: **full** for the main text of v5 (pp. 1–10, through data sharing, code and contributions). The references and appendices (pp. 11–16) were not read. The v5 title is "TabText: Language-Based Representations of Tabular Health Data for Predictive Modelling" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Kimberly Villalobos Carballo (first author; his PhD student, thesis 2024 per 04-mentorship.md), Liangyuan Na, Yu Ma, Léonard Boussioux, Cynthia Zeng, Luis Soenksen (p. 1). Bertsimas is last author; "D.B. inspired, supported, and supervised the presented work and edited the paper" (p. 10). · **Position in trajectory**: the health-analytics line of his group {AP ML}. It takes its feature processing from the group's patient-flow paper (Na, Villalobos Carballo, …, Bertsimas, "Patient outcome predictions improve operations at a large hospital network"; p. 6, ref. [14] p. 11). There is no optimization content.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML AP; no optimization model}: Tabular health records need heavy manual cleaning, and their context (column names, units, table metadata) is usually discarded (p. 2). Prior language-based approaches embed each data stream separately inside task-specific networks. None, the paper says, gives task-agnostic embeddings, studies augmentation of tabular features, or shows cross-institution performance (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Each patient-day becomes a paragraph of sentences ("attribute is value"); numbers are binned into words, missing values omitted and table metadata added (pp. 2–5). A Clinical-Longformer fine-tuned by masked-word prediction turns the paragraph into a fixed 768-dimensional embedding (p. 5). Any standard model can use the embedding, alone or concatenated with tabular features (pp. 2, 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Design choices were made on a 2017 development set from hospital HA: 60,000 + 10,000 patient-days, 63 laboratory columns, nine binary patient-flow targets (p. 3).
  - The evaluation uses HA's patient-days from Jan 2018 to Apr 2022 (160 columns) and five more hospitals of the same system; their records are not standardized (pp. 5–6).
  - Nine UCI health datasets are also used (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a. The design choices are selected by statistical comparison: BioGPT and Clinical-Longformer did not differ significantly (p. 4); 16 sentence configurations were ranked by median rank across tasks (p. 5); fine-tuning gave p < 0.0001 gains on 8 of 9 tasks (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: A temporal split trained on 2018–Feb 2020, validated on Mar–Dec 2020 and tested on Jan 2021–Apr 2022 at HA and five other hospitals.
  - Test AUC ranged from 0.694 to 0.941 (Table 1, p. 6).
  - Augmentation added 1–3 AUC points when the tabular baseline was ≤ 0.80 and training data ≤ 10k; gains were negligible or slightly negative for short-horizon mortality (Fig. 3, pp. 7–8).
  - On the UCI data, against FT-Transformer, LR and XGBoost, the gain was +0.039 for breast cancer and −0.010 for HD (Table 2, pp. 8–9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A preprocessing framework (p. 9). The limits are stated specifically (p. 9):
  - embedding overhead;
  - "less interpretable" embeddings;
  - evaluation within one health system;
  - errors in the data carry into the representation;
  - subgroup performance not studied.
  Code is released for the public datasets only; the hospital data cannot be shared (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 6, 11). The evaluation mimics deployment with a temporal hold-out and other hospitals, but it scores predictions (AUC) only. There is no decision metric and no hospital rule as a baseline. The decision use sits in the companion operations paper (ref. [14], p. 11). The abstract's "tested prospectively" means a temporal hold-out on retrospective data (p. 6).
- Heuristic 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 8–9). The null and negative cells are printed (HD −1 point; short-horizon mortality).
- Taste mark 5 (interpretability): tension ⚠ (p. 9). The authors concede that the embeddings are less interpretable than raw features.
- New-pattern candidate: **sponsor-and-edit senior authorship on student-led lines outside optimization** (p. 10). The contribution statement assigns him "inspired, supported, and supervised … and edited", and the paper has no optimization content. Same name as c28 S132 and c34 S682.
- New-pattern candidate: **multi-center cohorts as a validation instrument**, variant (p. 6). The model is trained at one hospital and tested at five others over a later period. Same name as c41 S679/S680.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Fix every design choice (language model, text template, fine-tuning) on a separate development year before the evaluation years are touched (pp. 3–5).
- Choose among template variants by median rank across all tasks, not by one task's score (Fig. 2b, p. 5).
- Report an augmentation gain against both baseline strength and training-set size, with 95% CIs over 10 seeds, so readers can see where the gain disappears (Fig. 3, pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "D.B. inspired, supported, and supervised the presented work and edited the paper." (p. 10)
- "The embeddings themselves are less interpretable than native tabular features" (p. 9)

### S300 · The backbone method for ultra-high dimensional sparse machine learning (Machine Learning 2022, DOI 10.1007/s10994-021-06123-2; arXiv 2006.06592)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v3 pp. 1–44: all sections, the four real-data tables and the proof of Theorem 1. The figure pages were read for captions and axes only; the references (pp. 45–48) were not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Vassilis Digalakis Jr. (MIT ORC; his PhD student, thesis 2023 per 04-mentorship.md) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the ML-under-an-optimization-lens line {ML IO}, first submitted 06/2020 (p. 1). It takes the exact sparse-regression solver (Bertsimas–Van Parys 2020, p ∼ 10⁵) and optimal trees (p ∼ 10³) beyond their limits (p. 2). The backbone device comes from the group's online taxi-routing work (pp. 3, 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO}: Exact MIO methods stop at p ∼ 10⁵ for sparse regression and p ∼ 10³ for trees (p. 2). The usual fixes for ultra-high dimensions are heuristic: screening is often suboptimal and feature extraction is uninterpretable (pp. 2–3). "Ultra-high dimensional" is defined operationally: p > n, and at least two orders of magnitude beyond the exact method's scalability limit (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The method has two phases.
  - First, build a backbone: the union of the supports returned by many tractable subproblems, built hierarchically until |B| ≤ Bmax (Algorithm 1, pp. 9–11). Each subproblem uses a sampled subset of features and a relaxed or heuristic fit.
  - Then solve the exact MIO on B (p. 9).
  - The components are a SIS screen keeping αp ≈ 10n features (pp. 11–12) and sampling weighted by marginal utility, inspired by the exponential mechanism (p. 13). Subproblems are fitted by the subgradient method or elastic net for regression and by CART for trees; the exact solve uses cutting planes or OCT (pp. 16–20).
  - Their backbone is the union of near-optimal solutions, unlike SAT's intersection of all optimal ones (pp. 7–8). The paper says the method "can loosely be viewed as a heuristic branch and price approach" (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The models are sparse ERM with a ridge term and ‖w‖₀ ≤ k, with k assumed known (pp. 4, 15), and depth-D trees with a minbucket constraint (p. 19).
  - The theory assumes a Gaussian design N(0, I), β ∈ {−1, 0, 1}ᵖ, Gaussian noise, log p = O(n^ξ) and γ = 1/n. It covers a simplified one-round version with uniform sampling (pp. 18, 40–41), and the authors call the model "very simple and rather unrealistic" (p. 18).
  - Synthetic data use correlation ρ^|i−j| with ρ = 0.6 or 0.9 (p. 21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1 (pp. 18–19): with α = Θ(n^{1−φ}/p) and M = Ω(log(αpk)/log(1/(1−β+βp_sub))), the probability of missing a relevant feature tends to 0.
  - Proof (pp. 41–44): a union bound over the screening failure (the Fan–Lv SIS theorem) and the per-feature misses. Relevant features left out of a subproblem are treated as extra Gaussian noise, so the Bertsimas–Van Parys support-recovery theorem applies inside the subproblem (Lemma 1, p. 43). The binomial count of the subproblems that contain a feature gives k(1−β+βp_sub)^M (p. 44).
  - Complexity is O(np + Mαβp log(αp) + M(αβp)²n) with LARS subproblems (p. 15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Xeon E5-2690, CentOS 7; Gurobi (version not given) and the commercial Interpretable AI package for SR-REL and OCT (pp. 23–24). The parameters were not tuned; they were set from Theorem 1 and empirical evidence (p. 24).
  - Synthetic data: up to 3×10⁸ features for regression, in under 10 h (p. 25), and 10⁵ for trees (p. 25). The baselines are SIS-ENET and CART, plus exact SR and OCT run on the full feature set (§5.6, pp. 28–30).
  - Four UCI datasets, each with 1,000 permuted copies of every feature, 25 runs each (pp. 36–37). The losses are printed:
    - housing R² 0.765 against SIS-ENET's 0.863 (p. 38);
    - OCT has a "slight edge" over BB at p = 2,000 (pp. 29–30);
    - RF has a higher AUC than BB (p. 39).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A framework, a theorem for a simplified version, and experiments. The authors put the main contribution in "practical relevance" (p. 18). Limits:
  - k assumed known (p. 4);
  - an idealized model (p. 18);
  - no fine-tuning (p. 24);
  - the tree sensitivity analysis omitted as "near-identical" (p. 30).
  Next steps: sparse SVMs, sparse PCA, clustering, distributed runs (p. 40).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 9, 17–18). The exact MIO formulation stays the target. The backbone only shrinks the instance until the class's own solver (cutting planes, OCT) applies.
- Method 4: evidence ✅ with variant ⚠ (pp. 8, 16, 23–24, 29, 38).
  - Evidence: warm starts (an incremental cross-validation warm start; a subgradient warm start for SR), a 5-minute limit per γ, and gaps printed but scoped "albeit for the reduced problem". The method duels SIS-ENET, RFE, DECO, CART, RF and oracles.
  - Variant: OCT is "a tailored local search procedure" run through the commercial Interpretable AI package (p. 24; also p. 7), so the tree results carry no certificate.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (p. 2). The "why now" is the sentence "far beyond what one would imagine a decade ago", with no solver × hardware figure. The scale limits come from the group's own papers.
- Method 6: evidence ✅, weak (pp. 23–24). The group's company software is the experimental platform.
- Heuristic 3: variant ⚠. When the exact model stalls at scale, the paper shrinks the instance rather than dropping to local search on the whole problem.
- New-pattern candidate: **shrink-then-solve with a printed quality/time knob** (pp. 34–36). The screening fraction α trades time against accuracy ("There is a trade-off between the two", p. 34), with β ≈ 0.5 and Bmax studied. Same name as c11 S160 and S073.
- New-pattern candidate: **cross-programme lemma reuse**, variant: a device rather than a lemma (pp. 3, 8). The backbone moves from online vehicle routing {AP} to sparse ML {ML}. Compare c07 S019.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Scale an exact sparse model with a union-of-supports backbone. Screen first, sample feature subsets with probability rising in marginal utility, fit a fast sparse heuristic to each subset, take the union, halve the number of subproblems per round until |B| ≤ Bmax, then solve exactly on B (pp. 9–15).
- Set the number of subproblems from a union bound plus a binomial count: P(miss j) ≤ (1−β+βp_sub)^M (pp. 43–44). The resulting M was used in the experiments (p. 34).
- To reuse a full-problem recovery theorem inside a subproblem, treat the relevant features left out of the subproblem as added Gaussian noise (Lemma 1, p. 43).
- Test support recovery on real data by appending 1,000 random permutations of each feature and counting how many selected features are originals, over 5 splits × 5 expansions (pp. 36–37).
- Name the formulation a reported optimality gap belongs to, original or reduced problem (p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "The model we examine is indeed very simple and rather unrealistic" (p. 18)
- "while providing optimality guarantees, albeit for the reduced problem" (p. 8)

### S333 · Distributionally robust causal inference with observational data (arXiv preprint 2022, arXiv 2210.08326)
Read level: **full**, v3: the main text pp. 1–22, Appendix A.2 (p. 27) and A.4 (p. 32). The proofs in A.1 and A.3 (pp. 26–31) were skimmed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Kosuke Imai (Harvard, Government and Statistics) and Michael Lingzhi Li (Harvard Business School; his former PhD student, PhD 2022 per 04-mentorship.md) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: robust and distributionally robust optimization carried into causal inference {RO ML}, with a statistician from outside his group. It cites Bertsimas and den Hertog (2022) for DRO (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO ML}: Unconfoundedness "often lacks credibility" (p. 2). The popular marginal sensitivity model (MSM) of Tan (2006) gives uninformative bounds when unobserved confounders strongly affect treatment and outcome (pp. 1, 7). The entry is to write the departure from unconfoundedness as an ambiguity set over counterfactual distributions and to bound the ATT by DRO (pp. 2, 5–6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Minimize the ATT over counterfactual distributions F01, written as reweightings of the observed control outcomes (pp. 5–6).
  - Proposition 1 shows that Tan's MSM is exactly one such ambiguity set (p. 7).
  - The new distributional sensitivity model (DSM) adds a condition: F01 must match the treated group's observed F11 up to a location shift, within KS distance δ. It also allows zero weights, and so lack of overlap (p. 8).
  - Discretizing the shift makes the DSM a MILO for Gurobi (p. 10).
  - Extensions cover difference-in-differences, change-in-changes, instrumental variables with a relaxed exclusion restriction, and many covariates via first-moment balancing (pp. 10–15).
- **D3 Model and data**: IID units with no interference and nonparametric outcome and treatment models (p. 4). Categorical covariates are handled first by conditioning on each value (p. 4). The DSM is credible when treatment-effect heterogeneity is small relative to the variation of the outcome (pp. 7–9). Proposition 2 assumes minimally k-Lipschitz CDFs (p. 8). The knobs are Γ (weight bound), δ (shape distance) and ε (relaxed parallel trends or exclusion) (pp. 8, 11, 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Proposition 1 is an equivalence between the MSM and a DRO problem (p. 7; proof p. 26).
  - Proposition 2 bounds the shift-KS distance by 3(kσ₀₁/2)^{2/3} via the Lipschitz condition plus Chebyshev, optimizing the split point (pp. 8–9; proof p. 27).
  - Theorem 1 shows that bounds on sampling weights are equivalent to a distance bound, a quasimetric for Γ < 2 and a metric for Γ ≥ 2 (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Simulation: 3 scenarios, n ∈ {100, 200, 500}, 1,000 runs. The DSM's bias is smaller than the MSM's, and the gap grows with Γ (pp. 15–17). Not discussed: the DSM's standard deviation is larger than the MSM's in every cell of Table 1 (p. 16).
  - NSW data with PSID/CPS controls, against the $886 experimental benchmark: the biases are smaller in magnitude than LaLonde's 1986 regression's (Tables 2–3, pp. 18–19).
  - BMC diabetes data, against Causal Forests and a doubly robust estimator; the DSM is argued to be "more realistic" by clinical plausibility (p. 20).
  - STAR data: as Γ grows the estimate turns negative (p. 21).
  - No solve times are reported, and Gurobi's version is not given (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A framework, a new sensitivity model and extensions (pp. 21–22). The one stated limit is that small samples make the DSM "overly conservative" (p. 21). The two-sentence conclusion names no next step (pp. 21–22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 5–7, 10). Sensitivity analysis, a problem owned by statistics, is written as DRO. Its incumbent is recovered exactly, and the new model is kept in the MILO class.
- Heuristic 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 7–15). A procedure practice already uses (Tan's MSM) is shown to be the exact robust problem over one ambiguity set, then extended (the DSM, DiD, IV).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 16, 18, 21). Γ and δ are interpretable knobs:
  - Γ is chosen by the minimum number of units kept: Γ = 25, 12 and 8 for 30, 60 and 100 units (p. 18);
  - δ is tied to effect heterogeneity by Proposition 2.
  The estimates are tabulated by knob, and the bad case is printed (STAR, p. 21). But there is no single price curve and no solution-independent violation bound.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 18–20). Scoring is by bias against experimental benchmarks, not by decisions. The BMC clinical reading rests on plausibility: an HbA1c change of ≥ 1.5 points is called "highly unlikely" (p. 20). It is not scored.
- New-pattern candidate: **subsume the incumbent as a special case** (Proposition 1, p. 7). Same name as c04, c10, c20, c31, c32 and s01.
- New-pattern candidate: **exact dictionary between a statistical procedure and a robust model, with its boundary** (pp. 7, 9–10). The MSM equals DRO over a weighting set, and its boundary is printed: the bound is non-informative when confounding is strong. Theorem 1 adds a weights ↔ metric dictionary. Same name as c19 S039, S085 and S104.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a sensitivity model into DRO: write the unobserved counterfactual distribution as a bounded reweighting of an observed one, minimize the effect over that set, and recover the incumbent model as one choice of set (pp. 6–7).
- Borrow shape information from the other arm. Require the counterfactual to match the treated outcomes up to a location shift, and make δ interpretable by bounding it with effect heterogeneity (Lipschitz plus Chebyshev, optimized over the split) (pp. 8–9, 27).
- Make a minimum-over-shifts KS constraint MILO-solvable: bound the shift by the outcome range, discretize it, and select a grid point with binaries (p. 10).
- Choose the weight bound Γ by the minimum effective sample it implies, not as an abstract odds ratio (pp. 18–20).
- Validate an observational estimator where an experimental answer exists: LaLonde's NSW with non-experimental controls, and STAR with school-permuted controls (Wilde–Hollister) (pp. 17–21).

**Quotes**:
- "We show that the sensitivity analysis based on this model solves the distributionally robust optimization problem with a particular ambiguity set." (p. 2)
- "This suggests that with a small sample size, the distributional sensitivity model may yield an overly conservative estimate." (p. 21)

### S429 · Sparse PCA: A geometric approach (arXiv preprint 2022, arXiv 2210.06590)
Read level: **full**, v1: §§1–2 and §§4–5 (pp. 1–17 and 22–31). The §3 extensions (disjoint-support blocks and structured sparse PCA, pp. 17–22) were skimmed; the references were not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Driss Lahlou Kitane (MIT ORC; his student per 04-mentorship.md) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the sparse PCA line {ML IO}, after Berk and Bertsimas (2019; branch-and-bound) and Bertsimas et al. (2022; branch-and-bound with SDO). Both of those build PCs one at a time by deflation (p. 3); this paper builds all PCs at once.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO}: Cardinality-controlled sparse PCA methods build PCs one by one by deflation, so overall optimality and orthogonality are lost (pp. 3–4). Penalty methods cannot fix k precisely (p. 2). Only Del Pia (2022, theory only) and Dey et al. (2022, p up to 2,000) build several orthogonal PCs at once (pp. 3–4). The aim: build all a PCs with a common support of size k at once, with guarantees, at p in the 10,000s (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - In the left-eigenvector form, the problem becomes choosing the k columns of X whose projections onto an a-dimensional subspace have the largest total squared norm (Prop. 1, pp. 6–7).
  - The nonconcave objective is replaced by the plain sum of the chosen columns' squared norms. A feasibility condition is added: the chosen submatrix must be within η of rank a, η(s) ≤ η (Props. 2–3, pp. 8–9).
  - The condition is enforced by lazily generated no-good cuts Σ_{i∈σ} s_i ≤ |σ| − 1 (pp. 9–10, 13).
  - Algorithm 2 lowers η from a large value and keeps the best support (pp. 14–15).
- **D3 Model and data**: Centered X (n×p), with a orthonormal PCs sharing one support of size ≤ k (p. 6). The low-rank-plus-noise picture is intuition only, "not a required assumption in any of the results" (p. 11). Lemma 9 assumes that problem (7) has a unique optimum (p. 14). a is chosen by the classical PCA elbow and η by Algorithm 2 (p. 22).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:

### S443 · Stochastic cutting planes for data-driven optimization (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2022, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2022.1205; arXiv 2103.02506)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1: the main text pp. 1–20 and the proof of Theorem 1 (Appendix B, pp. 26–29). Appendix A, the proof of Lemma 2 (pp. 21–25), was skimmed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC; his PhD student, PhD 2022 per 04-mentorship.md) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the scaling-exact-methods line {IO ML; DA via SAA}, received 30 Dec 2020 (p. 1). It generalizes the sampling trick of Bertsimas and Li (2018, matrix completion), which needed strong convexity (p. 4). It uses the Bertsimas–Van Parys (2020) convex-integer form of sparse regression (p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO ML; DA via SAA}: Outer approximation (Duran–Grossmann; Fletcher–Leyffer) needs f and ∇f on all N samples for every cut, which blocks scaling to N ≥ 10⁶ (p. 2). The time goes into cut generation, not the MILO master (p. 9). The entry is to transplant the SGD idea into an exact discrete method; the authors "could not find any detailed work" on it (p. 2). It is distinguished from randomized constraint generation (Calafiore–Campi), which samples constraints, not data (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Build each cut from a fresh random subset of n ≪ N samples, drawn without replacement (Algorithm 2, p. 10). The MILO master and the termination test are unchanged; the default is n = min{N, 10√N} (p. 12).
- **D3 Model and data**: The assumptions are:
  - convex, once-differentiable f and g (Assumption 1);
  - finite Z and compact Θ (Assumption 2);
  - concentration of f and ∇f on random subsets at rate √(log(1/ε)/n) (Assumption 3), without unbiasedness (p. 5).
  Separable losses qualify by Hoeffding's inequality without replacement (Lemma 1, p. 7). The non-separable sparse ridge objective also qualifies (Lemma 2, p. 8). Nonconvex problems can be run "but the solution would not carry any theoretical guarantees" (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Theorem 1 (pp. 10–11): finite termination with probability 1, E[K] ≤ (e−1)|Z| + 1, and ε-optimality with probability ≥ 1 − (2K+1)exp(−nε²/((2+√(p₁+p₂))²J)). The proof (pp. 26–29) has three parts:
  - Termination: a repeated integer point must show strictly increasing sampled values, or the loop stops. Independent samples make such an increasing run of length q have probability ≤ 1/q!, and the pigeonhole principle over |Z|(q−1)+1 iterations finishes the argument.
  - Feasibility comes from Fletcher–Leyffer.
  - Optimality comes from Lemmas 5–6: Fréchet's inequality, per-cut concentration and convexity.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: All runs use Julia 1.4.2 on a six-core i7-5820k with 24 GB.
  - Sparse regression, N up to 10⁶: 1,356 s against 46 s, with identical MAPE (Table 1, p. 12).
  - SVM on covertype, N up to 10⁵, against LIBSVM and scikit-learn SGD: 13× faster than the full method and 30× faster than LIBSVM, for about 0.5% accuracy (Table 2, p. 14). At N = 10³ the drop is 2.6 points (74.9% to 72.3%), which the text does not discuss.
  - The static stochastic knapsack (SAA, Kleywegt setup), with Gurobi 9.0.1: two orders of magnitude faster. The linear reformulation does not finish within 10,000 s at N = 10⁶ (Table 3, p. 16).
  - (N, n) agreement grids: n between O(log N) and O(√N) (Figs. 1–2, pp. 17–18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An algorithm, one theorem and three applications (pp. 3, 19). Stated limits: no guarantee without convexity (p. 5); the worst-case E[K] is exponential in p₁, defended by p₁ ≪ n ≪ N (p. 11). The one-paragraph conclusion names no limit or next step (p. 19). Not discussed: cuts built on samples need not under-estimate f(·; [N]), so the final η certifies nothing. The guarantee is probabilistic only (Algorithm 2, p. 10; Theorem 1, pp. 10–11).

**Method links**:
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 9–16).
  - Present: the heuristic warm start ("# Heuristic Warm Start", Algorithms 1–2) and the duels against the standard cutting plane, LIBSVM, SGD and the Gurobi linear reformulation.
  - Changed: the honest certificate becomes a high-probability ε-optimality statement, and the loss of a valid lower bound is not mentioned.
- Method 1: evidence ✅, weak (pp. 8, 16). The problems stay convex MINLO with a MILO master. On the SSKP, the device decides tractability, not the solver: cuts on the [·]⁺ cost succeed where the N-auxiliary-variable linear reformulation fails.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (p. 9). MILO progress (Bixby–Rothberg; Achterberg–Wunderling) is cited to move the bottleneck to cut generation, not to reopen an intractable problem. No figure is rebuilt.
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 17–18). The sample size n is the one knob, Theorem 1 bounds failure in n, and the measured curves are handed to the practitioner ("explore even smaller n"). The property priced is speed, and the bad cells (agreement below 100% on the 10 log N line) are shown only in the figure.
- New-pattern candidate: **carry deterministic devices into the stochastic version and measure what survives**, variant: an exact algorithm, not a model, is randomized (pp. 10–11, 26–29). Finite termination survives within a factor (e−1) of the deterministic |Z|; optimality survives only with high probability. Same name as c01 and s01.
- New-pattern candidate: **shrink-then-solve with a printed quality/time knob**, variant: the shrinking is per cut (pp. 17–18). Each cut sees n ≪ N samples, and Figs. 1–2 print agreement and optimality against n. Same name as c11; compare S300 in this batch.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before speeding up an exact method, profile it. Show that the master solve is cheap and that oracle work (cut generation) dominates, then attack only that part (p. 9).
- State the sampling assumption as concentration of the subsampled objective and gradient, not unbiasedness. Non-separable objectives can then qualify, e.g. via Woodbury plus finite-population Hoeffding (pp. 5, 8, 21–22).
- Prove finite termination of a randomized cutting-plane loop over a finite set with an exchangeability argument: an increasing run of q i.i.d. sampled values has probability ≤ 1/q!, so E[K] ≤ (e−1)|Z|+1 (pp. 26–27).
- Print a phase diagram over (N, n) with reference curves 10√N, 10∛N and 10 log N, showing where the sampled method agrees with the exact one (Figs. 1–2, pp. 17–18).
- Show that the gain comes from fresh randomness, not from size alone. The sampled method beats the full method run on a fixed sample of comparable size (p. 14).
