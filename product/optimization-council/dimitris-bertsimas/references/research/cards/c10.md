### S029 · Robust game theory (Mathematical Programming Ser. B 107, 2006, DOI 10.1007/s10107-005-0686-0)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–41 read, including all proofs and Tables 1–2; reference list pp. 41–43 skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michele Aghassi (MIT Operations Research Center, NSF graduate fellow, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO period (2001–2013); robust optimization carried into game theory; received June 2004, accepted March 2005 (p. 1); the same student's 2005 thesis also yields S155 (S155 p. 9, ref. [1]).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+LO}: Harsanyi's Bayesian games need a common-knowledge prior, which the authors liken to the stochastic-programming treatment of uncertainty (p. 3); the distribution-free alternative, the ex post equilibrium, may fail to exist (p. 3), and fails on an interval-cost inspection game unless the cost intervals are degenerate (p. 16). Entry: let each player optimize the worst case over a commonly known payoff uncertainty set and call the resulting equilibria *robust-optimization equilibria* (pp. 8, 10–11).
  - Transferable: when a field's solution concept needs a prior and its distribution-free alternative may not exist, define the worst-case analogue and show on the field's textbook example that the old concept fails where the new one exists.
- **D2 Key idea**: Replace the expectation in the best-response correspondence by an infimum over U (p. 10) and keep the rest of Nash's setting (finite, one-shot, simultaneous moves, mixed strategies; pp. 1–2, 8). Players maximize worst-case expected payoff, not expected worst-case payoff: the latter is in the authors' view overly pessimistic and collapses to a complete-information game with componentwise worst payoffs (footnote 4, p. 11). Results: ex post equilibria are robust-optimization equilibria (Lemma 1, p. 12); equilibria exist for bounded U (Theorem 2, pp. 18–19); for bounded polyhedral U they are a componentwise projection of the solution set of a multilinear system (Theorem 3, pp. 23–25); for box uncertainty with sign-constant payoff sensitivities the robust game equals a complete-information game with a worst-case payoff matrix Q (Theorem 4, pp. 28–29).
- **D3 Model and data**: N-player finite games (Definition 1, p. 8); payoff uncertainty set U commonly known, no distribution (p. 8); bounded U for existence (p. 18); bounded polyhedral U, in extreme-point or constraint form, for computation (pp. 22–23). Private information is deferred to §7 for simplicity (p. 4); there, computation needs finite type spaces and polyhedral projections (Theorem 9, p. 40), and infinite type spaces need a Banach-space fixed-point theorem (pp. 38–40). Instances: interval-cost inspection game, interval-cost free-rider game, network routing with an uncertain split of a known total demand (pp. 14–16, 26–27).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: (i) Existence: continuity of the worst-case expected payoff (Lemmas 2–3, p. 17) and concavity in the player's own strategy (Lemma 4, p. 18) feed Kakutani's theorem along the lines of Nash's first proof (pp. 16–19); symmetric equilibria by restricting the map to the diagonal (Theorem 5, pp. 33–34), which also shrinks the systems by a factor N (p. 34); infinite type spaces by Bohnenblust–Karlin (Theorems 7–8, pp. 38–40). (ii) Computation: each best response is a robust LP (17) (p. 24); Lemma 5, credited to the LP-duality proofs of the price-of-robustness paper [7], replaces the constraint z ≤ min over U by finitely many linear conditions (pp. 21–22); dualizing the player's LP once more yields the multilinear equilibrium system (pp. 24–25), whose sizes are tabulated against the nominal game (Table 1, p. 25). (iii) Solver: Courant's quadratic penalty with a pseudo-Newton method and Armijo steps (pp. 25–26); restarts may, but need not, find several solutions (p. 26). (iv) Comparison devices: closed-form robust equilibria via Theorem 4 (inspection game: shirk 2/5, inspect 4/5, against 1/3 and 2/3 in the nominal game, pp. 30–31); examples where the equilibrium set shrinks or grows and where zero-sum becomes non-fixed-sum (pp. 31–32).
  - Transferable: to compute equilibria with an inner worst case, write each best response as a robust LP and dualize twice (inner uncertainty, then the player's LP) to get one finite polynomial system; tabulate its size against the nominal system.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: MATLAB 6.5.0 on a 1.7 GHz Pentium IV with 512 MB (p. 26). Inspection game: 22 constraints in 10 variables, one run of 0.5 s and 71 iterations to (0.4, 0.8), the equilibrium Theorem 4 predicts (pp. 26–27). Free-rider game: 15 random restarts in 1.8458 min recover all three equilibria (p. 27). Network routing with N ≤ 5 players and a ≤ 5 paths, one run each capped at 50 iterations: the final penalty rises from 7.98×10⁻⁶ (N = 2, a = 2) to 15.32 (N = 5, a = 5) and the proportional error to 0.59 (Table 2, p. 27), so the larger instances end unsolved, while the text claims accuracy and speed for small games (p. 28). No baseline solver is run (Gambit and homotopy codes are only reviewed, pp. 20–21), and no certificate is given beyond the penalty value.
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Nash's existence result and Harsanyi's model, naming *robust games* after *Bayesian games* (p. 8); cites Morris and Wilson against the common prior (p. 3). Separates itself from max-min ambiguity models (Gilboa–Schmeidler, Dow–Werlang, Klibanoff, Lo, Marinacci) on three counts: those treat complete-information games, stay probabilistic (multiple priors, non-additive probabilities), and offer no computation (pp. 7–8); records Hyafil–Boutilier's minimax-regret model as appearing after submission (p. 8). Tells the RO lineage as Soyster (over-conservative) → Ben-Tal–Nemirovski and El Ghaoui (SOCP counterparts) → Bertsimas–Sim (the robust LP stays an LP at comparable conservatism) → Bertsimas–Pachamanova–Sim (general polyhedral sets) (pp. 5–6).
- **D7 Writing**: Contributions numbered with a section map (pp. 3–5); two anticipated reader objections announced (p. 12) and answered in their own subsections, §3.3 on combining equilibrium with a worst-case view and §3.4 on interpreting mixed strategies (pp. 13–14); an economic-intuition paragraph after each worked comparison (p. 31); the conclusion restates contributions and closes on a modest hope (p. 41).
  - Transferable: name the two objections a referee from the host field will raise and give each a subsection before the examples.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Concept, existence theorems, a computable formulation and a small numerical demonstration (p. 41). Limits stated or visible: the computation method is approximate with no convergence guarantee (p. 26); only small games are handled (p. 28; Table 2, p. 27); computation with private information needs finite type spaces (p. 40). No future directions are given (p. 41).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: an equilibrium problem owned by game theory is rewritten as one robust LP per player and, through the price-of-robustness LP-duality lemma, as a finite multilinear system (pp. 21–25); Theorem 4 maps a class of robust games onto nominal complete-information games so that existing Nash-equilibrium methods apply (abstract p. 1; pp. 28–29); the boundary is printed (polyhedral U, finite type spaces; pp. 23, 40).
- Roundtable Card blind spot *heuristics' convergence* ✅ evidence: the computational engine is a penalty pseudo-Newton method with no convergence or completeness guarantee (p. 26), and the largest instances end with penalty above 1 (p. 27).
- Method 3: not exercised; there is no budget knob or price curve, and robust and nominal games are compared by examples only (pp. 30–32).
- new-pattern candidate: *robust analogue of another field's canonical concept* (replace E by inf, re-prove existence and computability) (pp. 8, 10, 16–19, 23–25).
- new-pattern candidate: *subsume the incumbent as a special case* (ex post equilibria ⊆ robust-optimization equilibria; Lemma 1, p. 12; Lemma 6, p. 37).
- new-pattern candidate: *RO duality lemma as a portable single-level device* (Lemma 5, pp. 21–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace an expectation by an infimum over an uncertainty set in a field's equilibrium or optimality definition, then re-prove the canonical existence theorem with the same fixed-point argument (Kakutani: continuity plus concavity in own strategy; pp. 16–19).
- Remove an inner min over U from a constraint with the robust-counterpart duality lemma, in extreme-point and constraint forms, and tabulate the variable and constraint counts of each form (Lemma 5, p. 22; Table 1, p. 25).
- Find a sign-constant sensitivity condition under which the robust problem collapses to a nominal one with worst-case data, so existing solvers apply unchanged (Theorem 4, pp. 28–29).
- Show non-existence of the incumbent concept on the host field's own textbook example (inspection game, p. 16).
- Report per-instance final penalty and relative error under a fixed iteration cap, so unsolved instances stay visible (Table 2, p. 27).

**Quotes**:

### S071 · Dynamic pricing: A learning approach (Mathematical and Computational Models for Congestion Charging, Springer 2006, DOI 10.1007/0-387-29645-x_3)
Read level: **full**, of the August 2001 manuscript version, a preprint of the 2006 chapter (p. 1): body pp. 1–31 read, including the formula pages 7–20 (broken layout, legible structure); reference list pp. 32–35 checked for in-text use by search. The printed chapter was not read; all pages below are manuscript pages.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Georgia Perakis (MIT) · **Position in trajectory**: revenue management and pricing {AP} at the 2001 turn to RO; the manuscript is dated August 2001 (p. 1) and appeared as a 2006 chapter (work list), a slow pipeline of the kind Tension T5 records.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP+SD+DA}: A firm prices a perishable product over a finite horizon while the demand curve is unknown and must be learned (pp. 3–4); much current research takes demand to be an exogenous stochastic process with a given distribution (p. 3), and dynamics and competition are rarely modelled together except in differential games (pp. 3–4). Applications named: airline origin–destination pricing, hotels, car rental, cruise lines, retail, e-commerce (p. 4). Capacity is taken as given and costs are ignored, hence revenue maximization (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Key idea**: Price and estimate jointly in a dynamic program with incomplete state information: unobservable demand parameters are replaced by least-squares estimates, and the estimator's sufficient statistics compress the growing history into an eight-dimensional state (pp. 9–12). A five-dimensional approximation freezes the future noise variance (p. 13); two heuristics separate estimation from pricing, a one-dimensional certainty-equivalent DP and a myopic policy (pp. 14–15, 20). Under competition, demand coefficients vary slowly in time (Lipschitz bounds δ), own demand is fitted by an L1 regression that is an LP (p. 26), the rival's demand parameters are fitted by an inverse optimization that assumes the rival maximizes revenue (pp. 27–29), and the firm then prices myopically (pp. 28–29).
- **D3 Model and data**: One product, one firm, capacity c, horizon T (p. 5); linear demand d_t = β0 + β1 p_t + ε_t with Gaussian noise and a price interval (p. 6); unbiased variance estimate with t − 3 degrees of freedom (p. 9); the five-dimensional DP drops the dependence of future variances on prices (p. 13); the closed form assumes no noise and positive demand at p_max (p. 18). Competition: two firms, linear demand in both prices with slowly varying coefficients, own-price effect ≤ 0, cross-price effect ≥ 0 (pp. 24–26); the firm sees its own demand and both price paths but not the rival's demand (p. 24). All data are synthetic (pp. 21, 29–30).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Recursive least-squares update (Proposition 1, p. 7) with an explicit inverse (p. 8); a next-period variance forecast (Proposition 2, pp. 10–11) that identifies the running sums the state must carry (p. 12); the eight- and five-dimensional DP recursions (pp. 12–14). Deterministic one-dimensional DP: an averaging argument shows the optimal prices are equal and unique (Lemma 1, pp. 16–18); Theorem 2 gives the closed-form price, the larger of the unconstrained revenue maximizer and the sell-out price, clipped to the price interval, by a three-case proof (pp. 18–19); the one- and five-dimensional DPs coincide without noise (pp. 19–20); myopic pricing is optimal when capacity is ample (pp. 20–21). Competition: L1 fit with Lipschitz and sign constraints, transformable to an LP (p. 26); rival parameters fitted so that the rival's closed-form best price reproduces its observed prices, uncapacitated and capacitated (pp. 27–29); estimates averaged over the last N periods (p. 26). No convergence or regret result is proved for the joint scheme; asymptotics are delegated to the authors' earlier estimation paper [5] (p. 25).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Monopoly: true demand 60 − p + ε, prices {20, …, 40}, c = 400, T = 20 (p. 21); estimates converge toward the true parameters (Figures 1–2, pp. 21–22). Without noise, 1,000 runs: myopic mean revenue 12,194 against 15,688 for the one-dimensional DP, a 28.65% gain (Table 1, p. 23). With noise N(0, 16), T = 5, c = 125, 1,000 runs: myopic 3,884.6, one-dimensional DP 4,250.1 (+9.4%), five-dimensional DP 4,339.3 (+2.09%) (Table 2, p. 23); the five-dimensional DP has the largest revenue standard deviation (394.2), not discussed (p. 23). The eight-dimensional DP is not implemented, being beyond computational capabilities (p. 21). Other parameter values are said to agree, and gains to grow as capacity drops, but neither is shown (pp. 23–24). Duopoly: two linear demands, prices in [100, 900], T = 150, uncapacitated, 1,000 runs, policies random, price matching and optimization-based (Table 3, pp. 29–30); the tested optimization-based policy assumes the rival repeats its last price (p. 30), not the inverse-optimization forecast of §3.2. Against an optimizing rival, matching earns slightly more than optimizing (3,779,400 against 3,757,700), which the authors concede (pp. 30–31). The off-diagonal rows of Table 3 are exact mirror images, and in the symmetric optimizing–optimizing pairing the two firms' means differ (3,757,700 against 3,804,700), unexplained (p. 30). No run times, solver or hardware are reported.
- **D6 Positioning**: Contrasts itself with traditional revenue management (pricing decisions, capacity given; p. 3) and with demand-as-exogenous-process models (p. 3, refs [7], [8], [10], [11], [16], [17], [19], [29]); cites Cournot for oligopoly (p. 4) and Bertsekas for DP with incomplete state information (p. 9); reuses the authors' own time-varying-parameter estimation with Gamarnik and Tsitsiklis [5] and summarizes its convergence results (pp. 24–25). Ten listed references are never cited in the text, among them [4] Neuro-Dynamic Programming, [6] the LO textbook and [20] Kalyanam's Bayesian pricing model (pp. 32–35; checked by search).
- **D7 Writing**: The introduction is built from lists: four problem characteristics (a)–(d), application areas, two contributions (pp. 3–5); results close with a numbered Insights list (p. 24). The body hedges (computational results suggest, preliminary computational work, at least in this example; pp. 2, 31), yet the abstract and the conclusion state that optimization-based pricing raises revenue whatever policy the rival follows (pp. 2, 31), which Table 3 does not fully support (p. 30). Draft traces: typos (pp. 21, 24, 25, 26), no Theorem 1 before Theorem 2 (pp. 16–18), and a promised discussion of open questions (p. 5) that the conclusion does not contain (p. 31).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Models, DP algorithms of increasing computational intensity, a closed form and simulations (pp. 5, 31). Limits: the exact DP is intractable (pp. 13, 21); the tractable DPs are approximations (p. 13) or heuristics (p. 14); competitive pricing is myopic (p. 28) and tested only uncapacitated with two firms (p. 30); no learning guarantee for the joint scheme. No future directions are stated (p. 31).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: learning sits inside the decision (the estimates are carried in the DP state, pp. 9–14), and policies are scored by revenue, the decision metric, not by estimation error (pp. 23–24, 30); joint estimation-and-pricing DPs beat the separated heuristic, which beats myopic pricing (p. 23).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: no owner, partner data or current rule; demand is synthetic, and the baselines are myopic, random and price-matching policies (pp. 21, 29–30), whereas step 2 of the method prefers the stronger naive rule.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: in the monopoly case tractability is bought inside dynamic programming by state compression and approximation, not by an LP, conic or MIO reformulation (pp. 12–14); only the competitive estimation is kept in the LP class (p. 26). The boundary is printed, as in step 5 (eight-dimensional DP not computable, p. 21).
- Tension T6 (headline vs caveat) ✅ evidence: abstract and conclusion generalize past the concession on p. 31 (pp. 2, 30–31).
- new-pattern candidate: *inverse optimization of a rival's decisions* (pp. 27–29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Compress a learning DP: write the estimator's recursive update, list the running sums it needs, and make exactly those sums the state (pp. 7, 9–12).
- Rank policies by computational intensity (myopic → certainty-equivalent 1-D DP → approximate 5-D DP → exact 8-D DP) and report the revenue each step buys on the same simulated instances (Tables 1–2, p. 23).
- Fit time-varying demand coefficients by an L1 regression with Lipschitz and sign constraints, solved as an LP (p. 26).
- Infer a competitor's demand parameters by assuming it optimizes and fitting its closed-form best response to its observed prices (pp. 27–29).
- Solve the noiseless case in closed form (equal prices by an averaging argument; three-case proof) to explain what the DP policies do (pp. 16–19).

**Quotes**:
- "In this case, Firm 1 is confronted with an inverse optimization problem." (p. 27)

### S154 · Tight bounds on expected order statistics (Probability in the Engineering and Informational Sciences 20, 2006, DOI 10.1017/s0269964806060414)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–19 read; references pp. 19–20 skimmed. The extraction garbles mathematical symbols (a broken font layer: `+` for periods, `@ #` for brackets, missing inequality and minus signs); prose, theorem statements and tables are readable, and formulas were reconstructed from context.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Karthik Natarajan (NUS Mathematics), Chung-Piaw Teo (NUS Business School) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: moment-problem line {CP}, continuing the same authors' SIOPT 2004 paper (ref. [5], pp. 4, 19) and the option-bound work with Popescu (ref. [6], p. 14); Singapore–MIT Alliance funding (p. 19).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP+SD; AP application}: Classical bounds on the expected kth order statistic (Gumbel and Hartley–David under independence; Arnold–Groeneveld and Aven for dependent variables) are not tight under general means and variances (pp. 2–3). Entry: define the tight bound as the supremum over all distributions with the given moments, with no independence or distributional assumption (Definition 1, p. 2), and compute it by optimization (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: Because the maximum is convex in X, the tight bound on E[X_{n:n}] is a minimization over thresholds of the largest threshold plus the sum of single-variable worst-case E[(X_i − z_i)+] (Theorem 1, from [5] and Meilijson–Nadas, p. 4); each inner term has a closed form (Proposition 1, p. 5), the optimal thresholds coincide, and the bound becomes a strictly convex univariate problem solved by bisection (Theorem 2, pp. 5–6; algorithm p. 8). An n-atom distribution attains it (Theorem 3, pp. 6–7). With covariances, the moment-problem dual is an SDP (pp. 10–11). The kth order statistic is bounded through the average of the top n − k + 1 order statistics, giving a bound at least as tight as Arnold–Groeneveld's and tight for identical moments (Theorems 5–6, pp. 11–13).
- **D3 Model and data**: n ≥ 2 real random variables with given means and variances; positive variances for simplicity, relaxed later (pp. 2–3, 9–10); strong duality in the covariance case needs a positive definite covariance matrix (Isii, p. 11). Option example: lookback call on 10 discretely sampled prices, r = 5%, T = 1, means and variances from Andreasen (Table 1, p. 14); price nonnegativity is not modelled (p. 14); the covariance bound assumes uncorrelated prices (p. 16). Simulation: n = 30, means U[0, 50], variances U[100, 400], 100 samples (pp. 16–17).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: (i) Pointwise majorization of the maximum by a threshold plus the sum of positive parts, then expectation and minimization (proof sketch, p. 4). (ii) Tight inner bound from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, attained by a two-point distribution (Proposition 1, p. 5). (iii) Exchange argument that all optimal thresholds are equal, and the first-order condition (8) (p. 6). (iv) Constructive tightness: an n-atom extremal distribution built from the optimal threshold (Theorem 3, pp. 6–7). (v) Bracketing endpoints for bisection derived from the first-order condition (pp. 8–9), and closed-form bounds obtained by evaluating the objective at those endpoints (Theorem 4, p. 9). (vi) Moment-problem duality and the PSD representation of a nonnegative quadratic give an SDP solvable to ε in polynomial time (pp. 10–11). (vii) Sum-of-top-order-statistics relaxation for k < n (eq. 23, p. 11), with the same exchange argument (p. 12).
  - Transferable: for a bound on E[max] from marginal moments, majorize by thresholds, solve the univariate worst-case inner problem in closed form, then show the thresholds coincide.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Pentium II 550 MHz, Windows 2000, under one minute in total (p. 13). Lookback option, strikes 70–140 (Table 2, p. 15): the tight mean–variance bound lies below Arnold–Groeneveld and Aven at every strike and equals Boyle–Lin at the two highest strikes; Boyle–Lin is very loose for small strikes; closed-form bound (17) beats Arnold–Groeneveld and Aven on average, and (18) is tight for large strikes (p. 16); the SDP bound with (assumed zero) covariances is the lowest throughout (p. 15). Simulation (Tables 3–4, pp. 18–19): for the maximum, mean errors relative to the tight bound are 7.73% for (13), 108.93% for (14), 22.86% for Arnold–Groeneveld and 16.91% for Aven; for the second order statistic, 14.21% for (30), 1.04% for (31) and 3.13% for Arnold–Groeneveld. The weak bound of each pair is reported, and the authors grant that cases can be constructed where both new closed forms lose to the classical bounds (pp. 18–19). SeDuMi is named for the SDP (pp. 11, 16).
- **D6 Positioning**: Built on Meilijson–Nadas and the authors' SIOPT 2004 reformulation [5] (p. 4); generalizes Lai–Robbins and Ross from known marginals to known moments (p. 6); the inner closed form is credited to Jagannathan and Scarf (p. 5) and moment problems to Isii and Karlin–Studden (p. 10); the option-bound lineage runs Lo, Bertsimas–Popescu, Boyle–Lin (p. 14). Arnold–Groeneveld's bound is recovered exactly for identical means and variances (pp. 8, 13).
- **D7 Writing**: Three numbered contributions mapped to sections (p. 3); tractability is the stated point of the abstract (p. 1); comparatives are hedged (closed forms that seem promising, p. 3; simulations that seem to indicate, p. 18); a five-sentence summary with one stated open problem (p. 19).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Tight, efficiently computable bounds for the maximum under mean–variance (bisection) and covariance (SDP) information; improved but not tight bounds for k < n (p. 19). Limits: kth-order bounds are not tight in general because the kth order statistic is not convex (p. 12); constructed cases exist where the closed forms lose (pp. 18–19). Future: tight kth-order bounds under mean–variance and covariance information (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a statistics question is posed as an optimization over distributions with given moments (p. 2), reduced to a univariate convex problem and an SDP (pp. 5–6, 10–11), read through the moment dual with an extremal distribution constructed (pp. 6–7, 10–11), and its boundary is printed (k < n not tight, p. 12; open problem, p. 19); tractability is framed as a polynomial-time SDP plus a named solver (p. 11).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the duel-the-incumbent protocol without MIO: the incumbent closed-form bounds are run on a published applied example and on random moment data, and the certificate is tightness through an extremal distribution, not a gap (pp. 6–7, 15–19).
- new-pattern candidate: *subsume the incumbent as a special case* (Arnold–Groeneveld recovered for identical moments, pp. 8, 13; Lai–Robbins and Ross extended, p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Tight bound by majorization, a univariate worst case and coinciding thresholds, finished by bisection (pp. 4–8).
- Prove tightness constructively by building the extremal discrete distribution from the optimizer (pp. 6–7).
- Turn the bracketing interval of a one-dimensional search into two closed-form bounds by evaluating the objective at its endpoints (pp. 8–9).
- Test new bounds against every classical bound both on a published applied example and on random moment data, and report the error of the weak variant too (Tables 2–4, pp. 15–19).

**Quotes**:
- "Particularly, using optimization methods, we develop tractable approaches to compute bounds on the expected value of order statistics" (p. 1)
- "Finding tight bounds for the general kth-order statistic under mean–variance and possibly covariance information is a potential research area for the future" (p. 19)

### S155 · Solving asymmetric variational inequalities via convex optimization (Operations Research Letters 34, 2006, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2005.09.006)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–9 read; references pp. 9–10 skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michele Aghassi, Georgia Perakis (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO period; companion to S029 from Aghassi's 2005 thesis on robust optimization, game theory and variational inequalities (ref. [1], p. 9); Perakis's VI background (refs [22], [25], p. 10). Received May 2005, accepted September 2005 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP+LO; RO as the lens}: Optimization reformulations of a VI need a symmetric Jacobian (p. 2) or a merit function, which is usually at most once differentiable and needs an inner maximization, a bilevel form commercial solvers cannot take (pp. 2–3); most VI algorithms guarantee only asymptotic convergence, and commercial-grade interior-point VI software is not available to the extent convex QP software is (p. 2). Entry: reformulate asymmetric VIs over polyhedral or conic K as single-level, smooth problems that commercial convex solvers accept (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: x* solves the VI iff x* is optimal for the LP that F(x*) induces (Eaves), so LP duality gives a single-level system whose objective is that LP's duality gap (Theorem 1, Corollary 1, pp. 3–4); the result extends to conic K and to the PSD cone (Theorem 2, Corollary 2, pp. 5–6). Sufficient convexity conditions (each F_j concave, F(x)ᵀx convex) reduce to monotonicity for affine F, so monotone affine asymmetric VIs become convex linearly constrained QPs (Theorem 3, Corollary 3, p. 7). The same duality turns an MPEC with a polyhedral lower level into a single-level problem without complementarity constraints (Corollary 5, p. 8). A VI is a robust constraint in which x plays the uncertain parameter ranging over K, so robust optimization subsumes VIs and MPECs (pp. 6, 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: K polyhedral in standard form, or conic with strict-feasibility conditions for the converse direction (pp. 3, 5); no assumption that a solution exists (p. 3); convexity only under the stated sufficient conditions, with sign rules for variables outside the nonnegative orthant (Remark, p. 7); the MPEC case requires the lower-level region to change only in its coefficients (Condition 1, p. 8). No data; three analytic examples with logarithmic and square-root terms (pp. 8–9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: (i) Primal–dual pair LP(x*)/DLP(x*), with strong duality for necessity and weak duality for sufficiency (proof of Theorem 1, p. 4); a KKT-based proof is noted as an alternative (Remark, p. 4). (ii) The duality-gap objective is nonnegative on the feasible set, so optimal value zero certifies a solution, and infeasibility or a positive value certifies none (Corollary 1 and Remark, p. 4). (iii) A size count (Table 1, p. 6): polynomially many constraints replace infinitely many, so polynomial-time solvability of the reformulation certifies the VI's complexity without a VI-specific algorithm (p. 6). (iv) A scalar counterexample shows monotonicity is not sufficient for nonlinear F: F(x) = 1 − e^(−x) on [0, ∞) is concave and monotone, yet F(x)·x is not convex there (p. 7). (v) Convexity of Example 3 checked by symbolic Hessian eigenvalues in MAPLE (p. 9). (vi) The reformulation equals minimizing the classical primal gap function, lifted into a slightly higher-dimensional space where it is as smooth as F (p. 9).
  - Transferable: when a condition says that x* is optimal for the problem x* parametrizes, write that problem's dual and require a zero duality gap; the result is a single-level, smooth system.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a. There are no computations; the practical claim rests on the availability of commercial solvers (pp. 2, 7), and existence and convexity in the examples are argued analytically (pp. 8–9).
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits Eaves for the optimality characterization (p. 4) and the robust-optimization literature, citing Bertsimas–Sim, for the duality device (Remark, p. 4); placed against merit functions and the regularized gap function of Auchmuty and Fukushima, which settled the differentiability question from 1989 on (pp. 2–3, 9), and against the KKT-based single-level MPEC reformulation (Luo–Pang–Ralph), to which it is close but which brings complementarity constraints (p. 8); generalizes the known equivalence between complementarity problems and an optimization problem, recovered when A = 0, b = 0 (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Self-referential optimality by LP duality: x* solves VI(K, F) iff min F(x*)ᵀx over K equals F(x*)ᵀx*; impose dual feasibility and a zero gap (pp. 3–4).
- Use the duality gap itself as the objective: value zero certifies a solution, a positive value or infeasibility certifies there is none (p. 4).
- Certify a problem's complexity by an exact, polynomial-size reformulation into a polynomially solvable class instead of designing a tailored algorithm (p. 6).
- Kill a tempting sufficient condition with a one-dimensional counterexample (p. 7).
- Show that a deprecated tool (the nonsmooth primal gap function) becomes smooth once lifted by duality into a slightly larger space (p. 9).
