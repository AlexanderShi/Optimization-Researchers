### S470 · Interpretable Clustering via Optimal Trees (ML4H Workshop at NeurIPS 2018, arXiv 1812.00539)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–8 (abstract, §§1–4, Table 1, Figure 1 caption, references, Supplementary Material pp. 7–8). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Orfanoudaki and Wiberg match (p. 1). The list's year "1812" is the arXiv-id parse; the file is arXiv v1 of 3 December 2018 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Agni Orfanoudaki, Holly Wiberg (both MIT Sloan and ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the Optimal Trees programme (SW4) carried into unsupervised learning by two students of his health lineage (SKILL Academic Lineage), as a workshop paper (p. 1). The index lists a 2021 journal paper on interpretable clustering (S067, not read).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; AP (health) as motivation; IO as formulation, HS as solver}: Clustering algorithms "use heuristics to partition the feature space" and say little about why a point belongs to a cluster. In health care this blocks adoption, because researchers must explain decisions to gain patient trust and limit liability (abstract, p. 1). Existing fixes fall short (p. 2): centroids fail for elongated or non-isotropic clusters, and PCA plots hide the original variables. The two-step route (cluster, then fit CART or OCT on the labels) "does not prioritize interpretibility during cluster creation". The problem: build one tree whose leaves are the clusters, globally rather than greedily (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: ICOT modifies OCT. Each leaf is a cluster, and the objective is an internal validation index of the whole assignment (Silhouette or Dunn), with no complexity penalty (pp. 2–3, 7). The number of clusters is an output; maximum depth only caps it (p. 3). The MIO is not solved directly; ICOT runs a coordinate-descent local search (pp. 2–3):
  - grow a greedy tree, scanning splits on a randomly chosen feature (p. 3);
  - visit nodes in random order and delete, re-split or split a leaf whenever the objective improves (p. 3);
  - repeat from many greedy starts and keep the best tree (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**: Axis-parallel splits on features normalized to [0, 1], a minimum leaf size N_min and Euclidean distances (pp. 7–8). The abstract claims mixed numeric and categorical data (p. 1). Data: nine synthetic FCPS sets and 200 patients of the Framingham Offspring Cohort with 8 covariates (p. 4). The paper admits the limit of the class: clusters that parallel splits cannot separate (Atom, Hepta) are not recovered (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The supplement writes OCT's tree constraints with ε-separated splits (eqs. 2–10, pp. 7–8). The Silhouette objective (eqs. 12–19, p. 8) uses cluster sizes K_t, average distances c_it = (1/K_t) Σ d_ij z_jt, a big-M bound for the nearest other cluster (15) and s_i = (q_i − r_i)/m_i (18). My reading of pp. 7–8: as printed, (13), (14) and (18) are ratios or products of decision variables, so the model is not a linear MIO, and the paper does not say how it would be solved. There is no theorem, bound or convergence statement.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Table 1 (p. 4) compares ICOT with K-Means ("the current standard clustering practice"), with OCT fitted to the K-Means labels, and with the true labels. All are scored by Silhouette and Dunn on 9 FCPS sets. ICOT is at least as good as OCT everywhere (ties on Tetra and WingNut, and on TwoDiamonds by Silhouette). It matches or beats K-Means on 4/9 sets by Silhouette and 8/9 by Dunn, and the truth scores below ICOT on 5/9 and 2/9 (p. 4). Not reported: run times, depth, number of restarts, which index ICOT optimized for each column, solver or gap.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A method with "early results": clusters similar to K-Means, plus interpretability and no preset number of clusters (p. 5). Printed limits: parallel splits miss non-separable clusters, and cluster quality depends on the metric, which must be chosen for the cluster properties wanted (p. 4). Future work: health applications such as grouping patients and diagnoses (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1–4, 7–8): steps 1 and 5 are present. One joint tree replaces the two-step pipeline, and the incumbent (K-Means) and the two-step OCT are the opponents (pp. 2, 4). Steps 3–4 are absent: the MIO is only written down, the algorithm is local search with restarts from greedy trees, and no limit, solver, gap or certificate is reported. Yet the abstract says the method "approximates the globally optimal solution" (p. 1). The shape is that of Heuristic 3 and Tension T1 (Dunn's 2018 switch to local search).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3–4): the price of interpretability is accepted in words ("a slight decrease in cluster quality score", p. 4) but not priced. Depth is a knob (p. 3), but no depth–quality curve is shown.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–2): the Optimal Trees programme (refs. [1], [9]) extended by the next cohort at a health venue. The same pair later co-authored the 2025 book (SKILL Method 6).
- New-pattern candidate: *promote the evaluation metric to the training objective* (pp. 2–3, 7): the index normally used to choose K after clustering becomes the objective, which removes K as a tuning parameter.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Put the two-step pipeline (cluster with K-Means, then fit a tree to the labels) in the results table next to the joint model; it isolates the gain from building interpretability into the clusters (pp. 2, 4).
- Optimize an internal validation index directly, so that the number of clusters becomes an output and tree depth is only a cap (pp. 2–3).
- Score the ground-truth labels with each metric as well; when the truth loses, report it and treat the choice of metric as a modelling decision (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "we accept a slight decrease in cluster quality score for the gain in interpretability due to the importance of intuitive assignment rules in many settings" (p. 4)
- "recovering known labels in synthetic data does not necessarily translate to meaningful cluster assignment" (p. 4)

### S239 · An exact FCFS waiting time analysis for a general class of G/G/s queueing systems (Queueing Systems 1988, DOI 10.1007/bf01157853)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–16 (abstract, §§1–4, Lemma 2, figures, references). Displayed formulas are garbled by the OCR, so claims about formulas rest on the prose. First-page check: the title and the sole author D. Bertsimas (MIT Mathematics Department and ORC) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author; received 18 September 1987, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a queueing paper from his PhD years (PhD 1988, SKILL Academic Lineage). It builds on his own working paper OR 156-87 (ref. [2], p. 15), probably the paper listed as S168 (Operations Research 1990; not read). Applied probability before the probabilistic combinatorial optimization of the thesis.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD}: Explicit waiting-time distributions in multi-server queues "present substantial difficulties" (p. 1). Beyond exponential service, which "seldom holds in practice", the problems become "really challenging" (p. 2). The entry is his own steady-state result for the number in system [2] (pp. 1, 4), extended to the FCFS waiting time of the MGE_k/MGE_m/s queue (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Arrivals and services become stage channels (ATC, STC), which makes the system a continuous-time Markov chain (pp. 2–3). The derivation (pp. 6–8):
  - condition on the state an arrival sees, write next-event differential–difference equations and take Laplace transforms (pp. 6–7);
  - collapse the equations with the coefficients f(i, w_j) from [2], a two-step strategy that solves for H_j(θ) (p. 7);
  - invert by partial fractions to a finite sum of exponentials (Theorem 1, p. 5; eq. (20), p. 8).

  The waiting time is itself Coxian, so the class is closed under the analysis (p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: s identical servers, one FCFS line, and MGE interarrival and service times (p. 2). MGE distributions are Coxian with nonnegative branching probabilities and real rates, so their stages have a probabilistic meaning (p. 2). Denseness justifies the class: mixed Erlangs converge weakly to any distribution (Schassberger), which makes MGE "a practical modeling tool" (pp. 1–2). The tractability assumption is admitted: the roots w_j are assumed distinct, the author "did not find any case" where they are not (p. 4), and Lemma 2 proves distinctness only for m = 2 (pp. 10–11). Also ρ < 1 (p. 5). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Checks on the algebra: lim W(t) = Pr{T_q > 0} (p. 8), Little's formula and the M/G/s factorial-moment law (p. 9).
  - Unification: MGE/MGE/1 gives the G/G/1 sum of m exponentials, and MGE/M/s gives the single G/M/s exponential (pp. 9–10).
  - Lemma 2 (m = 2): by a sign and monotonicity argument the roots are positive, distinct and ordered, so the computation runs in real arithmetic (pp. 10–11).
  - Takahashi's PH/PH/s tail asymptotics are recovered, and the constants K_2, K_3 that he left uncomputed are now explicit (pp. 11–12).
  - The algorithm is cubic in k and in the number of roots, as far as the garbled formula can be read: polynomial if only s or only m varies, exponential if both vary (abstract, p. 1; p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: FORTRAN 77 on a SUN 3 for MGE_2/MGE_2/s, which is O(s³) and exact when both coefficients of variation exceed 1/2. Newton–Raphson starts each root from the previous one (pp. 12–13). The output is two curves of the waiting-time tail as C² varies (s = 10, ρ = 0.9; s = 5, ρ = 0.8) (pp. 13–15). There are no timings and no run of Ramaswami–Lucantoni's method. The comparison with it is argued only: a finite sum and separable, parallelizable root equations, against a truncated infinite sum and a nonlinear matrix equation (pp. 14–15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Exact and asymptotic expressions, unification and an algorithm (abstract, p. 1). Limits: the distinct-root assumption (p. 4), the exponential cost when s and m grow together (p. 12), and a parallel implementation still under investigation (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1–2, 8, 12): the device is a distribution class chosen to be dense (general enough) and closed under the analysis: the output is Coxian again (p. 8). The computational boundary is printed: polynomial in one parameter, exponential in both (p. 12). What is kept is an analysable class, not an LP or MIO. This is a sole-author SD paper with stated motives, which bears on the Domain Map's "co-authored papers only" label for SD.
- New-pattern candidate: *subsume the incumbent as a special case* (pp. 2, 9–10): G/G/1 (Wiener–Hopf) and G/M/s (imbedded Markov chain), "traditionally analysed with very different methods" (p. 2), come out as special cases.
- New-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device* (pp. 8–9, 11–12): Little's formula, the M/G/s moment law and Takahashi's asymptotics are re-derived as checks, and Takahashi's constants are made explicit.
- New-pattern candidate: *dense rational-transform class as the tractability device* (pp. 1–2, 8): a stage class dense in all distributions, whose rational Laplace transforms keep the analysis finite; it recurs in S346 (this batch).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Justify a special distribution family by a denseness theorem, then take the subclass whose stages have a real, nonnegative probabilistic reading, so that probabilistic arguments apply (pp. 1–2).
- After a long derivation, run named checks on the algebra: a limit, a conservation law (Little's formula) and known special cases (pp. 8–10).
- State complexity separately in each size parameter (polynomial if one varies, exponential if both) instead of giving one global verdict (p. 12).
- Use a proven ordering of the roots to warm-start Newton–Raphson from the previous root (pp. 12–13).

**Quotes**:
- "when we go beyond the exponentially assumption for the service time pdf, which seldom holds in practice, the relevant analytic and computational problems become really challenging" (p. 2)
- "It is remarkable that the waiting time pdf has also rational Laplace transform, i.e. it belongs to the Coxian class of distributions" (p. 8)

### S161 · Worst-case examples for the spacefilling curve heuristic for the Euclidean traveling salesman problem (Oper. Res. Lett. 1989, DOI 10.1016/0167-6377(89)90047-3)
Read level: **full, TR version**: TR pp. 1–7. The file is not the ORL print but the MIT technical report "On the Spacefilling Curve Heuristic for the Euclidean Traveling Salesman Problem" by Bertsimas and Grigni, dated December (the year is lost); cite it as [S161 TR p. N]. First-page check: the authors Bertsimas and Grigni and the subject match, and the title is the report title that Scholar shows for this entry (TR p. 1). The font layer drops the "fi" ligature, digits and most mathematical symbols ("spacelling", "Olg n"), so the constants, the recurrence and the theorem numbers cannot be read; claims rest on the prose.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michelangelo Grigni (MIT Department of Mathematics, NSF Graduate Fellow) (TR p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a note within the thesis programme. His thesis applies the curve heuristic to the PTSP (TR p. 5, ref. [2]), and the a priori optimization paper (S038, card c01) cites this result as the tightness of its O(log n) bound.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + HS; SD for the PTSP corollary}: Bartholdi and Platzman proved that their spacefilling-curve tour is within an O(log n) factor of the optimal Euclidean tour and conjectured that the worst-case ratio is O(1). The note refutes the conjecture (abstract, TR p. 1; TR p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Take n points spaced uniformly on a line; their optimal tour has constant length (TR p. 3). The curve's recursive ordering on triangles splits the instance into half-scale copies and reversed half-scale copies of itself; the copies traverse alike because the curve is reversible (TR p. 4). The path length therefore satisfies a recurrence with long jumps between consecutive subtriangles at every level, and grows with the recursion depth, i.e. like log n (TR pp. 3–5).
- **D3 Model and data**: Points in the unit square; the curve is a total order defined recursively on triangles (TR p. 2). The instance family is chosen for convenience of proof: "any reasonably uniform distribution" on the line would do (TR p. 3). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: A self-similar decomposition plus an inductive lemma on the first and last points visited in each subtriangle, which fixes the jump lengths and closes the recurrence (TR pp. 4–5). PTSP corollary: by "a similar analysis" the expected-length ratio is also of order log n in the worst case; no proof is given (§3, TR p. 5). Analogous line instances exist for the Hilbert curve (the diagonal) and the zigzag curve (a horizontal line) (TR pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: One sentence: numerical experiments "strongly suggest" that points on a random line also give an expected tour length of order log n (TR p. 6). No protocol, sizes or numbers are given.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The O(log n) bound is tight (TR p. 1). Two conjectures are left open: the same holds for all spacefilling curves, and for any ordering of the n×n mesh some subset has an induced tour a log n factor off optimal (TR p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (TR pp. 1–5): the guarantee of the heuristic that his thesis and S038 (card c01) use as a starting tour is audited. Its worst-case gap stays O(log n) and cannot be made a constant, so the curve tour carries no constant-factor certificate. This is a worst-case audit of a heuristic, not a solver gap.
- New-pattern candidate: *tightness audit of one's own bound* (TR pp. 1, 5): the conjecture would have given his thesis's PTSP heuristic a constant-factor guarantee; instead of assuming it, the note refutes it, for the PTSP too (TR p. 5). The negative result is the paper (compare Heuristic 4).
- New-pattern candidate: *tightness instance by symmetry and a forced repeating step* (TR pp. 3–5): the curve's recursion maps the line instance onto copies of itself, which forces a long jump at every level.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To refute a constant-factor conjecture for a heuristic defined by recursion, place points on a simple curve that the recursion maps onto half-scale copies of itself. Then write a recurrence for the tour length from the jumps between consecutive pieces (TR pp. 3–5).
- Try the same family on sibling heuristics by matching the line to each curve's geometry (Hilbert: the diagonal; zigzag: a horizontal line) (TR pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "We refute this conjecture by exhibiting a simple set Sn of n points" (TR p. 2)
- "Hence ak is the sum of the path lengths within each subtriangle together with the lengths of the jumps between consecutive subtriangles" (TR p. 4)

### S148 · The probabilistic minimum spanning tree problem (Networks 1990, DOI 10.1002/net.3230200302)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–31 (all sections, proofs, concluding remarks and references). Many displayed formulas are lost in the OCR, so claims about formulas rest on the prose. First-page check: the title and the sole author Dimitris J. Bertsimas (Sloan School) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author); he thanks his thesis adviser Amedeo Odoni and Patrick Jaillet (p. 30) · **Position in trajectory**: a thesis paper (received August 1988, p. 31). It is the tree member of the family of probabilistic combinatorial optimization problems, next to Jaillet's PTSP and his own PTSP, PVRP and facility-location work (p. 2). S038 (card c01) treats the PMST among its four a priori problems.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO; AP as motivation}: A "natural probabilistic variation" of the MST: nodes are present with given probabilities, and an a priori tree is pruned on each instance (pp. 1–2). The a priori strategy is argued against re-solving each instance (pp. 3–4):
  - Steiner reoptimization is NP-hard on every instance;
  - MST reoptimization is superlinear and not clearly better;
  - the a priori tree updates in O(n), in real time, without changing the network's structure.

  Applications are VLSI processors that fail, blocked communication centres and agents whose exposure must be minimized, admitted to be "some idealization of reality" (p. 4). The problem has never been defined before, the author says (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A closed form for the expected length of a given tree (Theorem 1, pp. 5–6). Edge e is used iff at least one node is present on each side of the cut it defines, so E[L_T] = Σ_{e∈T} c(e){1 − h(K_e) − h(V − K_e) + h(V)}. The form is computable in O(n) for independent presence (Theorem 2, pp. 6–7). It yields NP membership, the hardness proof and the structural results (p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: A graph, not necessarily complete, with a general presence law p(S) that allows dependence (p. 5); independent presence p_i (p. 6); equal p for complexity and structure (pp. 7, 14). The asymptotics use the random Euclidean model (uniform points in [0,1]^d) and the random-length model (uniform arc costs), both with equal p (p. 21). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - NP-complete even with unit costs and equal p, by reduction from Exact Cover by 3-Sets using properties of φ(k) (Proposition 3, Theorem 4, pp. 8–11). It stays NP-complete on complete graphs with costs in {1, M} (Theorem 5, pp. 12–13).
  - Hardness was first suspected from the limit p → 0, where the PMST tends to the NP-complete network design problem on a tree (pp. 11–12).
  - Polynomial cases: if the MST is a star, it is also the PMST (Theorems 6–7, p. 13).
  - The optimal value f(p) is continuous, increasing, piecewise differentiable, and concave for np > 2; Fig. 5 sketches it from the MST (p = 1) to network design (p → 0) (Proposition 8, pp. 14–15).
  - Bounds of the PMST against the MST, with an example showing that the bound "is the best possible" (Proposition 9, Corollary 10, pp. 15–17); a bound on MST reoptimization by backward induction (Proposition 12, pp. 18–20).
  - Asymptotics: reoptimization by Chernoff concentration (Theorem 14, pp. 22–24). The PMST uses Steele's subadditive Euclidean functionals, with an approximate monotonicity replacing the monotonicity that fails (Theorem 15, pp. 24–28); since the proof follows Steele's, "we omit the details" (p. 28). The random-length model goes through Frieze's ζ(3) (Theorem 16, Proposition 17, Conjecture 18, pp. 28–29).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none (no computation).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: In the Euclidean model the PMST is within constant factors of both reoptimization strategies, and in the random-length model asymptotically no worse than MST reoptimization (abstract, p. 1; p. 30). Limits: the PMST is suboptimal in the worst case (p. 30); Conjecture 18 is open; the Steiner strategy is not analysed in the random-length model for lack of sharp theorems (p. 29). Future work: solve network design as a sequence of PMST problems (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1–2, 5–13): formulation first. Re-solving is recast as one a priori problem with a closed-form objective, and the boundary is printed: NP-complete in the simplest case, polynomial when the MST is a star. The nominal class is not kept, since P becomes NP-complete. A sole-author SD/IO paper that states its reasons.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 5, 14–17, 30): the price of robustness is named, but in complexity rather than cost: "one pays for these nice properties (robustness, globality) by changing the complexity of the problem radically" (p. 5). Performance is priced against reoptimization by asymptotic constants and a tight bound. The presence probability p traces a curve f(p) from MST to network design (Fig. 5, p. 14), but p belongs to the data, not to a decision-maker.
- New-pattern candidate: *a priori solution with a cheap update rule, priced against re-solving* (pp. 3–4, 21–30); it recurs from S038 and S017 (card c01).
- New-pattern candidate: *carry deterministic devices into the stochastic version and measure what survives* (pp. 1, 5, 13, 16): the optimality of greedy does not survive. The optimality of star trees does, and so does the MST as a good solution for p > 1/2 (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *tightness audit of one's own bound* (pp. 16–17).
- New-pattern candidate: *limit cases of the new parameter locate the hardness* (pp. 11–12, 14–15): the parameter's two limits (MST at p = 1, network design as p → 0) bracket the problem and gave the first hint of hardness.

**Transferable techniques**:
- For an expected cost over random sub-instances, sum over edges the probability that each edge is needed, by inclusion–exclusion over the two sides of its cut. The closed form also puts the decision problem in NP (Theorems 1–2, pp. 5–7; p. 8).
- Before proving hardness, send the new parameter to its limits; if one limit is a known NP-complete problem, suspect hardness and borrow from that problem's reduction (pp. 11–12).
- When a Euclidean functional is subadditive but not monotone, prove an approximate monotonicity inequality and then follow Steele's route (pp. 25–28).

**Quotes**:
- "one pays for these nice properties (robustness, globality) by changing the complexity of the problem radically" (p. 5)
- "In fact, it is this observation that originally led us to suspect that the PMST problem is hard." (p. 12)

### S195 · An asymptotic determination of the minimum spanning tree and minimum matching constants in geometrical probability (Operations Research Letters 1990, DOI 10.1016/0167-6377(90)90066-e)
Read level: **full, preprint version**: preprint pp. 1–16. The file is a 16-page preprint dated August 1, 1989, not the ORL print; its running page numbers equal the file pages. Cite it as [S195 preprint p. N]. Formulas in the abstract and §§1–5 are partly garbled. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and van Ryzin match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Garrett van Ryzin (MIT ORC, p. 1; his student, card c01) · **Position in trajectory**: the probabilistic-analysis side of the thesis-era programme, written with his first student cohort. S158 (this batch) reuses its bounds as the first term and the upper bound of its expansion.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO}: For n uniform points in a d-dimensional ball, the lengths of the MST, the matching and the TSP grow like β(d) n^{(d−1)/d}. Determining the constants β(d) is "a long open problem" (abstract, p. 1; p. 3). The entry is an old tool from geometric probability, Crofton's method, which the authors call "surprisingly neglected" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Upper bounds come from Crofton's method (pp. 6–7):
  - grow the ball's volume from a to a + δa and condition on whether the new point falls in the shell;
  - a feasible tree joins that point to the nearest point of the inner MST, which turns the conditioning into a differential–difference inequality (p. 7);
  - a scaling argument reduces it to a difference inequality whose leading behaviour gives β_MST(d) ≤ R_d (pp. 9–10); matching is handled the same way (pp. 10–11).

  Lower bounds come from nearest-neighbour distances (Lemma 1, pp. 4–5). The two bounds meet as d → ∞: β_MST(d) ~ √(d/2πe), and the matching constant is half of it (pp. 12–13). Table 1's asymptotic column gives 7.65179 at d = 1000, the value of √(d/2πe) (p. 13).
- **D3 Model and data**: n i.i.d. uniform points in a d-ball of volume a, with n → ∞ and then d → ∞ (pp. 4, 12). Two admitted shortcuts. First, the difference and differential equations share their leading behaviour because ∞ is a regular singular point (Bender–Orszag); a trial solution is checked as "consistent with our a priori knowledge" (pp. 9–10). Second, for matching, the effect of the removed region vanishes as n → ∞ (p. 10). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Each bound is an algorithm. The Crofton construction is Gilbert's exodic tree (sort points by distance from the centre and attach each to its nearest predecessor). A new exodic matching is the matching analogue. Both are asymptotically optimal as d grows (pp. 11–12). The method extends to Steele's power-weighted MST functionals (pp. 13–14). For the TSP, nearest and second-nearest neighbours give the lower bound, and twice the MST or the BHH strip heuristic the upper bound. Hence β_TSP(d) = Θ(√d), and the bound from the MST beats BHH's for d = 3, 4 (Theorem 4, pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Computed tables, no simulation. Table 1 gives lower, upper and asymptotic values of β_MST(d) for d = 10 to 1000, e.g. 0.866331 and 0.928511 at d = 10 (p. 13). Table 2 gives the best known TSP bounds for d = 2 to 10 (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The constants are determined only asymptotically in d (p. 13), though the bounds are close even for small d (p. 13). A failure is printed: the method could not be extended to the TSP (p. 15). What remains is a conjecture that β_TSP(d) behaves like √(d/2πe) (pp. 4, 15; the formula is partly garbled).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11–13): the honest-gap habit in analytic form. The upper bound is a named heuristic's value and the lower bound a nearest-neighbour bound, and the gap at finite d is printed in Table 1, not only the limit d → ∞. This matches how card c01 read S016 and S032.
- New-pattern candidate: *asymptotic regimes for sharp constants* (pp. 12–13): d → ∞ is the regime in which the upper and lower bounds coincide.
- New-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device* (p. 11): Gilbert's exodic-tree constant for d = 2 is re-derived, "considerably easier", and for every d.
- New-pattern candidate: *print the looseness of your own constants and the conjecture that would close it* (pp. 4, 15): the failed TSP extension and the TSP conjecture.
- New-pattern candidate: *revive a neglected classical method from a neighbouring field* (pp. 3, 6): Crofton's method, from geometric probability, applied to a constant of combinatorial optimization.

**Transferable techniques**:

### S229 · Probabilistic analysis of the Held and Karp lower bound for the Euclidean traveling salesman problem (Mathematics of Operations Research 1991, DOI 10.1287/moor.16.1.72)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–18 (all sections, proofs and references). The OCR of the scan garbles most formulas, so claims about formulas rest on the prose. First-page check: the title and the authors Goemans and Bertsimas match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michel X. Goemans (first author; MIT ORC; his student, card c02) (pp. 1, 18) · **Position in trajectory**: received December 1988 (p. 1). A companion of S052 (card c02): it uses the new capacity formulation (7)–(9) of the bound from Goemans and Bertsimas [8] (SODA 1990) (pp. 2–3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + SD; LO as engine}: In computational studies the Held–Karp 1-tree bound is "extremely close" to the optimal tour, with relative gaps often below 1% (p. 1). The worst-case result, Wolsey's bound of at least 2/3 of the optimum under the triangle inequality, "does not capture" this (p. 2). The entry: explain practice by a probabilistic analysis of the LP relaxation (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Combine the polyhedral descriptions of the bound with Steele's subadditive Euclidean functionals (p. 2). HK is a Euclidean functional with finite variance (p. 5). Through the 1-tree form it is subadditive (Proposition 2, pp. 5–9) and monotone (Proposition 3, pp. 9–12), hence HK(X)/n^{(d−1)/d} → β_HK(d) almost surely (Theorem 4, p. 12). The nonuniform case follows from simple subadditivity and upper linearity, both proved with the capacity formulation (7)–(9) (Propositions 7–8, Theorem 9, pp. 14–16). For d = 2, complete convergence follows from Rhee–Talagrand (pp. 16–17).
- **D3 Model and data**: n i.i.d. points, first uniform in the d-cube, then from any distribution with bounded support and absolutely continuous part f(x)dx (pp. 1, 14). The new formulation is valid only under the triangle inequality (pp. 2–3). No data of their own; the gap estimate uses published estimates of other constants (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Any of five equivalent formulations of the bound may be used (pp. 4–5), and each property is proved in the one that suits it (pp. 3–5). They include the LP with subtour constraints, the capacity formulation in which 2 units can be sent between any pair (p. 3), convex combinations of 1-trees with average degree 2, and the Lagrangian dual (p. 4).
  - Subadditivity: product weights over all combinations of the subcubes' 1-trees, patched by deleting a root edge and linking adjacent subcubes (pp. 6–9).
  - Monotonicity: a rational basic solution is turned into equal-weight copies, edges are traded between 1-trees, and vertices are shortcut by the triangle inequality (pp. 9–12).
  - Upper linearity: centre points of the subcube faces plus a short tour patch (pp. 15–16).
  - Proposition 5: max(2β_M, β_MST) ≤ β_HK ≤ β_TSP. The 2β_M term comes from the matching polytope (M ≤ HK/2), and the β_MST term from HK being at least an MST on n − 1 points; also β_HK(d) = Θ(√d) (p. 13).
  - An aside: partitioning algorithms in the style of Karp are almost surely asymptotically optimal (p. 16).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: No runs. Published estimates of β_M(2), β_MST(2) and β_TSP(2) (0.35, 0.68, 0.72) put the asymptotic gap at "perhaps less than" (0.72 − 0.70)/0.70 = 3% (p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The first probabilistic analysis of an LP relaxation by subadditivity, as far as the authors know (p. 2), and a probabilistic explanation of the bound's quality in practice (pp. 1, 17). Open questions: whether β_HK(d) = β_TSP(d), and the exact values of the constants (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3–5, 14–16): formulation first, used as a proof device. Among equivalent formulations, the one in which the needed property has a direct construction of a feasible solution is chosen ("A more concise proof can be obtained using the new formulation", p. 5). Structure is read off the polytopes (1-trees, the matching polytope), not off a solver class.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 13): the LP bound serves as the certificate of tour quality, and its asymptotic gap is priced (about 3%) from published constants. There are no runs and no duel of their own.
- New-pattern candidate: *structural theorem explains computational success* (pp. 1–2, 13, 17): the motivating fact is computational folklore (gaps below 1%), and the theorem's job is to explain it. The pattern recurs from S025 and S015 (card c04).
- New-pattern candidate: *relaxation-centred IO* (pp. 1–3): the LP relaxation itself is the object of study, as in S052, S036, S269 (card c02) and S015 (card c04).
- New-pattern candidate: *staged companion papers from one model* (pp. 2–3): the formulation of S052 (card c02) carries this paper's nonuniform case.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To analyse an LP relaxation probabilistically, prove Steele's hypotheses (subadditivity, monotonicity, upper linearity) one at a time, each in the formulation where a feasible solution for the merged instance is easiest to build (pp. 5–16).
- Merge optimal solutions of subproblems into a feasible solution of the whole problem by taking product weights over all combinations of their supports (pp. 6–7).
- Price the gap of a new constant without computation: sandwich it between known constants of sibling problems through polyhedral relations (the matching polytope gives M ≤ HK/2) (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "However, this worst-case analysis does not capture the efficiency of the bound in practice." (p. 2)
- "We believe that the idea of combining polyhedral characterizations with probabilistic analysis has the potential to lead to very interesting results." (p. 17)

### S346 · Transient and busy period analysis of the GI/G/1 queue as a Hilbert factorization problem (Journal of Applied Probability 1991, DOI 10.1017/s0021900200042789)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13 (all sections and references). Displayed formulas are garbled, and part of p. 11 is scrambled by the OCR. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Keilson, Nakazato and Zhang match (p. 1). The list's "GIG/1" is the title's GI/G/1.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Julian Keilson (Sloan School), Daisuke Nakazato and Hongtao Zhang (ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received December 1989 (p. 1). The analytic half of a pair whose numerical half is Bertsimas and Nakazato's method-of-stages paper (ref. [2], pp. 12–13; S220, batch s02). Bertsimas's support came from MIT's Leaders for Manufacturing program and Draper Laboratory (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD; AP as motivation}: Transient and busy-period analyses have "long been considered as very difficult problems". Yet they matter when systems open or close, controls change, or convergence to steady state is slow, as in manufacturing start-ups and airport runways (p. 1). Recent work went numerical (randomization, integration of the Kolmogorov equations) because of the analytical complexity, and analytical results are rare (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Track the busy cycle B_I together with a signed quantity W⁺_{n+k}, which is the next waiting time when positive and minus the idle period otherwise (p. 4). This gives a two-dimensional Lindley recursion and one key formula (8) (p. 5), which in transforms is a Hilbert factorization problem (9) (pp. 5–6). The general Cauchy–Fourier solution is in closed form but not numerically useful (pp. 7–8). So they solve the cases in which one distribution has a rational Laplace transform (R/G/1, GI/R/1), with roots and Liouville's theorem (pp. 8–11).
- **D3 Model and data**: GI/G/1, FCFS, ρ < 1 (p. 6). The system starts idle, and the first arrival time is the forward recurrence time: "restrictive" for the waiting time, but not for the busy period, which regenerates (p. 2). One distribution must be in the rational class R (p. 8). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - The L roots in the right half-plane are counted by Rouché's theorem (p. 8).
  - Boundedness of both sides (Proposition 1, with a Chernoff bound on the numerator) lets Liouville's theorem give the unique solution (pp. 8–9).
  - Transforms (15)–(16), and moments by symbolic differentiation in Mathematica on a Macintosh II (pp. 9–10).
  - Checks: E[B_p] = 1/(μ − λ) for M/G/1 (p. 11), and agreement with the MGE/MGE results of [2] (p. 11). The classical GI/M/1 (Takács) and M/G/1 busy-period equations and Takács's integrodifferential equation are recovered (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none in this paper; the numerical inversion and its results are in [2] (pp. 12–13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Closed-form transforms and first two moments, and a route to time-domain distributions by numerical inversion (p. 12). Limits: the general solution is not numerically useful (p. 7), and a referee pointed to Asmussen's matrix-algorithmic alternative (p. 8). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 5–8): a problem that the field had handed to numerics is rewritten in exact analytic form (a two-dimensional Lindley process, then a Hilbert problem), and the boundary is printed. The general closed form is set aside, and the class is restricted to R/G/1 and GI/R/1, where the solution is computable. What is kept is an analysable class, not an LP or MIO.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 7–8): an early, analytic form of the skill's epigraph, "Practical tractability not polynomial solvability": a closed form is judged by whether it is numerically useful. There is no tool-change argument and no speed-up figure (compare S052, card c02).
- New-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device* (pp. 11–12): the GI/M/1 and M/G/1 busy-period results and Takács's equation are recovered.
- New-pattern candidate: *staged companion papers from one model* (pp. 11–13): this analytic paper and the method-of-stages paper with Nakazato share the model; the numbers live in the companion.
- New-pattern candidate: *dense rational-transform class as the tractability device* (pp. 2, 8): the emphasis on R/G/1 and GI/R/1 is justified "Due to the numerical tractabilty of the solution" (p. 2); it recurs from S239 (this batch).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a transient or busy-period question, add one state variable whose sign records how the cycle ended (a waiting time if positive, an idle time if negative), so that one recursion yields both distributions (pp. 4–5).
- For a factorization in transform space with one rational factor, count the roots by Rouché, then prove boundedness and apply Liouville's theorem to pin down the unique solution (pp. 8–9).
- Generate moment formulas by symbolic differentiation and check them on the M/G/1 special case (pp. 9–11).

### S158 · The minimum spanning tree constant in geometrical probability and under the independent model: a unified approach (The Annals of Applied Probability 1992, DOI 10.1214/aoap/1177005773)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–18 (all sections, proofs, conjectures and references). The OCR of the scan garbles most formulas, so claims about formulas rest on the prose. First-page check: the title and the authors Avram and Bertsimas match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Florin Avram (Northeastern University) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received April 1990 (p. 1). It follows his own S195 (this batch), whose bounds become the first term and the upper bound of the new expansion (pp. 15–16). A companion analysis of the greedy algorithm for the TSP and matching was then an unpublished manuscript (ref. [2], p. 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO}: Two open problems. The exact MST constant β_MST(d) is "a major open problem" (abstract, p. 1). And the Euclidean and independent random models "use entirely different techniques", so the authors call their unification another important problem (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Because greedy solves the MST exactly, T_n = ∫ (C_n(z) − 1) dz, where C_n(z) counts the components of the graph of distances below z (p. 4). E[C_n(z)] is a sum over k of the probability that a point lies in a component of exactly k points, and its scaling limit f_k(y) gives a series expansion of β_MST(d) (Theorem 1, pp. 4–5). The theorem is proved once under four conditions (isotropy, a scaling limit, domination, negligible large components) (pp. 2–3). The conditions are then verified for the Euclidean torus (Lemmas 3–4, Theorem 5, pp. 6–10) and for the independent model (Lemmas 6–7, Theorem 8, pp. 10–12).
- **D3 Model and data**: Random symmetric distances with F(x)/(c_d x^d) → 1 as x → 0, bounded by M (p. 2). The torus avoids boundary effects, and Jaillet proved the authors' conjecture that the constant is the same in the usual Euclidean model (pp. 2, 10). A Poissonized model is used as an equivalent (p. 3). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Tools: Fubini–Tonelli and dominated convergence (pp. 4–5); a domination bound from the number of trees on k points (k^{k−2}) (p. 8); summation by parts and a "pseudotree" on clusters, bounded by an MST on representatives (pp. 8–9); counting connected graphs in the independent model (pp. 10–11).
  - It recovers Frieze's ζ(3) (d = 1) and Timofeev's expansion, the latter through Kruskal's algorithm rather than Prim's (p. 12).
  - β_MST(d) is the integral of the number of clusters per site in continuum percolation (p. 10).
  - General d: the first term gives the lower bound and Hall's rightmost-point argument the upper bound, which the exodic tree of S195 attains exactly (pp. 15–16).
  - The power-weighted version is proved for a < d; the estimates break down at a = d (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Numerical integration in Mathematica of the first five terms for d = 2 gives the lower-bound ladder 0.5, 0.575957, 0.597831, 0.600822 (Theorem 9, pp. 13–15). The previous bound was 1/2 (p. 15). Under Conjecture 2 the bound rises to 0.608701 (p. 17). The abstract calls the result "very close to the empirically estimated value" (p. 1), but the paper prints no empirical value to compare with (pp. 13–17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: An exact series on the torus, valid in the usual Euclidean model through Jaillet's proof, and a degree of unification of the two models (pp. 2, 10). Limits: the f_k(y) get harder to obtain as k grows (p. 10), and better bounds are possible "With more work" (p. 15). Open: Conjectures 1–2 linking the two models as d → ∞ (pp. 16–17).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2–6, 10): one exact representation, obtained through the exact algorithm (Kruskal), serves two traditions (the Euclidean and the independent model) under one set of checkable conditions. The boundary is printed: a = d, and terms that get harder for large k. What is kept is an analysable expansion, not a solver class.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13–17): results are reported as a ladder of lower bounds. Each truncation is called a lower bound, the improvement that depends on a conjecture is labelled as such (p. 17), and no exact value is claimed numerically.
- New-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device* (pp. 12, 15–16): Frieze's ζ(3), Timofeev's expansion and the bounds of S195 all fall out.
- New-pattern candidate: *print the looseness of your own constants and the conjecture that would close it* (pp. 15, 17): Conjecture 2 and the bound it would give.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the value of a problem that greedy solves exactly as an integral over the threshold of a component count (T = ∫ (C(z) − 1) dz), and analyse the count instead of the optimum (p. 4).
- Prove a limit theorem under abstract conditions (isotropy, a scaling limit, domination, tail negligibility), then verify them per model, so that two random models share one proof (pp. 2–3).
