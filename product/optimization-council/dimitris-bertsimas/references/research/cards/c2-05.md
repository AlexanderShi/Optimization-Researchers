### S067 · Interpretable clustering: an optimization approach (Machine Learning 2021, DOI 10.1007/s10994-020-05896-2)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–25 of the manuscript: §§1–7, all tables and the acknowledgement; references (pp. 24–25) skimmed. The trees of Figs. 2 and 5–9 are images and are known only from the prose; the CUBT trees of Figs. 10–11 survive as labels. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Orfanoudaki and Wiberg match (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Agni Orfanoudaki and Holly Wiberg (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the journal version of the 2018 ML4H workshop paper S470 (card in s01); the acknowledgement thanks the reviewers of the "Machine Learning For Healthcare Workshop, NeurIPS 2018" (p. 24). It extends OCT (S011, card in c17; Bertsimas–Dunn 2017, 2019, pp. 2–3) to unsupervised learning. Against S470's card, the journal version adds mixed-variable distances (§3.2), two scaling heuristics (§3.3), a criterion-sensitivity table (§4.1.1), a run-time study (§4.2) and two more cases, Hubway rides and Cold War employment data (§§5.2–5.3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO as the written model; HS as the solver actually used; AP (health, market segmentation, economics) as cases}: Clustering algorithms "provide little insight into the rationale for cluster membership", and this blocks adoption when experts must explain recommendations (abstract, p. 1). Centroid summaries fail for elongated or non-isotropic clusters, and PCA plots hide the original variables (pp. 1–2). The two-step route (cluster, then fit a tree to the labels) builds the clusters with a different architecture from the tree that explains them (p. 2). CUBT grows trees greedily with one fixed criterion (p. 2). The EU "right to explanation" is cited as regulatory pressure (p. 2). The problem: one tree whose leaves are the clusters, built to optimize a cluster-validity criterion over the whole partition (pp. 2–3, 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: ICOT changes OCT in two ways (p. 5). The objective is a cluster-quality criterion (Silhouette or Dunn) with no complexity penalty, and each leaf is a cluster. Because the criterion rewards separation, the number of clusters is an output; maximum depth and the minimum leaf size N_C are optional (pp. 2, 9). The MIO is called the "framework"; the algorithm is coordinate descent (Algorithm 1, p. 8):
  - grow a greedy tree, splitting on a randomly chosen feature and scanning all thresholds;
  - visit nodes in random order and delete a split, re-split a node or split a leaf, keeping only improvements;
  - repeat from many greedy starts and keep the best tree.

  What changes against OCT: a split is scored by the criterion of the whole partition, not locally at the node, so each evaluation costs more; two scaling heuristics answer this (pp. 10–11). What stays: OCT's tree constraints (1)–(9) and its practice of warm starts plus local search (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Model and data**: Features normalized to [0, 1], axis-parallel splits, a minimum leaf size (pp. 3–4, 9). Distances: Euclidean on numerical features and a mismatch count on categorical ones, combined as α d^N + (1 − α) d^C, with α defaulting to the numerical share of the covariates (eq. 25, p. 10). Data: nine of the ten FCPS synthetic sets, the tenth having no clusters (pp. 11–12); 200 Framingham Offspring participants (p. 14); 1,000 Hubway trips from June–September 2012 (p. 18); 26 European countries by nine 1979 employment shares (p. 21). Tractability choices the paper states: coordinate descent instead of solving the MIO (p. 8), and heuristic pruning of thresholds (p. 10). An inconsistency in the Framingham description: "eight covariates" are announced and six are listed (p. 14), Table 5 has seven columns including CVD (p. 16), and the text later says p = 6 (p. 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - The Silhouette model (pp. 5–7) uses cluster sizes K_t (13), average distances c_it = (1/K_t) Σ d_ij z_jt (14), r_i = Σ c_it z_it (15), the second-closest cluster chosen by binaries γ_it with a big-M (16)–(18), m_i ≥ q_i, r_i (19)–(20) and s_i = (q_i − r_i)/m_i (21). My reading: (14), (15) and (21) are ratios or products of decision variables, so this is a mixed-integer nonlinear program, not a linear MIO; the S470 card found the same. Also my reading: (16) bounds q_i only from below while the objective rewards a large q_i, so as printed s_i can be pushed toward 1 for every point whatever the tree. Encoding the minimum over the other clusters would need q_i ≤ c_it for each other non-empty leaf. The model is never solved (p. 8), so this does not touch the results.
  - Fig. 1 sets a greedy tree (Silhouette 0.688) against "a globally optimal partition" (0.758); how optimality was established is not stated (p. 7).
  - Coordinate descent stops when no move improves (Algorithm 1, p. 8). There is no convergence or quality statement, and the number of restarts is not given.
  - Restricted geometric search: on a numerical feature, sort the n_k − 1 gaps between consecutive values and scan only thresholds above the T-th percentile of gap size (T = 0.9 keeps the 10 largest of 100) (p. 10).
  - K-means warm start: run K-means over several K, choose the assignment by the criterion, fit OCT to those labels, and start ICOT from that tree with every leaf its own cluster (p. 11).
  - No theorem, bound or complexity result.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - FCPS (Table 1, p. 12). Baselines: K-means, "the current standard clustering practice", and the two-step K-means + OCT; CUBT is left out because no implementation exists (p. 11). ICOT matches or beats the two-step method on every set (one tie, TwoDiamonds by Silhouette, p. 12), by an average of 25.7% (Silhouette) and 656% (Dunn) (p. 11). Against K-means it matches or wins on 8/9 sets by Dunn and 4/9 by Silhouette, with average changes of +8.8% (Dunn) and −4.2% (Silhouette) (p. 12). It fails where clusters cannot be separated by parallel splits (Atom, Hepta) (p. 12). ICOT scores above the true labels on 5/9 sets by Silhouette and 2/9 by Dunn (p. 12).
  - The contribution list gives other figures for the two-step comparison: 282% (Dunn) and 17% (Silhouette) (p. 3). My recomputation from Table 1 reproduces the §4.1 figures, up to rounding, as ratios of averages (Silhouette 0.502 vs 0.399; Dunn 0.173 vs 0.023). The averages in Table 2 (0.431 Silhouette when trained on Silhouette; 0.135 Dunn when trained on Dunn, p. 12) do not equal the means of Table 1's ICOT columns, and the text does not say which runs Table 2 used.
  - Criterion sensitivity (Table 2, p. 12): training on Silhouette costs 9.4% of the Dunn score, and training on Dunn costs 10.1% of the Silhouette score.
  - Run time (Fig. 4, Table 3, pp. 12–13): eight sets; the ninth was intractable for the unscaled method (p. 12). Geometric search at T = 0.99 cuts run time by 77.4% (Silhouette) and 59.1% (Dunn); adding the warm start gives 96.7% and 97.0%. Average quality changes by −0.25% (Silhouette) and +4.4% (Dunn) (p. 13). Without restriction (T = 0) the warm start is slightly slower (p. 12). Absolute times, machine, language and solver are not given. The claim of N in the thousands and P in the hundreds "within several hours" (p. 13) is not exercised [my reading]: the largest set has N = 4,096 (p. 12) and the largest feature count is 9 (pp. 19, 21).
  - Framingham (Table 4, p. 16): ICOT, trained on Dunn, beats K-means and OCT on both indices for N_C ∈ {1, 10, 20}. N_C = 1 gives 5 clusters and N_C = 10 gives 7, so leaf size and cluster count are not monotone (p. 14). The baselines run under other conditions: K-means gets one-hot categorical features (p. 14), and OCT fitted to its labels splits only on HDL and Age, which the authors put down to "the lack of data normalization" (p. 15).
  - Hubway (Table 7, p. 21): ICOT wins at α = 0.5 and 0.8, and K-means wins at α = 0.9 (Dunn 0.7737 and Silhouette 0.6874 against 0.0138 and 0.483). The authors blame K-means's lack of normalization; all methods are scored on ICOT's normalized distance matrix (p. 19).
  - Europe (Table 11, p. 23): ICOT 0.344 (Silhouette) and 0.346 (Dunn) against CUBT's 0.140/0.262 (K = 4) and 0.0438/0.259 (K = 5). The CUBT trees are copied from Fraiman et al.'s paper, whose trees and tables disagree (p. 22). The authors grant ICOT an edge on Silhouette, which it was trained on, and call Dunn "a neutral evaluation criterion" (p. 23).
  - Interpretability is never measured; it is argued from readings of the trees (pp. 14–15, 19, 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on OCT (pp. 2–3). It argues against centroid summaries and PCA plots (pp. 1–2) and against two-step hybrids (Jain et al. 1999; Hancock et al. 2003) (p. 2). Earlier tree-based clustering is reviewed (Liu et al. 2000; Chavent et al. 1999; Basak–Krishnapuram 2005; Fraiman et al.'s CUBT 2013): Liu et al. are faulted for not making clustering the primary objective, and CUBT for greedy splits and a fixed criterion (p. 2). The only interpretable-clustering rival is CUBT, met on CUBT's own example (p. 21). Self-citations: OCT, the 2019 book, and *The Analytics Edge* (Bertsimas, O'Hair, Pulleybank 2016, as listed on p. 24) as the source of the Hubway data (pp. 3, 17).
- **D7 Writing**: Six numbered contributions; only the fifth carries numbers (pp. 2–3). Strong optimality wording sits beside the heuristic: "globally optimal clustering trees" in the contribution list (p. 2), against "approximates the globally optimal solution" (p. 1) and "well-approximating the globally optimal solution" (p. 2). No gap or exact comparison supports either. The interpretability price is accepted in words (p. 23). Limits sit in §6 (pp. 23–24). The conclusion calls ICOT "an ideal tool for exploratory data analysis" (p. 24).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the method, a mixed-type distance, two scaling heuristics and three cases (pp. 2–3, 23–24). Limits printed: parallel splits miss non-separable clusters (p. 12); the implementation does not scale beyond thousands of observations and hundreds of covariates (p. 23); the tree is not hierarchical, so truncating it does not give the best shallower clustering (p. 24); the choice of metric is left to the user (p. 23); N_C must be cross-validated (p. 9). Future work: subsampling and feature screening for large data (p. 23); no other directions are given.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1–4, 8, 11–15, 19): step 1 is present (one joint tree replaces cluster-then-explain), and so is step 2 (a warm start from K-means plus OCT, p. 11). Steps 3–4 are absent: the MIO is not solved, and no time limit, solver, gap or certificate is reported, yet contribution 1 promises "globally optimal clustering trees" (p. 2). Step 5's matched conditions are not kept on real data: K-means runs without normalization while scoring uses ICOT's normalized distance (pp. 15, 19). This is the shape of Heuristic 3 and Tension T1.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 12, 23): the price of interpretability is quantified once, −4.2% Silhouette against K-means on FCPS (p. 12), and accepted in words (p. 23). The knobs N_C and α are shown at three settings each (pp. 14–21), depth is not varied, and no curve is drawn.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3–7, 23): a problem owned by statistics and data mining is written in OCT's class, but the model leaves the linear MIO class and is never solved. The boundary is printed as a scale limit (p. 23).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 17, 24): the Optimal Trees programme goes from workshop (S470) to journal with two students of the health cohort, who later co-wrote the 2025 book (SKILL Method 6). The Hubway case comes from his 2016 book *The Analytics Edge* (with O'Hair and Pulleybank; Dynamic Ideas, p. 24).
- Heuristic 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–2, 8): the exact model gives way to coordinate descent with restarts. The claim is softened in the abstract ("approximates", p. 1) and in contribution 2 ("well-approximating", p. 2), while contribution 1 keeps "globally optimal" (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: *promote the evaluation metric to the training objective* (pp. 4–5, 9): the second instance after S470 (s01). The validity index normally used after clustering becomes the objective, and K becomes an output.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Make an internal validation index the training objective, so that the number of clusters is an output and depth is only a cap (pp. 5, 9).
- (D4) Restricted geometric search: scan only the split thresholds with the largest gaps between consecutive sorted values. At T = 0.99 it cut run time by 77.4% (Silhouette) for an average quality change of −0.25% (pp. 10, 12–13).
- (D4) Warm-start a tree search from cluster labels: K-means over several K, chosen by the criterion, then a classification tree on those labels, with each leaf its own cluster (p. 11).
- (D3) For mixed data, take d = α d^N + (1 − α) d^C with α equal to the numerical share by default, instead of one-hot encoding the categories (pp. 9–10).
- (D5) Score the ground-truth labels with the same internal metrics; when the truth loses, report it (p. 12).
- (D5) Report the cross-criterion cost of training on one index (Table 2, p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "In practice, the algorithm is implemented using a coordinate-descent procedure which allows it to scale to much higher dimensions than directly solving the optimization problem." (p. 8)
- "We thus accept a slight decrease in the Silhouette Metric for the gain in interpretability." (p. 23)

### S127 · Machine learning for real-time heart disease prediction (IEEE Journal of Biomedical and Health Informatics 2021, DOI 10.1109/jbhi.2021.3066347)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–11: §§I–VII, the appendix (feature list, reliability-diagram notes) and the references. Tables I–XV and all figures survive only as captions, so table values are known only where the prose repeats them. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Mingardi and Stellato match (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Luca Mingardi (MIT ORC) and Bartolomeo Stellato (Princeton ORFE); Bertsimas is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an applied health paper of the 2019–2026 period (SKILL trajectory). Received 17 November 2020 and accepted 8 March 2021 (p. 1). Its ECG feature set is reused in the group's 2026 Hartford ejection-fraction paper (S678, card in c41, where it is ref. 9). The reference list cites no paper of his (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP; ML; no optimization model; Optuna's TPE tunes the hyperparameters}: Atrial fibrillation and other anomalies are often asymptomatic, and trained personnel must read the ECG (p. 1). Earlier studies used unrealistic data: only Normal or AF, clean signals, small samples. Deep-network projects use data that are not public, "complicating an objective comparison" (p. 1). The problem: classify short single-lead ECGs (9–61 s) into Normal, AF, Tachycardia, Bradycardia, Arrhythmia, Other and Noisy, in real time, for use on a wearable (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Hand-built features of the inter-beat timing of QRS complexes plus generic time-series descriptors go into XGBoost; 110 features are kept out of 880 (pp. 2–3). The wearable is simulated by keeping only lead II of the 12-lead hospital ECGs (p. 2). Five models are trained: one per dataset, plus two cross-dataset models, Chapman→Tianchi on Normal, Tachycardia and Bradycardia, and Physionet→Chapman on Normal and AF (p. 3). Outputs are calibrated by temperature scaling (pp. 4–5), and SHAP gives post-hoc explanations (p. 4). No part of the pipeline is an optimization model of his group's kind.
- **D3 Model and data**: Multi-class softmax with cross-entropy loss (eqs. 1–2, p. 3). Data (p. 2):
  - the Physionet 2017 challenge: 8,828 recordings from the AliveCor wearable, 300 Hz, 9–61 s, US population, labelled Normal, AF, Other or Noisy;
  - the 2019 Tianchi Hefei competition: 20,019 recordings, five labels;
  - Chapman University and Shaoxing People's Hospital: 10,646 recordings, four labels.

  The two Chinese sets are 10-s, 500-Hz, 12-lead hospital recordings with demographics (p. 2). Evaluation uses Physionet's 300 "external" validation ECGs and 70/30 splits elsewhere; there is one recording per patient, so patients cannot leak between training and test (p. 4). Assumptions the authors admit: labels from different expert panels are treated as equivalent, and the challenges' hidden test sets were not accessible, so the comparisons assume random splits (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (no proofs; the pipeline):
  - Signal processing: a Butterworth high-pass filter (0.5 Hz) and a band-pass filter, then standardization, in scikit-learn and SciPy (p. 2).
  - Features: R peaks by Pan–Tompkins; RR-interval statistics; nonlinear HRV indices such as CSI (p. 2). P, Q, S and T are taken in windows around each R peak (B − 0.35α, B − 0.1α, B + 0.1β, B + 0.35β) whose widths were set "through extensive experiments" (p. 3). Moving averages and derivative analysis were tried and dropped as less accurate and slower (p. 3). There are 109 distance features, and 742 TSFRESH descriptors that add "up to 7%" (p. 3).
  - Feature selection: XGBoost with default parameters on each of the five tasks; the union of each task's top 50 gives 110 features (250 minus 140 duplicates), called the minimum without loss (p. 3). The text does not say whether this selection used only training data.
  - Tuning: seven XGBoost hyperparameters by Optuna's TPE, maximizing the average 70-fold cross-validated F1 over 500 iterations, with the same ranges for all models (pp. 3–4). Python 3.7.5 (p. 4).
  - Calibration: temperature scaling; the expected calibration error (ECE) over 10 bins (eqs. 8–10, pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Metrics: accuracy, precision, recall and F1 (arithmetic and support-weighted means), ECE and latency (pp. 4–5, 7).
  - Results stated in the prose: weighted F1 0.94 on Physionet, with most errors in Other and Noisy; a Noisy call only asks for a new recording (p. 5). Chapman 0.96, with AF and Tachycardia confused (p. 5). Tianchi 0.99, with errors from scarce Arrhythmia and AF labels (p. 6). Chapman→Tianchi 0.99 (p. 6); Physionet→Chapman 0.93 (p. 7).
  - Calibration: ECE 0.035, 0.006, 0.001, 0.0008 and 0.02 for the five models. Temperature scaling helps all but Physionet, which the authors put down to its 300-ECG test set (pp. 5, 10–11).
  - Latency: under 30 ms per ECG for every signal (the longest is 61 s), timed by step with 95% CIs and with Physionet stratified by length; a wearable could predict "20 times per second" (p. 7). The machine is not stated.
  - Baselines: no rival model is rerun. Table XV sets the authors' scores against those of the original papers: Physionet average F1 0.93 against 0.83 for the 2017 challenge winner, Chapman 0.96 against 0.97, Tianchi 0.99 "in line" (p. 8). The authors say an objective comparison is impossible without the hidden test sets (p. 8).
  - Not reported: intervals on F1, a prospective or on-device test, clinical outcomes.
- **D6 Positioning**: QRS detection since Pan–Tompkins (1985) and the MIT-BIH database open the history (p. 1). Earlier machine-learning studies are faulted for unrealistic data; deep-network projects (an Apple-sponsored smartwatch study, 1 million 12-lead signals, 12 abnormalities) are set apart because their data are not public (p. 1). Two projects with public training sets are named: gradient-boosted trees with F1 0.97 [33] and a neural network with F1 0.86 [8] (p. 1). The paper claims a first, high performance "across hospitals, countries and recording standards" (p. 1; again p. 8). His group's optimization-based machine learning (optimal trees, sparse regression) is absent from the reference list (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract leads with the clinical problem and stacks numbers: almost 40 thousand ECGs, 7 classes, under 30 ms, F1 0.93–0.99, a first (p. 1). Superlatives recur: "extremely strong performance" and "enormous potential impact" (p. 8). Limits get their own section (§VI, p. 8), but the label-equivalence assumption is argued away from the models' own generalization (p. 8). Small slips: RR intervals "longer than 50 seconds" (p. 5), where the appendix defines pNN50 by 50 ms (p. 9); "a novel method to extract in real time 110 features" (p. 2), where §III-B extracts 880 and keeps 110 (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a feature pipeline, five calibrated XGBoost models, cross-dataset tests and latency evidence (p. 8). Limits printed: hidden test sets out of reach; different labellers per dataset (p. 8); Physionet's calibration not improved (p. 5). Future: real-time monitoring on wearables for patients at risk (p. 8). Not addressed: prospective validation, and the costs of the referral decision.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · contradiction ✗ (pp. 1, 5, 7–8): prediction accuracy is the end metric. A decision is named, sending every non-Normal call to a doctor (p. 5), but it is not scored. No owner's current rule (a cardiologist's reading, a device's built-in detector) is the baseline; the comparison is with other papers' published scores on other test sets (p. 8). This is SKILL Warning sign 4 in his own practice.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3–4, 8): there is no exact model. The incumbents' numbers are quoted, not rerun under matched conditions, though the tuning protocol (Optuna, 500 trials, 70-fold CV F1) is stated (pp. 3–4).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (cross-card; S678 in c41): the feature set becomes a component of the group's 2026 Hartford paper, one programme reusing its parts.
- Taste mark 5 · contradiction ✗ (p. 4): a black-box ensemble with post-hoc SHAP, where his machine-learning programme argues for models interpretable by design (compare S067, pp. 1–2, in this batch).
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 5): the dropped extraction methods are named (p. 3), and the case where calibration did not help is printed (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: *gradient-boosted trees plus post-hoc SHAP for applied clinical prediction* (p. 4; also S678 in c41): two health papers five years apart use XGBoost and SHAP, not optimal trees.
- New-pattern candidate: *shape the evaluation to the deployment device* (pp. 2, 7): reduce the training input to what the device records, and time the pipeline against the device's refresh rate.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3) Simulate the deployment sensor by cutting richer training data down to what the device records: lead II only, from 12-lead hospital ECGs (p. 2).
- (D5) Cross-source test: train on one source and test on another, on the labels the two share (pp. 3, 6–7).
- (D5) Calibrate multi-class outputs by temperature scaling and report the ECE with a reliability diagram per model (pp. 4–5, 10–11).
- (D5) Time each inference step with 95% CIs, stratified by input length, and state the refresh rate the latency allows (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "The datasets that we have worked on are proprietary data of external companies, thus we don’t have access to its entirety, preventing from making an objective comparison to evaluate directly the performances of our model." (p. 8)
- "On the contrary, the fact that our models generalize so well on such different populations is a good indicator that the labeling standards are very similar across datasets." (p. 8)

### S021 · Evaluation of individual and ensemble probabilistic forecasts of COVID-19 mortality in the United States (Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 2022, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2113561119)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–12: abstract, significance statement, results, discussion, methods, funding notes and the author list; references (p. 12) skimmed. The SI Appendix (the relative-WIS procedure, model descriptions in Tables S1–S2, Figs. S1–S9) is not in the file. Fig. 5's per-location values come out as unlabelled rows of numbers and are not used. First-page check: the title matches (p. 1). "Dimitris Bertsimas" (Sloan School, MIT) is in the author list (p. 11), and "D.B." is among those credited with "new reagents/analytic tools" (p. 1).
**Role**: core, in the organizers' voice · **Coauthors**: about 295 authors by my count of the list on pp. 10–11; the OCR superscripts make the count approximate. E. Y. Cramer, E. L. Ray, V. K. Lopez, J. Bracher and N. G. Reich wrote the paper, and Reich (UMass Amherst) is the corresponding author (p. 1). Bertsimas is listed with the MIT team: Michael L. Li, Omar Skali Lami, Saksham Soni and Hamza Tazi Bouardi (p. 11) · **Position in trajectory**: the US counterpart of the European hub evaluation S110 (card in c27) and of the German/Polish study S157 (card in s13). It scores the forecasts of DELPHI, whose model paper is S103 (card in c27). Received 24 July 2021, accepted 24 January 2022 (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP; DA only in that forecasts inform decisions; no optimization}: Forecasts informed healthcare staffing, school closures and the allocation of supplies, and the CDC used the hub's ensemble in official communication (p. 1). The question is how reliable they were: the accuracy and calibration of individual and ensemble forecasts of weekly incident COVID-19 deaths submitted to the US COVID-19 Forecast Hub from April 2020 to October 2021 (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A standardized, prospective hub. Each forecast has 23 quantiles and horizons of 1–20 weeks, and each team designates a primary model in advance (p. 9). The ensemble is an equal-weight mean of quantiles at first (EW23–EW29 of 2020) and the quantile-wise median from EW30 (p. 9). More complex ensembles gave modest gains but more variable performance (p. 9). Models are compared on a proper score, interval coverage, ranks and a relative score adjusted for the forecasts each model made (pp. 2, 10).
- **D3 Model and data**: Ground truth is JHU CSSE cumulative deaths, differenced to weekly incident deaths by epidemiological week (pp. 8–9). Eligibility (p. 9): a primary model; at least 25 of 51 focal locations; all 1–4-week targets; complete quantiles; eligible submissions in at least 60% (47) of the 79 weeks. Twenty-eight models qualify. Forecasts are scored on data as of 16 November 2021, skipping data first published in the last 2 weeks (p. 9). Negative weekly counts (11 cases) are not scored; American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands are excluded; aggregates use the 50 states only (p. 9). Of 3,555 submissions, 230 (6%) were submitted or updated more than 24 h late, with the teams attesting that only data available before the deadline were used (p. 9). Teams were asked for "unconditional" forecasts, but most models were conditional on assumptions about policies and behaviour (p. 1).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (no proofs; the scoring devices):
  - WIS, a weighted sum of interval scores, split into dispersion, underprediction and overprediction, with a worked example (p. 10). Proper scores give no incentive to report forecasts that differ from one's beliefs (p. 10).
  - A standardized rank 1 − (r − 1)/(n − 1) per forecasted observation, independent of scale and uniform if all models were equally good (p. 10).
  - Relative WIS and relative MAE, which adjust for the difficulty of each model's covered targets using all models; the procedure is in the SI, not read (p. 10).
  - Empirical coverage of the 50% and 95% prediction intervals (p. 10).
  - No statistical test of score differences is reported. The post hoc wave analysis is flagged as conceptually hard: focusing on change points "may reward" models that predict extreme changes even when none occur (ref. 21) (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Scale: 28 models, 1,791 submission files, 556,050 predictions, 55 locations and 79 weeks (p. 2). The methods give 3,555 included submissions (p. 9), and the two counts are not reconciled.
  - Ensemble: relative WIS 0.61, i.e. 39% less probabilistic error than the baseline. Seven more models reach 0.75 or less; 18 models are below 1 and 10 (including the baseline) at 1 or above (p. 2; Table 1, p. 4). Only the ensemble had near-nominal coverage of both intervals, and coverage was usually below nominal (p. 2). The ensemble was in the top half for more than 75% of observations but rarely the single best (pp. 2–3, 6), and it was the only model to beat the baseline in every location (pp. 4–5).
  - DELPHI ("CovidAnalytics-DELPHI", Table 1, p. 4): 11,064 forecasts, 95% interval coverage 0.82, 50% coverage 0.46, relative WIS 0.99, relative MAE 1.01. It is 17th of 28 by relative WIS (the order of Fig. 5, p. 8). It is among the 18 models below the baseline, by 0.01, and not among the 18 with MAE at or below the baseline's (p. 2).
  - Horizons (p. 5): 11 models beat the baseline at 1 week (WIS 24.9–34.3 against 35.8) and 19 at 4 weeks (39.9–65.2 against 70.1). Four-week forecasts carry about 76% more error than 1-week forecasts. For the two teams forecasting all states at 20 weeks, WIS was 2.9–4 times the 1-week value.
  - Waves (p. 4): models underpredicted rises and overpredicted falls; the ensemble's 95% intervals covered less than 80% of the observations in the North Dakota and Florida waves.
  - Code and data are public (GitHub, the Zoltar archive), the analysis uses R 4.0.2, and the EPIFORGE 2020 checklist is followed (p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning**: Ensembles from weather and climate forecasting, influenza, Ebola and dengue, and early COVID-19 ensemble studies (p. 2). The paper presents itself as confirming that work: it "supports prior results and confirms" the value of ensembles (p. 5). The CDC partnership and the use of the ensemble in official communication frame the work (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract and significance statement lead with numbers. The significance statement says the ensemble "exceeded the performance of all of the models that contributed to it" (p. 1). The discussion hedges to "most, if not all" (p. 5) and grants that the ensemble was "rarely the “most accurate” model for individual predictions" (p. 6). Key findings come as bullets (pp. 6–7), limitations take two paragraphs (pp. 7–8), and a CDC disclaimer is printed (p. 1).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: an evaluation, public data and code, and evidence for ensembles in public-health support (pp. 1, 6, 10). Limits (pp. 7–8): correlated errors; uneven coverage of locations and horizons; models changing over time; inclusion rules that may exclude useful approaches; revised ground truth; deaths only. The hub collected no experimental data on which model features help, so any such analysis would be observational and confounded (p. 8). Future: test which data streams (behaviour, mobility, variant prevalence) improve forecasts, and prepare submission formats for future pandemics (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠, and the source of its correction, now verified (pp. 2, 4, 9–10): an external judge scores time-stamped forecasts on the field's proper score against a naive persistence baseline, the stronger naive rule that Method 5 step 2 prefers. But forecasts are scored, not decisions. The skill's figure is confirmed: DELPHI's relative WIS is 0.99 against the baseline's 1.00 (Table 1, p. 4), with relative MAE 1.01 and 95% interval coverage 0.82. In S103 (c27), DELPHI ranked first in 6 of 13 weeks on national 4-week-ahead MAPE, a metric and window its authors chose (S103 pp. 14–16). Here, on the hub's aggregate state-level probabilistic score, it sits at the baseline. The two differ in metric, scope (national against 50 states) and window, so they differ in the judge, not in a fact [my reading].
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 4, 9, 11): his group's forecasts sit on an external, prospective scoreboard with rules fixed in advance (a primary model designated beforehand; attestations for late submissions), and a paper carrying his name prints the unflattering row.
- New-pattern candidate: *consortium co-authorship as a contributing modeller* (pp. 1, 10–11): the second instance after S110 (c27). His name stands on a paper written by others that scores his group's model. For the evidence ledger, treat it as external evaluation.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Score probabilistic forecasts by WIS and report its three components (dispersion, underprediction, overprediction) to show why a model loses (pp. 5, 10).
- (D5) Compare models that cover different targets by a standardized rank per target, 1 − (r − 1)/(n − 1), and plot its distribution (p. 10; Fig. 2, p. 5).
- (D5) Fix the evaluation in advance: one designated primary model, coverage thresholds, truth data frozen at a date, and the last two weeks of revisable data skipped (p. 9).
- (D5) Judge a new forecaster against both the naive persistence baseline and the multi-model median ensemble; the ensemble (relative WIS 0.61) is the incumbent to beat (pp. 2, 4, 9).

### S133 · Data-driven optimization: A reproducing kernel hilbert space approach (Operations Research 2022, DOI 10.1287/opre.2020.2069)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–43 of the submitted manuscript: §§1–8, all figure notes and the references. The electronic companion (the proofs of Propositions 2–5, the multidimensional representer theorem EC.2, the non-negative generalization EC.3) is not in the file, so the proofs are known only from the sketches in the main text. Plotted curves are not in the text; method rankings come from the prose. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Koduri match (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthor**: Nihal Koduri (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a DA programme paper after S008 (Bertsimas–Kallus, card in c21), beside S193 (Bertsimas–McCord, card in c20) and S139 (Bertsimas–Van Parys, card in c25). It recasts S008's weights as "local" learners and proposes "global" ones (pp. 3, 6–7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML; LO (two-stage LP costs) and convex quadratic programs as engines}: The problem is min_z E[c(z; y) | x = x0] given historical pairs (x^i, y^i) (eq. 1, p. 1). Predicting y and then optimizing ignores the uncertainty in y (p. 2). Bertsimas–Kallus weight the sample costs by kNN, CART, random-forest or kernel weights (eq. 2, pp. 2–3). The entry point: those learners are "local". They discard data far from x0, need much data, extrapolate poorly and suffer a curse of dimensionality, which is worse in prescription because the whole conditional distribution is needed (pp. 3–4). The problem is to build general, asymptotically optimal prescriptive methods on loss-minimizing ("global") learners (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Reframe what must be estimated: the conditional expectation of the cost, not the conditional distribution (p. 4).
  - Objective prediction (kerobj): for each z, regress c(z; Y) on X by kernel ridge regression. The result is again of the weighted form (2), with weights K(X, x0)^T(K̂ + λnI)^{−1} (eq. 9, p. 10). The idea is derived first with ordinary least squares (pp. 4–5).
  - Optimizer prediction (keropt): learn the decision rule directly, minimizing the empirical cost of z(x) over an RKHS with a norm penalty; the representer theorem turns it into a convex program in n·d variables (eqs. 12–14, pp. 15–16). With a linear kernel and λ = 0 it is the linear decision rule of Ban–Rudin and of S008's companion; the general kernel and the regularizer make the reformulation and the theory possible (p. 16).

  Kept from S008: decisions come from a weighted SAA-type problem (kerobj). Changed: the learner and the estimand.
- **D3 Model and data**: The data are i.i.d. Proposition 2 assumes a bounded kernel and bounded costs (p. 13). Proposition 4 assumes c(·; y) convex and Lipschitz with c(0; y) ≤ C0 (p. 19). Proposition 5 adds a compact X, a continuous optimal rule, a universal kernel, and λ → 0 with λ²n/log(1/λ) → ∞ (pp. 20–21). The authors grant that the boundedness assumptions give constants too large to be useful in finite samples (p. 14), and that convexity is needed anyway for tractability (p. 20). kerobj is convex in z only if its weights are non-negative. They are "effectively non-negative" in the experiments, and the experiments clip them at zero (p. 11). Data: simulated only. A newsvendor with a log-normal "newsworthiness" covariate, a normal temperature covariate and exponential noise (p. 27); a product-placement problem on a graph with d nodes and 2d arcs (pp. 32–34).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - The representer theorem (Proposition 1, p. 9; the multidimensional version EC.2 not read) turns both functional problems into finite convex programs (pp. 10, 16).
  - Proposition 2: a finite-sample bound for kerobj, uniform over z, from Rademacher complexity after the square loss is rewritten as a distance to the conditional expectation (pp. 12–13); the rate is √(log(1/δ)/n) (p. 14).
  - Proposition 3: asymptotic optimality of kerobj for a polynomial kernel of growing degree, via the Weierstrass approximation theorem (pp. 14–15).
  - Proposition 4: a finite-sample bound for keropt from algorithmic stability and McDiarmid's inequality (pp. 18–19).
  - Proposition 5: asymptotic optimality of keropt for universal kernels, in probability outside a null set; λ = n^(−1/3) qualifies (pp. 20–21).
  - Reformulation (15)–(16): a change of variables to the decision values plus spectral regularization λ_spec on an eigendecomposition of K̂ (pp. 17–18). Formulation (14) "has subpar scaling in n and gives solvers such as Gurobi numerical trouble" (p. 17). Formulation (16) solves in 40 s at n = 800 (about 90,000 variables), while (14) does not finish in 2 h at n = 300 (about 35,000). Spectral regularization alone does not help (p. 18).
  - Big-O analysis against kNN (pp. 11–12, 17): kerobj is about n times slower unless D(n, d) or d is O(n). keropt is n_test times faster when D(n, d) is O(n), as for two-stage linear costs.
  - Constraints (pp. 21–23): either add them to (16) and project, which "does not come with theoretical guarantees" (p. 22), or use a quadratic penalty ψ Σ max(0, f_j)², which keeps convexity and the guarantees (Corollary 1), with projection when a decision is infeasible.
  - Decisions that affect y (p. 23): past decisions join the kernel's inputs; the problem in z becomes smooth but nonconvex and is solved to a local minimum. Multistage (p. 24): a representer-theorem sketch whose asymptotic optimality "follows by a similar logic".
  - My reading: the theory and the implemented kerobj differ. Proposition 3 assumes a polynomial kernel, while the experiments use a Gaussian kernel and clip the weights at zero (pp. 11, 14, 26).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Methods (Table 1, p. 25): kerobj and keropt; S008's cart, nn and rf; point prediction by linear regression (pp) and by kNN (ppnn); SAA; and simopt, the true conditional optimum, available because the data are simulated.
  - Metric (p. 25): relative cost (C_saa − C)/(C_saa − C_simopt), so SAA scores 0 and the true optimum 1. Each figure also reports the prediction R² and a "prescriptive analog of the signal-to-noise ratio", (C_saa − C_simopt)/(C_saa − C_postopt), where postopt knows each realized y. My reading: the product of the two equals S008's coefficient of prescriptiveness P (c21 card, S008 eq. 22).
  - Protocol (pp. 25–26): every method's hyperparameters are chosen by decision cost on a validation set as large as the training set, capped at 100. The grids are 50 values of k, and 72–192 settings for trees and forests. The kernel ranges were found by hand on a small instance of each problem, with at most 100 combinations. Test sets have 100 points, and each plotted point averages 30 training/test draws. Software: scikit-learn 0.21.2, JuMP 0.19.2, Gurobi 8.1.1, a Gaussian kernel. The machine is not stated.
  - Newsvendor (pp. 27–33): keropt with n = 50 beats the best S008 method with n = 800. kerobj is worse than rf but better than nn. pp does well because the true model is linear, and ppnn does much worse. The ranking survives changes of signal-to-noise ratio and r = 4 (p. 27). keropt takes about 10 times nn's time and kerobj about rf's (pp. 28–29).
    - Extrapolation, training on covariate quantiles 0–40% and 60–100% and testing on 40–60%: only kerobj and keropt beat pp. In the reverse test (the outer 20% held out), keropt and pp recover the optimum and the others fail (pp. 29–30). Fig. 7 clips relative cost to [0, 1] because of the local methods' variance (p. 31).
    - Dimension: with dense coefficients, the local methods' relative cost shows "an exponential decrease", keropt stays stable, and kerobj does better than the local methods but is still cursed (p. 32). With sparse coefficients, rf and cart stay stable (p. 33).
  - Product placement (pp. 32–37): all methods are recast as LPs (Bertsimas–Tsitsiklis 1997, §6.5; p. 33). keropt with n = 50 beats S008's best method with n = 800; kerobj beats rf and nn. At d = 10, keropt with n = 25 beats every method except pp at n = 800, and pp does not converge to optimality (p. 34). Run times scale identically with a fixed test set; with a test set as large as the training set, keropt is about 10 times faster at n = 800 (p. 34). Truncation: holding out the inner 20% makes the problem easier for every method, with keropt still best; holding out the outer 20%, keropt and pp recover the optimum and the others fail (pp. 35–36). Covariate dimension hurts the local methods less than in the newsvendor; decision dimension changes nothing (pp. 36–37).
  - Not tested: real data, the projection variant, decision-dependent parameters (§6.3) and the multistage method (§6.4); no experiment uses them.
- **D6 Positioning** (pp. 5–7): Stochastic programming and robust optimization do not take data as a primitive. SAA, Delage–Ye's DRO and Bertsimas–Gupta–Kallus's data-driven RO take data but no auxiliary data. Machine learning predicts but does not prescribe. The closest work uses local methods (Hannah–Powell–Blei 2010; Hanasusanto–Kuhn 2013; Bertsimas–Kallus 2020; Bertsimas–McCord 2017). Claimed firsts: "the first general, asymptotically optimal approaches to prescriptive analytics based on loss-minimizing" learners (p. 6), and objective prediction with global learners "has not been done before" (p. 7). Ban–Rudin is limited to the newsvendor; S008's direct-rule suggestion lacked asymptotic optimality and was judged impractical by its own authors (p. 7). The critique targets his group's flagship and is framed as a change of perspective, after crediting S008's "very promising computational results" (p. 3). The reference list (pp. 41–43) has no entry for Elmachtoub and Grigas's decision-focused line, the comparator SKILL Method 5 names.
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract hedges: experiments "suggest that at least the second method" overcomes the curse of dimensionality (p. 1). The method is taught first with ordinary least squares, then generalized (pp. 4–5). After each proposition a paragraph says which assumptions bind and why (pp. 14, 15, 19–20). Admissions sit in the body: the useless constants (p. 14), the unguaranteed projection (p. 22), pp's "unfair advantage" (pp. 34, 38). The conclusion is stronger: keropt "performed substantially better than all existing methods in every condition tested" (p. 38), and its running time "scaled similarly to or better than" theirs (p. 40). - **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: two methods, finite-sample and asymptotic theory, a scalable reformulation, and extensions to constraints, decision-dependent parameters and multistage problems (pp. 6–7, 38). Limits printed: large constants (p. 14); kerobj's possibly negative weights (p. 11) and its curse of dimensionality (p. 32); no guarantee for the projection variant (p. 22); only local minima when decisions affect parameters (p. 23). Unprinted [my reading]: the data are synthetic only, and the multistage method is untested. §6.3 states no identification assumption, though the conclusion calls it "the causal inference setting" (p. 38); S008 states ignorability for the same move (c21 card, S008 p. 14). **Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Regress the cost, not the outcome: for each z, kernel-ridge-regress c(z; y^i) on x^i. The weights K(X, x0)^T(K̂ + λnI)^{−1} plug into an existing weighted-SAA solver; clip negative weights to keep convexity (pp. 4–5, 10–11).
- (D4) For decision rules in an RKHS, optimize over the decision values with the penalty z^T K̂^{−1} z, using an eigendecomposition with a small spectral floor. This turned a 2-hour non-finish into a 40-second solve (pp. 17–18).
- (D4) For constraints on a learned decision rule, a squared-hinge penalty keeps convexity and the consistency theorem; project at test time if a decision is infeasible (pp. 21–23).
- (D4) Finite-sample bounds: Rademacher complexity uniformly over z for objective prediction; algorithmic stability with McDiarmid's inequality for the regularized rule (pp. 12–13, 19).
- (D5) Report relative cost between SAA (0) and the simulated true optimum (1), with the prescriptive signal-to-noise ratio that says how much of the perfect-information gap is attainable at all (p. 25).
- (D5) Test extrapolation by training on the outer covariate quantiles and testing on the middle 20%, and the reverse (pp. 29–30, 35–36).
- (D5) Tune every method by decision cost on a validation set and publish the grids, including how the new method's ranges were set (pp. 25–26).
- (D7) Introduce the method with ordinary least squares, then generalize it to kernels (pp. 4–5).
