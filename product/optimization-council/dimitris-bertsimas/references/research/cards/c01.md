### S038 · A priori optimization (Operations Research 1990, DOI 10.1287/opre.38.6.1019)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–15, including Appendices A–B (pp. 12–14) and the references; p. 16 is blank. The displayed formulas of Theorems 2, 3, 5 and 6 are lost in the OCR, so statements about them come from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Patrick Jaillet (Ecole Nationale des Ponts et Chaussées), Amedeo R. Odoni (MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the thesis paper (received November 1988, p. 1), written with his adviser Odoni (note 01 §1.1) and with Jaillet, whose probabilistic TSP it generalizes (p. 5). Its closing agenda (p. 12) became S017 and S016/S032.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO; HS in §5; AP as motivation}: The paper states two motives: models of real problems in which randomness is a major concern, and the robustness, with respect to optimality, of optimal deterministic solutions when the instance changes (p. 1). The entry is a practitioner's chore. After one instance is solved, many variants must be solved again, and re-solving each one (the "reoptimization strategy") may mean exponentially many NP-hard solves with resources that may be lacking (pp. 1–2). The problem: choose an a priori solution f and a simple updating rule U that minimize the expected cost Σ p(S) L_f(S) over all 2^n instances (eq. (1), p. 2). This is done for the PTSP, PMST, PVRP (updating methods a and b) and PTSFLP (pp. 2–4). Daily mail delivery led Odoni, the third author, to formulate the PTSP (p. 5). Other applications: planning a package-distribution service, job-shop changeovers (p. 5), cash delivery to ATMs (pp. 5–6), and an organization of agents that minimizes exposure (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Solve once, then update by a trivial rule: skip absent nodes, or retrace the tree. The rules are chosen to be easy to apply and natural in practice (pp. 2–3). The paper then shows that in the random Euclidean model the best a priori solution matches reoptimization asymptotically (Theorems 2–3, pp. 7–8). Against the deterministic problem, the objective changes to an expectation over instances, while the combinatorial object (tour, tree, route) stays (p. 2). A second insight drives the heuristics. A spacefilling-curve tour restricted to any instance S is exactly the curve heuristic's tour on S, because sorting preserves order. So a probability-blind deterministic heuristic inherits a guarantee for every instance (pp. 9–10).
- **D3 Model and data**: A complete graph, with node i present with probability p_i (p. 1). The Euclidean theorems take n points i.i.d. uniform in the unit square, one common coverage probability p, and for the VRP a depot at (0, 0) with capacity Q (pp. 6–7). The routing approximation results assume the triangle inequality (p. 9). Theorem 7 holds even when presence is dependent (p. 10). The paper admits its main tractability choice: proofs are only sketched, with details deferred to the thesis (p. 4). There is no real data; the experiments use random uniform instances (pp. 11–12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Reoptimization behaves like the deterministic problem on np points. The proof combines binomial concentration (a Chernoff bound) with Steele's limit theorems (Theorem 2, p. 7; Appendix A, pp. 12–13).
  - The a priori problems are subadditive Euclidean functionals in Steele's sense (1981). For the PTSP, subadditivity is shown by building a closed walk from per-subsquare PTSP tours, joined by a TSP tour through "representative" points that are always present (Theorem 3, pp. 7–8; Appendix B, pp. 13–14).
  - All four decision problems are NP-complete (Theorem 4, p. 8). Membership comes from closed-form expected lengths, obtained by "looking at the probability of every link present" (formula (5), p. 8) and, for the tree, a closed form from Bertsimas (1990). PMST hardness is shown by reduction from EXACT COVER BY 3-SETS (p. 8).
  - Approximation, part 1: guarantees of deterministic heuristics carry over, but they weaken as p → 0, and there the optimal deterministic tour can be arbitrarily bad (Theorems 5–6, p. 9).
  - Approximation, part 2: the spacefilling-curve heuristic is O(log n) for the Euclidean PTSP and PVRP-b, whatever the probabilities (Theorem 7, pp. 9–10). Bertsimas & Grigni (1989) show this bound is tight, refuting Platzman and Bartholdi's conjecture of a constant guarantee (p. 10).
  - A location heuristic achieves O(log n) when the p_i are of order 1/log n or more (the symbol is garbled). It is fast because h(i + 1, T_SF) is updated from h(i, T_SF) in O(n) (Theorem 8, p. 10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Only the Euclidean PTSP is tested (p. 10).
  - Methods: the spacefilling curve, O(n log n) with heapsort, which is faster than evaluating its own expected length and usable when the p_i are unequal or unknown (p. 10). 2-p-opt and 1-shift local search for equal p, sped up by scanning the tours in a fixed order with auxiliary arrays (p. 11). Deterministic 2-opt and 3-opt from random starts serve for comparison (p. 11).
  - Protocol: 10 random 100-node instances are averaged. E[L] is scaled by √(np) and read against the line β_TSP ≈ 0.765 that the asymptotics predict (Figure 4, pp. 11–12).
  - Findings: 1-shift beats 2-p-opt. Spacefilling-curve starts cut the work, though they somewhat hurt 2-p-opt when p is large. For small p, 1-shift is "usually optimal", judged by convergence to the same tour from every start (p. 11). The suggested pipeline is the curve, then 3-opt for large p, then 1-shift (p. 11).
  - Failures are reported: beyond a few hundred nodes, run time and memory become excessive (pp. 11–12). For small p, 100 points are too few to reach the asymptote (p. 12). The threshold below which 3-opt stops helping is uncertain and problem-dependent (p. 11).
  - No timings, machine or real data are given.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper joins three literatures: probabilistic analysis in the Euclidean plane (Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley, Karp 1977, Steele 1981), analysis with random lengths (Karp 1979, Frieze, Steele), and the sparse PCOP literature (Jaillet; Bertsimas 1988) (pp. 4–5). Stochastic-programming work on VRPs (Stewart–Golden, Dror–Trudeau, Laporte–Louveaux) is set aside as differently defined, not attacked (p. 5). Network reliability theory is distinguished (p. 5). A published bound for a TSFLP heuristic was improved by Bertsimas (1989) (p. 5). Credit is explicit: Jaillet introduced the PTSP, Odoni formulated it, and Bertsimas (1988) introduced the a priori framework (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing**: The general framing comes first (any combinatorial problem), followed by two "natural questions": the measure of effectiveness and the updating rule (p. 2). The conjecture of exact asymptotic equivalence sits beside the theorem (pp. 2, 8). The paper puts its emphasis on concepts and results "rather than detailed derivations" (p. 4). The conclusion claims "excellent potential" (p. 12). Hedges sit on the empirical claims ("it is hoped", "seems", p. 11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a framework; asymptotic, complexity and approximation theory; a small heuristic study (pp. 4, 12). The limits are concrete: node count and memory, small p, and a bound that says nothing as p → 0 (pp. 9, 11–12). Future work: general random demands, called "especially important" for the VRP, and dynamic arrivals with queueing effects, the latter already begun with van Ryzin (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: formulation first. Re-solving every instance is recast as one a priori problem with an expected-cost objective (pp. 1–2), and the boundary is printed: NP-completeness (p. 8) and a size limit of a few hundred nodes (pp. 11–12). But the class is neither LP nor MIO and is not preserved; the analysis is probabilistic and heuristic.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: the property priced is one stable, easily updated solution. Against the reoptimization ideal, its price is asymptotically nil (p. 8), and the worst-case ratio grows as the coverage p → 0 (p. 9). There is no knob or curve; the price is a comparison of asymptotic constants.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: the spacefilling-curve tour warm-starts local search (p. 11), and the benchmark is a known asymptote (p. 10). Optimality is inferred from the same tour being reached from every start, with no certificate, though the hedge "usually" stays (p. 11).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅, for the lineage step only: the closing agenda (p. 12) names the next two problems. They became S017 (sole author) and S016/S032 (with his student van Ryzin, note 04 §B.6). The gate, course and book steps are not visible here.
- New-pattern candidate: *a priori solution with a cheap update rule, priced against re-solving* (pp. 1–2, 8–9; also S017). In 1990 this answers the premise of SKILL Heuristic 5 (one optimization solved repeatedly online) with an a priori policy instead of learning.
- New-pattern candidate: *asymptotic constant as the yardstick for heuristics* (pp. 10–12).
- New-pattern candidate: *asymptotic regimes for sharp constants* (pp. 6–8).
- New-pattern candidate: *carry deterministic devices into the stochastic version and measure what survives*. The paper's "first natural question" is how heuristics for the deterministic problem perform on the probabilistic one (Theorems 5–7, pp. 9–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When one problem is re-solved for many perturbed instances, define a problem that is solved once and updated by a trivial rule. Judge it by expected cost against the re-solve ideal, both in the worst case and asymptotically (pp. 1–2, 8–9).
- (D4) To evaluate an expected cost over 2^n instances, sum edge by edge, weighting each link by its probability of being used (p. 8; S017 p. 5 spells out the rule).
- (D4) To prove a √n law for a stochastic Euclidean functional, check Steele's conditions. For subadditivity, glue the subsquare solutions with a tour through designated, always-present representatives (pp. 13–14).
- (D4) Prefer heuristics whose output is unchanged by deleting points, such as sorting along a spacefilling curve. They inherit a guarantee on every instance and need no probabilities (pp. 9–10).
- (D5) Benchmark heuristics on random instances against the asymptotic constant (length/√(np) against β), and say when instances are too small to reach the asymptote (pp. 11–12).

**Quotes**:
- "The second motivation is an interest in investigating the robustness, with respect to optimality, of optimal solutions to deterministic problems, when the instances for which these problems have been solved are modified." (p. 1)
- "Yet, a priori strategies behave asymptotically equally well on average with reoptimization strategies on Euclidean problems." (p. 8)

### S016 · A stochastic and dynamic vehicle routing problem in the Euclidean plane (Operations Research 1991, DOI 10.1287/opre.39.4.601)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–15 with the references. Figures 1–5 survive only as extracted labels, and several displayed bounds are garbled, so the constants below come from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthor**: Garrett van Ryzin (MIT, p. 1; his PhD student, thesis 1991, note 04 §B.6) · **Position in trajectory**: the step announced in S038 that adds a dynamic component (S038 p. 12). The problem came from a colleague: Harilaos Psaraftis brought dynamic vehicle routing to Bertsimas's attention (acknowledgment, p. 14). Received June 1989, accepted October 1990 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD; AP as motivation}: The entry is a modelling gap. The TSP is a deterministic, static approximation of problems that are probabilistic and change over time, and minimizing travel ignores the cost of waiting (p. 1). The applications are chosen for the waiting objective: replenishing remote sites (waiting means inventory), taxi dispatching, emergency service, downtime under mobile repair, and sales leads (p. 1). The model has five features: waiting, not travel, as the objective; stochastic information; dynamic demands; real-time policies; queueing (p. 2). The problem is to find a routing policy that minimizes the average system time. Demands arrive as a Poisson process, at uniform independent locations, and each needs i.i.d. on-site service (pp. 1, 4). Earlier work leaves the gap open. Psaraftis's dynamic TSP yielded insights but no general results. A priori optimization handles randomness but is "inherently static". The stochastic queue median problem is a network special case run under FCFS (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Bracket the unknown optimal policy instead of computing it. Lower bounds valid for all policies (light and heavy traffic) come first; simple policies are then analysed and compared with them (p. 3). Insights:
  - A policy that stays stable for all ρ < 1 must cut the travel per service as ρ → 1 (p. 8).
  - Batching demands into TSP tours makes the sets a GI/G/1 queue, so classical queueing bounds apply (p. 10).
  - System time grows like λA/(1 − ρ)² rather than (1 − ρ)⁻¹, and it depends only on the first moment of the service time (pp. 6–7, 14).

  What changes against queueing theory: travel makes service requirements dependent, so the M/G/1 method cannot be applied as it stands (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**: A convex bounded region of area A, often taken as a square "to simplify the calculations" (the restriction is said to be relaxable, p. 4). One uncapacitated vehicle at unit speed; Poisson rate λ; i.i.d. uniform locations; i.i.d. on-site service with mean s̄ > 0 and a finite second moment; no preemption; ρ = λs̄; steady-state limits are assumed to exist (p. 4). There is no real data. The numerical example uses A = 1, s̄ = 0.1 and s² = 0.01 (zero variance) across a range of ρ (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Light-traffic bound: split system time into waiting due to travel, waiting due to others' service, and own service. The server is placed at its best a priori point (the median), and Little's law handles the service wait (Theorem 1, p. 5).
  - Heavy-traffic bound: T* grows at least like γ²λA/(1 − ρ)², with γ = 2/(3√(2π)) ≈ 0.266 (Theorem 2, p. 5). It comes from a bound on mean travel per service, built from a tagged demand, Wald's equation with a stopping time, and Lemma 1, which rests on the Haimovich–Magnanti extremal result for the k-median and on stochastic ordering (p. 6). The stability condition closes the argument (p. 5).
  - FCFS behaves as an M/G/1 queue (Pollaczek–Khinchin formula), with a worst-case light-traffic ratio of about 1.36 (p. 7).
  - SQM waits at the median, serves FCFS and returns after each service. It is asymptotically optimal as λ → 0 (Theorem 3, p. 8).
  - PART divides the region into m² subregions. A "projection rule" makes each starting point uniform and independent, which eases the analysis, and the policy is analysed as a symmetric cyclic queue. This proves that a stable policy exists for every ρ < 1 and that the stability condition does not depend on the region (Theorem 4, p. 9).
  - The TSP policy groups n demands into sets that form a GI/G/1 queue with Erlang interarrivals. It is analysed with Kingman's bound and the BHH asymptotics, and n is set by minimizing a convex upper bound after a change of variable y (pp. 10–11).
  - The SFC policy is bounded through a quadratic inequality from Jensen's inequality and the 2√(NA) path bound (p. 11).
  - The NN policy gets only a conditional bound, under an assumption (45) that is not proved (p. 12).
  - Ratios to the bound: about 15 for PART, or 4.2 if the conjectured γ = 1/2 holds (p. 9). The TSP policy's system time is about half of PART's (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Simulation is used only where the analysis fails or is loose (SFC, NN).
  - Protocol: batch means, with batches of 200 times the minimum average number in system from Theorem 2, and a t-test; runs stop when the 99% confidence interval is narrower than 10% of the estimate. Regenerative methods were rejected because busy periods are "almost nonterminating" at high utilization (p. 12).
  - Estimated constants: γ_SFC ≈ 0.66 and γ_NN ≈ 0.64, for ρ from 0.5 to 0.8 (p. 12).
  - The numerical example compares all policies, with the PART and TSP parameters tuned for each ρ (p. 13). Reported: FCFS and SQM become unstable above ρ = 0.2; NN is best and SFC close behind; NN beats SFC even in light traffic (p. 13).
  - Admitted limits: NN has no proved guarantee (p. 14); heuristic tours, used in practice, would add some time (p. 11).
  - No data and no solver are involved.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper calls its model a "departure" from static deterministic VRPs (pp. 1, 13). It proves Psaraftis's conjecture that at low arrival rates the problem behaves like a 1-median problem (pp. 2, 8). It calls its result "somewhat stronger" than the network location results because its bound covers all policies, not only FCFS ones (p. 8). It credits Bartholdi and Platzman for the SFC policy and thanks Platzman (pp. 11, 14). It places his own a priori optimization among the static approaches (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract states the results in words: optimal in light traffic, within a constant factor in heavy traffic, faster growth than in classical queues, stability independent of geometry (p. 1). A toolkit section collects every borrowed result first (pp. 3–4). Each bound is followed by "a few comments" on its physical meaning (pp. 6–7). The paper is candid about looseness: γ "is not tight" (p. 7); the PART factor is "quite large (about 15)" (p. 9); the SFC constant is "probably too large" (pp. 11–12); the NN assumption "has not been established formally" (p. 12). A simpler proof with a weaker constant was left out (p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The paper offers a model, lower bounds over all policies, policy analyses and simulation estimates (pp. 13–14). Practical outputs: a rule for sizing the routes of the TSP policy (p. 11); nonparametric SFC and NN policies called "self-regulating" (p. 13); TSP and SFC called fairer than NN because they partly keep FCFS order (p. 13). Limits: no guarantee for NN, loose constants, one uncapacitated vehicle (pp. 7, 9, 14). Future work: m vehicles, with system time ∝ λA/(m²(1 − ρ)²), and capacity, under which stability depends on the size of the region. Both were already under way (p. 14) and were published as S032.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: formulation first. The TSP's static approximation gives way to a queueing–routing model whose objective, waiting, is the real cost to the owner (pp. 1–2). What is preserved is an analysable queue (M/G/1, GI/G/1, cyclic), reached through policy design (pp. 7–11), not an LP or MIO class. This is practice evidence for SD, which SKILL.md voices from co-authored papers only.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: an honest certificate in analytic form. A lower bound over all policies serves as the certificate and the ratio of each policy to the bound as the gap. The looseness is printed, and the empirically best policy (NN) is never called guaranteed (pp. 7, 9, 12, 14). There is no warm start and no MIO.
- New-pattern candidate: *lower bound over all policies, then constant-factor policies* (pp. 3, 5–11).
- New-pattern candidate: *asymptotic regimes for sharp constants*, here light and heavy traffic, whose constants rank the policies (pp. 5–14).
- New-pattern candidate: *simulate only where proofs fail, with a stated statistical stopping rule* (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: *print the looseness of your own constants and the conjecture that would close it* (pp. 7, 9).
- New-pattern candidate: *carry deterministic devices into the stochastic version and measure what survives*. Optimal TSP tours and the spacefilling curve from static routing become dynamic policies, measured against the bound over all policies (pp. 10–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Lower-bound every policy by giving the server its best a priori position (the median), and by a nearest-neighbour argument on a tagged customer with Wald's equation (pp. 5–6).
- (D4) Make a dynamic policy analysable by batching demands into sets that form a GI/G/1 or cyclic queue. Then apply Kingman's bound with the asymptotics of Euclidean TSP tours (pp. 9–11).
- (D4) Add details that ease the analysis of a policy (a projection rule that makes starting points uniform and independent), and state that in practice a smarter rule would be used (p. 8).
- (D4) Set a policy's parameter by minimizing its upper bound, after rewriting the parameter as a ratio to the critical value allowed by stability (pp. 10–11).
- (D5) Estimate unknown constants by simulation, with batch means sized from the theoretical bound and a stopping rule on the width of the confidence interval. Plot the results against the predicted scaling variable λA/(1 − ρ)² to check the slope (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "Yet, in many practical applications, the TSP is a deterministic, static approximation to a problem which is, in reality, both probabilistic and time varying (dynamic)." (p. 1)
- "First, we establish some lower bounds on the average system time for all policies." (p. 3)

### S017 · A Vehicle Routing Problem with Stochastic Demand (Operations Research 1992, DOI 10.1287/opre.40.3.574)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–12 with the references. The displayed formulas of Theorems 1–5 and Propositions 3–5 are partly garbled, so statements rest on the prose and the legible parts.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none. He is the sole author and thanks Odoni and Jaillet in the first person (p. 12). · **Position in trajectory**: a paper from the thesis period (received September 1988; revised February and November 1990; accepted May 1991, p. 1). It is the step announced in S038 that generalizes demand beyond binary (S038 p. 12), and it extends the thesis from binary to arbitrary discrete demand (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO; AP as motivation}: The paper calls its problem a "natural probabilistic variation" of the VRP. One vehicle of limited capacity serves n fixed locations, and their demands are known only in distribution when the routes are designed; UPS is the example (p. 1). The entry is the operator's case against re-designing routes every day. Resources may be lacking; the effort may not pay; the operator may value regularity and personalization of service; and demand may be unknown until the visit (p. 1). The problem is to find an a priori sequence of minimum expected length under one of two strategies. Under Strategy a, the vehicle visits everyone in order, serves those with demand and returns to the depot when its capacity is reached; under Strategy b, it skips customers with no demand (pp. 1–2). Applications: planning a delivery company's resources, collecting cash from bank branches under a safety cap, replenishing ATMs, delivering parcels (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Make the expected cost of a fixed sequence computable, although there are (K + 1)^n demand instances. The device is to track the probability that capacity is reached (γ_i) or exceeded (δ_i) at each node, through a recursion on cumulative demand (Theorems 1–2, pp. 4–6). The a priori optimum and the reoptimization strategy are then bracketed by a radial lower bound and by upper bounds from the cyclic heuristic (pp. 7–9), and the random Euclidean limit is taken (pp. 10–11). The paper sets itself apart from stochastic programming, which solves small problems optimally, by offering an a priori solution, worst- and average-case bounds, and a comparison with reoptimization, "the best one can possibly do" (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**: A complete network with symmetric distances (the results can be modified for the nonsymmetric case) and the triangle inequality (p. 3). Integer capacity Q; independent discrete demands with known distributions; no demand larger than K ≤ Q (pp. 3, 6). The paper says this last assumption "removes the consideration of multiple returns to the depot from the same node" (p. 6). The sharper bounds need identically distributed demand (pp. 8–9). The asymptotics take i.i.d. uniform points in the unit square with the depot at the origin (p. 10). They are said to extend to d dimensions and to other densities; two dimensions were chosen because "the geometry is clearer" (p. 11). Strategy b assumes demand is known before the route starts, in which case reoptimization is "clearly better", so the model rests on practical constraints (p. 2). There is no data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Closed forms. Under Strategy a, the cost is the tour length plus Σ γ_i s(i, i + 1), where the detour is s(i, j) = d(i, 0) + d(0, j) − d(i, j). Strategy b adds PTSP terms in which d(i, j) counts when i and j are present and every node between them has zero demand. The probabilities f(m, r) come from a recursion, so evaluation takes polynomial time (Theorems 1–2, pp. 4–6).
  - Ordering: E[R_VRP] ≤ E[L^b] ≤ E[L^a], from the triangle inequality and optimality (Proposition 1, p. 7).
  - Lower bound: every subtour costs at least 2d(0, r) for each customer r on it. Weighting by demand and using the capacity gives the radial term (2/Q) Σ d(0, i) E[D_i], taken in a maximum with the TSP or PTSP bounds (Proposition 2, p. 7).
  - Upper bounds:
    - The cyclic heuristic of Haimovitch and Rinnooy Kan tries all n rotations of a TSP tour and keeps the one with the best expected length (p. 8).
    - Breakpoints are bounded through E[⌈W/Q⌉] (Proposition 3, p. 8).
    - Nonidentical demands are handled through min{E[D_i], 1} (Proposition 4, pp. 8–9).
    - Reoptimization is bounded from above via Altinkemer and Gavish (Proposition 5, p. 9).
  - Guarantees:
    - Started from the optimal TSP tour, the cyclic heuristic is within a factor of two for large n (Theorem 3, p. 9; the display is partly garbled). When every customer has positive demand, it is within 2 + O(1/n) of reoptimization itself (p. 9).
    - Started from Christofides's tour, it gives a polynomial heuristic with a constant factor (p. 9).
    - With nonidentical demands the ratio is at most Q + 1 (Theorem 4, p. 10).
  - Asymptotics: three regimes of capacity growth: Q constant, Q_n/√n → 0, and Q_n/√n → ∞. The radial term dominates in the first two, the local TSP term in the third, and Q_n = Θ(√n) is left open (Theorem 5, pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: None reported. A conjecture about nonidentical demands rests only on "preliminary computational experience", with no numbers (p. 10). A computational comparison of the a priori and reoptimization strategies is listed as future work (p. 12). Instances, solvers and metrics: n/a.
- **D6 Positioning**: Review volumes on the VRP serve as background. Stochastic VRPs solved by stochastic programming (Stewart–Golden, Dror–Trudeau, Laporte–Louveaux) are set apart, not criticized (p. 3). Jaillet's 1985 thesis is credited with the a priori idea, and Bertsimas's 1988 thesis with its generalization (p. 3). The paper builds on Haimovitch and Rinnooy Kan (the cyclic heuristic and the growth regimes) and on Altinkemer and Gavish (pp. 7–10). It recovers the Jaillet–Odoni closed form as a special case (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing**: The practical rationale comes first (the operator's constraints, p. 1), then a worked figure of the two strategies (pp. 1–2), a list of applications (pp. 2–3), and an outline that promises "limitations and applicability" in the conclusion (p. 3). Claims are graded by their evidence: "analytical evidence" (pp. 11–12); "It is a fair conclusion to say …" (p. 12). Weak bounds are named as such ("not particularly strong", p. 10). Conjectures are stated as conjectures: a constant factor independent of Q (p. 10), and a factor of 1 for Strategy b (p. 11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Evaluation algorithms, bounds, heuristics with guarantees, and asymptotic equivalence, summed up as "a serious and practical alternative" to reoptimization (p. 12), useful "especially in the absence of intense computational power" (p. 12). Limits: the bounds for nonidentical demand are not tight, an open problem (pp. 11–12); the Θ(√n) regime is not covered (p. 11); K ≤ Q is assumed (p. 6); there is no computational comparison (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: class preservation in a 1992 sense. The device keeps the stochastic objective as cheap to evaluate as a deterministic one (a polynomial closed form with a recursion), across (K + 1)^n instances (pp. 4–6). The optimization stays NP-hard and is handled by heuristics with guarantees, not by LP or MIO. The boundary is printed: the Θ(√n) regime and the loose bounds for nonidentical demand (pp. 10–12).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: the property the operator wants is regularity and personalization, with no daily recomputation (p. 1). It is priced against reoptimization: a factor of two in the worst case (p. 9), and nothing asymptotically when Q is constant or o(√n) (pp. 10–11). There is no knob or curve; the growth regime of Q plays that role.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the operator's constraints shape the formulation (pp. 1–2, 4). But the comparator is the full-information ideal of reoptimization, not the operator's current rule, and there is no data or field test (pp. 3, 12).
- Skill correction (not a method link): this is a sole-author SD paper that states its approach (p. 3) and judges it (p. 12). SKILL.md's Domain Map voices SD from co-authored papers only and records no stated methodology for it; that claim needs qualifying.
- New-pattern candidate: *a priori solution with a cheap update rule, priced against re-solving* (pp. 1–3, 11–12; also S038).
- New-pattern candidate: *lower bound over all policies, then constant-factor policies* (pp. 7–10).
- New-pattern candidate: *asymptotic regimes for sharp constants*, here by capacity growth (pp. 10–11).
- New-pattern candidate: *carry deterministic devices into the stochastic version and measure what survives*. The cyclic heuristic of the deterministic VRP, seeded with TSP, PTSP or Christofides tours, is analysed under stochastic demand (pp. 7–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) For a recourse cost triggered by capacity, compute the probability that capacity is hit at each node by a recursion on cumulative demand. The expected cost of a fixed sequence then takes polynomial time (pp. 4–6).
- (D4) Radial lower bound: each subtour costs at least twice the depot distance of any customer on it; weight by demand and divide by capacity (p. 7).
- (D4) Cyclic heuristic: evaluate every rotation of a good deterministic tour under the stochastic objective and keep the best. Bound the best by the average over all rotations (p. 8).
- (D4) Split an asymptotic analysis by how fast a structural parameter grows (capacity against √n). Say which cost term dominates in each regime, and name the regime left open (pp. 10–11).
- (D7) Before introducing an a priori model, list the operator's reasons not to re-solve: resources, effort, regularity, and when information arrives (p. 1).

**Quotes**:

### S005 · Simulated annealing (Statistical Science 1993, DOI 10.1214/ss/1177011077)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–6, the whole six-page review with its references.
**Role**: core · **Coauthor**: John Tsitsiklis (MIT EECS; both were associate professors, p. 1) · **Voice**: the two authors' own review, with no new theorems · **Position in trajectory**: the HS anchor. Its verdict is the 1993 side of Tension T2, written before simulated annealing became a working tool in his group from 2009 on (note 03 B5.5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {HS; the Markov-chain analysis is shared with SD}: Simulated annealing, proposed by Kirkpatrick, Gelatt and Vecchi (1983) and by Černý (1985), seeks the global minimum of a cost with many local minima (p. 1). The scope is finite sets only; continuous versions are only mentioned (p. 1). The stated goal is to describe the method, its convergence and its behaviour in applications (p. 1). The entry is the gap between use and justification, posed as two questions: does x(t) converge to the optimal set, and how fast? The first is "more or less completely answered"; much less is known about the second (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea** (the review's argument):
  - Simulated annealing is an inhomogeneous Markov chain. At a fixed temperature it is reversible, and its Gibbs distribution concentrates on the global minima as T → 0. Slow cooling remedies slow equilibration at low T (pp. 1–2).
  - Convergence is characterized (Hajek), but the time-asymptotic rates carry constants so large that they are "largely irrelevant" (p. 3).
  - The right yardstick is complexity: the hitting time of the optimal (or a near-optimal) set as a function of instance size, over a family of instances (p. 3).
  - Such results are rare (p. 3), rapid mixing at low temperature is an open problem (p. 4), and practical performance is mixed (pp. 4–5).

  The review moves the standard from convergence to complexity and to comparison at equal computing time.
- **D3 Model and data**: A finite state space; symmetric neighbourhoods (j ∈ S(i) iff i ∈ S(j)); positive probabilities for choosing a neighbour; a nonincreasing cooling schedule (p. 1). Reversibility uses q_ij = q_ji, a condition that can be relaxed (Faigle–Kern) (p. 1). The convergence results assume irreducibility and aperiodicity (p. 2), and d* > 0, so that some local minima are not optimal (p. 2). There is no data of their own.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (surveyed, not new):
  - Hajek's necessary and sufficient condition is Σ exp(−d*/T(t)) = ∞, so T(t) = d/log t converges if and only if d ≥ d* (p. 2). It is explained by attempts to escape a local minimum of depth d*, with the Borel–Cantelli lemma, and proved through exit-time statistics (p. 2).
  - A piecewise-constant schedule gives a relaxation time of about exp(kd*) from the second-largest eigenvalue. This is the same d*, a coincidence the authors call "far from obvious" (pp. 2–3).
  - Other proof routes: singularly perturbed chains with large-deviation bounds (Tsitsiklis 1988, 1989) (p. 3).
  - Speed: the probability of being outside the optimal set is at least A/t^a, so t ≥ (A/ε)^(1/a) is needed for accuracy ε (p. 3).
  - Baselines: the probability of never visiting the optimal set is at most A/(t*)^a for simulated annealing but at most B·e^(−bt*) for a random walk at fixed temperature. So the walk looks better asymptotically, and the analysis "cannot even establish" that simulated annealing beats exhaustive search (p. 3).
  - Complexity results:
    - Maximum matching is solved in polynomial expected time on graphs of bounded degree (Sasaki–Hajek), more slowly than other algorithms; no annealing-type algorithm does so without the degree bound (p. 3).
    - In Hajek's separable example, annealing is polynomial, against 3^N for exhaustive search, and beats multistart local search without using the separability (p. 3).
  - Open: rapid mixing at small temperature; Markov random fields in image processing; any theoretical case for decreasing over constant temperature (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation** (surveyed): Johnson et al. (1990–1992) tested graph partitioning, graph colouring, number partitioning and the TSP with geometric cooling (p. 4).
  - Graph partitioning: simulated annealing was at best about 5% better than Kernighan–Lin when both had the same computation time. It was better on sparse graphs and worse on some structured ones (p. 4).
  - Graph colouring: competitive with Johri–Matula, but far slower (p. 4).
  - TSP: better than repeated 2-opt and 3-opt, but a "consistent loser" to Lin–Kernighan (p. 4).
  - The cooling schedule changes solution quality by up to 10% (van Laarhoven–Aarts), and computation times are "excessive for some problems" (p. 5).
  - Statistical uses: generalized annealing for continuous problems, optimal experimental design, Bayesian variants (p. 5).
  - There are no experiments of their own.
- **D6 Positioning**: A neutral survey that gives credit freely (Hajek, Geman and Geman, Tsitsiklis, Sasaki–Hajek, Johnson et al.) (pp. 2–5). Simulated annealing is framed both as the Metropolis algorithm with cooling and as local search with occasional uphill moves (p. 2). The comparators are exhaustive search, a random walk, multistart local search and the best problem-specific heuristics (pp. 3–4).
- **D7 Writing**: The review is built on two questions (p. 2), and an intuitive reading follows each theorem (p. 2). The verdicts are plain: "largely irrelevant", "cannot even establish" (p. 3); a "definite lack of rigorous results" (p. 4). The close is balanced. Simulated annealing is generally applicable, easy to implement, and gives good solutions even when the structure of the problem is not understood; but more theory and experiments are needed (p. 5).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The review offers a synthesis and reframes what would justify simulated annealing: complexity over families of instances, and comparisons at equal computing time (pp. 3–4). Limits: finite sets only (p. 1); six pages. Future work: complexity results, rapid mixing at low temperature, a theory of cooling schedules, more experiments (pp. 3–5).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · evidence ✅: the review judges a heuristic by guarantees (p. 3) and by duels with the best specialized heuristic at equal computing time (p. 4). These are Method 4's steps of duelling the incumbent and of making no uncertified claims. SKILL.md lists the equal-budget protocol among its critic-informed checks, as something that is not his practice; yet this 1993 review adopts it as its criterion, in reporting Johnson et al. (p. 4).
- The closing rationale, good solutions where the structure is not understood (p. 5), foreshadows his later use of simulated annealing as a component, so Tension T2 is softer than SKILL.md states.
- New-pattern candidate: *judge an algorithm by complexity over a family of instances, not by time asymptotics on one instance* (p. 3). This adds a 1993 point at the complexity end of the drift recorded in Method 1's limitations (complexity class in 2004, solve time in 2019).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Explain a convergence condition by its mechanism: an attempt to escape a local minimum of depth d* succeeds with probability about exp(−d*/T), and the Borel–Cantelli lemma does the rest (p. 2).
- (D4) Before trusting a guarantee for a stochastic search, test it against two trivial baselines: exhaustive search, and a random walk at fixed temperature (p. 3).
- (D5) Compare a general metaheuristic with the best problem-specific heuristic at equal computing time, and report by class of instance (sparse against structured graphs) where each one wins (p. 4).
- (D2) State an algorithm's quality as the hitting time of the optimal or near-optimal set, as a function of instance size (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "One can trace the inadequacy of the analytical methods mentioned so far to the fact that they deal with one instance at a time." (p. 3)
- "As far as theory is concerned, there is at present a definite lack of rigorous results justifying the use of SA." (p. 4)

### S032 · Stochastic and dynamic vehicle routing in the Euclidean plane with multiple capacitated vehicles (Operations Research 1993, DOI 10.1287/opre.41.1.60)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17 with the references. Figures 1–2 survive only as extracted labels, and many displayed formulas are partly garbled, so the constants below come from the prose and the legible parts.
**Role**: core · **Coauthor**: Garrett van Ryzin (by then at Columbia University, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the sequel announced in S016 (S016 p. 14), published in a special issue on stochastic and dynamic models in transportation (p. 1). Received May 1990, accepted June 1992 (p. 1); funded by the NSF, Draper Laboratories and the UPS Foundation (p. 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD; AP as motivation}: Classical VRPs are static, deterministic and minimize travel; as models of practice they are "not always appropriate" (p. 1). Examples: a canonical utility-repair problem (p. 1), and delivery from orders that arrive in real time (p. 1). Another is hauling cars from a plant, where by Little's theorem minimizing waiting means minimizing finished-goods inventory; less-than-truckload consolidation and parcel post are others (p. 2). A second entry is self-critique: their 1991 model of a single uncapacitated vehicle is "somewhat unrealistic for most practical purposes" (p. 3). The problem is to find policies that minimize expected system time for m vehicles, each serving at most q customers before returning to its depot (p. 3). A mixed objective of travel and waiting is added (pp. 15–16).
- **D2 Core idea**: The 1991 bracket is kept: lower bounds for all policies, then policies within a constant factor of them (pp. 3–4). New moves:
  - Radial wedges feed a queue with superposed renewal inputs (ΣGI/G/m, Iglehart–Whitt). This removes the dependence on m and halves the best known system time for one server (pp. 8–9).
  - Capacity adds a radial collection term, so stability now depends on geometry: ρ + 2λr̄/(vq) < 1 for one vehicle (p. 3).
  - Devices from the static VRP, the tour partitioning of Haimovich and Rinnooy Kan and the radial collection cost, move into the dynamic model (pp. 3, 13–15).
  - The set-size variable, rewritten as a ratio y to its critical value, becomes a knob for trading travel against waiting (pp. 15–16).
- **D3 Model and data**: Only the boundedness of the region is assumed (p. 2). Arrivals are Poisson, locations uniform and independent, on-site service general with finite moments, and vehicles travel at velocity v (p. 2). There are m identical vehicles with assigned depots, each visiting at most q customers per tour (p. 10). Admitted simplifications:
  - Each tour visits exactly q demands, which "simplifies the analysis and seems quite reasonable for the heavy traffic case" (p. 10).
  - q > 1, since with q = 1 the system is an ordinary M/G/m queue (p. 10).
  - Steady-state limits exist (p. 4).
  - The multidepot results need depots whose Voronoi cells are identical (p. 15).

  There is no data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Light traffic: a bound through the m-median (Theorem 1, p. 4). mSQM places servers at the m medians and assigns demands by Voronoi cell; it is asymptotically optimal (Proposition 1, p. 6), also with capacity, because the bound comes from a relaxation (p. 12).
  - Heavy traffic: Theorem 2 with γ ≈ 0.266 (p. 4), via Lemma 1 (a tagged demand and Wald's equation) and a new Lemma 2. Lemma 2 simplifies the 1991 proof with a bound on the area of a union of discs that holds for any bounded measurable region (p. 5).
  - Uncapacitated policies:
    - Randomized assignment (RA): the guarantee grows with m (p. 7).
    - A G/G/m version of the TSP policy, analysed with Kingman's heavy-traffic limit for G/G/m and the BHH asymptotics, with a factor of order (m + 1)/2 (pp. 7–8).
    - The modified G/G/m policy with k wedges: its constant tends to β/√2 ≈ 0.51 (pp. 8–9).
    - D/G/m, which clusters demands in time and space; its proof is omitted (p. 9).
    - Independent partitioning, which carries any single-server constant over to m servers (pp. 9–10).
    - Conjecture 1: partitioning an optimal single-server policy gives an optimal m-server policy (p. 10).
  - Capacitated lower bound (Theorem 4, p. 10): pick two random points of a tour, then add the two radial legs and the nearest-neighbour hops between the points (Lemma 3, pp. 10–11). The resulting factor of ⅓ is flagged as a by-product of the proof (p. 11).
  - Capacitated policies: qRP (a square grid around the depot), qTP and MOD qTP (tour partitioning) all have constant-factor guarantees (pp. 12–15).
  - Theorem 5: in heavy traffic the median is the optimal depot location. The proof is by contradiction: any other site makes the lower bound blow up while qRP's upper bound stays finite (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: There is no simulation. One numerical illustration shows the trade-off: σ² = 0, A = 1, s̄ = 0.1 and ρ = 0.9 give y* = 0.906 and T = 578, and cutting travel per demand by 10% (y = 0.81) raises T by 21% to 702 (Figure 2, p. 16). All other comparisons are analytical (pp. 3–15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper argues against the classical VRP literature and against rolling-horizon practice (Brown–Graves; Powell; Psaraftis et al.), which it calls "inherently ad hoc" and short of the insight needed for strategic planning (p. 2). Related work: the PTSP and PVRP, queueing location, polling systems, machine-repairman models, and Psaraftis's dynamic TSP (p. 2). It claims priority: the 1991 paper gave "the first comprehensive set of results" (p. 2). It improves its own earlier constants and proofs (pp. 3, 5, 9).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Structure: the 1991 results are restated as the base, then each extension gets one paragraph with its "intuitively satisfying insights" (pp. 2–3), and each stability term gets a physical reading (radial collection, p. 3).
  - Candour: the paper judges its own results bluntly: "undesirable" (p. 7), "somewhat unsatisfactory" (p. 8), "somewhat troubling" (p. 11). Its conjectures carry an explicit non-proof: "we have not been able to prove this" (p. 16).
  - An inconsistency in the extracted text: the introduction says γ rises from 2/(3√(6π)) ≈ 0.153 to ≈ 0.266 (p. 3), yet the printed 1991 paper already states ≈ 0.266 (S016 p. 5). Here, Lemma 1's statement keeps the 6π form while its proof ends at 2π (pp. 4–5). A likely reason is that S032 was submitted (May 1990) before S016's final revision [inferred].
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: theory for m vehicles and for capacity, stability that depends on geometry, the result on depot location, and the mixed objective (p. 16). Open problems and limits:
  - Conjecture 1 remains unproved (p. 16).
  - The gap between γ and the policy constants is open. The authors conjecture it closes at β/√2, which would make the modified G/G/m and MOD qTP policies asymptotically optimal, "however, we have not been able to prove this" (p. 16).
  - Asymmetric multidepot cases "probably" need better bounds and policies (p. 15).
  - The analysis should move from the Euclidean metric to networks (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (early form): one interpretable knob, y (travel per demand against its critical value), indexes the trade-off between travel cost and system time. The curve T(y) and a worked price (10% less travel costs 21% more system time) go to a decision-maker, who sets y with a cost function g. Radial costs are flagged as not tradeable (pp. 15–16). What Method 3 has that this lacks: a probabilistic bound independent of the solution, and any question of solver class.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: the certificates are analytic lower bounds and the guarantees constant factors, and open gaps are printed as conjectures, not as results (pp. 10–11, 16).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: a model instead of ad hoc procedures. Rolling horizons are called "inherently ad hoc" for strategic planning (p. 2), and the boundary is printed: asymmetric depots (p. 15) and networks (p. 16). What is preserved is an analysable queue, not an LP or MIO class.
- New-pattern candidate: *lower bound over all policies, then constant-factor policies* (pp. 3–15).
- New-pattern candidate: *asymptotic regimes for sharp constants*, here light and heavy traffic (pp. 4–16).
- New-pattern candidate: *print the looseness of your own constants and the conjecture that would close it* (pp. 7–8, 11, 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *carry deterministic devices into the stochastic or dynamic version and measure what survives* (tour partitioning and the radial cost, pp. 3, 13–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Bound the distance to the nearest of n points by the area of n discs: P{Z* ≤ z} ≤ nπz²/A (p. 5).
- (D4) Prove that a design choice is optimal (a depot at the median) by contradiction: any other choice makes the lower bound blow up while the upper bound of a constructed policy stays finite (p. 13).
- (D4) Remove a fleet-size factor from a guarantee by splitting the region into k wedges whose superposed renewal streams feed a ΣGI/G/m queue, then let k grow (pp. 8–9).
- (D4/D5) Rewrite a policy's design variable as a ratio to its critical value, then plot the objective against it, so the decision-maker sees the trade-off curve (pp. 15–16).
- (D7) When a bound behaves oddly in a limit (⅓ of the uncapacitated constant), say which parameter is independent in the intended use, and trace the slack to the proof device (p. 11).
