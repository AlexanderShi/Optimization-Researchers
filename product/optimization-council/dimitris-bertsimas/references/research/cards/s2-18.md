### S708 · Reformulation-Perspectification Technique for nonconvex optimization problems (Optimization Online 2023, no DOI)
Read level: **partial**, in the 2026 revision. Read:
- in full: pp. 1–24 (abstract, §§1–4 with Theorem 1, Lemmas 1–3, Example 4, Algorithms 1–2 and the worked toy example), and pp. 35–42 (§8, §9 and the acknowledgements);
- statements only: §5 (pp. 24–25, 27–28) and §6 (Assumption 3, Theorem 3, Remark 9, Corollary 1; pp. 29–30);
- skimmed: §7 (pp. 30–34), and Appendix A's opening and A.6 (pp. 46, 50).

Not read: the proofs of §5 (pp. 25–29), the branching tables of Appendix A.1–A.5 (pp. 46–50), and Appendices B–F (pp. 50–69), which hold all convergence proofs. First-page check: the title matches, and Bertsimas is the first of five authors (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Danique de Moor and Dick den Hertog (Amsterdam Business School), Thodoris Koukouvinos (MIT ORC), Jianzhe Zhen (University of Chinese Academy of Sciences) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the first paper of the RPT line with den Hertog's group, which the cone-product paper S415 extends (card c2-08). It counts Selvi et al. (2022), an adjustable-robust method coauthored by den Hertog, as a special case of RPT (pp. 3, 45).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP+IO; AP (Dutch dike heightening) as one testbed}:
  - RLT (Sherali–Adams) builds convex relaxations by multiplying linear or quadratic inequalities only (p. 2). RPT targets problems whose nonconvex parts are sums of products of convex functions: concave minimization, DC programs, fractional programs (abstract, pp. 1–2).
  - Two recent convex-maximization methods, Selvi et al. (2022) and Ben-Tal–Roos's CoMax, give good bounds but cannot guarantee global optimality (p. 3).
  - Problem (1): minimize an SCC function (sum of convex times convex) subject to SCC constraints over a compact convex set (pp. 4–5).
    - SCC terms reduce to SLC terms (sum of linear times convex) through epigraph variables (p. 5).
    - DC terms reduce through the biconjugate (Example 1, pp. 5–6).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - **Perspectification**: for q − d⊤x ≥ 0, rewrite (q − d⊤x)c(x) as (q − d⊤x)c((qx − xx⊤d)/(q − d⊤x)) and replace xx⊤ by a matrix X. The result is a perspective, jointly convex in (x, X) (eq. 5, p. 8). With c linear this is RLT (Remark 1, p. 8).
  - **Reformulation**: pairwise products of linear × linear, linear × convex and, case by case, convex × convex constraints, each perspectified and linearized (pp. 9–14). An optional LMI tightens the relaxation at a cost in time (pp. 14–15).
  - **Upper bounds**: candidate points X*ᵢ/x*ᵢ and (V*⊤)ₖ/τ*ₖ are read off the relaxation solution. They are feasible under Lemma 3's conditions and are polished by Ipopt (pp. 15–16).
  - **Branching**: branch on the eigenvector of the eigenvalue of largest absolute value of R = X* − x*x*⊤. The new inequalities are multiplied with each other and with every node constraint, so the tangent and the secants arise as products (pp. 17–19).
  - **Relative to Anstreicher (2022)**:
    - kept: the eigenvector direction, the split point and the secants;
    - changed: the self-product tangent cuts off the parent point even when λ < 0, so the rule works without the semidefinite constraint, and each child is at least as tight (pp. 18–19).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions:
    - X nonempty and compact (Assumption 1, p. 5);
    - sign conditions on the factors (Assumption 2, p. 5);
    - node relaxations solved to accuracy ε̄ (Assumption 3, p. 29).
  - Admitted limits of the assumptions:
    - in the linear multiplicative problem the biconjugate set Y is unbounded, "one reason" for making no convergence claim there (p. 39);
    - the dike model is made compact only by added bounds xᵢ ≤ 300 (p. 41).
  - Instances (pp. 36–41):
    - sum-of-max-linear-terms maximization over three feasible sets: five instances from Selvi et al. plus ten-instance random groups;
    - log-sum-exp maximization;
    - linear multiplicative problems from Ryoo–Sahinidis;
    - ten QCQPs;
    - Dutch dike rings 10, 15 and 16 under three heightening schedules.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - **Theorem 1**: epigraph preprocessing gives a relaxation at least as tight. The proof drops two constraint sets and eliminates τ (pp. 11–12). Example 4 shows a strict gain: 13/4 without epigraph variables against the exact optimum 3 with them (pp. 12–13).
  - **Redundancy results**:
    - equality × convex products are implied once equalities are multiplied by x (Lemma 1, by the perspective at zero, p. 10);
    - epigraph variables for linear parts add nothing (Lemma 2, pp. 13–14);
    - a linear constraint redundant to other linear constraints adds nothing (Theorem 2, p. 25), while one redundant to nonlinear constraints can help (pp. 3, 14).
  - **Theorem 3**: a terminated run returns a δ-optimal incumbent; otherwise the global lower bounds converge to within ε̄ of the optimum (p. 30).
    - Remark 9: no claim that the upper bounds converge (p. 30).
    - Corollary 1: finite termination only when there are no nonconvex constraints, K = ∅ (p. 30).
    - The proofs (Appendix F) were not read.
  - **§7, special cases**: RPT recovers (pp. 30–34):
    - the hull relaxation of disjunctions;
    - Dantzig's generalized LP;
    - the Sherali–Adams convex hull of 0-1 mixed-integer convex sets, which enumerates all 2^nB subsets;
    - the approximate S-lemma;
    - Schaible's exact fractional reformulation.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - **Protocol** (p. 35):
    - hardware and software: MacBook Pro M3 Pro with 18 GB; Julia 1.12.5 with JuMP 1.29.4; Gurobi 13.0.0 for LPs and MOSEK 11.0.30 for conic nodes;
    - tolerance 10⁻⁴ and 1800 s per instance;
    - opponents: BARON 21.1.13, SCIP 10.0.0 and CPLEX 22.1.2, each with one thread and the same tolerance and limit;
    - every incumbent is rechecked for feasibility at 10⁻⁶.
  - **Where the incumbents win** (all printed):
    - the exact MIP on sum-of-max over X1 (Table 4, p. 37);
    - SCIP on the direct log-sum-exp formulation (Table 5, p. 38);
    - BARON on the log form of the multiplicative problem (Table 6, p. 39);
    - CPLEX on QCQPs 1, 2 and 5, and BARON on QCQP 9 (Table 7, p. 40).
  - **Where RPT wins**:
    - instances on which BARON times out (Table 4, p. 37);
    - the dike problem, which RPT-SDP-BB solves at the root on all nine instances in at most 8.71 s (Table 8, p. 41).
  - **Failures printed** (pp. 37–39, 41):
    - RPT-SDP-BB returns no feasible solution on instances 5 and 5r over X3;
    - MOSEK stalls on two root relaxations;
    - RPT-BB times out on five of nine dike instances.
  - [my reading] BARON is the 2021 version, while all other software is dated 2025–2026 (p. 35).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: the RPT relaxation, the branching rule with a convergence result, redundancy theory, and tests on five problem classes (pp. 3–4).
  - Stated limits:
    - tractability falls with many variables and constraints, since the variables are squared and all constraint pairs are multiplied (p. 42);
    - the LMI is not consistently advantageous (p. 37);
    - no branching rule is consistently the fastest, yet the main tables use U-RX "because it is the rule of Algorithm 1" (p. 50).
  - Future work (p. 42):
    - methods: partial multiplications, selective products, iterative constraint generation;
    - problem classes: MINLP, robust, adaptive and distributionally robust, polynomial and bilevel optimization.
  - Process: the acknowledgements disclose generative-AI use for the statements of Theorem 3 and Corollary 1 and for most of the initial proof text. The authors state that they checked and rewrote it (p. 42).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 8, 14–15, 42): the perspective device keeps each node a conic program that MOSEK solves, but exactness comes from branching, not from the reformulation. The boundary is printed: squaring the variables limits the size (p. 42).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 35–41): the duel with three incumbent solvers is at equal threads, tolerance and time limit, with feasibility rechecks. The certificates are the reported timeouts, and the paper says what they do not prove (p. 35). No incumbent warm-starts RPT.
- Heuristic 4 (not a Method) · evidence ✅ (pp. 37–41, 50): the losing classes, the no-solution cases and the unhelpful LMI are all reported in the main text.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To convexify (q − d⊤x)·c(x) with c convex and q − d⊤x ≥ 0, multiply and divide the argument by (q − d⊤x), replace xx⊤ by X, and use the joint convexity of the perspective (pp. 7–8).
- Before building a branch and bound, test whether epigraph variables tighten the root relaxation, and show strictness on a two-variable example (Theorem 1 and Example 4, pp. 11–13).
- Branch on the eigenvector of the largest-|λ| eigenvalue of X* − x*x*⊤ and multiply the new inequality with itself. The resulting tangent cuts off the parent point even when the residual is indefinite (pp. 17–19).
- Benchmark global solvers with one thread, equal tolerance and time limit, and an independent feasibility recheck. State in writing that zero timeouts do not prove optimality (p. 35).

**Quotes**:
- "A solver may also stop before the time limit without proving optimality, so a feasible objective value or a zero timeout count does not by itself establish optimality." (p. 35)
- "The statements of Theorem 3 and Corollary 1, the proof strategies developed in Appendix F, and most of the initial proof text were generated using OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra via ChatGPT and Anthropic’s Claude Fable 5.1." (p. 42)

### S259 · Reshaping national organ allocation policy (Operations Research 2024, DOI 10.1287/opre.2022.0035)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13. The file holds the INFORMS cover, abstract, §§1–5, Table 1, Figures 1–3 (captions and axis labels only), endnotes, references and author notes. Online appendices A–E are not in the file (not read). First-page check: the title matches, and Bertsimas is the third of six authors (pp. 1–2). Articles in Advance: received 20 January 2022, published online 20 November 2023, area of review "OR Practice" (p. 2).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors** (p. 2):
- Theodore Papalexopoulos (MIT ORC; PhD 2022 with a thesis on organ allocation policy design, p. 13);
- James Alcorn and Rebecca Goff (UNOS);
- Darren Stewart (NYU Grossman School of Medicine);
- Nikolaos Trichakis (MIT; corresponding author).

**Position in trajectory**: the lung stage of the OPTN allocation line; the kidney stages are S044 (card c14) and S476 (card c2-07). The authors report extending the collaboration to kidney, pancreas, heart and liver (pp. 2–3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA+HS+AP; ML as surrogate}:
  - In 2018 the OPTN decided to move all deceased-donor allocation to "continuous distribution". Candidates are ranked by a composite allocation score (CAS), whose attribute weights must balance efficiency, utility and fairness under the federal Final Rule (pp. 2–5).
  - Entry: weights were chosen by trial and error with the SRTR's simulators, which are slow and opaque. The kidney system took a decade and over 30 simulated options (p. 5).
  - Problem P(π): over the weight simplex, minimize one simulated outcome subject to bounds on the others. The set of instances π is not known in advance (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Policy design is treated as "an inverse control problem": given desired outcomes, find the parameters that achieve them (p. 6).
  - Three phases (pp. 6–7):
    1. sample policies uniformly over the domain and simulate them;
    2. fit an MILP-representable ML surrogate for each outcome, selected by cross-validation and judged by held-out R²;
    3. solve each stakeholder query as a MILP over the surrogates, then evaluate the solution with one simulator call.
  - Slack variables with an epigraph constraint handle infeasible requirements (pp. 7–8).
  - Sweeping one outcome's bound traces an approximate efficient frontier with its "critical points" (p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Domain: weight vectors on the six-attribute unit simplex (p. 9).
  - Black box: SRTR's Thoracic Simulated Allocation Model 2015, modified to take a CAS weight vector (p. 9):
    - 3,326 lungs and 6,546 candidates, 2009–2011;
    - 47 outcomes.
  - Sample: 10,000 weight vectors, each simulated 20 times (p. 9).
  - Surrogates (p. 9; Table 1, p. 10):
    - per outcome, the maximum or minimum of K affine functions, fitted with an ℓ2 loss on an 80/10/10 split;
    - out-of-sample R² between 0.85 and 0.99, mean 0.96.
  - Solver: CBC (p. 9).
  - Admitted:
    - surrogate solutions may violate the true constraints; a safety term is given in the online appendix, which was not read (p. 7);
    - the simulator assumes stable acceptance behaviour (p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a for proofs. Devices:
  - MILP representation of the piecewise-linear surrogates (Online Appendix C, not read; p. 9);
  - slack variables, with an epigraph constraint on the original objective, for infeasible requirement vectors (pp. 7–8);
  - frontier tracing by sweeping a bound (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - **Placement efficiency** (Fig. 2, p. 10): policies minimize waitlist deaths under a grid of transport-distance bounds. Pediatric weight is fixed at 20%, WLAUC and PTAUC weigh equally, and blood-type and height disparities may not rise above current policy. The knee is at ≈10% placement weight (median distance ≈175 nautical miles), and two other transport metrics show the same.
  - **Pediatric priority** (Fig. 3, pp. 10–11): the worst-case pediatric transplant rate reaches current policy at ≈10% weight and levels off at 15–20%. UNOS's informal analysis had suggested 30%.
  - **Validation and adoption** (pp. 9–10):
    - SRTR's independent simulations on an updated 2018–2019 cohort confirmed the 10% finding;
    - the committee adopted 10% and 20%;
    - the proposal went to public comment in August 2021, was ratified in October 2021 and approved by the board in December 2021;
    - it has been national policy since 9 March 2023.
  - **Effect** (p. 10; endnote 4, p. 12):
    - ≈20% fewer waitlist deaths than current policy, ≈118 over two simulated years;
    - SRTR estimates ≈40% (175 deaths);
    - the authors warn that the absolute numbers come from different cohorts.
    - [my reading] The introduction's 62 deaths averted per year (p. 3) is more than ≈118 over two years implies.
  - **Speed**: the MILPs are "solved to optimality in seconds" by open-source solvers, with no table of times (p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a policy-design framework, a deployed national lung policy, and a stated generalization to school choice and public housing (pp. 2–3, 11).
  - Stated limits (pp. 11–12):
    - the surrogates inherit the simulator's biases;
    - accuracy falls away from the current operating point (the chosen 10% weight is said to be unaffected);
    - post-transplant outcomes are hard to predict, so the analysis focuses on waitlist mortality;
    - the surrogates must be accurate;
    - the method suits low-dimensional policies only.
  - Unreported: an analysis that informed the biological-disadvantage weight (pp. 8–9).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 8–10): the paper has an owner, a decision, data and the current rule, plotted as the reference point in Figs. 2–3, and the policy was deployed nationally.
  - Before adoption, the SRTR, whose simulators the committees use, independently validated the results, and the stakeholders explored the trade-offs before the vote (pp. 8–10). This is the step Method 5's correction asks for.
  - Only projections are reported, no post-deployment outcomes [my reading].
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 8, 10–11): the property (breadth of distribution, pediatric access) is priced by one knob, a CAS weight. The committee receives a frontier with its knee. There is no solution-independent bound; the curve is simulated.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 6–7, 12): the surrogate class is chosen so that every query stays a MILP that an open-source solver solves in seconds. The boundary is printed: low dimension only.
- Research Taste mark 3 (not a Method) · evidence ✅ (p. 5): one optimization replaces the committees' trial and error.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace a slow, opaque simulator by one piecewise-linear surrogate per outcome (the max or min of K affine functions), fitted on uniformly sampled policies. Optimize over the surrogates as a MILP, then check each optimized policy with one simulator run (pp. 6–7, 9).
- Grow the training sample until held-out R² is acceptable, and print R² for each outcome (pp. 7, 10).
- Trace a frontier by sweeping one outcome's bound while holding equity outcomes no worse than current policy, and mark the knee for the decision-makers (p. 10).
- Let stakeholders debate target outcomes rather than weights; the optimizer translates outcomes into parameters (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "The OPTN committees’ simulation modeling has typically been conducted via trial and error, with simulated outcomes from initial policy ideas used to iteratively refine those ideas until acceptable predicted outcomes are achieved." (p. 5)
- "Within our framework, policymakers can instead focus on debating appropriate target outcomes, a setting where ethical theory and community sentiment is more likely to generate useful feedback." (p. 8)

### S375 · Congenital heart surgery machine learning-derived in-depth benchmarking tool (The Annals of Thoracic Surgery 2024, DOI 10.1016/j.athoracsur.2023.10.034)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–8 (abstract, introduction, patients and methods, results, comment, limitations, conclusions, references). Figures 1–4 have captions only. The Video and Supplemental Figures 1–2 are not in the file (not read). First-page check: the title matches, and Bertsimas is the last of nine authors (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors** (p. 1):
- George E. Sarris (Athens Heart Surgery Institute; corresponding author);
- Daisy Zhuo, Luca Mingardi, Jack Dunn and Jordan Levine (Alexandria Health);
- Zdzislaw Tobota and Bohdan Maruszewski (Children's Memorial Health Institute, Warsaw);
- Jose Fragata (Lisbon).

**Position in trajectory**: the third paper of a series on optimal classification trees (OCTs) in congenital heart surgery (refs. 1–2, 2021 and 2022; pp. 1–2, 8). It was presented at the Society of Thoracic Surgeons meeting in January 2023 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+AP}:
  - The earlier papers predicted risk with OCTs (ref. 1) and benchmarked hospitals on the 10 benchmark procedure groups (ref. 2) (pp. 1–2). Those groups cover only 44.6% of the database (41.6% of European data), so hospitals need case-adjusted performance for all procedures (p. 5).
  - The owner is the European Congenital Heart Surgeons Association (ECHSA) database and its participating hospitals. The tool serves self-assessment, explicitly not public comparison (pp. 6–8).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - OCTs are trained on pooled data, the "virtual hospital"; each leaf is a cohort of similar risk (p. 2).
  - A hospital's expected outcome applies the virtual hospital's leaf rates to its own case mix. Observed-to-expected (O/E) ratios are reported overall and per leaf cohort, and can be stratified by STAT Mortality Score or benchmark procedure (pp. 2, 4).
  - An online platform with selectable time windows delivers the analysis (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - 172,888 procedures in European centers, 1989–2022 (p. 2):
    - training: 121,022 procedures (1989–2018);
    - testing: 51,866 procedures (2010–2022).
    - [my reading] The two windows overlap in 2010–2018, so the split is not purely temporal (the counts add to the total).
  - Outcomes: hospital mortality, and prolonged ventilation and length of stay, defined as above the 85th percentile (p. 2).
  - Features: the STAT Mortality Score is added; diagnosis and year of surgery are omitted (p. 2).
  - 146 participating centers (p. 2).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a. The OCT method is "previously described" (p. 2). The statistical device is an O/E ratio with 90% CIs, chosen to lower false negatives (p. 2). How the CIs are computed is not stated in the file. [my reading] The text gives 95% CIs for Figure 2 (p. 2), while the Figure 2 caption says 90% (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - AUC: 0.866 for mortality, 0.851 for ventilation, 0.818 for length of stay (abstract; p. 2).
  - Raw rates: 4.9%, 14.5% and 15.0% (p. 2).
  - Flagged centers (p. 2):
    - mortality: 20.5% over- and 20.5% underperform;
    - ventilation: 18.5% and 19.9%;
    - length of stay: 18.5% and 30.1%.
  - Against ECHSA's STAT-score quality-of-care chart, the two methods agree for 48 of 60 centers on adjusted performance, 45 on case risk, and 41 on both (pp. 5–6).
  - [my reading] No comparator AUC (logistic or STAT-only model) is reported. Accuracy rises with center and procedure volume, per Supplemental Figures 1–2, which are not in the file (p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution: an applied tool (pp. 4, 8).
  - Stated limits (pp. 7–8):
    - machine learning needs large data, so small centers' trees are pruned to fewer cohorts;
    - the data are coarse: procedure-specific factors have been tracked only since the last year;
    - the data are European only, so the models are internally valid but may not transfer;
    - the benchmarks are not for public comparison.
  - Future (pp. 7–8): diagnosis-based long-term survival and quality of life; combined international databases; 1-year survival data, now being collected.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 5–6): the owner and its current tool (the STAT-score quality-of-care chart) are named, but the comparison counts agreement, not decision outcomes. No outcome after deployment is reported.
