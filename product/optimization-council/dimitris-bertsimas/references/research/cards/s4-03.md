### S906 · The Power and Limits of Decision Making with Confounded Data: The Case of Pricing (undated manuscript in the MSOM template; no DOI/arXiv on this row)
Read level: **full** for the main text, pp. 1–37 (abstract, §§1–7, Tables 1–4, Figures 1–4, references). E-companion: the proof of Theorem 2 in full (pp. 38–40), the openings of the proofs of Theorems 3, 4, 6 and 7 (pp. 40–44), and EC.2 in full (pp. 46–47); the middle of the asymptotic proofs (pp. 41–46) was skimmed. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Kallus match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nathan Kallus (Cornell Tech and Cornell University, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a manuscript version of S223 (card s17), later than the arXiv v2 that card read. It is a companion to From Predictive to Prescriptive Analytics (SW5), which it cites as Bertsimas and Kallus (2020) (pp. 4, 35).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA AP}: Most large-scale operational data are observational, such as transactional records. Historical prices and demand may therefore be confounded, and the data usually lack the confounders or the instrument that causal estimates need (pp. 1–3). Practice nonetheless optimizes the best prediction of demand given price, E[Y^obs | Z = z] (problem 2), instead of the mean of the potential demand curve (problem 1) (pp. 4–5). The entry point is that only the objective value of the final decision matters, not the model's bias. The paper states this as a display line (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two sides, as the title says.
  - Power: bound the true-profit ratio R(z̃)/R(z*) of the predictive price, using the structure of pricing and of pricing data (Theorems 2–4, pp. 13–15).
  - Limits: test H0: R(z*) = R(ẑ) for any data-driven rule, where covariates make the optimum identifiable (§4, pp. 15–27). The statistic is the gap between a non-parametric partial-means kernel optimum and the rule's decision, both scored by the kernel estimate (pp. 22, 24). The scale of its χ²₁ null is estimated by bootstrap (pp. 25–27).
  - A parametric prescriptive method based on the generalized propensity score (Hirano–Imbens) is added for comparison (§5, pp. 27–28).
  - Change from Besbes, Phillips and Zeevi (2010): their test of objective optimality concerns the predictive objective in experimental settings; this one concerns the causal objective on observational data (pp. 15–16, footnote 3).
- **D3 Model and data**: One price z, profit r(z) = z − c, potential demands Y(z), and iid observations of (Z, Y^obs) (p. 4). The bounds assume linear, non-increasing mean demand plus one condition on the confounding error E(z) = E[ε | Z = z] (p. 11):
  - its size relative to the market (Theorem 2, p. 13);
  - joint normality with non-positive correlation, motivated by promotions that coincide with price discounts (Theorem 3, p. 14);
  - E(z) non-increasing (Theorem 4, p. 15).

  The test assumes weak ignorability given covariates X (Assumption 1, p. 19); kernel and bandwidth conditions, which the authors call a choice rather than an assumption about the data (Assumption 2, pp. 22–23); a unique interior optimum and distributional regularity (Assumptions 3–4, p. 23); and a rule that converges slightly faster than the kernel optimum (Assumption 5, p. 25). Data: a simulated confounded instance (Example 3, p. 29) and the Columbia CPRM online auto-loan data in 16 segments (pp. 17, 31–32). Ignorability given the six covariates is argued, not tested (pp. 32–33).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Non-identifiability is shown by a parable. Alice's and Bob's demand models both reproduce the observed price–demand table exactly but recommend $20 and $28 (pp. 8–10). Theorem 1 extends this to hold under the Gauss–Markov assumptions (pp. 10–11).
  - Theorem 2's proof sandwiches the predictive maximizer between the largest and smallest maximizers over all error functions with |δ(z)| ≤ η; these reduce to monotone step functions taking ±η, and under Theorem 4's monotonicity to constants (pp. 38–41). Each bound is checked on a worked curve: 0.71 ≤ 0.75, 0.94 ≤ 0.96 and 0.79 ≤ 0.84 (pp. 14–15).
  - Theorem 6 gives asymptotic normality of the kernel objective and a 1/(nh³) rate for the profit shortfall, via Newey's partial-means results (pp. 24, 41–43). Theorem 7 decomposes the statistic into three terms, following Besbes et al.'s proof outline (pp. 25, 44). Theorem 8 makes one term asymptotically pivotal, which justifies the bootstrap (p. 26). EC.2 relaxes Assumption 5 and gives a conservative p-value from a weighted sum of two χ²₁ variables (pp. 46–47).
  - No optimization formulation beyond a one-dimensional argmax; the analysis is asymptotic statistics.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: In the simulated instance the predictive price earns zero true profit against an optimum of 31.25, and over 256 replicates the test needs a few hundred observations to reach its 5% size (pp. 30–31). On the auto loans (16 segments, n = 690–4,495, B = 100 bootstrap draws), the test rejects at p < 0.05 the parametric predictive rule in 9 segments, the non-parametric one in 13 and the prescriptive parametric rule in 2; weighted by n, the prescriptive rule recoups 70% and 36% of the profit the two predictive rules lose (Table 4, p. 32; pp. 33–34). Bandwidth constants come without a selection rule (p. 30); no software or run times are reported (pp. 29–34).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: non-identifiability, three bounds, a test with its asymptotics, and the empirical finding that predictive approaches have "both power and limits", while misspecified parametric models often suffice (pp. 7, 34). Stated limits:
  - the bounds cover only pricing with linear demand (pp. 13–15);
  - the confounding size in Theorems 2 and 4 is "neither known nor estimable" (p. 14);
  - the test applies only where H0 is identifiable (p. 17);
  - the null is not expected to hold exactly, and the bar should rise with n (pp. 33–34).

  Future work: similar bounds for other observational decision problems, built on each problem's structure (p. 13). My reading: the abstract's phrase "even when data is unconfounded" (p. 1) contradicts the thesis it introduces and is presumably a slip.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 16, 31–34): decisions are scored by true profit, not by model fit; H0 is stated in terms of the decision's revenue (p. 16). The baselines are the incumbent study's own regressions, data, segments and profit function (Besbes et al. 2010), re-tested under the causal objective.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13–15): optimality-ratio curves in one knob γ (Figure 2, p. 14). But γ measures confounding, which no decision-maker sets and which cannot be estimated (p. 14), so Theorem 3 swaps it for a ratio that can be estimated. This agrees with card S223.
- Tension T3 (not a method) · practice side (pp. 21–27, 41–47): the tools are kernel partial means, delta-method asymptotics and the bootstrap, with no LP, conic or MIO step.
- Tension T5 (not a method) · slow-pipeline side, inferred (p. 1; card S223): arXiv in 2016, then this MSOM-template version, then IJOO in 2023.
- New-pattern candidate: *hypothesis test of decision optimality* (pp. 15–27, 46–47).
- New-pattern candidate: *two-model parable for non-identifiability* (pp. 8–10, 20–21).
- New-pattern candidate: *assume only what can be estimated, worst-case the rest* (p. 14): Theorem 3 depends only on average demand relative to the market, which "can be unbiasedly and consistently estimated".
- New-pattern candidate: *multiplicity check before reading segment rejections* (pp. 33–34).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To show an optimal decision is not identifiable, build two complete potential-outcome models that reproduce the observed joint distribution exactly but imply different optimal decisions. Then show which covariate, with ignorability, singles one out (Tables 2–3, pp. 8–10, 20–21).
- Bound a plug-in decision's true-objective ratio by computing the extreme maximizers over all admissible model errors, after reducing those errors to step or constant functions at ±η (pp. 38–41).
- When a test statistic's scale is a nuisance, bootstrap an asymptotically pivotal companion quantity instead of estimating densities and second derivatives (Theorem 8, pp. 26–27).
- Before reading k rejections across m segments, compute their chance under a global null: with 16 segments at α = 0.05, two or more rejections occur about 19% of the time (p. 33).
- Re-test an incumbent study inside its own setting (its data, segments, estimators and r(z) = z − 2%), so that a different conclusion cannot be blamed on the setup (pp. 31–32).

**Quotes**:
- "predictive models may give biased causal estimates but still lead to good decisions." (p. 3)
- "Note that if the null hypothesis were actually true in all 16 segments (i.e., our prescriptive parametric approach is revenue optimal), we would still reject the null in two or more segments about 19% of the time." (p. 33)

### S907 · Multiperiod and Online Optimization for Fleet Defense: Centralized and Distributed Approaches (undated manuscript headed "Submitted to Operations Research"; no DOI/arXiv on this row)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–43 (abstract, §§1–6, Algorithms 1–2, Lemma 1, Proposition 1, Tables 1–7, references, and the e-companion proofs of Lemma 1 and Proposition 1). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Jaillet and Korolko match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Patrick Jaillet and Nikita Korolko (Operations Research Center, MIT; Korolko's listed e-mail is at Uber) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: by card a3-05, Part II of Korolko's 2017 thesis S641. It shares the toolkit and reporting of S445 (card s2-10): Julia/JuMP, Gurobi 6.5, a GREEDY opponent, and time to the optimum reported apart from time to proof. It continues a 2013 MIT Lincoln Laboratory working paper by Becker, Bertsimas and others (pp. 3, 31). Partly funded by the Office of Naval Research (p. 31).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO RO AP; CP for the conic reformulation}: Ships carrying hard-kill and soft-kill weapons must assign them to incoming threats, to maximize survival and to save weapons for later raids (pp. 1–2). The entry point is a gap in the predecessor. Becker et al. (2013) solve the exact model as a binary SOCP offline only and use a linearized heuristic online, and their distributed protocol broadcasts too much data for real use (p. 3). Earlier weapon–target work either linearizes (one weapon per threat) or uses heuristics; the convex formulation of Ahuja et al. is described as intractable at 20 weapons and 20 threats (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Four contributions (pp. 3–4):
  - LAZY solves the exact nonlinear mixed-binary model online: it drops the product constraints and adds tangent hyperplanes as lazy cuts (Algorithm 1, p. 9).
  - A multiperiod model adds binary destruction indicators, so that weapons on ships likely to be lost are not counted on later (pp. 10–13).
  - A three-step decentralized protocol: each ship solves its own problem and chooses which messages to broadcast under a budget; ships broadcast; each then re-solves a robust problem over what it was not told (pp. 13–19).
  - Experiments compare these with a greedy heuristic, the SOCP route, a myopic single-period rule, and the no-communication and centralized extremes (pp. 21–30).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Centralized model: threat lethality 1, common knowledge of targeting, and accurate efficacy estimates (p. 5). All probabilities lie in [ε, 1 − ε] (p. 7). Hard-kill weapons act independently, and at most one soft-kill weapon acts on each threat (pp. 5, 7). Each ship has a survival threshold γ_s and a priority α_s, with a large penalty λ on multiplicative slacks (p. 6).
  - Protocol: designed for one attack and hard-kill weapons only. Global data (targeting, thresholds, priorities, a minimum accuracy δ_s) are separated from private data (efficacies, costs) (p. 14).
  - Data: synthetic scenarios modelled on Becker et al., with efficacies drawn uniformly from ranges such as [0.5, 1] (pp. 22–28). No fleet data are used.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Both nonlinear constraint families take the form 1 ≤ ∏ u_i, which Becker et al. showed to be convex and representable by rotated second-order cones (p. 8).
  - Lemma 1 gives a closed-form separating hyperplane ∑ x_i/ŷ_i = n at a point ŷ: either the radial projection onto the boundary or the orthogonal projection, found by bisection on one KKT multiplier (pp. 9–10; proof pp. 41–42).
  - The multiperiod model links slacks and destruction indicators with big-M constraints, where M is "a large enough number" (pp. 11–12). After each raid, the decision maker re-solves with the realized survival vector (pp. 12–13).
  - In the protocol, broadcast binaries f_js make the companions' destruction probabilities linear, and a budget F̂_s caps the messages (pp. 15–16). The robust step uses box sets [0, Q̂], whose robust counterpart is the nominal problem at Q̂ (p. 18).
  - Proposition 1: the protocol never worsens a ship's objective, z3(s) ≤ z1(s), because the Step 1 solution stays feasible for Step 3 (p. 20; proof pp. 42–43).
  - No convergence or complexity statement is given for LAZY (my reading).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia/JuMP and Gurobi 6.5 on synthetic instances (p. 22). MIO beats GREEDY, a sort-and-assign heuristic the authors built, in 54% of 1,000 instances, where GREEDY spends 14.5% more weapons on average (p. 22). Nine cut variants racing in parallel reach the optimum in 3.93 s against the SOCP's 389 s (the reported 98.9×), but proof takes 16.91 s against 545 s, about 32× (my arithmetic; p. 23, Tables 2–3, pp. 35–36); the run count behind these tables is not stated, and the parallel arms are not matched in compute (my reading). 95% quantiles of time to proof reach 63 s for eight ships with 40 hard-kill weapons and 24 threats (Table 5, p. 38). The multiperiod model beats the myopic one (3.59 against 3.17 ships survive) while seeing the future raids' data (Table 6, p. 39; pp. 26–27). The protocol secures 3.2 ships, against 2.15 without communication and 4.94 centralized, but only F̂_s = 2 is run (p. 28; Table 7, p. 40).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The paper sums up its contributions (pp. 29–30): MIO beats a reasonable heuristic; lazy cuts are almost two orders of magnitude faster than the SOCP; typical sizes solve online; and the protocol is much better than no communication. Admitted limits:
  - the centralized model ignores where data are held, and it is slow (p. 29);
  - the protocol covers one attack and hard-kill weapons only (p. 14);
  - the protocol spends more weapons (pp. 20, 29).

  Future use: the lazy-cut and multiperiod devices for transportation with nonlinear effects and for resource allocation, and the communication variables for multi-agent optimization (p. 4). The conclusion names no open problem (p. 30). My reading: the abstract says the paper proves that the model can be solved efficiently. That claim rests on Lemma 1, which is a separation result, and on experiments (pp. 1, 9, 22–26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 8–10, 18): the exact nonlinear model stays in a solver class (convex mixed-binary, with a conic form or a lazy linear outer approximation). The robust protocol step reduces to the nominal problem at the worst-case Q̂.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 19, 21–26, 35–38): an exact solve with reported times to proof, and a duel with a heuristic. But there is no warm start and no stated time limit. The GREEDY opponent was built by the authors, and the predecessor's linearized heuristic and protocol (Becker et al. 2013) are compared only in words (p. 19). The 98.9× headline uses time to the optimum, not time to proof.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 25–26, 38): an exact formulation described as intractable at 20 weapons and 20 threats is reopened by a device (lazy tangent cuts), with times measured up to 40 hard-kill weapons and 24 threats. There is no solver-speed figure and no concession step.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 16, 19, 28): the message budget F̂_s is offered to the decision maker as a knob for communication, but only F̂_s = 2 is run; no price curve.
- Method 5 · not practised here (no link counted): synthetic instances only; no fleet data and no current doctrine as baseline.
- Tension T6 (not a method): the abstract's proof claim, read in D8.
- New-pattern candidate: *constraint generation as the scaling engine* (pp. 8–10, 22–23).
- New-pattern candidate: *report time to the hindsight-best solution separately from time to proof* (Tables 2–5, pp. 23–26, 35–38; the same device as S445).
- New-pattern candidate: *communication as a budgeted decision variable* (pp. 15–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a convex constraint γ ≤ ∏ x_i (products of survival or leak probabilities), keep the MIP linear: drop the constraint and add the closed-form tangent cut ∑ x_i/ŷ_i = n at a projected point. Run several cut rules in parallel and stop at the first proof (pp. 9–10, 23, 41–42).
- In a distributed protocol, let each agent's own optimization choose which facts to broadcast (binary message variables under a budget). Then treat what was not said as box uncertainty, whose robust counterpart is the nominal model at the upper bound (pp. 15–18).
- Prove that a coordination round never hurts by showing that the first-round solution stays feasible in the second-round problem (p. 20; pp. 42–43).
- Report 95% quantiles of time to the optimum and time to proof over many random instances, so that an operator can read off a stop time together with an optimality probability (p. 24; Table 4, p. 37).
- Bracket a decentralized method between the no-communication and the full-information centralized solutions on the same instances (Table 7, pp. 28–29, 40).

**Quotes**:
- "While this approach yields an optimal solution to the FDP that can serve as a benchmark, it cannot be used in real time." (p. 8)
- "If the survival threshold of ship s belongs, for instance, to the interval [0.9; 0.95] and the decision maker terminates the computation of the FDP after 0.6 s, then with 95 % probability the best integer solution found by this time is optimal." (p. 24)

### S909 · Optimal Selection of Health Care Providers (undated manuscript headed "Submitted to Manufacturing & Service Operations Management"; no DOI/arXiv on this row)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–31 (structured abstract, §§1–6, Formulation 1, Figures 1–12 through their captions and text layer, Appendices A–B, references). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Kung match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jerry Kung (Operations Research Center, MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: health-care analytics {AP DA}; by card a3-05, probably a chapter of Kung's 2017 thesis S736 (inferred). Its reference list cites none of his earlier claims-data work, such as the 2008 cost-prediction paper behind Method 5 (DOI 10.1287/opre.1080.0619) (p. 31).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO DA AP}: US health spending keeps rising without better quality, and opaque, widely varying prices are a main driver (pp. 1–3). A self-insured employer holds its employees' claims, and it can use them to find low-cost providers near its patients (pp. 1, 4). The question is posed in hindsight: had patients gone to nearby cheaper providers, how much would the employer have saved, and which providers and procedures matter most (pp. 7–9)?
- **D2 Core idea**: One MILO (Formulation 1, p. 13) reassigns historical demand, procedure by procedure, to providers within distance δ. It minimizes cost plus a penalty λ for each provider used. Zip-to-zip flow variables carry the demand, a budget φ limits how many procedure types may move, and capacities are multiples of observed volume (pp. 12–14). The problem splits exactly into the connected components of the "within δ" zip-code graph; a counterexample shows that solving neighbourhood by neighbourhood would not be exact (pp. 9–10). The output is a list of low-cost providers and of the procedures worth moving (about 100–200 types), for steering patients or negotiating contracts (pp. 1, 19, 27–28).
- **D3 Model and data**: All claims paid from 1 January 2011 to 1 July 2015 by a large self-insured employer in the southern US: 1.55 million claims, more than 14,000 patients, about 30,000 providers, 4,792 procedures and more than $151 million (p. 5). Cleaning, with locations re-derived from the CMS NPI file, leaves about 520,000 rows and $82.5 million (p. 8; pp. 30–31). The model covers procedures only, not drugs (p. 9), and only the largest region at δ = 50 km: about 77% of procedure costs, $63.6 million over 3.5 years, more than 21,000 providers and more than 3,600 procedures (p. 11). Assumptions:
  - perfect hindsight on demand and average prices (pp. 9, 12);
  - prices floored at the Medicare reference price, "To protect ourselves against data error" (p. 12);
  - capacity set at D̄^k times historical volume, for lack of capacity data (p. 12);
  - quality treated as unchanged, argued from the many diagnostic procedures selected (p. 28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The flow form needs only about 177,000 constraints and 184,000 variables for more than 1,300 zip codes, because constraints are created only for (procedure, zip code) pairs present in the data (p. 14). The big-M values in the procedure-budget constraints come from the data: M1 = D(D̄ − 1) and M2 = D (p. 13). The problem is described as related to minimum vertex cover (p. 11). With λ = 0 it reduces to the cheapest provider per procedure (p. 15). No theorem.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia/JuMP and Gurobi 6.5.0 on a quad-core i7-3770 with 32 GB (pp. 14–15). Of 360 settings of (λ, φ, D̄, δ), each with a 600 s limit, 240 were proved optimal and the rest ended within 0.23%, all with λ ≥ 2000 (p. 15). The only baseline is what the employer actually paid, $63.6 million (pp. 16–17); at δ = 40 km, D̄ = 2.5, φ = 100 and λ = 1000 the cost falls to $56.8 million, about 11% less, with half as many providers (p. 21). Sensitivity is one knob at a time: savings grow about linearly in δ, flatten after about 200 procedure types, and jump at D̄ = 2.5 and 3.0 because many providers appear only once or twice (pp. 17–22, 25). A colonoscopy/biopsy drill-down shows the average price falling from $1,279 to $825 for 284 patients, with 141 staying in their zip code and the others travelling 13 km (median) and 18.1 km (mean) (pp. 22–24). There is no hold-out evaluation and no field test.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a MILO solvable to optimality at scale, savings of about 10%, robustness under sensitivity analysis, and a route to implementation (p. 4). The conclusion calls the 10% conservative and sees up to 15% if the parameters are relaxed (p. 28). It calls the framework "widely applicable to all self-insuring employers in the US" (p. 29). Stated limits: no capacity data (p. 12); rows discarded in cleaning carried about 10% of cost (p. 30); travel is not penalized in the objective (p. 24); the results are "just the first step" (p. 27). My reading:
  - the evaluation is an in-sample replay: prices and demand are estimated, and savings are scored, on the same claims (p. 9);
  - the abstract's "average (maximum)" travel of 18 km (40 km) equals the mean for colonoscopy patients who changed zip code and the δ = 40 km cap (pp. 1, 24). Its 10% refers to procedure costs in the largest region (pp. 9, 11, 28);
  - the text is inconsistent in three places: the horizon (Figure 4's axis says 4 years, the text 3.5; pp. 16–17), the time limit (600 s, then 500 s; p. 15), and the number of providers given more than 5 extra procedures (7 on p. 25, 4 in Figure 12, p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 9, 11, 17, 21–28): the owner is the self-insured employer, the data are its claims, and the baseline is what it actually paid. The decision is scored in dollars, beside the patients' travel and the providers' added load. The paper closes with policies the employer could implement: provider lists, copay incentives and service contracts. There is no field test, and the replay is in-sample.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 9–15): provider selection is written as one MILO, kept tractable by an exact decomposition and a sparse flow form, with solve times measured on the real instance.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 14–15): solver, version, machine, time limit, certificates (240 of 360) and the worst gap (0.23%) are all reported. There is no warm start, and no duel with any method other than history.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 15–22): the trade-off between convenience and cost is priced by knobs: λ for the number of providers, δ for travel, φ for procedure types. Knees are read off the curves (φ ≈ 200; λ = 1000). There is no bound, and the curves vary one knob at a time.
- Tension T6 (not a method): the abstract's travel and savings figures, read in D8.
- New-pattern candidate: *counterfactual replay against the historical decision-maker* (pp. 9, 17, 21).
- New-pattern candidate: *exact decomposition by connected components, with a counterexample to local decomposition* (pp. 9–10).
- New-pattern candidate: *conservative input guards so the headline saving is understated* (pp. 12, 19, 28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split a reassignment problem into the connected components of its eligibility graph, which loses nothing, and show with a two-hop counterexample why solving each neighbourhood alone is not exact (pp. 9–10).
- When capacity data are missing, set capacity to a multiple of observed volume, sweep the multiple, and explain the jumps that integrality produces (pp. 12, 21–22, 25).
- Guard against data errors in the direction that shrinks the claimed saving: floor estimated unit prices at a public reference price (p. 12).
- Report the solve protocol over the whole parameter grid: the time limit, how many settings were proved optimal, the worst remaining gap, and which parameter drives the hardness (p. 15).
- Show the owner the users' burden beside the saving, for a representative procedure: price histograms, travel histograms and each provider's added load (pp. 22–27).

**Quotes**:
- "For our analysis, we formulate a model whose solution represents perfect hindsight; that is, given the historical claims data that we have collected so far, we consider the optimal re-assignment of patients to nearby low-cost providers." (p. 9)
- "In 240 of the parameter settings, provable optimality was achieved within the time limit." (p. 15)
