### S407 · Optimizing influenza vaccine composition: From predictions to prescriptions (Machine Learning for Healthcare Conference 2020, PMLR pp. 121–142 per the work list; no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–21 (abstract, §§1–5, references, Appendix A with Algorithm 1 and Tables 7–11). Figures 1–5 have no text layer beyond their captions. First-page check: the title and the authors Hari Bandi (ORC) and Dimitris Bertsimas (Sloan and ORC) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Hari Bandi · **Position in trajectory**: the predictive-to-prescriptive line (SW5) carried into public health. It reuses the group's own tools: tensor completion from Bertsimas–Pawlowski 2019 and Optimal Regression Trees from Bertsimas–Dunn 2017 (pp. 2–3, 7–8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA+RO+ML; AP (health)}:
  - The vaccine is reformulated every year, six to eight months ahead of the season (p. 2).
  - Strain-evolution predictors (fitness models, phylogenetics) "do not optimize the vaccine composition in anticipation of the uncertainty in their predictions" (p. 4).
  - The OR models of the WHO committee's choice (stochastic dynamic programming, optimal stopping, a stochastic game, a bilevel MIO) neither personalize by region nor quantify morbidity and mortality (p. 4).
  - Problem: prescribe one strain per subtype for a region from GISRS circulation data (pp. 1, 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: A three-stage pipeline (pp. 2–3, 7–10):
  - predict next season's circulation rates by tensor completion. The low-rank core may move only slowly between seasons (‖S^t − S^{t−1}‖_F ≤ λ), and the problem is solved by alternating SVDs of the unfoldings with an SOCP in the cores (pp. 7–8, 19);
  - choose the strain that minimizes the worst-case weighted antigenic distance over an uncertainty set built in the model's own latent space: Y = U S Vᵀ with ‖S − Ŝ‖_F² ≤ λ (p. 10);
  - score compositions by ORTs that predict morbidity and mortality from the three weighted distances (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - A 132-country × 1,206-virus × 32-season tensor (1987–2018), about 88% missing (pp. 2, 6).
  - HI-test distances d_uv = log(max_u h_uv / h_uv) between 1,377 viruses and 82 vaccine strains (pp. 6–7).
  - Morbidity and mortality from CDC, ECDC, GISRS and Iuliano et al.; past compositions from IRD (p. 6).
  - Assumption admitted for tractability: the latent weights "do not change drastically" between seasons (p. 3). r and λ are chosen by 5-fold cross-validation (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - The nominal choice is a one-hot z ∈ {0,1}^q (p. 9). Lemma 1, attributed to von Neumann, swaps min and max (p. 10). An epigraph variable then turns max_Y min_v into an SOCP, and the prescribed strain is ℓ = argmin_v at the worst-case Y* (p. 10).
  - [my derivation] Von Neumann's theorem covers mixed strategies over convex sets. Over the q vertices only max–min ≤ min–max holds, so (8) is a lower bound on the robust value Pᵢ, and ℓ is a best response to Y*, not necessarily the robust-optimal strain. With q = 82, solving one convex maximization per candidate would be exact.
  - The abstract and p. 5 call the SOCP "practically and theoretically tractable", but no solver, run time or convergence statement for Algorithm 1 is printed (pp. 5, 10, 19).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Retrospective seasons 2010–2018 against WHO (Northern hemisphere), FDA (USA) and EMA (Europe). ORTs are retrained on 20-year moving windows (R² 0.64–0.83 per season) and predict outcomes under both compositions (pp. 11–12).
  - The robust choice is better in 7 of 8 seasons in each table (pp. 11–12).
  - [my arithmetic] Eight-season totals of Tables 1–3 give reductions in illnesses and mortality of 9.4% and 7.8% (WHO), 8.5% and 9.6% (FDA), and 5.4% and 1.7% (EMA). The paper states ranges of 11–14% and 8–11%, 8–10% and 6–9%, and 10–12% and 9–11% without saying how they are computed (pp. 1, 11–12).
  - The outcome model can miss badly: USA 2011–12 records 9.3 million observed severe illnesses against 24.7 million predicted for the FDA vaccine actually used (p. 12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Three contributions: the restricted tensor completion, the set-based robust prescription and the retrospective comparison (p. 5).
  - The conclusion only repeats the percentages; no limits or future work are stated (p. 15).
  - [my reading] Several tables are internally inconsistent:
    - Table 8's distance columns repeat Table 4's digit for digit although the robust strains differ in five years, and its 2009 row lists the same strain with two distances (pp. 13, 20);
    - the text's count for EMA (five better seasons) does not match Table 8 (four) (pp. 13, 20);
    - Table 11's FDA column repeats Table 4's WHO column in 7 of 10 years (pp. 13, 21);
    - strains that Table 4 lists as H1N1 components (A/California/7/2009, A/Michigan/45/2015) appear as H3N2 prescriptions in Tables 5, 9 and 10 (pp. 13–14, 20). Model (5) puts no subtype restriction on v (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11–15): the institutions' actual compositions are the baseline, and the decision is scored in morbidity and mortality, as Method 5 asks. But the score comes from a learned outcome model, not from realized outcomes, and the headline ranges do not follow from the tables [my arithmetic].
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 9–10): the robust choice is kept in a conic class (an SOCP), but by a minimax swap applied outside its conditions [my derivation]. This is the stop-rule correction's case: test exactness on a small example.
- New-pattern candidate: *uncertainty set in the predictor's latent space* (pp. 9–10): the ball is placed around the learned low-rank core Ŝ, not around the raw rates, so robustness acts on a few factors.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the uncertainty set from your own predictor's structure: a Frobenius ball around the estimated low-rank core, mapped through the fixed factors U and V (p. 10).
- To make a tensor forecast over seasons tractable, fix the factors by SVDs of the mode-1 and mode-2 unfoldings, then solve one SOCP for the time-varying cores with a drift budget ‖S^t − S^{t−1}‖_F ≤ λ (pp. 7–8, 19).
- Correction [my derivation]: when the decision is one of q discrete options, solve q convex worst-case problems and take the minimum; do not swap min and max unless the choice may be mixed (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Although, some of these methods help identify a cluster or a set of viruses predicted to circulate in the future, they do not optimize the vaccine composition in anticipation of the uncertainty in their predictions." (p. 4)
- "The min-max in Problem (6) can be interchanged" (p. 10)

### S408 · Novel target discovery of existing therapies: Path to personalized cancer therapy (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2(1) 2020, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2019.0019)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–22 of the manuscript "Submitted to INFORMS Journal on Optimization" (abstract, §§1–4, Tables 1–5, Figures 1–3 captions and axes, references). First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Ying Daisy Zhuo (Sloan and ORC) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ying Daisy Zhuo · **Position in trajectory**: the cancer-analytics programme moves from aggregate clinical-trial data (S078; "the nature of the aggregate data prevents it from fully capturing individual heterogeneity", p. 4) to patient-level records and genomics at Dana-Farber.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+IO; AP (health)}:
  - A new drug costs about 2.5 billion (the currency sign is not printed) and takes 13.5 years, and 11% of trial drugs are approved (p. 2). Repurposing is cheaper, and targeted cancer drugs serve shrinking biomarker-defined cohorts (p. 2).
  - Finding new targets "relies on pure serendipity or anecdotal evidence", and the combinatorics defeat trial and error (p. 3).
  - Computational methods rest on incomplete curated databases with internal validation only, on in-vitro cell lines, and they ignore phenotypes (pp. 3–4).
  - Problem: infer binary therapy–target links w_jk from observational survival data (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**: A mechanism graph becomes an MIO regression (pp. 7–10, Figure 1):
  - log survival = β₀ + xᵀβ − Σ_ℓ r_ℓ s_iℓ, where pathway set S_ℓ is "abnormal" for patient i if some gene in it is mutated and not targeted by a therapy the patient received (pp. 7–8);
  - the logic is written as linear constraints on binaries (7c)–(7g), and the products r_ℓ s_iℓ are linearized with big-M (7h)–(7j) (pp. 9–10);
  - the objective is the sum of absolute errors plus λΣw_jk plus γ‖β‖₁, both penalties set by cross-validation (pp. 9–10);
  - bootstrap refits, including propensity-defined subsamples, turn the binary w_jk into selection frequencies, and a threshold gives a ranked list (pp. 11, 14–15).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Dana-Farber records 2004–2014: 3,118 patients, 6,928 regimens, 133 phenotype covariates, K = 176 mutations and m = 10 targeted therapies. Training uses regimens from 2004–11 and validation those from 2012–14 (pp. 6, 12–13).
  - TCGA: 1,098 patients and 299 mutations (pp. 13–14). Gene sets come from MSigDB/KEGG, and missing values are mean-imputed (p. 6).
  - Assumptions stated for causal reading: a Bayesian network with hidden variables is the true model, it has enough capacity, and the data are large enough. Because these cannot be verified, randomized experiments must confirm the links (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The MIO (7) is justified by solver progress: "a speedup of over several trillion times" (p. 9). Recovery is not proved; it is shown on synthetic data generated from the model (pp. 5, 12, 17–18). Software is R 3.3.3, Julia 0.6.0 and Gurobi 7.0 (p. 12). The value of M, the number of bootstrap replicates, solve times and gaps are not reported [my reading].
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Fit: out-of-sample MAE on log survival is 0.69 for the MIO against 0.71–0.73 for three L1-fitted linear models (Dana-Farber), and 0.91 against 0.92–0.99 (TCGA). No interval is given, although the text calls the gain significant (pp. 14–15).
  - Links against DrugBank: accuracy 95.8%, TPR 50%, FPR 4.0% at threshold 0.34 (Dana-Farber); 98.3%, 26.7% and 1.4% at 0.38 (TCGA) (pp. 15–16). Known pairs such as erlotinib–EGFR, pertuzumab–ERBB2 and cetuximab–EGFR are recovered, but trastuzumab–ERBB2 is not (p. 16).
  - [my arithmetic] Only 6 of 1,760 (Dana-Farber) and 15 of 3,588 (TCGA) pairs are known, so predicting "no link" everywhere scores 99.7% and 99.6%, above the reported accuracies. The informative figure is enrichment: precision is 3/73 (4.1%) and 4/53 (7.5%), about 12 and 18 times the base rates (Table 3, p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - The paper calls itself the first study to use observational in-vivo genomic and clinical data for drug discovery (p. 18).
  - Limits it states (pp. 18–19): confounding; a small gene panel ("296 measured mutations", while p. 13 gives K = 176 [my reading]); no immune-cell data; incomplete gene sets.
  - Future work: dosing information, and prospective in-vitro, animal and clinical validation (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 7–10): a problem owned by bioinformatics (which drug hits which target) is written as one MIO whose binary variables are the scientific output.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (p. 9): the "why now" is a borrowed speed-up figure with no citation and no timing of this model, the case Method 2's checkpoint names.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11–15): the incumbent linear models are dueled with a matched loss "for a fair comparison" (p. 11), but no solver limit, gap or certificate is reported.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 5, 14–19): treatment selection is promised as the "natural pathway" (p. 5), but the evaluation scores predictions (MAE) and recall of known links, not decisions.
- New-pattern candidate: *bootstrap selection frequency as the confidence of an MIO's binary output* (pp. 11, 14–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode a causal mechanism graph (therapy → target → pathway → outcome) as linear logical constraints on binaries, so that fitting the model answers the scientific question (pp. 8–10).
- Refit the MIO on bootstrap and propensity-defined subsamples, report each binary's selection frequency, and threshold it to a list of fixed length for experimental follow-up (pp. 11, 14–15).
- Fit the linear baselines with the same loss as the MIO (absolute error on log survival) so that the comparison isolates the structure (p. 11).
- Report precision against the base rate, not accuracy, when known positives are a fraction of a percent [my arithmetic, from Table 3, p. 15].

**Quotes**:
- "The current approach to find new targets of existing drugs in practice typically relies on pure serendipity or anecdotal evidence." (p. 3)
- "In recent years, advances in algorithms and computational hardware allowed a speedup of over several trillion times for solving MIO models, making even complex, large-scale problems tractable." (p. 9)

### S543 · Time series that are robust to regime changes (J. Mach. Learn. Res. 21 2020, JMLR paper 19-408; no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–31 (abstract, §§1–6, Theorem 1 and proof, Tables 1–6, acknowledgements, references). The templated result paragraphs for weekly and monthly refitting (pp. 18–27) were checked against the tables rather than read line by line. First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas (Sloan and ORC) and Ivan Paskov (ORC) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ivan Paskov · **Position in trajectory**: the third of Paskov's "stable" papers, after stable regression (S312) and stable classification (S365), both cited (p. 30). Funded by an IBM grant (p. 29).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+RO; IO as device}: Regime changes are handled either by tests that detect a break "after the fact" or by switching models (EARMA, Markov switching) that require the switching dynamics (pp. 1–3). Problem: build one AR(p) model, without a model of the switching process, that is robust to regime changes before they happen (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Instead of averaging models trained on resampled data, train one model against the worst synthetic training set (pp. 4–5):
  - (2): min over (ξ, γ) of max over z ∈ Z of Σ z_t (residual_t)² (p. 5);
  - Z1: all subsets of size k (p. 5);
  - Z2: k blocks of h consecutive periods (p. 6). Z2 is computed through a fused relaxation in which a penalty λΣ|z_{t+1} − z_t| and a multiplier Γ on Σz replace the block and cardinality constraints (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - AR(p) models with the moving-average order set to zero and the data differenced to stationarity (p. 4).
  - Beth Israel Deaconess emergency-department arrivals and bed requests, sampled every 24 and 8 hours. The turbulent window runs December 2019 to October 2020 (Covid-19) and the calm window January to November 2019, both confirmed by the hospital (p. 13).
  - Chronological splits: 15% test, then 70/30 train and validation; log and first differences (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 1: after Γ removes the cardinality row, the fused constraint matrix is totally unimodular (Ghouila-Houri, Hoffman–Kruskal), so the binary inner maximization equals its LP relaxation (p. 9).
  - LP duality then turns the min–max into one convex quadratic problem (p. 10). For Z1 this is the top-k-sum dual: min kθ + Σuᵢ subject to θ + uᵢ ≥ rᵢ², u ≥ 0 (eq. 8, p. 12).
  - [my derivation] The printed dual for Z2 has three sign slips: the objective subtracts λΣ(s1t + s2t), which leaves it unbounded below in s; the v2 row reads ≥ λ for ≥ −λ; and the last z row reads +p_{n−1} for −p_{n−1} (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Eight series under daily, weekly and monthly refitting, each with standard and rolling-window forecasts. Metrics are MSE and "model stability" (described as the standard deviation of the coefficients on p. 15, as their variance in the captions), with standard errors and p-values (pp. 13–27).
  - The only baseline is the regular OLS-fitted AR model, although switching and changepoint methods are reviewed (pp. 2–3, 15). Several gains are not significant (e.g., p = 0.212, 0.526 and 0.391 turbulent; 0.098 and 0.640 calm; pp. 16–18).
  - No hyperparameters (p, k, λ, Γ), solver or run times are reported, despite "our timing section" (p. 12) and "highly efficient" (p. 1) [my reading].
  - [my arithmetic] For emergency arrivals at 24 hours, the MSE gain of Z2 is larger in the calm window than in the turbulent one under every refit schedule: 3.9% against 1.9% daily, 7.4% against 0.9% weekly, 9.0% against 6.1% monthly (Tables 1–6, pp. 15–25). This reads more like regularization than robustness to the regime change.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The stated claims (pp. 3, 27–29):
  - gains in accuracy and especially stability;
  - gains that grow when models are refit less often;
  - no loss in calm periods.

  No limitations or future work are stated. Choosing between Z1 (more stable) and Z2 (more accurate) is "up to the user" (p. 27). The Discussion calls Z1's set "smaller" and therefore more conservative (p. 27). But p. 6 itself says that Z2's series form a subset of Z1's, so Z1 is the larger set [my reading].

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 8–12): a combinatorial worst case over training sets is made exact by total unimodularity, dualized, and kept as one convex QP.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 27–28): conservatism is indexed by k (and by λ and Γ), and the price is checked on a calm window. But no knob–property bound or curve is shown, and the headline says the price is zero.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13–16): only the naive incumbent (OLS AR) is dueled, and there are no timings or settings. Heuristic 1 and Taste mark 3 (replace randomization by optimization) are the paper's frame (p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate: *the worst training split as the uncertainty set* (pp. 4–6): validation-split randomness becomes a min–max over subsets or blocks, then made exact by total unimodularity.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To train against the worst k samples, minimize kθ + Σuᵢ subject to uᵢ ≥ lossᵢ − θ and u ≥ 0. The dual of the top-k sum keeps a convex loss convex (eq. 8, p. 12).
- Move a cardinality constraint into the objective with a multiplier (Γ), and linearize a fusion penalty with two slacks. The remaining constraint matrix is totally unimodular, so the binary inner problem can be replaced by its LP (pp. 8–9).
- Evaluate a robustness claim on a turbulent and a calm window separately, across refit frequencies, with fixed-window and rolling forecasts (pp. 13–14).

**Quotes**:
- "In this paper, we propose a methodology for constructing a single model that is explicitly designed to be robust against regime changes." (p. 2)
- "stable time series (Z1) employ a smaller uncertainty set than do stable time series (Z2), and hence can be viewed as more conservative." (p. 27)

### H007 · Robust Optimization with Side Data (manuscript, submitted to Mathematical Programming per the 2021 homepage entry; file dated May 8, 2023; no DOI)
Read level: **full** (main text): pp. 1–18 (abstract, §§1–5, Figures 1–13, references), Appendix A.3–A.4 (pp. 21–23) and Lemmas C.1–C.2 (pp. 25–26). Not read: Appendix A.1–A.2 (pp. 19–21), Appendix B (pp. 24–25) and the remaining proofs of Appendix C (pp. 26–32). First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Nihal Koduri match; the file is dated May 8, 2023 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nihal Koduri (ORC) · **Position in trajectory**: the side-information line, from Bertsimas–Kallus 2020 (S008) through Bertsimas–Koduri's RKHS paper (S133), reaches the constraints. It is a contextual version of data-driven RO (S013) (pp. 1–3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+DA}: An RO user must choose an uncertainty set, and data-driven sets use only past y (p. 1). But side data x (season, last quarter's earnings) narrow the possible y. Problem (CRO): min c(z) subject to max over y ∈ Y(x⁰) of f_j(z, y) ≤ 0, where Y(x⁰) is unknown and only (xⁱ, yⁱ) pairs are observed (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Start from the scenario approach (1), which replaces Y by the data points (Calafiore–Campi), and add context in one of two ways (p. 2):
  - local: keep only the k nearest neighbours of x⁰ (CRO-LOC), or the points sharing x⁰'s leaf in a CART, ORT or random forest (p. 9);
  - global: impose all scenarios on a decision rule z(x) in an RKHS with ‖z‖² ≤ η, then evaluate it at x⁰ (CRO-GLO).

  Adaptive problems are reduced to the same form through the second-stage value c̃₂(z₁, y) and an epigraph variable, giving LPs or QPs (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**: IID data. For Theorem 2.1 (p. 4):
  - continuity of c, f and Y(x);
  - f_j convex in z; Z convex and compact; Y(x) bounded;
  - Slater-type interior feasibility;
  - positive probability near x⁰ and near each feasible y;
  - k(n)/n → 0 and k(n) ≥ n^{1/b}.

  The global theorem adds a Gaussian kernel, Lipschitz c and f, bounded X, and continuous interior and optimal selections (p. 6). Experiments use synthetic data y = βᵀx × ε, with ε uniform on [0.9, 1.1] (pp. 11, 16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Lemma 2.1/C.1: the k-th neighbour converges to x⁰ almost surely, by the strong law, when k/n → 0 (pp. 5, 25).
  - Violation bounds use Borel–Cantelli with k(n) ≥ n^{1/b} (p. 5). Proposition 2.1 sandwiches the limit of the feasible sets between int Zₓ(x⁰) and Zₓ(x⁰) (p. 5).
