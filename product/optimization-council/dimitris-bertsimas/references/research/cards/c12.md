### S040 · Algorithm for cardinality-constrained quadratic optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2009, DOI 10.1007/s10589-007-9126-9)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–22 (body pp. 1–16; Appendix A, Tables 6–7, pp. 17–21, read row by row; references p. 22).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Romy Shioda (University of Waterloo, p. 1; listed in SKILL.md's ML lineage) · **Position in trajectory**: received 14 June 2006, revised 25 May 2007 (p. 1); the group's attempt at exact subset selection a decade before SW4 (AoS 2016), when the comparison point was CPLEX's MIQP with big-M constraints (p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + ML + AP (finance)}: Cardinality-constrained convex QPs (CCQO): a convex QP with |supp(x)| ≤ K and minimum levels x_i ≥ α_i on the support (problem (1), p. 2). Entry is a literature gap plus two applications. The gap: compared with linear IO, "quadratic mixed-integer optimization problems have received relatively little attention" (p. 2). The starting point is Bienstock's 1996 tailored branch-and-bound [4] (p. 2). The applications are K-subset selection in regression (eq. (7), p. 6) and mean-variance portfolio selection with sector-change limits, quadratic price-impact costs, a cardinality limit and minimum transaction levels (eqs. (8)–(9), pp. 8–9). The portfolio case is motivated by asset-management practice: managers tracking a benchmark of d securities who do not want to be seen as indexers, focused funds, and $100,000 separate accounts (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Branch directly on the continuous x, with no binary variables and no big-M (pp. 2, 10). At each node, solve the convex relaxation (2) (cardinality dropped, branched-up lower bounds kept; p. 3) with Lemke's pivoting method on the LCP formed by the QP's KKT conditions. It is warm-started from the parent's basis like dual simplex, the stated advantage over interior-point methods that "may need to solve from scratch at each node" (p. 3). On a down-branch the variable is deleted rather than fixed by x_s = 0, so subproblems never grow (pp. 2, 4). Lower bounds x_i ≥ α_i are enforced inside Lemke's method, "analogous to the simplex method with lower and upper-bounds" (p. 3). Root heuristic: re-optimize on the K largest |x*_i|, then run the branch-and-bound restricted to the K + W largest (W = 0.1d) with a node limit, seeded with that first upper bound (p. 6). Changes from Bienstock: Lemke pivoting replaces a primal feasible-descent QP solver with penalty warm starts, and bound constraints are not added (p. 2). The regression specialisation needs no Lemke at all, since the node relaxation has a closed form. It keeps v = X'Y from the root and deletes entries; the node value Y'Y − v'(X'X)⁻¹v costs only matrix-vector products; up-branches need no update (p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: Q symmetric PSD; Ax ≤ b; 0 ≤ x ≤ u (p. 2). Regression is kept unconstrained on purpose, "to highlight the merits of our tailored approach versus a general purpose software such as CPLEX" (p. 7). Portfolio: ticket (fixed) costs are left out as "often second order" (p. 8), and x0 = 0 in every instance (p. 13). Data are synthetic only. Regression: random X and β, Y = Xβ + ε with ε_i ~ N(0,1), five instances per (d, K) (p. 10). Portfolio: Σ = d/(d−1)·AᵀA with Gaussian A, Gaussian r and uniform c_i, five instances per (d, K, S) (p. 13). The authors admit that random data "may give unrealistically optimistic running times" (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No theorems; the analysis is linear-algebraic.
  - Lemke's augmentation uses a covering vector h and an artificial z0 (pp. 3–4). The warm start solves LCP(M_B̄, q_B̄) from the parent's basis B (p. 4).
  - Deleting row and column s rests on a rank-one identity, UB̄ = I − U_col·B_row (eq. (4)). Elementary row operations E then give the new inverse, with M_B̄ = E(M + U_col·N_row) and q_B̄ = E(q̃_B − q_s·U_col) (eqs. (5)–(6), pp. 4–5).
  - Only one column of B⁻¹ is needed, obtained from a Crout LU factorisation by back-substitution (pp. 5–6). If the reduced basis is singular, Lemke restarts from the identity basis, which the authors report happened rarely in practice (p. 5).
  - Side property: pivoting returns a basic KKT solution, so the relaxation's solution has minimum support, which matters when Q is only PSD (p. 14).
  - Search: depth-first, branching first on the largest |value| (pp. 10, 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Subset selection*, d ∈ {20, 50, 100, 500}, K from 5 to 400. Opponents: forward stepwise regression, whose times were not recorded because it solved almost all instances "in a fraction of a second", and CPLEX's MIQP on the big-M model with M = 100 (p. 10). The CPLEX version appears only in reference [8], the ILOG CPLEX 8.1 manual (p. 22); no machine is stated anywhere in the text. Metrics: RSS, CPU seconds, nodes, and the node at which the best solution was found (p. 10). Limits: 60 s (Table 1) and 3600 s (Table 2) (p. 11), with per-instance Tables 6–7 (pp. 17–21).
  - *Subset-selection results*: the exact methods beat forward regression in RSS "even when they do not solve to provable optimality" (p. 11). Wherever CPLEX was not optimal at 60 s, BnB's RSS was "consistently lower" (p. 11), and CPLEX did worse than forward regression for K large relative to d (100/80, 500/400) (p. 11). BnB solved all five (100, 80) instances in 8 minutes on average, while CPLEX failed on three of five in an hour (p. 12). 12-hour CPLEX runs did not beat BnB's one-hour solutions: 758,261 vs 690,532 on instance (100, 20, 1), and 56,390,541 vs 38,781 on (500, 400, 1) (p. 12). Printed limit: up to a million nodes to prove optimality even at moderate size, because free variables and few constraints prevent pruning (p. 13).
  - *Portfolio*, d ∈ {100, 200, 500}: LemkeBnB vs BarrierBnB (the same branch-and-bound with CPLEX's barrier at the nodes) vs CPLEX MIQP, at 120 s (Tables 3–4, p. 14) and 3600 s (Table 5, p. 15); "–" marks no feasible solution within 120 s (p. 13). Pivoting beats barrier: about 400 pivots from scratch vs about 5 to re-solve a node (p. 14). CPLEX processes nodes faster, by about 10× at K ≈ 0.5d and 2–3× at K ≈ 0.1d (pp. 14–15).
  - *Reading of Tables 3–4* (p. 14): without the root heuristic, CPLEX MIQP's upper bound is equal or better in all seven (d, K, S) rows (e.g. 19.02 vs 28.46; 81.86 vs 142.73). With the heuristic, LemkeBnB's is better in all seven. The portfolio advantage therefore rests on the heuristic, which the text half-concedes: "Running the heuristic, even for just 30 CPU seconds, brought significant improvement" (p. 15).
  - No lower bounds or gaps are reported anywhere (Tables 1–7).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Bienstock [4] (p. 2). Regression lineage: leaps-and-bounds and variable-discarding methods [2, 9, 10], and LAD subset selection by linear MIO [19] (p. 7). Portfolio lineage, sorted into three groups (pp. 7–8): approximations (LP or piecewise-linear objectives [24, 25], equal weights as a 0-1 problem [11], a polynomial special case [21]), heuristics (MIO-based [17], DP [5], GA, tabu search and SA [6]), and exact methods [20, 12, 15, 16]. The stated difference from all of them is Lemke's method (p. 8). Self-citation: his GMO portfolio MIP paper (Interfaces 1999, ref. [3], p. 22) as the source of practice's discrete constraints (p. 7). On CPLEX, he concedes its pivoting and branch-and-bound are "far superior to ours" and frames the test as tailored vs general (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Hedged abstract ("practical advantages for the special class of problems we consider", p. 1). Conclusions come as a numbered list after "we learn that" (pp. 15–16), worded modestly ("show some computational advantages", p. 15) and self-deprecatingly ("compensates for our lack of software engineering prowess", p. 15). Non-optimality is framed as practical: "Given the noise in the data, perhaps these solutions are sufficient in practice" (p. 13). Admissions sit in the body: random data may be optimistic (p. 13), CPLEX is faster per node (p. 14), and one CPLEX weakness is left unexplained ("It is not clear why", p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and computational (a tailored code), with no theory. The limits are named specifically (p. 16): depth-first search gets stuck in subtrees and leaves the root bound as the bound for most nodes; Lemke's method gives no choice of entering variable; data structures and memory handling need work. Future work: other principal pivoting methods, other CCQOs (p. 16). The goal is narrowed to finding "good (but not necessarily provably optimal) solutions quickly" (p. 16). Left out: constrained regression (p. 7), real data (p. 13), ticket costs (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 6, 10–13, 16). For: the greedy incumbent (forward regression) is the opponent and loses on RSS within 60 s, and both time limits are stated. Against: the warm start is the paper's own restricted-B&B heuristic, the duel is in-sample RSS on synthetic data, no gap is reported, and the claim is narrowed to good solutions found fast rather than certificates (pp. 13, 16). This is an early instance of Tension T1, and it also fits Heuristic 3's "change the claim".
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 6, 10, 12, 15). Subset selection is written exactly as an MIQP/CCQO (eq. (7)), but the remedy for a weak solver is a tailored algorithm. That is the reverse of Method 1's "the model, not the algorithm, is redesigned so existing solvers apply" (SKILL.md).
- Method 2: evidence ✅ (p. 12). The group's own 2007 finding that CPLEX's general MIQP was "not well suited" to subset selection at d = 100–500 shows the old belief was right when formed (step 2).
- Tension T4: evidence (p. 12). The group already named big-M weakness in 2007, years before the big-M critiques of OCT that SKILL.md records under T4.
- New-pattern candidate: **branch on the continuous variables, delete on down-branches** (no binaries, no big-M; pp. 2, 4, 10, 12).
- New-pattern candidate: **pick the node solver for warm starts** (pivoting over interior point inside branch-and-bound; pp. 3, 14, 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If the MIO needs big-M, also build a branch-and-bound that branches on the continuous variables (down-branch: delete the column; up-branch: x_s ≥ α_s enforced inside the pivoting). Race it against the solver's big-M model at equal time limits (pp. 2, 10, 12).
- Inside branch-and-bound, prefer a node solver that warm-starts from the parent's basis (dual simplex, or Lemke for QP KKT systems). Justify the choice with pivots per node vs from scratch: about 5 vs 400 here (p. 14).
- After deleting a variable, update the basis inverse by a rank-one elementary-row-operation step (eqs. (4)–(6)). Keep only an LU factorisation and extract the one column needed (pp. 4–6), and restart from the identity basis if the reduced basis is singular (p. 5).
- Root heuristic for cardinality problems: re-optimize on the K largest relaxation entries, then run the exact branch-and-bound on the K + W largest (W ≈ 0.1d) with a node cap, seeding the first upper bound (p. 6).
- Protocol: use two budgets, a short one to test whether good solutions come fast and a long one to test proof. Put averages in the body, per-instance tables in the appendix, and a "best node" column showing where incumbents are found (pp. 10–12, 17–21). Run the comparison with and without the heuristic so its share is visible (Tables 3–4, p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "These big-M constraints can lead to weak LP relaxations, making it hard to fathom nodes." (p. 12)
- "However, provable optimality may not be of critical importance to data mining practitioners who may be interested in finding a “good” solution “quickly”." (p. 13)

### S077 · Optimal bidding in online auctions (Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management 2009, DOI 10.1057/rpm.2008.49)
Read level: **full** (all 35 preprint pages). The file is an OCR of the working-paper preprint "Optimal Bidding in On-line Auctions" (December 2001, revised December 2002; p. 1), not the JRPM 2009 print. The OCR lost most table bodies (Tables 1–5 and 7–19; only fragments of Tables 6 and 13 survive, pp. 18, 28) and garbled parts of eqs. (1)–(17) and (19)–(38) (pp. 9–11, 21–25). Numbers below come from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jeffrey Hawkins (ORC, p. 1), Georgia Perakis (Sloan, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: 2001–2002 e-commerce work in the revenue-management and pricing strand (AP), contemporary with the price-of-robustness papers. The acknowledgement thanks an Associate Editor and referees (p. 33), so this preprint is already a post-review revision. A companion preprint on stochastically arriving auctions is cited (ref. [5], pp. 32, 34).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO + AP (revenue management and pricing); DA flavour}: How should a buyer bid for one item in an eBay auction, and for several items in simultaneous or overlapping auctions under one budget (abstract, p. 1)? The entry is an application at scale and features observed in its data:
  - eBay has over 42 million users and hosted over $9.3 billion of goods sold in 2001 (pp. 1–2).
  - Prices vary widely even for standard items (p. 2).
  - Hard stop times make sniping pay: for the Palm Pilot III, bids per second in the final ten seconds are over 100 times those in the final day (p. 3).
  - Last-second bids may fail to register (Roth and Ockenfels, p. 3).

  The model must (a) capture the essentials of online auctions, (b) give a computationally feasible algorithm directly usable by bidders, and (c) have parameters estimable from public data (p. 4). Hence the choice of "an optimization, as opposed to a game theoretic approach" (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: All other bidders are treated as a "population" whose maximum bid per period has a distribution depending on the time remaining and the listed price, estimated from eBay's public bid histories (pp. 5, 11–12).
  - *Single auction*: finite-horizon DP over 13 periods of shrinking length (5 days … 10 seconds), chosen to match rising bid intensity and to be convenient for bidders to follow (p. 6). State = (listed price, own proxy bid) (p. 7); acceptance probability p < 1 in the last period (p. 8); utility w·(A − x) for budget A (eq. (15), p. 10); Bellman equations (16)–(17) (pp. 10–11).
  - *Multiple simultaneous auctions*: exact DP is "barely feasible even for two auctions" and infeasible for three (p. 21), hence five approximations:
    - ADP1: 2^N linear value approximations fitted by regression on simulated states; works up to N = 5 (p. 22).
    - ADP2: N + 1 linear functions indexed by the number of auctions the agent leads (pp. 22–23).
    - IPA: single-auction DP values d_t^i(x, h, j) combined in a 0-1 IP with monotone bid-at-least-j variables, marginal-utility coefficients, one global budget row and proxy-bid rows (eqs. (24)–(27), pp. 23–24).
    - PIPA1: all pairs solved exactly by DP and combined in a pairwise IP (eqs. (29)–(38), pp. 24–25).
    - PIPA2: N/2 disjoint pairs only (p. 26).
  - *Overlapping auctions*: IPA with values indexed by each auction's time remaining and the remaining budget (pp. 32–33).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Scope: reserve-price auctions are excluded because eBay does not publish reserves (p. 2), and seller ratings are ignored (p. 4).
  - Population model: one competing (maximum) population bid per period (p. 12). No dependence on whether the agent leads was found, so that conditioning is dropped (p. 11). Bids are rounded to $10 (PDAs) or $50 (stamps) to avoid sparse distributions (pp. 11–12).
  - Data: 1772 Palm Pilot III auctions with 22478 bids over two weeks, and 4208 stamp-collection auctions with 50766 bids, chosen to capture private and common value auctions respectively (p. 12).
  - Acceptance probability: p is estimated at about 0.27 from the shape of the bid-arrival fractions (eq. (18), p. 14). Yet p = 0.8 is used, "based roughly on experience", after no qualitative difference was found across p (pp. 14, 17).
  - A richer state (the number of bids) is rejected: tractability would fall and estimation would suffer from sparse data (p. 10).
  - IPA ignores future budget constraints and the correlation of population bids across auctions (p. 24). Competitors' entrance probabilities are assumed independent across auctions (p. 32).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No theorems. Devices:
  - exact backward DP (eqs. (16)–(17), pp. 10–11), with population-bid probabilities capped at the budget (p. 11);
  - simulation-based least-squares ADP (eqs. (20)–(21), p. 22);
  - a threshold-variable IP whose objective telescopes: bidding j0 contributes exactly the single-auction value of j0 (p. 24);
  - a two-dimensional version with consistency constraints (32)–(35) (p. 25);
  - an estimate of the last-second acceptance probability from the ratio of observed to submitted bid fractions (eq. (18), p. 14).

  The authors themselves call IPA an approximation, because it ignores future budget constraints (p. 24).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Simulation with 10,000 runs per setting, drawing bids "from the same distribution for which the algorithm was constructed" (p. 15); there is no hold-out period. Baselines are the static rules bidders widely use: bid the budget at t = 0, at T − 1 or at T (p. 17). Metrics: win %, average utility, average spent per win (p. 17).
  - *Single auction*: DP "was clearly the best" in average utility, though it wins less often than bidding the budget early (p. 17). Palm Pilot III: $105.8 spent per win, and one real auction won for $92 (p. 17). The sentence introducing Table 5 says stamp collections, but its caption and A = 15 are the PDA setting (pp. 17–18). Stamps: 99% wins at $163.6 per win, against 98% and $168.4 for the next best (p. 17).
  - *Robustness to one competitor*: utility falls only slightly as the competitor's budget grows, except at equal budgets (p. 18). A DP told that the competitor enters with probability < 1 performs poorly (p. 20).
  - *Multiple auctions*, N = 2, 3, 6 (p. 26): IP methods beat ADP (p. 27); IPA is almost optimal against exact DP at N = 2 (pp. 27–29); PIPA1 is slightly better at much higher effort (p. 29); IPA and PIPA1 are identical under per-auction budgets (p. 29). IPA is "significantly faster" (p. 29), but no run times survive in the text. IPA struggles against a lowest-listed-price competitor when entry is uncertain (p. 32).
  - *Field use*: the first author applied the algorithm in over one thousand stamp auctions and "feels" it cut the amount paid per win by 20%, while granting this is hard to assess scientifically (p. 33; also pp. 5, 17).
  - Failures are printed (pp. 20, 32).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Auction theory: surveys, and Vickrey's second-price auction as the mechanism eBay resembles, with the two differences (identities and highest bid revealed; a hard stop) (p. 3).
  - Empirical late-bidding literature: Roth and Ockenfels, Bajari and Hortaçsu, Hahn, Hasker et al., Mizuta and Steiglitz (pp. 3–4).
  - Multiple-auction work: Oren and Rothkopf, Boutilier et al.'s DP approximation, Zheng, Stone and Greenwald, Bapna et al. (p. 4); ADP from Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (p. 22).

  The paper does not argue with game theory; it sets it aside for computability, estimation from data and few behavioural assumptions (pp. 4–5). It claims the DP-plus-IP coupling is new: "To the best of our knowledge, this method is new" (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract opens with eBay's scale numbers, then the methods (ADP and IP), then the two findings (p. 1).
  - Modelling choices are justified against the three requirements stated in the introduction (pp. 4, 10).
  - Contributions form a numbered list after "We feel that this paper makes the following contributions" (p. 5); "We feel" recurs (pp. 5, 33).
  - Results are distilled into lettered insights (a)–(d) (pp. 27–29).
  - Personal testimony is used as evidence, with a hedge (pp. 5, 17, 33). Its counts are worded differently: "win over one thousand stamp collections and individual stamps" (p. 17), against "buy over one hundred stamp collections" and use "in over one thousand auctions" (p. 33).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions: a DP model whose parameters are estimable from data, with an exact algorithm for one auction; ADP and IP approximations for several auctions, with IPA recommended (pp. 5, 29, 33). The authors suggest the method applies to weakly coupled DPs in general (pp. 5, 33).
  - Limits named: reserve auctions and seller reputation are out of scope (pp. 2, 4); IPA ignores future budget constraints and cross-auction correlation (p. 24) and struggles against competitive agents in multiple auctions (p. 32); exact DP is infeasible beyond two auctions (p. 21).
  - Future work: stochastically arriving auctions (companion preprint, p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 4, 11–12, 17, 33). The decision (the bid) is scored by utility, win rate and price paid against the rules bidders actually use (bid the budget at 0, T − 1 or T). The parameters come from public data, and the first author uses the method in the field.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 15, 33). The simulation draws from the same estimated distribution that built the policy, and the field evidence is self-reported and "difficult to assess scientifically"; the duel against the owner's rule is in-sample.
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 4, 23–24). A problem owned by auction theory and game theory is recast as optimization (DP), and the high-dimensional coupling is written as a 0-1 IP over single-auction value functions. But the IP is an admitted approximation (p. 24), not an exact class-keeping reformulation.
- Heuristic 4 (print negative results): evidence (pp. 20, 32).
- New-pattern candidate: **single-component DP values as IP coefficients** for weakly coupled DPs (pp. 5, 23–24, 33).
- New-pattern candidate: **author as first user**, i.e. self-experimentation as the field test (pp. 5, 17, 33).
- New-pattern candidate: **model requirements stated up front** (essentials, computable for the user, estimable from public data) (p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a weakly coupled DP (components linked only by a shared budget), solve each component exactly. Then choose the joint action by a 0-1 IP with monotone bid-at-least-j threshold binaries and marginal-value coefficients, so the objective telescopes to each component's value (pp. 23–24). Validate against exact DP at the largest size it can solve (N = 2) before scaling up (pp. 26–29).
- State the model's requirements first (captures the essentials; computable for the user; parameters estimable from public data), and reject richer states that fail them, saying why (pp. 4, 10).
- Use non-uniform decision epochs that shrink where activity concentrates and fall at times users can act on (p. 6).
- Estimate an unobservable failure rate (last-second bids that do not register) from how observed arrival fractions deviate from a scale-invariant pattern (eq. (18), Fig. 2, p. 14). Then check sensitivity over a range of values (pp. 14, 16–17).
- Test robustness against strategic competitors as well as the fitted population, including a misspecified prior on the competitor's entry, and print where the method loses (pp. 18–20, 30–32).

**Quotes**:
- "To achieve our goals we have taken an optimization, as opposed to a game theoretic approach." (p. 4)
- "While it is difficult to assess scientifically the effects, the first author feels the algorithm contributed to (a) increasing the probability of winning and (b) decreasing by 20% the amount paid per win." (p. 33)

### S042 · Optimality of affine policies in multistage robust optimization (Mathematics of Operations Research 2010, DOI 10.1287/moor.1100.0444; arXiv 0904.3986)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:0904.3986v1, 25 Apr 2009), pp. 1–35, appendix included: §7.1 DP solution (pp. 27–29), §7.2 zonotopes (pp. 29–31), §7.3 proof of Lemma 4.3 (pp. 31–33). The MOR 2010 print (pp. 363–394) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Dan A. Iancu (ORC, p. 1), Pablo A. Parrilo (LIDS, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the adaptive-RO strand of 2001–2013. SKILL.md's Domain Map anchors "adaptive policies with self-quantified limits" (RO) on this DOI.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO (+ SD: multistage control; AP: inventory)}: Affine decision rules in the observed disturbances had been advocated in stochastic programming (Rockafellar–Wets 1978), robust optimization (Ben-Tal et al. 2004) and robust and receding-horizon control. Papers showed how to compute them (LP, QP, conic, SDP) and gave some system-theoretic properties (pp. 2–3), but there had been "very little progress" on the quality of the objective values they deliver (p. 3). The paper takes robust modelling as given rather than justifying it (p. 1) and asks when affine policies are optimal for Problem 1.1. That problem is a one-dimensional, linear, time-varying system with box disturbances and box control constraints, and a finite-horizon min-max objective with convex coercive state costs and linear control costs c_k ≥ 0 (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - *Theorem 3.1* (p. 5): for every k there exist an affine control q_k(w) and an affine running cost z_k(w) such that q_k is robustly feasible, z_k dominates h_k, and the min-max value J_mM is unchanged.
  - *Two consequences*. Existential: affine policies are optimal. Computational: with piecewise-affine h_k, the optimal affine policies come from an LP, via the AARC and the standard reformulation of bi-affine robust constraints over boxes, with O(T²·max m_k) variables and constraints (pp. 5–6). State bounds can be absorbed into h_k as convex indicator barriers, so the result extends to them when the problem stays feasible (p. 6).
  - *Key insight*: under the induction hypothesis, the k disturbances act on the objective only through a 2-D affine image of the hypercube (cost so far, state), which is a zonogon. A function θ1 + f(θ2) with f convex is maximized at the k + 1 vertices on its right side (Lemma 4.1, Corollary 4.1, pp. 7–9). So an affine controller with k + 1 coefficients has enough freedom to reproduce the worst cases (p. 9).
  - *What changed vs the classical approach*: classical backward DP in the state x_k gives a piecewise-affine optimal policy with 3 pieces (pp. 3–4). The paper instead uses forward induction over the disturbance history (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: One-dimensional state; disturbances in independent intervals per period; controls in [L_k, U_k]; h_k convex and coercive; c_k ≥ 0; finite horizon; worst-case objective (pp. 1–2). α_k = β_k = γ_k = 1 without loss of generality, by rescaling (p. 3, fn. 1). Three assumptions are made for exposition only: a unit hypercube, a zonogon with the maximal number of vertices (p = k), and a vertex numbering; the degenerate case is handled by perturbation (p. 10). A unique minimizer of the convex g is assumed for simplicity (p. 10, fn. 4). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Proof skeleton:
  - (i) *DP solution in the state*: a 3-piece, continuous, non-increasing control law; convex J*_k and g_k; slope property P3 with f ∈ [0, 1] (P1–P5, p. 4; derivation §7.1, pp. 27–29).
  - (ii) *Forward induction*: base case q_1 = u*_1(x_1), with z_1 the linear interpolation of h_1 at the interval's endpoints (p. 7).
  - (iii) *Reduction to the right side*: a convex function over a polytope peaks at extreme points (Rockafellar §32), and with zonogon central symmetry only the right-side vertices matter (Lemma 4.1, p. 8). The right side and the zonogon hull are defined in Definitions 4.1–4.2 (pp. 8–9).
  - (iv) *Case analysis*: the zonogon is compared with the band where the control constraints are inactive, giving three trivial cases where u* is already affine and one non-trivial case (pp. 11–12; Lemma 4.3, proved in §7.3, pp. 31–33).
  - (v) *Algorithm 1*: matching (q equals u* at the vertices that matter) and alignment (generator segments share cotangents K_U, K_L) make the new zonogon equal to the zonogon hull of the worst-case points, so the max is unchanged (pp. 13–14, 16–17). The linear system is feasible by a count of k + 3 unknowns, and robust feasibility follows from −b_i ≤ q_i ≤ 0 (Lemma 4.4, p. 15).
  - (vi) *Algorithm 2* builds the affine cost the same way (p. 20; Lemma 4.7, pp. 20–21). Domination holds at hypercube vertices by induction on subset size, using the mediant inequality and the increasing increments of convex functions (Lemma 4.8, pp. 22–24). It then holds on the whole cube, since a convex function peaks at vertices (Lemma 4.9, p. 24). The zonogon facts (central symmetry, at most 2k vertices, adjacency) are only sketched (Lemma 7.2, pp. 30–31).
  - (vii) *Tightness*: a T = 4 counterexample with a cumulative control constraint, which also makes the state 2-D. The true min-max is ≈ 838.493 (an LP over the extreme points of the uncertainty set); affine policies with affine costs give ≈ 876.057 (a 4.4% gap); affine policies alone give 873.248 (pp. 25–26).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a beyond the counterexample: three LP values for (CEx) (pp. 25–26). The solver is unnamed; the AARC LP is said to be solvable with "commercially available software" (p. 6). The inventory section is interpretive, with no numerics (pp. 25–27).
- **D6 Positioning**: Lists RO groups in OR (Ben-Tal–Nemirovski; Bertsimas–Sim 2003, 2004; Bertsimas–Pachamanova–Sim 2004) and in control (pp. 1, 33–35). It credits the affine idea to stochastic programming and to Ben-Tal et al. (p. 2), earlier property results to Kerrigan–Maciejowski, and the equivalence of affine-disturbance and affine-state feedback to Goulart–Kerrigan (pp. 2–3). It claims "the first result of its kind" (p. 3). The inventory model is Ben-Tal et al.'s retailer-supplier setting (p. 25); the demand-fraction interpretation is compared with Muharremoglu–Tsitsiklis's unit decomposition (p. 27); the DP baseline goes back to Scarf et al. 1958 (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The main theorem comes early (§3) and is unpacked equation by equation into an existential and a computational result (p. 5). A proof roadmap precedes the proof, to "give some intuition behind the strategy of the proof" (p. 6). Figures 2–7 carry the geometry (pp. 8–20), and the technical lemma is moved to the appendix (p. 13). "We would like to" is the recurring voice. Claims are stated strongly: an "elegant and conceptually simple proof technique" (p. 1), and results that are "tight" (p. 3). Extensions are posed as three numbered questions, all answered negatively by one example (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions: theory (affine policies are optimal for 1-D problems), a computational corollary (an LP), and an inventory interpretation. In Proposition 5.1, each demand is at most fully met by later orders, and q_{k,t} is the fraction of demand w_t met by order q_k (pp. 26–27).
  - Limits printed: affine policies are suboptimal with cumulative control constraints or multi-dimensional states, and replacing costs by affine ones widens the gap (p. 26). Algorithm 1 needs the optimal value functions and controllers, so it is a proof device rather than a method (p. 27).
  - Future work (p. 27): mixed polyhedral state-control constraints, multi-dimensional systems, other uncertainty sets, and nonconvex or nonlinear costs; quantifying affine-policy performance where it is suboptimal, toward fast approximation algorithms; cuts for the AARC LP that mimic the affine controller; portfolio and operations applications.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To show a simple policy class loses nothing, track the 2-D image of the uncertainty (cost so far, state) under the candidate policy. If the objective has the form θ1 + convex(θ2), only the k + 1 right-side vertices of that zonogon matter (Lemma 4.1, Corollary 4.1, pp. 7–9).
- Build the policy by matching its values to the optimal policy at the worst-case vertices, and by alignment of the remaining generators, so that the image equals the zonogon hull of the worst-case points (Algorithm 1, system (37), pp. 13–14). Do the same to replace convex costs by dominating affine ones (Algorithm 2, pp. 19–20).
- When backward DP induction gives no handle on a policy class, try forward induction over time with an invariant that preserves the min-max value (pp. 6–7).
- Fence a theorem with a small computed counterexample. Compute the true min-max by an LP over the extreme points of the uncertainty set and the restricted policy's value by its own LP, and report the gap (pp. 25–26).
- Absorb state constraints into convex costs as indicator functions, to extend an optimality result without new proofs (p. 6).
- Translate the policy coefficients into the application's terms (the fraction of each demand met by each later order), to show what the theorem tells the decision-maker (Proposition 5.1, pp. 26–27).
