### S089 · Computational approaches to stochastic vehicle routing problems (Transportation Science 1995, DOI 10.1287/trsc.29.4.342)
**Read level**: full — the whole text of the 34-page MIT preprint (working paper) dated April 18, 1991 (preprint pp. 1–34, references included). The 1995 Transportation Science print was not read; every page reference in this card is a preprint page.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Philippe Chervi (Operations Research Center, MIT) and Michael Peterson (Sloan School, MIT; NSF graduate fellow) (p. 1) · **Position in the trajectory**: the computational companion to his a priori optimization line (Jaillet's PTSP, Bertsimas–Howell, Bertsimas's PVRP paper, the Bertsimas–Jaillet–Odoni survey; p. 4), NSF-funded (p. 1); the 1985–1995 SD period.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD HS AP}: two routing problems with random demand — the probabilistic TSP (each node present with probability p_i) and the 1-vehicle probabilistic VRP (random integer demands, capacity Q) — in which an a priori tour is fixed before demands are known (pp. 1–3). The entry is practical, not theoretical: re-optimization is better where practical, but it can be too costly or unwanted — a delivery firm may want drivers on the same route every day, and smaller firms lack the resources (p. 2). Four questions frame the study: how simple heuristics compare with re-optimization; at what size asymptotic behaviour emerges; what local improvement and dynamic programming add; what to tell practitioners (pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable: state the study as a short list of answerable questions and re-pose them at the start of the results (pp. 4–5, 27–28).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: (i) PTSP: build a tour by space-filling curve (SFC) or radial sort, then improve it by 2-interchange or 1-shift moves scored by closed-form expected-cost differences (pp. 5–11). SFC is chosen for its O(log n) worst-case ratio (Steele; shown tight by Bertsimas–Grigni) despite about 25% average error, hence the local improvement (p. 6); the 1-shift preserves tour structure, which Bertsimas–Howell argued pays off for small p (p. 10). (ii) PVRP: the cyclic heuristic takes a TSP tour, tries each of the n nodes as starting point and keeps the rotation of least expected cost (eq. 24, p. 18); it is refined by local improvement on that objective (p. 20) and by a DP over (node, load) that gives threshold rules for returning to the depot before the vehicle is full (pp. 21–22); the full cyclic heuristic keeps the best of 2n candidates (pp. 22–23). (iii) Benchmark: "pseudo-reoptimization" — sample scenarios, solve each sampled TSP or VRP by a strong heuristic, and use the a priori tour's exactly computable expected cost as a control variate (pp. 12, 25–26). Kept from deterministic routing: the classical move neighbourhoods; changed: the objective is the a priori expected cost.
- **D3 Model and data**: Euclidean distances only (p. 3); points uniform in the unit square (pp. 13, 24); independent node presence, homogeneous or heterogeneous (eq. 2, pp. 2, 13–14); PVRP demands independent integer random variables with known distributions and maximum K < Q (p. 3), tested with discretized normal and discrete uniform demands (p. 24); strategy a (all nodes visited) versus strategy b (zero-demand nodes skipped) (p. 3), with the theory under a visitation restriction and all testing restricted to strategy a (pp. 18, 23); local improvement only for i.i.d. demands, because return probabilities then depend only on position (p. 20); triangle inequality for the DP (p. 21). Admitted tractability choices: exact re-optimization was impossible (2^n scenarios for the PTSP; VRPs above 15 nodes intractable), so heuristic re-optimization stands in for it (pp. 12, 25–26).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: closed-form a priori expected lengths (eqs. 1, 10–11) with capacity-break probabilities from a recursion (eq. 8) (pp. 2–4); an incremental 2-interchange cost formula through precomputed arrays A and B under homogeneous p, derived from the "interactions" of the two swapped nodes with the inside and outside nodes (eq. 14, pp. 7–8), implemented in two phases that run in O(n²) on average (p. 8); heterogeneous and 1-shift analogues (eqs. 17–20, pp. 9–11; details deferred to Chervi's master's thesis, ref. [9], pp. 11, 34); the control-variate split of the re-optimization cost into a sampled residual plus an exactly computed term (eqs. 22–23, p. 12). Cited theory, not proved here: the cyclic heuristic's worst-case ratio of 2 under the visitation restriction and its asymptotic behaviour (Theorems 1–2, proofs in Bertsimas [7]), which make it asymptotically optimal when started from an optimal TSP tour (p. 19). Complexity accounting: the DP takes O(nK²) time and O(nK) space (p. 22); the full heuristic is not polynomial because local improvement is exponential in the worst case, while the DP-only version is O(n²K²) (p. 23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: a home-made C package for the Apple Macintosh (p. 13). PTSP: five random 100-node instances for each p = 0.1, …, 0.9 (45 combinations), a fixed-instance sweep over p, and five heterogeneous instances; four heuristic combinations against heuristic re-optimization over 50 sampled scenarios per instance (pp. 13–14). Stated results: all combinations except radial sort + 1-shift are close to re-optimization, "typically within 1%", and radial sort + 1-shift is up to 14% worse (p. 14); a 100-node 2-opt run takes 2–3 minutes on a Macintosh II and the 45 problems 1.5–4 hours (p. 16); heterogeneous runs took over twelve hours, so 2-opt was truncated, and the a priori heuristics beat heuristic re-optimization by "about 8%" for 1-shift (p. 17). My recomputation from Table 1 (p. 15): the three better combinations lie between −3.5% and +5.5% of the re-optimization estimate, only 9 of 27 cells are within ±1%, and in 7 of the 36 a priori cells (at p = 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and 0.6) an a priori value beats the estimate, so it is not the lower bound the method is meant to give (p. 12); Table 2 (p. 17) implies 10.1% and 12.8% for the two 1-shift columns. PVRP on a DEC/VAX 3100 workstation (pp. 23, 26): 20 random instances per (n, Q, distribution) type, n = 20–100, 100 sampled demand vectors per instance; baselines Clarke–Wright savings and the Christofides–Mingozzi–Toth clustering heuristic (pp. 26–27). Stated results: the cyclic heuristic beats clustering on every type, by about 2% to 16% (p. 28); Clarke–Wright wins below 50 nodes by "never more than 3%" (p. 28); the cyclic heuristic wins for non-identical demands and by 3% at 100 nodes (pp. 28, 30). My recomputation from Table 3 (p. 27): Clarke–Wright also wins all four identical-demand 50-node rows (by 0.2–4.5%) and the 20-node U[0,20] row by 3.2%. Ablation (Table 4, p. 31): DP gains about 4–8% (text: roughly 6%), local improvement under 1% in most rows at 2–3 times the run time (p. 31), and dropping DP costs 5–10% (p. 33); Table 3's cyclic entries for two 50-node rows equal Table 4's "no LI, DP" values rather than the full heuristic (pp. 27, 31). Asymptotics: the observed cost runs roughly parallel to 2nE[D]/Q up to n = 150 (Fig. 5, pp. 30–31), the PTSP asymptote is not reached at n = 100 (p. 16), and some confirming runs are "not shown" (p. 31). No exact optimum, bound or gap appears anywhere.
  - Note for synthesis: this 1991 software package and test protocol qualify note 06's description of 1988–1995 as "little computation".
- **D6 Positioning**: builds on Jaillet's PTSP, Bertsimas–Howell, Bertsimas's PVRP paper and the a priori optimization survey (p. 4); takes the SFC from Bartholdi–Platzman with Steele's bound and the Bertsimas–Grigni tightness result (p. 6) and the 1-shift from Bertsimas–Howell (p. 10); explains the clustering heuristic's losses by Haimovitch–Rinnooy Kan's asymptotic suboptimality of cluster-first route-second schemes (p. 28). The one feature it calls novel is the use of sampling with variance reduction to compare a priori and re-optimization strategies (p. 5). Critique of rivals is mild and mechanistic: re-optimization heuristics deteriorate when present points cluster (p. 17); Clarke–Wright suffers under widely varying demand (p. 28).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the abstract ends with a practitioner verdict (p. 1); the conclusion recasts a priori strategies as "robust" across realized demands and stresses implementability (p. 33), an early use of the robustness vocabulary in his record; mechanisms are offered as hedged guesses ("Our intuition is that", p. 16; "There is some explanation", p. 17; "quite probably due to clustering effects", p. 28); the prose summaries are more favourable than the tables (D5); the conclusion says the cyclic heuristic performs well "for the PTSP" where the PVRP is meant (p. 33).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic and empirical, with practitioner advice: SFC + 2-opt for the PTSP, the cyclic heuristic with DP return thresholds for the PVRP (p. 33). Stated limits: no exact re-optimization benchmark (pp. 12, 25–26); strategy b untested and the DP not adaptable to it (p. 23); heterogeneous 2-opt too slow and truncated (p. 17); asymptotic regime not reached at 100 nodes (p. 16). Future work: only the expectation that the method will also work for case b (p. 23).

**Method links**: Method 5 ⚠ variant — practitioner framing and recommendations (pp. 1–2, 5, 33), but synthetic instances and no owner, data or current rule; the baseline is an idealized re-optimization policy approximated by heuristics. Method 4 ⚠ variant — a duel against named incumbents (Clarke–Wright; Christofides–Mingozzi–Toth clustering) on 20 instances per type (pp. 26–28), but no exact solve, certificate or gap; the paper admits that the comparator itself is heuristic (pp. 25–26). New-pattern candidate: price of simplicity (pp. 2, 14, 17, 28, 33; S185 makes the same static-versus-dynamic comparison for polling). New-pattern candidate: control-variate pseudo-reoptimization (pp. 12, 25–26). New-pattern candidate: asymptotic optimality as the warrant for a simple heuristic, with experiments on where asymptotics emerge (pp. 4–5, 16, 19, 30–31). Also bears on Tension T2 (a heuristic engine endorsed in 1991) and Tension T6 (summaries more favourable than the tables).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Pseudo-reoptimization: estimate an intractable per-scenario-optimal benchmark by sampling scenarios, solving each with a strong heuristic, and using the a priori solution's exactly computable expected cost as a control variate (p. 12).
- Score local moves under uncertainty by incremental expected-cost differences from precomputed arrays, so that stochastic local search costs about as much as deterministic local search (pp. 7–8).
- Cyclic heuristic: try every rotation of a TSP tour as the starting point, then add a DP over (node, load) that yields threshold rules for preventive returns (pp. 18, 21–23).
- Run a 2 × 2 ablation of refinements (local improvement × DP) and report each refinement's run-time multiplier beside its gain (p. 31).
- Plot observed cost against the asymptotic formula as n grows to see where the asymptotic regime starts (pp. 15–16, 30–31).

**Quotes**:
- "Based on extensive testing we conclude that our heuristics are very close in terms of performance compared with re-optimization strategies and can be used by a practitioner." (p. 1)
- "What our results indicate is that simple a priori strategies are robust, in the sense that they perform well over a wide range of actual (realized) demands." (p. 33)

### S108 · Locating discretionary service facilities, II: maximizing market size, minimizing inconvenience (Operations Research 1995, DOI 10.1287/opre.43.4.623)
**Read level**: full — all 10 pages (file pp. 1–10 = printed pp. 623–632), references included.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Oded Berman (University of Toronto) and Richard C. Larson (MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in the trajectory**: received November 1992, accepted October 1994 (p. 1); funded by NSF DDM-9014751 and the PYI award DDM-9158118 with Draper Laboratory matching funds (p. 10); it sits with his LP-relaxation and approximation-algorithm line of about 1990–2001 [inferred from note 06, T3]; the paper does not say who did which part.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO AP}: customers buy discretionary services (petrol, cash machines, convenience stores) on preplanned trips such as the commute, so facilities should intercept path flows instead of minimizing home-to-facility distance as Hakimi-type models do (p. 1). The predecessor model (Fouska 1988; Berman–Larson–Fouska 1992, "BLF") assumed customers never leave the planned path; the entry is to relax that single behavioural assumption (p. 1). Three generalizations follow: P1 delta coverage (a detour of at most Δ from a path node), P2 maximize market size (the share willing to deviate falls with the detour), P3 minimize expected inconvenience (everyone must buy; an m-median) (pp. 1–2).
  - Transferable: generalize a predecessor's model by relaxing one behavioural assumption, then sort the resulting variants by the algorithmic structure each inherits (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: unify P1 and P2 as minimizing total customer disutility U(d) — a step function for P1, a concave increasing function for P2 (p. 2); reduce the location space to finite candidate sets (P1: the nodes plus the points at distance Δ from a node, Theorem 1; P2: the nodes, Theorem 2) (pp. 3–4), so that P1 reduces to BLF on the enlarged set (pp. 2, 4); show that the P1 and P2 objectives are nondecreasing submodular set functions, so the generic greedy inherits the Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher bound 1 − (1 − 1/m)^m, within 37% of optimal (pp. 5–7), which is tight for P1 with Δ = 0 (Theorem 4, pp. 7–8); P2 is also written as an integer program and solved by branch-and-bound (pp. 8–9). Kept from BLF: the flow-interception objective and its greedy heuristic (p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: a bidirectional urban network with path flows f_p known in advance, not decision variables (p. 2); identical facilities, so no customer stops twice (p. 2); three deviation behaviours (shortest-path detours, ordered node visits, out-and-back detours from a path node) (p. 3); for P2 a convex decreasing response g (p. 4), whose convexity is needed only for node optimality — submodularity holds without it (p. 7); in P3 customers take shortest deviation paths (p. 2). No real data: a 7-node illustrative network (p. 3), a 10-path worked example (Table I, p. 8) and random instances (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: (a) node optimality: along an arc the deviation distance is a lower envelope of linear functions of the position Q, hence piecewise-linear concave; composed with a convex decreasing g it is convex in Q, so the maximum sits at an endpoint, and reassigning paths cannot lower the objective (Theorem 2, p. 4); cases ii–iii are "proved in a similar fashion" (p. 4). (b) Submodularity by the marginal-gain inequality: for coverage sets N(S, Δ) (Prop. 1, p. 6) and, for P2, by a three-case analysis of α against D(p, S) ≥ D(p, T) (Prop. 2, p. 7); the proof twice calls g "nondecreasing" where the proposition's nonincreasing g is needed (p. 7). (c) A tight instance: a layered network A0; A1, …, Am; B1, …, Bm−2; C1, …, Cm with path weights chosen so that greedy takes A0, then B1, …, Bm−2, then one Ai, giving Z_G/Z* = 1 − (1 − 1/m)^m (Theorem 4, pp. 7–8). (d) The boundary: as an m-median, P3 cannot be approximated within a constant factor unless P = NP, and greedy can be arbitrarily bad (pp. 4–5). (e) An IP with location and assignment variables (p. 8) and a branch-and-bound with two upper bounds — UB1, the interception so far plus the largest residual gains of undetermined nodes (a minor modification of an earlier bound), and UB2, each path's best open or undetermined node, useful exactly when many variables are fixed to 0 and UB1 is weak — taking their minimum (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: greedy and branch-and-bound in C on a DEC 5810, on randomly generated networks, paths and flows with an exponential response g (p. 9). Table II: n = |P| ∈ {10, 30, 50, 100} (the text says 20–100) and m = 2–5; the greedy/B&B ratio ranges from 0.934 to 1, and B&B CPU time from 0 to 2,147 s (n = 100, m = 5) (p. 9). Conclusion: the greedy does "considerably better than its worst case bound" (p. 9). Table II is "a typical sample" of the test cases; the number of instances, the seeds and the spread are not given (p. 9). No real network data.
- **D6 Positioning**: extends Fouska and BLF and credits Hodgson (1990) for the same problem formulated independently as "flow-capturing", and Hodgson (1981) for P3 (pp. 1–2); Hakimi for node optimality of medians (p. 4); Nemhauser–Wolsey–Fisher and Nemhauser–Wolsey for the greedy bound, its tightness and the greedy's optimality within a large class of algorithms (pp. 5–7); Wolsey (1983) for the generic greedy (p. 5). It claims to improve BLF's published worst-case bound (p. 2). The tone is continuation and generalization, without polemic.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the abstract's headline is a precise guarantee, phrased as distance from the optimum ("within 37%") together with tightness (p. 1); motivation by a perceived change in shopping behaviour (p. 1); a 3-node example shows the three models choosing different optimal nodes, and P2's optimum moving with the decay rate b, before any theory (p. 5); the conclusion lists numbered main results (p. 9); proofs are terse (Theorem 1 omitted as "straightforward", p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: modelling (three generalizations), structure (node optimality, submodularity), approximation (a tight bound) and an exact method for P2 (pp. 9–10). Limits: P3 is left to m-median algorithms without a guarantee (pp. 4–5); the computation is small and synthetic (p. 9). Future work: competing facilities, and the polyhedral structure of P2 to improve the branch-and-bound (p. 10).

**Method links**: Method 1 ⚠ variant — P2 is written exactly as an IP (p. 8) and the hard boundary is printed (P3 inapproximable, pp. 4–5), but the central device is a structural property (submodularity) that certifies the greedy, and the exact method is a custom branch-and-bound rather than a model kept in a solver class. Method 4 ⚠ variant — the roles are reversed: the exact branch-and-bound audits the greedy (ratios 0.934–1, p. 9), and the greedy is endorsed with a tight guarantee (pp. 7–9); no warm start and no out-of-sample duel. New-pattern candidate: certify greedy by submodularity, then show tightness (pp. 5–8). New-pattern candidate: relax one assumption of the predecessor's model and reduce back to it (pp. 1–2, 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove that the objective is a nondecreasing submodular set function (marginal-gain inequality with a case analysis) to inherit the 1 − (1 − 1/m)^m greedy guarantee, then build a layered instance that attains it (pp. 5–8).
- Node optimality by lower envelopes: a piecewise-linear concave distance composed with a convex decreasing response is convex in position, so optima sit at endpoints and the candidate set becomes finite (p. 4).
- Before any theory, use a 3-node example to show that the competing formulations choose different optima and how the optimum moves with a model parameter (p. 5).
- In branch-and-bound, pair two upper bounds that are strong in complementary regimes (few versus many variables fixed to 0) and take their minimum (p. 9).
- Report the greedy/exact ratio and the exact CPU time for every (n, |P|, m) cell (Table II, p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "In this paper, we relax the assumption that, to be useful to a potential customer, a facility must be located at some point precisely on her preplanned travel path." (p. 1)
- "In particular, the greedy solution is always within 37% of optimal, and for one of the formulations we prove that the bound is tight." (p. 1)

### S185 · The achievable region method in the optimal control of queueing systems; formulations, bounds and policies (Queueing Systems 1995, DOI 10.1007/BF01149167)
**Read level**: full — all 53 pages (file pp. 1–53 = printed pp. 337–389). The prose was read throughout; the OCR of many formulas is garbled, so the branching-bandit parameter formulas of §2.3 and Tables 2–3 (pp. 13–18) were read for structure only; the reference list (pp. 51–53) was skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: sole author; the work reviewed is joint with Niño-Mora, Paschalidis and Tsitsiklis, Xu, Cryssikou, and Avram and Ricard (p. 4; references pp. 51–53) · **Position in the trajectory**: received April 1994, revised July 1995 (p. 1); a special-issue survey (editor Shaler Stidham, p. 51) at the peak of his SD programme, between the AAP 1994 and MOR 1996 papers it reviews (refs. [8], [6], p. 51); PYI-funded with Draper Laboratory matching funds (p. 51).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD LO IO CP}: in thirty years, the optimal control of stochastic systems has lacked a unified method of attack: each problem needs its own formulation and a somewhat ad hoc approach, and how close a proposed solution is to optimal is often unclear (p. 1). Mathematical programming, by contrast, works from a small set of formulations and a well-defined plan of attack that yields provably optimal solutions or a guaranteed degree of suboptimality (pp. 1–2). The entry is to transplant that plan: express the objective through a vector of performance measures and characterize the region of achievable performance by constraints that every policy satisfies (pp. 1, 3), for five problem classes he calls among the most important and richest in structure (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open by contrasting a field's ad hoc methods with a neighbouring field's standard plan of attack, then state the transplant in one paragraph (pp. 1–3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: optimize over achievable performance vectors, with relaxations that move progressively closer to the exact region, as valid inequalities do in integer programming (Fig. 1, p. 3). For indexable systems, work conservation laws make the region an extended (contra-)polymatroid whose vertices are the static priority rules (Theorem 1, p. 9); an adaptive greedy algorithm solves the LP and yields dual variables whose sums are generalized Gittins indices (pp. 11–12), so that Klimov's algorithm for multiclass queues and Gittins's for bandits turn out to be one algorithm (p. 19). Elsewhere: relaxation hierarchies (restless bandits, pp. 21–24; loss networks, p. 48), a convex lower bound (polling, pp. 27–28), potential-function LP relaxations (queueing networks, pp. 33–40) and new SDP-based nonlinear relaxations (pp. 4, 44–45), with heuristic policies read off the relaxations (pp. 24–25, 29–31, 48). Changed: the formulation replaces problem-specific interchange and DP arguments; kept: the classical Markovian models.
- **D3 Model and data**: finite-state Markov bandits with discounted rewards (p. 5); branching bandits under nonidling, nonpreemptive, nonanticipative policies (pp. 13–14); conservation laws as the structural assumption that makes the characterization exact (Definition 3, p. 8); restless bandits as a discounted MDP with independent initial states (p. 20); polling with Poisson arrivals, FIFO within each class and changeover times, the convex bound holding for static policies only (pp. 26–28); open networks with Poisson arrivals, exponential services and Markovian policies satisfying Assumption A, adopted "For technical reasons" (p. 33), randomization to convexify the region (p. 34), and phase-type extensions said to be easy (p. 43); loss networks with Poisson arrivals and exponential holding times (p. 47). No data: performance measures are expectations (pp. 3, 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: *Indexable systems*: conservation laws (Definition 3, p. 8) imply that the performance space is exactly an extended (contra-)polymatroid with priority-rule vertices (Theorem 1, p. 9; Klimov example with 2³ − 1 = 7 constraints and 3! = 6 vertices, pp. 6–8); the adaptive greedy algorithm (Tsoucas; Bertsimas–Niño-Mora) builds an optimal dual solution in O(n³), and the generalized Gittins indices are sums of its dual variables (pp. 11–12); indices decompose under a block condition on the matrix A (Theorem 4, p. 13). *Restless bandits*: Whittle's relaxation written as a first-order LP of size O(N|E_max|) (pp. 21–22); k-th order relaxations from MDPs on k-tuples of projects, with a conservation law obtained by counting the k-tuples that contain r active projects, monotone P¹ ⊇ … ⊇ P^N = P and polynomial for fixed k (pp. 22–24); a primal-dual index heuristic from the optimal reduced costs (pp. 24–25). *Polling*: a vacation-time decomposition plus v⁽²⁾ ≥ v² gives a convex program over a network flow with a side constraint, a lower bound for static policies (Theorem 5, pp. 27–28); closed forms by relaxing flow conservation (Theorem 6, pp. 28–29); a bound for dynamic policies (Theorem 7, p. 29); routing tables from a min–max closeness IP with connectivity relaxed and the table length bounded by N and L_max (pp. 30–31). *Networks*: the potential-function method — uniformize, write the recursion for E[R²(τ_{k+1}) | n(τ_k)], choose the f-parameters by condition (46) so that indicator terms aggregate before being bounded by 1, take steady-state expectations (Theorem 8, pp. 34–37), the proof given because it shows how potential functions build polyhedral approximations (pp. 34–35); the natural f-parameters are expected remaining processing times, which give the heavy-traffic denominator (p. 37); the nonparametric variant equates two quadratic forms in f that agree for all f (Theorem 9, pp. 38–39), an LP with O(R²) variables and constraints instead of O(2^R) constraints (p. 40), and yields a quadratic-size reformulation of the extended polymatroid, proving the polynomial-formulation conjecture for this case (Theorem 10, pp. 40–42); higher-order versions grow as O(R^i) and were generated with Maple (pp. 43–44); SDP strengthening by requiring the moment matrix Z = ∫ y y′ f(y) dy to be positive semidefinite (pp. 44–45). *Loss networks*: an exact MDP LP for one link (p. 47); first- and second-order relaxations give LP upper bounds (p. 48). *Boundary*: multiclass queueing networks are EXPTIME-hard and restless bandits PSPACE-hard (Papadimitriou–Tsitsiklis); polling is NP-hard through the TSP and, by modified proofs, PSPACE-hard; loss networks are also hard (the class name is garbled in the text); indexable systems are in P (Fig. 3, pp. 49–50).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: no new computation; the survey reports the cohort papers' results without instance counts, run times or solvers: the primal-dual heuristic and the second-order bound for restless bandits are "very close" (p. 25); in "extensive simulation results" the IP-built routing tables beat all static policies in the literature, stay within 3% of the lower bound and beat randomized policies, while dynamic policies win at low changeover times and utilization (p. 32); the network bounds are no weaker in the nonparametric version, comparable to Ou–Wein's pathwise bound, good in imbalanced traffic, asymptotically exact in some routing examples and extremely tight for closed networks (pp. 45–46); the loss-network policy stays within 0–3% of the upper bound (p. 48). The new SDP relaxations are untested (open problem 3, p. 51).
- **D6 Positioning**: credits a lineage — Coffman–Mitrani and Gelenbe–Mitrani (the multiclass M/G/1 performance space is a polyhedron), Federgruen–Groenevelt (polymatroids and the cµ rule), Shanthikumar–Yao (conservation laws imply a polymatroid), Tsoucas (Klimov's region) (pp. 3–4) — which Bertsimas–Niño-Mora generalize (p. 4; Table 3, p. 18). Whittle's restless-bandit relaxation is the first rung of the hierarchy (p. 21); Kelly's nonlinear loss-network bound is compared, the second-order relaxation being stronger under asymmetry (p. 48); the network bounds are measured against Ou–Wein (p. 46); Kumar–Kumar are credited with an independent derivation of the nonparametric method (p. 38). Earlier Gittins-index proofs are listed without criticism; the new view adds an independent algebraic proof and an algorithm as fast as the fastest known (Varaiya–Walrand–Buyukkoc) (pp. 5, 18). The frame is integer programming's sharper-formulation programme, polyhedral and SDP-based (Lovász–Schrijver) (p. 2). The tone is unification, not attack; the payoff is claimed both ways — a stochastic method proves a mathematical-programming conjecture (p. 40) — and the closing addresses both applied probabilists and mathematical optimizers (p. 51).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: a thesis-first abstract (p. 1); a field-contrast "plan of attack" opening (pp. 1–2); a two-panel analogy figure (Fig. 1, p. 3); every section runs problem → relaxation → bound → policy → reported performance; summary tables (Tables 1–3, pp. 12, 17–18); numbered implications (pp. 18–19); generic four-step recipes (pp. 46, 48–49); a limitations section with a complexity figure (pp. 48–50) and numbered open problems (p. 51).
