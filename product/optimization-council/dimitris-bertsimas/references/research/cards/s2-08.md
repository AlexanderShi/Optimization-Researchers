# Batch s2-08 · Dimitris Bertsimas · supplement (round 2)

**Batch intent**: Eight works from 2017–2019, read to see how the *modern optimization lens* behaves outside its home journals, in three settings:
- clinical prediction tools: S248 (kidney offers, Transplantation 2017) and S094 (liver allocation, American Journal of Transplantation 2019), written by one team of ORC researchers and transplant surgeons, and S190 (cancer mortality, JCO Clinical Cancer Informatics 2018), built on OCT and optimal-impute;
- decisions under competing models or rival solution methods: S249 (prostate screening under three published models, Health Care Management Science 2018), S257 (wildfire suppression, Monte Carlo tree search against MIO, Lincoln Laboratory Journal 2017) and S432 (multitarget tracking by MIO against probabilistic trackers, IEEE TAC 2018);
- methods papers of the Optimal Trees and large-scale estimation lines: H009 (trees with hyperplanes as powerful as neural networks, a manuscript submitted to JMLR in 2018) and S151 (city-scale travel times from taxi data, Operations Research 2019).

- Which incumbent is dueled, with what solver, limit and certificate, and where does it win (Method 4)?
- Is a property (interpretability, conservativeness) priced by a knob (Method 3)?
- Is a decision scored against the owner's current rule (Method 5)?

### S248 · Accept or decline? An analytics-based decision tool for kidney offer evaluation (Transplantation 2017, DOI 10.1097/TP.0000000000001824)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–7: abstract, introduction, Materials and Methods, Results, Discussion, Tables 1–4B and the figure captions. Figures 1–6 have no text layer beyond their captions. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Kung, Trichakis, Wojciechowski and Vagefi match, and Bertsimas is first and corresponding author (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jerry Kung (ORC; NSF Graduate Research Fellowship) and Nikolaos Trichakis (ORC); David Wojciechowski and Parsia A. Vagefi (Massachusetts General Hospital) (p. 1). The contribution statement credits problem definition to all five authors, development of the approach to Bertsimas, Kung and Trichakis, and implementation to Kung and Trichakis (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: the first paper of the organ-allocation line, which S094 (2019) continues with the same core team (S094 p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+AP (health); DA as the stated aim}:
  - When a deceased-donor kidney is offered, the decision to accept or decline rests on "a practitioner's experience and intuition" (abstract, p. 1).
  - Context: the waitlist nearly doubled, from about 50,000 to more than 96,000 by 2013, and up to 50% of high-KDPI kidneys are discarded (p. 1).
  - Entry: give the physician the value of waiting. The prediction problem is the probability that a patient will be offered a kidney at KDPI 0.2, 0.4 or 0.6 or better within 3, 6 or 12 months (pp. 1–2).
  - The decision itself is framed as an optimal stopping problem and left for later work (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: random forests on patient-specific registry and offer-history features, one model per KDPI threshold and horizon (p. 2). The 14 features include OPO, blood group, wait time, expected DR mismatches, offer frequency by KDPI band, mean match-run sequence number, cPRA and month (p. 2). The output feeds the physician's accept/decline judgement and serves as an "educational tool" for candidates (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Data: OPTN STAR waitlist data (April 1994–September 2013) and PTR match runs for kidneys that were eventually accepted (March 5, 2007–June 30, 2013). Of 38,806 match runs, 535 were removed; 287,283 waitlisted patients were included (p. 2).
  - Observations: 8 random dates per patient for each year on the waitlist, in the 10 most populous OPOs, with censoring rules for inactivity and for accepting a lower-quality kidney (p. 2).
  - Two data choices: offers declined by more than 50 candidates are excluded, because KDPI is imperfect; and only offers with sequence number at most 500 enter the offer-frequency features, a threshold set by validation (p. 2).
  - Admitted limits: the data contain only kidneys that were eventually accepted, so discards are missing and bias the data (p. 7); the data predate the December 2014 allocation change (pp. 5–6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a for optimization. The analysis lies in the evaluation design:
  - chronological training, validation and test windows separated by a gap equal to the label horizon (pp. 2–3);
  - the number of trees and of variables per tree chosen by validation AUC, then a refit on training plus validation before one test evaluation (p. 3);
  - rolling-horizon re-splits on 3 to 6 years of data, to estimate how much data a new allocation regime will need (pp. 3, 5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Out-of-sample AUC is 0.862–0.881 across the nine specifications (Table 1, p. 3). Calibration is checked within clusters of similar patients (p. 5), and a worked patient (J.S.) is run across five OPOs (Table 2, p. 6). AUC rises slightly and then levels off as data accrue (p. 5).
  - Baselines (p. 7): CART reaches AUC 0.814/0.809/0.792 against the forest's 0.878/0.881/0.870 (Table 3). Logistic regression reaches 0.852/0.851/0.841 (Table 4A), and its individual predictions are far off (CAOP 15.0% against an actual 3.4–3.7%, Table 4B).
  - [my reading] In Table 2, two of five predictions fall outside the actual range (TXGC 30.7% against 23.3–26.2%; CAOP 3.8% against 3.4–3.7%), though the text calls the predictions "very close" (pp. 5–6).
  - No software version or run time is given; predictions are computed "almost instantaneously" once the model is trained (p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contribution is personalized offer-probability predictions, presented as "proof of principle" for a decision aid (p. 7). Limits: discarded kidneys are missing (p. 7); random forests are "not easily interpretable", while CART loses AUC (p. 7); the new allocation rules need more data (p. 7). Future work: a dynamic optimization of accept/decline that includes the patient's condition on dialysis, and a controlled prospective assessment (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 5–6): the owner (physician and candidate) and the current rule (experience and intuition) are named, but the paper scores predictions (AUC, calibration), not decisions. The accept/decline policy and the prospective test are left for later (p. 6).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (p. 1): the paper opens the organ-allocation programme with a student (Kung) and a clinical partner (Vagefi); S094 continues it (S094 p. 1).
- Research Taste, mark 5 (interpretable where people act) · contradiction ✗ (p. 7): the random forest is chosen over CART for its AUC, and the loss of interpretability is admitted.
- New-pattern candidates: "price of simplicity" (p. 7), reversed here: CART's AUC loss is printed and the simple model is rejected. "Data-sufficiency curve for a regime change" (pp. 3, 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Separate chronological training, validation and test windows by a gap equal to the label horizon, so that no label leaks across splits (pp. 2–3).
- Refit on growing data windows to tell a partner how much data a new policy regime will need before the model can be retrained (pp. 3, 5).
- Report calibration inside clusters of similar patients, and one worked individual case, beside AUC (pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "the current experience-based paradigm lacks scientific rigor and is subject to the inaccuracies that plague anecdotal decision-making" (p. 1)
- "can be thought of as an optimal stopping problem: how long should a patient wait before accepting an offered kidney that best suits their needs?" (p. 6)

### S257 · Automated dynamic resource allocation for wildfire suppression (Lincoln Laboratory Journal 2017, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–22: introduction, the MDP and mixed-integer sidebars, the fire model, MCTS, the MIO formulation (given in prose only, p. 11), computational experiments, "Looking Forward", references, the appendix on the decision-support tool, and author biographies. Figures carry captions and plotted numbers only. First-page check: the title and the authors Griffith, Kochenderfer, Moss, Mišić, Gupta and Bertsimas match; Bertsimas is last of six (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: J. Daniel Griffith and Robert J. Moss (MIT Lincoln Laboratory); Mykel J. Kochenderfer (Stanford, formerly Lincoln Laboratory); Velibor V. Mišić (UCLA) and Vishal Gupta (USC), both ORC doctorates (p. 22). Lincoln Laboratory's Autonomous Systems Line committee supported the work (p. 18). · **Position in trajectory**: a laboratory-sponsored comparison of an MIO approach with a sampling-based planner, written with two former students; HS and IO meet SD (MDPs).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+IO+HS; AP (wildfire, defence autonomy)}:
  - Allocating suppression resources is "a subjective process", resting on commanders' decisions with insufficient information (p. 1).
  - Dynamic resource allocation (DRA) problems are cast as MDPs (p. 1). Entry: MCTS's performance on DRA "has not, until now, been empirically studied" (p. 2), and an MIO alternative is available.
  - Framing: approximate solution of an exact model (MCTS) against exact solution of an approximate model (MO, the MIO approach) (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - MCTS with double progressive widening (pp. 8–10). New actions come from a generator in the style of genetic algorithms (mutate, recombine, random), with selection weighted by Q(s,a). The rollout policy uses Floyd–Warshall reward-distance weights.
  - MO: the stochastic fire dynamics are replaced by a deterministic "smoothed" fluid model with continuous fuel and intensity (pp. 10–11). The model is a mixed-integer linear program with binary team assignments and binary fuel-sufficiency variables. It is re-solved at each step and only the first allocation is applied (model predictive control).
  - The fluid idea is credited to queueing networks (Avram, Bertsimas and Ricard) and to revenue management (Gallego and van Ryzin) (p. 10).
  - For heavily constrained cases, an LP relaxation with an index v(x) = Σ_i A(x,i) assigns the teams (p. 11) [my reading of a garbled formula].
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The MDP is a grid: each cell has a burning indicator B(x) and fuel F(x), neighbours ignite it through a sparse P(x,y), and suppression succeeds with probability Q(x), independently across attempts (pp. 4–6). Any resource is assumed to reach any cell in each step (p. 4).
  - P(x,y) is calibrated from the spread rates of the Canadian Forest Fire Behavior Prediction System (wind, slope, fuel) (p. 7).
  - The experiments use synthetic grids only (pp. 12–13):
    - grid one: k × k cells, rewards growing towards a corner fixed at −10, ignition probability 0.06, Q = 0.8;
    - grid two: exponential rewards e^{λi}, spread probability 0.02.
  - The MO's parameters are calibrated under no intervention, infinite fuel and a transmission rate of 1 (p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The MO constraints are given in words: intensity dynamics, fuel depletion, a fuel threshold, and at most one cell per team per period (p. 11). No bound links the fluid model to the MDP optimum; MCTS's only guarantee is asymptotic convergence "in the limit of samples" (p. 2). A sidebar licenses MIO by a "200-billion-factor speedup" over 25 years and promises evidence for this model (p. 3).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: a custom C++ MCTS, Gurobi Optimizer 5.0, 2.2 GHz cores with 4 GB of RAM, single-threaded (pp. 11–12). Baselines: a randomized suppression heuristic, "a naïve straw man", and the tailored FW heuristic (p. 12). Each setting is run 256 times (p. 13).
  - Scale: grids up to k = 30, with 664 burning cells on average (Table 1, p. 13). The MO is given at most 120 s per step. Most grids are solved to optimality, but a few iterations take much longer to find a feasible solution (pp. 13–14).
  - Results:
    - the MO beats MCTS on tail-hard instances, and additive-effects models favour the MO (p. 14);
    - at k = 10, ANOVA finds the MO–MCTS difference not significant (p. 16);
    - at k = 20, the MO beats FW for all team sizes, while MCTS is worse than FW with 16 or 32 teams (p. 17);
    - in grid two, the MO has "a marked edge" for small λ (p. 18).
  - A failure is printed: the MO sometimes needs hundreds of seconds to return a feasible solution (Fig. 10, p. 16).
  - [my reading] The Fig. 9 caption says the MO "always performs better than MCTS", while the text reports the k = 10 difference as not significant (p. 16).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the first application of MCTS to a DRA problem motivated by a real application, a fluid MIO formulation, and the comparison (p. 3). Limits and future work (p. 18):
  - calibrate with real wildfire data and compare with experienced decision makers;
  - MCTS's hyperparameters are poorly understood;
  - the MO is "only one way" to approximate the MDP;
  - a hybrid of the MO and MCTS.

  A prototype decision-support tool is built on NASA World Wind (appendix, pp. 19–21).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 13–16): the *why now* is a speed-up figure, 200 billion over 25 years (p. 3). SKILL Method 2 dates this figure to the 2014 Morse lecture and arXiv v1; it is not rebuilt for this 2017 paper. The paper does add measured solve times for its own model (pp. 13–14, 16).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 10–11): the MDP, a problem of the dynamic-programming tradition, enters the mixed-integer linear class only through a deterministic fluid approximation, "the exact solution to an approximate model".
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 11–17): the solver version, hardware, threads and time limits are stated, and runs that hit the limit are said to be typically not optimal (p. 14). Two baselines and statistical tests are reported. No heuristic warm-starts the MIO.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 18): the commanders' current practice is named but is not a baseline. A comparison with experienced decision makers is future work.
- New-pattern candidates: "deterministic fluid model first, randomness second" (p. 10); "exact model, approximate solution vs approximate model, exact solution" (pp. 2–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Frame a rival comparison as approximate solution of the exact model against exact solution of an approximate model, and test where each wins as the state and action spaces grow, at a fixed per-step budget (pp. 2–3, 13–17).
- Replace stochastic dynamics by a deterministic fluid version, solve it as a mixed-integer program, apply only the first decision, and re-solve (pp. 10–11).
- Name the naive baseline a straw man and add a second, tailored heuristic, so that gains are read against both (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "MCTS is attempting to approximate the solution to an exact model, whereas MO is attempting to find the exact solution to an approximate model" (p. 2)
- "Over the last 25 years, a 200-billion-factor speedup in these codes has resulted from algorithmic advances and improved computer hardware." (p. 3)

### H009 · Optimal classification and regression trees with hyperplanes are as powerful as classification and regression neural networks (manuscript submitted to the Journal of Machine Learning Research 2018, no DOI)
Read level: **partial**. Read:
- pp. 1–8: abstract, introduction, contributions, Tables 1–2 and the feedforward-network definition;
- pp. 10–19: the OCT-H definition (§2.4) and Theorems 1 and 2 with their proofs;
- the statements of Theorems 3–6 (pp. 23, 25, 30, 37);
- pp. 42–52: Theorem 7 and its proof, the §7 experiments (Tables 3–4), the conclusion and the references.

Not read beyond the theorem statements: the CNN and RNN definitions and constructions (§§2.2–2.3, 4–5; pp. 8–10, 22–42). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Mazumder and Sobiesk match. The file is dated April 1, 2018, in a JMLR template with the placeholder header "Submitted ?/17; Published ?/17" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Rahul Mazumder (Sloan) and Matthew Sobiesk (ORC) (p. 1); Jack Dunn is thanked (p. 50). · **Position in trajectory**: a theory companion to the Optimal Trees programme (SKILL SW4). The work list records it as a homepage entry, submitted to JMLR in 2018.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO through the trees}: Neural networks rely on training heuristics such as dropout and early stopping; it is unclear when and why they work; and with thousands of parameters they are not interpretable (pp. 2–3). Trees are interpretable, and OCT and OCT-H improve on CART (p. 3). Question: is OCT-H as powerful as neural networks? (abstract, p. 1; p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: constructive emulation in both directions:
  - a perceptron feedforward network with N1 first-layer nodes becomes a tree of depth N1 (Theorem 1, pp. 12–14). Each first-layer node becomes the split at one depth, the 2^{N1} leaves enumerate the first layer's binary outputs, and a forward pass computes each leaf's label;
  - a ReLU network becomes a tree of depth q − 1 + Σ N_ℓ (Theorem 2, pp. 16–19). On each branch the layer outputs are explicit linear functions of x, so the next layer's sign tests are hyperplanes; q − 1 levels of pairwise comparisons resolve the argmax output;
  - analogues for CNNs with max pooling and for one-layer RNNs (Theorems 3–6, pp. 23, 25, 30, 37), and for regression (pp. 15, 22);
  - the converse: an OCT-H with N1 splits and N2 leaves becomes a perceptron network with two hidden layers of N1 and N2 nodes, using weights 1/N_r and −2 and offset biases (Theorem 7, pp. 42–46). Hence the two are equivalent for classification (p. 47).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - All the theorems concern training-set accuracy (Theorems 1–7). Activations are perceptron or ReLU, and the RNNs have one hidden layer (Table 1, pp. 4–5).
  - The tree side assumes an optimal OCT-H of the stated depth: the optimal tree wins "by the optimality of the final OCT-H" (p. 15).
  - Experiments use seven data sets, the Framingham teaching data and six UCI sets, with 210 to 245,057 points (Table 3, p. 48).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The proofs are constructive (pp. 12, 42). The hinge is optimality: the constructed tree is feasible for the OCT-H problem, so the optimal tree is at least as accurate on the training set (p. 14). Depth grows linearly with the node counts, so the trees can have 2^{depth} leaves; the paper admits that its constructions require deep trees (p. 50).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup (p. 47): OCT and OCT-H are trained and cross-validated with the Optimal Tree software. The networks are built in TensorFlow with sigmoid activations, "a continuous approximation of the perceptron activation functions". They are sized from Theorem 7 (N1 = splits and N2 = leaves of the maximal tree), with 50 random starts and a grid over step sizes and regularization.
  - Results: accuracy is very similar on six of seven sets. On Letter Recognition, OCT-H reaches 80.3%, against 67.0% for the best network and 68.2% for OCT (pp. 49–50).
  - No run times, solver settings, gaps or repeated splits are reported [my reading].
  - [my reading] The contributions list says six data sets (p. 6), while §7 uses seven (p. 47). The experiments test sigmoid networks, which the theorems do not cover (p. 47).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions (pp. 6–7): equivalence in modeling power; OCT-H "might be the method of choice" where interpretability matters; cross-pollination, with stochastic gradient methods for trees and MIO and local search for networks. Limits: the deep trees "may be impractical to compute", and "more empirical research is needed" (p. 50).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 11, 14–15, 47): the theorem needs a globally optimal OCT-H. The experiments use the Optimal Tree software, which the paper itself calls near optimal (MIO and local search), and report no certificate (Tension T1). The duel at matched complexity, with the network sized from the tree by Theorem 7, does follow step 5 (p. 47).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 7, 48–50): interpretability is priced by a theorem ("without loss of modeling power", p. 7) and by an accuracy table across depths (pp. 48–49), not by a knob–bound curve. The paper admits that the depth the theorem needs is impractical (p. 50).
- New-pattern candidates: "subsume the incumbent as a special case" (every network is emulated by an OCT-H, pp. 12–19); "two-way constructive emulation to claim parity between model classes" (pp. 12–19, 42–47).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To argue that a new model class loses nothing against a dominant rival, build the rival inside your class constructively (training-set equivalence), prove the converse, and report the size blow-up openly (pp. 12–19, 42–47, 50).
- Use the converse construction to size the rival in the experiments (splits become first-layer nodes, leaves become second-layer nodes), so that the duel is at matched complexity (p. 47).

**Quotes**:
- "Since an OCT-H does at least as well as T1 in classifying the training data, it must do at least as well as N1 too." (p. 14)
- "While our constructions require deep trees that may be impractical to compute, we have also found that in seven data sets that the modeling power of OCT-Hs and FNNs is indeed very similar even if the trees have small depth." (p. 50)

### S190 · Applied informatics decision support tool for mortality predictions in patients with cancer (JCO Clinical Cancer Informatics 2018, DOI 10.1200/CCI.18.00003)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–11: abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, Tables 1–2, figure captions, author contributions, disclosures and references. The tree in Fig. 2 and the curves in Figs. 3–4 come through only as scattered labels. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Dunn, Pawlowski, Silberholz, Weinstein, Zhuo, Chen and Elfiky match. Bertsimas is first author; Elfiky (Dana-Farber) is the corresponding author (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jack Dunn, Colin Pawlowski, John Silberholz, Alexander Weinstein and Ying Daisy Zhuo (MIT); Eddy Chen (MGH Cancer Center); Aymen A. Elfiky (Dana-Farber and Brigham and Women's) (p. 10). Conception and design are credited to Bertsimas, Weinstein and Elfiky, and financial support to Elfiky (p. 10). · **Position in trajectory**: the group's own two methods, OCT and optimal-impute, carried into an oncology informatics journal with a public tool.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+AP (health); IO inside the methods}:
  - Oncologists rely on "intuition-based assessment" of treatment risk and mortality (abstract, p. 1). Physicians overestimate prognosis, and chemotherapy without clinical benefit is named the most pervasive and superfluous practice in oncology (p. 1).
  - Prior tools used small cohorts or SEER registry data at diagnosis, with AUC 0.71–0.87 (p. 1; Table 1, pp. 3–4). Black-box models would not suit interactive tools (p. 2).
  - Problem: mortality at 60, 90 and 180 days from the start of an anticancer regimen (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: OCT, chosen for interpretability without giving up accuracy (p. 4). Missing values are imputed by optimal-impute, which "frames the imputation task as a family of optimization problems" (p. 2). The trained tree becomes an adaptive web questionnaire whose questions are the tree's splits (www.oncomortality.com) (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - EHR data of the Dana-Farber/Brigham and Women's Cancer Center for 2004–2014, linked to Social Security death records; adults who received at least one anticancer treatment; a record is censored for an outcome when the date of death is missing and the last known date alive falls before that outcome's cutoff (p. 2).
  - 401 predictors, 289 of them gene-mutation variables (p. 8).
  - Treatment lines from 2004–2011 train and those from 2012–2014 validate (pp. 2, 4); parameters are tuned on "a separate holdout set" (p. 4).
  - 23,983 patients started 46,646 treatment lines; 5.6% of lines were followed by death within 60 days (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a in this paper. OCT and optimal-impute are cited (refs. 29–30, p. 11); no solver, depth, time limit or certificate is reported [my reading].

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO (robustness across models)+HS (local search)+AP (cancer screening); DA}:
  - Health decisions are informed by models whose assumptions are "largely unverifiable". - **D2 Core idea**: For a strategy s and models m_1, …, m_K, take the average A(s) and the pessimistic P(s) = min_k m_k(s). The construction is analogous to α-maxmin expected utility (pp. 4, 8).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - A systematic search found three eligible models (p. 4; Table 2, p. 5): 547 PubMed and 75 registry citations, 36 full texts, 3 models. G is a CISNET microsimulation; U and Z are discrete-time Markov models. A single reviewer screened the citations (p. 3).

- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:

  - Certificate: all 5.2 million biennial and 5.2 million annual strategies were enumerated for models Z and U (60.1 and 369.1 CPU hours). The local search had found the global optimum for both. No such check was run for G or for the frontier objectives (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Single-model optima gain 0.5, 4.8 and 6.7 months of QALE under G, U and Z. U's best strategy is worse than no screening under G, by about 6 days (p. 4).
  - The frontier has 64 strategies, 57 of them biennial (pp. 1, 6). None of the 22 expert-generated strategies lies on it: each is beaten by at least 0.5 months on the average at equal pessimism, and by at least 0.05 months on the pessimistic assessment at equal average (p. 4).

- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: optimization over millions of strategies instead of hundreds, and a frontier that gives decision makers "tunable control" between average and pessimistic performance (p. 8). Limits: the example "cannot inform public health decision making"; the main obstacle is finding validated models, so the method suits established modeling consortia (pp. 8–9). Extensions: ratios, rank-based aggregations, multi-criteria frontier surfaces, and aleatory uncertainty (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 8, 11–12): one knob, λ, gives a price curve between average and pessimistic performance that is handed to decision makers (p. 8). The bad case is printed: normalization changes the frontier (pp. 8, 12). Variant aspect: there is no solution-independent bound.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When several credible models disagree, optimize a convex combination of the average and the worst model's assessment over a grid of weights, and build the frontier from all solutions encountered (pp. 3–4, 11).

[my reading] The prose describes ρ as the share of pairs within the proximity threshold, the formula as the share outside it; the formula agrees with the paper's statement that higher values of ρ indicate easier scenarios (p. 8).

  - Admitted:
    - in hard scenarios the MIO, despite having the lowest objective, may overfit (p. 10);

  - [my reading] The false-alarm rates are printed as {0.1, 0.5, 0.1, 2.0} (p. 11), and a case with γ = 0.8 appears outside the tested range (p. 13).

- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 5–6, 15): a problem owned by Bayesian filtering is written as an MIO, kept linear by the choice of the ℓ1 norm. The boundary is printed: the joint unknown-P model is not tractable, so fixed-P models run in parallel.

- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 8–14): the protocol is followed: warm starts from fast heuristics; the solver, core count and time limit are stated; the duel is against GNN and JPDAF given full probabilistic knowledge; and the regimes where the incumbent wins are printed. - Give the probabilistic incumbents full knowledge of the noise model, so that any win by the assumption-free method is conservative (p. 8).

- **D2 Core idea**:

  - Tractable algorithm (eqs. 8–12, p. 7): fix each OD pair's path at the previous iteration's shortest path; replace the log loss by a convex max-ratio loss; use absolute values, so that each iteration is an SOCP. Then recompute the shortest paths, and stop when the mean path difference falls below δ = 0.5 (p. 8).

- **D3 Model and data**:

  - Synthetic data: a 192-node toy city and a 400-node grid, with lognormal noise (pp. 9–11).
  - Real data: the Manhattan OpenStreetMap graph (4,324 nodes, 9,518 arcs) and weekday taxi trips of May 2016 after outlier filters, about 415,000 for training and 275,000 for testing (p. 12). The set of OD pairs is capped at 100,000 (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:

  - The paper says the iteration "does not necessarily converge to the global optimum" (p. 6) and "empirically converges" (p. 7). The exact MIO is out of reach beyond about 1,000 data points (p. 6).
  - A floor on achievable error: the input log variance, estimated by comparing each trip with its k nearest trips (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:

  - [my reading] Table 2 gives 0.31 for the same morning window (p. 15), while the text gives just over 0.36 (p. 13). **Method links**:

- New-pattern candidates: "constraint generation as the scaling engine" (path generation, p. 6); "inner-easy/outer-hard decomposition" (fix the paths, solve an SOCP, recompute the paths, pp. 7–8); "restart agreement as a soft certificate" (pp. 14–15); "noise floor as the yardstick for an estimator" (p. 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Estimate the irreducible noise of the data, by comparing each observation with its k nearest neighbours, and judge the estimator by its distance from that floor (p. 13).
- Turn a min-over-paths constraint into linear constraints by fixing the combinatorial choice at the previous iterate's shortest path, recompute the choice, and stop on the mean change of the choice (pp. 7–8).
- Replace a nonconvex squared log-ratio loss by the convex (max(T/T̂, T̂/T) − 1)², which agrees with it to second order and treats over- and under-estimation symmetrically, and then by its absolute-value version, which is an SOCP (p. 7).
- Duel against the data-driven incumbent across data densities to show where each method wins (Table 3, p. 17).
