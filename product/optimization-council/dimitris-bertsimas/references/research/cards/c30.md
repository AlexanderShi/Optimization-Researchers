### S307 · Decarbonizing OCP (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management 2025, DOI 10.1287/msom.2022.0467; arXiv 2209.06341)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v2 (arXiv:2209.06341v2, 16 Jun 2023, p. 1). The main text (pp. 1–29) and the e-companion EC.1–EC.6 (file pp. 33–43) were read in full, including Tables EC.1–EC.3. The references (pp. 30–32) were skimmed. Figures 1–6 and EC.1–EC.8 are captions only in the text file. The MSOM print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ryan Cory-Wright (Imperial College Business School; IBM Thomas J. Watson Research Center) and Vassilis Digalakis Jr (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: an applied RO paper in energy from the 2019–2026 period, matching the skill's Latest note that RO continues in energy and insurance. It is a second paper with the OCP Group, after Becker et al. (2022) on OCP's operations, of which Bertsimas is a coauthor (cited p. 2, reference p. 30; S339 in the index, no open text). The arXiv identifier dates from September 2022; v2 is from June 2023 (p. 1). Cory-Wright is a former student in the skill's ML lineage, and Digalakis is also first coauthor of S317 in this batch.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + RO; DA for the data-driven set and the ML scenario reduction; LO as the engine; CP for the DRO layer}:
  - The abstract presents a collaboration with the OCP Group, a phosphate and fertilizer producer, in support of a green initiative. The problem is to decarbonize OCP's electricity supply by installing solar panels and batteries, minimizing time-discounted investment cost plus the cost of meeting the remaining demand from the Moroccan grid (p. 1).
  - The entry is the partner's pledge: full decarbonization of production with solar and batteries by 2027, and carbon neutrality by 2040. In 2020, 57% of OCP's energy needs were met from non-renewable sources (p. 2).
  - A second, framing entry: decarbonization is often seen as a luxury that developing economies cannot afford. The paper sets out to show by example that it can be profitable, "at least sometimes" (pp. 2–3).
  - The scope is narrowed at once: other emission sources are not addressed, so OCP's electricity supply is decarbonized, not OCP (p. 2).
  - The modelling perspective is a vertically integrated generator–consumer, or "prosumer", not a generator or a regulator. The model was designed with that prosumer, which the paper calls "arguably more physically realistic" (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - One multi-period LP co-optimizes yearly strategic decisions (kWh of batteries and kW of solar per node and year) and hourly operations (line flows, storage, discharge, purchases from two providers, sales). Two time scales are needed because battery value depends on hour-by-hour dynamics while assets degrade over years (pp. 9–12).
  - Tractability comes from scenario reduction: the 365 days of each year become about |D| = 10 typical days by k-means, with month-specific weights (pp. 8, 10, 15–16).
  - Two robustness layers sit on this SAA model. RO protects against errors in the daily solar forecast, through a data-driven uncertainty set imposed on aggregated constraints. DRO, with a KL ambiguity set on the scenario weights, protects against climate-driven drift (pp. 8, 16–23).
  - The choice between RO and DRO follows the amount of data behind each uncertainty source: much historical data for daily solar, little for seasonal climate shifts (p. 8).
  - A real-time LP, re-solved during the day with past decisions fixed, turns the plan into hourly operations (p. 14; EC.2, pp. 33–34).
  - Against classic capacity expansion (screening curves; stochastic or adaptive models for a generator or a regulator), the prosumer view and the two-layer robustification change. The LP core stays, and the paper says robustification does not change the SAA problem's tractability (pp. 5, 17).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Network and horizon: five nodes (Jorf, Safi, Benguerir, Youssoufia, Khouribga), 24 hours, 10 reduced scenarios, 12 months and 20 years (p. 11). Solar is allowed only at mining sites, batteries at all sites, and purchases from Nareva only at mining sites (p. 4).
  - Stated parameters: discount factor 0.96; solar degradation 0.995 and battery degradation 0.96 per year; hourly storage retention 0.997; transmission efficiency 0.99 (p. 11). Sales are capped at 20% of local production, for legal reasons and so that providers do not see OCP as a competitor (pp. 11, 13).
  - Data: one year of hourly solar capacity factors at three mining sites, simulated by OCP engineers with PVsyst 7.2 from several years of site data. It is extended to 20 years with published North African forecasts and a conservative worst-case total degradation of 1.5% (p. 15). Demand comes from OCP's sales forecasts and costs from manufacturer data (p. 24). On p. 8 the RO set is said to rest on "considerable amounts of historical data"; pp. 14–15 say this is one simulated year, "all OCP currently has access to".
  - Most parameter values are withheld as commercially sensitive (pp. 9, 11), and both the data and the code are withheld from the paper and its supplement (p. 24).
  - Simplifications the paper admits:
    - investments only at the start of each year (p. 9);
    - storage at hour 24 feeds hour 1 of the same scenario, so no load moves across days (p. 13), checked in EC.5 (pp. 40–41);
    - scenario weights vary by month but not by year, because one year of data cannot estimate annual deviations (p. 16);
    - in the SAA model the daily forecast is exact from the start of each day (p. 16).
  - Load shedding is allowed through an inequality and assumed rarely profitable (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - SAA LP (3a)–(3g) (p. 12, explained on pp. 12–13). The objective is discounted investment net of salvage, through ((ρ)^y − (ρ)^|Y|) factors, plus line rental and purchases minus sales. The constraints are the budget; Kirchhoff's law with losses, τ_a(f) = η_a f⁺ − f⁻; the daily sales cap; and the battery recursion.
  - Scenario reduction: k-means centroids, then month weights P^{d,m} from the type-1 Wasserstein LP (4) (pp. 15–16). The claim that clustering barely changes the investment rests on Wang et al. (2022), not on a proof (p. 15).
  - Data-driven uncertainty set (6) (pp. 18–20). It has three constraint families, each read off the exploratory figures (Figures 2–3, pp. 18–19):
    - a per-hour bound U_MAX = γmax × the largest within-cluster deviation;
    - an hour-to-hour smoothness bound U_SV = γC × the largest within-cluster change;
    - a CLT budget U_CLT = γCLT × √(|N||Y||M||H|) × σ^d.
  - Constraint aggregation (pp. 20–21, citing Roos and den Hertog 2020). Imposing the set constraint by constraint would set every capacity factor at its minimum and ignore the smoothness and CLT parts. So the demand constraints, and separately the sales constraints, are aggregated over nodes, hours and years for each scenario and month.
  - Lemma 1 dualizes the aggregated inner LP by strong LP duality into the linear counterpart (8); the same machinery gives (10) (pp. 21–22; proof EC.1, p. 33). The result is still an LP, and its size is counted: 2·|D|·(5·|N|·|H|·|M|·|Y| + 1) new variables and 2·|N|·|H|·|D|·|M|·|Y| + 2|D|·|M| new constraints (p. 22).
  - By the paper's own definition, the robust model's feasible set Z is (3b), (3e)–(3g), (8) and (10) (p. 23), and (3c)–(3d) are dropped (p. 22). Node-hour balance and the daily sales cap then hold only in aggregated robust form; the paper does not discuss what this does to hourly feasibility. The real-time LP keeps node-hour balance (p. 33).
  - DRO (12): the worst case over scenario weights within KL divergence δ of the empirical ones, for each month and year (p. 23). Lemma 2 turns it into dual exponential-cone constraints by strong conic duality, "essentially due to Kocuk (2020, Theorem 1)". The paper gives no proof (EC.1 proves only Lemma 1, p. 33).
  - The added size is counted: 2·|M|·|Y| + 2·|D|·|M|·|Y| variables and |D|·|M|·|Y| cones (p. 23). The DRO choice is justified from Van Parys et al. (2021), on out-of-sample disappointment, and Anderson and Philpott (2022), on small samples (p. 23).
  - Real-time LP (EC.3) with O(T(|N| + |A|)) variables (pp. 14, 33–34). End-of-day storage is set equal to the start of the day; Grinold-type end-effect corrections are named but not implemented (p. 34).
  - The NPV is measured against the no-investment operating cost, salvage excluded (p. 34).
  - There are no theorems beyond the two reformulation lemmas, and no complexity or approximation bounds.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Solver, version, machine and solve times are not reported. The text says only that the RO model "can thus be solved at scale" (p. 22) and that the DRO model suits "state-of-the-art conic solvers such as Mosek" (p. 23).
  - Tuning (p. 35):
    - within each month, the days are split at random 20/4/4 into training, validation and test;
    - the grid is (γmax, γC, γCLT, δ) ∈ {0, 1}³ × {0, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1.0}, with 10 reduced scenarios and the budget fixed at 20 billion MAD;
    - the whole process is repeated ten times.
  - Selection by cost and selection by emissions disagree (δ = 0.001 against δ = 1.0, p. 37). The final values (0.5, 0.5, 0.5, 0.01) combine cross-validation with discussions with OCP, and the three γ values lie off the {0, 1} grid (pp. 24, 39).
  - Gains over SAA (Tables EC.1–EC.3, pp. 37–38). At δ = 0.001 operating cost improves by 16.26% on validation, 16.51% on test and 16.92% on a perturbed test set, and CO2 by 2.55–3.47% across the RO settings and the three data sets. The chosen model saves an additional 0.4 billion MAD and cuts CO2 a further 3.5% (p. 39).
  - The perturbed test set multiplies each value by [1 + N(µ, (µ/10)²)] with µ drawn uniformly from [−0.25, 0.25] (p. 38).
  - In those tables, every RO setting except (1, 1, ·) gives the same numbers as no RO when δ ≤ 0.1, and 0.0 when δ = 0. RO adds about 0.8 points only at δ = 1.0 (pp. 37–38). The 16% headline is therefore carried by the DRO layer, while the text credits RO with "a small yet non-negligible improvement" (p. 39).
  - Budget curve against the no-investment baseline, which buys locally from the grid at an operating cost of 18 billion MAD on the test set (p. 25). Budgets B run over {0, 2.5, …, 50} billion MAD:
    - 10 billion gives a 70% emission cut and an NPV of 5 billion;
    - 20 billion gives 95% and an NPV of 2 billion;
    - 30 billion gives 97% and an NPV of −2.5 billion;
    - the NPV peaks near 7.5 billion (p. 25; EC.3, p. 34).
  - Policy reading:
    - investment is front-loaded in years 1–5, with a spike in year 6, when a cogeneration facility goes offline (p. 26);
    - solar comes first, and the battery share grows with the budget (pp. 26–27);
    - grid purchases fall in cheap late-evening hours, which the paper reads as load shifting (p. 27).
  - Robustness checks: 3–50 scenarios (p. 40) and 48- and 72-hour scenario sequences (pp. 40–41) both give negligible changes.
  - Failures printed: over-conservative settings worsen cost by about 4.7% against SAA (p. 37), and full decarbonization is unprofitable (pp. 25, 28).
  - Not scored: the pure-solar strategy that OCP was contemplating before the collaboration (p. 29).

The standard critique of RO is stated in its own terms: a suboptimal stochastic solution may do better on average (pp. 6–7). DRO (Delage–Ye 2010; Wiesemann et al. 2014; Van Parys et al. 2021) is presented as the remedy (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Choose the robustness device per uncertainty source by the data you have. Use RO where many observations shape a set (daily forecast errors) and DRO over scenario weights where data are thin (climate drift) (p. 8).
- (D3) Build the uncertainty set from an exploratory look at forecast errors. Write one constraint per observed regularity (bounded per hour, smooth from hour to hour, CLT in aggregate) and size each as a fraction γ of an empirical maximum or σ; then cross-validate γ (pp. 18–20, 35).
- (D4) When an uncertain parameter enters many constraints, aggregate them before robustifying, so that the adversary cannot set every entry to its worst value at once. Then dualize the aggregated inner LP (pp. 20–22, 33). Say what the aggregation drops from the nominal feasible set.
- (D4) Reduce days to typical days by k-means, then fit month-specific scenario weights with a Wasserstein transport LP, so that a single set of scenarios serves every month (pp. 10, 16).
- (D4) Put a KL ambiguity set on scenario weights and reformulate it with exponential cones, and report how many variables and cones it adds (p. 23).
- (D5) Cross-validate robustness parameters like ML hyperparameters. Split days within each month into training, validation and test sets; repeat ten times; report the mean and sd of the improvement over SAA on validation, on test and on a perturbed test set (pp. 35–38).
- (D5) Check a tractability simplification by relaxing it partially in an appendix: 48- and 72-hour scenario sequences for storage across days, and 3–50 scenarios for the reduction (pp. 13–14, 40–41).
- (D5) Sweep the owner's budget, report cost, emissions and NPV together with the point where NPV turns down, and hand the choice back to the owner (pp. 25, 34).

### S317 · Slowly varying regression under sparsity (Operations Research 2025, DOI 10.1287/opre.2022.0330; arXiv 2102.10773)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v5 (arXiv:2102.10773v5, 11 Nov 2023, p. 1).
- The main text (pp. 1–28) was read in full, and Appendices B–F (pp. 39–51) in full with all tables. The references (pp. 28–31) were skimmed.
- The Appendix A proofs (pp. 32–38) were followed but not re-derived line by line. The exceptions are Lemma 7 and Proposition 1, which I checked numerically (D4).
- Figures 1–5 are captions and axis labels only in the text file. The OR print was not read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vassilis Digalakis Jr (HEC Paris; MIT Operations Research Center), Michael Lingzhi Li (Harvard Business School) and Omar Skali Lami (McKinsey & Company; a footnote says he took part in discussions of an early version) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**:
- It belongs to the MIO-for-statistics line: S010 (2016; card in c16), then S061 (Bertsimas–Van Parys 2020, exact cutting planes for sparse regression), then S175 (Bertsimas–Cory-Wright–Pauphilet 2021, logical constraints). The paper places itself between S061 and S175 (p. 8).
- It cites his 1999 paper with Gamarnik and Tsitsiklis as early slowly varying work (pp. 5, 29; D006/S323 in the index).
- The arXiv identifier dates from February 2021; v5 is from November 2023. Its student coauthor is the same as in S307.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO; CP for the binary convex reformulation}:
  - Many regression problems are sparse and vary slowly and sparsely across time or space: building energy by hour of day, house prices by neighbourhood (p. 2).
  - A modeller choosing between one global model and separate per-vertex models loses either the dependence across vertices or interpretability, and separate models need enough data at each vertex (p. 2).
  - The problem: T regressions on a similarity graph, with least squares, a ridge term λβ and an ℓ2 fusion term λδ on edges. Three cardinality constraints apply: local sparsity KL per vertex, global sparsity KG over the union of supports, and KC total support changes across edges (eqs. (1)–(4), p. 3).
  - The entry is a belief: the exact formulation "is generally considered infeasible beyond toy scales" (D ≤ 10², T ≤ 10), hence relaxations such as the fused lasso and sum-of-norms (pp. 3–4). Exact sparse formulations had long been thought unscalable, a paradigm the group's recent work challenged (p. 7).
  - A second, methodological entry: the group's own convex binary reformulation of sparse ridge regression (Bertsimas–Van Parys 2020) "seems to be quite fortuitous". It offers no systematic reason and few hints for extension (pp. 7–8), and the paper sets out to find its key ingredients (p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Encode supports by binaries z (with s for the union and w for changes), and write every coefficient as Zβ, in the ridge and fusion terms as well (pp. 8–9, eq. (10)).
  - The inner least-squares problem then has a rank-deficient Hessian. Its minimizer is a Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse, and the resulting objective in z is neither convex nor differentiable (Lemma 2, p. 10).
  - The key insight: only binary z matter. So (Z(M + λβI)Z)†Z is replaced by (λβI + ZM)⁻¹Z, which coincides with it on binary points (Proposition 1, p. 11) and makes the objective convex in z (Theorem 1, p. 11).
  - The more natural candidate, (λβI + ZMZ)⁻¹Z, also coincides on binary points but is nonconvex (eq. (20), p. 11). A one-dimensional picture with m = 19, µ = 1 and λβ = 1 shows the difference (Figure 2, p. 12).
  - Around this core the paper builds:
    - outer approximation (Duran–Grossmann) with closed-form gradients (Algorithm 1; Lemma 3, pp. 13–14);
    - a polynomial-time heuristic, derived from a separable upper bound, for warm starts and for tuning (Algorithm 2; Lemma 5, Proposition 2, pp. 15–17);
    - bisection on the sparsity levels for tuning (Algorithms 3–4, pp. 17–20).
  - Against Bertsimas–Van Parys 2020, Zβ now appears in all terms, M is coupled through the graph, and there are three constraint families. The ridge-induced convexity and the cutting planes stay (pp. 7–11).
  - The device extends to any sparse convex quadratic ½xᵀ(M + λI)x − µᵀx (eq. (9), p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - λβ > 0 is needed for the identity and for convexity (Proposition 1 and Theorem 1, p. 11), and M must be PSD (Lemma 1, p. 10; proof p. 32).
  - Every vertex has the same number N of cases (p. 2). On real data this forces subsampling: N = 2,922 per month and N = 352 per neighbourhood group (pp. 45, 47).
  - The graph is given: chains for time, and adjacency or distance thresholds for space (pp. 44–48). Consistency conditions tie KL, KG and KC (p. 3).
  - The tuning guarantee assumes i.i.d. rows with finite second moments, a correctly specified linear model and N, N_V → ∞ (Proposition 3, pp. 18–19). It covers only the three sparsity levels; λβ and λδ are grid-searched (pp. 19–20).
  - Synthetic data (pp. 40–42):
    - an Erdős–Rényi similarity graph with dG(T − 1)log T/2 edges;
    - coefficients drawn from a normal truncated to [0.5, 1.5] times random signs, or pure binary in 5 of 26 settings, perturbed by up to σv between similar vertices, with support changes made by swapping features;
    - Toeplitz feature correlation ρd^|i−j| and Gaussian noise at SNR ξ;
    - defaults N = 3000, T = 10, D = 200, KL = 5, KG = 15, KC = 20, σv = 0.33, dG = 3, ρd = 0.9, ξ = 2.
  - Real data (pp. 25–27, 43–48): UCI appliances energy, by hour (T = 24) and by month (T = 5); Ames housing (T = 7); Beijing air quality (T = 12); and hourly weather in 36 cities (T = 36).
  - Arbitrary choices the paper admits: the hourly split (pp. 44–45), and the half-day horizon, the 1,000 km threshold and the ten lags (p. 48).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - MIO (10)–(16) (pp. 8–9): binaries z^t_d, s_d ≥ z^t_d, and w^{t,s}_d ≥ |z^t_d − z^s_d| written as two linear inequalities. No big-M constants are needed, because supports enter through Z.
  - Lemma 1 rewrites the problem as ½βᵀZ(M + λβI)Zβ − µᵀZβ, with M built from per-vertex Gram matrices plus a graph term on the diagonal and edges (p. 10; PSD proof p. 32).
  - Lemma 2 obtains the inner minimizer from the first-order condition and the pseudo-inverse (pp. 10, 32).
  - Proposition 1 rests on two lemmas (pp. 11, 32–33). Lemma 7 gives a closed form for the pseudo-inverse, checked against the Moore–Penrose conditions. Lemma 8 uses the binomial inverse theorem.
  - Theorem 1 proves convexity by a direct Hessian computation that ends in a PSD quadratic form (pp. 33–34).
  - Lemma 3 computes the gradient by the chain rule instead of TD finite differences (pp. 14, 34).
  - Lemma 4 evaluates the cost and gradient in O(T³KL² + T²KL³ + T²KL·D) operations instead of O(T³D³) (pp. 14, 34–36). The steps are reordering into a block-triangular K, blockwise inversion restricted to the support, and a block tri-diagonal recursion.
  - Theorem 2 proves finite termination through Fletcher–Leyffer (1994), since the feasible set is finite and zero is always feasible (pp. 14–15). A single branch-and-bound tree with lazy callbacks (Quesada–Grossmann 1992) is mentioned as an option (p. 14).
  - Lemma 5 gives a separable upper bound (pp. 15–16; proof pp. 36–37). The best multivariate fit is no worse than the average univariate fit, and the fusion term is bounded by a degree-weighted ridge. The result is an integer LP in z with closed-form per-entry losses.
  - Algorithm 2 (pp. 16–17): the LP relaxation of that integer LP is not integral. The algorithm rounds fractional entries up, then removes globally supported features with the largest average loss until the solution is feasible.
  - Proposition 2 gives the running time Õ(NTD + (TD)^{2+1/6} + T²KL²(T + KL)), bounding the LP step by Cohen–Lee–Song (2021) (pp. 17, 37). The code solves that LP with Gurobi (p. 39).
  - Proposition 3 shows that the validation costs become elbow-shaped as N, N_V → ∞: unbounded below the true sparsity levels, constant above them (pp. 17–19; proof p. 38). The bisection of Kenney et al. (2021) (Lemma 6) then recovers the levels one at a time, with the others held at their maxima.
  - [My check, not in the paper] Lemma 7 as printed reads (Z(M + λβI)Z)† = (λβI + ZMZ)⁻¹ − λβ(I − Z) (p. 32). For z = 0 the left side is 0 and the right side is (1/λβ − λβ)I, so the coefficient should be 1/λβ.
    - A numerical check with a random PSD M and λβ = 2.5 (scratch script) confirms the 1/λβ version, Proposition 1 and eq. (20). The slip does not reach Proposition 1, because (I − Z)Z = 0.
    - On random segments of [0, 1]^8 the same script found no convexity violations for the Proposition 1 relaxation and 117 in 20,000 for the eq. (20) relaxation, consistent with p. 11.
  - A small pseudo-code slip: Algorithm 3 takes K0_u as input, then resets Ku ← D, although Algorithm 4 calls it for KC with the upper bound D|E| (pp. 18, 20).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup (pp. 39–40):
    - all methods are implemented in Julia 1.6 with JuMP 0.21 and Gurobi 9.5 "for a fair comparison", on an Intel Xeon E5-2690 at 2.90 GHz under CentOS 7;
    - every method has a 900 s time limit, and all zeros are returned when no solution is found;
    - methods that took over an hour in preliminary runs are left out of the reported experiments.
  - The code is on GitHub (pp. 1, 5, 39).
  - Baselines (pp. 20, 39): static_cutplane, one sparse regression over all vertices (Bertsimas–Van Parys 2020); and sum-of-norms with ℓ1 or ℓ2 fusion, each with or without a lasso term (Ohlsson et al. 2010; Hallac et al. 2017; ADMM for ℓ2 with lasso).
  - Tuning (pp. 20, 40): holdout validation over a 5 × 5 grid for λβ and λδ (starting at N, halving), and bisection for the K's. The final refit uses √λβ* only for the paper's three methods and static_cutplane, because "we empirically observe that such an approach slightly improves the performance of these methods" (p. 40).
  - Metrics: Test R², MAE, support difference DS, MAC, tuning and refit time, gap, cut count and average cut time (p. 21; Table 4, p. 42).
  - Synthetic results: 26 settings × 10 datasets = 260 (p. 21). Mean ranks (Table 1, p. 22):
    - svar_cutplane is first on Test R² in every setting (rank 1.0) and first on MAC;
    - sum_of_norms_l1_lasso ranks best on MAE (1.76) and DS (1.43), and static_cutplane second on both (1.86 and 2.05), ahead of svar_cutplane (2.1 and 3.19);
    - the text names only the lasso variant, attributing its edge in part to its more exhaustive grid search (p. 22);
    - svar_cutplane is the slowest to tune (rank 5.57).
  - At low SNR (ξ = 0.5), Test R² is 0.195 against 0.179 for sum-of-norms (Table 5, p. 43).
  - Scaling (pp. 23–24): N up to 7,000, T up to 40 and D up to 900, with up to 10,000 decision variables. The method needs far fewer cuts than static_cutplane, and the time per cut grows quadratically in T and linearly in D, as Lemma 4 predicts.
  - Real data: 5 datasets × 10 random 60/20/20 splits. On mean ranks, svar_cutplane, svar_hybrid and sum_of_norms_l1 tie on Test R² (2.8), the svar variants with lower variance (pp. 23–25).
  - Table 9 (p. 48) shows that no svar variant has the best mean R² on any dataset, and the appendix says so:
    - sum-of-norms leads on Air Quality (0.516 vs 0.515), Appliances by hour (0.832 vs 0.828), Appliances by month (0.496 vs 0.491) and Meteorology (0.846 vs 0.838);
    - sum_of_norms_l2_lasso leads on Housing (0.955 vs 0.949).
  - The svar advantage is sparsity: on Housing, K̂G is 9.1 against 172.3 for sum_of_norms_l1 (Table 10, pp. 48–49).
  - Times including tuning (Table 11, p. 49): svar_cutplane takes 577–30,281 s, svar_hybrid 107–1,254 s and sum_of_norms_l1 64–33,693 s. The MIO-based methods time out without proving optimality in one case only (p. 49).
  - Appendix E (pp. 49–50) tests a family of relaxations indexed by µ ≥ 0, all coinciding on binary points, on 20 instances with T = 1. µ = 0 is best: 0.19 s and MAE 0.056, against 10.60 s and 0.150 at µ = 5.
  - Appendix F (pp. 50–51): the heuristic's LP relaxation is fully integral in 8–100% of 100 random instances, with 85–100% of variables integral, tested only for T, D ≤ 10.
  - Glitches:
    - Tables 6 and 8 print negative mean absolute values (−0.087 and −0.092) for sum_of_norms_l1_lasso at dG = 10 (pp. 44, 46);
    - Housing has N = 822 in the main text (p. 26) but N = 352 per group in Appendix D (p. 47), and 7 × 822 exceeds the dataset's 2,930 observations (p. 46).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Slowly varying regression is traced to Hastie–Tibshirani 1993 and to Bertsimas et al. 1999, described as Lipschitz nonparametric regression (p. 5). Popularity peaked with the fused lasso (Tibshirani et al. 2005) (p. 6).
  - The closest work is sum-of-norms (Ohlsson et al. 2010; Hallac et al. 2015, 2017), total variation (Wytock 2014) and splicing (Zhang et al. 2023) (p. 6). The paper differs by imposing sparsity and sparse variation exactly, which gives "more control to the modeler", and by a smooth ℓ2 fusion term (p. 6).
  - Spatially varying coefficient models are set apart because they estimate heterogeneity instead of constraining it (p. 6).
  - On solving sparse problems, it cites the group's line (Bertsimas et al. 2016; Bertsimas–Van Parys 2020), Hazimeh et al. 2022, and Wei et al. 2022 and Liu et al. 2023 on convexification with indicators (p. 7).
  - The group's 2020 result is praised as a "significant breakthrough" that raised scale by "multiple orders of magnitude", then called fortuitous (p. 7). This is a critique of the group's own earlier work, made in order to generalize it.
  - The paper places itself between Bertsimas–Van Parys 2020 and Bertsimas et al. 2021 (p. 8).
  - Credits: Lemma 6 to Kenney et al. (p. 18), termination to Fletcher–Leyffer (p. 15) and outer approximation to Duran–Grossmann (p. 13).

- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution levels: a modelling framework; a reformulation device that extends to sparse convex quadratics; exact, heuristic and tuning algorithms; and open-source software (pp. 4–5, 27).
  - Limits it states:
    - the heuristic's LP relaxation is not integral (p. 16);
    - tuning guarantees hold only in limited scenarios (p. 5);
    - the µ family does not help, and better convex relaxations are called a fruitful direction (p. 13);
    - lasso-type regularization may be integrated later (pp. 21, 28);
    - preprocessing choices are arbitrary (pp. 44–45, 48).
  - Limits it leaves unstated or understates:
    - on real data the convex incumbent matches or beats svar on R² (p. 48);
    - including tuning, svar_cutplane takes up to about 8.4 hours and is about 4–48 times slower than sum_of_norms_l1 on four of the five datasets (Table 11, p. 49), which strains the abstract's "comparable times" (p. 1); svar_hybrid is comparable;
    - the integrality test stops at T, D ≤ 10 (p. 51).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 2–11, 14, 50). A problem owned by statistics (varying-coefficient regression; the fused lasso) is written exactly as an MIO and carried into a binary convex program. The device is chosen to keep convexity among infinitely many relaxations that are exact on binary points (p. 11), and the block tri-diagonal structure cuts the cost of each cut (p. 14). The boundary is partly printed: the heuristic's LP is not integral (p. 16), and scale and times are reported (pp. 23–24, 49). Step 4, reading structure off the dual, is absent.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 7). The belief (exact sparse formulations unscalable; toy scales only) and its challenge are stated. But the *why now* is the group's own algorithmic advance (Bertsimas–Van Parys 2020), not a rebuilt solver × hardware figure.
- Method 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 13–24, 39–43, 49).
  - The protocol is followed: a heuristic warm start, then an exact cutting-plane solve with gap, cut count and times reported. All methods use one Julia and Gurobi 9.5 stack, one machine and one 900 s limit. The duels are run at validation-tuned complexity, on synthetic data with known truth and on real data, and the code is released.
  - The paper also adopts four of the five critic-informed checks in Method 4's stop rule: low SNR (ξ = 0.5, p. 43), equal time limits (p. 39), timing that includes tuning (p. 42) and released code (p. 39). LP-relaxation strength is tested only for the heuristic's LP (p. 51).
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 16–19, 22, 48).
  - The warm start is the authors' own LP-rounding heuristic, not the incumbent. A hybrid tunes with the heuristic and refits with the exact method.
  - On real data the convex incumbent equals or beats svar's R² on every dataset (p. 48), which is the Workflow D checkpoint case (the incumbent matches you). The abstract does not follow that checkpoint (p. 1).
  - The √λβ* refit tweak is applied to the paper's methods and the static baseline but not to sum-of-norms (p. 40).
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 5, 7–8, 39). A lineage step: S010, then S061, then S175, then this paper, carried by a student and released as code. It returns to his 1999 time-varying estimation work. A course or book is not visible.
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted):
  - Tension T6 evidence: the abstract against Tables 1, 9 and 11 (pp. 1, 22, 48–49).
  - Tension T1: certificates are reported, with one time-out (p. 49).
  - Tension T4: here the paper sides with formulation strength, choosing among relaxations by convexity and cut quality (pp. 11, 50), with no big-M.

- New-pattern candidate: **binary-exact convex extension**: among the functions that agree with the true objective on {0, 1}, pick a convex one, and show in one dimension that the natural choice is not convex (pp. 11–13).
- New-pattern candidate: **generalize your own fortuitous trick**: re-derive a lucky reformulation from the group's earlier paper to find the identity that carries it, then apply that identity to a wider class (pp. 7–8, 11).
- New-pattern candidate: **tune cheap, refit exact** (svar_hybrid; pp. 19, 22).
- New-pattern candidate: **bisection on elbow-shaped validation curves for cardinality hyperparameters** (pp. 17–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) When an inner QP has the Hessian Z(M + λI)Z with binary diagonal Z, write its minimizer through the pseudo-inverse. Then replace (Z(M + λI)Z)†Z by (λI + ZM)⁻¹Z, which takes the same values on binary points and is convex in z (pp. 10–11, 32–34).
- (D4) Before committing to a relaxation, plot the candidates for a single variable (T = D = 1) to see which is convex between 0 and 1 (Figure 2, p. 12).
- (D4) For outer approximation, compute cuts in closed form by the chain rule on K(z) = λI + ZM. Restrict inversion to the support block, and exploit the block tri-diagonal structure a chain or graph induces (Lemmas 3–4, pp. 14, 34–36).
- (D4) For a warm start, bound a coupled loss by a separable one: the average of univariate fits, with the fusion term bounded by a degree-weighted ridge. Solve the LP relaxation of the resulting integer LP, round up, and remove features by average loss until the solution is feasible (Lemma 5, Algorithm 2, pp. 15–17).
- (D5) Tune cardinality levels one at a time by bisection on the validation cost, holding the others at their maxima, and grid-search only the continuous penalties (Algorithms 3–4, pp. 17–20).
- (D5) Use the heuristic to tune and run the exact method once to refit. This costs about 70 s more on average than the heuristic alone and comes close to the exact method's accuracy (pp. 19, 22).
- (D5) Publish per-dataset raw tables beside mean-rank tables. Here the raw tables show what the ranks hide: R² margins of 0.001–0.008 and run times of up to 8.4 hours (Tables 1–2 vs 9–11, pp. 22, 25, 48–49).
