### S052 · Survivable networks, linear programming relaxations and the parsimonious property (Mathematical Programming 1993, DOI 10.1007/bf01580607)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–22: Sections 1–6, Appendix 1 (proof of Lemma 2, pp. 19–20) and Appendix 2 (sketch of Theorem 9, pp. 20–21); reference list skimmed. Subscripts and fractions are partly garbled in the extraction, so statements below follow the prose and the theorem text that survived.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michel X. Goemans (first and corresponding author, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: IO work of his first PhD cohort (Goemans is listed among his 1990 PhD graduates, note 01; received 1 July 1990, p. 1; NSF ECS-8717970 and MIT's Leaders for Manufacturing program, p. 1). Companion of the Goemans–Bertsimas MOR 1991 paper on the probabilistic analysis of the Held–Karp bound (ref. [12], pp. 3, 8). Its Theorem 6 is reused as a lemma in S269 (S269 p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO; LO as the engine; communication networks as motivation}: The survivable network design problem (SNDP): buy (possibly repeated) edges at minimum cost so that each pair i, j has at least r_ij = min(r_i, r_j) edge-disjoint paths. It contains the Steiner tree and k-edge-connected design and, with degree constraints, the TSP (pp. 1–2); fiber-optic networks are the motivating application (p. 2). Entry point: with arbitrary costs SNDP is NP-hard, which "typically" sends researchers to approximate solutions, mostly local-search heuristics (p. 2). The LP relaxation of the classical cut formulation is polynomial by the ellipsoid method or Karmarkar's algorithm, but "these computational approaches are not satisfactory in practice" and no efficient practical algorithm exists (p. 4). The paper enters through a structural property of that relaxation, not through a new solver (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: The parsimonious property (Theorem 1, p. 5): under the triangle inequality, forcing the degree of every vertex in any set D down to its minimum (max_j r_ij) does not change the LP relaxation value. One property, many consequences (p. 3):
  - Steiner (r_i = 0) vertices can be deleted from the Steiner LP (pp. 3, 9).
  - The Held–Karp (HK) bound equals the LP value of the 2-connected problem, so it lower-bounds the min-cost 2-connected network (Theorem 4, p. 8) and is monotone in the vertex set (Theorem 5, p. 8).
  - The Steiner LP is half the HK bound (Theorem 6, p. 9) and the k-connected LP is k/2 times it (p. 9), so Held–Karp's MST-based method computes both (p. 9).
  - What is unchanged: the classical cut formulation (p. 4). What is new: exact relations between the relaxations of different problems, then two heuristics measured against the LP bound. The tree heuristic takes a union of MSTs over nested vertex sets, one per connectivity level (p. 10). The improved tree heuristic adds a minimum matching on odd-degree vertices, generalizing Christofides (p. 16).
- **D3 Model and data**: Complete undirected graph with edge costs; edges may be chosen repeatedly (p. 4). Requirements r_ij = min(r_i, r_j) for the heuristics (p. 10). The triangle inequality is needed for Theorem 1 and is made harmless by Theorem 3: replacing costs by shortest-path lengths changes neither the IP nor the LP value (pp. 6–7). Rational data, so that a scaled LP solution is integral (p. 6). Theorem 9's tightness needs successive connectivity types to divide each other (p. 13). No data sets.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Proof devices:
  - Theorem 1: scale a rational LP optimum by k so that k·x_e and k·r_ij are even integers, read it as an Eulerian multigraph, and split off edge pairs with a strengthened Lovász lemma (Lemma 2, p. 5; proof in Appendix 1, pp. 19–20). Splitting keeps the connectivities and, under the triangle inequality, never raises cost (p. 6).
  - Metric closure without loss of generality, by a path-rerouting transformation (Theorem 3, pp. 6–7).
  - The heuristic's value is written as an LP over Edmonds' spanning-tree polytope; the scaled LP optimum (2 − 2/|S|)x* is feasible there, so the MST heuristic is within 2 − 2/|S| of the Steiner LP bound (Theorem 7, p. 11). The bound is "symmetrically tight": one instance attains it as an integrality gap, another as a heuristic gap (pp. 11–12, Figs. 2–3).
  - Telescoping over connectivity levels plus monotonicity gives the general ratio, at most 2·Σ_k (ρ_k − ρ_{k−1})/ρ_k (Theorem 8, pp. 12–13). A recursive graph construction shows it tight (Theorem 9, p. 13; Appendix 2, pp. 20–21), hence Θ(log r_max) and Θ(p) for p distinct types (Corollary 10, p. 14).
  - Splitting the optimal IP network into a 2-connected block plus attached trees, each part feasible for a relaxation, lowers the constant by one when ρ_1 = 1 (Theorem 11, pp. 14–15). The improved heuristic's analysis uses Edmonds' perfect-matching polytope (Theorem 13, p. 17).
  - Results: within twice the optimum for r_i ∈ {0,1,2}, {0,1,3} or {0,k} (p. 3; Corollaries 12, 14, 16, pp. 16–18). Running times O(pn²) for the tree heuristic and O(rn³) for the improved one, r being the number of gaps (pp. 11, 16–17).
  - Algorithmic side: partition bounds for Euclidean instances from subadditivity and upperlinearity, differing by at most (4 + √2)k (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a: no computation of its own; the heuristics are never run. The paper leans on Johnson's reported study, in which the HK bound showed heuristic tours within 1% of optimal for up to 100,000 vertices (p. 7), and on the empirical observation that HK is above 99% of the optimal tour (p. 8).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Held–Karp (pp. 7, 9), Lovász's splitting lemma (p. 5; Monma et al. used it in a similar context, p. 5) and Edmonds' polytopes (pp. 11, 17). Strengthens the 2 − 2/|S| results of Takahashi–Matsuyama, Kou et al., Plesnik and El-Arbi by measuring against the LP bound instead of the optimum (pp. 3, 11). Re-derives Cunningham's result as Theorem 4 (p. 8). Credits Agrawal–Klein–Ravi and Goemans–Williamson with generalizing Section 5 to arbitrary r_ij, and Bienstock et al. with using Theorem 7 (pp. 18–19). Predecessors are subsumed, not attacked.
- **D7 Writing**: The contribution is one named property with "numerous consequences" (abstract, p. 1). Results are flagged "surprising" or "astonishing" (pp. 3, 5, 9). A roadmap closes the introduction (p. 3). The long proof goes to an appendix: "lengthy and technical, and is sketched" (p. 14). Figures exhibit the worst-case instances (p. 12). Theorem–proof style with little hedging.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a structural theory of LP relaxations plus approximation guarantees with tight examples (pp. 3, 11–18). Limits: the property fails without the triangle inequality (p. 6), absorbed by Theorem 3; no practical algorithm for the general LP (p. 4); Theorem 9's tightness only under divisibility (p. 13). Future directions: degree constraints (k-TSP) and odd-cut constraints (matching-type problems), "It would be interesting to investigate whether the parsimonious property has important consequences for this broader class of problems" (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 5, 9, 11–14, 20–21). For: structure is read off the relaxation of a classical formulation (Theorem 1), and the boundary is printed with tight instances. Against: no other tradition's problem is rewritten as an LP, and no dual is used. Correction for SKILL.md's Domain Map (IO row), which cites this DOI for *LP-relaxation rounding*: this paper has no rounding step. Its approximation results bound MST and matching constructions by the LP value (pp. 10–18); in this batch LP rounding is S036's move (S036 pp. 6–7).
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 7, 9). The bound-as-certificate idea of step 4 in theory form: HK bounds certified heuristic tours within 1% (p. 7), and the k-connected LP value is "a very good lower bound" for judging heuristic networks (p. 9). No computation, no duel.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 4). The paper already separates polynomial solvability from practicality ("not satisfactory in practice", p. 4), an early root of the 2019 principle. But faced with NP-hardness it takes the route it calls typical, approximation with guarantees (p. 2): the belief Method 2 later concedes was right when formed and reopens with MIO solvers.
- Tension T4: evidence (pp. 11–12). Attention to the relaxation's quality (integrality-gap instances) fits the *strong formulations* side of T4.
- New-pattern candidate: **relaxation-centred IO** (the LP or Lagrangean relaxation as bound, yardstick and algorithmic lever; with S269, S036).
- New-pattern candidate: **bound, constructive heuristic, provable ratio, tightness instance** (pp. 10–18, 20–21; with S091).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before using triangle-inequality arguments, replace costs by shortest-path distances and prove that the IP and LP values do not change (Theorem 3, pp. 6–7).
- To apply graph lemmas to a fractional solution, scale a rational LP optimum until it is even-integral and read it as an Eulerian multigraph; then transform it (splitting off) without raising cost (p. 6).
- To bound a heuristic against the LP, write the heuristic's value as an LP over a known integral polytope (spanning tree, perfect matching) and show that a scaled LP-relaxation optimum is feasible for it (Theorem 7, p. 11; Theorem 13, p. 17).
- Prove tightness in both directions: one family where the integrality gap meets the ratio, one where the heuristic does (pp. 11–12).
- Sharpen a constant by splitting an optimal integral solution into parts that are each feasible for a different relaxation (Theorem 11, pp. 14–15).

**Quotes**:
- "The variety of consequences makes the parsimonious property particularly important." (p. 3)
- "Typically, such a negative result from complexity theory leads researchers to obtain approximate rather than optimal solutions." (p. 2)

### S091 · Stochastic and dynamic vehicle routing with general demand and interarrival time distributions (Advances in Applied Probability 1993, DOI 10.2307/1427801)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–32 (Sections 1–8 and references). Many formulas lost exponents and fractions in the extraction (integrals of powers of the density f), so constants and statements below come from the prose and surviving theorem text; where an exponent is lost, this card says so.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Garrett van Ryzin (Columbia University at publication, p. 1; Bertsimas's PhD student, thesis 1991, per note 04) · **Position in trajectory**: third paper of the dynamic traveling repairman line, after Operations Research 1991 and 1993 (refs. [6], [7]; pp. 1–2). Received 27 April 1992 (p. 1). Funding: his Presidential Young Investigator award with Draper Laboratory matching funds, NSF DDM-9014751, Draper Laboratory and the UPS Foundation (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + AP (vehicle routing); CP as a device}: The dynamic traveling repairman problem (DTRP): demands arrive over time at random points of a planar region and need on-site service; m vehicles must minimize the mean system time (pp. 1–2). Applications named: couriers, flowers, pizza, utility repair crews, equipment maintenance, and automobiles shipped from plants to dealers (p. 2). Entry point: the authors' earlier results assumed uniform locations and Poisson arrivals, but "one rarely encounters uniformly distributed locations", and some applications have more or less variable interarrival times (p. 3). The old proofs leaned on uniformity and PASTA, so new techniques were needed (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: A lower bound valid for every policy, with the unknown performance profile left free and then optimized:
  - Each small region is treated as a "spatial queue", with φ the time-average density of waiting demands (Little's law, pp. 5–6).
  - As a region shrinks, its thinned renewal input approaches Poisson, so a heavy-traffic distributional Little's law equates what departures leave behind with time averages (Lemma 2, pp. 7–8).
  - A nearest-neighbour argument turns this into a lower bound with φ free (Lemmas 3–5, pp. 8–11). For spatially unbiased policies φ = f (Theorem 1, p. 11). For biased policies, the bound is the optimum of a convex program over densities, solved in closed form by KKT conditions (Theorem 2, pp. 11–12).
  - Policies: partition the region into equal-probability wedges (unbiased) or cells sized to the local density (biased, piecewise-uniform f); batch demands per cell; serve batches FCFS by optimal TSP tours; optimize the batch size. Both are within about 1.8 of the bound as ρ → 1 (Theorems 3 and 5, pp. 13, 17).
  - Relative to [6] and [7]: the lower bound is a factor 2 stronger (p. 11), so the modified TSP policy's guarantee falls from 3.6 to about 1.8 (pp. 2–3). The policies are modifications of the earlier ones (p. 13).
- **D3 Model and data**: Closed bounded region in R²; stationary renewal arrivals with finite variance; i.i.d. general service times with two finite moments; i.i.d. locations with a continuous density f that is K-Lipschitz and bounded above and below (assumptions 1–5, pp. 3–4). The policy class is restricted by P1–P3: decisions only at completion epochs, a choice of a demand or the depot, and smooth bounded normalized waiting times. P3 is "needed primarily for technical reasons" and is narrower than the class allowed in the uniform case (pp. 4–5). All results are heavy-traffic limits, ρ → 1 (p. 4). Piecewise-uniform densities are smoothed in an ε-strip to meet the Lipschitz assumption (pp. 16–17) and are called "probably an adequate model" in practice (p. 17). Data: simulation only (p. 21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Devices:
  - Spatial queues and Little's law (p. 6); renewal thinning and its coefficient of variation (p. 7); the Bertsimas–Nakazato heavy-traffic relation for departures (pp. 7–8).
  - Stability condition plus the nearest-neighbour bound (Lemmas 3–5, pp. 8–11).
  - Convex program over φ, Lagrangian dual, KKT conditions sufficient by convexity (pp. 11–12).
  - Upper bounds: TSP asymptotics (Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley, Steele), the Karp–Steele variance bound and the Iglehart–Whitt heavy-traffic formula for superposed renewal inputs (pp. 13–16), then a change of variables to optimize the batch size (pp. 15–16).
  - The Hardy–Littlewood–Pólya inequality for Proposition 1: uniform demand is the worst case, and biased service is strictly better for every non-uniform density (pp. 23–24). A two-region density shows the ratio of unbiased to biased optima can be unbounded (pp. 24–25).
  - Tightness audit: an "optimal removal rule" attains Lemma 4's bound exactly, so the bound uses no routing structure and can only be improved by using more of it (Theorem 6, pp. 25–27).
  - Extensions: capacitated vehicles with Haimovich–Rinnooy Kan tour partitioning (Theorems 7–8, pp. 28–29); d dimensions, where the modified TSP policy is asymptotically optimal as d → ∞ (Theorem 11, p. 30).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Simulation of two policies that need no optimal tours, space-filling curve (SFC) and nearest neighbour (NN) (p. 20). Instance: a two-region piecewise-uniform density with ε = 10⁻⁴, λ = 1, s = 0.1, σ² = 0, ρ = 0.8, one simulation run for each of 11 values of δ from 0.05 to 0.9999, using the code of [6] (p. 21). Metric: average number in system. The slope of log N against log δ estimates each policy's distributional exponent: slopes 0.80 (SFC) and 1.37 (NN), giving 0.44 and 0.58 (pp. 22–23). Reading: SFC behaves roughly like an unbiased policy, NN between unbiased and optimally biased (p. 23). Uniform-case constants from [6] are quoted for comparison: SFC about 0.66, NN 0.64, modified TSP 0.51, lower bound 0.38 (p. 21). No replications or confidence intervals are reported (p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues and corrects the authors' own [6], [7]: the new bound is "weaker" in being only asymptotic, but it covers general f and arrivals and doubles the old bound (p. 11). Imports applied probability (PASTA, p. 3; Iglehart–Whitt, p. 14), geometric probability (Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley, Steele, Karp–Steele, pp. 13–14; Rhee, p. 30) and routing heuristics (Bartholdi–Platzman, p. 20; Haimovich–Rinnooy Kan, p. 28). Reuses his own distributional Little's law with Nakazato (p. 7). Psaraftis for background (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract lists four claims: generalization, improved bounds with constant-factor policies, a simple leading form, and dependence on the demand distribution (p. 1). Practical reasons for generalizing come before theoretical ones (p. 3). Conjectures are labelled with their basis, "We base this conjecture on heuristic arguments" (p. 16), and left open. An intuitive explanation follows the unbounded example (p. 25). Self-critical asides: the lower bound "is likely to be weak", and the 1.83 guarantee "too pessimistic" (pp. 27–28). The conclusion turns results into uses: strategic models for terminal location, fleet sizing and districting (p. 31).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: theory (bounds, constant-factor policies, a common leading form for system time in which only a functional of f changes between the uniform, unbiased and biased cases, p. 23; the inner exponents are lost in the extraction) plus a simulation protocol for policies that resist analysis (pp. 22–23). Limits: heavy traffic only; the technical policy class (p. 5); the unbiased policy's asymptotic optimality only conjectured (p. 16); no tight example for the biased bound (p. 27); optimal tours "difficult to compute" in practice (p. 20). Future directions: strategic planning models (p. 31) and a proof of the conjecture (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 11–12, 25–28). An applied-probability question, the best heavy-traffic system time over spatially biased policies, becomes a convex program over the performance density φ with a closed-form KKT solution. Step 5 (print the boundary) is the Section 6 tightness audit, which shows what the bound cannot see. A routing-side precursor, from the same years, of the performance-space move of SW1 (achievable region).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 11, 23–25). The price of a policy constraint: spatially unbiased service, "perhaps imposed as a matter of policy" (p. 11), costs nothing exactly when demand is uniform and strictly more otherwise (Proposition 1, pp. 23–24), and the price can be unbounded (pp. 24–25). No knob and no curve: a binary constraint priced by closed-form functionals. An equity price 18 years before the price-of-fairness paper (DOI 10.1287/opre.1100.0865).
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (p. 23). Suggests estimating a policy's constants from operating data of a "live" system to evaluate current operating practice: a suggestion only, with no owner, data or field test.
- Tension T3 (optimization, not probability): evidence (pp. 6–16, 23–30). The probability side is practised in full: renewal theory, heavy-traffic limits, geometric probability.
- New-pattern candidate: **bound, constructive policy, provable ratio, tightness check** (pp. 3, 11–17, 25–28, 30; with S052).
- New-pattern candidate: **tightness audit of one's own bound** (construct a non-routing process that meets the bound; pp. 25–28).
- New-pattern candidate: **static methods adapted to dynamic problems** (p. 31).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Shrink-the-region device: thin a renewal arrival stream into a vanishing ball so that it behaves like Poisson and PASTA-type equalities hold asymptotically (Lemma 2, pp. 7–8).
- Leave the unknown performance profile free in a lower bound, then minimize the bound over all admissible profiles by a convex program solved through KKT: one bound covers every policy at once (pp. 10–12).
- Batch-and-tour policy design: equal-probability (or density-sized) cells, batches of n/k demands, FCFS service by TSP tours, batch size optimized after a change of variables (pp. 13–17).
- Audit your bound by building an abstract process that meets it exactly; if one exists, a better bound must exploit more problem structure (pp. 25–27).
- For policies that resist analysis, assume the analytic form found for the tractable ones and estimate its exponent by log–log regression over a one-parameter family of inputs (pp. 22–23).
- Meet Lipschitz-type technical assumptions for piecewise-constant inputs by ε-strip smoothing, then let ε → 0 (pp. 16–17, 20).

**Quotes**:
- "In our previous analysis, uniformity and PASTA [20] were heavily exploited. We are therefore forced to develop new proof techniques that do not rely on these properties." (p. 3)
- "A recurring finding in our analysis is that static vehicle routing methods when properly adapted can yield near optimal or perhaps even optimal policies for dynamic routing problems." (p. 31)

### S036 · The multi-airport ground-holding problem in air traffic control (Operations Research 1994, DOI 10.1287/opre.42.2.249)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13, with Appendix A (final formulations, partly garbled) and Appendix B (heuristic pseudocode). Tables I–VI read row by row as extracted.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Peter B. Vranas (first author), Amedeo R. Odoni (all MIT, p. 1; Odoni was Bertsimas's own doctoral adviser, SKILL.md Academic Lineage) · **Position in trajectory**: an early air-transportation (AP) paper resting on Vranas's 1992 PhD thesis and paired with the 1994 Transportation Science paper on dynamic ground holding (references, p. 13). Supported by Draper Laboratory and his PYI award; two undergraduates did some of the computations (p. 13). Vranas was co-advised with Odoni (note 04).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + AP (air transportation); LO as the relaxation}: Decide how long each flight is held on the ground (and possibly in the air) so that total ground plus airborne delay cost over a network of airports is minimal (pp. 1–2). Entry point, four parts:
  - Congestion cost: about $2 billion a year for U.S. airlines, whose profits rarely exceed $1 billion; $5 billion in Europe in 1989 (p. 1).
  - Airport capacity that varies 2:1 or even 3:1 with weather (p. 1).
  - The FAA's command center relies on its controllers' judgment rather than on decision-support or optimization models (p. 1).
  - A literature restricted to single airports, which ignores "down-the-road" delay propagation through aircraft rotations and hub connections (pp. 1–2). Claim: "the multi-airport GHP is addressed for the first time" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: Time-indexed 0-1 assignment variables for take-off and landing periods, introduced "because the capacity constraints cannot be expressed in a simple linear way in terms of the more natural delay decision variables" (p. 3). One coupling constraint per pair of consecutive flights of an aircraft passes the delay beyond the slack on to the next flight (p. 4). Without the coupling constraints the model decomposes into the known single-airport problems (p. 4); with them, network effects enter "in such a simple way without loss of generality" (p. 5). Three formulations: P1, general (p. 4); P2, with infinite departure capacities and no airborne holds (p. 5); P3, with cancellations, the same size as P2 but never infeasible (pp. 5–6). An LP-rounding heuristic for P3 (pp. 6–7; Appendix B, p. 13).
- **D3 Model and data**: Static and deterministic: holds decided once at the start of the day, capacities fixed numbers (pp. 2–3). Linear delay costs, an approximation "widely used by the FAA and throughout the airline industry, for lack of a better alternative" (p. 4). Airborne delay costlier than ground delay (pp. 1, 4). Delay caps G_f = 4–5 and A_f = 2–3 periods of 15 minutes (pp. 2–3). Size of P2: F + F' + KT constraints and at most 5F variables when G_f = 4, so the number of flights, not of airports or periods, drives size (p. 5). Data are synthetic: "The scheduled arrival times were arbitrarily chosen" (p. 8); capacities sit at the feasibility border, found by trial and error (p. 8); cost slopes 50 for ground and 75 for airborne delay (pp. 8, 10); three cost classes, 100/50/20, for large, medium and small aircraft (p. 10). Probabilistic capacities are left out (p. 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No theorems. Devices:
  - Exchange argument: with infinite departure capacities, linear costs and airborne cost above ground cost, moving every airborne queueing delay into a ground hold lowers cost, so some optimum has no airborne delay (pp. 4–5). The infinite-capacity assumption is then justified "a posteriori" by computation (pp. 5, 10).
  - Variable elimination to assignment variables only (Appendix A, pp. 12–13).
  - Cancellation variables with large coefficients inside the coupling constraints, the authors' "technical tricks" (p. 6).
  - The decomposed problem D is an LP, because the single-airport constraint matrix is totally unimodular (Terrab) (p. 7).
  - Heuristic: group the fractional ("problematic") flights by aircraft, visit each aircraft's flights in schedule order, assign each to the earliest period with capacity slack or cancel it if there is none, and prune the next flight's periods so the coupling holds (pp. 6–7, 13). No guarantee is proved for it.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Solver and protocol: the commercial package MPSX, no custom algorithm; CPU times are given only to show solvability "in reasonable time" (p. 7). Each formulation is solved as the IP (I), its LP relaxation (L) and the decomposed model (D); "network effects" are the gap between v_I and v_D (pp. 7–8).
  - Instances: 3 airports, 1,800 flights, T = 100 around the feasibility border (Table I, p. 8); a series with T = 64, F = 1,000, 2,000 and 3,000 (2, 4 and 6 airports) and F'/F = 0.2–0.8 (Table II, pp. 8–9); finite departure capacities (Table III, p. 10); cancellation costs M = 100–10,000 (Table IV, p. 11); non-uniform capacities and three cost classes (Tables V–VI, pp. 10–11).
  - Findings: with identical costs v_L, v_D and v_I are close, yet D violates about 180–200 of the 600 coupling constraints (p. 8). IP times reach 25,021 CPU s (Table II, p. 9) and "can become excessive" (p. 8). About 10% of flights are fractional in L, which motivated the heuristic (p. 9). Different cost slopes for continued and other flights give v_D = 13,950 against v_L = 22,811 (p. 9); three cost classes give differences of 22–27% (p. 10; Table VI as extracted gives v_L 22%, 27% and 31% above v_D, p. 11). Finite departure capacities matter only with strongly non-uniform flight times (p. 10). Interdependent departure and arrival capacities save 35–40% (Vranas 1992, p. 10). The heuristic is close to v_L for low cancellation costs and poor for high ones, which the text expects and explains (p. 10).
  - Failures shown: infeasible capacity vectors (p. 8) and the heuristic's poor regime (pp. 10–11). As extracted, two rows sit uneasily with the prose, and neither is discussed. Table II's 2,000-flight, F'/F = 0.80 row gives v_D = 53,250 against v_I = 65,500, a gap of about 19% of v_I, while the text says the gap "is always small" (pp. 8–9). Table IV's 3,000-flight, F'/F = 0.80, M = 100 row gives v_H = 58,900 against v_L = 38,042 (p. 11), while the text calls the heuristic "quite close" for small cancellation costs (p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning**: Claims priority for the multi-airport problem (p. 2) after reviewing Odoni 1987, Andreatta–Romanin-Jacur 1987, Terrab 1990 and Richetta 1991, all single-airport (p. 2). Reuses Terrab's single-airport formulation, which is P2 without coupling (p. 5), and his total-unimodularity result (p. 7). On practice: the FAA's habit of treating all flights alike is called "incorrect" but "well-entrenched" (p. 12). There is no comparison with the controllers' actual decisions.
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract opens with a dollar figure and the ground-before-airborne premise and closes with a scale headline, 6 airports and 3,000 flights (p. 1). Simplicity is the recurring selling point: "remarkably simple" (p. 2), "Note the simplicity of P2" (p. 5), tractability attributed to simplicity (p. 11). Rival explanations of a surprising result are tested and rejected before the main one is offered (p. 9). Six numbered insights close the paper (pp. 11–12). **Transferable techniques**:
- Use time-indexed assignment variables when a capacity constraint is not linear in the natural (delay) variables (p. 3).
- Model propagation through a network by one linking inequality per consecutive pair. Measure its value as the gap between the coupled IP and the decomposed model, and also count the decomposed solution's violated links, because a small value gap can hide infeasibility (pp. 4, 7–8).
- Place test instances at the feasibility border, located by sweeping capacities, where the decision matters most (p. 8).
- Test your explanation of a surprising result by perturbing the suspected cause (cost slopes of continued vs other flights) and by ruling out rival explanations with runs you already have (p. 9).
- Remove infeasibility by adding cancel variables with cost M, then sweep M to map the regimes in which the extension changes the answer (pp. 5–6, 10).
- Drop a constraint class by an exchange argument, then check its impact computationally and report the check (pp. 4–5, 10).

### S269 · A technique for speeding up the solution of the Lagrangean dual (Mathematical Programming 1994, DOI 10.1007/bf01582057)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–23 (Sections 1–4, Tables 1–3; reference list skimmed). The text is an OCR layer with word errors (*out* for *our*, *orte* for *one*) and garbled formulas; the quotes avoid damaged passages.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: James B. Orlin (MIT Sloan, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: LO/IO algorithm theory of the early 1990s: received 29 April 1991, revised 8 January 1993 (p. 1); an IPCO 1992 version per note 01; supported by an NSF research initiation grant and his PYI award (p. 22). Uses S052's relations to compute Steiner and 2-connected bounds (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {LO + IO; CP for the nonlinear extension}: Compute LP relaxations and Lagrangean duals of combinatorial problems in provably fewer operations (p. 1). Entry point: large NP-hard instances are solved through LP relaxations or Lagrangean duals (p. 1), yet the standard tool for the dual, Held–Karp's subgradient optimization, "is not known to be a polynomial method" (p. 2). The scope is deliberate: problems "already known to be solvable in polynomial time", where ellipsoid-like methods give speed-ups (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: Represent the easy polyhedron S by its extreme points and rays (resolution theorem) and dualize. The dual has only m + 1 variables, one per complicating constraint plus one, and exponentially many constraints whose separation problem is linear optimization over S (pp. 3–4). Running Vaidya's cutting-plane algorithm (or the sliding-objective ellipsoid method) on this dual costs O(T(n)mL + M(m)mL), with T(n) the time to optimize over S (Theorem 1, p. 4). Variants:
  - several subproblems (Theorem 2, p. 6);
  - cost splitting (Lagrangean decomposition) for intersections of K polyhedra (Theorem 3, p. 7);
  - Lagrangean duals of integer programs (Theorem 4, p. 8);
  - complicating variables, in Benders style, which also returns the primal solution (Theorem 5, p. 9);
  - Lagrangean duals of arbitrary nonlinear programs, always concave maximizations (Theorem 6, p. 10).
  Unchanged: the bound itself. Changed: the space in which it is computed, of dimension m rather than n.
- **D3 Model and data**: Polyhedra without lines (p. 3); an oracle that optimizes linear functions over S; complexity counted in arithmetic operations, with input size L and fast matrix multiplication M(k) = O(k^2.38) (p. 4). Nonlinear problems: arbitrary functions and an ε-approximation, with a Turing-machine caveat (pp. 10–11). Two-stage stochastic programming with a discrete distribution of K scenarios (p. 20). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Devices:
  - Resolution theorem plus LP strong duality (p. 3); separation equals pricing (p. 4).
  - Vaidya's O(kL) iterations against the ellipsoid's O(k²L); only Vaidya's method benefits from fast matrix multiplication (p. 4).
  - Primal recovery: by complementary slackness when the optimum is unique, or by an interior-point solve over the O(mL) generated columns at cost O((mL)³L) (pp. 4–5). For Lagrangean duals, a generated constraint tight at the optimum yields a primal solution at no extra cost (Remark, p. 8).
  - Cost splitting wins when separation over S_r is harder than optimization over it, as for polymatroids (pp. 7, 18–19).
  - Applications with operation counts (pp. 12–21): the Held–Karp bound in O(n^3.38 L) against O(n^6.76 L) for Vaidya's method applied directly and O(n^8 L) for the ellipsoid, or O(n^4 L) against O(n^8 L) without fast matrix multiplication (pp. 12–13); Steiner and 2-connected bounds through S052's relations (p. 13); vehicle routing (pp. 13–14); multicommodity flow, where Vaidya's special algorithm still wins in some cases (pp. 14–15); flows with K side constraints at the cost of one when K is constant (p. 15); fixed-charge network design (pp. 16–17); p-median (pp. 17–18); K-polymatroid intersection (pp. 18–19); special-structure networks (p. 19); multi-item lot sizing, whose Lagrangean dual can beat the LP bound and for which no other polynomial approach was known (p. 20); two-stage stochastic programs, linear in K (pp. 20–21). Summary tables: Tables 1–2 (p. 11) and Table 3 (p. 21).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: None: no implementation, instance or timing. Comparisons are worst-case operation counts (Table 3, p. 21), and Plotkin–Shmoys–Tardos is left out of them because its running time is not polynomial (p. 13). The conclusion says practical competitiveness with subgradient optimization is unknown and hinges on Vaidya's algorithm, untested in practice (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Held–Karp's 1-tree relaxation (p. 2), the ellipsoid method and Vaidya's algorithm (p. 1). Credits Chandru and Trick with the independent use of the ellipsoid method for the Lagrangean dual and notes their different focus (p. 2); thanks a referee for pointing it out (p. 22). Compares with interior-point methods and with Vaidya's method applied directly (pp. 1, 12–21), and concedes where a rival dominates (multicommodity flow, p. 15). Uses its own S052 result as a lemma (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing**: Strong claims up front: the techniques "yield the fastest way to solve them" (abstract, p. 1; again p. 2), and "Surprisingly, our method is significantly faster" (p. 2). Hedging is concentrated in the last section (p. 22). The applications are offered as illustrations, not an exhaustive list (p. 12), and results are tabulated "for future reference" (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: algorithmic theory (worst-case complexity) for a dozen relaxations (pp. 11–21). Limits: practicality untested (p. 22); primal solutions for (P1)–(P3) only under uniqueness or at extra cost (p. 11); nonlinear duals only approximately (p. 11). Future directions: scheduling and sequencing applications, stronger Lagrangean duals by Lagrangean decomposition, and hybrids with subgradient methods that "can potentially lead to practical algorithms as well" (p. 22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a relaxation has few complicating constraints over an easy polyhedron, dualize over the polyhedron's extreme points. The dual's dimension is then the number of complicating constraints, and the subproblem optimizer is the separation oracle for any cutting-plane method (pp. 3–4).
- If separation over a polyhedron is harder than optimization over it, split the variable (x1 = x2 = …) so that each copy only needs optimization (cost splitting, p. 7; polymatroids, pp. 18–19).
- Recover a primal optimum from the cut that is tight at the dual optimum instead of re-solving (Remark, p. 8).
- Report algorithmic results as a problem / oracle / running-time table plus a comparison table against the best known method (Tables 1–3, pp. 11, 21).
- State in the conclusion what the complexity result cannot show: untested practicality, and dependence on a subroutine (p. 22).
