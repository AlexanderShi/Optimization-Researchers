### S003 · Theory and applications of robust optimization (SIAM Review 2011, DOI 10.1137/080734510; read as arXiv 1010.5445v1)
**Read level**: full — all 50 file pages of the arXiv preprint arXiv:1010.5445v1 ("First version: September 5, 2008", p. 1): body pp. 1–44 read in full, reference list pp. 44–50 read for positioning and self-citation counts. The SIAM Review typeset version was not available, and differences between the two were not checked; every page number in this card is an arXiv file page. The title page also prints "Revised version: November 26, 2024" (p. 1), a date after the arXiv stamp, presumably the date arXiv recompiled the source [inferred].
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: David B. Brown (Duke, Fuqua) and Constantine Caramanis (UT Austin, ECE) (p. 1); Caramanis's 2006 MIT PhD dissertation is ref. [52] (p. 46), and Brown co-authored [6], [23] and [24] with Bertsimas (pp. 44–45) · **Position in trajectory**: the stock-taking survey of the 2001–2010 RO programme, first written in 2008 and published in 2011, between the price-of-robustness era (SW3) and the data-driven sets of the late 2010s; S086 cites it as the general RO survey (S086 p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO CP IO ML AP}: not a single problem but a survey with a stated stance. It opens from the fragility of optimal solutions to parameter perturbations, which can leave a computed solution "potentially worthless" (p. 2), and separates RO from robust control (RO focuses on algorithms, geometry and tractability) and from stochastic optimization (set-based rather than probabilistic uncertainty) (p. 2). Two motivations are given: set-based uncertainty is natural in many applications, and computational tractability is "a primary motivation and goal" that "has largely influenced the theoretical trajectory" of the field (p. 2). Three organizing issues follow: tractability, conservativeness with probability guarantees, and flexibility (p. 3). The entry example is a portfolio problem whose exact stochastic version (expected return against loss probability) is NP-hard even for discrete return distributions (Benati–Rizzi; p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: open a survey with one small computational example that already shows every theme the survey will treat (here tractability, conservativeness and model error), before any theory (pp. 4–8).
- **D2 Core idea**: the organizing claim is that the tractability of a robust problem is decided by its inner "subproblem", the maximization of the constraint function over U; tractability is "tantamount" to the robust feasible set X(U) being convex with an efficient separation test (p. 10). The survey therefore arranges results as nominal class × uncertainty-set class → counterpart class (pp. 10–16) and uses the "budget of uncertainty" as the common knob for ellipsoidal (p. 10), cardinality-constrained (p. 12) and norm-based sets (p. 13). Its central counter-intuition: worst-case (max–min) formulations "need not be overly conservative" and in many cases resemble stochastic solutions (p. 3). The adaptive part (§4) carries the same programme to multi-stage problems through affine adaptability (pp. 24–27) and finite, piecewise-constant adaptability (pp. 28–29).
  - Transferable technique: organize a field's results as a two-way table, problem class × uncertainty-set class, each cell saying which class the counterpart lands in and whether it is NP-hard (pp. 10–16).
- **D3 Model and data**: general RO (2.3) with closed uncertainty sets and constraint-wise uncertainty (pp. 8–9). The WLOG reductions are stated: an uncertain objective moves into a constraint through an epigraph variable; a joint set can be replaced by its projections, constraint by constraint; certain constraints get singleton sets (p. 9). "Tractable" means reformulable into a problem with a polynomial-time algorithm, and "intractable" means that such an algorithm would imply P = NP (footnote 1, p. 3); a Slater condition is assumed (footnote 2, p. 10). Probability guarantees assume independent, symmetric, bounded coefficients (Theorem 4, p. 17) or known mean and covariance only (p. 17). The one data set is N = 300 monthly returns of n = 10 equity and debt indices starting in 1981 (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: list the WLOG reductions (epigraph, constraint-wise projection, singleton sets) before the taxonomy, so every later theorem can assume the simplest form (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: the technical spine is duality of the inner problem.
  - Polyhedral U: dualize max{aᵀx : Da ≤ d} with x fixed and substitute the dual; the counterpart is an LP whose size grows polynomially (pp. 10–12).
  - Ellipsoidal U: an SOCP (Theorem 1, Ben-Tal–Nemirovski, p. 11).
  - The cardinality-constrained (Γ) set: the set-selection subproblem is nonconvex, "However, one can show that the natural convex relaxation is exact", and dualizing gives an LP (p. 12).
  - General norms: dual-norm constraints, so ℓ1 and ℓ∞ give LPs and ℓ2 an SOCP (Theorem 2, p. 13).
  - Robust quadratic constraints: an SDP under a single ellipsoid via the S-lemma's hidden convexity (Theorem 3, p. 14), NP-hard for polyhedral sets or intersections of ellipsoids (pp. 13–14); the authors call the S-lemma the boundary between what can be solved exactly and what cannot (p. 14).
  - Robust SDP: NP-hard in general, with an inner approximation of quality ρ ≤ π√μ/2 for structured norm-bounded sets (p. 15).
  - Robust discrete optimization with Γ deviating cost coefficients: n + 1 nominal problems (also for approximation algorithms) and a logarithmic number for network flows (pp. 15–16), against the Kouvelis–Yu NP-hardness of scenario sets (p. 15).
  - Probability devices: 1 − e^{−Ω²/2} for ellipsoids of radius Ω (p. 17); P(violation) ≤ e^{−Γ²/(2|J|)} for the Γ-set (Theorem 4, p. 17); 1/(1 + Δ²) with mean and covariance only (p. 17).
  - Risk–set duality: a coherent risk constraint on a linear constraint equals a robust constraint over U = conv{E_Q[a]}, and CVaR gives a polytope, hence an LP (pp. 18–19).
  - Adaptability: the two-stage problem is NP-hard through an indefinite quadratic (example (4.12), p. 24); the affinely adjustable counterpart becomes a conic program, an LP over the orthant, under fixed recourse (Theorem 5, pp. 25–26), and has an SDP approximation with a stated tightness factor under non-fixed recourse (Theorems 6–7, pp. 26–27); conditions for the optimality of affine policies are cited ([29], p. 28); finite adaptability is NP-hard for an optimal 2-partition but efficient when the uncertainty dimension, the decision dimension or the number of uncertain constraints is small (pp. 28–29); a static robust solution is within a factor of two of the fully adaptable one under symmetry (Bertsimas–Goyal, pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable technique: for a robust constraint max_{a∈U} aᵀx ≤ b, fix x, dualize the inner maximization and substitute the dual; the counterpart inherits the class of the dual (pp. 10–12).
  - Transferable technique: place every tractable reformulation next to its nearest NP-hard neighbour (same nominal class, slightly richer set), so the reader sees where tractability ends (pp. 13–15, 23–24).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: the survey's only original experiment is the portfolio example.
  - Set-up: three RO sets, R_Q (an ellipsoid; an SOCP), R_D (the D-norm or Γ set; an LP) and R_T (tail averages; an LP), each solved at 200 parameter values (600 RO problems), against the exact stochastic program (minimum loss probability under a return target), formulated as a MIP and solved at 8 return levels (pp. 5–6); MOSEK in Matlab on a 2.13 GHz, 2 GB laptop, solver version not given (p. 6).
  - Nominal model: the exact program wins by at most about 2–3% in loss probability; R_D is conservative; the exact instances take from seconds to over 2.1 hours, about 5.2 hours for the 8 instances, against under 10 minutes for all 600 RO instances, and Figure 1 prints the solve times on the frontier (p. 6).
  - Out-of-model test: each of the N × n return values is perturbed by a random number uniform on [0.99, 1.01] or [0.98, 1.02] (and a 5% level is also run); R_D's frontier hardly moves, the exact solutions are the most sensitive (their loss probability rises by up to 5–6%), and at the 2% level they are always beaten by one of the robust models (pp. 6–7).
  - Admitted limit: "only one random trial", followed by the claim that "such results are typical" (p. 7).
  - Others' evidence is summarized in §5.1.5, including a negative result: Goldfarb–Iyengar's robust portfolios "did not always outperform" the classical ones on real data (p. 35).
  - Transferable technique: print the exact method's solve time next to each of its points on the quality frontier, so the cost of exactness sits beside its benefit (Figure 1, p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: re-evaluate every solution under perturbed versions of the data-generating model (1%, 2%, 5%) and report which formulation degrades least (pp. 6–7).
- **D6 Positioning**: Ben-Tal–Nemirovski and El Ghaoui et al. (late 1990s), together with fast interior-point methods for semidefinite optimization, are credited with the "massive flurry" of RO work (p. 2). The survey positions itself as a condensed, higher-level complement to the Ben-Tal–El Ghaoui–Nemirovski book plus a broad catalogue of applications (p. 4), and sets RO apart from sensitivity analysis (p. 9). Dynamic programming is not reviewed; robust DP is said to rest on rectangularity, under which the decision-maker gains nothing from adapting to past realizations (p. 23). Others' theorems are quoted with the attribution in the theorem header (pp. 11, 13, 17, 25–27, 29). Self-citation: 16 of 136 references carry Bertsimas's name ([6], [23]–[37]) and 11 carry Caramanis's (pp. 44–50); in §5.2.1 the robust least-squares result is El Ghaoui–Lebret's, while the Lasso and SVM results are Caramanis's work with Xu and Mannor (pp. 36–37).
  - Transferable technique: position a survey by naming the book it complements and saying what it does differently (a condensed method summary plus applications) (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the theme order follows the stance: tractability (§2), then probability and the choice of sets (§3), adaptability (§4) and applications (§5) (pp. 8–43). The main message is framed as a "perhaps surprising implication" (p. 3); hedging is moderate and personal ("we would guess", p. 7). Theorems are followed by an "intuition" sentence (p. 11). The term "budget of uncertainty" is used for ellipsoids, cardinality sets and norm sets alike, building a shared vocabulary around the authors' own construct (pp. 3, 10, 12). The closing section is specific and self-critical (pp. 43–44). Applications (§5, pp. 30–43) take about as much space as the theory (§§2–4, pp. 8–29).
  - Transferable technique: close a survey with numbered open problems, each saying what is known, what is missing and why it matters in practice (pp. 43–44).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: contribution: a consolidation of static RO tractability, probability guarantees and risk-measure connections, the then-recent results on adaptability, and an applications map (abstract, p. 1; p. 8). Stated limits: the robust counterpart of a general convex problem is intractable (p. 10); robust SDP is intractable (p. 15); "most adaptable RO problems currently remain intractable" (p. 43); price-of-robustness studies "have been largely empirical" (p. 43); most RO models "are not directly connected to data" (p. 43). Future directions: tractable adaptable RO, theoretical bounds on when robustness is cheap or expensive, and a data-driven theory of RO (pp. 43–44). Not stated by the authors: the motivating experiment is a single random trial on one 10-asset data set with no solver version (pp. 5–7).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a catalogue of class-keeping devices (polyhedral set → LP, ellipsoid → SOCP, Γ-set → LP, norm set → dual norm, robust 0–1 cost uncertainty → n + 1 nominal solves), printed next to the boundary (polyhedral or ellipsoid-intersection quadratic constraints, robust SDP and two-stage problems are NP-hard) (pp. 10–16, 23–24). On the C2 drift: "tractable" is defined by polynomial time (footnote 1, p. 3), while the opening example argues from measured solve times (p. 6).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence, with a self-identified gap: the budget-of-uncertainty knob trades robustness against performance, with a priori probability bounds (pp. 3, 17), but the survey lists the theory of the price of robustness as open because the existing studies, including Bertsimas–Sim [34], "have been largely empirical" (p. 43). The probability side was proved; the cost side was not.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (an inverted duel): the exact, solver-certified MIP is the slow incumbent and the tractable robust approximations are the challengers; exactness wins slightly under the nominal model and loses under perturbation (pp. 6–7). The lesson recorded is that exact optimization of a misspecified model buys little.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: a why-now argument is given (late-1990s theory plus fast interior-point methods, p. 2), but it is not quantified.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: a survey written with two earlier co-authors (Caramanis's dissertation is ref. [52]); the reference list maps the RO cohort ([23]–[36]) (pp. 44–46); future direction 3, data-driven RO, names the programme's next step (pp. 43–44).
- Heuristic 1 ✅ evidence (co-authored text): Tikhonov-regularized least squares, the Lasso and norm-regularized SVMs are presented as solutions of robust problems; "robustifying" a solution gives regularity, and sparsity is "a consequence of robustness" (p. 37).
- New-pattern candidate: *out-of-model stress test*: evaluate robust and nominal decisions under data-generating models outside their design assumptions (pp. 6–7). It recurs in S006 (pp. 5, 7) and S086 (pp. 10–14) in this batch.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Open with one runnable example that shows the field's central trade-offs and the cost of the exact alternative (pp. 4–8).
- [D2] Organize results as nominal class × uncertainty-set class → counterpart class (pp. 10–16).
- [D4] Dualize the inner maximization with x fixed and substitute the dual (pp. 10–12).
- [D4] For a cardinality-type set, check whether the natural convex relaxation of the inner selection problem is exact before declaring the counterpart nonconvex (p. 12).
- [D4] State the a priori violation bound as a function of the knob (e^{−Γ²/(2|J|)}), so the knob can be set from a target probability (p. 17).
- [D5] Print the exact method's solve times on the quality frontier, and re-run all methods under 1–5% perturbations of the data (pp. 6–7).
- [D7] Close with numbered open problems, including admissions about the field's own evidence base (pp. 43–44).

**Quotes**:
- "computational tractability is also a primary motivation and goal. It is this latter objective that has largely influenced the theoretical trajectory of Robust Optimization" (p. 2)
- "These studies, however, have been largely empirical. Of interest are theoretical bounds to gain an understanding of when robustness is cheap or expensive." (p. 43)

### S012 · The price of fairness (Operations Research 2011, DOI 10.1287/opre.1100.0865)
**Read level**: full — all 15 pages: body with every proof (pp. 1–14) and references (pp. 14–15). Extraction caveats: many mathematical symbols are lost or replaced (Greek letters, ≤, ∈), and decimal points are dropped in the prose on p. 11 ("86%" there means 8.6%, as Table 1 on the same page gives 0.086).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vivek F. Farias and Nikolaos Trichakis (MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the first paper of the fairness line with Farias and Trichakis, received July 2009 and accepted March 2010 (p. 1). The reference list shows the surrounding cohort: working papers on equitable operational objectives (Bertsimas, Farias & Trichakis 2009a) and on multi-account portfolios under fairness (Bertsimas, Iancu & Trichakis 2009b), and the ATFM fairness paper with Gupta (pp. 14–15). S026 generalizes it (S026 p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP CP}: the entry is an implementation failure. The FAA must reallocate slots when weather cuts capacity, and delays cost an estimated $10 billion in direct operating costs in the 12 months to September 2008 (p. 1). Optimization proposals that minimize total delay cost, including Bertsimas & Stock-Patterson (1998, 2000), promise large savings over current practice, but most remain unimplemented, ostensibly because some airlines would lose relative to the status quo (p. 1). Fairness has been characterized axiomatically, but beyond qualitative economics there has been "little work" quantifying its trade-offs (p. 1). The question is restated quantitatively: the price of fairness (POF) is the relative loss in the sum of utilities when a fair allocation replaces the utilitarian one (pp. 2, 4), to be bounded for proportional fairness (PF) and max-min fairness (MMF) (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when efficient optimization proposals sit unimplemented, name the property the stakeholders demand and make its price the research question (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: abstract every allocation problem into its utility set U ⊂ R^n_+, the set of utility vectors the players can reach simultaneously (p. 3), and bound the utilitarian optimum through one supporting hyperplane of U at the fair point. For PF, the first-order optimality condition of max Σ log u_j is itself a supporting hyperplane, with β_j = 1/(n u_j^PF) (pp. 5, 8). For MMF, the max-min level λ puts λe on the boundary of U, and the supporting-hyperplane theorem gives the hyperplane (pp. 9–10). Over the box 0 ≤ u ≤ e, the utilitarian value is then at most the value of the LP relaxation of a knapsack problem (p. 8). The bounds depend on n alone: POF(PF) ≤ 1 − (2√n − 1)/n and POF(MMF) ≤ 1 − 4n/(n + 1)² (Theorem 1, p. 7); unequal maximum utilities are handled by diagonal scaling (Theorem 2, pp. 11–12).
  - Transferable technique: work in the outcome (utility) space instead of the decision space; a convex outcome set plus one supporting hyperplane turns a bound on the efficient optimum into a knapsack LP (pp. 3, 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: n players and a central decision maker with complete knowledge of the players' preferences and absolute control of the allocation (p. 3); a one-shot allocation with no time dimension (§2, p. 3); strategic misreporting set aside, with a pointer to the price of anarchy (p. 3). Efficiency is the sum of utilities (p. 4). Assumption 1: U is compact and convex (p. 6), justified by randomization over indivisible resources and by concave, nondecreasing utilities (p. 7). Proposition 1 gives primitive sufficient conditions: a compact, convex, monotone resource set and separable, concave, nondecreasing, bounded utilities with f(0) = 0 (p. 7). Theorem 1 assumes equal maximum achievable utilities, defended as the usual normalization (pp. 7, 11). There are no data.
  - Transferable technique: after the structural assumption, give a proposition with primitive conditions that imply it (p. 7) and an example that breaks the result without it (§5.2, p. 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: the proof skeleton (pp. 7–12):
  1. WLOG normalize the maximum utilities to 1, and WLOG take U monotone, because every scheme considered selects Pareto points (p. 7).
  2. PF: the first-order condition gives the supporting hyperplane (6)–(7), with 1/n ≤ β_j ≤ 1 (8) (p. 8).
  3. Bound SYSTEM(U) by the knapsack LP (10), whose closed-form value is cited from the authors' own LP textbook (Bertsimas & Tsitsiklis 1997) (p. 8).
  4. Reduce the infimum over β to a two-variable problem F2 by a convex-optimization argument; F2 = (2√n − 1)/n (p. 9).
  5. MMF: take the ray re, the max-min level λ and its supporting hyperplane, prove the hyperplane's normal nonnegative through monotonicity, and bound the ratio by nλ/(n + 1 − 1/λ) ≥ 4n/(n + 1)², minimized over λ (pp. 9–10).
  6. Theorem 2 follows from the scale invariance of PF and MMF ((1), (2)) applied to the normalized problem (pp. 11–12).
  Tightness: a line-graph network with k unit-capacity links, k single-link flows and long flows that cross all links attains the MMF bound with n = 2k − 1 (and the Theorem 2 bound with utility weight M) and the PF bound with n = k² (pp. 12–14). Without convexity, a three-point utility set gives a POF arbitrarily close to 1 already for n = 2 (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: prove tightness on the application community's own standard topology (line-graph bandwidth networks), not on a pathological construction (pp. 12–14).
  - Transferable technique: use the scheme's scale invariance to extend a bound from normalized to unnormalized utilities (pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a in the computational sense. The numbers are the bound itself, tabulated for n = 2–5 (PF 0.086, 0.179, 0.250, 0.306; MMF 0.111, 0.250, 0.360, 0.444; Table 1, p. 11) and plotted for n ≤ 20 (Figure 2, p. 11), plus the exact tight examples (pp. 12–14). One failure is reported: tightness of the PF bound with unequal maximum utilities could not be established (p. 14). The evaluation on real ATFM instances is announced as under way (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: network fairness studies are "typically numerical", built on specific topologies (p. 2); the polymatroid result of Chakrabarty et al. is "somewhat restrictive" (p. 2). Approximation-algorithm notions of "approximately fair" solutions are questioned: averaging the PF, MMF and utilitarian solutions is within a factor of 3 of each criterion but "might not be Pareto", and approximate fairness has no axiomatic basis (pp. 2–3). The price of anarchy (selfish misreporting) is set apart as a different source of inefficiency (p. 3). Foundations come from Nash, Kalai–Smorodinsky, Rawls and Young (pp. 3–5). The paper calls its analysis "the first of its kind" (p. 2) and says it gives "a sound theoretical basis to prior empirical work" (p. 11). Self-citations: 6 of 43 references (the two ATFM papers of 1998 and 2000, the LP textbook, two working papers, Bertsimas & Gupta) (pp. 14–15). The price of robustness is not cited (pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable technique: when a neighbouring field offers "approximately fair" solutions, show by a simple construction (averaging) that its approximation notion loses an axiom you care about (Pareto optimality) (pp. 2–3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: a dollar figure and a named agency open the paper (p. 1); the contributions are a numbered list in plain words (p. 2); the axioms are stated and then glossed one by one (p. 5). After the main theorem come bullet remarks with numbers and an exclamation mark for n = 2 (p. 11). Tightness is announced as a surprise ("The surprising answer is that our bounds are in fact tight", p. 12). The conclusion makes an ethical claim: the price is "effectively inescapable" if allocations are to be "ethically acceptable and implementable" (p. 14). Admissions are explicit ("We are unable to establish", p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: follow a worst-case theorem with a small-n table, a plot against n and two sentences on what a decision maker should conclude (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theoretical: tight, instance-free POF bounds for PF and MMF over all compact convex utility sets, with two insights: the price is small when there are few players, and PF is much cheaper than MMF when there are many (pp. 11, 14). Limits: convexity is needed, since otherwise the POF can approach 1 (p. 14); PF tightness with unequal maxima is open (p. 14); the model is static (p. 3). Future: special families of utility sets with smaller prices that are relevant to practice, the polymatroid case being "perhaps too narrow" (p. 14); real ATFM instances (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (the paper SKILL.md cites under Method 3): property and stakeholders named (p. 1); a solution-independent bound, uniform over compact convex U (Theorems 1–2, pp. 7, 11); the curve against n with a table (p. 11); the bad case published, since a nonconvex U makes the price arbitrarily large (p. 14); tightness on the rival community's standard examples (pp. 12–14). ⚠ Variant: there is no continuous knob here. The decision maker chooses between two schemes, and the bound's single parameter is the number of players (p. 2); S026 adds the one-parameter knob.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: structure is read off the dual (the PF optimality condition as a supporting hyperplane; the knapsack LP from the authors' LP textbook) (p. 8), but to bound a price, not to build a formulation for a solver.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the owner's problem (the FAA and the airlines) motivates the paper (p. 1), but no current rule is evaluated; the real-instance study is deferred (p. 14). Fairness is framed as the condition for implementation (pp. 1, 14), which bears on Method 5's stakeholder correction.
- Method 6 ✅ weak evidence: a fairness cohort around one student (Trichakis) and one co-adviser (Farias), with sibling working papers on ATFM and multi-account portfolios (pp. 2, 14–15).
- New-pattern candidate: *implementability-first entry*: start from why efficient optimization proposals are not adopted, and price the property whose absence blocks adoption (pp. 1, 14). It recurs in S026 (pp. 11–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Turn the question of why the optimal solution is not implemented into the question of what the property the stakeholders demand costs (p. 1).
- [D2] Bound the efficient optimum through one supporting hyperplane at the fair point plus a knapsack LP (p. 8).
- [D3] Pair the structural assumption with a sufficient-condition proposition and a counterexample without it (pp. 7, 14).
- [D4] Take the outcome set monotone WLOG when every scheme under study selects Pareto points (p. 7).
- [D4] Prove tightness on the application's canonical topology (line graph) (pp. 12–14).
- [D7] Right after the theorem: a table for small n, a plot against n and a managerial reading (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "the vast majority of these proposals remain unimplemented. The ostensible reason for this is fairness" (p. 1)
- "It is our belief that the “price” of fairness is effectively inescapable if the allocations prescribed by a given scheme are to be ethically acceptable and implementable." (p. 14)

### S006 · Adaptive robust optimization for the security constrained unit commitment problem (IEEE Transactions on Power Systems 28(1) 2013, online 2012, DOI 10.1109/TPWRS.2012.2205021)
**Read level**: full — all 12 pages, including the appendix with the deterministic UC model (pp. 10–11), the references and the author biographies (pp. 11–12). Extraction caveat: the displayed equations (1)–(24) and most inline symbols are missing from the text file (only the equation numbers survive), so the formulation is read from the prose; some numbers inside sentences are also missing (for example the lower end of the total-cost saving range, p. 6, and the scaling formula of the budget rule, p. 6).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Eugene Litvinov, Jinye Zhao and Tongxin Zheng (ISO New England, Department of Business Architecture & Technology) and Xu Andy Sun (MIT PhD 2011, then Georgia Tech) (pp. 1, 12) · **Position in trajectory**: received 16 March 2011, accepted 25 May 2012, published online 24 July 2012 (p. 1): the RO programme applied to an operator's live system with partner co-authors; SKILL.md cites it under Method 5 for reporting its own run time.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO IO AP}: the entry is a change in the application. Wind, solar and price-responsive demand raise supply and demand uncertainty for the system operators (p. 1). Two incumbents are named, each with its weakness (pp. 1–2):
  - reserve adjustment, the industry's practice: easy to implement, but reserves set "by some ad-hoc rules" can be uneconomic, and capacity can still fall short when load deviates, which the paper says is "confirmed by the ISO’s operational experience" (p. 1);
  - stochastic programming: the distribution is hard to identify, the guarantees are only probabilistic, and high reliability needs many scenarios, which makes the problem computationally intensive (pp. 1–2).
  The problem: a two-stage adaptive robust security-constrained unit commitment (SCUC) with uncertain nodal net injections in a budgeted set (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: introduce the problem by naming the incumbent in practice and the incumbent in the literature, each with one concrete failure (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: the first stage chooses the commitment (binary) decisions against all injections in the set; the second stage, the dispatch, is fully adaptive, its functional form "determined implicitly by the optimization problem, as opposed to being presumed" affine (p. 3). The min–max–min model (6) is recast as (7); dualizing the dispatch LP (8) turns the inner max–min into a bilinear program (9) over two polyhedra (p. 3). The worst-case cost R(x) is convex and piecewise linear in x, as a maximum over extreme points (10), but evaluating it is NP-hard (p. 4). Solution method: two levels. An outer Benders-type cutting-plane loop has a MIP master (11); an inner outer-approximation (OA) loop, borrowed from MINLP (Duran–Grossmann; Fletcher–Leyffer), linearizes the bilinear term (pp. 2, 4). Kept from the deterministic SCUC: network, ramping, transmission-security and basic reserve constraints (pp. 2, 9). Changed: the ad hoc reserve adjustment is replaced by the budgeted set (pp. 5, 9).
  - Transferable technique: keep the industry's rule for the risks your uncertainty set does not model (here the basic reserve for generator contingencies) and let the robust model replace only the ad hoc part (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**: commitment variables (on/off, start-up, shut-down) over a 24-hour horizon; dispatch, reserves and flows continuous (p. 2). Costs are linear, with piecewise-linear convex supply curves, admitted as "a common assumption" (pp. 9, 11). Uncertainty: for each period, a budgeted set around the nominal nodal injections, with deviation ranges and a budget running from 0 (the nominal singleton) up to the full box (p. 3). A slack in the energy balance keeps the inner problem feasible, and feasibility for every commitment is assumed via penalty terms (pp. 3–4). Not modelled: generator outages and correlations between loads (pp. 9–10). Data: the ISO-NE system with 312 generating units, 174 loads and 2,816 nodes, 4 representative transmission constraints (1,876 in a further test), and the day-ahead market data of a normal winter day; average capacity 31,929 MW against an average forecast load of 16,232 MW (p. 5). A higher-variation case uses a 20% deviation range (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: state which risks the uncertainty set covers and which stay with the existing rule, so the comparison is made at the same coverage (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Reformulation: min–max–min (6) → (7) → dual (8) → bilinear (9); R(x) is convex piecewise linear (10); evaluating it means maximizing a convex function, which is NP-hard (pp. 3–4).
  - Outer Benders loop: a master MIP (11) with lower and upper bounds and a tolerance, accelerated by adding dispatch constraints to the master when the bounds move slowly (p. 4).
  - Inner OA loop: linearize the bilinear term around the current point and solve LPs (the set is polyhedral); "only a local optimum is guaranteed", which the authors check by trying different initial conditions (p. 4).
  - Theorem 1: the cuts are valid, because OA's dual solutions are extreme points of the dual polytope and R(x) is the maximum over such points (p. 5). Fig. 1 shows the loop with the simultaneous feasibility test (SFT) for security constraints (p. 5).
  - Scope: the inner method works for any polyhedral set (p. 5) and could be extended to ellipsoidal sets (p. 9).
  - A tested alternative: an MIO linearization of the bilinear term showed "slow convergence" on large instances (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: write the worst-case recourse as a maximum over the extreme points of the dual polytope; any extreme dual point found by a local method then still gives a valid cut (pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Baseline: reserve adjustment (ResAdj), the deterministic UC at expected load with a reserve requirement made of a basic level plus a part proportional to the total load variation, so that "the uncertainty budget also controls the level of conservatism of the ResAdj solution" (p. 5). The baseline gets the same knob.

  - Transferable technique: report your run time next to the baseline's, together with the quality cost of loosening the tolerances (pp. 5–6).
- **D6 Positioning**: the prior work falls into two groups, reserve requirements [1]–[4] and stochastic UC [5]–[9] (p. 1). **Read level**: full for the article — all 17 pages: body pp. 1–16 and references pp. 16–17. The online appendix (cited in the text for the near-worst-case constructions, §B; the unimodality claim, §C; and the ATFM model, §D) is not in the file and was not read; the article itself contains no proofs of Theorems 1–2 (pp. 7–10). Extraction caveats: Greek letters (α, θ, Θ) are lost or mis-mapped, and decimal points are garbled ("706%" stands for 7.6%, "005" for 0.5, "105 bf" for 1.5 b_f); numbers are read with that mapping, which the text's own arithmetic supports (a 7.6% price of fairness is "within about 93%" of the efficient total, p. 15).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vivek F. Farias (MIT Sloan) and Nikolaos Trichakis (then at Harvard Business School) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received 1 December 2010, accepted 21 December 2011 (p. 1); the sequel to S012, which it reconstructs as special cases (p. 4); the case-study data came from Bill Moser and Mark Weber of Lincoln Labs and from Shubham Gupta (p. 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP CP}: the entry is a design question that managers face: how to approach "designing the “right” objective" for a resource allocation problem (abstract, p. 1). In call centres, healthcare scheduling, ATFM and organ allocation the natural objective is utilitarian (p. 1), and the design of the objective "is nonetheless frequently executed in an ad hoc fashion" (p. 2). Inequity is the root tension when monetary compensation is not implementable (p. 2). The problem: choose the inequality-aversion parameter α of the α-fair family, guided by worst-case curves for the efficiency lost (the price of fairness, POF) and the fairness lost (the price of efficiency, POE) (pp. 2, 7–10).
  - Transferable technique: recast the choice of an objective as the choice of one parameter in an axiomatically grounded family (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Atkinson's constant-elasticity welfare function, W_α(u) = Σ u_j^{1−α}/(1 − α) (Σ log u_j at α = 1), nests the utilitarian (α = 0), proportionally fair (α = 1) and max-min fair (α → ∞) allocations, so one knob spans the debate (pp. 2, 6). Two instance-free curves are derived. Read through the garbled glyphs, they are POF ≤ 1 − Θ(n^{−α/(α+1)}) (Theorem 1; pp. 2, 7) and POE ≤ 1 − Θ(n^{−1/α}) (Theorem 2; pp. 2, 10); their limits match S012's PF and MMF orders. POE is the relative loss in the minimum utility against the max-min allocation. The minimum utility is chosen because, among easy-to-interpret metrics, it is the only one that keeps MMF optimal among Pareto allocations; footnote 2 gives a two-point counterexample for the range, standard deviation, coefficient of variation, Jain, Theil, mean-log-deviation and Gini metrics (p. 9). The curves become managerial prescriptions (§3.3, pp. 10–11), and a "new, unified geometric framework" reconstructs S012's PF and MMF bounds (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: for two competing goods, define a price in each direction (efficiency lost to fairness, fairness lost to efficiency) and plot the two against each other, parametrized by the knob (Figure 5, p. 11).
- **D3 Model and data**: the S012 framework: n players, a central decision maker and a utility set U ⊂ R^n_+ (Samuelson's utility possibility set) (p. 5); utilities that are not quasi-linear, so no cash transfers (pp. 2, 5). Efficiency is the utilitarian sum, justified twice: practice uses it, and it is the only admissible efficiency criterion when transfers are allowed (pp. 5–6). The theorems assume U compact and convex with equal maximum achievable utilities (pp. 7, 10); a nonconvex U is convexified by randomization (p. 7); unequal maxima give the bound (2), which depends on the ratio B/L (p. 8). Case study: the Bertsimas & Stock-Patterson (1998) ATFM model with binary flight–sector–time variables (pp. 12–13); airborne delay weighted 1.5 against ground delay (p. 13); each airline's utility is its delay reduction relative to the FAA's ration-by-schedule (RBS) (pp. 12–13), measured in flight delay or in passenger delay (p. 13). Data: 4 airlines (as many as the large U.S. airlines), 54 airports, about 4,000 flights, two days with severe weather (May 13, 2005 and July 27, 2006) (pp. 13–14). Not discussed: whether the ATFM utility set, built from binary schedules, is convex [inferred gap; the general remark on randomization is on p. 7].
  - Transferable technique: define each stakeholder's utility as improvement over the current rule (delay reduction against RBS), so the status quo is the zero point of every axis (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Theorem 1 states the POF bound as 1 minus the minimum over x ∈ [1, n] of an explicit expression (p. 7). The negative of that function is unimodal (online appendix §C, not read), so the exact bound is easy to compute for any n and α (p. 7). The version for unequal maxima, (2), gets worse as B/L grows (p. 8). Near-worst-case examples: line-graph bandwidth networks with y links, and a knapsack-style single resource consumed at two rates θ1 and θ2 by l and n − l players; the gap between the bound and the examples averages 0.005 and is at most 0.023 (pp. 8–9). Theorem 2's POE bound involves the root of an equation and is tight on a single-resource example with n − 1 players at rate θ1 and one at rate θ2 (p. 10). The authors call the POF bounds "near-tight" (p. 8). No proof is in the article (pp. 7–10).
  - Transferable technique: when a worst-case bound is a one-dimensional minimization, prove its objective unimodal so that practitioners can compute the exact bound for their own n and α (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Design: for each day, solve the ATFM model under the utilitarian objective, α = 0.5, 1 and 2, and max-min fairness, for both utility definitions (p. 13).
  - Metrics: the system delay reduction, each airline's reduction and the minimum among them (pp. 13–14); the empirical POF and POE against α, with the theoretical bounds plotted (Figures 6–7, pp. 14–15).
  - Assumption check on the data: under flight delay, the coefficients of variation of the airlines' maximum utilities are 0.015 and 0.007, which supports equal maxima (p. 14). Passenger delay breaks equality (airlines 3 and 4 carry about 1.6 times more passengers per flight), so the bound (2) with B = 1.6 and L = 1 is used (p. 14).
  - Results: the utilitarian plan cuts delay by roughly 10% against RBS but leaves some airlines with no gain (pp. 13–14). The balancing prescription α = 2 costs about 7.6% POF and 3.8% POE, about 93% of the maximal efficiency and 97% of the max-min minimum. The instance-specific curves would have chosen α = 1 (6.5% POF, 7.7% POE), so "we lost very little" (p. 15). The utilitarian choice would exclude one airline entirely (a POE of 100%) (p. 15).
  - Conservativeness, reported: with a 20% efficiency tolerance, the worst-case curve picks α of about 0.5, whose actual loss is 5% for flight delays, so full max-min fairness would have fitted the tolerance; for passenger delays the actual loss is about 15% (pp. 15–16).

### S086 · Inverse optimization: A new perspective on the Black-Litterman model (Operations Research 2012, DOI 10.1287/opre.1120.1115)

The electronic companion (Tables EC.1–EC.6, Figures EC.1–EC.3 and the case L = 0) is not in the file and was not read (pp. 3, 5, 6, 11, 12, 14). Extraction caveats: Greek letters are replaced by other glyphs (μ appears as "Ì", Σ as "è"), and decimal points in tables and some numbers are garbled ("12087%" stands for 12.87%); numbers are read with that mapping where the prose confirms them (15.73%, pp. 12–13).

The biography presents the paper as part of a programme "in understanding the implications of inverse optimization in a variety of fields" (p. 15). The first two authors were partly funded by a Citigroup grant to MIT (p. 14). It cites S003 as the RO survey (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP RO AP}: the entry is a widely used industry tool with known limits. The Black-Litterman (BL) model underlies Goldman Sachs's published allocation recommendations and the analytics of firms such as Zephyr, BlackRock and Neuberger Berman (p. 1), but it allows views only on returns, not on volatility or market dynamics, and it rests on the mean-variance paradigm (p. 1). Prior extensions stay inside the statistical framework and often lead to nonconvex optimization (pp. 1–2); "reverse optimization" handles constrained problems only through ad hoc arguments and still blends views statistically (p. 2). The problem: characterize the BL estimator as the solution of a convex inverse-optimization problem, then generalize it (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: take an industry-standard estimator with a statistical derivation and ask which optimization problem it solves (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: replace BL's statistical framework with inverse optimization (abstract, p. 1). In equilibrium the market portfolio is optimal for the investors' forward problem; its KKT conditions, read with the data as unknowns, characterize every (μ, Σ, r_f, L) that makes it optimal (Proposition 1, p. 5). After the change of variables Σ̄ = 2δΣ this becomes an LMI system that treats μ and Σ "on the same footing" (Theorem 1, p. 5). Views are added as constraints; since the system may become infeasible, one solves for the smallest-norm perturbation (12), an SDP (13) (p. 5). With Σ fixed at the historical estimate, the solution is exactly BL's estimate (Proposition 2(b), p. 5), so the inverse-optimization (IO) framework generalizes BL (p. 6). The extensions follow (pp. 6–9):
  - a recipe for constrained forward problems (budget, no-shorting, margin) (p. 6);
  - views on volatility and a factor-model structure as linear or semidefinite constraints, giving the MV-IO estimator (p. 6);
  - risk beyond variance through the robust problem (19), whose uncertainty sets reproduce Markowitz, VaR and CVaR under Gaussian returns (Proposition 3, p. 7) and general coherent risk measures (Proposition 4, p. 7);
  - a conic-duality characterization of equilibrium (Theorem 2, p. 8), giving the RMV-IO estimator with volatility uncertainty (pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable technique: the paper's own "simple recipe": argue that the observed decision is optimal for a forward problem in equilibrium, solve the corresponding inverse problem, and use its solution in place of the equilibrium system (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: Markowitz with risk-free lending and borrowing and a risk threshold L (pp. 2–3); a CAPM-type equilibrium in which all investors solve the same problem with investor-specific L (pp. 3, 7); Σ ≻ 0 and L > 0 (Proposition 1, p. 5). For (19): a conic-representable U with a strictly interior point, and a unique optimum for some L (pp. 7–8). Gaussian returns are needed only for the VaR and CVaR equivalences (p. 7). The general RMV-IO system cannot be solved as it stands, so parameters are fixed a priori (k = 3 factors, ε = 10⁻⁵, the factor share taken from the historical spectrum), and the scale z, like BL's δ, is set exogenously to match BL's equilibrium market return, "primarily for ease of comparison" (p. 9). Data: 10 GICS sectors proxied by MSCI sector indices from DataStream, market shares from S&P 500 weights (WRDS), 24 months of history as of May 1, 1998 (pp. 3, 14); the backtest uses MSCI sector indices from June 1992 to December 2009, the 3-month T-bill and rolling 60-month windows (p. 12).
  - Transferable technique: when a scale parameter is not identifiable (z, δ), fix it at the incumbent's value so that the comparison isolates the new idea (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Under Slater's condition, the KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient, so they define the inverse feasible set (Proposition 1, p. 5).
  - A change of variables makes the system linear in the unknown data: an LMI (Theorem 1, p. 5).
  - A Schur complement gives an SDP (Proposition 2(a), p. 5); the least-squares closed form (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀb recovers BL (Proposition 2(b), p. 5).
  - The closed-form minimum of a linear function over an ellipsoid yields the Markowitz, VaR and CVaR equivalences (Proposition 3, p. 7); risk measures correspond to convex uncertainty sets (Proposition 4, citing Bertsimas & Brown 2009 and Natarajan et al. 2009, p. 7).
  - Scaling (Proposition 5) and aggregation over investors (Proposition 6) show that the market portfolio is optimal for some L* (pp. 7–8).
  - Double conic duality: the inner minimization over r ∈ U is dualized under Slater's condition, giving the conic program (22); (22) is dualized again, giving (23); primal–dual optimality is written as the system (21) (Theorem 2, p. 8).
  - Admitted: "It is not clear how to solve this system" in general (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: to invert a robust (min–max) forward problem, dualize the inner problem so the forward problem becomes one conic program, then use strong duality again to write optimality as a conic feasibility system in the unknown data (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Stated aim: not to decide which procedure "outperforms" the other, but to identify "when and how" to use the extra flexibility (p. 9).
  - Fair comparison: views on returns only (BL cannot take volatility views), and factor structure imposed on BL's covariance estimate so that differences can be attributed to IO (p. 9). Endnotes 5–6: Tsay's truncated-spectrum estimator was used, and the experiments were repeated with the full sample covariance and a rank-3 estimator, with similar results (p. 14).
  - Simulations: L = 12.87% (the market portfolio's historical standard deviation) and r_f = 0 (p. 10). They test sensitivity to the accuracy of the view under CAPM (Table 2), rising confidence in a wrong view (Table 3), a random perturbation of the covariance (Figures 1–2; 10,000 simulations in Table EC.5) and a view portfolio lying in the factor space (about 2% against about 84% projection on the first three eigenvectors) (pp. 10–12).
  - Backtest: monthly from June 1998, target standard deviation 15.73%, a Markowitz comparator and one static view (p. 12); a systematic-bias design, perfect foresight plus an offset (p. 13); a 72-month window as a robustness check (p. 14).
  - Metrics: average return, overall standard deviation, maximum standard deviation over a rolling 24-month window, and the Sharpe ratio (pp. 10, 12).
  - Findings: the IO portfolios take less risk than BL, by up to 6% in overall and 11% in maximum standard deviation, and stay closer to the target (p. 12). Conclusions about the average return depend on the out-of-sample window; shifting its start by 18 months changes BL's picture (pp. 12–13). With accurate views all methods beat the market, by up to 20% a year, and BL earns more but takes more risk (p. 13). BL wins when the views are correct (pp. 10–11, 13); when the view lies in the factor space, the methods perform alike (p. 12).
  - Software: CVX (Grant & Boyd; version 1.21 in the reference) (p. 14, endnote 3; p. 15). Transaction costs are not modelled (not mentioned in the text).
  - Transferable technique: before claiming a winner in a backtest, rerun it on a shifted window and report when the conclusion flips (pp. 12–13).
  - Transferable technique: give the baseline the new method's structural ingredient (here a factor-structured covariance) so the comparison isolates the idea, then repeat with the baseline's native setting (pp. 9, 14).
  - Transferable technique: separate the accuracy of the inputs from the model's assumptions with stylized simulations before any backtest (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: the BL literature (Black–Litterman 1992; He–Litterman 1999; Walters' survey); non-Gaussian and Bayesian extensions (Giacometti et al.; Martellini–Ziemann; Meucci; Pástor; Pástor–Stambaugh) (pp. 1–2); and the inverse-optimization lineage (Heuberger's survey; Ahuja–Orlin through LP duality; Iyengar–Kang for conic programs) (p. 2). Reverse optimization (He–Litterman; Herold; Da Silva et al.) is said to rely on ad hoc arguments (p. 2). Claims: the first rigorous inverse-optimization interpretation of BL, unifying several threads (Herold; Krishnan–Mains; Martellini–Ziemann) (p. 2); Herold's results are re-derived as a special case (p. 6). An endnote corrects the record: BL relied on Theil's mixed estimation, and the authors do not know who first read it as a Bayesian update (p. 14). Self-citations: 3 of 35 references (Bertsimas & Brown 2009; S003; Bertsimas, Lauprete & Samarov 2004) (p. 15).
  - Transferable technique: show that the incumbent is a special case of your formulation, then list the separate threads it unifies (pp. 2, 6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: "The key insight is …" appears in both the abstract and the introduction (pp. 1–2). One running example, a 10-sector table that gains a column for each new estimator, is carried through the paper (Table 1, pp. 4–9). The contributions are numbered (p. 2). §5 opens with its key insights as bullets before the experiments (p. 10). Over-reading is explicitly warned against ("One might be tempted to infer … This inference, though, heavily depends on the choice of out-of-sample window", p. 12). The conclusion is hedged ("Computational evidence suggests", p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: carry one running numerical example through every section and add one column per new estimator to the same table (Table 1, pp. 4–9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: contributions: a reformulation (the IO reading of BL, which generalizes it), two new estimators and computational evidence (pp. 2, 14). Stated limits: the general RMV-IO system cannot be solved without fixing parameters (p. 9); exogenous scale parameters (pp. 3, 9); BL wins with accurate views (p. 13); no advantage when the view lies in the factor space (p. 12); window-dependent inferences (pp. 12, 14). Future: shrinkage-based IO estimators (Ledoit–Wolf), a data-driven CVaR estimator, and the properties of IO estimators under specific view-generation mechanisms (p. 14). Not stated by the authors: a single static view over about 11.5 years, 10 sectors only, no transaction costs.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a central model owned by another tradition, the statistical (Bayesian) BL estimator used in industry, is rewritten exactly as a convex (LMI/SDP) problem that recovers the original as a special case, and the structure is read off KKT and conic duality (pp. 2, 5, 8).
- Heuristic 1 ⚠ variant (an inverse rather than a robust problem): BL works well in practice (pp. 1, 3), so the authors find the optimization problem it solves exactly and extend it from there (pp. 5–9); the robust problem appears only in the extension (19) (p. 7).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the duel against the incumbent at matched complexity (factor-structured BL covariance; return views only) and the report of where the method wins (risk control; wrong or uncertain views) and where it loses (accurate views) are there (pp. 9–13), but with no MIO, warm start or certificate.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the incumbent is the industry's standard estimator rather than a partner's current rule; decisions are scored by portfolio return, risk and Sharpe ratio in simulation and backtest; there is no partner or field test (pp. 1, 9–14). Industry funding (Citigroup) is disclosed (p. 14).
- Method 6 ✅ weak evidence: inverse optimization is presented as a research programme across fields, carried by a PhD student (p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *nest-and-recover*: prove that the incumbent is the optimum of your convex formulation under a restriction, then relax the restriction (Proposition 2(b), pp. 5–6); compare S026 (pp. 4, 6).
- New-pattern candidate: *out-of-model stress test*: wrong views held with rising confidence, a perturbed covariance, shifted backtest windows (pp. 10–14); compare S003 (arXiv pp. 6–7) and S006 (pp. 5, 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
