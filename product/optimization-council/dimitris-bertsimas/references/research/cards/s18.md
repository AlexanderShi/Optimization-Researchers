### S309 · Global flood prediction: a multimodal machine learning approach (arXiv preprint 2023, arXiv 2301.12548)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–6 (abstract, §§1–5, Table 1, references, Appendix A). First-page check: the title and the authors Cynthia Zeng and Dimitris Bertsimas (both MIT) match (p. 1). The file is arXiv v1 of 29 Jan 2023 (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Cynthia Zeng (MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the multimodal-AI and climate strand of 2019–2026 (SKILL Research Trajectory), in a six-page paper. The same student co-authors S384 (S384 p. 1), and S338 names this paper as a future application of adaptive multimodal feature selection (S338 p. 35).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; AP (natural disasters)}: Floods are costly, and climate change is raising their frequency and scale (p. 1). Physical hydrological models dominate short-term prediction but lose forecasting power at long horizons, because simulation errors escalate. Earlier ML work used a single modality: rainfall, water levels or remote sensing (p. 1). Entry: predict whether a 1° × 1° grid cell floods within the next 1, 2 or 5 years, from tabular disaster history plus the Wikipedia geography text of the location. The paper claims the first global, multi-year ML flood model and the first use of text in flood prediction (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Key idea**: The pipeline has three steps (Figure 2, pp. 2–3):
  - gather the tabular disaster history and the Wikipedia text (p. 2);
  - embed each modality separately (pp. 3–4): 24 yearly disaster statistics plus the year; DistilBERT paragraph embeddings, either averaged from the second-to-last layer, or fine-tuned on a label "more than two historical floods", or taken from a frozen model with a trained (796, 32) sigmoid layer;
  - concatenate the embeddings and classify with XGBoost (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**: The data join GDIS (9,924 geocoded disasters, 1960–2018) to EM-DAT damage data. There are 2,852 grid cells with a recorded disaster, and text was found for 2,775 of them (Geography or Summary section, with a synonym search) (p. 2). Positives are under 0.1% of the whole data set, so prediction is restricted to the 881 cells with at least two historical floods, on the stated assumption that some locations are not flood-prone (p. 6). The train/test split is random, 70/30, and a 3-fold cross-validated grid search tunes on AUC (pp. 4, 6). Two things are not stated: whether the random split respects time, and whether the fine-tuning split (70% of the 2,852 text locations, p. 6) is aligned with the downstream test split (my reading of pp. 4, 6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a. There is no optimization model, theorem or bound, only pipeline engineering (pp. 2–4, 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Table 1 (p. 4) reports out-of-sample ROCAUC, F1, accuracy and balanced accuracy at horizons of 1, 2 and 5 years. The baseline is persistence (the next N years repeat this year's outcome), likened to the agencies' flood-risk mapping. The table's header is garbled. I read its columns as baseline | statistics only | DistilBERT | fine-tuned | transfer | two deltas, which is consistent with the text's +35% and +42% claims. On that reading, transfer-learning multimodal ROCAUC is 0.772/0.764/0.767, against 0.544/0.534/0.539 for the baseline and 0.742/0.726/0.715 for statistics only. Transfer over statistics only gains +4% to +7% in ROCAUC, but −1%, −1% and +3% in balanced accuracy. Because of the class imbalance, the baseline keeps the best plain accuracy (0.895 at 1 year), and the paper prints this (p. 4). Compute was Colab and MIT SuperCloud GPUs (p. 6); no run times are given.
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper positions itself against physical hydrology and single-modality ML (p. 1) and leads with first-ever claims (pp. 1–2). Its numbers are internally inconsistent:
  - ROCAUC is 75–77% in the abstract (p. 1) but "70% - 75%" in §4 (p. 4);
  - the study has 818 cells on p. 4 but 881 on p. 6;
  - the text cites Table 1 as "Table 4" (p. 4);
  - the text says pre-trained and fine-tuned DistilBERT do not improve on statistics only (p. 4), yet the fine-tuned column has the higher ROCAUC at all three horizons (my reading of Table 1).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The stated contributions are the multimodal framework, the ROCAUC results, and a claim that the framework generalizes to wildfires, earthquakes, droughts and extreme weather (p. 2). No limitation is stated; the conclusion restates the results (p. 4). The decision that the forecasts would serve is named only as long-term planning in disaster management (p. 1, p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 4): the baseline imitates the agencies' current rule (persistence, compared with FEMA-style risk mapping). There is no owner, no decision and no decision metric, and predictions are scored by ROCAUC. This is Taste warning sign 4 (prediction accuracy with no decision value) in the group's own output.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (p. 2; S338 p. 35; S384 p. 1): a student's sustainability strand. The framework is pitched for other disasters (p. 2) and taken up by the same cohort: Zeng co-authors S384, and Bertsimas and Boussioux cite this paper as a next application (S338 p. 35).
- new-pattern candidate: **multimodal = per-modality embeddings concatenated into one tabular learner** (pp. 2–3). S338 p. 35 describes the same recipe for two other group works (Soenksen et al. 2022; Bertsimas et al. 2022).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use the encyclopedia text of each location (the Wikipedia Geography section, with a synonym search and a Summary fallback) as a cheap static modality. Embed it with a frozen language model plus a small trained projection, and test it against statistics-only features (pp. 2–4).
- Report the gain of the added modality over the single-modality model in its own delta column, not only the gain over the baseline. This makes the marginal value of the new data source visible (Table 1, p. 4).
- Keep the agencies' persistence rule as the baseline. Report imbalance-robust metrics (ROCAUC, balanced accuracy) beside plain accuracy, and print the metric on which the baseline still wins (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "This approach aims to mark previously flooded region as high risk, which is similar to the flood risk mapping procedure employed by agencies such as FEMA." (p. 4)
- "This filtering criterion is based on the assumption that some grid locations are not prone to flooding risk." (p. 6)

### S338 · Ensemble modeling for time series forecasting: an adaptive robust optimization approach (arXiv preprint 2023, arXiv 2304.04308)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–36 (abstract; §§1–6, including Theorem 1, the proof of Lemma 1, Corollary 1 and Algorithm 1; all synthetic and real-world experiments; the conclusion) and Appendix A, pp. 41–43. The references (pp. 37–41) were skimmed. First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Léonard Boussioux (corresponding author, MIT ORC) match (p. 1). The file is arXiv v1 of 9 Apr 2023, in JMLR submission format (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Léonard Boussioux (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: robust-optimization machinery (adaptive robust optimization and decision rules, citing Bertsimas and den Hertog 2022, p. 2) carried into ML forecasting within the sustainability strand of 2019–2026. The data come from the group's Safi deployment (S384) and from Boussioux's Hurricast models (pp. 27–29, 33).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+RO; AP (pollution, energy, tropical cyclones)}: No forecasting method wins consistently, because model drift changes each method's accuracy over time (p. 2). Fixed weighted ensembles suffer under drift, and dynamic ensemblers (bandit methods such as Exp3, passive-aggressive) minimize regret but do not necessarily enforce robustness, which can lead to critical errors (pp. 2, 4–5). Entry: treat the ensemble weights as the wait-and-see decisions of a multi-stage robust problem (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea**: A time-varying linear ensemble y_t ≈ X_tᵀβ_t (pp. 6–7) is stacked into a block-diagonal compact form (eq. 3, p. 8) and robustified against perturbations Δ of the forecast matrix (eq. 4, p. 8). The weights follow an affine decision rule β_t = β0 + V0 Z_t. Z_t stacks the members' errors over the last τ steps, which serve as the proxy for the revealed uncertainties (pp. 9–10). With norm-ball uncertainty sets the min–max equals a regularized regression, called Adaptive Ridge (p = q = 2) or Adaptive Lasso (p = 2, q = 1) (Theorem 1, p. 12; Corollary 1, p. 13). λ and τ are chosen by chronological validation (Algorithm 1, p. 15).
- **D3 Model and data**: The model assumes T regularly spaced forecasts and targets, all observed, at any lead time (p. 7). Uncertainty sets have the form {‖Δ‖ ≤ λ} with λ fixed (p. 11). The members must be trained on data disjoint from the ensemble's (p. 14). Data:
  - synthetic: a sine signal (4,000 steps, period 500) with m members whose error means and spreads are random and drift, either linearly or by Bernoulli switches (pp. 17, 21–22);
  - Safi hourly wind-speed forecasts from 6 models (8,494 forecasts, 2019–2022) (p. 29);
  - UCI appliance energy, with 10 linear models selected from Lazy Predict (pp. 31–32);
  - 24-hour tropical-cyclone intensity forecasts from 7 members (870 North Atlantic and 849 Eastern Pacific cases) (pp. 33–34).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The equivalence applies the Bertsimas–Copenhaver lemma, max over Δ ∈ U(h,g) of g(z + Δβ) = g(z) + λh(β). Its proof is reproduced with a rank-one worst-case perturbation λ z vᵀ/g(z) (pp. 12–13). Xu et al.'s robust-regression theorem covers the Frobenius sets (p. 13). The compact block-diagonal form is what lets these known equivalences apply (p. 11). My reading of pp. 9–11: Z_t is built from observed past errors, so Ω_adapt depends only on (X, y, τ). The rule therefore adapts to data rather than to the adversary's Δ, and the min–max is a static robust regression in (β0, V0), whose regularizer acts on the stacked time-varying weights β (eq. 9, p. 11). No generalization or regret bound is given.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol (pp. 16–19): synthetic runs repeat over 30 seeds and report MAE, RMSE, MAPE and CVaR at 5% and 15%. Synthetic splits are chronological, 50/25/25. Baselines are the best member in hindsight (chosen on test MAPE), the ensemble mean, Exp3, passive-aggressive and ridge. Software and hardware: Julia 1.6, JuMP, Gurobi, 4 Xeon Platinum 8260 cores.
  - Synthetic results: adaptive ridge is best at every ensemble size, with gains flattening after about 15 members (pp. 20–21), and keeps its lead under drift (p. 21). It loses to passive-aggressive and ridge below about 500 training samples (p. 24). Its best window is τ = 3–4 (p. 24).
  - Safi (Table 2, pp. 29–30): adaptive ridge beats the best member in hindsight by 8% in MAE, 17% in RMSE and 26%/14% in CVaR.
  - Energy (Table 3, p. 32): 15% in MAE, 26% in RMSE and 27%/28% in CVaR, but MAPE is slightly worse (23.5 vs 23.2).
  - Cyclones (Table 5, p. 35): adaptive ridge beats the official NHC forecast OFCL by 12%/17% in MAE and 5%/21% in CVaR 15% (North Atlantic/Eastern Pacific). Ridge is slightly better on North Atlantic CVaR.
  - Training time is 0.4 s with 100 samples and 239 s with 3,000, and 1,361 s at τ = 25 (Appendix Tables 6–7, pp. 41–42).
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper positions itself against static linear ensembles and regret-based dynamic ensemblers (pp. 3–5), and builds on Bertsimas–Copenhaver and Bertsimas–den Hertog (pp. 2, 10). The abstract leads with numbers (16–26% and 14–28%, p. 1), and the synthetic section is framed as guidance for practitioners (p. 14). Small slips: σ_k is drawn from Uniform(−0.5, 0.5) on p. 17, but Table 9 gives [0, 0.5] (p. 43); the text puts Exp3's MAPE at 27.6%, but Table 3 shows 27.1 (p. 32); and "36" and "30" models are both reported as trained (p. 32).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are the method, the robustness equivalence, a hyperparameter study and three applications (p. 3). The limits are concrete:
  - the method needs a training set of some size; the paper recommends a few hundred points (p. 36);
  - it overfits with a large τ or little data (pp. 24, 26–27);
  - correlated members may need pre-selection and a sum-to-one constraint (p. 30).

  Future directions: adaptive feature selection in multimodal models (p. 35) and other time-series domains (p. 36).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 8–13, 19, 36): a learning problem owned by forecasting is written as an adaptive robust optimization problem and kept in class. With an affine rule, the min–max becomes a convex regularized regression solved with JuMP and Gurobi. Heuristic 1 is visible as well: ridge and lasso ensembles are reread as robust regressions (pp. 10–12).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 18–19, 24, 26, 32, 35): the duel against the incumbent ensemblers runs on identical chronological splits, against an oracle opponent (the best member in hindsight), with software and cores stated. The regimes where the method loses are printed. The problem is convex, so the MIO, warm-start and certificate steps do not arise.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 27, 33–35): in the cyclone case the opponents include the NHC's operational consensus forecasts (OFCL, FSSE), the owner's current rule, and the Safi case is motivated by production shutdowns. Scoring stays on forecast error, though, with CVaR as a proxy for the worst decisions (p. 35); no decision is scored.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 27–29, 33, 35, 37, 41): the cohort's own deployments supply the data (Safi forecasts from Bertsimas et al. 2023, which the reference list identifies as S384, p. 37; Hurricast forecasts from Boussioux et al. 2022), and the next application is named (Zeng and Bertsimas 2023 = S309, reference list p. 41).
- new-pattern candidate: **model weights as wait-and-see decisions** (adaptive-robust-optimization decision rules inside learning) (pp. 2–3, 9–10).
- new-pattern candidate: **a methods paper built on the forecast archive of the group's own deployment** (pp. 27–29, 33).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To robustify a time-varying regression, stack the per-period coefficient vectors into one block-diagonal design X̃. Published robust ↔ regularized equivalences then apply verbatim (pp. 8, 11–13).
- Make the coefficients an affine function of the members' last τ errors (β_t = β0 + V0 Z_t). The problem stays convex in (β0, V0), and weights may turn negative to correct member bias (pp. 9–10, 27, 30).
- Benchmark an ensembler against the best member in hindsight, chosen on the test set (an oracle no online method can match), and against the operational consensus forecast where one exists (pp. 18, 34).
- When the worst errors drive decisions, report CVaR of the absolute error beside MAE and RMSE. Its discrete form is a small optimization problem that the authors solve in Julia (pp. 16, 35).
- Sweep the training-set size to find the regime where the method loses, and state the data requirement in the conclusion (pp. 24, 36).

**Quotes**:
- "This is convenient for the practitioner as a min-max formulation can be converted to a minimization problem with a regularizer on the variables." (p. 10)
- "Adaptive ridge is not suitable when there is little data available." (p. 26)

### S369 · Improving stability in decision tree models (arXiv preprint 2023, arXiv 2305.17299)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–29 (abstract, §§1–5, Figures 1–7, Tables 1–3, conclusion, acknowledgements) and the proofs in Appendix A plus Appendix B, pp. 34–37. The references (pp. 29–34) were not read. First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Vassilis Digalakis Jr (MIT) match (p. 1). The file is arXiv v1 of 26 May 2023, headed "Article submitted" (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Vassilis Digalakis Jr (MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the Optimal Trees programme (SW4) continued in the health-care setting of 2019–2026. It follows the same pair's work on optimal trees with feature selection (p. 3) and uses data from MGH, Hartford Hospital and MSK (p. 22). Acknowledged: Yu Ma, Agni Orfanoudaki and Wolfram Wiesemann (p. 29).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; AP (health care)}: The instability of decision trees is well documented but has received less work than their accuracy and robustness (p. 1, pp. 2–4). Example 1: when a model is retrained as more patients are recorded, it can change notably, which harms physicians' trust and adoption (p. 2). The stated target is Breiman's 1996 question whether a more stable single-tree version of CART exists (p. 4). Existing measures are semantic, are structural summaries, or are edit distances that treat logically equivalent trees as different (pp. 4–5).
- **D2 Key idea**: There are two parts, a distance and a training method.
  - **Distance**: a tree is a set of paths, each given by its feature bounds, category sets and label, so the order of splits is irrelevant (pp. 8–9). A path distance (normalized differences of ranges, plus λ times a label mismatch, with λ = 2D balancing the two) and a path weight are defined (pp. 9–10). The tree distance is a minimum-cost matching of paths in which unmatched paths pay their weight (ILP (3), p. 11). It is a metric (Proposition 1, p. 11), it is solvable as an LP (Corollary 1, p. 12), and it is normalized by the bound 2^D(2D + λ) (Proposition 2, p. 12).
  - **Training**: split the training data into X0 and the full X. On each, bootstrap many CART trees with depth 3–12 and minimum leaf sizes {3, 5, 10, 30, 50}. Score each tree trained on X by its mean distance to the X0 trees and by its holdout AUC. Keep the Pareto frontier, and pick from it with an application function, for example the most stable tree within ε of the best AUC (pp. 15–16).
- **D3 Model and data**: Classification with numerical and categorical features over bounded ranges (p. 8). The trees are axis-parallel CART trees from scikit-learn (p. 7). The method assumes that stability is negatively correlated with the distance, so a "centroid" tree is likely to be stable (p. 16). Data: six health case studies. Five come from MGH, Hartford Hospital and MSK (930–35,954 patients, 17–42 features, outcome prevalence 1.6–31%), and one is the UCI breast-cancer set (Table 2, p. 22). Splits are 67/33, repeated 10 times (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - The metric property comes from a path-level metric (an ℓ1-type decomposition plus a case analysis on labels) lifted to trees through the matched paths (Lemma 1, Proposition 1, pp. 34–35).
  - Integrality comes from total unimodularity of the assignment constraints after padding with dummy paths (pp. 35–36).
  - The normalizing bound comes from constructing two maximally distant trees (p. 36).
  - My reading of pp. 9 and 35: distinct trees with the same path collection are at distance zero (p. 9), so the metric is a metric on path collections, that is, on trees up to the order of their splits.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Pareto frontiers are clear in 4 of the 6 studies. A frontier has at most 9 trees, about 5 on average (pp. 16–17).
  - The AUC-maximizing Pareto tree beats CART tuned by cross-validation and the most stable Pareto tree in every study, and beats random forest in Sarcoma and TAVR (p. 18).
  - Mean distance stays at or below 5% of the maximum, except in breast cancer (p. 19). In 5 of 6 studies the Pareto trees are half as deep as random forest's largest tree and have 2–10× fewer nodes (p. 19).
  - In aggregate, AUC is 0.800 against 0.763 and distance 4.056 against 2.511, a 38% stability gain for 4.6% of AUC (Table 1, p. 21). In the sarcoma retraining example the distance is 11.21% with cross-validation and 0.18% with the Pareto method (p. 13).
  - Software and hardware: Gurobi 9.5, Python 3.7, Xeon E5-2690 (p. 7). No run times are given, and "significantly" is used without a statistical test (p. 18, my reading).
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper opens with Breiman's grades for trees (A+ on interpretability, B on prediction) and closes by answering his question: such a tree may exist (pp. 2, 28). It positions itself against stabilization by averaging (random forests, boosting) and against heuristic tweaks (p. 4). One claim over-reaches: the most stable Pareto tree beats CART with cross-validation in mean AUC in only 3 of 6 studies (4 of 6 in standard deviation), yet the text concludes that it "strictly dominates" (p. 18).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are the distance metric, the stable-training method and six case studies (p. 6). Printed limits:
  - stabilization by averaging still reaches higher stability (p. 28);
  - the normalizing bound is conservative, which keeps the distance percentages low (pp. 14, 37).

  Future directions: the distance as a regularizer in tree training (p. 27), and a three-dimensional Pareto frontier that adds an interpretability score (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 15–17, 21): the property is stability, wanted by physicians who must trust retrained models (p. 2). Each data set gets a curve, its Pareto frontier (Figure 2, p. 17), and the selection knob is the tolerance ε (p. 16). The price is printed in the paper's own words, "The price of a 38% improvement in stability" (p. 21), and so are the flat-frontier cases (p. 16). An empirical frontier takes the place of a solution-independent bound.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 11–12, 36): the distance is written as an assignment-type ILP whose LP relaxation is integral, so a polynomial-time LP computes it.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3–4, 7, 18): the trees are heuristic CART trees from scikit-learn, not MIO trees. The incumbents (CART with cross-validation, random forest) are the opponents, and there is no gap or certificate. The paper credits optimal trees with more stability (p. 3) but makes its own method algorithm-agnostic (p. 4).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 12, 22): the owner is the physician, the hospital partners supply the data, and the current rule (retraining with 5-fold cross-validation) is the baseline. But no decision is scored, only AUC and tree structure.
- new-pattern candidate: **retraining stability as the distance to models fitted on an earlier batch of data** (pp. 14–16).
- new-pattern candidate: **structural model distance as an optimal assignment that the LP solves exactly** (pp. 11–12, 36).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure the stability of a model trained on all the data as its mean distance to a bootstrap collection trained on an earlier subset. This mimics retraining as data grow (pp. 15–16).
- Define a distance between combinatorial objects as a minimum-cost assignment of their parts, with unmatched parts paying a weight. Pad with dummy parts to get total unimodularity, and an LP then solves it (pp. 11–12, 36).
- Normalize a distance by a provable worst-case bound to report it as a percentage, and say when that bound is conservative (pp. 12, 14, 37).
- Select from the Pareto frontier with an explicit application function (for example, the most stable model within ε of the best AUC) instead of cross-validation's single score (p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "The price of a 38% improvement in stability is, on average, 0.04 (or 4.625%) in AUC." (p. 21)
- "The proposed methodology relies on the assumption that decision trees’ stability is negatively correlated with the proposed tree distance metric." (p. 16)

### S383 · Interpretable matrix completion: A discrete optimization approach (INFORMS Journal on Computing 35(5) 2023, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2022.0022)
Read level: **full** (arXiv v3): main text pp. 1–23 (abstract, §§1–6, Theorems 1–4, Proposition 1, Algorithms 1–2, Tables 1–3, conclusion), plus the opening of the proof of Theorem 2 (pp. 24–25) and the proof skeleton of Theorem 4 (p. 34). Appendices B, C and E were not read. First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC) match (p. 1). The file is arXiv 1812.06647 v3 of 3 Mar 2020, headed "Submitted to Operations Research" (p. 1); the IJOC 2023 print was not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the ML programme under a modern optimization lens (SW4). The binary convex reformulation of Bertsimas and van Parys's sparse regression (2020) is carried to matrix completion (pp. 4, 6). The paper was first posted in 2018 (arXiv id) and printed in 2023.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+IO}: The latent features of matrix completion cannot be interpreted. Inductive matrix completion makes them linear combinations of known features, which are still hard to read: their units are mixed, and the same feature can enter twice with opposite signs (pp. 2–3). Entry: require the k latent features to be *selected* from the p known features (pp. 3–4), which makes the problem discrete (p. 4). - **D2 Key idea**:
  - Problem (4): squared loss on the observed entries plus a Tikhonov term (1/γ)‖U‖², with X = U S Bᵀ, where S is a diagonal 0/1 selection with k ones (pp. 6–7).
  - Theorem 1: minimizing out U row by row in closed form gives a binary convex problem min_s c(s) (pp. 7–8).
  - CutPlanes: outer approximation (Duran–Grossmann) started from a heuristic warm start; each iteration solves an MILP over s (pp. 8–10). With Woodbury's identity a cut costs O(|Ω|(p + k²) + nk³) (pp. 10–11).
  - OptComplete: cuts built from random subsamples of g rows and f columns, at a cost of O(k√(mn) log √(mn) (p + k²)) (pp. 12–17).
  - The two-sided case reduces to sparse regression (pp. 11–12).
- **D3 Model and data**: The rank k is small compared with p, and the product features B are known. The loss is squared, with a ridge term added "for robustness purposes" (p. 7). Theorem 4 assumes feasibility and uses a convexity parameter a, which is positive when no two supports tie in cost (Proposition 1, pp. 15–16). Synthetic data: A = UV + E, with U and V uniform on [0, 1] and E ~ N(0, 0.01), and B formed by V plus p − k confounding rows (p. 17). The side information is thus exactly predictive by construction (my reading). Netflix: 471,268 users × 14,538 movies, with 59 TMDB features (p. 21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Theorems 2–4 build the guarantee in three steps.
  - Theorem 2: Hoeffding's inequality for sampling without replacement bounds the subsampled α_i and gradient terms (pp. 13–14, 24–25).
  - Theorem 3: the same bound holds at the level of the cost, and it depends on g, not on f (p. 15).
  - Theorem 4: outer approximation terminates in C steps (Fletcher–Leyffer). It fails only if an approximate cut cuts off the optimal support s*, which the convexity slack a²θ²/2 together with Theorem 3 makes exponentially unlikely: the success probability is at least 1 − kC·exp(−D a⁴ g/(p + k)) (pp. 16, 34).

  My reading of pp. 13–16: the paper says the theorem "requires no assumptions on the data" (p. 16), yet the guarantee weakens with the data-dependent parameter a, and C is not known in advance. Algorithm 2 also samples with replacement (p. 14), while Theorems 2–3 assume sampling without replacement (pp. 13, 15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol (pp. 17–19): Gurobi 8.1.0 on 16 cores; 10 runs per setting, medians reported; MAPE on the missing entries. Baselines: IMC (Matlab 2018b), SoftImpute-ALS (SIALS; R 3.4.4) and CutPlanes. Time limit 20 h.

- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper positions itself against nuclear-norm and alternating methods for general completion, and against inductive completion that assumes perfectly predictive side information (pp. 5–6). It names its approach "interpretable, as opposed to inductive" (p. 4). The conclusion is two sentences and claims to exceed the benchmarks on both scalability and accuracy (p. 23).

- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3–4, 6–12): a problem owned by statistics and recommender systems is written exactly as a binary convex program (Theorem 1) and solved as a sequence of MILPs by outer approximation in Gurobi. The two-sided variant reduces to the group's sparse regression (pp. 11–12).

- new-pattern candidate: **stochastic cutting planes**: outer approximation with subsampled cuts and a high-probability optimality guarantee (pp. 12–16).
- new-pattern candidate: **interpretability as the selection of named features, not their combination** (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When latent factors must be readable, replace *a linear combination of features* by *a selection of k features*. Rescaling U absorbs the magnitudes in S, so S can be binary without loss of generality (pp. 3–4, 7).
- Eliminate the continuous variables in closed form (a ridge regression for each row) to get a binary convex objective, then solve it by outer approximation with an MILP master problem (pp. 7–10).
- Use the Woodbury identity to cut the cost of each cut from an m × m inverse to a k × k inverse (pp. 10–11).
- When full cuts are too expensive, build them from subsamples of rows and columns. Bound the failure probability by concentration, a slack of strong-convexity type, and a union bound over the iterations (pp. 13–16, 34).
- Size the subsamples from the sample complexity of completion (Candès–Tao, O(kN log N)) and fix the constants empirically (g0 = 100, c = 1) (pp. 16–17).

It builds on the mixed-projection and matrix-perspective papers of Bertsimas et al. 2022 and 2023 (pp. 2–3, 6). - **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+CP+IO}: Matrix completion is solved by heuristics without an instance-wise certificate (abstract, p. 1). Low-rank problems cannot be modelled as mixed-integer conic programs, so off-the-shelf solvers do not apply (p. 2). The previous exact method solved only n = m = 50 with k = 1, and left gaps above 100% for n ≥ 60 or k > 1 (pp. 2, 4). - **D2 Key idea**:
  - **Formulation**: the mixed-projection formulation models rank through a projection matrix Y with tr(Y) ≤ k and a matrix-perspective constraint (eq. 2, p. 6). Its SDP relaxation runs over conv(Y), with the lifted constraint Y ⪰ UUᵀ (eq. 3, p. 6).
  - **Branching** on eigenvector disjunctions: at a fractional Y, take the most negative eigenvector x of UUᵀ − Y and impose the reverse inequality piece by piece, through a piecewise-linear upper approximation of u². This gives 2^k child regions, or q^k with q pieces (eq. 6, pp. 8–10).
  - **Incumbents** come from alternating minimization (Burer–Monteiro) run at the nodes, started from a truncated SVD or from the SVD of the relaxed Y (pp. 14–15).

All data are synthetic: A = UV + 0.1Z with Gaussian factors, and an observed set with at least one entry in each row and column, of size proportional to n log n (pp. 19–20). Sizes: square n ≤ 100 at the root and n ≤ 50 in the design tests; for scale, rectangular 50 × m with m ≤ 2,500 and k ≤ 5 (pp. 20, 23).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Proposition 1: McCormick disjunctions over fewer than 2^(n−4) regions leave the root bound unchanged (p. 7).
  - Proposition 2: an eigenvector disjunction separates any fractional optimum of the relaxation (p. 9).
  - Theorem 1: the method converges finitely to an ε-feasible "superoptimal" solution. The proof is a packing argument: the iterates never revisit a ball of radius ε/2 in a weighted norm, and the feasible set is bounded (pp. 11–12).
  - Proposition 3: the minors-based SDP is at least as strong as the perspective relaxation (pp. 16–17).
  - Remark 1 admits that the lifting creates symmetry under permutations of U's columns (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Julia 1.9, Mosek 10.0, Xeon Platinum 8260 (MIT Supercloud) (p. 18). Code and experiments are on GitHub (p. 19).
  - Root node: Shor LMIs on the minors cut the median rank-one root gap from about 10⁻² to 10⁻⁴ for n ≥ 50, at about 10× the time (p. 20).
  - Design tests (1-hour limit, 20 instances, n ≤ 50, k = 1; Tables 1–2, pp. 20–22): eigenvector disjunctions give final gaps about one order of magnitude smaller than McCormick disjunctions. Alternating minimization at the child nodes gains another order, and best-first search wins.
  - Gurobi's nonconvex QCQP solver and the earlier multi-tree method were not competitive (p. 22).
  - Scale tests (3-hour limit, 10 instances; pp. 23–24): gaps shrink at every (m, k), mostly for k = 1. For k = 2 the root gap falls from 38% (m = 100) to 9% (m = 2,500).
  - Out-of-sample MSE is 1–10% better than alternating minimization at the root, most at small m (pp. 24–25).
  - Memory grows as O(K·min{m, n}²) in the number K of nodes explored (p. 26).
  - No real data set is used.
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper positions itself against commercial solvers built on McCormick disjunctions ("challenges the ongoing practice", p. 7) and against the guarantees for Burer–Monteiro methods (p. 3). It builds on Saxena et al.'s eigenvector cuts but uses them for branching instead (pp. 3–4, 8). Its scale claims are hedged ("certifiable optimality or near optimality", p. 1). The test-error reduction is "2%–50%" in the abstract (p. 1), "1%–50%" in §5.5 and the conclusion (pp. 26–27), and "consistently in the 1-10% range" in §5.4 (p. 25).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are eigenvector branching, a complete branch-and-bound, and minors-based relaxations with valid inequalities (p. 4). Printed limits:
  - the relaxations' benefit depends on the rank and on the number of observed entries; future work should find scalable relaxations for k > 1 (p. 25);
  - memory (p. 26);
  - one fixed time limit may make the gains look smaller at larger k and m, and the paper knows no fair alternative (p. 24);
  - the advantage of q = 4 pieces vanishes as n grows (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 14–15, 18–26): one global model replaces the local heuristic, and the heuristic serves both as the warm start at the nodes and as the out-of-sample opponent. **Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 6, 9–11, 18–19): a control problem owned by optimal control and queueing theory is analysed through its dual (costate) variables, and the structure read off them (hyperplanes) chooses the model class (OCT-H), so that the learned object contains the optimum. The control problem itself is not reformulated as an LP, conic or MIO problem. The boundary is printed (pp. 18–19). Heuristic 5 is in plain view: the problem is solved offline, the optimizer's strategy is learned, and the policy is applied online in milliseconds (pp. 2–3, 19–21).
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 23–27): two properties are priced. Interpretability uses the sparsity knob ("the price we have to pay", p. 23) and a table of accuracy and training time (p. 24). Robustness uses Γ: the nominal loss is set against the gain under noise, including the bad case (criss-cross at nominal parameters). Γ is fixed by hand (0.1 and 0.25, p. 25), the situation the SKILL's Method 3 correction describes (*knobs get fixed arbitrarily*).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 4, 8, 10, 20): the claims of *exact* and *optimal* rest on representability and 100% test accuracy, not on a certificate for the trained tree. The opponent is the exact SCLP solver, compared on speed, not a heuristic. This is Tension T1.
- new-pattern candidate: **structure theorem first, then a learner whose hypothesis class contains the optimal policy** (pp. 9–11).
- new-pattern candidate: **training data generated and augmented through the problem's invariances** (scale invariance) (pp. 6, 11–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before learning a policy, prove its structural form from the optimality conditions (here, PMP costates give hyperplanes through the origin), and choose a learner that represents that form exactly (OCT-H) (pp. 9–10).
- Use scale invariance (u*(αx0) = u*(x0), and V(αx0) = α²V(x0)) to sample initial states only on the unit sphere and to augment the data by scaling (pp. 6, 11–12).
- Partition the state space by which classes are empty and train one tree per cell. Boundary conditions x_i = 0 become splits x_i ≤ ε (pp. 11–12, 15).
- Report the speed-up as the solver's time per instance divided by the policy's prediction time, and report the solver time itself as well (pp. 19, 29).
- Evaluate learned robust and nominal policies on perturbed instances by their maximum and mean suboptimality against the true optimum, projecting the controls to keep trajectories feasible (pp. 25–26).
