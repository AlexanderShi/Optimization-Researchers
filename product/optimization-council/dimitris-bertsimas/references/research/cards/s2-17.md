### S376 · High-performance pediatric surgical risk calculator: a novel algorithm based on machine learning and pediatric NSQIP data (The American Journal of Surgery 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.amjsurg.2023.03.009)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–7 (abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1–7, references). Appendices 1–9 are online supplements and are not in the file. First-page check: the title matches, and Dimitris Bertsimas is the first author (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Li and Nova Zhang (MIT ORC); Carlos Estrada and Hsin-Hsiao Scott Wang (Boston Children's Hospital, pediatric urology; Wang is the corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: health-care period {ML AP}.
- It is the pediatric counterpart of the group's adult emergency-surgery calculator POTTER (ref. 12; INDEX S041).
- It follows two pediatric-urology OCT papers with the same Boston Children's group (refs 10–11, p. 7).
- Received August 2022, accepted March 2023 (p. 1); no funding (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+AP}:
  - The ACS NSQIP Pediatric calculator (Kraemer et al.) and other calculators "suffer from unstable internal validation" and vary across subspecialties. Procedure-specific scores do not generalize (p. 1).
  - Entry: carry the group's OCT calculator approach (POTTER) to the pediatric NSQIP registry, "one of the largest" repositories of children's surgery (p. 2).
  - Stated hypothesis: beat the ACS NSQIP Pediatric calculator on 30-day mortality and morbidity (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Optimal classification trees for four outcomes: mortality, and any, major and minor morbidity. Missing values are filled by optimal-tree imputation (p. 2).
  - Each model is fitted on four variable sets: all variables, no ASA class, no labs, neither (p. 2).
  - A probability threshold is chosen to keep the true-positive rate while pushing the false-positive rate toward 0. The same threshold simplifies the displayed tree (p. 2).
  - The incumbent is re-implemented: logistic regression by Kraemer's method, trained on the same 2012–2017 data and tested on 2018 (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Pediatric NSQIP 2012–2018, with 133 preoperative, 65 intraoperative and 160 postoperative attributes. Preoperative variables with under 30% missing data are kept, and CPT and ICD codes are collapsed into CCS categories, leaving 74 preoperative variables (p. 2).
  - Temporal split: 431,148 training cases (2012–2017) and 108,604 test cases (2018) (pp. 1–2).
  - Deaths are rare: 1,553 among the 431,148 training cases (Table 2, p. 3).
  - Major and minor complications follow a published list (Table 1, p. 2).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a (empirical). The text asserts that OCT "is solved to global optimality" by mixed-integer optimization (p. 2). It reports no depth, complexity parameter, solver, time limit or gap. The software named is Julia 0.6.0, R 4.0.5 and SAS 9.4 (p. 2).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Test AUCs (Tables 4–7, pp. 5–6): mortality 0.94–0.96; major morbidity 0.90–0.91; any morbidity 0.86; minor morbidity 0.69–0.70.
  - Against the re-fitted logistic regression (p. 5): major 0.90 vs 0.82; any 0.86 vs 0.80; minor 0.69 vs 0.66. Mortality shows no significant difference, and the logistic AUC for mortality is not printed.
  - Dropping ASA class and labs costs little (pp. 4–5).
  - Missing [my reading]: confidence intervals for the AUC differences, although 95% CIs are named as the criterion (p. 2), and any calibration.
  - The example tree's root holds about 267,342 patients against 431,148 training cases, which is not explained (p. 4) [my reading].
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: one model for all pediatric procedures in NSQIP, and a free web page and iOS app called "Pediatric Surgical Risk" (pp. 5, 7).
  - Stated limits (pp. 6–7): registry weaknesses (coding errors, selection bias); procedures outside NSQIP; subgroups not yet tested; weak prediction of minor morbidity.
  - Not stated [my reading]: prospective or external validation; calibration; how tree depth was chosen.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 5): the incumbent's method is re-fitted on the same training years and scored on the same test year, rather than quoted from its paper (step 5).
- Method 4 · contradiction ✗ (p. 2): the trees are called globally optimal, but no certificate, gap, time limit or solver is reported (step 4; Tension T1).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 5–7): the users (families, clinicians, ICU planners) and the current tool are named, and the model ships as an app. But predictions are scored (AUC), not decisions, and no use or outcome after release is measured.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 5–7): the weak minor-morbidity model (AUC 0.69) and the tie on mortality are printed in the body.
- New-pattern candidate: *drop the costly or rater-dependent inputs and show the model holds*. ASA class is observer-dependent and labs are hard to obtain, and the trees on display are the models without labs and ASA class (pp. 2, 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- With a multi-year registry, train on the early years and test on the last one. This temporal hold-out mimics deployment (p. 2).
- Re-fit the incumbent's published method on your own training split, so both models face the same test set (p. 2).
- Fit the model on nested variable sets (all variables; without the rater-dependent score; without labs; without both), then deploy the cheapest set that holds up (pp. 2, 4–5).
- Tabulate the test cases your model catches and the incumbent misses (Appendix 9, summarized on p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "This method is similar to classic classification and regression tree; however, it is solved to global optimality with a novel method (mixed integer optimization) which provides superior performance." (p. 2)
- "To our surprise, ASA score and lab data did not play significant roles in our pediatric surgical morbidity and mortality prediction model." (p. 5)

### S377 · A novel algorithm for a broad class of nonconvex optimization problems (Optimization Online 2023, no DOI)
Read level: **partial**:
- read: pp. 1–17 and 20–38 (abstract, §§1–3, Algorithm 1, the toy problem's set-up and result, the statements of §5 with Examples 5–6, §§6–8);
- skimmed: pp. 17–20 (the toy problem's iterations) and the proofs on pp. 20–23;
- not read: references (pp. 38–42) and Appendices A–E (pp. 42–60).

First-page check: the title matches. Bertsimas is the first of five authors, listed alphabetically, and Jianzhe Zhen is the corresponding author (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Danique de Moor and Dick den Hertog (Amsterdam Business School), Thodoris Koukouvinos (MIT ORC), Jianzhe Zhen (ETH Zürich; corresponding) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the first paper of the RPT line with den Hertog's group.
- The cone-product paper continues it (S415, card in c2-08).
- Its branching idea comes from Zhen et al. (2022) on disjoint bilinear problems (pp. 14–15).
- The second author is funded by NWO (p. 38).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP+IO; RO as the source of the branching idea; HS as components}:
  - Class: nonconvex problems whose nonconvex parts are sums of products of convex functions. This covers nonconvex QPs, mixed-binary LPs, concave minimization, difference-of-convex and fractional problems (pp. 1–2).
  - Gap: RLT (Sherali–Adams) multiplies only linear or quadratic constraints (p. 2). Selvi et al. (2020) and CoMax (Ben-Tal–Roos 2022) carry no guarantee of reaching the global optimum and cannot handle nonconvex constraints (p. 3).
  - BARON and SCIP are named as the state of the art (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: RPT-BB, in five steps (p. 7):
  1. Introduce epigraph variables for the convex parts.
  2. Reformulate and perspectify (pp. 8–12):
     - multiply pairs of constraints: linear × linear as in RLT, linear × convex, and some convex × convex;
     - write each product in perspective form;
     - replace xxᵀ by a matrix U.
  3. Optionally add the LMI [U x; xᵀ 1] ⪰ 0 (p. 12).
  4. Take upper bounds from candidate points read off the relaxation: columns of U divided by x_i, and later also eigenvectors of U. Ipopt or mountain climbing then polishes them (pp. 13–14, 24).
  5. Run a spatial branch and bound (pp. 14–16):
     - the cut is a maximum-margin hyperplane through the analytic center that splits the candidate points; a small MIO finds it (problem (11), pp. 14–15);
     - the node with the lowest lower bound is expanded next (p. 16).

  The relaxation and the partitioning rule change; the branch-and-bound frame of BARON-type methods does not (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions: X is nonempty and compact (Assumption 1), with sign conditions on the factors (Assumption 2) (p. 5). Difference-of-convex terms go through the biconjugate when its infimum is attained (pp. 5–6).
  - Convergence needs Lipschitz factors and recession functions (Theorem 2). In the biconjugate case it also needs bounded conjugate domains (Proposition 1) (pp. 25–27). The paper says the linear multiplicative problems violate these conditions (p. 34).
  - Instances (pp. 29–37):
    - random instances of four convex-maximization classes, taken from Zhen et al. (2022) and Selvi et al. (2020);
    - QCQPs;
    - linear multiplicative problems in the style of Ryoo–Sahinidis;
    - dike rings 10, 15 and 16 of the Dutch dike-heightening model of Eijgenraam et al. (2017).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - A perspective is jointly convex, so each product becomes a convex constraint in (x, U) (p. 8).
  - Lemma 1: an equality times a convex constraint is redundant once Ud = qx is imposed (p. 10).
  - Theorem 1: linear constraints that are redundant to other linear constraints add nothing. The proof is LP duality (p. 20).
  - Lemma 2: for x ≥ 0 the candidate points are feasible (p. 13).
  - Convergence (pp. 25–27):
    - Adaptation A bisects the bounding box every d levels (p. 25);
    - Theorem 2 shows |U_ij − x_i x_j| ≤ 4ε(|α_j| + ε) on a box of radius ε, then passes the bound through the Lipschitz constants (pp. 25–26);
    - the counterexample c(x) = x eˣ shows that the recession condition is needed (pp. 26–27).
  - No rate or complexity bound is given [my reading].
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup (pp. 28–35):
    - one Intel i9 2.3 GHz core with 16 GB;
    - MOSEK 9.2.45, Gurobi 9.0.2, SCIP 8.0.2, BARON 20.10.16 and CPLEX 22.1.0, with the versions of Julia, JuMP, Pyomo and docplex;
    - gap 10⁻⁴ and a 3600 s limit;
    - many rows are averages over 10 random instances (Tables 3–6, pp. 30–35).
  - RPT-BB solves more instances than BARON and SCIP (p. 1).
  - Where it loses:
    - the exact MIO reformulation is fastest on most polyhedral instances (p. 29);
    - BARON is fastest on most small instances (pp. 31–33).
  - Linear multiplicative problems with 8–30 products: RPT-BB proves optimality where BARON cannot within an hour (p. 34). Table 6 gives 909 s, 305 s and 55 s, so "in seconds" fits only the last (p. 35). With 200 variables and 3 products, BARON and SCIP win (p. 35).
  - Dikes: RPT-SDP-BB solves every instance at the root in under a second (p. 37). On ring 16, plain RPT-BB leaves a larger gap than BARON (Table 9, p. 38).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: RPT, RPT-BB and the computational comparison (pp. 3–4).
  - Stated limits: multiplying all pairs of constraints makes the method "less tractable" at scale, and fewer products could be used (p. 38). Linear multiplicative problems show the trade-off (p. 35).
  - Future work: hyperplanes in the y-space (p. 16); multiplying the SLC constraints themselves (p. 38).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 7–13, 28, 35, 38): a class that global solvers (BARON, SCIP) own is relaxed, node by node, into convex conic programs that MOSEK solves (perspective, exponential and second-order cones, optional SDP). The tractability boundary is printed: all-pairs products grow too fast with many variables and constraints.
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 13–16, 28–38):
  - local solvers (Ipopt, mountain climbing) supply upper bounds inside an exact branch and bound;
  - every run states solver versions, machine, gap and time limit, and uncertified entries are starred;
  - the incumbents (BARON, SCIP, CPLEX, the exact MIO) are dueled, and their wins are printed.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 31–38): the abstract itself says that BARON is faster on small instances (p. 1).
- New-pattern candidate: *split the critical points across the children*. The branching rule reuses a lesson from robust optimization: a split that leaves all worst-case scenarios on one side does not improve the bound (pp. 14–15).
- New-pattern candidate: *show that known relaxations are special cases*. Remark 3 lists disjunctive, generalized linear and fractional optimization and the approximate S-lemma (p. 12; Appendix B not read).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Multiply a linear inequality b − aᵀx ≥ 0 by a convex constraint h(x) ≤ 0, and write the product as (b − aᵀx)·h((bx − Ua)/(b − aᵀx)) ≤ 0 with U standing for xxᵀ. This gives a valid convex cut that RLT cannot form (p. 9).
- Read primal candidates off a lifted relaxation (the columns U_i/x_i and the eigenvectors of U), and use them both as upper bounds and as warm starts for a local solver (pp. 13–14, 24).
- Branch on a hyperplane through the analytic center that separates the current candidate points with maximum margin. Interleave coordinate bisection every d levels, so that convergence can be proved (pp. 14–15, 25).
- After a convergence theorem, give a one-line counterexample that shows which assumption is needed (pp. 26–27).

**Quotes**:
- "In particular, our approach solves more instances to global optimality for the considered problems than BARON and SCIP." (p. 1)
- "Since we are multiplying all constraints in the feasible region, the method becomes less tractable in problems involving a large number of variables and constraints." (p. 38)

### S394 · Automated segmentation of sacral chordoma and surrounding muscles using deep learning ensemble (International Journal of Radiation Oncology, Biology, Physics 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.ijrobp.2023.03.078)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–11 (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion). References (pp. 11–12) were not read. The appendices with the t-tests and preprocessing details are online supplements and are not in the file. First-page check: the title matches, and Bertsimas is the fourth of ten authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors** (p. 1):
- MIT ORC and Sloan: Leonard Boussioux (corresponding; equal contribution with Ma), Yu Ma, Nancy Knight Thomas;
- MGH radiation oncology and Harvard Medical School: Nadya Shusharina, Jennifer Pursley, Yen-Lin Chen, Thomas F. DeLaney, Jack Qian and Thomas Bortfeld. Qian and Bortfeld jointly supervised the study.

**Position in trajectory**: an imaging work of the health-care period, led by his former student Boussioux (now at the University of Washington) and funded by an NCI grant (R01CA266275) (p. 1). Bertsimas is not the first, corresponding or supervising author, so this card is weak evidence of his own practice.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+AP}:
  - Manual contouring for radiotherapy planning is slow and needs experts; rare tumors such as sacral chordomas are the hardest case (p. 1).
  - Soft-tissue segmentation is little studied, and deep-learning evaluations are "often not pushed enough" for clinical use (p. 2).
  - Problem: segment the gross tumor volume (GTV) and five muscle groups automatically. The muscles are paths along which the tumor spreads, so this is a step toward automating the clinical target volume (pp. 2, 11).
- **D2 Core idea**: a pool of six 3D U-Net models (standard and residual architectures, each with Dice, cross-entropy and weighted cross-entropy loss) is combined in two ways (pp. 2, 4–5):
  - a voxel-wise average;
  - an "optimal ensemble", whose weights on the simplex maximize the summed validation IoU of the eight structures.

  An independent oncologist then times the edits needed for clinical use, without knowing which contour is which (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - 48 consecutive MGH patients treated with definitive proton therapy, 1999–2019 (p. 2). The GTVs come from six oncologists over 20 years; one oncologist re-contoured the muscles for the study (p. 3).
  - Split: 30 training, 5 validation and 8 test patients. Five patients whose piriformis the tumor had obliterated form a separate outlier test set; their GTVs average 1622 cm³ against 378 cm³ (p. 3).
  - Preprocessing: resampled to 2 mm voxels, cropped and padded to 182 × 157 × 216, with left and right muscles merged (p. 3).
  - The research data are not available (p. 1).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - The ensemble objective is not convex, so the weights are found by Adam on the validation predictions (learning rate 10⁻³, tolerance 10⁻⁴). Four models keep nonzero weights: 0.2, 0.2, 0.28 and 0.32 (p. 5). No guarantee is claimed.
  - Models are selected on IoU and tested on other metrics: volumetric Dice, surface Dice at 2 and 3 mm, and the average symmetric surface distance (ASSD). The point is not to reward overfitting to the selection metric (p. 5).
  - The 2 and 3 mm tolerances follow from the 2 mm resolution and from clinical precision (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - On the 8 test patients the ensembles have higher averages and lower variance than every standalone model. They have the better minimum in 31 of 36 (Dice) and 35 of 36 (ASSD) model–structure pairs (p. 6).
  - The optimal ensemble improves on the best standalone model by 1.7% in volumetric Dice and 14% in ASSD (p. 6; Table 2, p. 7).
  - On the 5 outliers the ensembles no longer win systematically (p. 9).
  - Blinded review of 5 test patients: GTV editing takes 6 minutes for the ground truth, 6 for the best standalone model and 11 for the optimal ensemble (Table 4, p. 9). The abstract's 19-minute total belongs to the best standalone model and equals the ground truth's total (pp. 1, 9) [my arithmetic].
  - Training took about 2 days on a Tesla V100 with 4 CPU cores; inference takes under 1 minute per patient (p. 4).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a four-item list: the model pool, the two ensembles, the four metrics and the qualitative review (p. 2).
  - Stated limits (pp. 10–11):
    - the optimal ensemble's GTVs need more editing than the best single model's; the authors conjecture this comes from flaws in the ground truth;
    - small data for a rare tumor, and GTVs contoured in mixed styles;
    - weaker results on very large tumors;
    - no dosimetric evaluation, so the geometric analysis is called a worst case;
    - CT images only.
  - Future work: MRI as extra input channels, more pelvic structures, delineation of the clinical target volume (pp. 10–11).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · contradiction ✗ (p. 5): "optimal ensemble" names a local Adam solution of a nonconvex objective, with no certificate (step 4; Workflow E's correction on *optimal* in names and titles).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 6, 9–10):
  - in line with it: the clinicians' current practice (manual contouring, reported as 40 minutes for muscles and 10–60 minutes for a GTV) frames the benefit, and a blinded expert's editing time is a decision-relevant metric;
  - short of it: the time saved is taken from reported durations, not measured against the current workflow on the same patients, and no treatment-plan outcome is scored.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 9–10): the ensemble's longer GTV editing time and the outlier results are printed in the body.
- New-pattern candidate: *metric-optimal is not clinic-optimal*. Report a blinded expert's editing time beside the overlap metrics, and print where the two disagree (pp. 6, 9–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Keep the extreme cases (here, tumors that obliterate a structure) as a separate outlier test set instead of dropping them, and report both test sets (pp. 3, 8–9).
- Select models on one metric (validation IoU) and test them on others (volumetric and surface Dice, ASSD), so that the test does not reward overfitting to the selection metric (p. 5).
- Anonymize the automated and the ground-truth contours, and have an independent expert time the edits per structure, with the ground truth as a reference row (pp. 6, 9).
- Derive the surface-distance tolerances from the image resolution and the clinically acceptable error, and state the reasoning (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Contrary to standard weighted average ensembles in machine learning that can be obtained with linear regression (e.g., on a tabular task), this 3D objective function (Equation 1) is not convex." (p. 5)
- "This reflects the limitations of only using quantitative metrics and the importance of complementing with a qualitative clinical use assessment." (p. 10)

### S418 · Holistic prescriptive analytics for continuous and constrained optimization problems (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2023, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2022.0080)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–18 (abstract, §§1–8, Appendices A–D, references). First-page check: the title matches, and the authors are Bertsimas (corresponding) and Omar Skali Lami (p. 2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Omar Skali Lami (MIT ORC) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the prescriptive-analytics programme (SW5).
- It extends optimal prescriptive trees (S096, card in c2-04) to other learners and to continuous, constrained decisions, and places itself after Bertsimas–Kallus (S008, card in c21) (pp. 2–4).
- Received November 2020, accepted June 2022 (p. 2).
- Skali Lami is also a coauthor of the DELPHI paper (S103, card in c27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA+ML; IO for the assignment step; HS for the local search}:
  - Predict-then-optimize fits the predictor first and decides second. Bertsimas–Kallus (2020) still trains first and prescribes second, although it does not plug in point predictions (p. 2).
  - Optimal prescriptive trees predict and prescribe jointly, but only for discrete, low-cardinality, unconstrained decisions (pp. 3–4).
  - Demirović et al. called for better direct methods, and the paper presents itself as the answer (p. 4).
  - Problem: one model that clusters, predicts and prescribes, for many learners and for continuous, constrained decisions (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: minimize λ·(prediction error on the training data) + (1 − λ)·(prescriptive cost on the training and new points), jointly over the cluster assignments, one predictor per cluster and the decisions (Problems (3)–(5), pp. 4–5).
  - Constraints: a minimum cluster size, and the new point joins the nearest cluster (p. 5).
  - Solution method: alternate over predictors, decisions and assignments with random restarts, warm-started by k-means and the observed treatments (Algorithm 1, pp. 7–8).
  - Quadratic cost: Algorithm 2 turns the joint fit into an ordinary MSE fit on an augmented data set. It takes p copies of each training row and q copies of each row with the prescribed decision and a target of zero, so any MSE learner, CART included, can be used (Proposition 1, pp. 8–9).
  - With trees as learners, the decision step enumerates the reachable leaves when there are no constraints, and is an MIO when there are (p. 11).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - λ ∈ [0, 1] is chosen by cross-validation (pp. 4–5), yet the tuning grid runs up to 10³ (Appendix C, p. 17) [my reading]. Clusters use the ℓ2 distance (p. 5).
  - Synthetic data: n = 10,000 and p = 4, with five tree-defined groups whose treatment effects differ in sign. The cost is y², and the split is 60/20/20 (pp. 11–12).
  - Assortment data: 25,000 sales records, a shelf-space constraint and linear revenue. No inventory limit and no cannibalization are assumed (pp. 13–14).
  - Diabetes data: a proprietary set of 100,000 patients and 11 treatments; the objective is average blood sugar (p. 14).
  - On real data the counterfactuals are unknown, so an XGBoost or neural-network model, whichever does best out of sample, is treated as the ground truth (p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Proposition 1 is proved by expanding the augmented loss (pp. 8–9).
  - Complexity: O(T_max(kρ₁((p + q)n/k) + ρ₂(n))), given the cost of fitting and of prescribing (p. 9).
  - Convergence to a local minimum is asserted without proof (p. 8) [my reading]. With T_max = 2 the method reduces to a clustered predict-then-optimize (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Baselines: predict-then-optimize, SAA, the Bertsimas–Kallus weights, Tulabandhula–Rudin joint fitting with and without elastic net, and prescriptive trees from Interpretable AI (pp. 11, 14). Hardware: a 2.3 GHz quad-core i7 with 16 GB (p. 18).

  - Assortment: revenue +17% (Table 4, p. 15).
  - Diabetes: cost −14% against the baseline and 7% better than Bertsimas–Kallus. Prescriptive trees tie on cost and have the higher R², 85 against 82 (p. 14; Table 5, p. 16).
  - The claimed 5%–17% margin over Bertsimas–Kallus (p. 4) is a difference in percentage points of the baseline (17 − 12, 14 − 7, 30 − 13) [my arithmetic].
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a six-item list (p. 4).
  - Extensions: probabilistic cluster assignment and warm-started retraining (p. 16).
  - Limits stated as such: none [my reading]. Unstated: no certificate for the local search; scoring by a learned model; proprietary data in the medical case.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11–16): decisions are scored by prescriptive cost against predict-then-optimize and the group's own earlier prescriptive methods. But on real data a learned model stands in for the outcomes, and there is no owner's rule and no field test.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 7–8, 11–12, 18):
  - in line with it: one joint model replaces a sequential pipeline (step 1), warm-started by k-means and the observed treatments (step 2);
  - short of it: the model is solved by alternating local search with restarts to a local minimum, with no gap (the Heuristic 3 route), and the machine is named but no solver.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 8–9, 11, 13): the joint problem is a nonconvex mixed-integer model, and the solver class is kept only block by block:
  - a convex fit when the learner and the cost are convex;
  - an LP decision step in the assortment case;
  - an assignment problem for the clusters;
  - an MIO decision step for constrained trees.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (p. 14): the paper prints that the group's earlier prescriptive trees tie on cost and predict better.
- New-pattern candidate: *reduce the loss to data*. Write a weighted predictive-prescriptive loss as a plain MSE fit on duplicated and zero-target rows, so that any off-the-shelf learner takes the decision into account (pp. 8–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If the decision cost is y² and λ = p/(p + q), add p copies of each training row and q copies of each row with the prescribed decision and target 0, then fit any MSE learner (Algorithm 2 and Proposition 1, pp. 8–9).

### S428 · Data-driven COVID-19 vaccine development for Janssen (INFORMS Journal on Applied Analytics 2023, DOI 10.1287/inte.2022.1150)

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC); Xinggang Liu, Jennings Xu and Najat Khan (Janssen R&D Data Science) (p. 2). Janssen funded the work (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the applied write-up of Janssen's use of DELPHI.

- The keywords include the Edelman Award (p. 2). The biographies list Bertsimas as an Edelman finalist four times and Li as a 2021–2022 finalist (p. 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP+DA; RO named for the choice of sites but not formulated; HS in the calibration}:
  - A phase III trial's length scales as T ∝ (1/√N)·(1/I)·(1/p_eff). With the sample size fixed and the efficacy given, incidence at the sites is the lever: choose sites that will become hotspots (p. 3).
  - Setting up sites took 4–5 months even when accelerated, so hotspots had to be predicted months ahead (p. 4).
  - Long-horizon epidemic forecasts had failed before (Ebola, Zika), and policy changes made them harder (p. 3). The paper says no analytics effort had succeeded in this setting before (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - DELPHI is an 11-state extension of the SEIR model (pp. 4–5, 13). It adds undetected cases, a societal-response curve (an arctan with a resurgence bump) and a mortality rate that changes over time.
  - Instead of forecasting government policy, DELPHI simulates scenarios (pp. 7–8). Intervention strengths for five policy categories are estimated from the fitted response curves of 167 areas (p. 7).
  - Janssen screened countries in May 2020 and then counties, keeping the sites that stayed hotspots across policy paths. Demographic and diversity filters were added (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Thirteen parameters are fitted per area. The biological parameters are fixed from a review of more than 190 papers (pp. 12–13).
  - Fitting uses a recency-weighted MSE on cases and deaths over a rolling window of up to four months (p. 13):
    - a truncated-Newton refit runs daily, within 10% of the previous values;
    - dual annealing runs weekly, within 50%, and is kept only if it wins on the last two weeks.
  - Intervention data come from OxCGRT and IHME, January–May 2020 (p. 7). A hotspot is a site with at least 1.4% annualized incidence of moderate-to-severe COVID-19 (p. 9).
  - Assumption: a policy switch shifts the response by the difference in intervention strength, on a piecewise-linear scale through (0%, 2) and (100%, 0) (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Intervention strength is s_i = 1 − γ̄_i/γ̄_1 (p. 7). The scenario correction is clipped to [0, 2] (pp. 13–14). The "robust optimization" used to select sites is not written down (p. 9) [my reading].
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Forecasts: median MAPE of 5.8% for cases and 10.6% for deaths over 15 days in 167 areas (Table 1, p. 6).
  - Against the CDC ensemble, on four-week-ahead US deaths: DELPHI ranks first in 6 of 13 weeks and never below fourth of eight models. The text calls this consistently outperforming (p. 6).
  - Trial (Table 3, p. 9): 230 sites and 43,783 participants. Site incidence was 5.34% against 2.06% for their areas [figures read from cells that lost their "±"], and 91% of the sites were hotspots (pp. 9–11).
  - The headline savings of 6–8 weeks (about 33%) and 15,000 participants (60,000 → 45,000) come with no stated counterfactual (pp. 2, 12) [my reading].
  - The "over 90% accuracy" is the share of chosen sites that became hotspots, which the paper also calls "high specificity" (p. 11). It is a precision, not a specificity [my reading].
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: the "first documented implementation" (p. 12). Later uses at Johnson & Johnson: choosing sites for an RSV trial (83% county accuracy), demand planning for Medical Devices, and return-to-work planning for 130,000 employees (p. 11).
  - Limits: there is no limitations section [my reading]. The unreliability of long-term forecasts is acknowledged, and the scenario tool is designed around it (p. 7).
  - Not stated: the counterfactual behind the savings; a comparison with Janssen's pre-DELPHI shortlist; the site-selection model itself.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3–4, 8–12):
  - in line with it: owner, decision and deployment are clear, and the decision is scored (site incidence, share of hotspots, trial length and size);
  - short of it: the baseline is the average incidence of the areas, not the sites Janssen would otherwise have chosen (steps 1–2), and the savings have no counterfactual.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 11, 16): one applied paper per deployment, in the INFORMS applied journal with an Edelman framing, and adoption spread across the company (RSV, Medical Devices, return-to-work).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 4–5, 8–9, 13): the problem is entered through epidemiology's own model (SEIR → DELPHI), with an optimization-based calibration. The site selection is called robust optimization, but no formulation or class is given.
- Tension T6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 6, 11): "consistently outperforms" (first in 6 of 13 weeks) and "over 90% accuracy" (a precision) are headlines that go beyond the body.
- New-pattern candidate: *scenarios instead of a forecast of the unforecastable driver*. Simulate each feasible policy path and keep the decisions that are good under all of them (pp. 7–9).
- New-pattern candidate: *find the lever in the domain formula*. T ∝ (1/√N)·(1/I)·(1/p_eff) leaves incidence as the only lever once the sample size and the efficacy are fixed (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Start from the formula that governs the outcome, and name the one lever you control (p. 3).
- Estimate each policy category's effect from the fitted model's own response parameter across many areas, then reuse these strengths to simulate policy switches (pp. 7, 13–14).
- Calibrate on two tracks: a bounded local refit daily and a global search weekly, keeping the global solution only if it wins on the latest two weeks (p. 13).
- Judge the chosen sites by their realized outcome against the background rate of their region, with a significance test (Table 3, pp. 9–11).

- "we enumerate the first successful implementation of using machine learning models to accelerate phase III vaccine trials" (p. 2)
- "Therefore, to provide useful insights, we avoided attempting to predict the government response and used DELPHI to create scenario analyses instead." (p. 7)

- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - The screening problem is a one-dimensional minimization, solved by enumerating months (p. 5).
  - The mortality model uses ROSE oversampling and drops insignificant features before refitting (pp. 5–6).
  - The delay coefficient is read causally; no identification argument is given (pp. 6, 10) [my reading].
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
