### S192 · On the performance of affine policies for two-stage adaptive optimization: a geometric perspective (Mathematical Programming 153(2) 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10107-014-0818-5)
Read level: **full**: pp. 3–20 read (whole article, tables and references); pp. 1–2 are the offprint cover and copyright page.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Hoda Bidkhori (MIT Sloan, p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: RO period, adaptive strand. Received 25 March 2013, accepted 11 September 2014 (p. 3). It continues the group's geometric bounds for static and affine policies (Bertsimas–Goyal; Bertsimas–Goyal–Sun, refs [9]–[11], p. 20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+LO}: The setting is a two-stage adjustable robust LP whose right-hand side b lies in a convex compact set U ⊆ Rᵐ₊ (p. 4). Affine policies are widely used because the best one is tractable and they do well in practice, yet no general performance bound exists (p. 4); the fully adaptive problem resists approximation within O(log m) (Feige et al., p. 4). The entry point is the limit of the group's own bounds: the geometric bound 1+ρ/s covers static solutions only (p. 5), and the affine-policy bound O(√m) does not depend on U (pp. 5–6).
- **D2 Key idea**: The paper adds a third geometric constant, the simplex dilation factor λ(U). It is the smallest λ for which a simplex S containing the symmetry point b0 satisfies S ⊂ U ⊂ λ(S − b0) + b0 (p. 8). With it, z_Aff ≤ λ(1+ρ/s)/(λ+ρ/s) · z_Adapt (Theorem 3, p. 11; stated p. 6). By the algebra of Theorem 3, the bound equals 1 when λ = 1 (affine policies are optimal on a simplex, Theorem 2, p. 10) and tends to the static bound 1+ρ/s as λ grows.
- **D3 Model and data**: Uncertainty sits only in the right-hand side, with c, d ≥ 0 and nonnegative decisions (p. 4). The constants are the symmetry s (pp. 6–7), the translation factor ρ (p. 7) and λ (p. 8). λ equals m for a ball (Lemma 4, pp. 9–10) and is stated for p-norm balls and budget sets with proofs omitted (Lemma 5, p. 10). For any convex body λ ≤ m+2 (Theorem 1, after Lagarias–Ziegler, p. 10). A multi-item inventory model is rewritten into the standard form (Lemma 7, pp. 16–18).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): The proof takes the affine policy that is optimal on the inscribed simplex, recentres it at b0, and scales the first stage by α and the slope by α/λ. Feasibility on U follows by mapping each b to (b − b0)/λ + b0 ∈ S and from Lemma 6 (p. 10). The cost satisfies z_Aff(U) ≤ α·z_Adapt(S) ≤ α·z_Adapt(U) (pp. 11–12). Tables 1–3 evaluate the bound for nine families of sets; for budget sets Δk the affine bound is k²/(k²+m) times the static one (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Random inventory instances (CPLEX 12.5, p. 16) compare a posteriori bounds, the geometric affine bound and the static bound (Table 4, p. 19): Δ*(5,25) 2.78/3/5, Δ*(9,81) 3.21/5/9 and DU*(3,9,9) 2.06/2.07/3. The data are posted online (p. 19), and several Table 4 cells are merged in the extraction.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory: an a priori bound for a tractable policy class that depends on the geometry of U. Its limits are two stages and right-hand-side uncertainty only (p. 4); the stated future work is multistage and piecewise affine policies (p. 19). The bound is called "quite close" to the a posteriori bounds (p. 18), but Table 4 shows the distance growing with k (3.21 against 5 at Δ*(9,81), p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the class-keeping device, affine policies (tractable, p. 4), is inherited rather than designed. The work prices that device a priori through the geometry of U (Theorem 3, p. 11), and it prints the boundary of the exact problem (O(log m) inapproximability, p. 4).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: a "price" of the tractable policy class, given as a solution-independent bound (pp. 6, 11), tabulated across standard sets (pp. 14–15) and checked against instance bounds (p. 19). No knob is handed to a decision-maker, and the widening gap at larger k is not discussed.
- new-pattern candidate: *own earlier result's limit as the entry* (Bertsimas–Goyal bounds ignore U; the Bertsimas–Goyal–Sun bound covers static policies only; pp. 5–6).
- new-pattern candidate: *a priori bound checked against instance-wise a posteriori bounds* (Table 4, pp. 18–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To bound a restricted policy class, find a sub-object on which the class is exactly optimal (a simplex, for affine policies). Measure the real set's distance from it by one dilation constant, then transport the optimal policy by recentring and scaling (pp. 8, 10–12).
- Tabulate the old bound, the new bound and their ratio for a catalogue of standard uncertainty sets, so readers see where the gain is large (Tables 1–3, pp. 14–15).
- Check an a priori worst-case bound against a posteriori bounds on random instances, and publish the instance data (Table 4, p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "While it has been observed empirically that these policies perform well in practice, to the best of our knowledge, there are no theoretical performance bounds for affine policies in general." (p. 4)
- "We find that the geometric a priori bound is quite close to the a posteriori bounds for random instances of the inventory control problem." (p. 18)

### S206 · OR Forum—Tenure Analytics: Models for Predicting Research Impact (Operations Research 63(6) 2015, DOI 10.1287/opre.2015.1447)
Read level: **full**: pp. 2–16 read (article and Appendices A–B); p. 1 is the INFORMS cover; the reference list was skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Erik Brynjolfsson (MIT Sloan), Shachar Reichman (Tel Aviv University and MIT Sloan), John Silberholz (MIT ORC) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: analytics period. Received June 2014, accepted September 2015 (p. 2), funded by Thomson Reuters through the MIT Center for Digital Business (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA+ML+AP}: Tenure decisions rest mainly on subjective assessments (abstract, p. 2). Prediction studies use late-career data, and none has asked whether early-career predictions could improve committees' decisions (p. 3). The entry point is a "Moneyball moment" for academia (p. 2) on an OR bibliometric database, with a backtest against actual tenure decisions (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: Network centrality from three networks (citation, coauthorship and a combined dual network), measured as betweenness, closeness, clustering and PageRank five years after first publication (pp. 4–5, 8). It feeds random forests, or logistic regression for the INFORMS-fellow outcome (pp. 7–9). The tenure rule ranks scholars by their average predicted rank on eight metrics and tenures as many as the committees did (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**: Web of Science gives 198,310 OR papers from 1975–2012, plus citing and cited papers, for 752,562 papers and 2,206,116 citations (p. 3). Names are disambiguated by a logistic model trained on WOS–SSRN matches (test AUC 0.921) followed by clustering; 19.9% of clusters were lumped and 10.2% split on 166 checked scholars (pp. 13–14). The prediction set has 43,215 scholars with a 70/30 split (p. 7; 43,047 on p. 4). The tenure set was built by hand (685 → 203 → 75 → 61 → 54) and fixed before any comparison; ambiguous labels were checked by IRB-approved e-mail interviews (p. 5). Admitted simplifications: a fixed number of tenure slots (p. 12), and 16-year outcomes that are projections from hierarchical linear models (R² 0.88–0.93) (pp. 6, 15–16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): n/a beyond the evaluation design: selection at matched capacity (p. 9), bootstrap percentile confidence intervals (pp. 8–10), and a MAPE that divides by max(y, 1) (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: On prediction, the centrality model more than halves MAPE against the citation-only baseline on paper counts (Table 4, p. 8) and predicts fellows with AUC 0.97, against 0.82 and 0.94 for the other two models (p. 9). On tenure, it disagrees with committees on 16 of 54 scholars and gains 1.3 A-journal papers, 38 citations and 0.8 h-index in projected 16-year outcomes (pp. 9–10). The four-metric model picks the same set, and a rule of 5-year citations ≥ 9 matches the centrality model on citations and h-index only (pp. 10–11). A balance check finds no shift in awards, editorships, gender or subfield (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The model is offered as a decision aid, not a replacement (p. 12). Stated limits: the sample is small, the tenure decision may itself change outcomes, and slots are fixed (p. 12). Deployment needs field-wide disambiguation and recalibration, and candidates may game centrality (pp. 12–13). Committees "might favor the four-metric models" (p. 13), so the headline network features do not change the tenure decision itself (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: decisions, not predictions, are scored against the owner's current rule (the committees) at the same capacity, on outcomes the owner values (pp. 9–11). A stronger naive rule is also run (pp. 10–11), the evaluation's blind spot is printed (p. 12) and the use is framed as a decision aid (p. 12). There is no field test.
- new-pattern candidate: *Counterfactual replay against the historical decision-maker* (pp. 9–11).
- new-pattern candidate: *level the field and say which way it still tilts*. The model tenures as many scholars as the committees did, and the committees had information the model lacked; the tilt is against the model (pp. 9, 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Backtest a predictor as a decision rule. Give it the decision-maker's capacity and candidate pool, then compare the realised outcomes of the two selected sets (mean, and share of top performers) with bootstrap CIs (pp. 9–10).
- Fix the evaluation cohort before running any comparison, and say so (p. 5).
- Add a balance table on secondary attributes (awards, teaching, gender, subfield) so the new rule is shown not to shift them (p. 12).
- Report whether the feature behind the prediction gains changes the decision at all (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Importantly, no studies to date have addressed whether early-career predictions of scholars’ future academic success can be used to improve the decisions made by tenure committees today." (p. 3)
- "Finally, the analysis treats the number of tenure slots across the programs studied as a fixed resource, an assumption made to simplify the comparison of the proposed model’s choices against those of tenure committees." (p. 12)

### S244 · Robust fluid processing networks (IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control 60(3) 2015, DOI 10.1109/TAC.2014.2352711)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13 read. The bodies of Tables I–VII are missing from the extraction, so only numbers stated in the text are used; author biographies and references were skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ebrahim Nasrabadi (MIT ORC and Boston University), Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis (Boston University) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a bridge between SD and RO. Received August 2013, published online August 2014 (p. 1). It builds on the Luo–Bertsimas SCLP algorithm and the fluid-control line (pp. 2, 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+RO+LO}: Sequencing in multiclass networks is an intractable stochastic dynamic program (p. 1). Brownian models are intractable beyond one dimension, and fluid models are tractable but ignore variance (p. 1). Existing translations of fluid policies handle uncertainty after the fact, mainly for stability (p. 2). The entry point is to put the uncertainty inside the fluid optimal control problem by robust optimization (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: Service times and arrival rates get budgeted, time-varying interval uncertainty per server (Γ_j, Δ_j; p. 5). The robust counterpart is again an SCLP, and arrival-rate uncertainty drops out of the optimal robust control (Theorem 1, pp. 6–7). The policy re-solves at each epoch (model predictive control) and gives priority by p*_i (RFP, p. 7). For a single server the robust problem is solved by at most n LPs and yields a robust cμ-rule (Theorem 2, pp. 8–9), which carries over to Klimov's problem (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**: A deterministic continuous fluid model with linear dynamics over [0, T] (pp. 3–4) and budget-type uncertainty sets (p. 5); the policy needs no distributional assumption (p. 2). Simulations use Poisson arrivals and exponential services with τ̃ = 0.25τ and c = e, plus hyper-exponential variants with CoV 1.0–1.4 (pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): LP duality of the inner maximization at each t gives the robust constraints, and the worst-case arrival term does not depend on u (p. 6). Optimality of the piecewise-constant single-server construction follows from SCLP complementary slackness (pp. 8–9). SCLP is NP-hard in general but is solved at "hundreds of variables and constraints" (p. 7). A tolerance ω reuses the solutions of nearby states (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: The policy is simulated on four network families against exact DP (where computable), optimized target-pursuing, best-threshold, LBFS, FCFS and cμ policies, and the nominal fluid policy (pp. 10–12). In balanced heavy traffic it is within 2.1% of the threshold policy and more than 12.7% better than the nominal fluid policy (p. 10). The reported RFP uses the best Γ found by several simulation runs (p. 10), and run counts and hardware are not in the extracted text.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are labelled modelling power, insights and performance (p. 2). The paper admits that stability of RFP is not established (p. 13) and claims scale of tens of classes and servers (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a control problem owned by queueing and stochastic DP is written as an SCLP whose robust counterpart stays an SCLP (pp. 2, 6, 13). The dual reading shows arrival uncertainty is irrelevant (p. 7), and the boundary is printed: SCLP is NP-hard in general (p. 7) and there is no stability proof (p. 13).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: Γ is the knob, and its effect is plotted against CoV (Fig. 2, p. 11), but it is tuned by simulation (p. 10) and carries no bound.
- new-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device* (an alternative proof of the cμ-rule's optimality, a robust cμ-rule, and Klimov's problem; p. 9).
- new-pattern candidate: *subsume the incumbent as a special case* (Γ = 0 gives the classical fluid policy, p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Robustify the deterministic approximation (the fluid model) rather than the stochastic system, and prove the counterpart stays in the approximation's class so its solvers apply unchanged (Theorem 1, pp. 6–7).
- Before modelling an uncertain parameter, check whether it drops out of the robust optimum (arrival rates, p. 7).
- Test a heuristic policy against exact DP where it is computable, and against the limit-regime policy in the regime that favours that policy (pp. 10–11).
- Cache the policies of visited states with a tolerance ω, and plot solve counts against ω (Fig. 3, p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "We show that the robust fluid model still remains within the class of SCLPs. Thus, it preserves the computational tractability of the classical fluid problem, and all solution techniques for SCLPs remain applicable." (p. 2)
- "Admittedly, we have not established stability of the class of robust fluid policies we introduced." (p. 13)

### S360 · Optimizing over coherent risk measures and non-convexities: a robust mixed integer optimization approach (Computational Optimization and Applications 62(3) 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10589-015-9755-3)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–27 read; Examples 1–4 (pp. 9–12) were read for the sets they define, and the references were skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Akiko Takeda (University of Tokyo, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO period. Received 7 July 2014 (p. 1). It builds on the Bertsimas–Brown correspondence between risk measures and uncertainty sets (p. 2) and on Takeda's CVaR–SVM work (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+IO+ML+AP}: Coherent risk minimization is robust optimization over a set U (p. 2). Adding the norm equality ‖v_S‖ = c fixes leverage in portfolios and rules out v = 0 in classification (pp. 1–2, 15), at the price of convexity. Practitioners had been advised to leave such constraints out for lack of optimality theory (pp. 3, 15).
- **D2 Key idea**: The paper offers two routes. (i) A test for when the equality can be relaxed to "≤" without loss: c ≤ τ plus a strict value gap (Theorem 1, p. 6); for K = Rⁿ, the relaxation is exact iff 0 ∉ U (Theorem 2, p. 8); and one LP gives the CVaR threshold ν̂ (Remark 2, pp. 18–19). (ii) Otherwise, with ℓ1 or ℓ∞ norms, an exact MIO with v = v⁺ − v⁻ and binaries (eqs. (21)–(22), pp. 13–14). A new coherent measure comes from a squared-Mahalanobis Bregman set, U = conv(Z) ∩ ellipsoid (Example 4, pp. 10–12).
- **D3 Model and data**: A finite scenario space with reference probabilities (p. 5), bounded U (p. 5) and feasible c (Assumption 1, p. 4). The data are Nikkei 225 stocks, with 270 monthly and 1,178 weekly returns in rolling windows (pp. 19–20), and four UCI data sets (p. 25).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): A convex-combination argument proves Theorem 1 (p. 6) and a separating hyperplane proves Theorem 2 (p. 8). LP duality of the inner maximization gives the risk terms (pp. 10, 12–13), and the MIO is disjunctive with bound c (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Matlab with CPLEX 12 (p. 19). Rolling out-of-sample portfolios beat the model's own ablations (NormConst, NoNormConst) on test return, Sharpe ratio and turnover, with higher return variance than NormConst (Tables 1–4, pp. 21–24). Global ℓ1/ℓ∞ classifiers beat the local ℓ2 Eν-SVM "in most values of ν" but are slower in the nonconvex cases, and no times or gaps are tabulated (pp. 25–26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are exactness conditions, MIO formulations and a new risk measure (pp. 1, 26–27). The ℓ2 norm stays hard (p. 13), and the MIO helps only at "the proper problem size" (p. 15). The portfolio baselines are ablations of the same model plus a swap to the mean–standard deviation risk measure; no 1/N rule is run (pp. 20–24). The stated future work is to fit uncertainty sets to data (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a nonconvex constraint that practitioners avoid is either certified away (the convex relaxation is exact under a computable condition) or written exactly as MIO (pp. 6–8, 13–14). The boundary is printed: the ℓ2 norm and problem size (pp. 13, 15).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the exact global MIO duels the incumbent local-search Eν-SVM out of sample (p. 25), with no warm start, time limit, gap or certificate reported.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: it reopens a constraint the literature advised leaving out (pp. 3, 15), relying on MIO solvers but without a quantified "why now".
- new-pattern candidate: *Rival method as a relaxation of the exact formulation* (ν-SVM is the convex relaxation of Eν-SVM, and the norm-constrained portfolio model (24) relaxes (23); Theorem 2 says exactly when the relaxation is exact; pp. 15, 18).
- new-pattern candidate: *subsume the incumbent as a special case* (hard-margin SVM, ν-SVM and Eν-SVM, and the mean–standard deviation and norm-constrained portfolio models are members of one family; pp. 12, 15, 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before branching on a nonconvex equality, solve the problem without it and compare the solution's norm with the target. A computable threshold says when the convex relaxation is already exact (Theorem 1, p. 6; Remark 2, pp. 18–19).
- Write ‖v‖₁ = c or ‖v‖∞ = c as an MIO with split variables v⁺ and v⁻, one binary per coordinate, and a cover constraint for ∞ (pp. 13–14).
- Intersect any convex uncertainty set with conv(Z) to make its risk measure coherent (pp. 9, 11–12).
- Report how often the nonconvex case actually arises across rolling windows (nonconv.ratio, p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "Though they recommended that such constraints be left out of analyses because there is no theory to support the required optimality conditions, we can obtain a global optimal solution for nonconvex portfolio optimization including leverage requirement by reformulating it as an MIO formulation." (p. 3)
- "As a future work, we want to consider a method for constructing a suitable uncertainty set U that fits to a dataset in each application." (p. 27)

### S087 · Multistage robust mixed-integer optimization with adaptive partitions (Operations Research 64(4) 2016, DOI 10.1287/opre.2016.1515)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–12, 20–22, 33–35 and 41–53 of the submitted manuscript were read (introduction, Theorem 1, Voronoi partitions, Algorithm 1, implementation, lower bound, experiments, conclusions), plus the statements of Propositions 4–5 (pp. 35–36). Worked examples, the nested and multistage derivations (pp. 12–20, 22–33) and the proofs and Theorem 2 of pp. 35–41 were located but not read in full.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Iain Dunning (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO period, adaptive strand. It follows the group's finite adaptability (Bertsimas–Caramanis 2010) and piecewise decision rules (Bertsimas–Georghiou 2013) (pp. 3–4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+IO}: Affine and polynomial rules do not allow discrete recourse (p. 3). The group's piecewise rules struggle with fractionality as stages grow (p. 3), and K-adaptability relies on big-M constraints (p. 4). The entry point is that solvable AMIO sizes "lag significantly behind" continuous adaptive optimization (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea**: Solve the static problem by cutting planes, read off the active (minimum-slack) scenarios, and partition U by their Voronoi diagram; then iterate (pp. 8–12). By Theorem 1, a partition cannot improve unless it separates the active scenarios (pp. 9–10). There is a non-nested and a nested (monotone) variant (pp. 11–16); affine rules can be mixed in (p. 17), and nonanticipativity is enforced across stages (pp. 21–33).
- **D3 Model and data**: Constraints and objective are linear in ξ, and decisions are mixed-integer; polyhedral U keeps the Voronoi cells polyhedral (p. 11). Instances come from the rivals' own papers: capital budgeting from Hanasusanto et al. (the text gives N ∈ {5, …, 30} on p. 42, while Tables 1–2 report N = 10 and N = 40 with 20 instances each, p. 45) and lot sizing from Bertsimas–Georghiou (30 instances, T = 2–10; pp. 45–47), plus a network extension (p. 50). The improvement bound needs right-hand-side uncertainty and continuous problems (p. 41).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Theorem 1 is proved by restricting attention to the active set (pp. 9–10). Voronoi cells are U plus N − 1 linear cuts and need no vertex enumeration (p. 11). Proposition 3 maps the fully adaptive policy onto the samples, which gives z_lower ≤ z_full and a stop rule based on the gap (pp. 34–35). The non-nested objective can worsen between iterations (Proposition 4, p. 35); the nested one cannot (p. 33; Proposition 5, p. 36).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: In capital budgeting the nested variant improves on the static solution by 77% in 3.2 s at N = 10. The non-nested variant reaches 110% in 388 s at N = 40, matching Hanasusanto et al., whose method left almost all N = 20 instances unsolved in 2 h; gaps stay at 18–34% (pp. 43, 45). In lot sizing the average gap falls from 15.5–24.7% to 9.3–16.2% in four iterations (Tables 3–4, p. 48). The manuscript states no solver, version or hardware (checked in full), and it calls the comparison with Postek–den Hertog imprecise because their solver settings and hardware differ (p. 43).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are an algorithm, bounds and a nonanticipativity treatment (pp. 5–6). Lower bounds can be weak (p. 52): "AMIO has yet to see" an advance like cutting planes (p. 52). Network instances with T = 6 and N ≥ 3 do not solve within 1,200 s (Table 5, p. 51). The stated future work is local optimization of the partitions and warm starts (p. 53).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the partitioned problem is the nominal MIO with second-stage variables duplicated, with no big-M or piecewise restructuring (p. 42). The speed-up is attributed to preserving that structure (pp. 43, 47), and the boundary is printed (Table 5, p. 51).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the protocol appears in robust-optimization form. Each solution carries a gap against a lower bound on the fully adaptive optimum (pp. 34–35, 45–51), and rival methods are dueled on their own instances (pp. 41–48). Unlike step 3 of Method 4, no solver, version or machine is stated.
- new-pattern candidate: *own predecessor system's limit as the entry point* (Bertsimas–Georghiou 2013's fractionality, p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: *binding scenarios bridge the restricted policy and the sampled lower bound*. Active scenarios define the partitions (pp. 9–11), and the samples build the lower bound (p. 34); S138 does the same in this batch.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Seed a Voronoi partition of the uncertainty set with the binding scenarios of the static robust solution. A partition that leaves all of them in one cell cannot help (Theorem 1, pp. 9–11).
- Pair any restricted adaptive policy with a sampled relaxation that keeps nonanticipativity, and stop on the relative gap (Proposition 3, pp. 34–35).
- Tabulate time, improvement and gap per iteration, so readers can choose their own stopping point (Tables 1–2, p. 45).
- Compare variable counts with the rival formulation: O(KN) binaries and O(2^K·KN) continuous variables against (1 + 2^(k−1))N binaries (p. 42).

**Quotes**:
- "While our method requires multiple solves and has more binary variables, we suggest the reason our approach is far more tractable is that it preserves the relatively simple problem structure, allowing the MIO solver to branch smarter and generate good solutions using heuristics." (p. 43)
- "We would draw an analogy again with integer optimization and the ability of cutting planes to significantly tighten the fractionality gap - AMIO has yet to see an equivalent development." (p. 52)

### S138 · Duality in two-stage adaptive linear optimization: Faster computation and stronger bounds (INFORMS Journal on Computing 28(3) 2016, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2016.0689)
Read level: **full**: pp. 2–13 read; p. 1 is the INFORMS cover. The extraction renders "." as "0" and "," as "1" in Tables 3, 5, 6 and 8, and the values were decoded on that basis.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Frans J. C. T. de Ruiter (Tilburg University, visiting the MIT ORC; pp. 2, 12) · **Position in trajectory**: RO period, adaptive strand. Received August 2015, published online 24 May 2016 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+LO}: The setting is two-stage adaptive LO with fixed, continuous recourse and polyhedral U (pp. 3–4). Affine policies are the tractable standard, and their suboptimality must be bounded instance by instance (pp. 2, 7). The entry point is the known one-step dualization over the recourse (Lemma 1, p. 4), which this paper takes one step further.
- **D2 Key idea**: Dualizing first over the recourse and then over the uncertain parameters gives another two-stage adaptive LO. It has the same here-and-now decisions but swapped dimensions (Theorem 1, p. 4; Table 1, p. 5). Under affine policies the two are equivalent, and the primal policy is read off the dual (Theorem 2, pp. 6–7). The dual trades hard affine constraints for easy sign restrictions (Table 2, p. 7). Binding scenarios of both affine policies strengthen the lower bound of Hadjiyiannis et al. (Algorithm 1, p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: A fixed recourse matrix, continuous y ≥ 0, right-hand-side uncertainty in {ζ ≥ 0 : Dζ ≤ d}, and integer first-stage decisions allowed (pp. 3–4). The test problems are network lot sizing with N = 10–100 and Γ = 20√N (p. 9) and facility location with C = 10–50 (p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Lemma 1 is applied first; then, for fixed w, LP duality over ζ and a positive-homogeneity argument normalize w to a simplex (pp. 4–5). The static case recovers the usual robust counterpart (pp. 5–6). Theorem 2 follows from explicit variable maps, with the proof omitted (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi 6.0.3, JuMP and an i7-4770 machine (p. 9). The dual is an order of magnitude faster in lot sizing, and the combined bound halves the gap (10.3–12.3% against 14.0–23.8%; Table 3, p. 9). Adding constraints to U shrinks the speed-up until it disappears; the counts of affine constraints are equal at p = 400 (Fig. 2, p. 10). Negative results are printed: random samples give useless bounds even at 10⁵ (p. 7), cutting planes are slower (p. 7), and the bound is weakest at Γ = 100% (box uncertainty), where static policies are provably optimal (pp. 11–12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an equivalent dual model with computational and bounding gains (p. 12). It needs continuous, fixed recourse and polyhedral U (p. 3), and the bound gains depend on the problem (p. 10). The text claims "about 50 times" more affine constraints in the primal (p. 10), while Table 5 as decoded (8,841 against 882, matching Table 2's formulas) gives about 10 times. For C = 10 the dual is slower at three of six budgets (Table 6, p. 11), which goes undiscussed.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: dualizing twice keeps the problem in its class (Theorem 1, p. 4), the dimensions are read off the dual (Table 1, p. 5), and the boundary is printed (fixed, continuous recourse; polyhedral U; p. 3).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the affine policy's suboptimality is certified by a sampled lower bound and reported per instance with solver, version and machine (pp. 8–9), and failures are printed (pp. 7, 12). There is no out-of-sample duel with an incumbent.
- new-pattern candidate: *binding scenarios bridge the restricted policy and the sampled lower bound* (binding scenarios from the primal and dual affine policies build the lower-bound problem, pp. 7–8; also S087).
- new-pattern candidate: *mechanism check by dialing the predicted cause*. Raise p toward the point where the affine-constraint counts are equal (p = 400) and watch the speed-up shrink, then disappear (Fig. 2, p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Dualize an adjustable robust LP twice, over the recourse and then over the uncertainty, to get an equivalent adjustable LP of different size, then apply any existing method to whichever form is smaller (Theorem 1, p. 4; Table 1, p. 5).
- Count affine constraints and sign restrictions separately for each formulation, since solvers treat them differently (Table 2, p. 7).
- Test the explanation for a speed-up by varying the parameter it names toward the predicted crossover (Fig. 2, p. 10).
- Build a sampled lower bound from scenarios that bind under the computed policies, not from random samples (p. 7; Algorithm 1, p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "The equivalent dualized formulation we derive is again a two-stage adaptive linear optimization model." (p. 2)
- "This is surprising, because for this case (box uncertainty) we know that affine (in fact, static) policies are provably optimal (Ben-Tal et al. 2009, Theorem 14.2.4)." (p. 12)

### S191 · On the power of randomization in network interdiction (Operations Research Letters 44(1) 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2015.11.005; arXiv 1312.3478)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–9 of arXiv:1312.3478v1 (12 December 2013) read, all proofs included; v1 has no conclusion section.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ebrahim Nasrabadi, James B. Orlin (MIT, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO and IO; follows Bertsimas–Nasrabadi–Stiller on robust and adaptive network flows (pp. 1, 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {LO+IO+RO}: Network interdiction removes Γ arcs to minimize the maximum flow. It is a game in which the interdictor commits to a pure strategy and the flow player responds (pp. 1–2). The entry point is to let the interdictor randomize, which the authors call "a more realistic model" for protecting infrastructure (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea**: Randomized interdiction equals the adaptive maximum-flow problem, in which the flow player commits first (Theorems 1–2, pp. 3–4). Complexity results transfer from that problem: the arc-based version is polynomial for fixed Γ and strongly NP-hard in general, and the path-based version is NP-hard for fixed Γ ≥ 2 (Theorems 3–4, p. 5). For Γ = 1 both versions reduce to one LP (Theorem 5, p. 5). The paper proves tight ratios of Γ+1, Γ+1 and Γ between the values (Theorem 6, p. 5) and derives approximations from one parametric LO (pp. 5–8).
- **D3 Model and data**: A single-commodity s–t maximum flow in which exactly Γ arcs are removed; mixed strategies have finite support (pp. 2–3). The arc-based and path-based versions differ only in the randomized game (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Wald's minimax theorem and concavity in the flow (p. 4); LP duality for Γ = 1 (p. 5). The LO model caps each arc's flow at θ and charges Γθ (eq. (17), p. 5). A perturbation θ* ± ε counts the arcs at the cap on two minimum cuts (Lemma 2, p. 6), which gives the ratios (Lemmas 3–4, pp. 6–7). Families of parallel-arc networks show each bound is tight (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none; the LO model's computational record is cited from earlier work (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a model, complexity results and tight approximation ratios (pp. 1–2). The model has a single commodity and a cardinality budget, and the arc-based version for general Γ remains strongly NP-hard (p. 5). v1 gives no future directions and has rough edges: "Z^Path_RNO" (pp. 7–8), and Lemma 1 cited as "Theorem 1" (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a game owned by the network-interdiction literature is rewritten via minimax as an adaptive maximum-flow problem (pp. 3–4). The case Γ = 1 becomes one LP by duality (p. 5), one LO model yields all the approximations (pp. 5–8), and the boundary is printed (Theorems 3–4, p. 5).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: "the power of randomization" is a priced property, a tight worst-case ratio in the knob Γ (Theorem 6, p. 5; pp. 7–8), rather than a curve handed to a decision-maker.
- new-pattern candidate: *bound, constructive policy, provable ratio, tightness check* (pp. 5–8).
- new-pattern candidate: *tightness audit of one's own bound* (every ratio is attained asymptotically by parallel-arc networks, pp. 7–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To value a modelling extension such as randomization, prove the extended problem equals a known one with the order of moves swapped, then import that problem's complexity results (Theorems 1–4, pp. 3–5).
- Cap per-arc flow by a scalar θ and charge Γθ in one parametric LP, then perturb θ to count the arcs at the cap on minimum cuts (eq. (17), p. 5; Lemma 2, p. 6).
- Prove tightness with parallel-arc families whose ratio tends to the bound as K grows (pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "This shows that randomization helps the interdictor to perform as well as when she has perfect information of how flow is routed through the network." (p. 2)
- "This provides a more realistic model for various applications such as protecting critical infrastructures against terrorism or enemy’s attacks." (p. 1)

### S182 · Certifiably optimal low rank factor analysis (Journal of Machine Learning Research 18(29) 2017, arXiv 1604.06837)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–10, 15–17, 24–37, 39–40 and 42–43 of arXiv:1604.06837v1 (23 April 2016) read (introduction, reformulations, branch-and-bound overview, experiments, certificates, conclusions), plus the statements of Theorems 2–3 (pp. 12–13) and 6–7 (p. 21). Sections 3.1–3.3 and 4.2–4.6 (pp. 11–24, apart from those statements), 5.4 (pp. 37–42) and the proofs (pp. 43–45) were not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Martin S. Copenhaver, Rahul Mazumder (MIT, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: ML period, under the "modern optimization lens" brand (p. 7); a certificate-heavy relative of the best-subset line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+CP+IO}: The problem is rank-constrained factor analysis, Σ ≈ Θ + Φ with Θ ⪰ 0 of low rank, Φ diagonal and nonnegative, and Σ − Φ ⪰ 0 (pp. 1–2). Standard least-squares and likelihood methods can return negative variances or an indefinite Σ − Φ, which breaks the reading of explained variance (pp. 2–3, 5–6). SDO-based methods scale only to p of about 20–30 (pp. 5–6).
- **D2 Key idea**: The problem has three exact reformulations: an eigenvalue problem in Φ (Proposition 1, p. 8), a smooth problem with convex compact constraints (Theorem 1, p. 9), and a concave minimization (Proposition 3, p. 10). Conditional gradient computes upper bounds with first-order SDO subproblems (pp. 10, 15). For q = 1, a branch and bound with McCormick envelopes and Weyl bounds supplies the certificates (Algorithm 3, pp. 16–17; p. 32). MRFA, MTFA and a PSD-constrained MINRES are special cases (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**: Any covariance matrix, with no data assumptions (pp. 1, 7), and Schatten-q loss with q ∈ {1, 2} (p. 3). The synthetic classes A1, A2 and B1–B3 have equal common and unique variance (pp. 25, 37), and there are five real data sets (p. 29).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Branch and bound over envelope relaxations terminates finitely within an additive TOL (Theorems 4–5, p. 16). Weyl's inequality gives cheap root bounds (pp. 21, 32), and conditional gradient reaches stationary points at an O(1/K) rate (Theorems 2–3, pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Against the R packages' standard estimators and the authors' own MTFA, (CFAq) recovers Φ exactly (as MTFA does) and keeps Σ − Φ PSD where the Category (B) estimators do not (Table 1, p. 28). Certificates run in julia with MOSEK/SCS at additive TOL 0.1 under a 400,000 s cap (p. 33). A2 instances with p ≥ 20 and r* ≥ 3 hit the cap (Table 4, p. 35), and the p = 1,000–4,000 runs report only root Weyl bounds, with gaps of 0.09–8.73 and no times (Table 11, p. 40).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are reformulations, algorithms, certificates and comparisons (pp. 7, 42–43). The cost of certification grows with the rank and with p (p. 7). Runtimes on the shared cluster are unreliable and the implementation is serial (p. 32), and an additive gap of 0.1 is large next to objectives of 0.06–1 (Table 2, p. 33). The stated future work is other matrix-estimation problems (p. 43). There is a slip between pages: p. 30 says Category (B) explains the most variance, but the Figure 3 caption gives (CFA1) (p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: a heuristic supplies the incumbent, an exact branch and bound certifies it with stated solvers, tolerance and cap, and the rival packages' estimators are the opponents (pp. 26, 32–34); the heuristic stayed the incumbent in all but three instances (p. 34). ⚠ The certificate is dropped at scale (Table 4, p. 35; Table 11, p. 40), while the contributions claim optimality proofs "in minutes for some problems on the order of p = 4000" (p. 7). Table 11 gives no p = 4,000 root gap below 0.11 against the 0.1 tolerance used elsewhere (pp. 33, 40).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a rank-constrained statistics problem is written exactly as a smooth SDO and as a concave minimization (pp. 8–10), unifying earlier estimators (p. 9).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the problem is called "previously intractable" (pp. 1, 43), with the enabler named as warm-started first-order SDO (hundreds of nodes per 100 s, against about three with interior-point methods; p. 34) rather than a solver × hardware figure.
- new-pattern candidate: *Rival method as a relaxation of the exact formulation* (MTFA's trace is the convex surrogate of the rank, and (CFAq) reaches full explained variance at a smaller rank than MTFA; pp. 4, 29).
- new-pattern candidate: *subsume the incumbent as a special case* (MRFA, MTFA and PSD-constrained MINRES, p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace a rank constraint by the variational form over {W : 0 ⪯ W ⪯ I, Tr W = p − r}, which makes the objective smooth or concave (Theorem 1, p. 9; Proposition 3, p. 10).
- Compute two root bounds of different kinds (an envelope relaxation and an eigenvalue inequality) and keep the better one at each node (pp. 32–34).
- When runtimes are noisy, report node counts beside the times (p. 32, footnote 13).
- Give every competitor a column for violation of the constraint the rivals ignore (λmin(Σ − Φ̂), Table 1, p. 28).

**Quotes**:
- "To our knowledge, this is the first paper that demonstrates how a previously intractable rank-constrained optimization problem can be solved to provable optimality by coupling developments in convex analysis and in discrete optimization." (p. 1)
- "It is particularly crucial to note that the optimal solutions for all problems we consider are found very quickly, and that vast majority of computational time is then spent on proving optimality." (p. 7)
