### S132 · Universal neurons in gpt2 language models (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2401.12181)
Read level: **full**: main text pp. 1–13, including Acknowledgments and Author Contribution (p. 13); references pp. 13–17 scanned (no optimization work cited; the group's own 2023 preprint on p. 15); Appendix A pp. 18–19 in full; Appendix B (pp. 19–33) consists of figure pages, of which only the captions are in the text layer (images not read).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Wes Gurnee (MIT; corresponding author, led the project), Theo Horsley (University of Cambridge), Zifan Carl Guo, Tara Rezaei Kheirkhah, Qinyi Sun and Will Hathaway (MIT), Neel Nanda (senior author; no affiliation printed) and Dimitris Bertsimas (MIT; senior author) (p. 1; roles p. 13) · **Position in trajectory**: the LLM strand of 2019–2026; a sequel to the group's sparse-probing preprint (Gurnee, Nanda, Pauly, Harvey, Troitskii and Bertsimas 2023, cited p. 15). Gurnee 2024 is in the ML lineage of the CV thesis list [01 §1.5], and note 06 records this preprint as student-led with Bertsimas last of eight authors [06 §1]. The contribution statement (p. 13) is a primary statement of his role in such a last-author paper, consistent with note 01's reading of last-author order as a co-advised student's paper [01 §1.4].

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML: mechanistic interpretability, outside the council's optimization scope; there is no optimization model anywhere in the paper}: The question is whether neural mechanisms are universal across models (abstract, p. 1). Two motivations: LLMs are deployed "in high-stakes settings" without an understanding of how they decide (p. 1), and Olah et al. (2020b)'s universality hypothesis, read both as a discovery strategy and as a triage rule for the field: if mechanisms are universal, detailed circuit analyses generalize; if not, effort should go to scalable automated methods (p. 1). Working hypothesis: universal neurons are more likely to be monosemantic (p. 2). Entry: the smallest variation, five random seeds of one architecture trained on the same data, chosen as "the most favorable experimental conditions" so that the result bounds universality under larger changes (pp. 2, 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Universality as an unsupervised filter. For each neuron of model a, take its maximum Pearson activation correlation with any neuron of each other seed over 100 million tokens, subtract the same maximum computed against a random Gaussian rotation of that layer (a basis with no privilege), and average over models: the excess correlation ϱ (eqs. 2–3, p. 4). Neurons with ϱ > 0.5 are then studied (p. 5): their weight and activation statistics (p. 5); families found by several hundred automated labelling tests (p. 6); and functional roles read from weights rather than activations: prediction, suppression and partition neurons (pp. 7–8), entropy neurons acting through the final LayerNorm (p. 9), and attention-deactivation neurons acting through attention to the BOS token (p. 10). What changes relative to prior work: from universal features in vision models and representational-similarity measures (p. 3) to single neurons of language models across seeds, and from Geva et al.'s *promoting* to promoting and suppressing (p. 8). What stays: the neuron basis and standard tooling (TransformerLens, p. 13).
- **D3 Model and data**: Autoregressive transformers (p. 4): replications of GPT2-small and GPT2-medium, five seeds each (a–e; Karamcheti et al. 2021, p. 4; the Hugging Face stanford-crfm models, p. 18); Pythia-160m for the correlation repeat (p. 5; Figure 13 caption, p. 20); five Pythia models from 410M to 6.9B parameters for the depth pattern (p. 8). A neuron is one GeLU unit of an MLP layer, studied through its activations or through its rows of W_in and W_out (p. 4). Data: 100 million tokens of the Pile test set at context length 512, excluding padding, BOS and newline tokens (p. 18). Weights are preprocessed (LayerNorm folded in; writing and unembedding weights centred) so that cosines have mean 0 (p. 18); hyperparameters in Table 1 (p. 19). Assumptions adopted, as the authors admit: a seed change is "the smallest change" (p. 4); the 0.5 threshold has no principled basis (p. 5); labels come from the vocabulary, spaCy, Pile subsets and an SVD topic model, which leaves many interpretable neurons uncategorized (pp. 6–7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for proofs, reformulations or bounds; the work is empirical. Analysis devices: a random-rotation baseline to test whether the neuron basis is privileged (p. 4); the excess correlation (eq. 3, p. 4); a variance-reduction score for a binary explanation y, 1 − ((1−β)σ²(v|y=0) + βσ²(v|y=1))/σ²(v) (eq. 4, p. 6); moments of the vocabulary-effect vector W_U w_out (high kurtosis with positive skew marks prediction, with negative skew suppression; high variance marks partition) (pp. 7–8); a causal intervention that fixes one neuron's activation, compared with 20 random neurons from the last two layers outside the top decile of norm and the bottom decile of logit variance (p. 9); a score h_n = W_outᵀW_Qᵀk_BOS checked by zero path ablation (p. 10); and a streaming computation of all pairwise correlations from running sums, since storing the activations would take 36 petabytes (p. 18).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: 1,253 of the 98,304 neurons of GPT2-medium-a have ϱ > 0.5 (p. 5). The share is 1.23% for GPT2-medium and 1.26% for Pythia-160M but 4.16% for GPT2-small, an outlier left unexplained (pp. 5, 11–12). Max-over-models and min-over-models correlations differ by 0.049 on average, 0.105 for ϱ > 0.5 (p. 5). Families: unigram, alphabet (18 of 26 letters), previous-token, position, syntax and semantic neurons (pp. 6–7). The depth pattern of prediction and suppression neurons holds across the five GPT2-medium seeds and is repeated on Pythia to rule out an artifact of tied embeddings (p. 8). An entropy neuron (L23.945) and its antipode (L22.2882, cosine −0.886), with a more dramatic pair in GPT2-small (pp. 9–10); the median head's BOS output norm is 19.4 times smaller than that of other tokens (p. 10). Baselines: random rotation (p. 4), random neurons (p. 9), random directions (p. 10). Failures and loose ends are printed: a section of results the authors "do not fully understand" (pp. 11–12) and cases where raising the entropy neuron lowered entropy (p. 10). Code and data are released (p. 18); compute ran on MIT Supercloud (p. 13). No solver, benchmark or decision metric is involved.
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Olah et al. (2020b) and the circuits programme (p. 1); reviews universal mechanisms, representational similarity (which has "yielded mixed conclusions", p. 3) and single-neuron studies, including the pitfalls of causal claims (p. 3). Follows Voita et al. (2023) for position neurons (pp. 6–7); refines Geva et al. (2022) (p. 8); claims "the first documented mechanism for uncertainty quantification in language models" (p. 10). Cites the group's 2023 sparse-probing preprint (p. 15). No optimization or operations research work is cited (pp. 13–17).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract runs question → method with numbers (100 million tokens, five seeds, 1-5%) → families → three functional roles (p. 1); Figure 1 serves as a visual abstract (p. 2). Hypothesis-first prose ("we hypothesized", p. 2; "We hypothesize", pp. 9–10) with frequent hedges ("speculations", p. 5; "We intuit", p. 8; "potentially", pp. 7, 13) and an informal register ("A particularly fun subclass", p. 6). Unexplained observations get their own section, "Additional Mysteries", instead of being dropped (p. 11); limitations and future work are concrete (pp. 12–13). An Author Contribution paragraph assigns roles by initials (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: empirical science, with released analysis code (p. 18) and no algorithm or theory. Stated findings: only about 1-5% of neurons are universal, "another piece of evidence that individual neurons are not the appropriate unit of analysis for most network behaviours"; universality is an effective unsupervised route to interpretable components; families; functional roles; antipodal pairs (p. 12). Limits, stated specifically: models of hundreds of millions of parameters because training many seeds is expensive; seed-only universality within one model family; tokenization blocks cross-family study; all-pairs correlations do not scale; interpretations rest on manual analysis or algorithmic supervision; the neuron basis ignores most of the computation (pp. 12–13). Future work: an overcomplete dictionary basis, correlations between adjacent layers only for larger models, explanations written by LLMs, training dynamics and downstream circuits (p. 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Test whether coordinates carry meaning with a random-rotation null: recompute the statistic after multiplying the layer by a random Gaussian matrix and report only the excess over that baseline (eqs. 2–3, p. 4).
- (D5) Compute all pairwise correlations over 10^8 samples from running sums (means, sums of squares, cross-products) instead of storing the data (p. 18).
- (D4) Auto-label units with a variance-reduction score for each binary candidate explanation, run over hundreds of programmatic labels (eq. 4, p. 6).
- (D4) Classify effect vectors by moments: kurtosis for concentration, the sign of the skew for promote vs suppress, variance for partition (pp. 7–8).
- (D5) Intervene on a selected unit against a matched random control set that excludes the extremes of the selection criteria (p. 9), and repeat on a second model family to rule out an architectural artifact (p. 8).
- (D7) Report unexplained observations in a section of their own and close with a role-by-initials contribution statement (pp. 11, 13).

### S330 · Robust and adaptive optimization under a large language model lens (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2501.00568)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–33, every prompt (Prompts 2.1–2.6, pp. 9–11, 21–23), every transcript (pp. 11–31) and the references (pp. 32–33). Version read: arXiv v1 (31 Dec 2024), marked as submitted to the INFORMS Journal on Computing (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Georgios Margaritis (Operations Research Center, MIT, p. 1; his PhD student, thesis 2026 [04 Sources T30]) · **Position in trajectory**: The skill cites this preprint for Method 2 step 6 and in its 2019–2026 row; note 06 dates it beside his Nov–Dec 2024 faculty-newsletter essay on educating the world for the era of AI [06 §1]. It is a late branch of the RO programme: every reformulation rule in the prompts comes from his book with den Hertog (pp. 2, 5–7, 20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + ML; LO and conic counterparts as the content taught}: The entry is an adoption gap, not a tractability gap. Optimization "hasn't been as popular" as machine learning because modelling needs an expert who recognizes structure, while ML packages let non-experts train models "by writing just a few lines of code" (pp. 1–2). RO and ARO need still more expertise: identify the uncertain parameters, choose sets, embed them, and apply theorems to reach a tractable counterpart, a process that can be "complex and time-consuming" (pp. 2–3). The paper asserts, without a test, that ChatGPT "performs very poorly out-of-the-box" on RO and ARO for lack of training data (p. 4). Question: can prompting make ChatGPT (i) formulate a problem, (ii) add user-specified uncertainty, (iii) derive the tractable robust counterpart and (iv) write executable code (abstract, p. 1; pp. 3–4)?
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The expert workflow becomes a four-step chain of thought (Wei et al. 2023): generate a nominal LO problem, introduce uncertain parameters, derive the robust counterpart, write Julia code with JuMP and Gurobi (p. 8; examples pp. 11–14). Context prompts are given only for steps 2–4, since ChatGPT already formulates basic problems (p. 8). They carry the book's rules: the robustification template (a + Pz)ᵀx ≤ b for all z ∈ Z (Prompt 2.1, p. 9); closed-form counterparts per set (Prompt 2.2, p. 9) or the support-function calculus with intersections and Minkowski sums (Prompt 2.3, p. 10); linearization of ℓ1 and ℓ∞ terms and a second-order cone for ℓ2 (Prompt 2.4, p. 11); for ARO, the two-stage form with decision rules (Prompt 2.5, pp. 21–22) and the counterpart under a linear decision rule (Prompt 2.6, pp. 22–23). What changes relative to NL4Opt and its successors: the target is the theorem-application step of a niche field rather than text-to-LP (pp. 3–4). What stays: an off-the-shelf model (ChatGPT 3.5, p. 4) used by in-context learning only (pp. 4, 8), and a standard solver for the solving (pp. 4, 13).
- **D3 Model and data**: Class: LO, min cᵀx s.t. Ax ≤ b, with (c, A, b) in a user-specified set U (p. 5), reduced to row-wise uncertainty in the a_i alone by the epigraph and projection arguments (pp. 5–6). Sets: p-norm balls, polyhedra and intersections (p. 7), plus Minkowski sums and an entropy-type set in the prompts (p. 10). ARO with fixed recourse, a constant right-hand side and a linear decision rule (pp. 20–21). Assumption adopted for tractability, as stated: optimizing over all decision rules is NP-hard, and restricting y(z) to a class "doesn't guarantee optimality, but it is necessary to make the problem tractable" (p. 20). Data: small instances (a user-defined four-variable LP, p. 11; a diet problem with five foods generated by ChatGPT, pp. 15–18; a generic ARO, pp. 23–27; three small failure cases, pp. 28–30), with the radius r set to 0.1 in the first code (p. 13) and the sets supplied by the user (p. 5). Model: ChatGPT 3.5 (p. 4). The number of sessions, the sampling settings and how the shown transcripts were selected are not stated, and responses are cut ("we may omit parts of the response", p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No new theorem, bound or algorithm. The analysis restates Bertsimas and den Hertog (2022) as a teaching sequence: an epigraph moves objective uncertainty into the constraints (eq. 3, p. 5); row-wise projections U_i, with an uncertain b absorbed into the coefficients (p. 6); the support-function counterpart āᵀx + δ*(Pᵀx|Z) ≤ b (eqs. 7–9, pp. 6–7); dual-norm counterparts for p-norm balls (eq. 11, p. 7); LP duality for polyhedral sets (eqs. 12–13, p. 7); splitting for intersections (eqs. 14–15, p. 7); and the linear decision rule y(z) = u + Vᵀz, which turns a fixed-recourse ARO constraint into a static robust one handled by the same tools (eqs. 17–20, pp. 20–21). The algorithm is the prompt design (pp. 8–11, 21–23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Evaluation is by curated transcript, judged by the authors' inspection: two RO examples (pp. 11–19), two ARO examples (pp. 23–27) and three failure cases in the Limitations section (run-to-run inconsistency, p. 28; a wrong rearrangement giving a = [−99, 1] instead of [−99, −1], p. 29; an intersection treated by the Minkowski-sum rule, pp. 29–31). There is no count of trials, no success rate, no test set and no baseline: the out-of-the-box failure (p. 4) is not measured, and there is no human or software comparison. No solver output is reported for any generated program (pp. 13–19). The extrapolation to a quadratic decision rule is a formulation only; no counterpart is attempted (p. 24). [my reading] RO Example 1 is presented as a success (p. 14), but the printed counterpart attaches r·‖x‖₂ to the ≤ 300 constraint, which was given ∞-norm uncertainty, and r·‖x‖₁ to the ≥ 500 constraint, which was given a Euclidean ball (request p. 11; ChatGPT's own step 2, p. 12; counterpart p. 13). By the rules of Prompt 2.2 (p. 9) the two norms are swapped; the code follows the swap (pp. 13–14), and the commentary praises the minus sign in front of the 1-norm term without noticing it (p. 14). [my computation] In RO Example 2 the code has no radius r and repeats one nutrient row for each of five requirements (pp. 17–19). The LP transcribed from that code is infeasible (re-solved for this card with SciPy's HiGHS: meeting 2,200 nutrient units costs at least 110 against a budget of 50), while the text says the code "solves the problem with example data" (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Related work is three items on natural language to optimization: the NL4Opt competition, where ChatGPT already did well (Ramamonjison et al. 2023); a BERT/BART pipeline (Ramamonjison et al. 2022); and a position paper (Tsouros et al. 2023) (p. 3). The distinction claimed: RO and ARO are niche and need theorems, so "we argue that our approach is the first approach in that direction" (p. 4). The RO background is his own: Bertsimas and den Hertog (2022) and Bertsimas, Brown and Caramanis (2011) (pp. 2, 5–7, 20). [my reading] The reference list (pp. 32–33) cites no robust-optimization modelling software and no non-LLM route to counterparts, so step (iii) is never compared with one; the only evaluation literature is Frieder et al. (2023) on ChatGPT's mathematics (p. 29).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract lists capabilities (i)–(iv), then "little to no errors", then "some instances of erroneous execution" (p. 1); the contribution is "two-fold" (p. 4); a tutorial voice ("Let's", pp. 6–7, 20); each transcript is followed by bullet commentary on the challenges ChatGPT overcame (pp. 14, 19, 24); failures are confined to the Limitations section (pp. 27–31); the conclusion calls the results "surprising" and closes on making optimization popular, as ML packages did for ML (pp. 31–32). The title reuses the *under a … lens* brand (p. 1). Typos and notation slips mark an early draft ("higlhy", p. 1; "sub-filed", p. 2; "we attempt to Large Language Models", p. 3; āᵀX for āᵀx in eq. 13 and v^i against w^i in eqs. 14–15, p. 7; Vᵀ D in eq. 20, p. 20, where Prompt 2.6 has Vᵀ d, p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: a demonstration (no theory, algorithm, released software or benchmark). Stated: with prompting, ChatGPT "can learn how to reformulate RO and ARO models" for basic sets and simple problems, and LLMs adapt to niche areas with little in-context training, "a procedure which resembles the human learning process" (p. 4). Limits, stated concretely with examples: inconsistency across runs, algebraic manipulation, choosing and applying theorems (pp. 28–31); the errors are attributed to "inherent weaknesses of Large Language Models" (p. 31). Future: LLMs adapted to optimization as "a helpful learning resource for non-experts" and as a formulation aid that raises the field's visibility (pp. 31–32); no experiment, benchmark or tool is proposed. [my reading] The abstract's "little to no errors" (p. 1) rests on showcased transcripts, and both RO showcases contain problems the commentary does not mention (D5).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 3–7, 9–11, 20–23). The prompts teach Method 1's class-keeping toolkit: counterparts that stay LP or SOCP (dual norms, LP duality for polyhedra, linearized ℓ1 and ℓ∞, a cone for ℓ2) and a linear decision rule that keeps ARO a static robust problem, with its approximation admitted (p. 20). The goal is a problem that "can be solved by traditional optimizers efficiently" (p. 3) with standard solvers (p. 4). Evidence of the toolkit as he teaches it, not of new formulation work.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Split an expert reformulation into stages (nominal model → uncertainty → counterpart → code) and give the LLM rule prompts only for the stages it cannot already do (p. 8).
- (D2) Prepare two tiers of rule prompts, closed forms per set and a general support-function calculus with combination rules, and test whether the model can use the general one (pp. 9–10, 19).
- (D5) Probe extrapolation by asking for a construct the prompts never describe (a quadratic decision rule after only linear ones, p. 24), and probe robustness by merging two steps into one prompt (p. 19).
- (D5) Organize failures by type, with one minimal reproducible transcript each: run-to-run inconsistency, algebraic rearrangement, wrong theorem (pp. 28–31).
- (D4) Teach robust counterparts in this order: epigraph, row-wise projection, support function, closed forms per set, combination rules, then linearization and cones for the solver (pp. 5–7, 11).
- (D5) [critic-informed, from this card's checks; not the paper's practice] Check every generated counterpart mechanically against a reference counterpart and run every generated program, reporting solver status; here inspection let a swapped norm and an infeasible instance through (pp. 13–19).
