# Batch s2-10 · Dimitris Bertsimas · supplement (round 2)

**Batch intent**: Eight supplement works from 2019–2020, read to see what his group does when an exact model stalls or a problem leaves the solver class, and whose voice a paper of this period carries. The works form four strands:
- AP decision problems solved by decomposition and local methods: airlift planning for USTRANSCOM (S304, with Chang, Mišić and Mundru) and joint frequency-setting and pricing on Tokyo and Boston transit networks (S180, with Ng and Yan).
- Statistics and ML under the MIO lens: exceptional responders in randomized trials (S326, with Korolko and Weinstein) and Gaussian-mixture estimation by CDF discrepancy (S358, with Hari Bandi and Mazumder).
- Robust and adaptive optimization carried into new territory: the K-server problem of online algorithms (S445, with Jaillet and Korolko) and the unpublished *Price of flexibility* manuscript (S488, with Bidkhori and Dunning).
- Health-care communication: a review of ML in oncology (S111, with Wiberg) and the COVID-19 Forecast Hub's first ensemble evaluation, on which he is a consortium author (S063).

Questions asked of each work:
1. When the exact model stalls, what replaces it (decomposition, column generation, local search, sequential LP), and does the claim change with it (Method 1 stop rule, Heuristic 3)?
2. What stands behind "optimal" or "near-optimal": a proved gap, an approximate bound, or a gap known only in hindsight (Method 4, Tension T1)?
3. Which incumbent is dueled, and with equal information and budget (Method 4's critic-informed checks)?
4. Is a property priced by a knob, with the bad case printed (Method 3)?
5. Is the decision scored against the owner's current rule, and was anything field-tested (Method 5)?

### S304 · The airlift planning problem (Transportation Science 2019, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2018.0847)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–24 (INFORMS cover, abstract, §§1–6, Algorithms 1–4, Tables 1–14, references; displayed formulas read through the prose). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Chang, Mišić and Mundru match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Allison Chang (MIT Lincoln Laboratory), Velibor V. Mišić (UCLA Anderson) and Nishanth Mundru (MIT ORC) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: AP air-transportation line, received December 2016 and funded by USTRANSCOM (p. 2). It sets itself against his ATFM models (S015, c04; S043, c06; S037, c2-03) and the dynamic-resource-allocation framework (S210, s2-06) (p. 6). Mišić (PhD 2016) and Chang (PhD 2012) are listed among his graduates (04-mentorship).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP; IO as formulation and engine; HS for the construction and local-search heuristics}: USTRANSCOM must assign passengers and cargo to aircraft and sequence pickups and drop-offs with little delay and few aircraft hours; airlift costs $9,000–$12,000 per flying hour (p. 2). Planners schedule one requirement at a time, a process "largely manual and ad hoc" (p. 3). The problem is a multi-depot pickup-and-delivery problem with time windows (NP-hard), plus crew rest, maximum active time, hard and soft windows and a delay-plus-uptime objective. No earlier paper has this combination at this scale (pp. 3–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: A slot-based MIP (1) defines the problem but is not the route to solving it (pp. 7–9). The method separates assignment from sequencing:
  - a single-aircraft MIP (2) gives the cost f(a, S) of flying requirement set S on aircraft a, with "by" variables for balanced branching (pp. 10–12);
  - a set-partitioning master with an assignment constraint (the aircraft are heterogeneous) picks one set per aircraft (p. 12);
  - constraint generation on the dual of its LP relaxation prices sets by add/drop local search from random starts (Algorithms 1–2, pp. 13–14);
  - warm starts come from a randomized insertion heuristic H1 (10 runs) and a move-one-requirement local search H2. Both call the single-aircraft MIP as a black box and re-optimize every changed sequence exactly (pp. 4, 14–15);
  - a final restricted master MIP gives the integer plan (p. 15).
- **D3 Model and data**: Deterministic requirements; heterogeneous aircraft with weight capacities; windows [ℓ, u + B] with lateness penalized after u; rest parameters γ and δ (p. 7). Aircraft are decoupled, with no maximum-on-ground constraints (p. 6), and all priorities and the uptime weight are set to 1 (p. 17). Data: simulated T instances on 10 of 174 continental USAF bases with C-130 types, A = R ∈ {10, 20, 50, 100} (pp. 15–16); R instances from USTRANSCOM's internal benchmark of 111 requirements, with ports, slacks and aircraft filled in at random because the data were sensitive or missing (pp. 16–17).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Transition variables y stay continuous because forcing constraints make them binary (p. 9). A precedence formulation was set aside: its big-M constraints are weaker, and it solved no faster (p. 9). The heuristic pricing does not provably solve the separation problem, so the LP bound is approximate; restarts from random sets lower the risk (p. 14). The restricted master's integer plan is not guaranteed optimal (pp. 6, 13). No theorem.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi 6.5, Julia/JuMP, a 12-core Xeon with 96 GB; single-aircraft MIPs capped at 2 s inside all phases, CG at 12 h (p. 17). The full MIP solves A = R = 10 (up to 8,918 s) but not even its relaxation at 20 within 3 h (Table 2, p. 18). The baseline orders requirements by earliest pickup, as planners do, but sequences each aircraft by MIP, which makes it "more sophisticated than current USTRANSCOM practice" (p. 17). Against it, delay plus uptime falls by 8.4–11.8% on average on simulated instances (approximate gaps 0.00–1.32%, Table 3, p. 18) and by 15.9–38.0% on planning instances (gaps 0.53–6.44%, Table 10, p. 22); H1 + H2 take up to about 6 h at 100 aircraft (pp. 19, 22). Exact pricing at A = R ∈ {10, 20} moves the bound by 0.00% and 0.17% but takes 37,456 s against 333 s at 20 (Tables 8–9, p. 21). My reading: the text calls the CG plan optimal at A = R = 20, where Table 8 supports optimality at 10 (SolGap 0.00%) and 0.28% at 20 (p. 21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a joint MIP, the decomposition with a three-phase method, and tests on simulated and USTRANSCOM planning data (p. 3). The conclusion reports almost no delay and over 10% less aircraft time than current practice, within operational time (p. 23). Admitted limits: no optimality guarantee for the final plan (p. 6), an approximate bound (p. 14), and exact pricing too slow beyond A = R = 20 (p. 20). Future: unknown future requirements, weights and travel times, through adaptive and robust optimization (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 14–21): two heuristic warm starts, a stated limit, solver and machine, a gap for every instance set, and a duel with the owner's rule. The gap rests on heuristic pricing, so the paper calls it approximate and audits it against exact pricing where that can be done (pp. 18, 20–21). The overall shape is Heuristic 3: the exact model stalls (Table 2, p. 18), so the authors decompose, search locally and keep exact MIP for small checks.
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 16–17, 21–23): the owner, its planning data and its current one-at-a-time rule, with the baseline made stronger than practice. Decisions are scored by delay, uptime and aircraft used, and the method's own run times are printed. No field test is reported.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 9–13): the defining MIP leaves the tractable range, and the device changes (set partitioning over a MIP oracle), not the solver. The class is kept only through heuristic pricing, which trades exactness for an approximate bound. Formulation strength is weighed explicitly (big-M precedence rejected, p. 9; "by" variables, p. 12), unlike the pattern behind Tension T4.
- New-pattern candidate: *small exact MIP as a black-box oracle inside heuristics and column generation* (pp. 5, 10–15).
- New-pattern candidate: *audit an approximate bound with an exact computation on small instances* (pp. 20–21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When even the relaxation of the full MIP will not solve, split assignment from sequencing: make the per-resource sequencing MIP an oracle f(a, S) and price its sets in a partitioning-with-assignment master (pp. 9–13).
- If pricing is heuristic, call the bound approximate and audit it on instances small enough for exact pricing, reporting BoundGap, SolGap and the time ratio (pp. 14, 20–21).
- Build the baseline from the owner's described practice and make it deliberately stronger than that practice, so the gain is not inflated (p. 17).
- Use "by" variables (event done by slot p) next to "at" variables so that branching splits the solution space evenly (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "At present, planners schedule each requirement one at a time and look for opportunities to combine some missions with others to reduce transportation costs." (p. 3)
- "The main message of this experiment is that while the lower bound produced by CG is not theoretically guaranteed to be a true lower bound, empirically we have some assurance that it should be close (if not exactly equal) to the true lower bound." (p. 21)

### S326 · Identifying exceptional responders in randomized trials: An optimization approach (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2019, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2018.0006)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–14 (INFORMS cover, abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1–4, endnotes, references). The online appendix, which describes the two comparator heuristics and practical considerations, is not in the file. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Korolko and Weinstein match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nikita Korolko and Alexander M. Weinstein (MIT ORC) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: ML for health in the first volume of IJOO, special issue on machine learning and optimization (p. 2). Its significance test is the bootstrap of *The power of optimization over randomization* (S124, c15) (pp. 9, 13). Korolko is also on S445 in this batch.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO (fractional 0–1 programming); AP (clinical trials)}: A trial that fails overall may still contain a subgroup with an exceptional effect, and trials are expensive ($32.5 billion on investigational-drug trials in 2010) (p. 2). Existing methods (Cox interactions, bump hunting, virtual twins, recursive partitioning) give interpretable subgroups but "entail greedily solving a sequence of optimization problems" (pp. 2–3). The problem: find an axis-parallel box that maximizes the average treatment effect (ATE). Its bounds come from prespecified cut points, at most S0 covariates are restricted, and each arm's count in the box is bounded (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: A fractional 0–1 MIO (2) with bound choices L and U, memberships z and restriction indicators q (pp. 4–5). The ratio objective is linearized exactly. Each arm's count in the box is bounded, so its reciprocal takes few values and is expanded in binaries θ; the products zθ become continuous variables ζ (pp. 6–7). Around the MIO: a bootstrap test on the 10%-trimmed ATE at α = 0.01 (p. 7); sequential boxes with an overlap bound ρ = 0.2 and the Holm correction (p. 8); and a CART-like recursive heuristic for warm starts (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**: Randomized two-arm data (X, T, v); the decision maker sets the cut points (for instance at clinical thresholds), S0 and the count bounds (p. 4). Simulation: n = 100, four uniform covariates, an effect δ0 where x1, x2 ≤ 0.5, N(0, 1) noise, 250 samples (p. 9). Real data: three public trials, of diethylstilbestrol for prostate cancer (252 subjects), periodontal therapy (500 training and 323 validation subjects) and D-penicillamine for primary biliary cirrhosis (312) (pp. 11–13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The linearized model has Θ(n²) continuous and Θ(n) binary variables (pp. 3, 7). If an instance does not reach the tolerance, the decision maker can "lower the resolution of the cut points" (p. 7); valid inequalities of Tawarmalani et al. are offered for larger instances (p. 10). No theorem.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia and Gurobi 6.5 (p. 9). In simulation the false-positive rate is 12.4% at α = 0.01, and it rises to 25.2% at S = 10. Significant subsets appear in 76.8% of runs. The threshold TPR is 68.0%, against 66.4% for the warm-start heuristic alone (pp. 9–10, Table 2). Time to provable optimality runs from 33 s at n = 100 to 14,535 s at n = 1,000 (Table 3, p. 11). Real cases: a box with +18.2 months of survival (p = 0.001), which the Kehl–Ulm comparator also finds (p. 12); a periodontal box whose training effect "does not fully hold" in validation (p. 12); and a best PBC box with p = 0.03, declared not significant (p. 13). My reading: the paper calls an FPR far above α "stable and low" (p. 10). This points to testing on the same data that selected the box, and selective inference is not discussed.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the MIO and its linearization, a significance procedure, multiple boxes and a fast heuristic (p. 13). Limits: train–test splits shrink already small subsets (pp. 7–8); the validation shortfall (p. 12); the null case (p. 13). Future: observational data, more than two arms (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 6–7, 11): a ratio objective owned by fractional programming is rewritten exactly as a mixed-binary linear model that commercial solvers accept. The boundary is printed as time to optimality against n.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 9–12): one global MIO replaces greedy partitioning, warm-started by a greedy heuristic, with provable optimality and times reported. But in simulation the only opponent is its own heuristic, which nearly matches it (66.4% against 68.0%). "our method guarantees optimality" (p. 11) refers to the training objective, not to the error rates.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 7, 11–13): the decision is whether to test a subgroup further. A held-out validation is used once, and a negative real case is printed (Heuristic 4). No trial sponsor is a partner, and nothing is field-tested.
- New-pattern candidate: *linearize a ratio objective by binary expansion of the reciprocal of a bounded integer denominator* (pp. 6–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For an average over a selected subset, bound the subset's size and expand 1/|subset| in binaries over its few possible values. Then linearize the products and keep the product variables continuous, so that the number of binaries stays linear in n (pp. 6–7).
- Restrict boxes to prespecified clinical cut points and cap the number of restricted covariates; this gives interpretability and a smaller MIO at once (p. 4).
- Print a real case in which the method finds nothing significant next to the positive cases (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "With the power of MIO, we can find optimal interpretable solutions directly by solving a single global formulation instead of using a greedy, recursive approach." (p. 3)
- "Although the reduction in the prevalence of anomalies for training-set subjects in the box does not fully hold in the validation set" (p. 12)

### S358 · Learning a mixture of gaussians via mixed-integer optimization (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2019, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2018.0009)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–21 (INFORMS cover, abstract, §§1–5, Lemma 1, Algorithms 1–2, Tables 1–3, references; the values plotted in Figures 1–4 are not in the text). First-page check: the title and the authors Bandi, Bertsimas and Mazumder match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Hari Bandi (MIT ORC) and Rahul Mazumder (MIT ORC and Sloan) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the ML-under-MIO line (SW4), in the same IJOO special issue as S326 (p. 2). Mazumder is the best-subset co-author (SKILL SW4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO as engine; CP for the covariance step}: EM, the standard estimator, is a local search on a multimodal likelihood (p. 2). Provable methods need the components separated by a multiple of the largest standard deviation, and moment methods fit the first six moments (pp. 2–3). Entry: choose the parameters that minimize the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) or total-variation (TV) distance between the mixture's CDF and the empirical CDF. As an MIO this needs no separation assumption and is tractable "because of significant improvement in speed-ups of MIO solvers in the last two decades" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: With the weights known, substitute s = 1/σ and t = μ/σ and replace Φ by a piecewise-linear (PWL) function encoded with binaries, which gives an MILP (pp. 4–6).
  - Breakpoints come from a shortest-path DP over a 1,000-point grid on [−3, 3]; ten breakpoints give a maximum error of 0.00059 (pp. 7–8).
  - The n max-discrepancy constraints are added lazily through solver callbacks (p. 8).
  - A TV variant counts the samples in intervals μ ± δσ, δ = 1, 2, 3, with big-M and McCormick linearizations (pp. 9–12).
  - Unknown weights: alternate between the MIO and an LP in π, from several starts (pp. 12–13).
  - Multivariate data: fit d² random 1-D projections, align the components' labels by an MIQO, then fit covariances by an SDO (pp. 14–15).
- **D3 Model and data**: K known; weights known in the core models (pp. 4, 9); i.i.d. samples. Admitted: the big-M formulations "are weak" (p. 10); the KS model is less robust than EM to adding or removing a few points (p. 7); the alternation has no convergence proof (p. 12). Data: synthetic two-component mixtures with separations 1, 2 and 0–6 and n = 100–2,000 (p. 16); five UCI sets, each split 70/30 five times (pp. 18, 20).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Lemma 1 bounds the effect of the PWL surrogate on the KS objective by the surrogate's maximum error (p. 5; no proof in the text read). Each segment's maximum error has a closed form at the tangent point, and these errors are the arc costs of the DP (p. 7). Model sizes are counted: K(6n + K(p − 1)) binaries for TV (p. 12) and K²d² for the alignment MIQO (p. 15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia 0.6, Gurobi 6.5.2, a four-core 2.3 GHz Xeon with 16 GB (p. 16). Synthetic data: the MAPE of the means is 60–70% and of the covariances 50–60% below EM's, regardless of n, and the edge widens as separation shrinks (p. 17). KS takes 109–595 s and TV 222–2,039 s, against EM's 16.8–18.6 s, for n = 100–1,000 (Table 1, p. 19). UCI data: KS and TV beat EM by 1.7–7.5 accuracy points (my arithmetic) but trail CART and random forests on all five sets, a comparison the authors call "not fair" (Table 2, p. 20). Training takes 1.8–169 min, against EM's 0.1–4 min (Table 3, p. 20). Not reported: MIO gaps, EM's initialization, and why EM shows exactly 1,000 iterations in every synthetic row (Table 1, p. 19) but 686–29,375 on real data (Table 3, p. 20).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: two MIO estimators, an optimal-breakpoint method, a multivariate algorithm and experiments (p. 3). The accuracy gain comes "by paying a price in training time" (p. 21). Future: other univariate families (p. 21). My reading: the abstract says the MIOs are "practically solvable for data sets with n in the tens of thousands in minutes" (p. 2), yet the census set (n = 45,222) takes 126–169 min (Table 3, p. 20). "optimally recovers" (p. 2) holds only for the PWL surrogate with known weights (pp. 5, 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 4–12): a nonconvex estimation problem is kept in MILP through a PWL surrogate with a stated error bound. The price is approximation and big-M constraints that the authors call weak (Tension T4).
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (p. 3): the "why now" is solver speed-up, stated without a rebuilt figure.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 16–20): the MIO duels a local-search incumbent (EM) on synthetic data with known truth and on real data. The discriminative baselines are printed although they win. But no gap is reported, EM's set-up is not stated, and the "optimal" claim covers only the surrogate (Tension T1).
- New-pattern candidate: *PWL surrogate with DP-optimized breakpoints and an a priori error bound* (pp. 5, 7–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To put a smooth nonconvex function such as Φ into an MILP, choose the PWL breakpoints by a shortest-path DP on a fine grid. Use each segment's closed-form maximum error as the arc cost, and carry the error into the objective bound (pp. 5, 7–8).
- For a minimax over n data points, start with a few constraints and add the most violated one at each incumbent through a lazy-constraint callback (p. 8).
- When multivariate parameters come from 1-D projections, align component labels across projections with a small MIQO before fitting covariances by SDO (pp. 14–15).

**Quotes**:
- "Although, the “Big-M” formulations are weak, for tractability of the problem, we linearize constraints (10) using McCormick type linearization" (p. 10)
- "Although we have compared the performance of our algorithms with the state-of-the-art methods in classification, we believe the comparison is not fair." (p. 20)

### S445 · The K-server problem via a modern optimization lens (European Journal of Operational Research 2019, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2018.12.044)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–28 of the MIT DSpace author's final manuscript (cover, abstract, §§1–8, Proposition 1, Tables 1–3, figure captions, references; the values plotted in Figures 1–5 are not in the text). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Jaillet and Korolko match (p. 2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Patrick Jaillet and Nikita Korolko (MIT ORC) (p. 2); ONR grants (p. 26) · **Position in trajectory**: robust and adaptive optimization carried to online algorithms, a CS topic, under the programme brand "via a modern optimization lens" (title, p. 2). Jaillet co-advised Korolko's thesis (04-mentorship).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO (adaptive); IO as engine; the problem is owned by online algorithms and competitive analysis}: In the K-server problem each request must be served when it arrives, and quality is judged by the competitive ratio against the clairvoyant optimum (p. 2). The Work Function Algorithm (WFA) is the strongest known online rule, 2K − 1 competitive; its truncated form w-WFA is non-competitive but works in practice (p. 6). Entry: past observations often support "data-driven assumptions about the future", so a method should use both the past and an uncertainty set for the future (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Offline K-server becomes a mixed-binary LP after products and absolute values are linearized (pp. 6–7).
  - WFA's decision becomes one consolidated MIO, equivalent to its K separate problems (Proposition 1, pp. 7–8).
  - RO(T) adds polyhedral uncertainty sets on the next T requests and writes server positions through product binaries (pp. 9–11).
  - AARO(T) makes future assignments affine in the uncertain locations and keeps the current assignment binary (pp. 11–13).
  - HARO(w, T) puts a w-WFA block, the current step and an AARO(T) block into one MIO (pp. 13–15); a weight λ on the future block is offered (p. 17).
- **D3 Model and data**: Requests appear one at a time in [0, 1] or [0, 1]^D with ℓ1 or ℓ∞ distances, or in a finite metric space through location indicators relaxed to [0, 1] (pp. 3–4, 16). Uncertainty sets are neighbourhoods of radius 1/n around the true future locations (pp. 17, 19–20). A distortion probability μ replaces true centres by random points (p. 22). Admitted: the competitive ratio used is empirical, not theoretical, because the RO methods see approximate future locations by design (p. 15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Proposition 1 models WFA's outer arg min with the binary assignment variables (p. 8). Robust counterparts follow by standard duality (p. 11). AARO relaxes RO, because products of binaries become affine expressions (p. 13). No competitive-ratio bound.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia/JuMP and Gurobi 6.5 with 16 GB; OPT and WFA run as network flows; RAND, HARMONIC and BALANCE dropped as worse; 10 instances of 1,000 requests (pp. 18–19). RO-based methods reach empirical ratios of 1.08–1.16. RO alone is too conservative when little is known. HARO matches GREEDY and WFA with no information (n = 1) and beats them as information grows, at 2.35–4.11 s per step (p. 21). With distorted sets HARO falls to the level of the best online algorithm beyond μ = 35% (p. 23). The reported times (1.2–44 s, Table 1) are to the assignment that later proves optimal; proving it takes "minutes to tens of minutes" (p. 24). Tables 2–3 use one instance per cell (p. 25) and show HARO best everywhere, with a mean advantage of 2–26% (p. 26). My arithmetic: the 26% is the gap to GREEDY (1.56 against 1.30), while WFA scores 1.39 in that cell (Table 3, p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: MIO formulations of offline K-server and WFA, RO and AARO counterparts, and HARO with its tractability, performance and stability (pp. 3, 26). Limits: no theoretical guarantee (p. 15); the gain depends on the quality of the information (pp. 21, 23). No future work is stated (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 6–15): a CS-owned problem is written exactly as an MIO, and its robust and affine counterparts stay robust MILPs by duality. The boundary is printed as the per-step proof time (p. 24).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 15, 18–26): the incumbents (GREEDY, WFA) are dueled in their standard implementations, but the RO methods receive future information that the incumbents lack. The paper states the asymmetry and prints where the advantage vanishes (n = 1; μ > 35%), which meets the "equal budgets" check only in part. Times are to the solution that is optimal in hindsight, not to a certificate.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 17–25): the information level n, the distortion μ and the windows (w, T) act as knobs with empirical price curves and printed bad cases, but no bound.
- New-pattern candidate: *fold the incumbent heuristic's decision rule into the optimization model, then extend it* (pp. 7–8, 13–15).
- New-pattern candidate: *report time to the hindsight-best solution separately from time to proof* (p. 24; S488 pp. 25–27).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the incumbent online rule as one MIO whose binary choice reproduces its arg min (Proposition 1); then add new terms inside the same model (pp. 7–8, 13–15).
- Measure the value of forecasts on one scale (the interval radius around the truth) plus a corruption rate, and report the break-even points against the incumbents that use no information (pp. 17, 21–23).
- Compute OPT with a polynomial network-flow model, and report the average ratio to it with a convergence plot that justifies the sequence length and the number of instances (pp. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "First, all presented RO-based algorithms by design have access to approximate locations of future requests, while the key idea of competitive ratio analysis is to measure the price of not knowing the future." (p. 15)

Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–38 of an undated INFORMS-template manuscript "Submitted to Operations Research" (abstract, §§1–6, Examples 1–3, Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Lemmas 1–2 with proofs, Table 1, figure captions, references; the values plotted in Figures 2–10 are not in the text). Signs of an unfinished draft: an empty acknowledgments heading (p. 36), a broken sentence among the contributions (p. 3), and one Simchi-Levi–Wei 2015 paper listed twice (p. 38) (my reading).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO (two-stage adaptive); IO as reformulation engine; AP (manufacturing operations)}: Process flexibility lets plants build several products, and each added link costs money (pp. 1–2). The theory of long chains (Jordan–Graves and successors) covers balanced, homogeneous systems, and the design guidelines are heuristics (pp. 3–4). The only optimizing approach for unbalanced, nonhomogeneous systems, Mak–Shen's stochastic program, must be relaxed before it can be solved (p. 4). Entry: lower the price of flexibility, the cost of added links, by ARO for systems with unequal plants, products, margins and link costs (pp. 2, 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Links x are chosen here and now, and production y(d) after demand is seen (p. 7).
  - Under absolute profit the worst case is low demand, where flexibility earns nothing and still costs (Example 1, p. 8). Profit is therefore normalized by total revenue, an objective like a competitive ratio (pp. 9–10).
  - Affine policies y(d) = Qd + q turn the model into an MILP for polyhedral sets and a mixed-integer SOCP for ellipsoids (p. 11).
  - Budget sets with Γ, also correlated through a Cholesky factor (pp. 12–13).
  - Iancu–Trichakis's Pareto robustness is extended to two-stage affine ARO: fix the worst-case value z* and maximize profit at one interior scenario (Theorem 1, Corollary 1, pp. 16–18). Relaxed Pareto lowers the requirement to αz* (p. 19).
  - Two lemmas compare short and long chains (pp. 20–23).
- **D3 Model and data**: Plants with capacities, products with uncertain demand in U, link costs B_ij, production costs and prices (p. 7). Γ is chosen by solving for several values and comparing the designs in simulation, a process "related to" cross-validation (p. 12). Data are synthetic only: 100 random instances with n = m = 5 and 100 normal demand draws each, with mean perturbations η ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.9} (pp. 30, 32–33).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Robust duality gives the MILP (p. 11). Theorem 1 is proved by contradiction: two solutions that agree on m + 1 affinely independent scenarios agree on all of U, because the objective is affine in d (pp. 17–18). Lemma 1 uses vertex covers to show that two short chains beat a long chain when 2B3 > B1 + B2 + 2(p − T)c (pp. 20–21). Lemma 2 uses explicit demand vectors to show that, under symmetry, no other 2n-link design dominates the long chain in every scenario (pp. 21–23). My reading: the manuscript calls the affine model a "relaxation" whose value is "an upper bound" (pp. 11, 16). Restricting y(d) to affine functions shrinks the feasible set of a maximization, so the affine value is a lower bound.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi 6.0.4 with JuMP and JuMPeR on a 2.6 GHz i7 laptop (p. 26). At n = m = 7, 75% of instances reach an "actual" gap below 5% (measured against the eventual best design) within 5 s, while only about 60% reach a proved gap below 1% after 40 s. At 15 × 15, an actual gap of 2% takes about 1 h and a proved gap of 5% about 2 h (p. 25; Figs. 3–4, p. 27). Pareto designs are at least as good as the initial ones in about 70–95% of instances, but worse in up to 32 of 100 (Table 1, p. 31). The Γ = 3 relaxed-Pareto design ties an SAA version of Mak–Shen without perturbation and wins as the perturbation grows (p. 33). Against a long chain it earns about 98% of the revenue at 70% of the cost (p. 35).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: ARO for nonhomogeneous systems, Pareto efficiency for two-stage ARO, and the chain results (pp. 2–3). The conclusion claims designs with over 90% of the clairvoyant profit and a price 30% below the long chain's (p. 36). Limits: Pareto ties are rare, hence the relaxation (p. 19); affine policies only; synthetic data. No future work is stated (p. 36).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–3, 12, 19, 25–36): the property is named and priced; the knobs are Γ and α; Γ is set by simulation; revenue is compared with price across designs; and the bad cases are printed (Γ = 0 and Γ = 5 do poorly; Pareto designs are worse in up to 32% of instances). Both parts of Method 3's correction are absorbed: Iancu–Trichakis's Pareto critique (pp. 14–19) and a knob tuned on data (p. 12). This is Heuristic 2's pattern of answering a critique inside the critic's frame. The manuscript stayed unpublished, as Method 3's limitation records.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 10–11): the two-stage robust design stays an MILP or MISOCP through affine policies. The device is an approximation, mislabelled as a relaxation (my reading).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 23–27): the manuscript honestly separates the solver's "perceived" gap from the "actual" gap known only afterwards. But the headline "near-provably-optimal" solutions in about an hour (p. 23) sit beside a proved 5% gap at about 2 h (p. 25).
- New-pattern candidate: *normalize the worst-case objective so that the adversary cannot pick the trivial scenario* (pp. 8–10).
- New-pattern candidate: *report time to the hindsight-best solution separately from time to proof* (pp. 25–27; S445 p. 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If the worst case of an absolute objective is a trivial scenario that never rewards the feature you are pricing, divide by an optimistic benchmark (total revenue), and check the change on a 5 × 5 example with hand-computed worst cases (pp. 8–10).
- After the robust solve, fix the worst-case value and maximize at an interior scenario to pick a Pareto-efficient design; relax the fixed value by α to trade worst case for average case (pp. 16–19).
- Plot over solve time the share of instances whose actual gap, and whose proved gap, is below 1%, 2% and 5% (pp. 25–27).

**Quotes**:
- "in the low demand scenario, no benefit is realized from the flexibility yet we still pay a price for the flexibility" (p. 8)
- "While proving a design is optimal may be desirable after a model is finalized, of more immediate interest is the time to find “good” solutions." (p. 25)

### S180 · Joint frequency-setting and pricing optimization on multimodal transit networks at scale (Transportation Science 2020, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2019.0959)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–27 of the INFORMS-template manuscript "Submitted to Transportation Science", a revised version that thanks the referees (abstract, §§1–6, Appendix A, Tables 1–7, figure captions, references). The journal print was not seen. First-page check: the title (printed with "Multi-Modal") and the authors Bertsimas, Ng and Yan match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yee Sian Ng and Julia Yan (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: AP transportation, funded by Mitsubishi Electric, which also supplied the Japanese data (p. 23). The Boston demand comes from the generative model of Bertsimas and Yan 2018 (S359, c2-03) (p. 20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP (urban transit); LO as engine; the choice-dependent problem is nonconvex and continuous, the NLP delegation's territory}: Transit systems face crowding (over a third of New York subway delays) and tight budgets (p. 1). Timetabling and frequency-setting papers take demand as fixed, and pricing papers take the schedule as fixed (pp. 2–4). Entry: set frequencies and fares together while commuters choose routes, at city scale, claimed as a first (pp. 1, 4, 22).
- **D2 Core idea**: The binary departure schedule is relaxed to fluid rates, so frequency-setting with known route choices is an LP over boarding flows (pp. 5–9). When choices respond to frequency, fare and crowding (multinomial or nested logit), the problem is nonconvex (pp. 10–12). It is solved by sequential LP: linearize the choice probabilities at the incumbent, bound the step in frequencies and fares, lag the occupancy term, and restart from random points (pp. 12–14). Flat, line-based and distance-based fares enter as linear constraints (pp. 9–10). A system optimum, in which the operator dictates routes, gives an LP lower bound (pp. 11–12).
- **D3 Model and data**: 15-minute periods, a budget on services, capacities, travel times from Google Maps, and routes up to the second-shortest path (pp. 5, 14–15). Utility is linear in waiting and in-vehicle time, fare and a convex discomfort term with a soft capacity at 80% (pp. 10–11, 16). The coefficients come from survey shares and income ratios, with β1 = 1 set arbitrarily (pp. 23–24), and the logit model is used because data were limited (p. 14). Data: public Kanagawa (Tokyo) demand data, 57 stations on 5 lines, 5–9 pm (p. 14); private MBTA hourly counts turned into OD matrices, 74 stops on 4 services, with stations within 0.5 mile treated as substitutes and buses at 30% of a train's capacity and cost (pp. 20–21). The fluid model ignores the wait between vehicle arrivals (p. 6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: An LP when choices are known (p. 9). No convergence or optimality guarantee for the sequential LP; local optima are countered by restarts (p. 13). The system optimum and the flat-fare policy bracket the priced policies (p. 19). No theorem.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia/JuMP and Gurobi 7.5 on 8 cores of a Xeon E5-2650 with 64 GB (p. 14). On the full Kanagawa network (362,477 commuters) median runs take 218–554 s over 30 starts, and some starts stop at poorer local optima (Table 5 and p. 19). At B = 280 the wait per commuter is 6.73 min under the system optimum, 10.22 with frequencies alone, 8.30 with line fares and 8.74 with distance fares (Table 6, p. 20). Variable fares lower mean utility at budgets up to 360 (p. 19). In Boston the gains are smaller (distance fares cut waiting by 5.5% at B = 55), and no fare scheme dominates across the two networks (p. 22). My reading: the text says distance pricing gives "a lower mean commuter utility" than line pricing (p. 19), but Table 6 shows the opposite at every budget up to 360 (−39.3 against −43.9 at B = 280, p. 20).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A unified framework for joint frequencies and fares with choice-responsive demand at city scale (p. 4). The experiments are not "a policy prescription" (p. 14), and with mixed synthetic and real data a comparison with the current system is "difficult" (p. 22). Future: farebox recovery and ridership, and more cities (p. 23). My reading: no certificate stands behind the abstract's "near-optimality in minutes" (p. 1). The only bound is the dictatorial system optimum, 1.57 min per commuter below the best policy at B = 280 (Table 6, p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 6, 9, 12–14): the class is kept by relaxing the timetable to fluid rates and by solving the nonconvex choice problem as a sequence of LPs, with run times measured at city scale. But the class holds only locally, and no global gap is given.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 14, 19–23): a partner supplies data; decisions are scored on waiting time and mean utility, and the trade-off between them is printed. The baseline is an optimized flat-fare policy, not the operators' current schedule, and nothing is field-tested; the authors say so (pp. 14, 22).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 13, 19): labelled local search with random restarts and progress plots, but "near-optimality" without a gap (Tension T1).
- New-pattern candidate: *bracket a nonconvex policy between an operator-dictated LP bound and the policy without the new lever* (pp. 11–12, 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Relax a binary timetable to fluid departure rates where crowding, not headway, causes the waiting, and state what the relaxation ignores (p. 6).
- Solve a choice-dependent design by sequential LP: linearize the choice probabilities at the incumbent, box the step, lag the dense coupling term, restart from random points, and plot the objective against time for each start (pp. 12–14, 18–19).
- When no choice data exist, derive utility coefficients from survey shares and income ratios, and label them illustrative (pp. 14, 23–24).
