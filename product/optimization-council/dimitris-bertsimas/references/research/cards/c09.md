### S066 · Simulation-based booking limits for airline revenue management (Operations Research 2005, DOI 10.1287/opre.1040.0164)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1–17: all sections, the appendix (clustering, LP-BL and EMSRb methods, p. 16) and the reference list. Title and authors (Bertsimas, de Boer) checked on p. 1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Sanne de Boer (Operations Research Center, MIT, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an air-transportation revenue-management paper of 2001–2005. It was received January 2001, revised November 2001 and December 2003, and accepted December 2003 (p. 1), with MIT-Singapore Alliance funding (p. 16). It sits beside Bertsimas–Popescu's network RM working paper (cited p. 4), outside the budgeted-RO line of the same years.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + HS + SD; LO as engine}:
  - The network LP models of airline practice are "deterministic, static, and partitioned" and ignore stochastic, dynamic demand and nesting. Most systems use only their duals and then solve leg-based problems that cannot capture all network effects (p. 2). Exact stochastic DP explodes with seats, classes and itineraries (p. 1).
  - The entry is an application gap: airlines implement DAVN virtual nesting with EMSR-BL or LP-BL booking limits (pp. 2–3), and no model optimizes that policy (pp. 4, 10).
  - Goal: booking limits that handle stochastic and dynamic demand and network nesting, are "practically feasible for realistically sized problems" and raise revenue (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**:
  - Keep DAVN as the control framework, and keep the LP Model (1) to fix the nesting order and bucket mapping in each time window (pp. 3, 5, 10).
  - Estimate the expected revenue of a booking-limit vector by simulating bookings up to the next revision, plus an estimated value function for the rest of the season (pp. 2, 5).
  - Improve the EMSR-BL or LP-BL limits by a stochastic-gradient search on finite differences (pp. 5–7). The value functions are estimated offline by a backward recursion over windows (pp. 7–9).
  - What changes relative to DAVN with EMSR-BL or LP-BL: only step 3, the booking-limit calculation. It now accounts for future reoptimizations and for the operational nesting rule, standard or theft (pp. 3, 10, 15).
  - The authors name three "most important ideas" (p. 10): the LP for the combinatorial nesting order, simulation plus ADP for end-of-window revenue, and the stochastic gradient.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Booking classes by origin, destination and fare; leg capacities; at most 10 buckets per leg (pp. 2–3, 11).
  - T time windows with reoptimization at each one; the nesting order is fixed within a window (p. 5).
  - Demand is a Pólya process (pp. 10–11): a nonhomogeneous Poisson process with beta-shaped intensity, scaled by a gamma-distributed total demand. Early bookings therefore carry information, and remaining demand stays negative binomial. Lyle (1970) and de Boer (1999) are cited as support (p. 11).
  - Admitted simplifications: no overbooking, cancellations, no-shows or buy-down, and independent demand per class (p. 11).
  - The value function is separable and piecewise linear, built from the unconditional prior demand model (pp. 8, 11).
  - When capacity is perturbed during value-function estimation, booking limits are not reoptimized, because that "would be too time consuming" (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: no theorems. Devices:
  - LP duals of Model (1) give displacement-adjusted leg revenues and the nesting order (p. 3).
  - The booking-limit choice is written as a DP over windows (eqs. 2–3, p. 5).
  - Expected revenue depends only on the integer part of the limits, so finite differences replace gradients (p. 6). A "parallel bookkeeping" estimator computes them (pp. 6–7).
  - The step rule caps each change at Δmax, interpolates when a finite difference changes sign, and projects onto the nesting constraints (p. 7).
  - Value-function points are placed where simulated states fall: mean ± 3 sd per leg, on qL axis points rather than a q^L grid (p. 8).
  - The value function is interpolated between a chord (lower bound) and the tangent envelope (upper bound), which single-leg concavity justifies (pp. 8–9, Figure 1).
  - Self-analysis: the convergence conditions of stochastic quasigradient methods "are not met" (p. 6). The method has no theoretical justification and may not converge. In a two-leg example both finite differences are zero, yet a joint increase would raise revenue (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: a C++ simulator runs EMSR-BL, LP-BL, SBL from each starting point, and bid prices (BP), under standard and theft nesting. All policies run on the same simulated request sequences (p. 11).
  - Single leg, five classes, factor grid over load factor, CV, fare ratio and arrival order, 100,000 runs (pp. 11–12). Over EMSR-BL: +0.01–0.68% under standard nesting and +1.80–7.93% under theft nesting. Over LP-BL: up to +2.94% and +11.79% (Tables 1–3, p. 12).
  - Single-hub network with five cities and 30 itineraries (Table 4, p. 13).
  - Exact benchmark: single leg, 100 seats, six classes, 2,500 runs, against the SDP optimum of $26,594.5. With 20 updates, SBL's gap falls below 0.17% at 95% confidence (Table 5, p. 13).
  - Ablation: without value functions (SBL_NVV) the gains shrink, and under theft nesting they turn negative (−0.11%, Table 7, p. 14).
  - Hub networks with 5, 10 and 15 spokes, 1,000 runs: +0.05–0.61% over EMSR-BL and +0.24–1.29% over LP-BL (Table 8, p. 14).
  - Own computing cost: value-function estimation took about 50–60 CPU hours for 15 spokes on a Pentium IV; the online step takes seconds (Table 9 and text, p. 15).
  - SBL with 20 updates beats BP updated 20 to 100 times by 1.03–2.67% (Table 10, p. 15).
  - Limits reported:
    - convergence is non-monotone (pp. 12–13);
    - "slower" settings in one test case "may be part of the reason" for its better result (p. 14);
    - w = 0.5 was chosen "without much tuning" (p. 15).
  - No LP solver is named.
- **D6 Literature positioning**:
  - It builds on Glover et al.'s LP (p. 2), Smith–Penn's DAVN, Williamson's LP-BL and Belobaba's EMSRb (pp. 2–3), Robinson's simulation-based optimization (p. 5) and Bertsimas–Popescu's certainty-equivalent ADP (p. 4).
  - It judges earlier attempts by name (p. 4):
    - Wollmer's binary model has too many variables.
    - Williamson and de Boer et al. (2002, the coauthor's own work) found the deterministic LP the best input for LP-BL.
    - Curry's method cannot nest all classes on a leg.
    - Prorating has no "intuitive explanation".
  - Self-positioning: other methods have "better theoretical justification" but oversimplify, or optimize a different policy from the one implemented (p. 10).
  - Dynamic bid prices are out of scope (pp. 3, 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Numbered contributions include a "first to" claim (p. 2).
  - The abstract and the conclusion both hedge with "Preliminary simulation experiments suggest" (pp. 1, 16), and the heuristic status is stated on pp. 7 and 10.
  - Results are summarized as "Insights Gained" bullets (p. 15). The motivation opens on an industry anecdote, Delta's $50 million estimate (p. 1).
  - Gains of 0.05–0.61% over EMSR-BL are called "practically significant" by appeal to airline scale (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm with simulation evidence and no theory. The limits are concrete:
  - a simplified demand model and few test problems (p. 16);
  - no overbooking, cancellations or buy-down (p. 11);
  - no convergence guarantee (p. 7);
  - dynamic bid prices not compared (p. 15).
  Future work: "more extensive testing" (p. 16), and value functions updated as forecasts change (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- **Method 5 ✅ evidence**: the baselines are the rules airlines run (p. 3): DAVN with EMSR-BL, "currently being used by only a handful of airlines worldwide", LP-BL, and Simpson's bid prices. They are scored by revenue on common simulated request streams (p. 11), and the method's own computing cost is reported (p. 15). Caveat: demand is a synthetic Pólya process, with no partner data and no field test (pp. 11, 16).
- **Method 4 ⚠ variant**: the incumbent heuristic is both the starting point and the opponent (pp. 10–11), but the solver is a stochastic-gradient heuristic. An exact-DP benchmark on a single-leg instance takes the place of a certificate (p. 13), and the paper calls its method a heuristic (pp. 7, 10).
- **Method 1 ✗ contradiction**: the network problem is not written as an exact LP or MIO. The integer program is set aside as "computationally unattractive" (p. 2), the LP only fixes the nesting order (p. 10), and the decision is optimized by simulation with "no theoretical justification" (p. 7). This is an HS data point for Tension T2: a heuristic engine as the main method, already in 2005.
- **Also**:
  - Heuristic 4 ✅: negative and failed cases are printed in the body, namely the −0.11% ablation, the two-leg stall and non-monotone convergence (pp. 7, 12–14).
  - Tension T6: the "practically significant" headline rests on gains of 0.05–0.61% (p. 14).
- **New-pattern candidates**:
  - *optimize the policy actually deployed* (pp. 3, 10);
  - *exact optimum on a small solvable instance as the certificate for a heuristic* (p. 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Parallel-bookkeeping finite differences: in each simulated path, branch a counterfactual path, with that one limit raised by a seat, only when exactly one binding limit rejects a request, at most once per bucket. n paths then estimate all N finite differences (pp. 6–7).
- Common random numbers: run every policy on the same simulated request sequences so that revenue differences are sharp (p. 11).
- Benchmark a heuristic against the exact DP optimum on a small instance, and report the gap as a 95%-confidence bound (p. 13).
- Ablate the component claimed to be essential (here the value functions) and show the gain shrinks or turns negative (pp. 13–14).
- Put value-function grid points where simulated states fall: mean ± 3 sd per leg, on the axes only (qL points instead of q^L) (p. 8).
- Interpolate a concave value function as a weighted average of the chord (lower bound) and the tangent envelope (upper bound) (pp. 8–9).
- For a noisy finite-difference search over integers: cap each step, interpolate back when the sign flips, project onto the feasible set, and stop at kmax (p. 7).
- Separate heavy estimation (weekly, offline), improvement (overnight) and implementation (online), and time each part (pp. 10, 15).

**Quotes**:
- "Attempting to solve this type of optimization problem with a stochastic gradient method has no theoretical justification, but is a heuristic based on the intuition behind gradient descent methods." (p. 7)
- "most of the methods that have been proposed in the literature have a better theoretical justification, but they either oversimplify the problem or are directed at optimizing a different policy than the one that is actually implemented" (p. 10)

### S014 · A robust optimization approach to inventory theory (Operations Research 2006, DOI 10.1287/opre.1050.0238)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1–19: all sections, including the proofs of Lemmas 3.1, 3.4 and 4.1 and Theorems 3.2–4.3. Figures were read through their captions, axes and the text that describes them; references skimmed. Title and authors (Bertsimas, Thiele) checked on p. 1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Aurélie Thiele (Lehigh University, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the inventory application of SW3's budgeted robustness, which the paper calls "the technique we will use" (p. 2). Received April 2003; accepted November 2004 after three revisions (p. 1). Thiele's 2004 MIT thesis on robust supply-chain and revenue management is listed in S027 (S027 p. 39), and the 2006 tutorial restates this paper (S027 pp. 16–21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + SD; LO and IO as engines; inventory as the AP testbed}:
  - DP, the standard tool since Clark–Scarf, "suffers from dimensionality problems and assumes full knowledge of the demand distribution" (abstract, p. 1).
  - Simulation-based and ADP alternatives need distributions or are hard to implement, so practice falls back on intuitive suboptimal policies (p. 1).
  - Scarf's min-max approach and its extensions still rely on DP (p. 2).
  - The paper states four requirements for a replacement (p. 2): distribution-free, valid for any network topology, intuitive, and tractable while revealing the structure of the optimal policy.
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**:
  - Each period's demand is an uncertain parameter in an interval, with budgets Γ_k on the cumulative scaled deviations up to period k (p. 4).
  - Each holding and shortage constraint is robustified by LP duality (pp. 4–5).
  - Theorem 3.2 (p. 6): the robust optimum is the nominal optimum for a modified, explicitly computable demand, w'_k = w̄_k + (p−h)/(p+h)·(A_k − A_{k−1}).
  - Kept: the deterministic LP/MIP inventory model and the base-stock structure. Changed: no distribution and no DP recursion (pp. 2, 6).
  - Extensions: capacitated orders (Theorem 3.3, p. 7); capacitated inventory with a time-varying, decreasing capacity (Lemma 3.4, Theorem 3.5, p. 7); tree networks through echelons (Theorem 4.2, p. 9); capacitated networks (Theorem 4.3, p. 10).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Finite horizon and discrete time; zero lead time, with constant lead times allowed (Remark 3, p. 4); backlogging and linear dynamics (pp. 3–4).
  - Costs: ordering cost K·1{u>0} + c·u, and a convex piecewise-linear holding and shortage cost with p > c (pp. 3–4).
  - Demand w_k ∈ [w̄_k − ŵ_k, w̄_k + ŵ_k]. Budgets are nondecreasing and rise by at most 1 per period (p. 4); networks have one budget sequence per sink node (pp. 8–9).
  - Budget selection assumes uncorrelated demands with known means and variances (p. 10). It uses approximations that ignore fixed costs, which the paper says serve only to choose Γ (p. 11).
  - The structural results need tree networks; for general supply chains the reformulation "loses some of its meaning" (p. 10).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 2.1 (Bertsimas–Sim): strong duality on a per-row auxiliary LP gives the robust LP counterpart (p. 3).
  - The same device per period (auxiliary LP 13, p. 4) gives formulation (14), an LP if K = 0 and a MIP if K > 0. Its duals q and r measure sensitivities and the worst-case cumulative deviation (p. 5).
  - Remark 1 (p. 4): the worst cases of a period's holding and shortage constraints are opposite, so no single demand realizes both.
  - Remark 2 (p. 4): one budget over the whole horizon would overprotect the early periods.
  - Lemma 3.1: an explicit dual-feasible solution proves base-stock optimality for the nominal problem (pp. 5–6).
  - Proof of Theorem 3.2 (p. 6): fix q* and r*, use max{h(x + A), p(−x + A)} = max{hx', −px'} + 2ph/(p+h)·A, and change the stock variable (eq. 23).
  - Lemma 4.1: fix the coupling right-hand sides, or dualize them (Lagrangean relaxation), to decouple the echelons (p. 9).
  - A two-installation counterexample shows that in general networks even the nominal optimal policy need not be base stock (Figure 1, pp. 9–10).
  - Lemma 5.1: a tight bound on E[max(0, X − a)] from mean and variance (Lo 1987; Bertsimas–Popescu 2002), used to choose the budgets (p. 11).
  - Closed form (44): Γ_k = min{(σ/ŵ)·√((k+1)/(1−α²)), k+1} with α = (p−h)/(p+h), so Γ_k grows like √(k+1) (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Single station: T = 20, c = 1, h = 4, p = 6, i.i.d. demand with mean 100 and sd 20, ŵ = 2σ (p. 11).
  - Protocol (p. 12): DP is computed with an assumed distribution, a binomial (parameters not stated) or a five-point Gaussian. Both policies are scored under realized gamma, lognormal or Gaussian demand with the same two moments, on 1,000 samples, by R = 100·(E[DP] − E[ROB])/E[DP].
  - Results:
    - The robust policy leads by up to 10–13% when DP assumed a binomial, and by at most 0.4% when DP assumed a Gaussian (p. 12).
    - DP wins below a holding-cost threshold h0 (p. 13). That threshold is about 2.5 for p = 6: the text reads "25", and Figure 7's h axis runs from 1 to 10.
    - Fill rates are also compared (p. 13).
  - Networks: a series system (T = 10, K = 10) and a tree network (T = 5) against myopic policies, reoptimized every period with a budget-shifting rule (pp. 13, 16).
    - r lies mostly in 20–45% when a binomial is assumed and 15–35% when a Gaussian is assumed (p. 16).
    - In the tree, myopic wins with small probability, and the robust advantage grows with the horizon (p. 17).
  - Not reported: the solver, run times, or a nominal (Γ = 0) baseline.
- **D6 Literature positioning**:
  - Inventory theory: it positions against DP (Clark–Scarf, Bertsekas), IPA and stochastic approximation (Glasserman, Fu, Tayur), ADP (Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis) and Scarf's min-max line, all of which need distributions or DP (pp. 1–2).
  - RO: it retells the line Soyster → Ben-Tal–Nemirovski and El Ghaoui (ellipsoids give conic quadratic programs) → Bertsimas–Sim (polyhedral sets, linear counterparts, a discrete extension) (p. 2).
  - The core tools are the authors' own: Bertsimas–Sim 2003 and 2004 (pp. 2–3) and Bertsimas–Popescu 2002 (p. 11).
  - It claims validation "on benchmark problems where the optimal stochastic policy is already known" and on supply chains where it is not (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Four numbered contributions, the first hedged: "preliminary computational results are quite promising" (p. 2), "very promising" in the abstract (p. 1).
  - The class-preservation claim recurs from abstract to conclusion (pp. 1, 2, 5, 9, 19).
  - Theorems are followed by managerial remarks: safety stock rises when shortage costs more than holding (p. 7), the robust model as a "reasonable worst-case" approach (p. 4), and its "striking similarities with the nominal problem" (p. 7).
  - The conclusion says the robust policy "often outperforms the nominal policy" (p. 19), a comparison that §5 does not report.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory (the structure of robust policies, closed-form thresholds) plus a computational study.
  - Stated limits:
    - general non-tree chains lose the base-stock structure and the modified-demand reading (pp. 9–10);
    - budget selection rests on approximations (p. 11);
    - DP wins at low holding cost (p. 13);
    - short horizons favour myopic policies (p. 17).
  - There is no future-work list.
  - Not discussed: whether the per-period, per-constraint worst cases can occur together across periods. Remark 1 covers only the two constraints of a single period (p. 4). This is the device that Method 1's correction targets; the critique is recorded in note 05 §4.3, and the critics were not re-read here.

**Method links**:
- **Method 1 ✅ evidence**: the robust model stays an LP (no fixed cost) or a MIP (fixed cost) for any tree topology (pp. 1, 5, 9). The duals are read as worst-case cumulative deviations (p. 5), and the boundary is printed for general networks (pp. 9–10). 🔴 The per-constraint device (Remark 1, p. 4) is the one Method 1's correction says to test on a two-period example.
- **Method 3 ⚠ variant**: the budgets Γ_k are the knob "to trade off performance and protection" (abstract, p. 1), but no price curve is published. Instead the knob is set from mean and variance by minimizing a worst-case expected-cost bound (Lemma 5.1, Algorithm 5.2, pp. 10–11; closed form p. 12). This is Method 3's correction (set the knob from data), already practised with moments in 2006.
- **Method 5 ⚠ variant**: policies are scored by realized cost against DP built on an assumed distribution and against practitioners' myopic policies (pp. 1, 12, 16–17). There is no owner's rule and no partner data.
- **Tension T6 ✅**: the conclusion's "often outperforms the nominal policy" (p. 19) has no matching experiment in §5.
- **New-pattern candidates**:
  - *robust = nominal on modified data* (Theorems 3.2, 3.3, 3.5, 4.2 and 4.3; pp. 6–7, 9–10);
  - *misspecification duel* (p. 12);
  - *knob set by a distributional bound* (pp. 10–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Budget cumulative deviations period by period, with 0 ≤ Γ_k − Γ_{k−1} ≤ 1. A single budget over the horizon would overprotect the early periods (p. 4, Remark 2).
- To get policy structure from a robust LP: fix the optimal duals, rewrite the robust constraints as nominal constraints on a shifted state, and inherit the nominal policy's structure (proof of Theorem 3.2, p. 6).
- Decouple a network by fixing or dualizing the coupling constraints, then apply single-node results (Lemma 4.1, p. 9).
- Choose Γ by minimizing a distribution-free worst-case expected cost built from known means and variances (Lemma 5.1, Algorithm 5.2, pp. 10–11).
- Score policies under a realized distribution different from the one the incumbent assumed, with the first two moments held equal (p. 12).
- In rolling-horizon use, shift the budgets instead of re-optimizing them, so that the first period is not overprotected at every step (eq. 45, p. 16).
- Diagnostic suggested by Remark 1 (p. 4) and not run in the paper: check whether a single demand path attains the worst case of all per-period constraints at once. If not, the counterpart is conservative.

**Quotes**:
- "the robust problem is of the same difficulty as the nominal problem, that is, a linear programming problem when there are no fixed costs, and a mixed-integer programming problem when fixed costs are present" (p. 1)
- "there is no feasible demand in the uncertain set that realizes both constraints of each pair" (p. 4)

### S022 · Tractable approximations to robust conic optimization problems (Mathematical Programming Ser. B 2006, DOI 10.1007/s10107-005-0677-1)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1–32: all sections, Appendix A (proof of Proposition 1, pp. 27–28) and Appendix B (reduced formulations under independent uncertainties, pp. 28–31); references skimmed. Title and authors (Bertsimas, Sim) checked on p. 1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Melvyn Sim (NUS Business School, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: carries SW3's class-keeping budgeted robustness from LP to SOCP, SDP and general cones. Received April 2004, accepted July 2005; both authors acknowledge Singapore-MIT Alliance support (p. 1). Sim's 2004 MIT thesis *Robust optimization* is listed in S027 (S027 p. 39).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + CP}:
  - Chance constraints are non-convex and generally intractable (p. 2).
  - Earlier robust counterparts show "a lateral increase in complexity": LPs become SOCPs, SOCPs become SDPs, and SDPs become NP-hard (abstract, p. 1).
  - Two criteria are set first (p. 2):
    - (a) keep the nominal problem's tractability "both theoretically and most importantly practically": LPs stay LPs, SOCPs stay SOCPs, SDPs stay SDPs;
    - (b) give a guarantee on the probability of feasibility under natural distributions.
  - A class-by-class audit (LP, QCQP, SOCP, SDP, general cones) shows where the literature fails each criterion (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**:
  - The data are written as D̃ = D0 + Σ_j ΔD_j z̃_j (p. 3).
  - The classical robust constraint is replaced by the relaxed constraint (8): f(x, D0) + Σ_j [f(x, ΔD_j)v_j + f(x, −ΔD_j)w_j] ≥ 0 for all v, w ≥ 0 with ||v + w|| ≤ Ω (p. 6).
  - This constraint implies classical robustness when f is concave and positively homogeneous in D, and equals it when f is linear in D (Proposition 2, pp. 6–7).
  - It reduces to f(x, D0) ≥ ||s||_* with s_j = max{−f(x, ΔD_j), −f(x, −ΔD_j)} (Theorem 1, p. 8). That adds at most |N| + 1 variables, 2|N| constraints "of the same nature as the nominal problem" and one dual-norm constraint (p. 12).
  - Kept from Bertsimas–Sim: the knob Ω and sets defined by norms. Changed: robustness is defined through perturbation directions, so the nominal constraint fixes the class of the counterpart (pp. 6–8, 12).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The z̃_j are i.i.d. with mean zero; |N| ranges from a few factors to one per data entry (pp. 3–4).
  - Norms are "absolute" (||u|| = || |u| ||), which covers l_p, l2∩l∞ and l1∩l∞ (p. 4).
  - Assumption 1: f is concave and positively homogeneous in D (p. 5). This covers LP, QCQP (through its SOCP form), SOCP, and SDP (through λ_min) (p. 5, Table 1).
  - The probability results assume z̃ ~ N(0, I) and the l2 norm (Theorem 2(b), p. 15; Theorem 4(b), p. 22).
  - General cones are closed, convex and pointed, with nonempty interior (pp. 3, 20).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - The perturbation is split into nonnegative parts, using the monotonicity of absolute norms (Proposition 1, p. 4; Proposition 2, pp. 6–7). An equality between two maximization problems gives the dual-norm form (proof of Theorem 1, pp. 8–9).
  - g(x, ·) is a seminorm, and for each class g(x, ΔD_j) = ||H(x, ΔD_j)||_g with H linear (Proposition 3, Table 3, pp. 10–12). A table gives the size and class of the counterpart for each norm (Table 4, p. 13; derivations in Appendix B, pp. 28–31).
  - Norm-equivalence constants α = α1·α2 (Proposition 4, p. 14): 1 for LP and SOCP(1), √2 for QCQP and SOCP(2), √m for SDP.
  - Theorem 2: P(violation) ≤ (√e·Ω/α)·exp(−Ω²/(2α²)) (pp. 15–18). The proof applies Markov's inequality to a Gaussian quadratic form, then a spectral decomposition, Jensen's inequality and an optimized choice of θ and β.
  - The bound is compared with a structure-blind χ² bound (19) (p. 18) and with Nemirovski's large-deviation bounds (Theorem 3, pp. 19–20).
  - General cones:
    - f(x, D) = max{θ : A(x, D) ⪰_K θV}, with conic duality (Proposition 5, pp. 20–21);
    - the norm ||·||_{K,V} (Proposition 6, pp. 21–22) and Theorem 4 (pp. 22–23).
  - For homogeneous cones the bound does not depend on V (Theorem 5, p. 24); α = √2 for the second-order cone and √m for the PSD cone (Proposition 7, pp. 24–26).
  - A feasibility-preserving update of V serves as a heuristic (Theorem 6, pp. 26–27).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - None; the paper gives only illustrative bound calculations (Table 6, p. 19). They show the Ω needed for ε from 10^−1 to 10^−6 with m = n = 100 and |N| = 495,000. For example, ε = 10^−6 needs Ω = 5.68 for LP and 56.8 for SDP, against 706.9 for the structure-blind bound.
  - Computational support is left to Bertsimas and Brown's control application (p. 7).
- **D6 Literature positioning**:
  - It credits Ben-Tal–Nemirovski and El Ghaoui et al. for the robust problem (3) (p. 2), and Nemirovski for tractable SDP approximations with guarantees (p. 3).
  - It extends Bertsimas–Sim and Bertsimas–Pachamanova–Sim (pp. 2, 4, 10).
  - It concedes that Nemirovski's results give stronger bounds (p. 20).
  - It claims novelty for (a) and (b) together, and for general cones, where "there are no results available" (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The introduction opens with the two criteria, and the conclusion answers them point by point, hedged with "we feel" (p. 2) and "we believe" (p. 27).
  - Theorem–proof–remark style, with tables that summarize results per class (Tables 1–7).
  - Gaps are stated plainly: no theoretical evidence of closeness (p. 7), the looseness of the bound (Remark 1, p. 18), and an open problem (p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory, namely a tractable, class-keeping robust counterpart for general conic constraints with a probability bound.
  - Limits:
    - The counterpart is conservative, and its closeness to classical robustness is unproven (p. 7).
    - The bound loosens with ρ, which can be as large as the rank of R (Remark 1, p. 18). The proof's last step uses B^ρ ≤ B, so a solution designed for ε may in fact be over-protected. (In the extracted sentence a control character stands where the symbol before n should be, p. 18.)
    - The perturbations are assumed i.i.d. Gaussian (pp. 15, 27).
    - Making V a variable, or the parameter λ in the SOCP form of a quadratic constraint, would break convexity; this is left open (p. 27).
  - Observation on the LP case:
    - For LPs the class is kept only under polyhedral norms (l1, l∞, l1∩l∞). The paper's bound is proved for the l2 norm, and under l2 a robust LP becomes an SOCP (Table 4, p. 13; Theorem 2(b), p. 15).
    - The text says the l2 norm maintains "the nature of the original conic optimization problem of SOCP and SDP" (p. 13). LP bounds for polyhedral sets are cited from earlier work (p. 2).
  - Future work: the iterative heuristic for V and the open question on λ (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- **Method 1 ✅ evidence**: class preservation is criterion (a) and the headline result (abstract, pp. 2, 27). The device, not the solver, is redesigned (Theorem 1, p. 8; Table 4, p. 13), and the boundary is printed (pp. 7, 18, 20, 27). A nuance on what "tractable" means (02 C2): in 2006 the paper puts practical tractability first ("most importantly practically", p. 2) but measures it only by class and size counts (Table 4, p. 13), with no solve times.
- **Method 3 ⚠ variant**: one knob Ω, a solution-independent violation bound, and a lookup table of Ω per class and ε (Theorem 2, p. 15; Table 6, p. 19), described as "explicit ways for selecting parameters that control the robustness" (p. 3). There is no objective-loss or price curve, because there are no computations.
- **New-pattern candidates**:
  - *criteria-first, class-by-class audit of prior art* (pp. 2–3);
  - *knob set by a distributional bound* (pp. 15, 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the perturbation as D0 + Σ ΔD_j z_j and split each z_j into nonnegative parts (v, w ≥ 0, ||v + w|| ≤ Ω). For concave, positively homogeneous constraints this gives a conservative counterpart in the nominal class (pp. 6–8).
- Choose the norm from a size table: l2 adds one variable and one SOC constraint, while l1∩l∞ and l2∩l∞ add O(|N|) variables and constraints (Table 4, p. 13).
- Make a probability bound structure-aware (Proposition 4, Theorem 2, pp. 14–18). Bound the ratio α between the problem's own norm and the Euclidean or Frobenius norm, then apply a Chernoff-type bound to a Gaussian quadratic form. Show the gain against a structure-blind bound (pp. 18–19).
- For an arbitrary cone, define f(x, D) = max{θ : A(x, D) ⪰_K θV} and obtain concavity and homogeneity from conic duality (Proposition 5, pp. 20–21).
- When perturbations are independent and entry-wise, conic constraints on single-entry perturbations collapse to linear ones (p. 13; Appendix B, pp. 28–31).
- Publish the Ω values for each target ε and problem class, so that users can set the knob without solving anything (Table 6, p. 19).

**Read level**: full. The indexed file is the authors' preprint dated March 2006 (39 pp.; p. 1 prints the first author as "Dimtris Bertsimas"). Read pp. 1–39, including the reference list. Page numbers are preprint pages; the printed TutORials pagination is not in the file.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Aurélie Thiele (Lehigh University, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a teaching synthesis of the 2003–2006 RO programme (pp. 3, 8–35; references pp. 36–39). It draws on:
- Bertsimas–Sim (budgets, discrete problems);

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + DA + SD; LO and IO as engines; examples from AP}:
  - Traditional decision models assume accurate parameters and known distributions, which practice rarely has (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Imperfect information threatens both feasibility and cost (p. 2).
  - The need for "an alternative, non-probabilistic, theory" is called pressing (p. 2): volatile tastes, innovation and shorter life cycles make information scarce and quickly obsolete.
  - Three aims (p. 3): handle static estimation errors, make dynamic decisions tractable, and build uncertainty sets from data and risk preferences.
  - The paper gives a [stated-joint] rationale for the budget Γ, though not its origin: two operations-management maxims and a random-walk picture (pp. 3–6).
- **D2 Key idea and structure**:
  - Static:
    - Range forecasts, and the greater accuracy of aggregate forecasts, become intervals plus a budget Γ_i per constraint (pp. 3–6).
    - Strong duality gives an LP counterpart (eq. 12, p. 7).
    - Binary problems are solved as n nominal subproblems (pp. 12–13).
    - The approach extends to general polyhedral sets and norms (pp. 13–14).
  - Dynamic:
    - With per-period budgets, the robust inventory LP is equivalent to a deterministic problem with modified demand, hence a base-stock policy (pp. 16–20).
    - Dynamic budgets come from robust Bellman equations (pp. 22–23).
    - Affine adaptability (Ben-Tal et al.) and finite adaptability (Bertsimas–Caramanis) follow (pp. 23–30).
  - Data-driven:
    - A coherent risk measure on the sample distribution equals a robust constraint over A = conv{Σ q_i a_i : q ∈ Q}.
    - Comonotone risk measures give polyhedral sets from a single weight vector q (pp. 30–35).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - LPs with uncertainty in A, without loss of generality (p. 4); independent coefficients in symmetric intervals (p. 5).
  - The violation bound holds for symmetric distributions on the interval (p. 8).
  - Example 2.2 uses the CLT with a known variance, and a worst-case expected cost over symmetric distributions (pp. 10–11).
  - Dynamic models have linear dynamics, convex costs and one source of uncertainty per period (pp. 16, 22).
  - The data-driven part uses N historical observations, assumed distinct, as the sample distribution (p. 31).
  - The tutorial restricts itself to LPs and polyhedral sets (pp. 14, 36).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Strong duality on each row's inner LP, with the optimal p and q given in closed form (pp. 7–8).
  - The n-subproblem decomposition for robust 0–1 problems (pp. 12–13).
  - A robust Bellman recursion with z_t ∈ {−1, 0, 1} and a two-dimensional cost-to-go, computed by increasing the budget and decreasing time (p. 23).
  - K-adaptability:
    - Under right-hand-side uncertainty, the robust problem is the deterministic problem at the corner b̃0 (p. 26).
    - For K = 2 there is a covering-pair characterization (p. 27).
    - A test based on directions of dual unboundedness: 2-adaptability gains more than η if and only if the family A_η misses the partition (pp. 29–30).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn managers' forecasting practice into a set: a range forecast becomes an interval, and "aggregate forecasts are more accurate" becomes a budget on the sum of scaled deviations (pp. 3–6).
- Take a one-dimensional example with a closed-form robust solution and plot cost against the knob, to show both failure modes: nominal and worst case (pp. 10–12).
- Size Γ by Γ ≥ 1 + Φ^{-1}(1 − ε)·√n and point out the √n scaling (p. 8).
- For robust 0–1 problems with coefficient uncertainty, solve n nominal subproblems of the same size and keep the best (pp. 12–13).
- For dynamic budgets, run a robust Bellman recursion with z_t ∈ {−1, 0, 1} and the remaining budget as a second state (p. 23).
- Build a polyhedral uncertainty set from N data points and a weight vector q. q = (1/N)e gives the sample mean, and q = (1, 0, …, 0) gives the convex hull of the data (pp. 33–35).
- Before solving, test whether K-adaptability can pay: build A_η from the directions of dual unboundedness and check whether the partition avoids it (pp. 29–30).
