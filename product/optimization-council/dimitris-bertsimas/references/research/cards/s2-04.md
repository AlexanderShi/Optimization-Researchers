### S074 · Restaurant Revenue Management (Operations Research 2003, DOI 10.1287/opre.51.3.472.14956)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–15 (abstract, §§1–7, Tables 1–5, endnotes; displayed formulas read through the surrounding prose). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Shioda match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Romy Shioda (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an AP paper of the revenue-management line, received January 2001 (p. 1); its reservation model borrows Karaesmen and van Ryzin's airline overbooking device (pp. 7–9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP; IO as engine, SD for the dynamic and stochastic layer}: Floor managers seat parties first-come-first-serve, by intuition (p. 1), although point-of-sale systems already record arrivals, party sizes and service phases (pp. 1, 3). The authors know of no rigorous dynamic seating model (p. 2). The lever is nesting: when to hold a large table for a larger party while smaller parties wait (p. 1). Exact dynamic programming is ruled out by the curse of dimensionality (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: An IP is re-solved at every arrival and departure. It uses expected demand, penalties for waiting and for seating small parties at large tables, and FCFS order within each party size (eqs. 1–9, pp. 3–5). Two refinements follow: a stochastic IP with three demand scenarios (pp. 6, 12), and an ADP whose value function is the IP's optimal value at the post-decision state (eq. 19, p. 7). With reservations, a stochastic-gradient method sets booking levels in advance, and the ADP seats parties online (pp. 7–11).
- **D3 Model and data**: Even party sizes, revenue increasing in size, service as phases with expected durations; every party waits exactly Max periods and then leaves; only expected values enter the IP (pp. 2–3). The booking model approximates binomial no-shows by Poisson and has no nesting and no queues (pp. 8–9). Data: a "contrived restaurant" with 9 tables and 16 periods of 15 minutes, at load factors 0.68, 0.93 and 1.54 with constant or varied arrivals (pp. 11–12). For reservations: Soto's in Atlanta, 90 and 120 customers, about 30% with reservations, 10% no-shows (pp. 13–14). The penalty weights were "chosen from empirical testing" (p. 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: FCFS within a party size is enforced by big-M binaries (eqs. 5–8, pp. 4–5). A bid-price benchmark comes from the duals of the LP relaxation (eq. 20, p. 7). The on-the-day booking model is a single-commodity min-cost flow, so its value is jointly concave and submodular; this gives the Kushner–Clark convergence of the stochastic gradient to Kuhn–Tucker points (Theorem 1, p. 9). The paper proves no theorem of its own.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: CPLEX on a Pentium II under Linux (p. 12). Against Full-Nesting FCFS, ADP gains 2.11–2.96% at low and medium load and 12.67–13.13% at high load, with similar waits and 0.21–1.07 s per decision; the airline bid-price rule loses to FCFS in all six scenarios (Tables 2–3, p. 13). My reading of Table 2: the stochastic IP earns less than the deterministic IP in three of six scenarios, although the text says revenue rises monotonically with sophistication (p. 13). Full Nesting, the 0% reference, is the best FCFS variant in only three scenarios; at low, constant load the 1-Up FCFS rule beats both IPs, and ADP's margin over it is 0.47% (my arithmetic). With reservations, DSAR gains 3.42% and 7.26% over Full-Nesting FCFS (Tables 4–5, p. 14). The text gives "3.5% to 6.9%" over all FCFS models at 90 customers (p. 14); by my arithmetic Table 4 gives 3.4–6.0%.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Three contributions: IP, stochastic-IP and ADP seating policies; a stochastic-gradient booking model; and revenue gains over FCFS without longer waits (p. 2). The conclusion says optimization has "a role to play" (p. 15). Future work: movable tables, balking and reneging, and more empirical tests through online reservation providers (p. 15). Admitted limits: simplified waiting behaviour (p. 3) and restaurant-dependent parameters, to be tuned by simulation (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 7, 12–15): the baseline is the industry's rule (FCFS), built in three versions, and decisions are scored on revenue together with waits and the share served; one owner supplied data for the reservation study (pp. 13, 15). But the main study uses a contrived restaurant, nothing is field-tested, and the fixed reference is the strongest FCFS variant in only half the scenarios (Table 2, p. 13). Step 2 ("prefer the stronger naive rule") holds only in part.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 4, 7): a DP-owned problem is re-solved as a small IP at each event, and the IP value stands in for the value function. The authors call the IP "a viable heuristic" (p. 4), not an exact reformulation.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 12): M and γ are knobs for properties the owner cares about (waiting time, table fit), but they were set by trial on the evaluation simulation and no revenue–waiting curve is published. This is the pattern behind Method 3's correction (knobs fixed arbitrarily).
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 3): the "why now" is data, not solver speed: point-of-sale systems already collect what the model needs.
- New-pattern candidate: *math programming as the value function inside dynamic programming* (p. 7; also S142 pp. 10–11): approximate an intractable DP by re-solving an IP or LP whose optimal value scores each candidate action.

**Transferable techniques**:
- One-step lookahead with an IP value: score each feasible seating by immediate revenue minus a nesting cost plus the IP value of the post-decision state; deciding party by party in arrival order makes the fairness constraints unnecessary (eq. 19, p. 7).
- In a stochastic-gradient scheme, choose an inner model whose value is a min-cost flow; network structure then supplies the concavity and submodularity that the convergence theorem needs (p. 9).
- Implement the incumbent rule in several variants and print all of them, together with the rival school's heuristic (bid prices from LP duals), next to your methods (pp. 7, 13).
- Report the run time per decision to show that the policy can run online (Table 3, p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "However, capturing the states of the system exactly in a stochastic dynamic programming sense would be computationally intractable, thus making our IP model a viable heuristic." (p. 4)
- "These values were chosen from empirical testing, thus they may not be the optimal." (p. 12)

### S207 · From fluid relaxations to practical algorithms for high-multiplicity job-shop scheduling: The holding cost objective (Operations Research 2003, DOI 10.1287/opre.51.5.798.16748)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–16 (abstract, §§1–6, Tables 4–9; the algebra of Lemmas 2, 4 and 5 on pp. 5–6 and 10–11 skimmed). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Gamarnik and Sethuraman match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: David Gamarnik (IBM T. J. Watson), Jay Sethuraman (Columbia) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the holding-cost sequel in a fluid-relaxation scheduling line after Bertsimas–Gamarnik (1999) and Bertsimas–Sethuraman (2002) for makespan (p. 1); the fluid LP is solved with Luo–Bertsimas (1999) (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD IO; LO as engine}: Minimize total holding cost, with weighted completion time as a special case, in a job shop holding N copies of each job type (p. 1). The weighted-completion job shop has no PTAS unless P = NP (p. 3). Queueing papers had turned fluid solutions into policies heuristically, mostly without performance analysis (p. 2). The entry: round the optimal fluid solution so that the schedule is asymptotically optimal as N grows (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Solve the fluid relaxation once, make its breakpoint queue lengths integral, and discretize piece by piece with a revised fluid-synchronization algorithm. The one change from the makespan version: no task may start before its nominal start time, because early processing raises holding costs downstream (pp. 3, 6–9).
- **D3 Model and data**: A deterministic, nonpreemptive job shop with integer processing times and α_i·N initial jobs of each type, without arrivals (pp. 2–4). The fluid optimum is a lower bound only when the weights do not depend on the stage; in general the paper proves Z_JS ≥ Z_F − O(N) and says it could not prove more (pp. 4–5). Data: 20 OR-library instances with 5–20 machines and 5–50 job types, all weights 1 (p. 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Z_F = CN² by scaling, and fluidizing an optimal discrete schedule gives Z_JS ≥ Z_F − O(N) (Theorem 1, pp. 4–5). Integral breakpoints cost O(N) extra (Lemma 2, Proposition 2, pp. 5–6). The per-piece discretization error is O(N); summed over a constant number of pieces it gives Z_D ≤ Z_F + O(N) and Z_D/Z_JS ≤ 1 + O(1/N) (Theorem 6, pp. 11–12). A single-machine example shows that every fluid-based algorithm has O(N) error in the worst case (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Every run took under two minutes on a SUN workstation (p. 12). Relative error against the fluid lower bound falls from 1.27–2.89 at N = 1 to 0.007–0.025 at N = 500 (Table 4, p. 12). Against eight dispatch rules of similar implementation complexity, FSA-HC is best on 6 of 20 instances at N = 1, SRPT wins all but three at N = 10, and FSA-HC wins everywhere at N = 500; the best rule plateaus at 0.04–0.395 up to N = 10,000 (Tables 5–9, pp. 13–16). The losing regime is stated in the abstract (p. 1).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Claimed: the algorithm, with an explicit O(1/N) rate and a single fluid solve, and the conceptual point that a makespan method can serve the weighted-completion objective (pp. 2–3). Printed limits: the complexity of the fluid control problem is open (pp. 4, 13); the hidden constant is about an order of magnitude worse than for makespan (p. 13); at low multiplicity the best dispatch rule does better (p. 1); the conjectured limit of the rules' errors is left unproved (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2–4, 12–13): a problem owned by discrete scheduling is entered through the queueing tradition's fluid model, a continuous LP. The device is relax-and-round with an asymptotic guarantee, not an exact class-keeping reformulation. The boundary is printed (open complexity, O(N) worst-case error).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 12–16): the duel with incumbent rules at matched implementation complexity and the statement of where the method wins (moderate to high multiplicity) are present, and the losing regime is in the abstract (Heuristic 4). There is no exact solve and no warm start; the gap to the fluid lower bound plays the part of a certificate (p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate: *scale sweep against a relaxation yardstick* (Tables 4–9, pp. 12–16): replicate the instance N times, measure every method's gap to the relaxation bound, and report the N at which the asymptotically optimal method overtakes the heuristics.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure relative error against the relaxation's lower bound over a sweep of the scaling parameter; where the relaxation is a valid lower bound, the gap also bounds the true optimality gap (pp. 12–13).
- Proof skeleton for asymptotic optimality: fluidize the discrete optimum to bound the relaxation from below; make breakpoints integral at O(N) cost; bound each piece's discretization error by O(N); sum over a constant number of pieces (pp. 4–6, 10–12).
- Choose opponent heuristics of the same implementation complexity and say so; report the best one per instance and its plateau at large N (pp. 13, 16).

**Quotes**:
- "For problems of low to moderate multiplicity,however,the relative errors of the heuristic methods are comparable to those of the proposed algorithm,and the best of these methods performs better overall than the proposed method." (p. 1)
- "While we have been unable to establish this result in general,we can prove the following theorem." (p. 4)

### S246 · Dynamic classification of online customers (Proceedings of the 2003 SIAM International Conference on Data Mining 2003, DOI 10.1137/1.9781611972733.10)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–12 (abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1–2, figure captions and text; the reference list not read). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Mersereau and Patel match (p. 1). The file is an OCR of a scan with garbled formulas and figure axes.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Adam J. Mersereau (MIT ORC), Nitin R. Patel (MIT Sloan) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an e-commerce data-mining paper supported by a grant from MIT's Center for eBusiness (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML SD; AP as testbed}: A merchant sees a visit click by click and must act while the customer is still on the site; classifiers that work in feature spaces of fixed dimension (SVMs, trees) cannot take sequences of variable length without losing information (pp. 1–2). The problem couples classification with stopping: after each click, classify the visit as buy or non-buy, or wait (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two layers (pp. 3–7). First, the purchase probability by Bayes' rule on a mixture of a buy and a non-buy Markov chain (zero-, first-, second- and pruned variable-order chains, with covariates or inter-click times). Second, decision rules on those estimates: either two fixed thresholds chosen by exhaustive search on validation data, or time-dependent rules from a finite-horizon DP.
- **D3 Model and data**: Clicks mapped to 11 URL categories by keyword search (pp. 7–8); classifications are irreversible (p. 3). In the DP the discretized estimates evolve as a nonstationary Markov chain, an assumption separate from the navigation model (p. 6). Data: about 200,000 visits from a large computer retailer over two months, 10.7 clicks per visit, about 2% with a purchase, split by user 50/33/17 into training, validation and test sets (p. 7). The cleaning filters could not run in real time, which the authors admit (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Maximum-likelihood fits by normalized transition counts (p. 3); a transition tree pruned by data coverage (p. 4); logistic links for covariates (p. 5); a DP over discretized probability states, solved in time proportional to H|P|² (pp. 6–7). No theorem.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Second-order and pruned models give the best lift on visits clipped at a random click; covariates and inter-click times add little (Fig. 3, p. 9). Calibration is monotone but does not match the estimates in magnitude (p. 9). On about 31,000 test visits, threshold and DP rules detect more purchase and non-purchase visits, and sooner, than a naive rule that waits for a Checkout page; but the naive rule is more precise (48% correct buy labels against at most 38% at 90% detection, p. 10), and the cost-based comparison of threshold and DP rules is inconclusive (p. 11). The DP rules' cost parameters were tuned by trial and error (p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: incremental probability models, two time-sensitive decision frameworks and an illustration on real web logs (pp. 1–2). Future work: other sites and goods, prediction at the user level, multi-way targets such as segments, and customer sequences across channels (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 6–7, 10–11): decisions (what and when), not predictions, are optimized and scored on detection, speed, precision and cost. But estimation and decision are separate stages, the baseline is a rule the authors built from domain knowledge rather than the retailer's own practice, and there is no field test.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 10–11): the optimized (DP) rules do not separate from the simpler threshold class, and the naive rule wins on precision; both results are printed (Heuristic 4).
- New-pattern candidate: *decide when as well as what* (pp. 1–2, 6–7): an optimal-stopping layer on top of a learned probability, so that the timing of the action is optimized too.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Optimal stopping on a learned score: discretize the estimates into bins, estimate per-click transition matrices between bins on a validation set, and solve the finite-horizon DP (pp. 6–7, 9).
- Use the observed purchase frequency of each bin on validation data as the bin's probability, not the model's average estimate (p. 9).
- Clip each sequence at a random point before computing lift curves, so that the evaluation measures prediction during the process (p. 9).
- Split by user into a training set (estimator), a validation set (decision-rule parameters) and a test set (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "That is, we recognize that when to make a decision may be as important as what decision to make." (p. 1)
- "In the absence of a set of realistic cost parameters, this comparison method has been inconclusive in distinguishing the FT and DDR classifiers." (p. 11)

### S055 · A robust optimization approach to supply chain management (International Conference on Integer Programming and Combinatorial Optimization 2004, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-25960-2_7)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–9 (all sections and the reference list). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Thiele match (p. 1). The file is a 9-page two-column version without a venue line or printed page numbers; it defers the choice of Γ and further experiments to "the full paper" (p. 8). It was not compared with the LNCS print.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Aurélie Thiele (MIT LIDS, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the start of the robust-inventory line with Thiele; S122 (2005) cites the Operations Research version as to appear and her 2004 thesis (S122 pp. 30–31).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO AP; LO and IO as engines}: Dynamic programming yields insightful policies for simple systems (basestock for series systems, after Clark and Scarf), but it assumes known distributions and suffers from the curse of dimensionality, so practice uses simpler, suboptimal policies (p. 1). The aim: a method that needs little distributional information, scales to networks and keeps the structure of the optimal policy (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Demands are uncertain parameters in intervals, with budgets Γ_k on the cumulative scaled deviations; the Bertsimas–Sim robust counterpart is applied (Theorem 2.1, p. 2). The robust optimum turns out to be the nominal optimum for a modified, deterministic demand, so the basestock structure survives (Theorem 3.3, p. 4; Theorems 3.4–3.5, p. 5; Theorems 4.1–4.2, pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Finite horizon, zero lead times, backlogging, piecewise-linear convex holding and shortage costs, a fixed ordering cost modelled by binaries, and p > c (pp. 2–3). Γ_k grows by at most 1 per period, which the authors read as a reasonable worst case (p. 3). Tree networks are handled through echelons, with the coupling constraints made linear (pp. 5–6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The robust problem is an LP without fixed costs and an MIP with them (p. 3). Fixing the optimal protection variables reduces it to a nominal inventory problem in a modified stock variable, plus an extra-cost term (proof, pp. 4–5); thresholds come in closed form for i.i.d. demand (p. 5). My reading of Theorem 3.1 and eq. (14) (pp. 3–4): the protection A_k enters the holding and the shortage constraint of every period, so each period is protected against its own worst case; the paper gives no small example that checks exactness. A two-installation example shows that a non-tree network can have a non-basestock optimum even without uncertainty (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: One single-station test: T = 10, mean demand 100 in [0, 200]. The DP policy is computed under a binomial or a 7-point Gaussian approximation; both policies are evaluated on 1,000 samples of Gamma, lognormal or Gaussian demand with the same mean and standard deviation (p. 8). The robust policy beats DP by up to 8% in the first case and by at most 0.3% (not significant) in the second; DP wins for p/h above about 3 (pp. 8–9). Results are given in prose; there are no tables.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Five claimed features, repeated word for word in the abstract and the conclusion (pp. 1, 9). Printed limits: general networks lose the basestock structure and the meaning of the modified demand (p. 7); DP wins at high p/h (p. 9). An unresolved reference "(??)" (p. 5) marks an unpolished version.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–5, 7): a DP-owned problem is written as a robust LP or MIP that keeps the nominal class; structure is read off the solution (modified demand, basestock); the boundary is printed by the non-tree counterexample (p. 7).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3–5): a per-period robust counterpart with no exactness check, the device that Method 1's correction flags in the 2006 Operations Research version [05 §4.3]. Whether those critiques apply verbatim to this version was not checked here.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2–4, 8): Γ_k is the conservatism knob and the extra cost of robustness is a closed-form term (Theorem 3.3(d), p. 4); but Γ_k is fixed by a formula taken from "the full paper", and no price curve is shown (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: *policy-structure inheritance* (pp. 4–5, 7–8; also S122 pp. 7, 20): prove that the robust (or data-driven) optimum is the nominal policy applied to modified data, so that known structure and closed-form thresholds carry over.
- New-pattern candidate: *moment-matched misspecification duel* (pp. 8–9; also S122 pp. 21–27): compare against the stochastic optimum computed under a distribution that matches the true one only in mean and standard deviation.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Show that the robust problem equals the nominal problem with modified data; the policy class (basestock) and closed-form thresholds then carry over (Theorem 3.3, p. 4; remark, p. 5).
- Use budgets on cumulative deviations that grow by at most one per period as a multiperiod uncertainty set (p. 3).
- Test against the DP policy computed under a distribution that matches only the first two moments, over several true distributions, and report where DP wins (pp. 8–9).
- Mark the edge of a structural result with a two-node counterexample (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "The robust problem is of the same class as the nominal problem, that is, a linear programming problem if there are no fixed costs or a mixed integer programming problem if fixed costs are present, independently of the topology of the network." (p. 1)
- "the robust policy outperforms dynamic programming by at most 0.3%, which is not statistically significant." (p. 8)

### S122 · A data-driven approach to newsvendor problems (MIT working paper 2005, no DOI)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–9, the theorem statements on pp. 11–12, 14 and 16, and pp. 20–31 (abstract, §§1–3, Theorems 4.1–4.4, the closing remark of §4.3, §§5–6, references). Not read: the proofs, §4.1.2 (recourse) and the remaining remarks on pp. 10–11, 13, 15 and 17–19. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Thiele match; the working paper is dated May 2005 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Aurélie Thiele (Lehigh, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: continues the robust-inventory line with Thiele (refs. [3] and [15], pp. 30–31) and borrows one-sided trimming from his portfolio paper with Lauprete and Samarov (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO DA; AP as testbed, LO as engine}: Newsvendor theory usually assumes a known demand distribution and a risk-neutral decision-maker. Scarf's moment minimax needs correct first two moments and has no link to risk preferences, and utilities are hard to articulate (pp. 1–2). The entry: work directly with demand data and a single risk parameter (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Maximize the average of the N_α worst sample profits (a one-sided trimmed mean, the sample tail conditional expectation); the trimming factor α is the only knob (pp. 2–5). The problem is an LP when profit is concave and piecewise linear (Theorem 2.2, pp. 5–6). In the classical case the optimal order is a ranked historical demand, Q* = d(j) with j = ⌈m/(m+t)·N_α⌉ (Theorem 3.1, p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: Integer, nonnegative demand observations; profit concave in the order; a convex or polyhedral feasible set (pp. 5–7). Non-dominance for all increasing concave utilities comes from Levy and Kroll (Theorem 2.1, p. 4). The model assumes no distribution; the experiments draw i.i.d. demand (pp. 21–22).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The sum of the N_α smallest values is an LP, and its dual turns the trimmed objective into linear constraints (pp. 5–6). A breakpoint argument gives the closed form (p. 7); with m = t and α = 0 the order is the median, not Scarf's mean (p. 8). The extensions keep LP or MIP form and rank-based solutions: holding and shortage costs (Theorem 4.1, p. 9), a fixed ordering cost (Theorem 4.2, p. 12), several items under a budget as an MIP (Theorem 4.3, p. 14), and several periods as an LP with a basestock-type policy (Theorem 4.4, p. 16; p. 20).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Classical newsvendor only: gamma, lognormal or Gaussian demand with mean 100 and coefficient of variation 0.1–1, N = 20–500 data points, α in steps of 0.05, c = 10 with five prices and five salvage values. The order is fitted on N points and its profit scored on the (N+1)-st, 5,000 times; metrics are CVaR, the coefficient of variation of profit and the ratio of relative decreases of standard deviation and mean (pp. 20–22). Trends are similar across distribution families and values of N; CVaR falls most for α ≤ 0.2; the recommended α is 0.10–0.15 (pp. 22–28). The only comparator is α = 0, the risk-neutral sample order. Training and test demand come from the same distribution, so the shift that motivates the paper (Scarf's remark, p. 1) is not tested (my reading).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: data instead of distributions, one knob, LP tractability, single- and multi-period models, and rank-based closed forms (p. 3). The conclusion reports "strong empirical performance" (p. 30) without an external baseline, and states no limits and no future work (pp. 29–30).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–4, 20–28): one interpretable knob (α) for risk aversion, justified by non-dominance (p. 4); the class is kept (LP); the risk–return curve is published with a recommended setting (pp. 27–28). The setting comes from simulation, not from an owner's data.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 5–8, 20): the owning traditions are named (the known-distribution newsvendor, Scarf's minimax, utility theory); the data-driven problem stays an LP through duality, and its structure is read off as an order statistic.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 21–22): decisions are scored out of sample, but only against the method's own α = 0 version; there is no owner, no real data, and no Scarf order in the numerical study.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 30–31): one thesis programme (Thiele's) carries the robust and data-driven inventory line, and a device moves from his finance work to inventory.
- New-pattern candidate: *policy-structure inheritance* (pp. 7, 12, 20; also S055 pp. 4–5): the data-driven optimum is a ranked historical demand and keeps basestock form.
- New-pattern candidate: *moment-matched misspecification test* (pp. 21–27; also S055 pp. 8–9): vary the distribution family at a fixed mean and coefficient of variation to argue that performance depends on the first two moments only.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the average of the worst N_α outcomes as an LP through the dual of the sorted-sum LP; this works for any decision problem with concave piecewise-linear profit (Theorem 2.2, pp. 5–6).

- Compare analytically with the moment-based minimax answer (median against mean) to show when data and moments disagree (p. 8).
- Report the risk–return trade-off as the ratio of relative decreases in standard deviation and mean, and recommend the knob range where it is favourable (pp. 21, 25–28).

### S142 · Special Issue Papers: Dynamic pricing and inventory control for multiple products (Journal of Revenue and Pricing Management 2005, DOI 10.1057/palgrave.rpm.5170117)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–16 (abstract, §§1–6, Tables 1–7); the appendix proofs (pp. 16–18) were not read. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and de Boer match (p. 1). The file is the SSRN copy of the manuscript dated March 2004 (p. 1); page references are its pages, not those of the journal print.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Sanne de Boer (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: airline pricing with an ORC student; the DPMP decomposition is credited to Bertsimas et al. (2001) (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP SD; CP and LO as engines}: Airlines either fix fares and control seat availability or price dynamically. Few papers treat joint pricing and allocation over a network, and the multi-period problem is a DP whose state is the remaining capacity of every resource (pp. 1–2, 5). Deterministic fluid models ignore randomness (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three steps. (i) In the single-period problem, each product's price is eliminated as a function of its availability; under log-concave noise the resulting revenue function is concave (Young 1978), so Frank–Wolfe solves the problem (Theorem 1, pp. 6–7). (ii) Static and dynamic revenue are bracketed by deterministic programs whose gap depends on the demand coefficient of variation (Theorems 2–4, pp. 8–9). (iii) DPMP: a one-dimensional pricing DP per product with its production fixed, piecewise-linear value functions, and an LP that allocates resources across products (pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Model and data**: Demand D = α(p) + β(p)Z with E[Z] = 1; no cross-elasticities, which the authors call a simplification left for further study (p. 4); lost sales, no salvage; inventory partitioned by product, which they call suboptimal for many applications such as airlines (p. 4). Concavity fails in a numerical example with Normal noise (p. 7); concavity of the DPMP value functions is unproved but held in every experiment (p. 11). Data: synthetic single-hub networks, linear demand with Erlang noise, parameters from Weatherford (1997) (pp. 11–12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Jensen's inequality gives J ≤ J_det (Theorem 2, p. 8). A lower bound depending on the coefficient of variation gives J_LB ≤ static ≤ dynamic ≤ J_UB, with equality in the limit as the coefficient of variation goes to 0 (Theorems 3–4, p. 9). Concave piecewise-linear value functions turn the allocation into an LP (pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Every policy is evaluated over 1,000 simulation runs (p. 13). Centralized pricing gains up to 22.3% over p∞, a benchmark the authors call "not a good one" (Tables 1–2, pp. 13–14). Dynamic over static policies gains 2.8–38.6% (Table 3, p. 14). DPMP gains 0.13–4.46% over the deterministic CEC policy (Table 4, p. 15) and 0.01–2.36% over OLFC, with one loss (−0.04%) and gains shrinking as the network grows (Tables 5–7, pp. 15–16).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a stochastic DP model for periodic multi-product pricing, convexity of the static case, DPMP, and bounds that depend on the coefficient of variation (p. 3). Limits: no cross-elasticities, partitioned inventory control, unproved concavity, and a decomposition that loses value as the network grows (pp. 4, 11, 15). The authors reinterpret earlier asymptotic-optimality results for deterministic models as an effect of reduced randomness rather than of scale (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 6–11): a DP owned by revenue management is turned into a concave program (static case) and an LP over one-dimensional value functions (dynamic case); the boundary is printed (the Normal counterexample, p. 7; unproved concavity, p. 11).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11, 14–16): DPMP duels the standard heuristics for large DPs (CEC, OLFC) and prints where its gain vanishes (Heuristic 4); there is no exact solve and no certificate.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11, 13–14): synthetic data only, and the 22% headline is measured against a benchmark the authors call weak, the reverse of step 2 (prefer the stronger naive rule).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 8–9, 14–16): the value of modelling randomness is indexed by one quantity, the coefficient of variation, with proven bounds and curves over CV²; but the index is a property of demand, not a knob that the decision-maker sets.
- New-pattern candidate: *math programming inside dynamic programming* (pp. 10–11; also S074 p. 7): an LP over separable value-function approximations allocates the shared resources, and each product's DP then sets its price.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Eliminate prices product by product as functions of availability, then optimize over availabilities only; under log-concavity a non-concave joint problem becomes concave (pp. 6–7).
- Bracket the value of stochastic and dynamic policies between deterministic programs whose gap is a function of the coefficient of variation (Theorems 3–4, p. 9).
- Separate the sources of value with a ladder of comparisons: centralized against decentralized prices, dynamic against static, stochastic against deterministic, and accounting for future price changes against OLFC (pp. 13–16).

**Quotes**:
- "Since we did not have real data to run numerical experiments, we have written a computer program that automatically generates test problems according to prespecified demand, network and fare settings." (p. 11)
- "The benchmark pricing policy p∞ is not a good one, since it can be shown (Young, 1978) it systematically sets the prices too low." (p. 14)

### S499 · Multivariate exponential integral approximations: a moment approach (Technical Report, Operations Research Center, MIT 2006, no DOI)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–4 (abstract, introduction, contributions, the bivariate framework and the methodology), the opening of §2.3 (pp. 6–7), the statements of Lemma 1 and Theorem 1 (pp. 8, 10–12), and §4.3 with Tables 1–3 (pp. 13–15). Not read: the general-case recursions (pp. 4–6), the details of the relaxation (pp. 7–8), the proofs (pp. 8–11) and the derivations of §§4.1–4.2 (pp. 12–13). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Doan and Lasserre match; the report is dated January 2007 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Xuan Vinh Doan (MIT ORC), Jean Lasserre (LAAS, Toulouse) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the moment-problem line (SKILL SW2) carried from probability bounds to numerical integration, with a student and Lasserre (my reading of the topic match).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP}: Multivariate integrals, such as normal probabilities over compact sets, are computed by Monte Carlo or by cubature; most cubature formulas are restricted to special sets such as boxes and simplices, and constructing them needs orthogonal polynomials (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: The integral of g·e^h over Ω is a linear functional of the moments of the measure e^h dx on Ω. Integration by parts gives linear recursions among those moments. Maximizing and minimizing the functional under moment- and localizing-matrix constraints gives two hierarchies of SDPs, an upper and a lower bound (pp. 2–4).
- **D3 Model and data**: g and h are polynomials; Ω is compact and defined by iterated polynomial bounds (eq. 9, p. 4); in the normal case h is quadratic (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Lemma 1: the recursions define moment sequences of measures (p. 8). Theorem 1: both SDPs are solvable for large r and their values converge monotonically to the integral; added bound constraints become inactive in the limit (pp. 10–12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Matlab with SeDuMi on a Pentium III, random normal distributions on [−1, 1]^n, tolerance 5×10⁻⁵ on the half-gap between the bounds (pp. 13–14). For n = 1 and n = 2 the tolerance is reached at average order 3.5 and 4.3, in up to about 80 s; for n = 3, order r = 5 cannot run for lack of memory, and r = 4 takes more than 2000 s (Tables 1–3, pp. 14–15). There is no comparison with Monte Carlo or cubature.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the bound framework, the convergence proof, and computations for hypercube and order-statistic probabilities (p. 2). Future work: eliminate moment variables through the recursions; extend to rational integrands (p. 15). The closing sentence says run times "are yet very promising; however" the method is plausible to implement (p. 15), probably a dropped "not" (my reading).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–4, 10–14): a numerical-analysis question becomes a pair of SDP hierarchies over moments; the boundary is printed (memory at n = 3 and r = 5; time at r = 4).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 13–15): an honest certificate (the half-gap between the bounds is the reported error), but no duel with the incumbent methods named in the introduction.
- New-pattern candidate: *two-sided relaxation sandwich* (pp. 4, 14–15; also S498 pp. 10–11): solve a maximizing and a minimizing relaxation and report half the gap as the error certificate.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Integration by parts turns the integral of a polynomial times the exponential of a polynomial into linear relations among moments, which enter an SDP as linear constraints (p. 3).
- Bracket the target between a maximizing and a minimizing relaxation, and raise the order until the half-gap meets the tolerance (pp. 4, 13–14).

**Quotes**:
- "Both are SDP-relaxations of the original problem (1), and so their respective optimal values provide upper and lower bounds on ρ." (p. 4)
- "Due to the large amount of memory needed for SeDuMi routine, we cannot run the algorithms with r = 5 for the case n = 3 to meet the 4-digit accuracy requirement." (p. 14)

### S498 · Optimal data fitting: a moment approach (Research report, Sloan School of Management, MIT 2007, no DOI)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–5 (abstract, introduction, formulation, the relaxation, Theorem 1), the discussion and statements on pp. 6–9 (Proposition 2, Theorems 2–3), the algorithm and its termination argument (pp. 10–11), and the computational results (pp. 13–16). Not read: the proofs (pp. 5–9) and the ellipsoid formulations of §§4.2.1 and 4.3.1 (pp. 11–14). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Doan and Lasserre match; the report is dated January 2007 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Xuan Vinh Doan (MIT ORC), Jean Lasserre (LAAS) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: companion of S499 in the same moment programme.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP ML}: Minimum-volume covering ellipsoids and ellipsoidal separation of two point sets have LMI formulations solved by interior-point methods, but these can become intractable for large data sets, and semi-algebraic sets other than ellipsoids may be wanted (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Instead of one constraint per data point, require that the support of a probability measure on the data lie in the set {θ ≥ 0}; this replaces |S1| + |S2| constraints by two localizing-matrix LMIs (pp. 2, 4). The relaxation converges finitely in the moment order (Theorem 1, p. 5) and is exact at r = deg θ for suitably chosen measures (Theorem 3, p. 9); an active-set algorithm searches for those measures (p. 10).
- **D3 Model and data**: θ is a polynomial of degree d, Θ convex, f convex, with Slater's condition (p. 9). Data: synthetic normal clusters, mapped affinely into the unit ball (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Positive semidefinite localizing matrices are necessary for the support condition (p. 4). The relaxation is exact when max |S_i| ≤ C(n+r, r), which the authors judge "not very useful" for practical algorithms (Proposition 2, pp. 6–7). A Helly-type theorem of Ben-Tal et al. (Theorem 2, p. 8) gives exactness at r = d (Theorem 3, p. 9). Termination with no violating points certifies optimality through a sandwich of inequalities (pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Matlab 7.1 with YALMIP 3 and SDPT3 3.3 on a Pentium III (p. 13). Covering ellipsoids are found for up to 100,000 points in three dimensions in about 6 iterations, and for 10,000 points in ten dimensions; the separation version detects infeasibility (pp. 13–16). No run times are reported, and the cited LMI and interior-point methods are not run; preparing the moment matrices costs O(n⁷m) (p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the relaxation, finite and exact convergence results, and a practical algorithm (p. 2). Limits: the order r0 in Theorem 1 is unknown (p. 6), and the method suits low dimensions (p. 13). Future work: heuristics for a good initial subset and a problem-specific SDP code (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–4, 7, 9, 13): data fitting, owned by convex programming and robust statistics, is recast as moment SDPs that keep the class; the boundary is printed (Proposition 2 impractical; high dimension).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 10–11, 13–16): the algorithm's termination comes with an optimality certificate, but the incumbent algorithms are not run and no times are given; the abstract still calls the results "encouraging" (p. 1).
- New-pattern candidate: *aggregate per-point constraints into one moment constraint, then choose the measure* (pp. 2, 4, 10): exactness is recovered by choosing which points carry the measure, found by an active-set loop.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace the per-point constraints θ(x) ≥ 0 for every x in S by one positive-semidefinite localizing-matrix constraint on the moments of a measure supported on S (p. 4).
- Active-set loop: put a uniform measure on the current supporting set, add the points that the current solution violates, and stop when none does, which certifies optimality (pp. 10–11).
- Map the data affinely into the unit ball so that the moments have comparable magnitudes (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "instead of imposing a constraint for each point in the dataset, we require that the support of any probability measure μ that is generated on the dataset is contained in Ω." (p. 2)
- "Clearly, this algorithm is more suitable for datasets in low dimensions with a large number of points." (p. 13)
