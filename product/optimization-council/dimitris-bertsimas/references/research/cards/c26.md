### S250 · Mixed-projection conic optimization: A new paradigm for modeling rank constraints (Operations Research 2022, DOI 10.1287/opre.2021.2182; arXiv 2009.10395)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–49 of arXiv v2.
- Body pp. 1–37; references pp. 37–40 scanned for positioning.
- Appendices A–C (proofs, conjugates, matrix identities; pp. 40–48) read in full. Appendix D (in-out pseudocode, p. 48) read; of Figure 8 (p. 49) only the caption is in the text layer.
- Figures 6–8 are images and so are not in the text layer. For Figures 1–5, the axis labels and the in-text descriptions were read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ryan Cory-Wright (MIT Operations Research Center) and Jean Pauphilet (London Business School) (p. 1); both are in SKILL.md's ML lineage. · **Position in trajectory**: The file is the 2 April 2021 revision (p. 1). The paper is the matrix sequel to the group's 2019 "unified approach to mixed-integer optimization problems with logical constraints" (arXiv 1907.02109, cited pp. 9–11, 37); Table 1 maps it entry by entry onto the new setting (p. 11). It leans on the same years' sparse-regression papers (Bertsimas & Van Parys 2020; Bertsimas, Pauphilet & van Parys 2020) and on the sparse-portfolio paper (Bertsimas & Cory-Wright 2018) (pp. 8, 17, 25, 37–38).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + CP; ML (matrix completion) and sensor location as test families}:
  - Entry: an inherited intractability verdict plus a representability gap. Influential low-rank works (Candès & Plan 2010; Recht et al. 2010) "characterize low-rank optimization as intractable" and advocate relaxations or heuristics without assumption-free guarantees (pp. 1–2).
  - Rank constraints cannot be written in mixed-integer convex form (Lubin et al. 2021), so the MICO advances that made best subset and sparse portfolios solvable "do not benefit" rank problems (p. 2). As the paper puts it, no generic code certifies them "at even moderate sizes" (p. 1).
  - Problem (1): min ⟨C,X⟩ + λ·Rank(X) s.t. AX = B, Rank(X) ≤ k, X ∈ K, with K a proper cone, so linear, quadratic, semidefinite, exponential and power constraints are all covered (p. 2).
  - Examples: matrix completion (Netflix and Amazon as motivation), minimum-dimension Euclidean distance embedding, and QCQO. QCQO is shown to be rank-constrained, and rank-constrained problems with linear, SOC or SDP constraints are QCQOs (pp. 3–4).
  - Two "algorithmic barriers" are named: a harder complexity class, and no representation by binaries (p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *The lift.* Orthogonal projection matrices (Y² = Y) are the matrix analogue of binaries. Rank(X) ≤ k holds iff there is an orthogonal projection Y with tr(Y) ≤ k and X = YX (Proposition 2, p. 10); in the diagonal case this recovers cardinality constraints (p. 10).
  - *The device.* A regularizer is added: a spectral-norm bound (the matrix big-M) or a Frobenius penalty (the matrix ridge, whose relaxation is a matrix perspective). By strong duality it makes the inner value function f(Y) convex and Lipschitz in Y (Theorem 3, Lemma 3, pp. 12–16). This isolates the hardness in the set of projection matrices (p. 12).
  - *The algorithms*, copied from MICO:
    - Outer approximation over projection matrices (Algorithm 1). The master problem goes to Gurobi 9.0's nonconvex-QCQO spatial branch-and-bound via Y = UUᵀ, UᵀU = I with SOC strengthening; the conic subproblems go to Mosek (pp. 19–21).
    - SDP relaxations (Lemmas 4–5, p. 17).
    - Greedy eigenvalue rounding with a bound (Theorem 5, p. 26), followed by Burer–Monteiro polishing (p. 27).
    - An accelerated alternating-minimization method for the relaxation at scale (Algorithm 2, pp. 24–25).
  - *What changed vs the nearest classics*:
    - Binaries become projection matrices, relative to the group's own 2019 MICO framework (Table 1, p. 11).
    - The exact model replaces the nuclear-norm relaxation; the nuclear norm reappears as the relaxation of the spectral case (Lemmas 7–8, p. 18).
    - Unlike Peng & Xia (2005), they optimize over projection matrices directly, and for problems with no MIO reformulation (pp. 6–7).
  - *What did not change*: the solver is a generic spatial branch-and-bound, "not tailored to projection matrices" (p. 1).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - *Problem class*: a conic problem with a rank penalty or constraint.
  - *Assumptions*: Assumption 1, one of the two regularizers (p. 12). Assumption 2, each subproblem is either infeasible or strongly dual; Slater suffices (p. 14).
  - *Tractability assumptions the authors admit*:
    - Regularization is to be introduced "artificially" when the problem does not have it. The Frobenius version biases the objective by at most (1/2γ)‖X*‖²_F; the spectral version is exact for M large enough (p. 12).
    - The dual variables must be bounded for Lipschitz continuity (p. 16), and the rounding bound needs σmax(α*) ≤ L (p. 26).
    - M is computed by conic programs and is not the smallest valid value (pp. 18–19).
    - The PSD constraint is replaced by SOC inequalities, because Gurobi cannot model PSD constraints (p. 21, fn 4).
    - Gurobi's piecewise-linear translation of the QCQO master "introduces some error" (p. 27, fn 9).
  - *Data: synthetic only.*
    - Matrix completion: M = M_L M_Rᵀ with i.i.d. Gaussian factors and an observed proportion p (pp. 27–28); noiseless where the paper says so (pp. 23, 29).
    - Sensor location: points uniform in [−0.5, 0.5]^k, with noisy squared distances (z ~ N(0, 0.01)) observed within a radio range (p. 34).
    - Netflix appears only as motivation (pp. 3, 6); no real data set is used.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - *Complexity map*:
    - ∃R-hard, by reduction from unit-distance graph embedding in R² (Schaefer 2013) to Euclidean distance embedding (Theorem 1, pp. 9, 41).
    - In ∃R when K is the PSD cone, hence ∃R-complete, via Proposition 2 and Tarski–Seidenberg (Theorem 2, pp. 9, 41). This improves the EXPTIME bound stated by others (Remark 1, p. 9).
    - With X required to be integral the problem is undecidable, by Jeroslow's quadratic-integer reduction and the Guttman rank identity (Theorem 6, pp. 41–42); a regularizer restores finite solvability (Remark 8, p. 41).
  - *Duality engine*:
    - Both regularizers have conjugates that are linear in Y (Lemma 2 and Table 2, pp. 13–14; Appendix B, by first-order conditions and Schur complements, pp. 45–47).
    - The substitution V = YX followed by Fenchel duality gives the saddle point (Theorem 3, pp. 14–15). Remark 4 reads regularization as a relaxation of the dual constraint Yα = 0 (p. 15).
    - Optimality cuts come from Danskin's theorem and feasibility cuts from conic duality (pp. 15–16).
  - *Relaxations*:
    - Convex hull of the rank-k projection matrices: {0 ⪯ Y ⪯ I, tr Y ≤ k} (Lemma 1, Overton–Womersley, p. 11).
    - The bi-duals are a matrix perspective relaxation (Lemma 4) and −MY ⪯ X ⪯ MY (Lemma 5) (p. 17).
    - Lemma 6 generalizes the reverse Huber penalty to singular values; it is the convex envelope, and it still promotes low rank under tr(X) = k, where the nuclear norm cannot (pp. 17–18).
    - Lemmas 7–8: the spectral case's relaxation is nuclear-norm minimization with ‖X‖σ ≤ M (p. 18).
  - *Convergence*: outer approximation never revisits a ball of radius ε/L, so a packing (volume) argument bounds the iterations by (LK/ε + 1)^{n²}, where K is the radius of a ball containing the feasible set (Theorem 4, pp. 20, 43–44).
  - *Relaxation certificate*: h(α) minus the k largest eigenvalues of (γ/2)ααᵀ (Lemma 10, pp. 24–25, 44). Tightness when the k-th and (k+1)-th eigenvalues differ is asserted by analogy with sparse regression ("one can show"), not proved here (p. 25).
  - *Rounding*: any rounding that keeps the relaxation's eigenbasis loses at most (γ/2)L²·min(|R|, n−k) under the Frobenius regularizer, or ML·min(|R|, n−k) under the spectral one, where R is the set of fractional eigenvalues. Greedy rounding (keep the k largest) minimizes the worst-case Lipschitz bound (Theorem 5, p. 26; proof pp. 44–45).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Setup*: Julia 1.3, JuMP 0.20.1, Gurobi 9.0.1 (NonConvex = 2, FuncPiece parameters at their minimum) and Mosek 9.1, on one Xeon E5-2690 v4 core with 32 GB (pp. 27, fn 9). Code is released on GitHub (p. 27).
  - *Relaxation at scale* (Table 3, p. 28): Algorithm 2, run to a 0.1% duality gap, matches Mosek's MSE.
    - Mosek runs out of memory at n = 250 (16 GB laptop) and n = 300 (100 GB cluster). Algorithm 2 reaches n = 600 in 38,470 s.
    - For n = 50 and 100 Mosek is faster (2.28 s vs 17.28 s; 47.20 s vs 79.01 s). The table is honest on this point.
  - *Heuristic duel* (Figures 2–3, pp. 28–30): greedy rounding, with and without Burer–Monteiro, against nuclear norm and SVD-initialised Burer–Monteiro. n = 100, 25 matrices per (p, r), using Candès–Recht's protocol plus a Frobenius term.
    - Rounding and local search beat the nuclear norm.
    - Greedy initialisation gives "dramatically lower" MSE where no method recovers the matrix exactly (p. 29).
  - *Exact solves* (Table 4, pp. 30–31): rank-1 completion, n ∈ {10, 20}, 20 matrices per row, with the same seeds for every method (p. 30).
    - Gurobi on the direct QCQO hits its 3,600 s limit in 11 of 12 rows, with gaps up to 4.745. Single-tree outer approximation often hits 30,000 s.
    - Multi-tree outer approximation is fastest, e.g. 252.3 s with gap 0.0019 at n = 10, p = 0.1, γ = 20/p.
    - Gaps are reported as absolute values, because Gurobi's relative gap depends on its piecewise approximation, and it sometimes reports optimality while a 1–2% gap remains (p. 30, fn 11).
  - *Scaling* (Table 5, p. 32): rank 1–3, n ∈ {10, 20, 30}.
    - For rank ≥ 2, every row at n = 20 and n = 30 ran longer than 170,000 s.
    - At n = 30, 15 of 18 rows average the 50-cut limit, and absolute gaps range from 0.0089 to 14.60.
    - The text calls the growth with rank "not too excessively" (p. 31), and the summary says problems with n in the tens are solved "in hours" (p. 36).
  - *Out of sample* (Figure 4, p. 32): n ∈ {25, 50}, r = 1. Outer approximation's relative MSE is "around 0.6% lower" than Burer–Monteiro for p ≥ 0.2, with paired p-values of 2×10⁻⁵¹ and 2×10⁻¹²⁹ (fn 12). No gaps or limits are reported for these runs, and Table 5 stops at n = 30.
  - *Regularization* (Figures 5–7, pp. 33–35):
    - Larger γ (less regularization) gives a larger relaxation gap and a harder problem. At γ = 500/p, the zero-gap region coincides with exact recovery (p. 33).
    - The gap tracks runtime for single-tree only (pp. 33–34).
    - Single-tree is an order of magnitude slower than multi-tree, and less stable (p. 34).
  - *Sensor location* (Table 6, pp. 34–36): rank 2–3, n ∈ {10, 20}, λ = n². Times range from 45 s to 73,000 s, with absolute gaps ≤ 0.0149.
  - *Failures reported*: Mosek's memory limits (Table 3); Gurobi's numerical instability and its failure to accept warm starts (pp. 30–31, 36); Gurobi as the bottleneck beyond n ≈ 50 (pp. 31, 36). Strengthening is measured too: the in-out cuts and the SOC inequalities are "vitally important" for the lower bound (Figure 1, p. 23).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Three literatures are surveyed, each with its gap stated:
    - Global optimization: no branch-and-cut work certifies low-rank problems (p. 4).
    - Complementarity and algebraic methods stop at about n = 10 (p. 5).
    - Rounding schemes stop at rank one (p. 5).
  - The paper extends the authors' own MICO framework (Bertsimas et al. 2019) and borrows Duran–Grossmann outer approximation, Quesada–Grossmann single-tree search, the in-out stabilisation of Ben-Ameur & Neto (2007) as implemented by Fischetti et al. (2017), Günlük–Linderoth perspectives and Pilanci et al.'s reverse Huber penalty (pp. 9–11, 17, 20–22, 48).
  - Peng & Xia (2005) are credited with projection matrices for k-means, and the two differences are stated (pp. 6–7).
  - It disagrees openly but politely with the NP-hardness reading, citing Vandenberghe & Boyd 1996 as an example: "In our opinion, this argument needs to be revisited" (p. 8).
  - *Guest-team links* (members seated in this council):
    - Burer–Monteiro is both the polishing step and the opponent (pp. 6, 27–29, 32).
    - Grossmann's outer approximation and single-tree search form the algorithmic backbone, and his 2002 counterexample is the stated reason nonconvex decomposition needs care (pp. 4, 20–21).
    - Belotti et al. 2013 (with Leyffer) is cited for decomposition beating one-shot solves (p. 19).

    - Exact solutions reach n = 30 and near-exact ones n = 600 (p. 1).

**Method links**:

**Transferable techniques**:
- To model a nonconvex parsimony constraint that has no MIO representation, look for its idempotent object (z² = z becomes Y² = Y) and write X = YX. Then build a two-column dictionary from the solved case to the new one, marking which entries are proved and which are only claimed (Proposition 2, Table 1, pp. 10–11).
- Pick a regularizer (a bound or a ridge term) whose conjugate is linear in the discrete-like variable. The inner value function is then a maximum of linear functions, so every evaluation yields a cut via Danskin's theorem. The regularizer also guarantees attainment and makes infeasibility certifiable (Lemma 2, Theorem 3, Remark 5, pp. 13–16).
- Take big-M constants from auxiliary conic programs (maximum trace, maximum log-det, entrywise maxima), and say that they are not the smallest (pp. 18–19).
- Show that the field's standard convex surrogate is exactly your relaxation in a special case (here the nuclear norm is the spectral-regularized relaxation). Your exact method's gains are then measured against your own lower bound (Lemmas 7–8, p. 18).
- Prove finite convergence of a cutting-plane method by packing: Lipschitz continuity forbids revisiting a ball of radius ε/L, so a volume ratio bounds the number of iterations (pp. 43–44).
- Round a relaxation in its eigenbasis by keeping the k largest eigenvalues. Bound the loss by the Lipschitz constant times the number of fractional eigenvalues, then polish with a local method started from the rounded point (Theorem 5, pp. 26–27).
- When the solver's relative gap is unreliable (for example under piecewise approximation), report absolute gaps against an independently computed bound, run every method on the same seeds and report paired p-values (pp. 30, 32).
- Warm-start the lower bound of a decomposition with cuts from a stabilised (in-out) cutting-plane solve of the relaxation, and show the root-bound gain in a convergence plot (pp. 22–23, 48).
- Sweep the regularization parameter on a log scale and plot runtime and error together, so the reader sees what tractability costs (Figures 5–7, pp. 33–35).

### S035 · Finding neurons in a haystack: Case studies with sparse probing (arXiv preprint 2023, arXiv 2305.01610)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–40 of arXiv v2.
- Body pp. 1–12; acknowledgements and author contributions p. 13; references pp. 13–17 scanned.
- The appendices read in full: FAQ (Appendix A, pp. 17–22), experimental details (Appendix B, pp. 22–25) and the superposition construction (Appendix C, p. 26).
- Appendix D (pp. 27–40) consists of figures, and only their captions are in the text layer. The same holds for Figures 1–5 in the body; their content was taken from the text.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Wes Gurnee (MIT; corresponding author), Neel Nanda (independent), Matthew Pauly and Katherine Harvey (Harvard), Dmitrii Troitskii (Northeastern) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: 2023 (v2 dated 2 June 2023, p. 1), in the LLM phase of SKILL.md's 2019–2026 period.
- By the paper's own contribution statement (p. 13):
  - Bertsimas, last author, "inspired, supported, and advised the investigation in addition to editing the paper".
  - Gurnee "proposed, led, and executed most of the research" and wrote the paper.
  - Nanda "supervised most of the technical and methodological aspects".
- The collaboration started in the SERI MATS programme (p. 13).
- The bridge to Bertsimas's programme is the group's exact sparse classifier: Bertsimas, Pauphilet & Van Parys, Machine Learning 2021 (ref. [17], p. 14).
- This is advised work, and therefore weak evidence of his own craft.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO as the tool}:
  - *Entry*: an opacity problem plus probing's known pitfalls. The question is how human-interpretable features are represented in LLM neurons (abstract, p. 1). A 1:1 map from features to neurons is impossible once features outnumber neurons (superposition, p. 1).
  - Probing has two known faults: it conflates ranking quality with classification quality [13], and probes can memorise [14] (p. 2).
  - *Problem stated*: which of the (d choose k) neuron subsets is most predictive, "an N P-hard combinatorial optimization problem" for which fast heuristics and tractable optimal algorithms exist (p. 4).
  - Sparsity k is swept to measure how sparse the representations are and how this changes with scale (p. 1).
  - *Motivation*: "the development of safe AI systems" (p. 12).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *Sparse probing*: a linear probe on the post-GELU MLP activations, restricted to at most k neurons (p. 4). k = 1 localises single neurons; sweeping k from 256 down to 1 measures representational sparsity (p. 8).
  - *Two new selectors*:
    - Adaptive thresholding: repeatedly retrain elastic-net logistic probes on the neurons with the k_t largest coefficients.
    - Optimal sparse probing (OSP): a cardinality-constrained hinge-loss SVM, solved "to provable optimality using a tractable cutting plane algorithm [17]" for small k (pp. 4, 25).
  - Every method starts with a mean-difference filter and ends by retraining a logistic probe on the selected neurons (pp. 4, 25).
  - Probe results are then treated as leads and corroborated by case studies: ablations, vocabulary (unembedding) analysis, weight statistics and random-basis controls (pp. 7–12, 19–20).
  - *What changed vs the probing literature*: from per-neuron scores ranked one at a time (mean difference, mutual information, L1 coefficients; p. 4) to subset selection under a capacity limit (p. 2).
  - *What did not change*: the supervised probing paradigm and its datasets (p. 11).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - *Models*: Pythia V0 suite (since updated), 70M–6.9B parameters, with 12,288–524,288 neurons. All were trained on The Pile in the same data order (p. 5, fn 4; Table 1, p. 22).
  - *Data*: 14 probing datasets in 10 collections, giving more than 100 one-vs-all binary features: natural and programming languages, Pile subset, part of speech, dependencies, morphology, text features, compound words, LaTeX and Wikidata facts (p. 5; Tables 2–3, pp. 23–24).
  - *Assumptions*: features are linear directions (A.13, pp. 20–21); post-nonlinearity neurons form a privileged basis (p. 4; A.15, p. 22); only binary features are covered (p. 12).
  - *Tractability choices admitted*:
    - Probes see only the top 1,024 neurons by mean difference; OSP sees only the top 50, with a 60 s timeout (p. 25).
    - OSP's optimality holds for an ℓ2 level that "often needs to be turned fairly high to speed up convergence" (p. 25).
    - Hyperparameters were chosen on a small subset and then reused, and class weights are balanced (p. 25).
    - Mean aggregation over multi-token spans weakens specificity (p. 5).
  - *Reporting inconsistency*: Appendix B.2 says the linguistic labels come from "the well known Penn Treebank Corpus", but Table 2 lists EWT as their source (p. 23).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (light; no theorems):
  - Formulations of the selectors: mean difference, mutual information, L1 logistic regression, F-statistic, OSP and adaptive thresholding (pp. 24–25).
  - A pigeonhole argument: superposition implies polysemanticity (p. 6).
  - An idealised construction: n mutually exclusive binary features are placed on a circle in two dimensions and recovered by ReLU(WWᵀx + b), with α²(1 − cos 2π/n) = 1 and b = −α² cos(2π/n). Large weight norms with negative biases therefore act as a fingerprint of superposition (Appendix C, p. 26; used on pp. 6–7).
  - Interference is split into alternating and simultaneous kinds, which explains why mutually exclusive n-grams suit superposition (A.6–A.8, pp. 18–19).
  - OSP's algorithm and guarantee are borrowed from [17], not re-derived (p. 25).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Metric*: out-of-sample F1, with MCC as a check, "largely the same" (p. 5, fn 3). Precision and recall are read as feature granularity (pp. 5, 11).
  - *Selector comparison* (Table 4, p. 25): k = 1–8 over all models and features, best layer.
    - OSP: F1 0.831–0.902 in 53.0 s. Mean difference: 0.832–0.890 in 0.29 s. L1 logistic: 0.818–0.906 in 7.8 s. Random neurons: 0.573–0.755.
    - "The top methods are always within 1% of each other" (p. 25).
    - OSP's timeout "is frequently reached especially for larger values of k" (p. 25).
  - *Random baselines*: random orthogonal projections (Figure 7, p. 27), and random Gaussian bases for compound words (A.10, pp. 19–20; Figures 13–14, pp. 33–34).
  - *Case studies*:
    - 21 compound words: single neurons are polysemantic, but three summed neurons separate "social security" almost perfectly (pp. 6–7).
    - Context neurons for French, Go and USPTO text. Ablating one raises the loss by 8% per French sequence in the 70M model, against 0.2% in the 6.9B model (pp. 7–8).
    - Scale: sparsity rises on average, but individual features follow different dynamics (Figure 4, pp. 8–9).
    - Refinement tools: per-token mean activations, output-logit effects, precision vs recall, and raw counts vs distributional statistics (pp. 9–11).
  - *Negative results printed* (A.16, p. 22):
    - No sparse "falsehood" neuron: dense probes need k = 1,024 to reach 90%.
    - Prefix and suffix features are not sparse.
    - Ablating factual neurons had limited effect.
  - Code and data are released (p. 5). Compute: MIT Supercloud (p. 13).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper sits inside probing (Belinkov's survey [3]; the pitfalls in [13–15]) and mechanistic interpretability (Olah, Elhage et al.). It builds on toy-model superposition results [4, 11] and cites neuroscience coding theory as "consilience" (pp. 3, 7).
  - *Contrasts* (p. 11): gradient-based and causal-intervention localisation are "too slow or coarse-grained". Sparse autoencoders are unsupervised and attach no meaning to features.
  - Operations research enters only through [16, 17] (Bertsimas, Pauphilet, Van Parys) and a cardinality survey [67] (pp. 2, 4, 13–15).
  - *Tone toward prior work*: it reformulates rather than attacks; the method "addresses several shortcomings" (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - *Structure*:
    - A case-study narrative ("zooming in"), opened by a four-bullet list of findings (p. 2).
    - Explicit strengths-and-weaknesses sections for both the method and the findings (pp. 11–12).
    - A 17-question FAQ appendix (pp. 17–22).
  - *Hedging is dense*:

    - The "first example" of superposition "in the wild" is introduced with "We believe" (p. 7).
  - The conclusion argues for an empirical style closer to the natural sciences and ends with "just start looking!" (p. 12).
  - An author-contribution statement is printed (p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - *Contribution levels*: a probing methodology with practical guidance; empirical findings (superposition in early layers, context neurons in middle layers, scale dynamics); released code and datasets (pp. 2, 5, 12).
  - *Limits*, all specific:
    - Probing gives little causal insight. It cannot see features spread over several layers, cannot tell superposition from a union of features, may miss redundant neurons, and needs aggregation for multi-token features (p. 11).
    - Only one model family is studied, at most 6.9B parameters, with binary features only, and much of the analysis is ad hoc (p. 12).
  - *Future work* (p. 12):
    - Architectures without superposition, or sparse coding, validated by probing.
    - Probing properties of the output.
    - How neurons develop across training checkpoints.
    - A library of probing datasets for "bias, fairness, safety, and high-stakes decision making".

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a field ranks candidates one at a time (top k by a univariate score), pose the k-subset problem instead. Run the exact solver on a pre-filtered pool for small k, and report it beside the cheap heuristics in one table that includes runtimes (pp. 4, 25).
- Sweep the cardinality k from large to small (adaptive thresholding) and read the F1-vs-k curve as a measurement of how sparsely a system encodes a feature. Compare the curves across model sizes (pp. 8–9, 25).
- Add random baselines at the same k: random neurons, and random rotations of the activation space, which test whether the basis itself is meaningful (pp. 19–20, 25, 27).
- Read precision and recall separately. High recall with low precision suggests a coarser feature (or superposition); low recall with high precision suggests a finer one (pp. 5, 11).
- Design negative examples that separate the target from its correlates (is_politician vs is_political vs is_person). For rare features, check raw counts, not only distributional statistics (pp. 4, 11).
- Corroborate each unit a probe finds with independent kinds of evidence: ablation with loss by context, output-logit effects through the unembedding, and a theoretical fingerprint such as weight norm × bias (pp. 7–10, 26).
- Print the negative results and an author-contribution statement (pp. 13, 22).
