- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP (oncology prognosis); IO as a small engine for the matching; DA only as motivation}:
  - The entry is an assumption the authors name and challenge. Prognostic models are trained on full observational data, on the assumption that the natural distribution of risk levels "is optimal for model learning" (p. 2). They hypothesize that "risk distribution imbalance" biases a model toward dense risk strata (pp. 2–3).
  - The stakes come from the clinical decision at the extremes. Very high-risk CRLM patients may get intensified perioperative chemotherapy, and very low-risk patients surgery alone (p. 3).
  - The question is stated in the abstract's PURPOSE: can performance in under-represented high- and low-risk subgroups be improved by "targeted restructuring of the training dataset" instead of by modifying the model architecture (p. 2)?
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Two stages (pp. 3–4):
    - stratify patients by baseline five-year mortality risk, using a Cox model fitted on the surgery-alone arm and applied to both arms;
    - within each stratum, undersample by a one-to-one minimum-distance matching between the two treatment arms.
  - The result is a training set that is uniform across low-, medium- and high-risk strata and balanced between arms. Two treatment-specific Cox models (Models 3A/3B) are trained on it (pp. 3–4, 8).
  - What changes and what stays:
    - The matching machinery of causal inference (propensity and prognostic scores) is kept. Its purpose changes from removing confounding to equalizing representation for learning (pp. 4, 8).
    - Against SMOTE: undersampling within risk strata replaces synthetic oversampling of events (p. 9).
    - The Cox model itself is unchanged (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Development cohort: 2,375 surgical CRLM patients, of whom 1,799 had complete records on adjuvant chemotherapy and overall survival: 1,197 surgery + chemotherapy and 602 surgery alone (pp. 2, 6).
  - Ten prognostic variables were chosen from a literature review (p. 6; Table 1, pp. 10–12). The target is five-year overall survival (p. 3).
  - Every model is a Cox proportional-hazards model on the same variables (pp. 2–3):
    - Model 1 is trained on the full cohort (the field's standard);
    - Models 2A/2B are trained per arm, unbalanced, to isolate the effect of balancing;
    - Models 3A/3B are trained per arm, balanced (pp. 3–4).
  - Eight strata were chosen as a balance between resolution and "intuitive presentation in probability deciles" (p. 6). The threshold α (minimum matched pairs per stratum) was tuned by internal validation (p. 5).
  - External cohorts (p. 7):
    - French: n = 660, surgery + chemotherapy, Clermont-Ferrand;
    - Dutch: n = 1,058, surgery alone, Rotterdam.
  - Adopted for simplicity: no caliper, because "stratification already ensures within-stratum comparability" (p. 4); features standardized by the cohort mean and SD (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Problem1 (p. 5) minimizes the sum of standardized Euclidean distances d_ij·z_ij over matched pairs. Each patient on each side is matched at most once, ∑∑z_ij ≥ α, and z is binary.
  - When the smaller arm in a stratum has fewer than α patients, all of them are kept and each is matched to the nearest patient of the larger arm (pp. 4–5). A relaxed version is in the appendix (not read; p. 5).
  - Measured solve time: 0.8 seconds on average for eight strata, so the method "does not incur computational overhead" at this size (footnote, p. 6).
  - [inferred] The constraint set is a bipartite matching with a cardinality floor, that is, a min-cost-flow problem whose LP relaxation is integral. The paper calls it an integer optimization problem and does not use the network structure (p. 4). At this size it makes no practical difference.
  - Evaluation devices:
    - Efron's bias-corrected bootstrap for internal validation. The paper argues for it over a train–test split because a split would leave too few tail patients to evaluate (p. 5).
    - External validation reports per-stratum Harrell's C and time-dependent AUC. The strata come from a Cox model fitted on the external cohort itself and "used only for binning" (pp. 5–6).
  - No theorem; proofs n/a.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:

A 15% gain appears only in the bias-corrected 5-year AUC of 3A 1-1 (0.7429 vs 0.6451, pp. 12–13). The in-sample C gain reaches 13.8% (0.6993 vs 0.6144, p. 12).
  - Calibration is "more variable" (p. 7). In Table 3 the bias-corrected ICI improves for 3A (0.0225 and 0.0305, against 0.0340) and worsens for 3B (0.0531 and 0.0755) (pp. 12–13). The intervals of Models 3A/3B overlap Model 1's in Tables 2–3 (pp. 12–13), and no test is reported.

  - The Discussion instead says "up to 54%" (0.47 to 0.73 in the Dutch leftmost stratum, p. 8). The two figures are not reconciled in the text. Figure 3's values have no text layer, and the figure also shows S = 7–10 binnings (p. 14).
  - Baselines: Model 1, the "current gold standard" (p. 7); SMOTE in the appendix (not read), with "neither resampling method universally outperforms the other" (p. 9).
  - Sensitivity: 7–10 strata and subsets of prognostic factors, in the appendix (not read; p. 7). Uncertainty in the tail strata is acknowledged (p. 7).
  - Not in the file:

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When a learner underperforms in sparse regions of a risk score, rebalance the training set and retrain the unchanged model. Stratify on a baseline risk model, then match within strata. Keep an unbalanced model of the same class (2A/2B) to isolate the effect of balancing (pp. 3–5).
- (D4) Write undersampling as a minimum-distance one-to-one matching with a cardinality floor α per stratum. Standardize the features and tune α by internal validation (pp. 4–5). [inferred] Solve it as a min-cost flow, whose LP relaxation is integral.
- (D5) When the subgroups you care about are small, use the bias-corrected bootstrap instead of a train–test split (p. 5).
- (D5) Report external results per risk stratum, binning the external cohort with its own risk model, separate from the evaluated models. Show the tails and the middle side by side, so that a tail gain cannot hide a loss in the middle (pp. 5–6, 8). [inferred] Say that the binning model uses the outcomes.
- (D7) When an abstract quotes a percentage gain, name the column it comes from: C or AUC, in-sample or bias-corrected (pp. 7, 12–13).

### S666 · Online LLM Personalization from Pairwise Preferences Across Users (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2610.17203)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:2610.17203v1 [cs.LG], 12 Oct 2026, p. 1).
- Read in full: the main text (pp. 1–12) and Appendices A–F (pp. 17–45), including the profiles and prompts in Appendix D (pp. 25–31).
- Scanned for their reported patterns, not cell by cell: the simulator results (Table 16, p. 36) and the tuning grids (Table 17, p. 38).
- Skimmed: the references (pp. 12–16).
- Figures 1–5 are captions plus axis labels in the text layer.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Romain Puech and Georgy Salakhutdinov (equal contribution), both MIT (p. 1). Bertsimas is last author (p. 1).

**Position in trajectory**: the education strand of his Open Learning period, recorded in note 06 T13 as research following his role.
- Puech is a PhD student, and Salakhutdinov is a new student on the 2026 homepage list (note 06).
- Puech's earlier tutoring paper (Puech, Macina, Chatain, Sachan & Kapur, ACL Findings 2025) is cited (pp. 11, 15). [inferred] The tutoring topic came with the student.
- It is a 2026 bandit paper, but it does not cite Bertsimas's own bandit or achievable-region work: no Bertsimas paper is in the reference list (pp. 12–16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD (contextual dueling bandits) + ML (LLM personalization); AP (tutoring) as the case study}:
  - Entry: an LLM can answer in several styles, but users' preferred styles are hidden and vary across users and questions (abstract, p. 1).
  - The platform starts without preference data, and users arrive once for a short session. Later users "can benefit only from the log the policy itself created" (p. 2).
  - The gap in prior work (pp. 2–3):
    - multi-user methods pool recurring users in clusters (COLDB);
    - per-user methods (UserAlign, T-POP, CoLSTIM) learn each user from scratch.
  - The problem is stated formally: minimize expected cumulative strong regret over admissible policies (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - CODuel learns a population model from completed sessions (p. 7). It is a Gaussian mixture plus an isotropic fallback component, fitted by EM-style updates. The fallback's weight is at least α/(α+n), the new-cluster probability of a Dirichlet process.
  - Each new user starts from that prior. Within the session, the component posteriors and weights are updated after each choice (pp. 7–8).
  - Pairs are chosen by a one-step lookahead, a knowledge-gradient rule adapted to strong regret, averaged over a bank of recent questions (eq. 6, p. 8).
  - An evidence gate turns transfer on only while the population learner predicts completed users' choices better than a no-transfer learner (p. 8).
  - A linear recalibration of the rater's persona scores is learned from completed sessions and shared by all users (p. 8).
  - What changes against Meta-TS and HierTS: a mixture replaces a single population mean, regret is paid on both displayed answers, and lookahead replaces sampling.
  - Latent-bandit TS and UCB over the same components cost +213 and +128 in excess regret, "because a sampled or optimistic component's favorite pair is paid in strong regret whenever that component is wrong" (p. 41).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model:
    - linear utility f_i(p,q) = θ_iᵀφ(p,q) (eq. 1, p. 3);
    - Bradley–Terry–Luce (BTL) feedback with known β (eq. 2, p. 4);
    - θ_i drawn i.i.d. from an unknown P with ‖θ_i‖ ≤ Bθ and fixed within a session; questions may be chosen adversarially (p. 3).
  - Strong regret sums the gaps of both displayed answers. The floor G_T makes even an oracle pay the gap to the second-best persona (p. 4).
  - TutorDuel (pp. 5–6):
    - K = 6 persona prompts;
    - 24 questions per session over four topics, in definition–computation–why chains;
    - answers by gpt-5.6-terra and questions by gpt-5.6-sol;
    - features φ = [persona indicator, rater score] ∈ R⁷, scored by TypeSafe Jev at about $0.03 per 1,000 questions;
    - judge gpt-6-luna, asked in both display orders and both label assignments, for 16 sampled choices per comparison.
  - Evaluation utilities are ridge BTL fits normalized with κ = 8 logits (eq. 4, p. 6; eq. 9, p. 21).
  - Rosters (pp. 5–6, 28–29):
    - development: 300 arrivals, 8 profiles;
    - held-out topics;
    - held-out profiles: 8 new profiles;
    - Composite: 350 distinct students out of 3,072 trait combinations;
    - Quirky: 350 students, each with two personal quirks added.
  - Numerical simulator: d = 8, four structured populations plus a null control (p. 5; Appendix A, p. 17).
  - Approximations admitted (p. 11): linear utilities and BTL only approximate the judge; Laplace inference and the lookahead are approximate; the population is assumed stationary.
  - In TutorDuel, Bθ is a prior scale fitted on the development roster (1.84; 2.70 for the second judge), not a known bound (p. 45).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - No theorem: "We provide no regret bound" (p. 11).
  - Devices:
    - Laplace-approximated Bayesian logistic regression per user (p. 7);
    - EM with responsibilities from Laplace marginal likelihoods and rules for adding and removing components. The M-step pools weighted comparisons instead of averaging posterior means (pp. 7, 44);
    - a probit approximation for predicted choice probabilities (p. 44);
    - the lookahead objective J_t (eq. 6, p. 8);
    - the gate, a prequential log-likelihood-ratio test at z = 0.5 standard errors, opened only after n_min = 8 sessions (p. 8).
  - Evaluation constructs:
    - the benchmark score, the share of random pairs' excess regret that a policy removes (p. 5);
    - a per-user linear oracle fitted in hindsight by grid and coordinate descent, so "the oracle's true score is at least this high" (p. 5);
    - the judge's probabilities, taken from smoothed log-odds of sampled choices rather than from token probabilities (p. 19).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Results (Table 1, p. 9): CODuel scores 48, 44 and 46 on held-out profiles, Composite and Quirky. The best comparator scores 33, 38 and 38. CODuel reaches 54–57% of the oracle's score. The runs use 64 seeds on held-out profiles and 32 on the other rosters (p. 18).
  - With the second judge (TypeSafe Jev, comparators re-tuned), the scores are 59, 55 and 53 against 45, 44 and 40 (p. 23).
  - Human check (p. 6; Table 4, p. 22): two authors labelled 204 comparisons. The judge agrees with them 74.0% and 73.0% of the time, against 69.6% between the authors. One of the two authors wrote the profiles (p. 22).
  - Baselines (pp. 6–7, 33):
    - references: random pairs, best fixed pair, best fixed pair per user (hindsight), per-user linear oracle;
    - comparison methods: RUCB, pooled, CoLSTIM, COLDB, COLDB-LR, Meta-TS, HierTS, AdaTS;
    - ablations: no transfer, no recalibration.
  - Equal-effort tuning:
    - 16 tuning seeds separate from the evaluation seeds; grids extended when a selection lies on an edge (p. 37);
    - an optimistic second chance picks each comparator's best configuration on the evaluation roster itself. The best then reaches 34/39/40, against CODuel's 48/44/46 (p. 37; Table 18, p. 39);
    - all policies see the same randomness (pp. 4–5).
  - Negative controls (pp. 10–11; Tables 24–25, pp. 42–43): on the null population and the scrambled roster, CODuel stays within 1% of no transfer. Without the gate, it is 13% worse on the null population.
  - Failures and null results are printed:
    - lookahead is not significant on the development roster (p. 10) and worse than greedy on Composite (Table 22, p. 41);
    - per-profile scores range from 29 to 61 (Table 9, p. 32);
    - Meta-TS and HierTS fall below no transfer on held-out profiles (p. 9).
  - Simulator: CODuel is best in all 16 structured settings (pp. 11, 36).
  - Compute: from the stored dataset, "every experiment runs on CPUs without calling an LLM API" (p. 11). No wall-clock times are reported.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Five literatures (pp. 2–3): contextual dueling bandits (Yue et al. 2012; Bengs et al. 2021, 2022; Saha 2021; COLDB), learning priors across tasks (Meta-TS, AdaTS, HierTS, MixTS), latent bandits, personalization across users, and simulated users and benchmarks.
  - Each literature is set apart by one protocol difference: "Our users instead arrive once, and their preferences need not form discrete clusters" (p. 2).
  - Critique is mild and stays on protocol: UserAlign reports win rate and query count "rather than cumulative regret on displayed pairs" (p. 3).
  - The knowledge gradient is credited to Frazier, Powell & Dayanik 2008 and Ryzhov, Powell & Frazier 2012 (p. 8).
  - Known weaknesses of LLM simulation are cited against the paper's own data (Santurkar et al. 2023; Bisbee et al. 2024; p. 3).
  - His own bandit work is not cited (pp. 12–16).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Modest claims: "We propose CODuel as a baseline" (p. 2). The gap to the oracle is offered "as a target for future methods" (p. 2).
  - The abstract states its scope: "On students and topics never used in development" (p. 1).
  - Numbers carry intervals throughout. Diagnostics go to the appendices, each summarized in one sentence in the main text.
  - Ethics and reproducibility statements (p. 11), and an LLM-use statement (p. 12) that describes the research process:
    - LLMs drafted the prompts, the student profiles and parts of the text, and the authors edited them;
    - coding agents implemented the code and searched CODuel variants on development seeds, under a protocol fixed in advance;
    - every reported number is recomputed by scripts from the stored results.
  - One typo: "to starts each new user" (p. 2).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a problem formulation, a reproducible benchmark (data on Hugging Face, code on GitHub, CPU-only reruns) and a baseline method (pp. 2, 11–12).
  - Limits it states (p. 11):
    - the utilities are fitted from the same judge that generates the feedback;
    - human judgments are limited to two authors;
    - no human satisfaction or educational outcomes were measured, and a preferred style need not support learning;
    - the intervals ignore the adaptive development process;
    - stationarity is assumed;
    - there is no regret bound and no guarantee against harmful transfer.
  - Future: narrow the gap to the oracle and evaluate with human users (p. 11).
  - Ethics: TutorDuel is "a testbed for learning algorithms rather than as evidence of what real students prefer". A deployed tutor should combine preferences with measures of learning (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 4–6, 11).
  - Decisions, not predictions, are scored: strong regret on the displayed pairs.
  - The strongest naive rule, the best fixed pair chosen in hindsight, is a reference (p. 6), as step 2 prefers.
  - But there is no owner, the users are simulated, and the paper says that the scored preference "need not be what best supports learning" (p. 11). There is no field test.
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 4–5, 37–39).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3) When users must see two options, score strong regret on both and subtract the distinct-pair floor, so that the excess regret is measured against what is attainable. Report the share of a random policy's excess removed (pp. 4–5).
- (D5) Fix every design choice on a development roster and run the held-out rosters (new topics, new profiles, composed students) only afterwards. Add redrawn rosters to show the spread across rosters (pp. 4, 6, 32–33).
- (D5) Give the comparators equal tuning seeds, separate from the evaluation seeds, and extend any grid whose optimum lies on an edge. Then report each comparator's optimistic best configuration on the evaluation roster as a second chance (pp. 37–39).
- (D5) Test any transfer method on a negative control with no shared structure, and set the threshold of the transfer gate before running it (pp. 6, 10–11, 43).
- (D5) Validate an LLM judge against two humans' agreement with each other, not against one human taken as truth. Check its calibration by confidence band and repeat the benchmark with a second judge from another model family (pp. 6, 21–24).
- (D4) Gate a population prior by its prequential log-likelihood advantage on completed users, so that harmful transfer switches off (p. 8).
- (D7) Write an LLM-use statement that says which parts agents generated, which protocol bound them and how the numbers were rechecked (p. 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP (education) + ML (LLM generation pipeline); IO only in the reviewer assignment}:
  - Entry: lifelong learners from different industries watch the same recorded lecture, although they will apply the material in different workplaces (abstract, p. 1).
  - A generic academic example such as iris species "stays far from the workplace", and rewriting slides and narration by hand for every industry does not scale (p. 1).

- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Regenerate a whole lecture from the seed transcript instead of editing the video, because replacing one example "tends to break neighboring slides and narration" (p. 3).
  - Compile code and markup (HTML slides with rendered math, chart code, a narration transcript) into a video, instead of generating pixels, which handle equations and diagrams badly (p. 3).
  - A fixed five-stage DAG (pp. 3–4):
    - learning objectives written first (backward design, Bloom verbs);
    - a plan around a retrieved domain paper, which a judge checks for topic fit and industry fit;
    - narration in one pass;
    - visuals in parallel;
    - progressive reveal of slide elements as they are spoken.
  - Each stage runs a bounded generate–validate–refine loop: Claude Sonnet 4.6 generates, GPT-5.2 validates, for at most three iterations. Using a validator from another model family "reduces self-enhancement bias" (p. 3).
  - Programmatic checks run wherever possible (duration arithmetic, schema, missing images, overflow). The LLM is reserved for semantic judgments (p. 3).
  - Mayer's multimedia principles serve as both prompts and validation criteria (p. 3). When a visual fails, only the execution step is retried (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Inputs: a seed transcript S; a learner profile π (healthcare, finance, energy or a generic audience); a duration d (medium, 5–15 min; long, up to about 45 min); optional objectives (p. 3).

  - Assumptions: the content that matters is spoken or inferable from speech (p. 3). There is no talking head, justified by Mayer's image principle (p. 3).

- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - n/a for proofs, reformulations and bounds.
  - The only optimization is the reviewer assignment, an "optimal matching problem" solved by MIO (p. 5):

    - the objective maximizes the minutes reviewed;
    - each reviewer is capped at 120 minutes and 6 videos.
  - Statistical devices:
    - reviewer-clustered bootstrap intervals;

**Position in trajectory**: the Open Learning period; wildfire detection extends his application range (note 06 §8).
- The paper reuses the simulation environment and monitoring architecture of the group's own benchmark (pp. 3, 27). That benchmark is WFDroneBench (Puech, Ye, de Moor, Trišović & Bertsimas, arXiv 2609.11829), S522 in the index, not read in this batch.
- The index lists an earlier wildfire item, S257 (Lincoln Laboratory Journal 2017, dynamic resource allocation for wildfire suppression; no open text).
- The related work cites Boussioux, Jacquillat et al. 2026 on suppression (p. 2; ref. [28], p. 28). Boussioux is one of his PhD graduates (2023, note 04).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP (wildfire) + IO (placement MIO; routing MIOs) + HS (PSO heuristic for team-orienteering routes); DA (a risk map drives placement and routing)}:
  - The entry is an allocation question at state scale: "current monitoring systems lack a quantitative framework for allocating detection infrastructure at scale" (abstract, p. 1).
  - Prior work studied placement, routing, integrated planning and suppression separately. Few statewide analyses consider "jointly ... infrastructure deployment, operational constraints, and drone routing strategies" (pp. 2–3).
  - It is stated as an investment question, "to quantify the investment required for rapid, large-scale wildfire detection" (p. 1). California is the case; the method is general (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - One placement-and-allocation MIO (pp. 15–18):
    - it chooses ground sensors, charging stations and drones per station under a budget, to maximize risk-weighted coverage;
    - drone counts are ladders of ordered binaries;
    - a station's coverage is a piecewise-linear, diminishing-returns function of its drone count, precomputed by a greedy set cover over patrol paths.
  - Three routing strategies run on the placed network (pp. 18–22):
    - TOP: Chao et al.'s team orienteering problem, solved by an adapted PSO heuristic;
    - MaxCov: a rolling-horizon MIO from the group's benchmark [14];
    - LinearMinTime: a new rolling-horizon MIO with a linear surrogate objective.
  - Risk dynamics for routing: a patrolled cell's accumulated risk resets to zero and then regrows linearly, a first-order Taylor approximation of 1 − (1 − r0)^k (p. 19).
  - Evaluation replays 3,693 historical ignitions (2021–2024) against plans built from pre-2021 inputs (pp. 1, 13, 24).
  - What is new relative to [14] (pp. 3, 18, 31–32):
    - a statewide case on real data replaces synthetic benchmark instances, adding costs and a comparison with the incumbent;
    - LinearMinTime;
    - the PSO accelerations.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Grid and hardware (Table 4, p. 22):
    - a 1 km data grid of 1309 × 805 cells, at hourly steps;
    - drone speed 600 m/min and certain-detection radius 2,900 m;
    - a one-hour battery (k = 7 substeps) and a 50 km transmission range, which is non-binding (p. 24).
  - The operational cell equals one drone footprint (c = 5 data cells). Finer planning "would convey little additional operational information while scaling the decision problem quadratically" (p. 15).
  - Costs are five-year total cost of ownership, hardware only: $100,000 per ground sensor, $150,000 per charging station and $50,000 per drone (p. 23).
    - They were calibrated "in consultation with a group of California-based researchers actively developing and deploying drones for wildfire detection", then cross-checked against public figures (p. 23).
  - Data (pp. 13–14):
    - the Pyrologix static risk map, calibrated to 2006–2020;
    - USFS ignitions 2021–2024, with 30 points outside the state boundary excluded;
    - Census urban areas;
    - WFPI 2020, chosen over newer releases "To avoid temporal leakage".
  - Assumptions admitted (pp. 11–12, 24): a deterministic certain-detection radius; hourly time; fixed hardware parameters; immediate battery swaps; no collisions; hardware cost only, not a permitted and staffed programme; a static risk map.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Placement MIO (eqs. 1–17, pp. 17–18):
    - budget, station-before-drone and ordering constraints;
    - no co-location of the two infrastructure types;
    - each cell assigned to one station within a reachability set computed by BFS;
    - a coverage combination of sensors and drones;
    - an ε-weighted cost regularizer that avoids "arbitrary over-deployment when the budget is nonbinding" (p. 17).
  - TOP (eqs. 18–27, pp. 19–20) has artificial depots, battery constraints and symmetry breaking. "the displayed model does not include subtour-elimination constraints": connectivity is enforced inside the PSO (p. 20).
  - LinearMinTime (eqs. 28–40, pp. 21–22): the exact expected detection time "leads to a nonlinear stochastic integer program". It is replaced by a linear surrogate, cumulative undetected risk, with visit-history variables (p. 21).
  - Conservativeness argument (p. 24): the step-function detection model lower-bounds detections beyond r*, and repeated passes make the detection rate insensitive to the miss probability inside r*. [inferred] This is an argument, not a proof.
  - Scale invariance (p. 8): with no ground sensors in the optimum, scaling aerial costs by α is the same as scaling the budget by 1/α, so the budget frontier doubles as a cost sensitivity analysis.
  - PSO acceleration (pp. 31–32), each change verified to leave solution quality unchanged:
    - the split dynamic program runs over depot markers only, from O(nm) to about O(k m log k), roughly sevenfold faster;
    - moves that cannot change the split are skipped (40–85% of moves);
    - untouched routes are cached, making local search about four times faster.
  - Computation reported (pp. 24–25):
    - placement at $75M as one MIO (Gurobi 12, 32 CPUs), stopped at the 1,800 s limit with a 0.01% gap;
    - routing time per simulated hour, mean (median): 31.9 (28.4) s for TOP, 107.0 (106.7) s for MaxCov, 184.2 (210.4) s for LinearMinTime;
