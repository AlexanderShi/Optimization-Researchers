### S678 · A Multimodal and Explainable Machine Learning Approach to Diagnosing Multi-Class Ejection Fraction from Electrocardiograms (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2604.25942)
Read level: **full**. Read: main text pp. 1–17 (abstract, introduction, results, discussion, methods); declarations and references pp. 18–20; Tables 1–4 and the captions of Figures 1–3, pp. 21–25; the supplement pp. 26–43. The supplement comprises cohort flowchart S1, Supplementary Tables 1–2, Supplemental Note 1, captions S2–S7 and the software specifications. In Supplementary Tables 1–2 the vitals and demographics were read row by row, the code and medication rows for their class pattern. The figures are images that the text layer does not carry. First-page check: title as listed; Dimitris Bertsimas is last and corresponding author (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Catherine Ning and Cindy Beini Wang (MIT ORC); Yu Ma (MIT ORC and Wisconsin School of Business); Sean McMahon, Joseph Radojevic and Steven Zweibel, MD (Heart and Vascular Institute, Hartford Hospital) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a 2026 Hartford HealthCare paper of the health programme (SKILL.md trajectory row 2019–2026). It is framed by the group's HAIM framework (Soenksen et al. 2022, ref. 8; INDEX S023, card in c23). It reuses the ECG feature set of the group's 2021 heart-disease paper (ref. 9; INDEX S127) (pp. 4, 13, 20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + ML; no optimization model}: The entry is an access gap plus a granularity gap. LVEF is assessed by echocardiography or cardiac MRI, which are costly, slow and need specialists, while the 12-lead ECG is cheap and routine (p. 3). LVEF "has not historically been evaluated from ECGs", because the signal is imperceptible to human readers (p. 3). Prior ECG-AI is mostly binary, with one cutoff (<35% in Attia et al.), or targets long-term events, diastolic dysfunction or longitudinal change (pp. 3–4). Most models use ECG or EHR alone, and the high performers are deep networks with "computational and operational barriers to deployment" (p. 4). Stated problem: four-class LVEF stratification at clinically used thresholds (<30, 30–40, 40–50, ≥50%) from engineered ECG features plus EHR, with feature-level explanations and a pipeline cheap enough to deploy (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Each 10-s ECG becomes tabular features: 127 clinically motivated features from established ECG feature sets (ref. 9) and 777 tsfresh descriptors, concatenated across leads (pp. 13–14). These are joined with six months of structured EHR: top-50 diagnosis and top-50 medication groupings, demographics, body measurements (p. 14). One gradient-boosted tree classifier (XGBoost, multi:softprob) is trained on the four classes (pp. 14, 43).
  - Explanations are post hoc (pp. 8, 16–17): class-specific SHAP values on the held-out test set; a clinician-readable relabelling of feature names, used for display only; bootstrap stability of the top-10 rankings; dependence plots for the top ECG features.
  - Changed relative to the ECG-AI incumbents: tabular fusion of two modalities instead of a deep network on raw waveforms, and four strata instead of one cutoff (pp. 4, 8–9).
  - Unchanged: an off-the-shelf greedy ensemble, chosen over logistic regression and random forest for validation performance and efficiency (p. 14). None of the group's optimal trees is among the families tried (p. 14).
- **D3 Model and data**: Retrospective, one health system (Hartford HealthCare). Adult outpatients with an ECG within 14 days of a physician-reviewed echocardiogram, March 2020–November 2023; the closest ECG is kept (p. 12). The flowchart goes from 1,497,519 ECGs to 36,784 pairs (p. 26).
  - Development cohort: 36,784 ECGs from 30,952 patients; 88.35% normal, 5.93% mild, 3.47% moderate, 2.26% severe (p. 12). Patient-level, class-stratified 80/10/10 split: 29,427/3,678/3,679 (pp. 14, 26).
  - Temporal cohort: January 2024–July 2025, 19,966 ECGs from 18,057 patients through the same pipeline, with similar prevalences (p. 15).
  - Signal processing: 0.5-Hz order-5 Butterworth high-pass, powerline removal, per-lead z-scores. Leads III, aVR, aVL and aVF are derived from I and II by Einthoven's law (p. 13).
  - Leakage guard: heart-failure ICD-10 I50 and other LVEF-defining codes are excluded (p. 14).
  - XGBoost settings: learning rate 0.08, depth 7, minimum child weight 5, tuned "to achieve best per-class AUC scores" on a split the text does not name (p. 43).
  - Missing-data handling is not described anywhere in the methods (pp. 12–17, 43). [My reading] The summary tables suggest missing vitals recorded as zero. Body temperature has median 0.0 in both cohorts, with IQR 0.0–0.0 in the temporal cohort. Systolic blood pressure has a lower quartile of 0.0 in each reduced-EF class, against 105.0 in the normal class (pp. 29, 33).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for optimization: no formulation, algorithm analysis or proof. Statistical devices:
  - One-vs-rest AUROC per class as the primary metric, with 95% CIs by bootstrapping the test set (p. 15); the software note puts the bootstrap at "ten random resamples" (p. 43).
  - Per-class thresholds that maximize one-vs-rest F1 on the validation set, then applied to the test set (p. 15).
  - SHAP on the margin output, with mean absolute SHAP as global importance (p. 16).
  - Ranking stability from B = 20 bootstrap resamples of the test set, with top-10 sets compared by pairwise Jaccard similarity and feature frequency (p. 16).
  - χ² and Kruskal–Wallis tests for the cohort tables (p. 14).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: The baselines are the same pipeline restricted to EHR only and to ECG only (Tables 1 and 3, pp. 21–22).
  - Internal test AUROCs (severe, moderate, mild, normal): multimodal 0.95, 0.92, 0.82, 0.91; ECG only 0.94, 0.88, 0.80, 0.89; EHR only 0.87, 0.86, 0.76, 0.84 (p. 21). The largest fusion gain is for the moderate class, 0.88 to 0.92 (p. 5).
  - Temporal cohort: multimodal 0.93, 0.89, 0.81, 0.89, against ECG only 0.92, 0.86, 0.78, 0.86 (p. 22).
  - Weak results are printed (pp. 5–6, 21–22). F1 for the reduced classes is 0.27–0.35 internally and 0.27–0.31 temporally. The normal class has specificity 0.26 and 0.29, because the F1-optimal threshold for the dominant class is permissive. AUROC is therefore declared the primary summary.
  - Explanations: the top features include Lead I and V6 QR-amplitude statistics, spectral entropy, cardiomyopathy codes, systolic blood pressure and sex. The most stable appear in 95–100% of resamples (p. 7).
  - Costs reported: about 3.33 minutes of training on one NVIDIA L40s GPU without hyperparameter search; the bootstrap takes 0.12–0.17 minutes (pp. 15, 43).
  - Not reported: the validation scores of the other model families (p. 14); any run of an external ECG-AI model; calibration, explicitly not claimed (p. 10); subgroup results; any referral or decision outcome.
  - Prior work is compared by numbers on other data: binary AUC 0.91 at 50%, and 0.92–0.93 at 30% and 40%, with the caveat that "the datasets are different" (p. 9). [My reading] These binary AUCs are not in the tables, and their computation is not described. A 30% cut is the severe-versus-rest split that Table 1 reports at 0.95 (p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself among four neighbours: ECG-AI for systolic dysfunction (Attia et al. 2019), a multitask adverse-event model (ECG-MACE), an AI-ECG model of diastolic function and filling pressure, and longitudinal EHR models. Each is called complementary: the framework "complements—rather than duplicates—existing risk-stratification tools" (pp. 3–4, 9). Deep-learning incumbents are criticized only for deployment cost, supported by an arXiv paper on AI for regulatory affairs (ref. 7) (pp. 4, 8–9, 20). Self-citation sets the lineage (pp. 4, 13, 20): the "Holistic AI in Medicine paradigm" (HAIM, ref. 8), and the 2021 heart-disease paper whose ECG features are reused (ref. 9). [My reading] Ref. 1 is cited for LVEF as a diagnostic and prognostic marker (p. 3), but it is a meta-analysis of physicians' ECG-reading accuracy (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Clinical-journal layout: Results and Discussion come before Methods, followed by data, code, ethics, funding and contribution statements (pp. 5–19).
  - The abstract opens with the access problem and carries four AUROCs and one use statement, a screening and triage aid (pp. 1–2). The contributions are three numbered items in the introduction (p. 4).
  - Hedging is heavy around explanations. SHAP directions are associations, "not as evidence of causality" (p. 35). Aortic stenosis raising the normal-class score is explained by case mix, not protection (p. 36).
  - The choice of AUROC is defended by showing the threshold pathology rather than hiding it (pp. 5–6, 21).
  - Author contributions: CN and YM built the models; CN and CW wrote the first draft; Bertsimas co-conceived the study and approved the final version (pp. 18–19).
  - Slips: Supplemental Note 1 numbers its sections 1, 2, 4 (pp. 35–37). The text excludes poor-quality echocardiograms, while the flowchart removes low-quality ECGs (pp. 12, 26). "Human Ethics and Consent to Participate" is "Not applicable" beside an IRB approval (p. 18). The CIs use ten bootstrap resamples (pp. 15, 43), against B = 20 for ranking stability (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: an application, a deployable four-class LVEF screening model with internal and temporal validation and feature-level explanations; no new method or theory (pp. 4, 11).
  - Limits stated concretely (pp. 9–10): one health system and outpatients only; class imbalance weakens the threshold metrics; prospective evaluation is needed to see effects on referrals and downstream testing; probabilities are not calibrated, so site-specific thresholds and recalibration are needed before probability-based use.
  - Uses proposed (pp. 10–11): targeted echo referral in primary care; monitoring between scans in heart-failure and cardio-oncology clinics; fewer unnecessary echocardiograms.
  - Unstated: how missing values were handled, and whether missingness drives the blood-pressure attributions [my reading, pp. 29, 36]; no comparison with current referral practice; code only on request (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: contradiction ✗ (pp. 5–6, 9–10, 21–22). Predictions are scored (AUROC, F1, sensitivity, specificity), not referral decisions. Nothing is compared with the partner's current echocardiography referral practice. The pattern is the same as in the HAIM and COVID-19 calculator cards (S023, S054).
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 9–10, 12). Steps 1 and 6 are followed. There is a partner (Hartford HealthCare), its data and a named decision (who should get confirmatory imaging). The paper states plainly what the retrospective evaluation cannot show and requires prospective evaluation before use.
- Method 4: contradiction ✗ (pp. 4, 9, 14, 43). The final learner is a greedy boosted ensemble, picked on validation from three standard families, without the group's optimal trees, an exact model or a certificate. The external incumbent (deep ECG-AI) is not run, only quoted from other data.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 3–4). A diagnosis thought out of reach from the ECG is reopened because AI can read signals "imperceptible to human analysis". The why-now is qualitative, with no measured figure.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 4, 13, 18–20). The programme machinery shows. A hospital partner funds the work (Hartford HealthCare's Center for AI Innovation), beside his healthcare grants. The coauthors are ORC-affiliated (Ning, Wang, Ma), one of them now also at Wisconsin. The group's HAIM framing and 2021 ECG features are reused. The ORC coauthors built the models and drafted; he co-conceived and approved.
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted):
  - Taste mark 5 variant: explainability is post-hoc SHAP on a boosted ensemble, explicitly not "an intrinsic property of the learning algorithm" (p. 8). This departs from the interpretable-by-design stance of Optimal Trees.
  - Heuristic 4 evidence: weak minority-class F1 and low normal-class specificity are printed in the body (pp. 5–6).
  - Heuristic 9 variant: the abstract opens with an access gap, not a community belief, and leads with four AUROCs (pp. 1–2).
  - Tension T6: "a deployable framework" (p. 11) beside prospective evaluation still required (p. 10).
  - Method 1: n/a; there is no formulation.
- New-pattern candidate: **frozen modular extractors producing one patient vector**. HAIM-style fusion: the ECG becomes clinical and tsfresh features, concatenated with the EHR into one tabular row for one boosted learner, the same design as S023 (pp. 4, 13–14).
- New-pattern candidate: **re-pose the target at the clinicians' decision strata**. Four guideline LVEF strata replace one binary cutoff; binary AUCs are reported only to connect with prior work (pp. 4, 9).
- New-pattern candidate: **temporal external validation on a later cohort**. The model is frozen on 2020–2023 data and scored on 2024–2025 data through the identical inclusion and feature pipeline (pp. 6, 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Tabularize a waveform modality (domain features plus generic tsfresh descriptors) so that it fuses with the EHR in one tree learner. Keep a map from raw feature names to clinician labels for display only, so that explanations are readable without touching model inputs (pp. 13–14, 16).
- (D5) Report a modality ablation ladder on one split (EHR only, ECG only, fused), with CIs, for both the internal and the temporal cohort (pp. 21–22).
- (D5) For temporal external validation, freeze the model, rerun the identical inclusion and feature pipeline on a later period, and print the same tables (p. 15).
- (D5) Choose per-class thresholds on validation and evaluate them on test. State why AUROC is primary when the F1-optimal threshold of a dominant class collapses its specificity (pp. 5–6, 15, 21).
- (D4) Check explanation stability: bootstrap the test set, recompute mean |SHAP|, and report top-k Jaccard overlap and feature frequencies (p. 16).
- (D3) Guard against label leakage by dropping label-defining diagnosis codes from the EHR features (heart-failure I50 and other LVEF-defining codes) (p. 14).
- (D5) Report training and bootstrap times with the hardware (pp. 15, 43).

**Quotes**:
- "Rather than treating “explainability” as an intrinsic property of the learning algorithm, we use post-hoc feature attribution (SHAP) to identify which engineered ECG and EHR features contribute most strongly to the model outputs." (p. 8)
- "Consistent with a screening and prioritization application, we emphasize discriminatory performance for identifying patients at higher risk of reduced LVEF rather than interpreting predicted probabilities as calibrated estimates of absolute risk across classes." (p. 10)

### S679 · Robustifying and Selecting Cohort-Appropriate Prognostic Models under Distributional Shifts (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2604.16537)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:2604.16537v1 [stat.ME], 16 Apr 2026, p. 1). Read: main text pp. 1–23, references pp. 24–25, supplementary appendix pp. 26–28. The figures survive only as captions, panel titles and axis ticks. First-page check: title as listed; authors Dimitris Bertsimas, Carol Gao, Angelos Koulouras and Georgios Antonios Margonis, the corresponding author (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Carol Gao and Angelos Koulouras (MIT ORC); Georgios Antonios Margonis (MSKCC and Charité; corresponding author) (p. 1). The preprint carries no funding, ethics, data, code or competing-interest statement (none found in pp. 1–28) · **Position in trajectory**: part of the Bertsimas–Margonis line on colorectal liver metastases (CRLM). It uses the group's Optimal Survival Trees (ref. 4; INDEX S162) through the Interpretable AI modules, version 1.10.2 (pp. 9, 24). Companion to S680, which uses a different six-center CRLM set: Kumamoto and Bergen in place of Rotterdam and Clermont-Ferrand (S679 p. 4; S680 p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP; RO in name only: reweighting toward one proxy distribution, with no uncertainty set or worst case; DA through decision curves}: The entry is a field standard challenged. External validation "is widely regarded as the gold standard" (p. 1), yet calibration "is not purely an intrinsic property of the model" (p. 2). One or two external cohorts can look good because they share the training cohort's risk distribution, which "challenges the credibility of many published prognostic tools" (p. 2). Risk distributions are not known a priori (p. 3). The question: is there one model that does well everywhere, or should models be chosen per target cohort (p. 3)? It is answered from two seats (pp. 1–3): the model developer, who trains a "best-on-average" model, and the end user, who picks the "best-for-their-cohort" published model. Formally, covariate shift and concept shift, p_train(y|x) ≠ p_test(y|x), act on the expected loss (eqs. 1–2, p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Part I, importance weighting toward a meta-analysis proxy of the wider CRLM population. The ideal weight p_test/p_train recovers the test loss (eqs. 3–6, p. 5). The target is unknown, so published meta-analysis summaries stand in for it (p. 5). The concept weight of eq. (8) is implemented in three steps (eqs. 9–10, Algorithm 1, pp. 6–8). Training patients are stratified into m buckets of predicted risk p̂_train(y|x). The bucket's mass under a simulated recurrence risk R ~ N(μ_y, σ_y) from the meta-analysis is computed. Each bucket is then weighted by that mass divided by its mass in training.
  - The joint weight multiplies the concept weight by a covariate density ratio p̂_meta(x)/p̂_train(x) from Gaussian KDEs (eq. 11, pp. 7–8). X_meta is simulated as multivariate normal, with meta-analysis means and variances and the training cohort's covariances (Assumption 1, p. 7).
  - Part II ranks published models by the distance between the mean 5-year Kaplan–Meier estimates of the target cohort and of the development cohort (p. 9). For one scalar this is the absolute difference.
  - Changed vs the covariate-shift literature: no target data are needed at training time; summary-level literature data replace them (p. 20). Unchanged: the model classes (Cox, RSF, OST) are used off the shelf (p. 9).
  - [My reading] The implemented weight depends on a patient only through the stratum of predicted risk, not on the observed outcome y_i that enters eq. (8). It is a density ratio of the risk score, in effect a covariate-shift weight on a one-dimensional summary of x. The paper concedes it may "partially reduce" covariate mismatch (p. 6) but does not show that it estimates eq. (8). Its conclusion that "correcting for concept shift alone is generally sufficient" (p. 13) inherits this labelling.
- **D3 Model and data**: Six tertiary centers (JHH, CCF, UOR, YCU, CHU, Graz). Adults with complete (R0/R1) resection of the primary tumour and all liver metastases (p. 4).
  - Table 1 (p. 11), surgery alone: JHH 157, CCF 225, YCU 117, Graz 165, UOR 1,058. Surgery plus chemotherapy: JHH 388, CCF 308, YCU 70, Graz 109, CHU 648. The mean 5-year RFS ranges over 22–62% for surgery alone and 10–60% with chemotherapy (p. 10).
  - Covariates: metastatic lymph nodes, number and maximum size of metastases, laterality, CEA, extrahepatic disease, margin (p. 10). Outcome: 5-year recurrence (Algorithm 1, p. 8).
  - Meta-analysis: Bosma et al. 2021 on perioperative chemotherapy, with eight risk strata (p. 12). The individual studies used, refs. 7–10, are four randomized trials (p. 12 footnote; p. 24).
  - Assumptions admitted: constant pairwise covariances across cohorts (Assumption 1, p. 7); normal laws for recurrence risk and covariates (pp. 6–7); the concept weight corrects concept shift and only partially covariate shift (p. 6); the weighting model must estimate p_train(y|x) accurately (p. 6).
  - Not stated: the bandwidth value ("a chosen bandwidth", "fixed bandwidth", pp. 8, 12); which risk model produces p̂_train(y|x) (p. 6); the tuning of Cox, RSF and OST (p. 9); how censoring enters the ICI, which eq. (12) writes for binary y (p. 9). The word censoring does not occur in the text.
  - [My reading] The meta-analytic "general population" is built from trial populations (refs. 7–10, p. 24), and the paper does not discuss how representative they are.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No optimization formulation. The "theoretical justification" claimed in contribution 1 (p. 3) is the importance-weighting identity (eqs. 3–6, p. 5), with the joint ratio factored into concept × covariate weights (p. 7). Statistical devices:
  - KL divergence between Gaussian-KDE densities of z = [x, y] (p. 9).
  - ICI (eq. 12, p. 9).
  - Spearman's ρ between the ranks of ICI and of KL over cohort pairs (eq. 13, p. 10).
  - A Wilcoxon signed-rank test of weighted vs unweighted ICI (p. 10).
  - DCA net benefit against treat-all and treat-none over thresholds 0.10–0.70, restricted to surgery-alone patients so that observed recurrence approximates baseline risk (p. 10).
  - [My reading] Two points are not discussed. First, with five training and four test cohorts per arm, the Spearman tests use 20 pairs that share cohorts, so the pairs are not independent (pp. 12–13). Second, ICI is bounded below by the gap between mean predicted and mean observed risk. Part of the Part II correlation with a mean-outcome distance is therefore expected by construction.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - (i) Shift vs calibration: Spearman ρ = 0.614 (p = 0.004) for surgery alone and 0.738 (p < 0.001) with chemotherapy (pp. 2, 11). ICI runs from below 0.10 in similar targets to about 0.30 or more in dissimilar ones. The JHH model has ICI below 0.10 at UOR and YCU and nearly 0.35 at Graz (p. 11).
  - (ii) Part I: weighting improved average external ICI for 3 of 5 surgery-alone and 4 of 5 chemotherapy training cohorts. The gains run from about 5% (Graz, chemotherapy) to more than 50% (YCU, chemotherapy) (pp. 12–13). Concept weighting beats joint weighting, which is blamed on noisy meta-analytic reporting (p. 13). On the aggregated external cohort the Wilcoxon statistic is 7.0, with p = 0.037 (p. 13). The gains are largest where unweighted calibration was worst (p. 13). The C-index is roughly unchanged (Fig. 4, p. 14). [My reading] Figs. 3–4 average over OST and RSF only, while Figs. 1–2 average over Cox, OST and RSF (pp. 12–15); Cox is dropped without comment.
  - (iii) Part II: in every surgery-alone target, the model from the most similar cohort has the lowest or second-lowest ICI (p. 14), with ρ = 0.803 and 0.737, p < 0.001 (p. 15).
  - DCA: for the JHH target, YCU- and UOR-trained models beat treat-all and treat-none over a range of thresholds, while the CCF and Graz models add little (p. 15). For the CCF target, the Graz model is the best calibrated, but its curve overlaps treat-all (p. 15). The predicted-risk histograms explain why: about 0.2–0.45 for the Graz model on CCF, against about 0.3–0.9 for the YCU and UOR models on JHH (pp. 15–16, Fig. 10). Calibration plots show the matching under- and over-estimation (p. 16).
  - Printed failures: the weighting did not help in 2 of 5 and 1 of 5 training cohorts; the Graz-to-CCF case is calibrated but of little use.
  - Not reported: the number of pairs behind the Wilcoxon test; per-model results for the weighting; any comparison with recalibration methods (only argued, p. 23); run times.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on clinical-prediction guidance: Alba et al., for whom calibration is the most important property, and Vickers' principle that calibration is a joint property of model and cohort, which the study claims to support with multicenter data (pp. 16–17).
  - It audits the field's habits: the C-index is reported routinely, and fewer than half of CRLM models assess calibration (ref. 12, p. 17).
  - The covariate-shift ML literature (Shimodaira; Bickel et al.; kernel mean matching; importance-weighted CV) is credited and set apart: it assumes target data are observable, this paper does not (pp. 19–20).
  - Concept-shift work (Nguyen et al.; Tian et al.) is described as detection-oriented, and this paper as "more pragmatic" (p. 20).
  - Recalibration (Platt scaling, isotonic regression) is set apart as needing target data and blurring selection with evaluation (p. 23).
  - No robust or distributionally robust optimization work is cited, despite "Robustifying" in the title (refs. 1–19, pp. 24–25).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A structured abstract (Introduction, Methods, Results, Conclusion) with five p-values (pp. 1–2). It opens by naming the gold standard, then "we challenge the assumption" (p. 1). The contributions are three numbered items (p. 3). The two-seat structure runs through Methods, Results and Discussion as Parts I and II (pp. 5, 8, 12, 14, 18, 21). A worked example shows a well-calibrated model with a poor C-index: predicted risks of 40%, 42% and 43% in reversed order (p. 17). The conclusions hedge ("suggest", "may"), yet the closing claim is strong: "highly practical for clinicians" (p. 23).
  - Slips: Algorithm 1's Step 4 has no content (p. 8). The bandwidth is written b in the KDE and h in its argument (p. 9). KL is defined over q̂ but evaluated with p̂ (p. 9). The Fig. 4 caption describes ICI error bars on a C-index plot (p. 15). The Fig. 5 caption says "trained in each surgery-alone cohort", while its panels are "Tested on" (p. 17). The Fig. 6 caption mixes KL divergence and KM distance (p. 18). Typos: "that s typically" (p. 18); "approaches approaches" (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Four levels: an empirical demonstration (calibration tracks cohort similarity), a weighting method (meta-analysis-informed), a selection rule (outcome-space distance) and a clinical-utility check (DCA). No optimization or theory beyond the weighting identity.
  - Limits stated: one disease, CRLM; the meta-analysis targets are approximations; the selection rule uses simple summaries (p. 23); concept weighting does not fix pure covariate shift (p. 6); inconsistent reporting hampers joint weighting (p. 13).
  - Unstated: small cohorts (70 to 1,058 patients, p. 11); dependence among the pairs in the tests; the handling of censoring; no comparison with recalibration or with DRO. [My reading] The selection rule needs the target cohort's own 5-year outcome rate (pp. 8–9), the same kind of target information that recalibration-in-the-large would use.

**Method links**:
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 10, 15–16, 22–23). The owner is named: a clinician choosing which published model to adopt. Models are judged by DCA net benefit as well as by calibration, and "calibration and clinical utility, although related, are not identical" (p. 23).
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (p. 10). The decision baselines are treat-all and treat-none, not the centers' current rule. DCA uses observed recurrence in surgery-alone patients as a proxy for baseline risk. The evaluation is retrospective only.
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 12–14, 23). The property is transportability ("best-on-average"). Its price is measured in discrimination (C-index unchanged), and the bad cases are printed: 3 of 5 and 4 of 5 cohorts improved; Graz-to-CCF is calibrated but useless. But there is no knob, curve or bound: the number of strata is fixed at eight (p. 12).
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 9, 12–15). The group's MIO-based learner (OST, via Interpretable AI 1.10.2) is one of three model classes beside Cox and RSF. There is no claim that it wins, and no solve times or certificate. Results are averaged over the classes.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 9, 24). An MSKCC partner with multicenter data, ORC-affiliated coauthors, and the group's own OST and company software.
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted):
  - Heuristic 4 evidence: the failures are printed in the body (pp. 12–16).
  - Tension T3 evidence: a stat.ME paper whose devices are KDE, KL divergence, Spearman and Wilcoxon tests, with no optimization (pp. 1, 8–10).
  - RO domain: "robust" here means average-case transport toward one proxy distribution, not a worst case over a set (pp. 5–7).
  - Methods 1 and 2 not linked: there is no formulation, and the belief challenged concerns validation practice, with no "why now".
- New-pattern candidate: **audit the field's headline metric before competing on it**. Calibration is set against the routinely reported C-index, and external validation as gold standard is put to a multicenter test (pp. 1–2, 17).
- New-pattern candidate: **published summary statistics as a proxy target distribution**. Meta-analysis means and SDs are simulated into a target distribution for importance weights (pp. 5–7, 12).
- New-pattern candidate: **two seats, two methods: the developer's best-on-average vs the user's best-for-their-cohort** (pp. 3, 5, 8, 22–23).
- New-pattern candidate: **calibration is not utility: check the spread of predictions**. A decision curve plus a histogram of predicted risks catches calibrated-but-useless models (pp. 15–16, 23).
- New-pattern candidate: **multi-center cohorts as a validation instrument**. Every ordered pair of centers is a train–test experiment, and similarity is correlated with external calibration (pp. 10–15); S680 uses multicenter data the same way.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Before claiming generalizability, measure train–target similarity (KL divergence on a KDE of [x, y]) and show whether it predicts external calibration across all pairs of available cohorts (pp. 9–11).
- (D2) Importance-weight the training loss toward a target known only through published summaries (Algorithm 1, pp. 6–8). Stratify by predicted risk, simulate the target's risk distribution, and weight by the ratio of stratum masses. Say which shift the weight actually corrects.
- (D2) To choose among existing models for a new site, rank them by the distance between the outcome summaries (KM 5-year rate) of development and target cohorts, then confirm with DCA (pp. 8–9, 14–16).
- (D5) Pair every calibration result with a decision curve and a histogram of predicted risks, so that calibrated-but-useless models are caught (pp. 15–16, 21).
- (D5) Report weighting gains per training cohort, including those where the method fails, and test the aggregate with a paired signed-rank test (pp. 12–13).
- (D7) Explain why calibration and discrimination differ with a three-patient numeric example (p. 17).

### S680 · Observing the unobserved confounding through its effects: toward randomized trial-like estimates from real-world survival data (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2604.12137)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:2604.12137v1 [stat.AP], 13 Apr 2026, p. 1). Read: main text pp. 1–14 with Tables 1–7 (pp. 9, 12–15); references pp. 15–16; appendix pp. 17–26 (sensitivity analyses, permutation and ablation experiments, statistical tables). The figures survive only as captions and axis ticks, so bar heights are not described. First-page check: title as listed; authors Vasiliki Stoumpou, Dimitris Bertsimas, Samuel Singer and Georgios Antonios Margonis (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vasiliki Stoumpou (MIT ORC); Samuel Singer (MSKCC surgery); Georgios Antonios Margonis (MIT Sloan, MSKCC and Charité; contact author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the third step of a Bertsimas–Margonis line the paper cites (pp. 2, 8, 15). The earlier steps are prognostic matching from "The road to precision medicine" (2024, ref. 3; INDEX S393), the CRLM cohort of "The road to clinical trial emulation" (2025, ref. 11; INDEX S527), and the group's interpretable GIST model (2023, ref. 8; INDEX S271). Companion to S679.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP; DA as the purpose (treatment-effect estimates that guide therapy); CP only as the entropy-balancing engine}: The entry is an impossibility belief and the practice built on it. RCTs are "costly, time-consuming and often infeasible", while observational estimates are limited by unobserved confounding (p. 1). One cannot adjust for what is not measured (p. 1, quoted below). In practice the problem is assumed away, and some observational estimates were later contradicted by trials: for retroperitoneal sarcoma, the radiotherapy analyses (ref. 1) against STRASS (ref. 2) (pp. 1, 15). The premise is that the aggregate effect of unmeasured prognostic factors can be inferred from outcomes even when the factors themselves cannot (abstract, p. 1; pp. 2–3). The problem as stated: make an observational survival cohort mimic an RCT, balanced in both observed and unobserved covariates, and then estimate the treatment HR (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Three steps (Fig. 1, p. 3).
  - Step 1, a latent factor per patient: the patient's pseudo-RMST minus the mean pseudo-RMST of its k nearest neighbors (eqs. 1–3, 7, p. 4). Distance is standardized Euclidean in X, optionally with a prognostic score s. Neighbors come from the same treatment arm and have the opposite trajectory: an event patient is compared with patients who survived longer, a censored patient with patients who had an earlier event. The decomposition (5)–(6) reads U as a contrast in the unobserved component when X_j ≈ X_i (p. 4). A signed, winsorized percentile normalization within each arm gives Ũ ∈ [−1, 1] (p. 5).
  - Step 2, balance (X, s, Ũ) with one of three standard methods. (a) Prognostic matching, plus one scalar weight w on treated patients without an event, solving a mean-matching equation with w ∈ [0.5, 20] (eq. 8, p. 5). (b) Entropy balancing as a convex program, matching first moments of X, s and Ũ and second moments of s and Ũ (eq. 9, pp. 5–6). (c) IPTW with a logistic propensity model on (X, s, Ũ), weighting controls by p/(1−p) (eq. 10, p. 6).
  - Step 3: a weighted Cox model with the treatment indicator and X (and s), but without Ũ, to avoid instability (p. 6).
  - Changed vs standard practice: one extra balancing covariate built from outcomes. Unchanged: the three balancing methods and the Cox model (pp. 2, 5–6). Not attempted: recovering any specific latent variable (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**: The structural model is Y_i = f_t(X_i, V_i) + ε_i with f_t unrestricted (eq. 4, p. 4).
  - Assumptions admitted: within-arm neighbors share the treatment–covariate interaction (p. 3); the "key comparability assumption" that Ũ's direction means the same in both arms (p. 5); Ũ should be balanced under randomization (p. 5).
  - Not specified: the RMST horizon τ (p. 4); the winsorization levels (p. 5); the k values beyond the sensitivity axes, 3 or 4 to 10 (p. 20).
  - Observational cohorts with trial benchmarks (pp. 8–9):
    - MSKCC GIST: n = 480, 105 treated with adjuvant imatinib; crude event rates 32.4% treated against 13.9% untreated, the confounding-by-indication setting; benchmark HR 0.35.
    - MSKCC retroperitoneal sarcoma (RPS): n = 201, 29 treated with radiotherapy, 3 treated events; benchmark per-protocol HR 0.75 on abdominal RFS.
    - Multi-institutional CRLM: n = 778, 140 treated, selected to match JCOG0603's inclusion criteria; benchmark HR 0.67.
  - Two RCTs: STRASS (n = 265, ITT HR 1.00) and an adjuvant brachytherapy trial, BRT (n = 162) (pp. 8, 12).
  - Six CRLM centers with death and recurrence endpoints: Graz/Vienna 128, Johns Hopkins 461, Kumamoto 90, Bergen 220, Yokohama 176, Cleveland 338 (p. 13).
  - Prognostic scores: external models where available, and internal Cox, random-forest and XGBoost models cross-fitted on untreated patients (pp. 4, 8, 22).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Optimization appears only as engines: the directional kNN written as an arg min over k-subsets (eqs. 1–2, p. 4); Hainmueller's entropy-balancing convex program (eq. 9, p. 6); a one-dimensional weight equation with a box constraint (eq. 8, p. 5). The analytic argument is a heuristic decomposition (eqs. 5–6, p. 4): Ũ approximates f_t(X_i, V_i) − E[f_t(X_i, V)|N_i,k] when neighbors are close in X. There is no identification theorem, bias bound or variance analysis.
  - Inference design (pp. 6–7): pre-specified hyperparameter grids, identical for the baseline and augmented runs; results summarized over all configurations rather than the best; paired comparisons collapsed to dataset × method cells, so that configurations are not treated as independent; an exact sign test as primary analysis, with Wilcoxon signed-rank as sensitivity analysis.
  - Further tests (pp. 6–7): TOST equivalence with margin log(1.10) for the RCTs; the mean absolute pairwise log-HR difference across centers (eq. 13) for cross-center consistency; a binomial test on the share of "informative" center pairs whose X-adjusted survival gap narrows when Ũ is added.
  - Controls (pp. 17, 23–25): permutations of Y within arm before Ũ is built, of Ũ within arm and of Ũ globally; ablations replacing Ũ by the signed outcome, by the signed RMST without neighbor differencing, and by random direction-preserving neighbors.
  - [My reading] The equivalence test is described as TOST but applied as a 95% CI inside ± log(1.10) (pp. 6, 9). Table 2.2's CIs equal the mean ± 1.96 standard errors (e.g., BRT matching, 0.075 ± 1.96 × 0.0085, p. 25), and the figures define their standard errors "across configurations" (pp. 10–12). The equivalence intervals may thus measure spread over hyperparameter settings rather than sampling uncertainty; the paper does not say which.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Validation 1, observational cohorts vs RCT benchmarks: Ũ reduced the absolute log-HR error in 9 of 9 dataset × method cells; one-sided sign and Wilcoxon p = 0.00195; median cell-level gain 0.278, mean 0.344 (Table 2, p. 9). Per cell, as matching / entropy balancing / IPTW (Table 2.1, p. 25): GIST 0.33/0.26/0.33, RPS 0.08/0.59/0.90, CRLM 0.28/0.16/0.16. RPS is called unstable because it has few treated events; IPTW is the most stable method; entropy balancing sometimes overshoots (p. 8).
  - Validation 2, RCTs as a negative control: Ũ's standardized mean differences are mostly below or near 0.1, comparable to those of the baseline covariates (p. 9; Fig. 6, p. 13). Equivalence holds in 4 of 6 dataset–method combinations, with mean |∆shift| 0.081 (Table 4, p. 12; Table 2.2, p. 25).
  - Validation 3, six CRLM centers. Within-center balancing on (X, Ũ) reduced the mean pairwise log-HR dispersion in 6 of 6 cells, p = 0.0156, mean reduction 0.147 (Table 6, p. 14). For case mix, the test uses center pairs where X-adjustment widened the 5-year survival gap. Adding Ũ narrowed the gap in 75–100% of these pairs. 8 of 12 binomial tests have p < 0.05; four do not (0.0547, 0.0898, 0.0730, 0.0625) (Table 7, p. 15). Entropy balancing evaluates only 16 and 6 of the 30 pairs, without explanation (p. 15).
  - Sensitivity (pp. 17–22): matching bins (4–10), IPTW clipping (0.01–0.05), entropy moments (1–2), k, the prognostic score in the distance, and the prognostic-score model.
  - Controls (pp. 23–25): all permutations lose the improvement. The sign ablations are unstable and distort GIST and BRT. Random neighbors match the full method in CRLM but are worse in GIST and BRT (p. 25).
  - Not reported: run times; the τ used; the number of configurations per cell; sampling uncertainty of each HR (for example by bootstrap), as distinct from spread across configurations [my reading].
  - Abstract vs tables: the abstract gives p = 0.001 for the 9-of-9 result (p. 1), Table 2 gives 0.00195 (p. 9). The abstract's "approximately ten-fold" reduction "in the strongest settings" (p. 1) cannot be checked from the text layer, since Fig. 2 survives only as axis ticks. [My reading] The largest cell-level gains are in RPS (p. 25), the dataset with 3 treated events (p. 9).
  - [My reading] On the controls. Ũ's sign follows the event indicator "typically" by construction (pp. 4–5), and the matching variant reweights only treated patients without an event (p. 5). The adjustment therefore acts partly on the outcome mix of the treated arm. The Y-permutation control (p. 24) breaks Ũ's tie to each patient's own outcome as well as to X, so its failure does not by itself separate latent structure from outcome injection. The random-neighbor ablation keeps patients' own outcomes and matches the full method in CRLM (p. 25); the authors attribute this to weak prognostic covariates (p. 25). The signed-RMST ablation keeps own outcomes without neighbors and distorts the estimates (p. 25), which argues against pure outcome injection.
- **D6 Positioning**: Standard tools are named and extended, not criticized (pp. 2, 4–6, 15): prognostic matching (the group's own, ref. 3), entropy balancing (Hainmueller), IPTW (citing Rosenbaum) and RMST pseudo-observations (Andersen et al.). Existing approaches "generally do not directly address this problem" and rely on the assumption that unobserved confounding is limited (p. 2). The benchmark trials are DeMatteo et al. for imatinib, STRASS, JCOG0603 and Pisters et al. for brachytherapy (pp. 8, 15–16). [My reading] The reference list cites no work from the causal-inference literature on unmeasured confounding: no sensitivity analysis, instrumental variables, proximal inference or negative-control frameworks (refs. 1–18, pp. 15–16). This records an absence; it is not a claim about which of these would apply.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A Lancet-style "Research in context" box: evidence before this study, added value, implications (p. 2). The structured abstract gives numbers for all three validation settings (p. 1). The title states the idea as a paradox, "Observing the unobserved". The discussion calibrates its claims: improved agreement with the trial "should not be interpreted as implying" that observational estimates ought to match it exactly (pp. 10–11); immortal time bias is outside the scope (p. 14); the method is not a replacement for RCTs (p. 14). The appendix is more assertive: the permutation results "strongly suggests" (p. 24), and the ablations "demonstrate" (p. 25), that there is no mechanical outcome injection.
  - Slips: the abstract's p = 0.001 vs Table 2's 0.00195 (pp. 1, 9); a 95% CI printed as "[-0.1025, -0.796]" in Table 2.2 (p. 25); TOST called a "two-sided statistical test" (p. 9); notation switching between U and Ũ (pp. 2–7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Three levels: a method (latent factor plus balancing), an evaluation protocol (benchmark, negative control, cross-center checks) and an application to surgical oncology.
  - Limits stated (pp. 7–8, 11–14): no correction of immortal time bias; residual differences from eligibility, endpoint definitions (abdominal RFS vs local recurrence) and follow-up; treatment not standardized across centers; the cross-center analysis restricted to a diagnostic subset of pairs; a small treated group in RPS; confirmation needed "in independent external datasets".
  - Future work: subgroups with differential benefit; precision oncology (p. 14).
  - Unstated: no formal conditions under which Ũ removes bias; how Ũ's dependence on outcomes interacts with the weights [my reading, pp. 4–5]; no run times; no code statement. The data cannot be shared (p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Build a latent-prognosis covariate as a residual of pseudo-RMST against directional neighbors (same arm, opposite trajectory), normalized by signed within-arm percentiles. Balance on it, but keep it out of the outcome model (pp. 3–6).
- (D5) Validate a causal adjustment three ways (pp. 6–9, 12–15): observational cohorts with published RCT effects; RCT data as a negative control, which the method must leave unchanged (SMDs below 0.1); and the cross-site dispersion of effect estimates.
- (D4) Run the baseline and augmented methods over identical pre-specified hyperparameter grids, average across all configurations, and test cell-level summaries with an exact sign test, with Wilcoxon as sensitivity analysis (p. 6).
- (D4) When the claim is "does no harm", test equivalence against a pre-specified clinical margin such as log(1.10). Say whether the interval reflects sampling or configuration spread (pp. 6, 9, 25).
- (D5) Probe a data-derived covariate with permutations (of the outcome before construction, within arm, globally) and with ablations of each construction step. Include one control that keeps each patient's own outcome but scrambles the covariates (pp. 23–25).
- (D5) When a test runs on a diagnostic subset, state the selection rule and that other pairs may move either way (p. 7).
