### H002 · A Simulated Annealing Approach to Designing Optimal Policy Trees (submitted to Machine Learning 2023, no DOI or arXiv)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–20, all of it:

The file is the submitted manuscript, a journal template with an empty "Editor:" line (p. 1). First-page check: the title and the authors, Bertsimas and Sujichantararat, match (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthor**: Suleeporn Sujichantararat (MIT EECS, p. 1) · **Voice**: per the authors' contribution statement, Bertsimas "introduced the problem, supervised the work and wrote and edited the paper"; the student "implemented the algorithms, wrote and edited the paper". Jack Dunn is thanked for discussions (p. 19). · **Position in trajectory**: the optimal-trees line, OCT (2017) → Optimal Prescriptive Trees (Bertsimas, Dunn, Mundru 2019) → Optimal Policy Trees (Amram, Dunn, Zhuo 2022) → OCT-SA → OPT-SA (pp. 1–2, 4). It marks the 2023 end of Tension T2: simulated annealing as the group's working engine.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {HS + ML + DA; no MIO is solved}:
  - Optimal Prescriptive Trees put reward estimation and treatment assignment in one objective. This "may lead to poor rewards estimation with highly efficient treatment assignment and vice versa" (p. 1).
  - Optimal Policy Trees (OPT) separate the two. But they still grow trees by OCT-style local search, which can stop in "local minimas" (p. 2).
  - The entry is a transfer: annealing improved on OCT, so the same framework is applied to OPT (pp. 2, 4).
  - The problem: choose a depth-limited tree whose leaves prescribe the treatment with the best mean estimated reward. Whether that is a maximum or a minimum depends on the data set (pp. 2–3, 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep OPT's three moves: re-optimize a parallel split, or replace a node by its left or right subtree (p. 3). Keep OCT-SA's annealing shell. Change only the score:
  - the mean reward replaces Gini impurity, and maximizations become minimizations;
  - a factor β rescales the score into [0, 1], "like the Gini impurity score" (p. 6).

  The acceptance rule takes a better neighbour always, an equal one never, and a worse one with probability e^(−ΔE/(β·temp_k)). Cooling is geometric with α = 0.95 (eqs. (3)–(5), p. 6; Algorithm 5, pp. 12–13).

  Neighbours are chosen by rank annealing, which is inherited from OCT-SA (p. 8). The schedule is cut into r temperature bands ending at iterations S/r, S/(r−1), …, S/2, S. In the first band the move taken is the r-th best split; in each later band it is the next better one, ending with the best (Algorithm 2, pp. 8–9; worked example with S = 90 and r = 3, p. 9). The authors say this converges faster than random proposals "while also preventing overshooting" (p. 9).

  OPT's and CART's trees are among the warm starts (p. 8). The reported model is the best of 103 annealed trees (p. 13).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Rewards Γ_i(t) come from the direct method f_t(x_i) or from a doubly robust correction with a propensity model p(x, t) (eqs. (1)–(2), pp. 3–4). The doubly robust method is used everywhere except grocery pricing, where "an acceptable propensity score estimator cannot be trained" (p. 6). The reward models are random forests with 100 trees (1,000 for grocery pricing) (p. 13).
  - A penalty γ·cp, where cp is the number of branching nodes, is the only tuned hyperparameter. The Markov chain length l = 2 and the radius r = 3 are carried over from OCT-SA, after the problem is reformulated "to resemble that of OCT-SA" (pp. 6, 13).
  - Data: the grocery-pricing set, "the only public real-world dataset" in Amram et al.'s paper, plus nine UCI sets (p. 13; Table 1, p. 14). n runs from 301 to 97,295 and p from 5 to 14; outcomes and treatments are continuous or binary, and all features are made numeric.
  - Several treatments are recorded attributes rather than levers: acceleration, km driven, house age, MLOGP (Table 1, p. 14). The paper's own example seeks "the optimal house age in order to minimize the house price of unit area" (p. 3). [my reading] The benchmark tests the search on estimated rewards. It does not test prescriptions that someone could carry out.
  - Visible design choices:
    - α = 0.95 is said to suit "the 61 real-world datasets we used to evaluate OPT-SA" (p. 6), but only 10 are evaluated (p. 13); 61 is OCT-SA's count (p. 4).
    - The band mapping was designed "based on empirical experiments of the 10 datasets", the same sets used for evaluation (p. 9).
    - A minbucket of 3% is the highest value for most data sets "before OPT throws an error" (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - There is no theorem or proof. The one theoretical claim is borrowed: annealing "guarantees to provide a global optimal solution asymptotically under an appropriate cooling schedule", citing Kirkpatrick et al. and Bertsimas–Tsitsiklis 1993 (p. 2). The first contribution repeats it for OPT-SA (p. 3).
  - The schedule actually run is geometric. It starts at temperature 1 and stops below 0.01, or after two temperatures without a move (pp. 2, 6–8). [my reading, using card c01 on S005, p. 2] The 1993 review reports Hajek's condition Σ exp(−d*/T(t)) = ∞, which T(t) = d/log t with d ≥ d* satisfies. Under geometric cooling the sum is finite, so the cited guarantee does not cover the algorithm as run.
  - Algorithm design: five routines (Figure 1, p. 5; Algorithms 1–5, pp. 7–12). Candidate cut points are midpoints of sorted unique values. One subtree is kept per distinct score, so the ranks are well defined (Algorithm 4, pp. 11–12).
  - The pseudo-code carries traces of OCT-SA: Algorithm 1's comment "Update the best tree if Gini impurity score is lower" (p. 7), "giniparallel" and "Neighbor classification subtree" (p. 10), and "ginineighbor" (p. 12). Its acceptance test draws "RandomNumber(0.1, 1)" (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol (p. 13):
    - The data are split train:validation:test = 35:15:50. Half is held out because a separate reward estimator is fit on the test set "to avoid information leakage".
    - Results are averaged over 5 splits, with standard deviations, at depths 2–5.
    - γ is tuned on validation, then the model is retrained on training plus validation data from 103 warm starts.
  - Software: Python 3.8.15 for OPT-SA; OPT from Interpretable AI 3.2.0 in Julia 1.9.0; CART from scikit-learn; a Windows laptop and MIT Supercloud (p. 8).
  - Metric: the out-of-sample mean of *estimated* rewards (p. 13; Table 2, p. 14).
  - Results at depth 5 (Table 2, p. 14):
    - Against OPT: 3 wins, 1 tie (grocery pricing), 6 losses. The wins are wine white +0.0102 ± 0.0591, used car +0.0163 ± 0.1137 and qsar fish toxicity +0.0586 ± 0.0632.
    - Against CART: 4 wins (auto mpg, credit approval, wine white, qsar), 1 tie, 5 losses.
    - [my arithmetic] Each win over OPT is smaller than its ± spread. No significance test is reported.
  - Example trees for qsar on one split (pp. 15–17):
    - OPT-SA: 9 splits, out-of-sample score 4.3270 (Figure 2, p. 15).
    - OPT: one split, 4.4770 (Figure 3, p. 16; the caption says "OCT model").
    - CART: 8 splits, 4.3620 (Figure 4, p. 17).
  - Runtime on qsar: CART and OPT take about one minute; OPT-SA takes 12, 24, 29 and 31 minutes at depths 2–5 (Figure 5 and text, p. 18). Two causes are given: the cooling schedule fixes more iterations than OPT's stopping rule, and the prototype is in Python (p. 18).
  - [my reading] OPT's tree is one of the warm starts, and Algorithm 1 replaces T_best only when the training score improves (pp. 7–8). So the six losses to OPT arise out of sample, or from the penalty. The paper does not analyse this, and time budgets are not equalized.
  - Sample sizes differ between Tables 1 and 2, for example grocery 97,295 against 92,588 and qsar 908 against 634. The text gives qsar 6 features where Table 1 has 5 (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Credit goes to the group's own line: Optimal Prescriptive Trees, Amram et al.'s OPT, OCT and OCT-SA (pp. 1–2, 4). Annealing is credited to Kirkpatrick et al. and Bertsimas–Tsitsiklis (p. 2), propensity scores to Rosenbaum–Rubin (p. 4).
  - No method outside the group's trees and CART is cited or compared, for example no other policy-learning method (pp. 13–14, 19–20).
  - The reference list has nine entries; neither OCT nor OCT-SA has an entry (pp. 19–20).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract states the mixed count plainly: OPT-SA "outperforms OPT on 3 out of 10 datasets and outperforms Classification And Regression Trees (CART) on 4 out of 10 datasets" (p. 1).
  - There are two contributions: the first annealing algorithm for policy trees "that guarantees to provide optimal solution", and results "while maintaining interpretability" (p. 3).
  - The conclusion opens unhedged: annealing "performs better than local search in reaching global optimality" (p. 19). It then hedges, "there might be certain types of datasets that benefit more", to be tested on more data (p. 19).
  - Traces of OCT-SA appear in the text, the pseudo-code and a caption (pp. 6, 7, 10, 12, 16). The runtime paragraph says "OPT runs slower than CART" where OPT-SA is meant (p. 18).
  - A simple annealer gets five pseudo-code blocks (pp. 7–12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: an algorithm (the annealing shell with rank annealing, ported to policy trees); code at bit.ly/opt_sa and public data (p. 19); a benchmark on 10 data sets.
  - Stated limits: no gain on most data sets; a runtime of 12–31 minutes against about one; the Python prototype (pp. 18–19).
  - Future work: more data sets "to prove or disprove this hypothesis" (p. 19); a Julia or Cython implementation (p. 18).
  - Unstated limits: no proof, bound or certificate; a guarantee claimed for a schedule it does not cover; settings tuned on the evaluation sets; no equal-time comparison; estimated rather than observed rewards; no significance tests.

- New-pattern candidate: **port the search shell, swap the objective**. OCT-SA's annealer is reused for OPT by changing only the score, rescaling it into the old range and copying the hyperparameters (pp. 2, 6, 13).
- New-pattern candidate: **rank-annealed neighbour choice**. Temperature bands take the r-th best move, then the (r−1)-th, and so on down to the best (pp. 8–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Replace random neighbour proposals in annealing by rank annealing. Cut the schedule into r temperature bands; take the r-th best move in the hottest band, and the best move in the coldest (Algorithm 2, pp. 8–9).
- (D2) When porting a metaheuristic to a new objective, rescale the new score into the range of the old one (β), so that the cooling schedule and acceptance rule carry over (p. 6).
- (D5) In policy learning from observational data, fit a separate reward model on the test split, so that evaluation rewards do not leak from training (the 35:15:50 split, p. 13).
- (D5) Report each method's improvement over each baseline per data set as mean ± standard deviation over splits, with ties shown, and plot runtime against depth (Table 2, p. 14; Figure 5, p. 18).

### S113 · A data-driven approach to multistage stochastic linear optimization (Management Science 2023, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2022.4352)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–25, the journal print behind an INFORMS download cover (p. 1).
- Read: §§1–9, Tables 1–2, the endnotes, and the captions of and prose about Figures 1–5. The references were skimmed (pp. 24–25).
- **Not read**: the online appendix, which is not in the file. It holds the proofs of Theorems 1–3 and Propositions 1, 3 and 4, and Appendices A–K.

First-page check: the title and the authors, Bertsimas, Shtern and Sturt, match (pp. 1–2). Received April 5, 2020; accepted August 22, 2021 (p. 2).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shimrit Shtern (Technion) and Bradley Sturt (University of Illinois at Chicago) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: Sturt's sample-robust-optimization line in the DA programme. Its two-stage companion is a technical note by the same authors (Operations Research 2022, p. 24). The line was later merged with McCord's in S150 (card c27). The paper reuses his own RO devices (pp. 12, 21):
- finite adaptability (Bertsimas–Caramanis 2010);
- adaptive partitions (Bertsimas–Dunning 2016);
- moment-based adaptive DRO (Bertsimas, Sim and Zhang 2019).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + DA; SD as the multistage setting; LO and IO as engines; AP (a publisher's supply chain) as motivation}:
  - The setting is Dantzig's multistage stochastic linear optimization, with the distribution unknown and only i.i.d. historical sample paths available (pp. 2, 5). The examples are life-cycle demand of similar products, daily wind patterns, asset returns and ride usage (p. 2).
  - The usual route is estimate-then-optimize: fit a parametric model such as ARMA, then solve. This is needed because scenario-tree methods require the correlation structure across time (pp. 2–3).
  - Nonparametric approaches for time-correlated uncertainty are "surprisingly scarce" (Pflug–Pichler; Hanasusanto–Kuhn; Krokhmal–Uryasev). None is known to be asymptotically optimal (p. 3).
  - Example 1 shows why the naive repair fails (pp. 6–7). With a continuous first-stage demand, sample-path SAA picks rules keyed to each historical value. These rules are infeasible with probability one, and their cost converges to E[ξ₁ + ξ₂] instead of the stochastic optimum.
  - The problem: minimize expected linear cost under almost-sure linear constraints, over all measurable decision rules, continuous or integer (eq. (1), p. 5). Only a convex superset Ξ of the support is known (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Put a ball of radius ε_N around each sample path, average the N worst-case costs, and require feasibility on the union of the balls (Problem (2), p. 6).
  - The robustness serves a new purpose. It is not worst-case protection, risk aversion or a finite-sample guarantee: it is "a tool to avoid overfitting" as N grows (p. 4).
  - The ball is chosen for three reasons (p. 6). One parameter estimates both the expectation and the support. With the right ε the problem is near-optimal. And the sets share a structure that reformulations can exploit.
  - Kept from robust optimization: decision-rule approximations (linear rules, finite adaptability) and duality (pp. 10–13).
  - Changed: the worst case is averaged over many sets rather than taken over one (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Costs and constraint matrices are affine in ξ (p. 5).
  - Assumption 1: light tails, E exp(‖ξ‖^a) < ∞ for some a > 1 that need not be known. Gaussian processes qualify; exponential ones do not (p. 7).
  - Assumption 2: ε_N = κN^(−1/max{3, d+1}) (p. 7).
  - Assumption 3: some near-optimal rule is bounded by L(1 + ‖z‖) on the union of the sets. The paper calls this "mild" and checks it for every example in Online Appendix A, which was not read (p. 7).
  - The reformulations add box sets (Assumption 4, p. 11) and rectangular intersections with the partition (Assumption 5, p. 13).
  - The §7 data are synthetic (p. 18):
    - First-half demand is truncated normal with mean 6 and standard deviation 2.5. Second-half demand has mean 2(6 − ξ₁r)², chosen for "its relative simplicity" and because its structure is hard to identify from limited data.
    - Costs are c = 0.25, h = 0.05, b = 0.5 and f ∈ {0, 0.1}.
    - There are R ∈ {2, 3, 4} retailers, because the publisher's sales representatives manage up to five.
  - §8 uses See and Sim's (2010) nonstationary autoregressive demand (p. 22): μ = 200; ς = 40 for T = 5 and ς = 20 for T = 10; α ∈ {0, 0.25, 0.5}; capacity 260; c = 0.1, h = 0.02, b = 0.2 and b_T = 2.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1 (p. 8) holds almost surely: J_ ≤ liminf J_N ≤ limsup J_N ≤ J̄.
    - J_ is the limit of chance-constrained variants (p. 7); J̄ adds an expanded support and a local upper-semicontinuous envelope of the expectation (p. 8).
    - The theorem allows unbounded decisions and uncertainty, integer components and unrestricted decision rules (p. 8).
    - Its proof (Appendix C) was not read.
  - Theorem 2 (p. 8) is a uniform bound for all measurable f. The out-of-sample expectation on the union is at most the average in-sample worst case plus M_N·sup|f|, where M_N = N^(−1/((d+1)(d+2)))·log N. It is offered for "independent interest"; the proof (Appendix D) was not read.
  - Proposition 1 (p. 8): in Example 1 the lower bound equals J*, and so does the upper bound if a continuous optimal x₂* exists. Robustness provably removes the overfitting.
  - Examples 2–4 (p. 9) construct gaps:
    - Example 2 has an integer first stage and Ξ = [0, 2] ⊋ S = [0, 1]. In Table 1 [my reading of a garbled table], for a tail exponent k < 3 the limit is the upper bound. At k = 3 the liminf and limsup split between the bounds, and for k > 3 the limit is the lower bound.
    - Example 3 has a chance-constrained relaxation that is infeasible for the true problem.
    - Example 4 shows that the envelope is needed.
    - The authors note that the gap examples "seem to require intricate construction" (p. 9).
  - Theorem 3 (pp. 9–10) starts from a "(seemingly tautological)" observation: P(feasible) ≥ P(ξ ∈ ∪ sets). This turns out-of-sample feasibility into nonparametric support estimation (Devroye–Wise 1980), and gives a polynomial-logarithmic rate for P(ξ ∉ ∪ sets).
  - Theorem 4 (pp. 10–11, proved in the text): with box sets, the linear-decision-rule approximation (4) is an LP with O(md) auxiliary variables and O(md + mN) constraints.
    - The device: for each box, the inner LP has the dual optimal solutions [d_t]₊ and [−d_t]₊. These do not depend on the sample index, so one set of dual variables serves all N sets, and O(Nmd) falls to O(md) (p. 11).
    - Corollary 1 does the same for finite adaptability, with O(N + Kmd) variables (p. 13); its proof is "omitted".
    - Non-anticipativity across regions comes from Bertsimas–Dunning (Proposition 2, p. 12).
  - Proposition 3 (p. 14): with these balls, Problem (2) is ∞-Wasserstein DRO. So Theorem 1 gives convergence for ∞-WDRO "for the first time", under Esfahani–Kuhn's assumptions (pp. 5, 14).
  - Proposition 4 (p. 14): p-WDRO with p < ∞ and ε > 0 forces feasibility on all of Ξ, so it is not asymptotically optimal in general. In Remark 2's example no linear rule is feasible under p-WDRO, while one is feasible under SRO (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - §7, three-stage replenishment with one warehouse and R retailers, modelled on Avrahami et al.'s (2014) case of an Israeli magazine publisher (pp. 15–19).
    - The nonlinear model is rewritten exactly as the three-stage stochastic MILP (7) (p. 16; Appendix J.1, not read).
    - Baselines: "SAA Independence", which assumes stagewise independence as Avrahami et al. do (pp. 16–17), and "AR Linear": a Gaussian linear model fitted by maximum likelihood, plus a scenario tree with Ñ = 50 (p. 17). Larger Ñ "did not have any discernible impact" (p. 18).
    - SRO-FA uses ℓ∞ balls and finite adaptability with one box per historical first-stage demand, giving the MILP (9) (pp. 17–18).
    - Design (p. 18):
      - N ∈ {50, …, 800} with f = 0, and N ∈ {50, …, 282} with f = 0.1;
      - 50 training sets per N, and one test set of 10,000 paths;
      - ε chosen by five-fold cross-validation;
      - a perfect-information benchmark: a scenario tree from the true distribution (Ñ = 800 or 200), over 50 replications.
    - Results (Figures 1–3, pp. 18–20):
      - A positive ε is "essential" for the best out-of-sample cost. At ε = 0 the in-sample cost does not converge (pp. 18–19).
      - The cross-validated ε comes close to "SRO-FA Best", whose ε is chosen after the fact on the test set (p. 19).
      - SRO-FA "significantly" outperforms both baselines (p. 19).
      - The text gives no numbers and no tests. The main text names no solver, hardware or run times.
  - §8, single-product multistage inventory with T ∈ {5, 10} (pp. 19–24).
    - Baselines (p. 21):
      - SAA-LDR, with ε = 0;
      - Approx PCM, a data-driven version of his moment-based adaptive DRO (Bertsimas, Sim and Zhang 2019) with lifted linear rules;
      - DDP and RDDP, Hanasusanto–Kuhn's kernel dynamic programming;
      - WDRO-LDR, 1-Wasserstein DRO with the ℓ1 norm.
    - Design: 100 training sets per N ∈ {10, 25, 50, 100}, and 10,000 test paths. DDP and RDDP are tested on the first 1,000 paths only, because of their cost (endnote 2, p. 24). ε is chosen from the grid {b·10^a} by five-fold cross-validation (p. 21).
    - Table 2 (p. 22). For T = 10, α = 0 and N = 10:

      | Method | Out-of-sample cost |
      |---|---|
      | SRO-LDR | 208.9 (1.0) |
      | SAA-LDR | 293.9 (70.1) |
      | Approx PCM | 215.3 |
      | DDP | 5,211.4 |
      | WDRO-LDR | 5,800.3 |
      | DP estimate | 206 |

      - At N = 100, SAA-LDR ties SRO-LDR (206.3 against 206.2).
      - At T = 5, α = 0.5, SAA-LDR is marginally lower at N = 50 (110.7 against 110.8) and N = 100 (111.6 against 111.7).

    - SRO-LDR takes under one second (p. 23; Figure 4, p. 21).
    - Admissions:
      - With a fixed, finite-dimensional rule space a positive ε "is not necessary"; it pays off only at N ∈ {10, 25} (p. 23).
      - WDRO-LDR collapses to static rules (p. 23).
      - The linear rules are projected onto [0, x̄_t] before costing. Appendix K (not read) is reported to show that SRO-LDR is feasible on more than 93% of test paths, with an average ℓ1 distance under two units (pp. 23–24).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The robust-optimization lineage: Soyster; Ben-Tal and Nemirovski; Ben-Tal et al. 2004 (adjustable robustness); and decision-rule surveys, including his own with Brown and Caramanis (p. 3).
  - The paper states the critique of RO without rebuttal: RO "does not aspire to find solutions that perform well on average", and RO and stochastic linear optimization count as "distinct" frameworks (pp. 3–4).
  - The DRO lineage: Scarf; Delage–Ye; Wiesemann et al.; Wasserstein sets (Pflug–Wozabal; Esfahani–Kuhn); φ-divergences; and hypothesis tests, in his own 2018 paper with Gupta and Kallus (p. 4).
  - The closest work is set apart (p. 4):
    - Xu, Caramanis and Mannor (2012) and Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al. (2019) derive nonparametric guarantees in ML from robustness, but they need continuity and no support constraints.
    - Erdoğan–Iyengar needs a finite VC dimension.
  - Proposition 4 and Remark 1 criticize Esfahani–Kuhn's finite-sample argument for p-WDRO (p. 14).
  - His own Approx PCM appears as a baseline and loses throughout (pp. 21–22).
