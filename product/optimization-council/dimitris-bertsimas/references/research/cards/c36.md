### D022 · Strike Price Optimization for ISO New England's Day-Ahead Ancillary Services (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2608.29366)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (29 August 2026). The main text (pp. 1–8) was read in full and the references (pp. 8–9) skimmed. The extracted reference list stops in the middle of entry [16] (p. 9). Title and Bertsimas (last author) confirmed on p. 1.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Karl Zhu (MIT Operations Research Center); Parviz Alivand, Jinye Zhao and Tongxin Zheng (ISO New England, Holyoke, MA) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a 2026 power-market design paper written with the operator's own staff and submitted to IEEE Transactions on Energy Markets, Policy and Regulation (p. 1).
- Zhao and Zheng also co-authored the 2012 adaptive robust unit-commitment paper (card S006 in c13, pp. 1, 12). The ISO-NE partnership therefore spans at least 2011–2026 [inferred from the two author lists].
- It carries the standard disclaimer that the views are the authors', not ISO New England's (p. 1).
- It cites no Bertsimas paper, not even S006 (references, pp. 8–9).
- The work list notes it is not on his Scholar profile or DBLP.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + RO + IO; LO in the feasibility checks; DA as the setting, a decision taken from a predicted price distribution}: The entry is the owner's current rule, under cost pressure.
  - The mechanism. Since 1 March 2025, ISO-NE's Day-Ahead Ancillary Services Initiative (DASI) co-optimizes energy with four reserve products, settled as financial call options on the real-time price. One system-wide strike K_t is announced per hour (p. 1).
  - Awarded resources have no physical obligation to produce, so the strike is the main lever for real-time performance (p. 1).
  - The trade-off: raising K lowers competitive reserve offers (efficiency) but weakens the incentive to pay preparation costs and stay available (reliability) (pp. 1–2).
  - The current rule is K = E[P_t] + 10, with the expectation taken from a conditional Gaussian mixture model (GMM) (p. 1).
    - The authors grant that the rule is intuitive and simple.
    - Then they name what it misses: E[P_t] is not an estimate of the marginal costs of the resources the system needs, and the rule ignores preparation costs, capabilities, product eligibility and the nested reserve structure (pp. 1–2).
  - Why now:
    - Before launch, ISO-NE projected about $140 million a year in added costs. The Internal Market Monitor (IMM) reported $974 million of incremental cost over the first 12 months. ISO-NE then proposed a floor on the strike (p. 2).
    - The paper hedges that these costs reflect many parts of the market design (p. 2).
  - Problem statement: choose the highest strike at which enough resources remain certified incentive-compatible to meet every reserve requirement (pp. 2, 5).
  - Claimed gap: to the authors' knowledge, no prior work optimizes the DASI strike explicitly (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Certify each resource's incentive without the unobservable counterfactual, then count certified capacity.
  - The difficulty: a resource's incentive to prepare depends on the price distribution that would hold if it became available, and market data never show it.
  - The certificate: two reduced-form assumptions on price impact replace that distribution by a tight lower bound, computable from the observed conditional distribution (Theorem 6, pp. 3–4).
  - The reduction:
    - Each resource enters the system problem through a single threshold κ_i, and any finite optimum sits at one of these thresholds (Proposition 7, Theorem 8, pp. 4–6).
    - The mixed-integer nonlinear model (6) thus reduces to independent scalar root-findings plus at most |G| + 1 linear feasibility checks (Algorithm 1, p. 6).
  - Changed relative to the current rule: resource-specific marginal and preparation costs, capabilities, product eligibility and the nested reserve structure enter the choice (p. 2).
  - Kept:
    - the efficiency–reliability rationale behind the rule, on which the paper says it builds (p. 2);
    - ISO-NE's GMM;
    - the offer decomposition the ISO already assumes (Assumption 1, p. 2, from [16]);
    - the convention that the price distribution does not depend on K (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The model, per hour (Table I and (6)–(7), pp. 3, 5–6):
    - each resource i in G has a marginal cost c_i,t, a preparation cost A_i,t, a price-impact bound ϕ_i, product capabilities R_i,a,t and a 30-minute cap;
    - A_i,t is sunk once incurred and does not depend on later production (p. 3);
    - requirement categories Q have nested product sets, {TMSR}, {TMSR, TMNSR} and {TMSR, TMNSR, TMOR} (p. 5);
    - a binary z_i,t marks a resource counted as certified.
  - Payoffs are risk-neutral expectations (eqs. (1)–(4), p. 3):
    - available: E[(P^w − c)^+] − E[(P^w − K)^+] − A;
    - unavailable: −E[(P − K)^+].
    - The offer side carries a risk premium that decreases in K (Assumption 1, pp. 2–3).
  - Tractability assumptions, stated as such:
    - F_t is fixed with respect to K, consistent with ISO-NE's methodology (p. 2);
    - availability has no price impact below marginal cost (Assumption 4, p. 4);
    - above marginal cost, availability lowers the upper-tail survival function by at most a fraction ϕ (Assumption 5, p. 4), with ϕ_i constant across hours (p. 4);
    - the density is strictly positive above c, for the threshold result (Proposition 7, p. 4).
  - Data (Section V, p. 7):
    - the first 12 months of ISO-NE's conditional GMMs (1 March 2025 – 28 February 2026), reconstructed from the public memorandum [15] plus internally available model features;
    - representative heat rates of 7 MMBtu/MWh (combined cycle, Gas CC) and 11 (combustion turbine, Gas CT), matching the market monitor's revisions;
    - the Algonquin Citygate day-ahead gas price g;
    - c = α·HR·g and A = (1 − α)·HR·g, with salvage fraction α = 0.8 and ϕ = 0.2 as the baseline. Both are called reduced-form assumptions.
  - Admitted to be unobservable: resource-level parameters, especially preparation costs and price-impact bounds (pp. 2, 6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Offers decrease in K: (P − K)^+ is pointwise monotone in K, and the risk premium decreases (Proposition 2, p. 3).
  - The certificate (Theorem 6, p. 4):
    - The tail-integral identity E[(Y − a)^+] = ∫_a^∞ P(Y > p) dp rewrites the incentive (4).
    - Below c, the counterfactual terms cancel exactly (Assumption 4). Above c, the band of Assumption 5 gives I(K) ≥ (1 − ϕ)E[(P − c)^+] + ϕE[(P − K)^+] − A.
    - Tightness: the extremal counterfactual F^w = F + ϕF̄ above c attains the bound, so no uniformly stronger bound exists without further assumptions.
  - The threshold structure (Proposition 7, pp. 4–5):
    - dI/dK = 0 below c and −ϕP(P > K) above c.
    - So the certificate is negative for every K, nonnegative for every K, or crosses zero once at some κ ≥ c.
    - The crossing case needs ϕ > 0.
  - Threshold optimality (Theorem 8, pp. 5–6):
    - Counting every certified resource preserves feasibility, and the certified set changes only at thresholds.
    - An optimum strictly between thresholds could be raised to the next one, a contradiction.
    - Infeasibility and unboundedness are read off K = 0 and off the always-certified set (p. 6).
  - Algorithm 1 (p. 6): classify each resource; test the always-certified set for unboundedness; then try the finite thresholds in decreasing order. At most |G| + 1 linear checks are needed, and all nonlinear work is scalar root-finding.
  - [my reading] What carries the strike's effect:
    - With ϕ = 0 the certificate is constant in K (p. 5), and so is the exact incentive (4). With no price impact, the close-out charge (P − K)^+ is paid whether or not the resource prepares, and it cancels (p. 3).
    - In this model, then, the strike affects the preparation incentive only through the resource's own price impact.
    - Neither the introduction, which calls the strike the primary performance mechanism (p. 1), nor Section II.B's narrative of weakening incentives (p. 3) makes this explicit.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Illustration (Figure 1, pp. 5–6): the GMM for 1 July 2025 at 12:00 p.m. A resource with c = 40, A = 10 and ϕ = 0.2 has κ = 60.8.
  - Toy optimization (Table II, pp. 6–7):
    - three gas units with (c, A) = (35, 5), (37, 7) and (40, 10), capabilities of 600, 500 and 400 MW, and one 1,000 MW requirement;
    - thresholds of 145.4, 64.6 and 46.5;
    - K* = $64.6/MWh, attained at a threshold.
  - Empirical regime study (Table III, p. 7). The higher heat rate makes Gas CT the resource more likely to constrain the strike (pp. 7–8).

  - Sensitivity (Figure 2, p. 8): regime shares over a grid of α and ϕ. Raising ϕ moves hours from always-certified to finite thresholds and leaves the never-certified share unchanged (p. 7).
  - What is absent:
    - A system-wide calculation, deliberately omitted: it would need a fleet study of hourly marginal costs, preparation costs, capabilities, eligibility and price impacts (p. 6).
    - Run times. None are needed for root-finding and LP checks, and none are reported.

  - [my arithmetic, not in the paper] The current rule on the same toy hour:

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) An incentive that depends on a counterfactual distribution you cannot observe:
  - Bound the counterfactual by a reduced-form band: unchanged below cost, and the survival function cut by at most a fraction ϕ above it.
  - Lower-bound the incentive through the tail-integral identity.
  - Prove tightness by exhibiting the band's extreme distribution (Theorem 6, pp. 3–4).
- (D4) For problems that maximize a scalar parameter subject to enough qualifying agents covering the requirements (Proposition 7, Theorem 8, Algorithm 1, pp. 4–6):
  - Show that each agent qualifies on an interval bounded by one threshold. The optimum then sits at a threshold.
  - A sorted scan with LP feasibility checks solves the MINLP exactly.
  - Read infeasibility and unboundedness off the parameter's extreme values.
- (D4) Differentiate the certificate in the design lever to see which modelling term carries the lever's effect (dI/dK = −ϕP(P > K), p. 5). Then ask whether the lever still matters when that term vanishes [my reading, D4].
- (D3) Split one fuel commitment into marginal cost and sunk preparation cost with a single salvage fraction, so that the parts add up without double counting (p. 7).
- (D5) Per resource type, over a year of hourly distributions, report:
  - the regime shares (never, finite, always certified) and the quantiles of the finite thresholds (Table III, p. 7);
  - a regime map over the reduced-form parameters (Figure 2, p. 8).
- (D7) When the inputs to a system-wide number are unobservable, say so. Publish the framework with the resource-level inputs instead of a headline built on simplified assumptions (pp. 2, 6, 8).

### S298 · Global optimization via optimal decision trees (Journal of Global Optimization 95(1), 23–63, 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10898-023-01311-x; arXiv 2202.06017)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (12 February 2022, headed "Submitted to Operations Research").
- Read in full: the main text (pp. 1–45), Appendix A (pp. 45–49) and the acknowledgments (p. 50). The references (pp. 50–52) were skimmed.
- The JOGO print was not read, so the pages below are arXiv pages; cite as [S298 arXiv p. N].
- Several displayed formulas are garbled in the extraction ((7), (8), (15), (16)). Statements about them rest on the legible parts and the prose.
- Title and both authors confirmed on p. 1.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Berk Öztürk (MIT Department of Aeronautics and Astronautics) (p. 1). Michael Luu is thanked for the satellite formulation, and the work was funded by DARPA contract HR0011-17-2-0028 (p. 50) · **Position in trajectory**: the DA line of optimization with learned constraints (team.json domain DA), applied to global optimization.
- Building blocks: the Bertsimas–Dunn hyperplane trees OCT-H and ORT-H (2017, 2019; see card S011 in c17 for OCT) (p. 3).
- Its closest relative is Maragno et al. (2021), on which Bertsimas is a co-author, cited as the more general data-driven framework (pp. 6, 44).
- It cites Bertsimas–Stellato's *The voice of optimization* (card S112 in c23) as a parallel use of ML in optimization (p. 5).
- Dates: arXiv v1 is from February 2022 (p. 1). The work list gives the 2026 JOGO volume, and the DOI carries a 2023 article number [metadata, not read].
- Drafting [inferred]: a two-author paper speaks of "the author" in the singular four times (pp. 39, 43, 45, 50), which suggests one drafting author. SKILL.md (How to Use) notes that much post-2010 writing is his students'.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + IO + ML; HS for the black-box claim; AP for the aerospace cases}: The entry is a scope restriction in the owning tradition.
  - Global optimizers reduce problems to structured forms and so accept only some mathematical primitives. BARON's manual is quoted as handling exp, ln, x^α and β^x (p. 3).
  - Real constraints also involve trigonometric functions, signomials, piecewise-discontinuous functions and black boxes such as simulation outputs (p. 3).
  - The incumbent families, each given a weakness (pp. 2–3):
    - gradient-based NLP solvers (CONOPT, IPOPT) depend on good starting points and fail with integer variables;
    - outer approximation needs special structure and has had less commercial success (p. 2);
    - MIO branch-and-reduce (BARON) scales worse.
  - The problem: the general MINLP (1) with arbitrary explicit or inexplicit constraints, under one stated requirement, a bounded domain for the variables in nonlinear constraints (pp. 1, 3).
  - Why now: asserted as MIO's speed plus recent ML (abstract, p. 1), with no figure.
- **D2 Core idea**: Learn each hard constraint with an optimal hyperplane tree, embed the tree exactly as an MIO, then repair against the true functions.
  - Constraints: each nonlinear inequality g_i(x) ≥ 0 is replaced by an OCT-H trained on sampled feasibility labels. Each feasible leaf is a polyhedron, so the constraint becomes a union of polyhedra (pp. 3–4, 14–15). Equalities and objectives get their own devices (pp. 16–18).
  - Solve and repair: CPLEX solves the resulting MILO. Its near-feasible, near-optimal point is then repaired by projected gradient descent (PGD), using automatic differentiation (AD) of the true constraints (pp. 4, 19–22).
  - Changed relative to BARON-style global optimization: no restriction on primitives and no branching on the true functions. The constraints only need to be queried (p. 4).
  - Kept: MIO branch-and-bound as the engine (p. 19).
  - Why trees: they are MIO-representable, and also tunable, accurate and interpretable (p. 6). Hyperplane splits give accuracy with smaller trees (pp. 7, 13).
  - The implementation is called OCT-HaGOn (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Standard form (2), p. 9: linear constraints go to the MIO unchanged; nonlinear and all black-box constraints are approximated; bounds are required. Missing bounds come from min/max LPs over the linear constraints (eq. (3), p. 10).
  - Sampling, per constraint (pp. 10–13):
    - hypercube corners ("boundary sampling"; only a limited subset in practice);
    - an optimal Latin hypercube, found by a permutation genetic algorithm that is stopped early;
    - kNN quasi-Newton sampling: k = p + 1 neighbours, and secant steps between infeasible centres and their feasible neighbours, to put points near the boundary (Algorithm 1, pp. 12–13).
  - Learning (pp. 13–14):
    - Trees are trained on all the data, with almost no complexity penalty, because the data are noise-free.
    - Base parameters (Table 1): maximum depth 5, complexity 10^−6, minbucket 0.01 (0.02 for ORT-H), 10 random tree restarts, 5 hyperplane restarts.
    - Losses: weighted misclassification for OCT-H; 1 − R² for ORT-H.
  - Assumptions admitted only later: the constraints are cheap to evaluate, and the repair step needs them to be AD-differentiable (p. 40).
  - Instances:
    - 5 small and 6 larger MINLPLib problems (Tables 3 and 5, pp. 29–30);
    - Golinski's speed reducer (7 variables, 11 constraints; Appendix A.3, pp. 31, 46);
    - satellite on-orbit servicing (OOS) with 7 clients, from Luu–Hastings 2020 (pp. 32–37; Appendix A.4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Inequalities (pp. 15–16):
    - The big-M disjunction (7) over feasible leaves is given first, and the difficulty of choosing M is named (p. 15).
    - The implemented alternative is a big-M-free extended formulation (8): copies y_l with x·z_l ≤ y_l ≤ x̄·z_l, Σ y_l = x and Σ z_l = 1.
    - The stated reasons for implementing it: it is tractable and big-M free, and it is locally ideal (Vielma 2015), which the paper says gives computational advantages over the big-M variant (p. 16).
  - Equalities (pp. 16–17): fit an OCT-H to the relaxation h(x) ≥ 0, then require x to lie in one feasible and one infeasible leaf at once, that is on a separating face ((9)–(11)).
  - Objectives and separable constraints aᵀx + b ≥ g(x) (pp. 17–18): fit an ORT-H to g, then refit in each leaf the tightest lower-bounding hyperplane on the leaf's points. This gives an approximate relaxation, which the repair step tightens.
  - Repair (pp. 19–22):
    - constraints are linearized at x* by forward-mode AD;
    - violated inequalities and every equality get squared-penalized slacks;
    - the step is bounded by α·exp(−rt/T) in normalized coordinates;
    - when x* violates a constraint beyond tolerance, a projection step with penalty β is taken ((13)–(16));
    - default parameters are in Table 2 (p. 22);
    - each step is an MI-quadratic problem solved by CPLEX (pp. 19, 45);
    - no convergence result is stated.
  - Size counts (pp. 41–43):
    - binaries are linear in the number of leaves, O(2^d(1 + |I| + |J|));
    - disjunctive constraints are O(2^d·d·(1 + |I| + |J|)), called super-exponential in the depth d;
    - solution time could be exponential in the worst case (p. 42), though this has not been observed so far (p. 43).
  - No approximation-error bound and no optimality guarantee: the method is approximate and certifies nothing global (p. 39).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Worked example (Section 4, pp. 22–28): a Duran–Grossmann MINLP with its objective made linear.
    - A depth-3 OCT-H for g1 is 97% accurate on 554 samples (Figure 2, p. 25).
    - The MIO gives −7.685 (infeasible), repaired to −7.021. PGD stops at its 100-iteration cap (pp. 27–28).
  - Small benchmarks (Table 4, p. 29):
    - the same objective as BARON on all five, but slower, e.g. minlp 29.9 s against 0.120 s and nlp3 17.7 s against 1.265 s;
    - tree training takes most of the time; MIO and repair together take under 5% (p. 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Embed a trained hyperplane tree in an MIO with the extended formulation (pp. 15–16):
  - one copy y_l and one binary z_l per feasible leaf;
  - x·z_l ≤ y_l ≤ x̄·z_l, Σ y_l = x, Σ z_l = 1.
  - It needs no big-M and is locally ideal (Vielma 2015).
- (D4) Approximate an equality h(x) = 0 by classifying h(x) ≥ 0, then force x into one feasible and one infeasible leaf at once (pp. 16–17).
- (D4) For an objective or a separable constraint aᵀx + b ≥ g(x), regress g with a tree. Then refit in each leaf the tightest lower-bounding hyperplane on the leaf's samples. The result is a cheap approximate relaxation (pp. 17–18).
- (D3) Get sampling bounds from min/max LPs over the linear constraints (eq. (3), p. 10).
- (D3) Sample for constraint learning in stages (pp. 10–13):

  - a near-optimal Latin hypercube (genetic algorithm stopped early);

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; outside the domain's optimization-lens scope: nothing is fitted or optimized on the target data}:
  - The task: time series classification (TSC), benchmarked on the UCR (univariate) and UEA (multivariate) archives (p. 1).
  - The gap between three families (pp. 1–2):
    - HIVE-COTE 2.0 (HC2) is the most accurate but trains for hours to days per dataset;
    - Rocket-type random convolutional features come close at a fraction of the cost;
    - TSC foundation models, used as frozen feature extractors, match neither.
  - The opening: TabPFN, a pretrained tabular foundation model, has matured. It classifies in context, with no gradient updates on the target data, and unlike the linear classifiers usually paired with Rocket it can represent non-linear decision rules (p. 2).
  - The question, which the authors answer experimentally (p. 2): can a training-free pipeline of random convolutional features and in-context tabular classification reach the accuracy of the methods that dominate these archives?
- **D2 Core idea**: Two training-free stages, glued by grouping.
  - Rocket features (random kernels, each giving its maximum and its proportion of positive values) feed TabPFN v2.5 (p. 4).
  - The mismatch: Rocket's default 10,000 kernels produce 20,000 features, while TabPFN v2.5 is designed for up to 2,000.
  - The fix: split the kernels into G groups of 1,000, classify each group in context, and average the class probabilities (eq. (1), p. 4).
  - With G = 10 the kernel budget equals Rocket's default, so the comparison with Rocket isolates the classifier: ridge regression against in-context learning (p. 4).
  - Changed relative to Rocket: the classifier and the grouping. Kept: the kernels, z-normalization and the aeon toolkit (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Scope: datasets within TabPFN's limits (at most 10 classes, at most 50,000 training cases). That leaves 103 UCR and 23 UEA datasets; all 25 UCR and 7 UEA exclusions are due to the class limit and are listed (pp. 4, 14).
  - Comparisons run on the intersection with the bake-off benchmark of Middlehurst et al. (2021): 92 UCR and 20 UEA datasets (pp. 4, 6, 8).
  - Protocol: 30 stratified resamples per dataset; resample 0 is the archive split and 1–29 are seeded; the metric is mean accuracy (p. 4).
  - Baselines: HC2, its four components and the other published methods are taken from Middlehurst et al. (2021). MOMENT, Mantis and MantisV2 are run by the authors under the same protocol (p. 5).
  - Fixed settings: TabPFN's internal ensemble e = 8 (pp. 3, 9); G ∈ {1, 10} and k = 1,000, chosen without tuning (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (no optimization model; statistical and ablation devices only):
