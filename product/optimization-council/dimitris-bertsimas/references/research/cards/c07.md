Cross-card notes (each point is carded below):
- **One model, three staged papers.** The budget model came out as three papers. S001 (received September 2001, p. 1) has the LP, the bounds and the illustrations. S004 (received January 2002, citing S001 as a 2001 working paper, pp. 1, 22) has discrete optimization, complexity and network flows. S019 (received August 2003, p. 1) has the norm unification and distribution-free bounds. Paragraphs recur almost word for word: the "nature will be restricted" paragraph (S001 p. 3 = S004 p. 4) and the knapsack paragraph (S001 p. 10 ≈ S004 pp. 16–17).
- **Solver-version mismatch.** The same 200-item robust knapsack is reported with the same data, the same PC and the same 0.05–50 s range in two papers. S001 says CPLEX 6.0 solved it (p. 10); S004 says CPLEX 7.0 (p. 16).
- **Stress tests outside the design model.** Three papers judge decisions by simulating a different perturbation model from the one they were designed for (S001 p. 12, S004 pp. 18–22, S034 pp. 17–19). This is a new-pattern candidate.
- **Math programs inside DP.** Both ADP papers put a math program inside DP. S070 uses a base heuristic and a truncated-LP bound as the cost-to-go (pp. 3–5); S034 uses an LP relaxation as the value function (pp. 8–9). Both judge quality against relaxation bounds, not MIO certificates (S070 p. 9; S034 pp. 7, 21). This is his own route through ADP, not the ADP tradition the council does not voice.
- **The pre-speed-up baseline.** In 2002 his own matched-quality test has CPLEX 6.0 losing to an LP-rounding heuristic (S070 pp. 13–15). This is the state of affairs that Method 2 later concedes and re-measures.

### S070 · An approximate dynamic programming approach to multidimensional knapsack problems (Management Science 2002, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.48.4.550.208)
**Read level**: full. All 16 file pages were read:
- the abstract and §1 (pp. 1–2);
- §2, the MKP and its DP (pp. 2–3);
- §3, the ADP base-heuristic method and adaptive fixing (pp. 3–6);
- §4, parametric and nonparametric ADP (pp. 7–8);
- §5, the computations, Tables 1–6 (pp. 9–15);
- §6, the conclusions (p. 15).

The references (pp. 15–16) were skimmed. The extracted tables have lost their decimal points (e.g. "001 392" is 0.01 s and 3.92% in Table 2, p. 10), so the numbers below are those restated in the text unless they are marked as read off a table.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ramazan Demir (MIT Operations Research Center; alum e-mail on p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the IO and approximation years just before robust optimization.
- Received September 2000, and "with the authors 10 months for 2 revisions" (p. 16).
- Note 06 lists "ADP for integer programs (Demir 2000)" among his 2000–2004 PhD theses [06 §2.2].
- The paper announces a companion paper on generic binary IP (Bertsimas and Demir 2001, p. 15).
- Supported by NSF grant DMI-9610486 and the MIT–Singapore alliance (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+HS; SD in the probabilistic-analysis motivation of §4.1}: The entry is method-first.
  - ADP's record is mostly in stochastic optimization. The paper asks "whether ADP methods represent a realistic alternative for large scale MKPs", judged on speed, quality of solutions and robustness. The MKP serves as "a reasonably rich class of integer programs" (p. 2).
  - The MKP is motivated by applications: combinatorial auctions, project selection, cargo loading, capital budgeting and asset-backed securitization. Set cover also becomes an MKP once its variables are complemented (pp. 1, 3).
  - The MKP is NP-hard in the strong sense, and exact DP needs O(n b*^m) calculations, impractical "for even moderate m" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: to test a method family where its record is thinnest, pick a well-benchmarked problem class that has a literature of heuristics. Fix the evaluation questions before reporting. Here there are five, ending "Most importantly, is ADP for MKPs a competitive method worth further study?" (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The MKP is rewritten as a DP over items and residual capacity (recursion (3)–(4), p. 3), and the value function F(k, b) is replaced by cheap estimates of three kinds:
  - (a) the value of a base heuristic on the subproblem (ADP-BH, pp. 3–5);
  - (b) a linear function of the capacity vector, fitted stage by stage (ADP-P, p. 7);
  - (c) kernel-weighted interpolation over sampled states (ADP-N, p. 8).

  ADP-BH is a look-ahead scheme in the lineage the paper cites (Sarkar et al.; Bertsimas–Teo–Vohra, p. 2). It does not use the raw heuristic value. Both branch estimates are rescaled to the same percentage deviation below an upper bound U, the truncated LP value, giving (1 − δ*)U (p. 4). Three further devices sit around it (pp. 4–5):
  - reduced-cost fixing, with the heuristic value as the lower bound (p. 4);
  - early termination once the heuristic value equals the LP bound on the relevant subproblems (pp. 4–5);
  - "lag variable fixing", which trades quality for time (p. 5).

  The new base heuristic is adaptive fixing (AF, Figure 2, p. 6). It repeatedly solves the LP relaxation, fixes variables at 1 when they are 1 and at 0 when they are below a threshold δ, and sets the least fractional variable to 0, until the LP is integral. δ = 0.25 is used for speed, and δ = 1 is the truncation heuristic (p. 6).
  - Transferable: when a heuristic's value stands in for the cost-to-go in a two-branch look-ahead, scale both branch estimates to the same gap below a common upper bound. Heuristic bias then cannot decide the branch (p. 4).
  - Transferable: LP-driven adaptive rounding (fix the obvious variables, zero the least fractional one, re-solve) gives a primal heuristic with a single quality/time knob δ (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The model is the MKP with nonnegative c, A and b (p. 2).
  - The random instances have uncorrelated, weakly correlated and strongly correlated profits, 10 instances per class and size (m, n), generated with the parameters of Table 1 (p. 9). Capacities are b_i = 0.5 Σ_j a_ij (p. 9), and sizes go up to m = 100, n = 1000 (Tables 2–6).
  - The literature instances are Chu and Beasley's largest: m = 30, n = 250 or 500, with tightness ratios 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 (p. 11).
  - ADP-P rests on the probabilistic model of Meanti et al. and Piersma: i.i.d. columns and profits, b = nβ. Under it, Z_LP/n and Z_MKP/n converge to the same constant, and that is the stated reason for an approximation linear in b (p. 7).
  - The state-space sample is a uniform grid. Stratified samples did no better, and random ones did worse (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: The paper designs algorithms but proves no guarantee: no theorem bounds the error of ADP-BH, and the only theory is the imported asymptotic result (p. 7). The devices are:
  - the DP recursion and its complexity count (p. 3);
  - reduced-cost fixing with the heuristic as the incumbent bound, citing Nemhauser–Wolsey (p. 4);
  - value estimates scaled by the LP bound (p. 4);
  - optimality tests for subproblems when the heuristic value equals the upper bound (pp. 4–5);
  - least-absolute-deviation fitting rewritten as an LP, citing his LO textbook, and solved with CPLEX (p. 7);
  - kernel regression with a Fan–Gijbels kernel (p. 8);
  - a greedy fill-up of the slack as a final local improvement for ADP-P and ADP-N (p. 8).
  - Transferable: let an asymptotic probabilistic analysis choose the parametric form of the value function (Z grows linearly in n, so approximate linearly in b). Then test that choice against a nonparametric alternative (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: The criteria are fixed in advance: quality, time, and "robustness". Robustness here means low variation in the computing resources needed across instances of the same size (p. 9). Quality is PE, the percentage deviation from the LP-relaxation value (p. 9). Since the LP value bounds the optimum, every PE is also an upper bound on the true gap [my reading; the paper does not say so]. Runs are on a Dell Precision 410 under Linux, and the instances were posted online (p. 9, footnote 1). Results:
  - **Base heuristics**: AF (H2) beats five other heuristics (truncation H1 and the literature heuristics H3–H6) on quality and time. Its average PE is 1.14, 1.81 and 2.49 for uncorrelated, weakly and strongly correlated instances (p. 10).
  - **ADP-P and ADP-N**: they match or beat H1 and H3–H6 but need tuning of the sample size, and larger samples (50–250) did not help (pp. 10–11). Both are "not competitive" (pp. 1, 15).
  - **ADP-H2**: average PE 0.68, 1.11 and 1.51, and 0.17, 0.34 and 0.47 at m = 100, n = 1000. That is about a 30% improvement over H2 (p. 11).
  - **Against the Chu–Beasley GA** on its largest instances: the GA's reported times are halved for machine speed (p. 11). The GA wins on quality (average PE 0.56 against 0.74–0.80) but takes 1,287.15 s on average against 87.06 s (p. 13).
  - **Against CPLEX 6.0** (p. 13): the relative-gap tolerance is set to PE(ADP-H2)/100, so CPLEX has to reach the heuristic's quality. Tree memory is capped at 250 MB and time at 6,000 s. CPLEX runs both with default settings (CPLEX-D) and with tuned ones (CPLEX-N: cuts off, periodic heuristic, depth-first search).
    - CPLEX reached that quality within the limit on 44 of 120 (D) and 45 of 120 (N) instances (p. 15).
    - Where both settings did, they averaged 565.84 s and 592.19 s, against 7.52 s for ADP-H2 (pp. 13, 15).
    - Where CPLEX hit the limit, its PE was 3.94 (D) and 2.35 (N), against 0.93 for ADP-H2 at 380.26 s on average (p. 15).
  - **Reported limits**: the GA's quality edge (p. 13); ADP-P and ADP-N not competitive (p. 15); min/avg/max spreads in Tables 3 and 6 (pp. 10, 14).
  - Transferable: benchmark a heuristic against a MIP solver at matched quality. Set the solver's relative-gap tolerance to the bound gap the heuristic achieved, give it time and memory limits, run default and tuned settings, and report both time-to-quality and quality-at-the-limit (pp. 13–15).
  - Transferable: when a rival's times come from another machine, state the speed ratio and rescale explicitly (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: The ADP literature is reviewed as a toolkit (p. 2): Bellman; Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis neuro-dynamic programming; look-ahead schemes (Sarkar et al.; his own facility-location work with Teo and Vohra). MKP heuristics are grouped as greedy, aggregation, tabu and genetic (p. 3). Exact methods get one clause: "limited success for large instances" (p. 3). The Chu–Beasley GA is chosen as the strongest rival because GAs "seem to produce high-quality solutions" and it attempted the largest instances in the literature (pp. 3, 11). Predecessors are judged by numbers only, never criticized.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract lists four findings, (a)–(d). The last is negative: parametric and nonparametric ADP are "not competitive" (p. 1).
  - The introduction states the objective as a question (p. 2), and §5 opens with five numbered questions (p. 9).
  - The conclusions give three numbered findings: a serendipitous one (AF, "not the main focus", was "surprisingly strong"), a positive one and a negative one (p. 15).
  - The register is hedged throughout: "suggests", "encouraging", "seems an attractive alternative" (pp. 1, 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and computational:
  - an ADP framework that wraps any base heuristic;
  - a new LP-rounding heuristic;
  - a negative verdict on parametric and nonparametric value approximation for this class (p. 15).

  Limits are reported as findings, not as caveats: the GA's better quality (p. 13) and the sample-size tuning (p. 11). There are no guarantees and no instances beyond n = 1000. The stated next step is ADP for generic binary IP (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. The protocol already exists in 2002, in heuristic form:
  - The incumbent heuristic gives the starting solution (x_BEST from the base heuristic, p. 4).
  - The LP bound stands in for an MIO certificate: PE against the LP value (p. 9), plus subproblem optimality when the heuristic value meets the bound (pp. 4–5).
  - The duel is with the field's incumbents: the literature heuristics, the Chu–Beasley GA on its own instances, and CPLEX 6.0 with default and tuned settings (pp. 10–15).
  - The machine, the limits and the speed normalization are all stated (pp. 9, 11, 13).

  Here, though, the heuristic is the product and the exact solver is the loser.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant, a pre-speed-up data point. In his own matched-quality test, CPLEX 6.0 needs about 75 times as long to reach ADP-H2's quality (565.84 s against 7.52 s), and on most instances it fails to reach that quality within 6,000 s (pp. 13, 15). This is the belief that Method 2 later concedes was right when formed. The protocol is a ready template for re-measuring it.
- Heuristic 4 ✅ evidence. The negative result is printed in the abstract and the conclusions (pp. 1, 15), and the GA's quality advantage is reported (p. 13).
- Tension T2 ✅ evidence. The deliverable is a heuristic engine, judged "a promising computational approach ... worthy of further investigation" (p. 1).
- Domain Map, IO ("LP-relaxation rounding") ✅ evidence: the adaptive-fixing heuristic (p. 6).
- Research Taste mark 6 ⚠: the entry is method-first (ADP tried on a testbed class), not an owner's problem with data (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: matched-quality benchmarking against a commercial MIP solver (pp. 13–15).
- New-pattern candidate: math-programming value approximations inside DP (base heuristic or LP bound as the cost-to-go, pp. 3–5); also in S034.
- New-pattern candidate: a bound-referenced quality yardstick for heuristics (PE against the LP value, p. 9); also in S034.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Matched-quality solver benchmark: set the MIP solver's gap tolerance to the heuristic's achieved bound gap, cap time and memory, run default and tuned settings, and report time-to-quality, quality-at-the-limit and the number of instances that reach the target (pp. 13–15).
- In a two-branch look-ahead, scale both heuristic estimates to the same percentage gap below a common LP bound before comparing them (p. 4).
- Adaptive LP fixing as a primal heuristic. Fix the variables at 1, zero those below δ, then zero the least fractional variable and re-solve. δ trades time against quality, and δ = 1 gives plain truncation (p. 6).
- Report heuristic quality as the percentage deviation from the LP bound, so that every table entry is also an upper bound on the optimality gap (p. 9).
- Define an algorithm's robustness as the spread of time and quality across instances of the same size, and report min/avg/max (pp. 9–10, 14).
- Rescale a rival's published times by a stated machine-speed ratio (p. 11), and publish your own instances (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "the ADP approach based on parametric and nonparametric approximations, while producing reasonable solutions, is not competitive." (p. 1)
- "Although not the main focus of the paper the adaptive fixing heuristic for the MKP was surprisingly strong. It is fast and accurate." (p. 15)

### S004 · Robust discrete optimization and network flows (Mathematical Programming Ser. B 2003, DOI 10.1007/s10107-003-0396-4)
**Read level**: full. Pp. 1–23 were read: the introduction (pp. 1–3); all theorems and proofs in §§2–5 (pp. 3–15); the experiments of §6 (pp. 15–22); the conclusions (p. 22). The references (pp. 22–23) were skimmed. The extraction drops the Γ glyph in most formulas and sentences (e.g. "by varying a single parameter ." on p. 22). Γ was restored from the figure legends (pp. 19–21) and from context.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Melvyn Sim (MIT ORC graduate student on an NUS scholarship, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the discrete-optimization companion of S001.
- Received January 2002 (p. 1).
- Cites "The Price of Robustness" as a 2001 ORC working paper, reference [7] (p. 22).
- The budget model's first IO extension, written while Sim was a student; his thesis is dated 2004 in note 06 [06 §2.2].

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+IO; LO for network flows}: The paper enters through two gaps.
  - Stochastic programming needs distributions that are rarely known in practice, and its scenario models grow drastically in size (p. 1).
  - Existing robust approaches each fail somewhere (p. 2). Soyster is over-conservative. The ellipsoidal counterparts of Ben-Tal–Nemirovski and El Ghaoui are conic quadratic and "cannot be directly applied to discrete optimization". The scenario robustness of Kouvelis–Yu, and minimax regret, make many polynomially solvable problems NP-hard. Averbakh's polynomial case "does not seem to generalize".
  - The stated goal has two parts: to control the degree of conservatism, and to be "computationally tractable both practically and theoretically" (pp. 1–2).
  - Transferable: define the target as two requirements, here tunable conservatism and preserved tractability, and record which requirement each rival fails (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: S001's budget model is carried over to MIP and combinatorial problems, with four results:
  - (i) **Robust MIP.** With uncertainty in both A and c, the robust MIP is a MIP "of moderately larger size" (pp. 1–2; Theorem 1, pp. 5–6).
  - (ii) **Cost-only 0–1 problems.** With only cost uncertainty on a 0–1 problem, min c'x + max over |S| ≤ Γ of Σ_{j∈S} d_j x_j equals the minimum of n + 1 nominal problems with modified costs (Theorem 3, p. 8; Algorithm A, p. 10). Only f + 1 problems are needed when the d_j take f distinct values, and only two when all d_j are equal (p. 10).
  - (iii) **Approximation transfer.** Using an α-approximation inside Algorithm A keeps the α guarantee (Algorithm B, Theorem 4, pp. 10–11).
  - (iv) **Robust minimum-cost flow.** For fixed θ the inner problem is a minimum-cost flow on a network in which every uncertain arc becomes a four-arc gadget (Theorem 5, Fig. 1, pp. 12–13). Z(θ) is convex and |A|-Lipschitz (Theorem 6, pp. 14–15), so a binary search over θ needs 2⌈log2(|A|θ̄/ε)⌉ + 3 flow problems (Theorem 7, p. 15).
  - Transferable: when the adversary chooses a budgeted subset, dualize it into one threshold θ plus positive parts. On 0–1 variables, max(d_j x_j − θ, 0) = max(d_j − θ, 0)x_j. The objective is then piecewise linear in θ with breakpoints at the d_j, and only θ ∈ {d_1, …, d_n, 0} need be tried (pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable: to keep a network structure, model the positive part max(x − θ/d, 0) by splitting each arc into a branch of capacity θ/d and a cost-d overflow branch (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - **Uncertainty model U.** Constraint coefficients are independent, symmetric and bounded in [a_ij − â_ij, a_ij + â_ij], with unknown distribution. Costs lie in [c_j, c_j + d_j] (pp. 3–4). The stance: "We feel that it is unlikely that we know the exact distribution of these coefficients" (p. 3).
  - **Protection levels.** Γ_i ∈ [0, |J_i|] may be fractional for constraints; Γ_0 for the objective is an integer (p. 4). An uncertain right-hand side is absorbed by an extra variable (p. 3).
  - **0–1 requirement.** It is "critically used" in equation (18), which fails for general integers (p. 10).
  - **Cost uncertainty.** No probabilistic assumption is made on costs, hence "no theoretical claims regarding suboptimality" (p. 7).
  - **Network flows.** Arc capacities are taken as finite without loss of generality (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: The proof skeletons:
  - The inner maximum is written as an LP over z ∈ [0, 1] with Σz ≤ Γ, then dualized by strong duality (Theorem 1, pp. 5–6). The counterpart has 2n + m + l variables (p. 6).
  - The probability bound is imported from S001 as Theorem 2, with a remark that it does not depend on x* (pp. 6–7).
  - The θ-axis is split into intervals; a linear function of θ is optimal at an endpoint (Theorem 3, pp. 8–9).
  - Approximation transfer: Z_B ≤ α(G_l − d_l) + d_l ≤ αG_l (Theorem 4, p. 11). A counter-remark follows: TSP under the triangle inequality (α = 3/2) does not transfer, because the modified instances may violate the inequality (p. 11).
  - Network flows: the arc-splitting gadget (Theorem 5, p. 13); convexity, from feasible convex combinations, and a Lipschitz constant, from Lagrangian duality (Theorem 6, pp. 14–15); binary search (Theorem 7, p. 15). Srinivasan–Thompson's parametric simplex would give all Γ at once, but "it is not polynomial" (p. 13).
  - [My reading] As extracted, the proof of Theorem 7 bounds the error additively, |Z(θ̂) − Z(θ*)| ≤ ε, while the statement is relative: Z* ≤ Ẑ ≤ (1 + ε)Z* (p. 15). The two agree only when Z* ≥ 1, for example with integral costs. The text does not comment on this.
  - Transferable: when the robust problem is a one-parameter family of nominal problems, prove convexity and a Lipschitz constant in that parameter, then binary search with an explicit count of nominal solves (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: three small studies (pp. 15–22).
  - **Robust knapsack.** n = 200, b = 4,000, weights 20–29, profits 16–77 and δ = 10% of each weight. It was solved with CPLEX 7.0 on a Pentium II 400 PC in 0.05–50 s (p. 16).
    - Table 1: Γ = 36.8 gives a violation bound of 5.71×10⁻³ for a 1.54% loss, and full protection (Γ = 200) costs 5.5% (p. 16).
    - Over twenty instances, a 2% profit reduction brings the bound below 10⁻³, and this "did not seem to depend a lot on the specific instance" (Fig. 2, p. 17).
    - S001 reports the same experiment, on the same PC and with the same range of times, as CPLEX 6.0 (S001 p. 10).
  - **Robust sorting.** Choose k = 100 items of 200 under cost uncertainty. Algorithm A found all Γ ∈ {0, …, 100} "in less than a second", while CPLEX 7.0 on the compact MIP (30) took 30–80 minutes for a single Γ (p. 18).
    - Evaluation uses a perturbation model the robust problem was not built on: each cost jumps to c_j + d_j with probability 0.2, over 20,000 samples (p. 18).
    - The standard deviation falls 32.1% while the nominal cost rises 20.37% (Table 2, p. 19). Γ = 20 dominates the nominal solution in distribution, and Γ = 100 "appears over conservative" (pp. 18–19).
    - The authors note that the model does not optimize σ; σ was chosen for its "intuitive appeal" (p. 18).
  - **Robust shortest path.** A random digraph with 300 nodes and 1,475 arcs, with deviations up to 8 times the nominal cost. Algorithm A calls Dijkstra |A| + 1 times (p. 22).
    - With ρ = 0.1 and 20,000 scenarios, cost variability falls as Γ grows (p. 22).
    - Γ = 0 and Γ = 3 do not dominate each other, so the choice of Γ is left to the decision maker's risk preference (p. 22).
  - **Baselines.** Γ = 0 and full protection, plus CPLEX on the compact MIP. There is no computational comparison with Kouvelis–Yu or minimax regret, and no failure case is reported.
  - Transferable: judge a robust solution by simulating a perturbation model other than the uncertainty set it was built for (costs jump with probability p). Compare the whole cost distributions (CDF dominance), not only the means, and say when the reported statistic is not the one the model optimizes (pp. 18–22).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper is placed against stochastic programming (Dantzig 1955; scenario growth; for integers, Schultz et al., where the requirements are "even more severe"), Soyster, Ben-Tal–Nemirovski and El Ghaoui (conic), Kouvelis–Yu and minimax regret (NP-hardness), and Averbakh (a special case) (pp. 1–2). Its own predecessor [7] is credited for the LP approach and for Theorem 2 (pp. 2, 6). The conclusion claims a unique position: "Unlike all other approaches ..." (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract is a list of contributions with the complexity results as the headline (p. 1). The contributions (a)–(c) are repeated in §1 (pp. 2–3).
  - "surprisingly" announces the polynomiality result (p. 7).
  - Each theorem is followed by a Remark that says where it breaks: the 0–1 requirement (p. 10), the triangle inequality (p. 11), the parametric method not being polynomial (p. 13).
  - The conclusion is hedged: "We feel that the proposed approach has the potential of being practically useful" (p. 22).
  - Passages are shared almost word for word with S001 (the "nature will be restricted" paragraph, p. 4; the knapsack paragraph, pp. 16–17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory (complexity- and approximability-preservation theorems and algorithms) plus small computations. The stated limits are:
  - the 0–1 requirement (p. 10);
  - approximation transfer fails when the modified instances leave the approximable subclass (p. 11);
  - no claims about suboptimality under cost uncertainty (p. 7);
  - the parametric method is not polynomial (p. 13).

  There is no future work beyond practical usefulness for combinatorial and network-flow problems (p. 22). Note 05 records later work that cuts the n + 1 subproblems; it was not read here [05 §4.2].

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. This is the source of Method 1 step 3's "n + 1 nominal solves". The robust MIP stays a MIP (pp. 5–6). Cost-robust 0–1 problems keep polynomial solvability (pp. 8–10) and α-approximability (pp. 10–11). Robust minimum-cost flow becomes a short sequence of minimum-cost flows (pp. 12–15). The boundaries are printed (pp. 10, 11, 13).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant, concerning its Limitations line. In 2003, "tractable" already pairs complexity class with measured time: "both practically and theoretically" (pp. 1–2), with Algorithm A under a second against CPLEX's 30–80 minutes per Γ (p. 18). The 2004 → 2019 drift recorded in SKILL.md looks like a shift of emphasis, not of meaning.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. There is one knob Γ per constraint and one for the objective (p. 4), and a violation bound that does not depend on the solution (p. 6). Price curves are drawn for the knapsack, sorting and shortest path (pp. 16–22). The over-conservative end is shown (p. 19), and a case where no Γ dominates is handed to the decision maker's risk preference (p. 22). There is no bad case like PILOT4 here.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the duel is a structure-exploiting algorithm against a general MIP solver on the same robust model (p. 18), not a heuristic warm start inside MIO.
- New-pattern candidate: an out-of-model stress test of robust decisions (pp. 18–22; also S001 p. 12 and S034 pp. 17–19).
- New-pattern candidate: oracle reduction. Solve the robust problem with a counted number of calls to the nominal solver, so that complexity and approximation guarantees carry over (pp. 8–15).

- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - **LP counterpart.** By LP duality of the protection LP (Proposition 1, Theorem 1, pp. 3–4).
  - **Reduction of the violation event** to Pr(Σ γ_ij η_ij ≥ Γ_i) with γ_ij ≤ 1, by an exchange argument (Proposition 2, pp. 4–5).

  - **Best possible bound.** B(n, Γ), by induction on n, tight for symmetric ±1 variables (Theorem 3(a)–(b); appendix pp. 16–18).

  - **Bounds in use.** Four bounds are compared numerically for n = 10, 100 and 2,000 (Figs. 1–3, pp. 6–7). They are translated into the Γ needed for a 1% violation probability (Table 1, p. 8): 33.9 of 200 coefficients (17%), but all 5 of 5 (p. 6).

- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: One theorem covers all the cases.
  - **Dual-norm counterpart.** For U = {Ã : ‖M(vec(Ã) − vec(Ā))‖ ≤ Ω} with M invertible, constraint i becomes ā_i'x + Ω‖M^{-1}x_i‖* ≤ b_i, where ‖·‖* is the dual norm (Theorem 2, p. 3).
  - **Known cases recovered.** The self-dual Euclidean norm gives the SOCP of Ben-Tal–Nemirovski and El Ghaoui. The L1 and L∞ norms give LPs (pp. 3–4).
  - **The D-norm.** The budget set of [7] is a norm, the "D-norm" (p. 4). Its dual is max(‖s‖∞, ‖s‖1/p) (Proposition 2, p. 4), so the LP of [7] follows from Theorem 2 (p. 5).
  - **Proximity.** Bounds relate the D-norm and its dual to the Euclidean norm (Proposition 3, pp. 5–6).
  - Transferable: to unify rival uncertainty models, write each as a norm ball and read the counterpart's class off the dual norm. The question of which class becomes whether the dual norm is LP- or SOCP-representable (pp. 3–5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - **Uncertainty set.** A norm ball around the nominal matrix, with an invertible scaling M (p. 3). Theorem 1, for a general compact convex set S, is taken from Ben-Tal–Nemirovski's lecture notes (pp. 2–3).
  - **Probabilistic model** for the guarantees: an arbitrary, unknown distribution with known mean and covariance Σ, arbitrary dependencies allowed, and M = Σ^{-1/2} (p. 6).
  - **Trade-off.** This model is chosen for breadth, at the price of weaker bounds (pp. 6–7). There are no data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - **Support function** for a compact convex S (Theorem 1, p. 3).
  - **Change of variables** to the unit ball, plus the definition of the dual norm (Theorem 2, p. 3).
  - **Dual of the D-norm**, by applying LP duality twice (Proposition 2, p. 4).
  - **Norm proximity**: maximize a convex function over a polytope, so the optimum is at an extreme point; minimize via KKT (Proposition 3, pp. 5–6).
  - **Violation bound** from the Bertsimas–Popescu inequality P(X ∈ S) ≤ 1/(1 + d²), with d² obtained from a KKT solution of the Mahalanobis projection (Theorem 3, p. 6). It is specialized to the D-norm, its dual and the Euclidean norm (p. 6).
  - Transferable: when only the mean and covariance are known, bound the violation probability of a robust constraint by the one-sided Chebyshev bound with the Mahalanobis distance to the violation halfspace. The bound then depends only on the ratio of the dual norm to the Euclidean norm of Σ^{1/2}x (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. The counterpart's class is read off the dual norm (pp. 3–5), and the budget LP is recovered as a special case (p. 5). This is "read structure off the dual" in its plainest form.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant. The violation guarantee becomes distribution-free given the mean and covariance (Theorem 3, p. 6). It is weaker than [7]'s, but it holds under dependence (pp. 6–7). There is no price curve and no bad case, because there are no computations.
- New-pattern candidate: unify, then compare, through a dual object: norm to dual norm, with proximity bounds (pp. 2–6).
- New-pattern candidate: lemma reuse across programmes. An optimal inequality from the CP programme (Bertsimas–Popescu) is the probability engine of an RO result (p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: staged companion papers built from one model (S001 → S004 → S019).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Put rival uncertainty sets on a common footing as norm balls, and read each counterpart's class off its dual norm (pp. 3–5).
- Compute an unfamiliar dual norm by writing the norm as an LP and dualizing twice (p. 4).
- Bound how far a new norm is from the Euclidean norm, using extreme points for the maximum and KKT for the minimum. Then carry the constants into the probability guarantees (pp. 5–6).
- Distribution-free violation bound from the mean and covariance: the one-sided Chebyshev inequality with the Mahalanobis distance to the violation set (p. 6).
- When a new bound is weaker than an earlier one under stronger assumptions, close the paper by stating that trade-off explicitly (pp. 6–7).
