### S669 · Catastrophe Insurance Pricing: An Adaptive Robust Optimization Approach (Variance 2026, DOI 10.66573/001c.167995; read as arXiv 2405.07068v1)
Read level: **full**. The arXiv v1 preprint, pp. 1–39, including the references (pp. 32–34) and Appendices A–C (pp. 35–39). Figures are known from their captions only. The *Variance* print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Cynthia Zeng, Operations Research Center, MIT (p. 1); a former student of his, now on the faculty of NYU Abu Dhabi (note 04 §B.12). · **Position in trajectory**: The 2019–2026 period. RO is carried into climate-driven insurance {RO AP}, using the toolkit of the 2022 book with den Hertog: the CLT set, the epigraph step and the affine decision rules are all cited to it (pp. 7, 13–14). This is the insurance half of the direction SKILL.md infers ("RO continuing in energy and insurance").

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + AP; DA through the ML-informed set}: The paper enters through an application in which an institution is failing.
  - Catastrophe insurance covers rare, large events, for which "traditional actuarial methods fall short" for lack of historical data. Climate change makes reliance on history underestimate the risk (pp. 2–3).
  - The NFIP holds more than 95% of US flood underwriting risk and ran a deficit of 19 billion dollars as of 2023, while private insurers withdraw from regions deemed "uninsurable" (p. 3).
  - The problem: set premiums p_{i,t} for N locations over T years to minimize the total premium, damped by demand. The premiums must cover losses plus a buffer δ and change slowly from year to year (eqs. (1)–(4), pp. 6–7), and the future losses are uncertain (p. 7).
  - The paper claims the first ARO approach to pricing disaster insurance (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Premiums are treated as robust decisions, and then as adaptive ones, against losses drawn from two uncertainty sets.
  - The retrospective set is a CLT set around each location's historical mean (eq. (7), p. 8).
  - The prospective set caps losses using the ML probability q_{i,k} of a major flood within k years, widened by ε (eqs. (9)–(10), p. 9).
  - The losses enter one constraint, so the inner maximization has a closed form. The robust model is the nominal one with worst-case total losses L^CLT_i = T·l̄_i + γ2·σ_i·√T and L^ML_i = Θ·min{1, q_{i,k} + ε} (Proposition and proof, pp. 11–13).
  - The ARO step makes each premium affine in the previous year's loss (eq. (27), p. 14) and derives the counterpart by LP duality (pp. 15–20).
  - Relative to CAT modelling and actuarial pricing, uncertainty becomes a set and ML forecasts become set parameters. The accounting of premiums against losses stays (pp. 4–7).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Decisions: state-level annual premiums for 52 jurisdictions (the 50 states, Puerto Rico and the US Virgin Islands), set for 2013–2022 (p. 21).
  - Loss model: the CLT set assumes that the yearly losses of one location are i.i.d., with that location's historical mean and standard deviation; there is one set per location (pp. 7–8). The ML set assumes that more than one major event in k years is negligible (p. 9).
  - Demand damping is piecewise linear and common to all states ("For the illustrative purpose of this work"), with P0 = P^max_hist/10, c_min = 0.2 and m = 1/P^max_hist (p. 22). The decline rate is estimated from several states' data, with scatter plots shown for Louisiana and New York (pp. 22, 36).
  - The ARO model is simplified twice. It drops demand damping "to allow the derivation of a robust counter part", a step justified by the later sensitivity analysis, and its constraints use only the CLT set (eq. (28), pp. 14–15). Slow variation is imposed on the rule's coefficients α and β (γ3, γ4) instead of on the premiums (p. 14).
  - Data: FEMA's OpenFEMA NFIP redacted claims (2,570,089 claim lines dated from 1970; 1975–2022 used) and redacted policies (228,664 lines, 2009–2022), retrieved 1 March 2023 (pp. 20–21, 32–33). DC, Guam, American Samoa and the Northern Mariana Islands are dropped for thin records (p. 21).
  - Splits: the optimization parameters are estimated on 1975–2012 "to avoid data spoilage" (p. 21). Appendix B.2 gives 1975–2011 for training the ML models and 2012–2022 for testing them (p. 37).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - **Closed-form inner problems.** The inner maximization splits into a CLT subproblem and an ML subproblem, and the larger of the two is taken (pp. 10–11).
    - In the CLT subproblem, the absolute value is dropped because the adversary maximizes, and the sum constraint binds (pp. 11–12).
    - In the ML subproblem, the binaries z are relaxed to [0, 1] "Without loss of generality", because their sum is treated as one variable in [0, 1] (p. 12).
  - **Decomposition by location**: the constraints of different locations do not interact (p. 14).
  - **ARO**:
    - Premiums are affine in last year's loss, following Chapter 7 of the 2022 book (pp. 13–14). The epigraph step of its Chapter 2 moves the uncertain objective into a constraint (pp. 14–15).
    - The inner LP of each uncertain constraint, over the polyhedral CLT set, is dualized by strong duality. The inner minimum is then dropped because any feasible dual point suffices (pp. 15–19).
    - The result, (52)–(56), is a linear program in α, β, Ω and the dual variables (pp. 19–20). The class is the card's reading; the paper does not name it.
  - **The convexity claim is asserted, not shown.**
    - The paper chooses piecewise-linear damping "To preserve the convexity property" (p. 13), calls the nominal model an LP (pp. 6, 29) and calls the solved problems convex (pp. 26, 39).
    - Card's check: with (57), f(p)·p = p − m·p·(p − P0) on the damping segment. This is a concave quadratic, so minimizing objective (1) is not a convex problem there.
    - The models are solved with Ipopt and Gurobi; the paper does not say which model went to which solver (p. 39).
  - **No probability statement** links γ2 or ε to a coverage level. γ2 is described as how close the losses stay to the normal distribution (p. 8), and a larger ε means less confidence in the ML forecasts (p. 9).
  - **Slips in the formulas of this version**:
    - √n in (6) but √T in (7) (p. 8);
    - (21) carries an extra factor in (T1 − T0) that (16) and (20) lack (p. 12);
    - f maps to {0, 1} although it is a fraction of demand (pp. 6, 13);
    - "except for constraint 3", where the coverage constraint (2) is meant (p. 7);
    - the c_j of the dual programs are never defined (pp. 16–19).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - **Design.** Premiums for 2013–2022 are tested out of sample, at state level, against two baselines (pp. 4, 23–25):
    - Hist, the NFIP's actual historical premiums;
    - CMA, the cumulative moving average of past losses (eq. (58)).
  - Three models are tested: RO1 (CLT set), RO2 (CLT and ML sets) and ARO (Table 2, p. 25).
  - **ML risk models**:
    - Logistic regression and XGBoost, tuned by 3-fold cross-validation over small grids (Table 6, p. 37).
    - Features: the state (one-hot) and the current and past 1–5 years' losses (p. 37).
    - Targets: a state-year loss above the 90th, 95th or 99th percentile within 3, 5 or 10 years (pp. 22–23).
    - Out-of-sample AUC ranges from 0.743 to 0.992 (Table 1, p. 24). Higher accuracy at longer horizons is put down to more balanced data and fewer test samples (pp. 23, 38).
  - **Metrics.** Effectiveness is the number of insolvent states, that is, states whose cumulative premium falls below their cumulative loss. Efficiency is the total surplus or deficit and the absolute deviation from actual losses (pp. 24, 28).
  - **Knobs.** γ1 = 50,000 and δ = 10,000 are fixed (p. 24). γ2 is swept over 0–1.5 (Figure 2, p. 26) and over 0–2 (Tables 3–4, pp. 28, 30), with no damping and with two damping rates (p. 26).
  - **Results**:
    - Hist leaves all 52 states insolvent; CMA leaves 36. The robust models fall from 32–36 insolvent states at γ2 = 0 to 11–12 at γ2 = 2 (Table 3, p. 28).
    - The surplus of the Figure 2 model breaks even for γ2 between 0.6 and 0.7 (p. 26). In Table 4, RO1 and RO2 cross zero between 0.6 and 0.8, and ARO between 0.8 and 1.0 (p. 30).
    - At γ2 = 2, ARO reaches 12 insolvent states with a surplus of 3.82e9, against 12 states and 18.41e9 for RO1 (Tables 3–4, pp. 28, 30). This is the basis of the abstract's "least insurance premium" claim (p. 1).
    - RO2 is more conservative than RO1, "albeit not significantly" (p. 28). Damping matters less than γ2 (p. 26).
  - **Not reported**:
    - which threshold Θ, horizon k, ε and classifier feed RO2;
    - the values of γ3 and γ4;
    - how the affine rule is applied to the realized test losses;
    - any solve time.
    - The code is said to be public "at [repository link]", a placeholder (p. 39). The machine was a MacBook Pro with 4 Intel CPUs, running Julia 1.7 and Python 3.9 (p. 39).
  - **Selection on the test window.** γ2 is swept on 2013–2022 itself, and the recommended "conservative parameter values" are read off those results. No validation split for γ2 is described (pp. 26–30).
  - **Not remarked on in the text**:
    - Hist has the lowest absolute deviation in Table 4: 1.98e10, against 2.25e10–4.17e10 for the robust models (p. 30).
    - The text gives CMA's loss as "8 million" (pp. 25, 28), where Table 4 shows −8.31e9 (p. 30).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper joins three literatures (pp. 4–5).
  - **CAT modelling.** The proprietary models (AIR Worldwide, RMS, EQECAT) and FEMA's open HAZUS. Its difficulties are scarce data for rare events and many "what-if" scenarios that are hard to combine (p. 4).
  - **Disaster management.** Budget allocation, robust relief logistics, and two stochastic-optimization studies of flood-insurance design by Ermolieva and co-authors (p. 5).
  - **Climate finance** (p. 5).
  - Prior work is restated, not criticized. The paper's own study "fills in the gap" with a robust framework fed by ML forecasts (p. 5).
  - Robust optimization is cited to two of his own works, [5] and the 2022 book [6] (p. 5). Reference [5] reads "Robust Optimization. Princeton University Press, 2011" under Bertsimas, Brown and Caramanis (p. 32). The publication list (works.json) records their 2011 work as the *SIAM Review* survey *Theory and applications of robust optimization* (S003), so [5] is garbled in this version.

  - The contribution is stated three times in nearly the same words: in the abstract, the contribution list and the conclusion (pp. 1, 3–4, 29–31). The policy advice comes early: the contribution list already recommends ARO "with conservative parameter values" (p. 4), and the conclusion repeats it almost word for word (p. 30).

  - Tables are summarized favourably. The unfavourable cells, Hist's absolute deviation and RO2 being close to RO1, get little or no comment (pp. 28–30).
  - Many slips mark this as a draft:

- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a modelling framework (two uncertainty sets, and an ARO extension with an LP counterpart) and an application study on NFIP data (pp. 3–4, 29–31).
  - Limits are hardly written. There is no limitations paragraph and no future-work list. - Heuristics and tensions (not counted):
  - Tension T6, evidence. The abstract features ML-predicted emerging risks and the ARO model's "least insurance premium" (p. 1), but the recommended ARO model has no ML set (pp. 14–15). "8 million" stands against −8.31e9, and Hist's absolute deviation goes unremarked (pp. 25, 28, 30).

- New-pattern candidate: **ML forecasts as uncertainty-set parameters**. A classifier's probability of a major event, widened by a confidence radius ε, defines a prospective set beside a retrospective set fitted to history (pp. 8–9, 22–23). Compare S307's "robustness layer matched to the data behind each uncertainty source" (card c30).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) When the uncertain data enter a single constraint through their sum, solve the inner problem in closed form: drop the absolute value, let the sum constraint bind, and substitute the worst-case total into the nominal model (pp. 10–13).
- (D4) For an adaptive pricing rule, make the price affine in the last period's realized loss. Move the smoothness constraints onto the rule's coefficients, and dualize the inner LP of each uncertain constraint separately (pp. 14–20).
- (D3) Turn a classifier's probability q of a threshold event within k years into the set {|Σz − q| ≤ ε, Σz ≤ 1, Σl ≤ Θ·Σz}. Its worst case is Θ·min{1, q + ε} (pp. 8–9, 12–13).
- (D5) Score a pricing policy against both the owner's actual historical prices and a naive moving-average rule. Use a failure count (insolvent units) and a cost measure (surplus, absolute deviation), and plot failures against cost as the knob varies (pp. 23–29).
- (D5) Set the knob on a validation window that is not the window reporting the results (the card's lesson from pp. 26–30, where γ2 is swept on the test years).

### S672 · Robust Optimization for Green Ammonia Production (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2606.08335)
Read level: **full**. arXiv v1, pp. 1–29, including the references (pp. 25–26) and Appendices A–B (pp. 27–29). Figures are known from their captions only.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Karl Zhu (Operations Research Center, MIT), Yassine Bohafid and Omar Kadir (OCP Group, Casablanca) (p. 1). Zhu is named among his students in a 2025 homepage post (note 04). · **Position in trajectory**: The 2019–2026 period: the energy half of "RO continuing in energy and insurance" {RO IO AP}.
- It continues the OCP partnership (notes 03 and 06; card c30), and it revisits S307's device. The "Bertsimas et al. (2023)" it cites is S307's arXiv version, arXiv:2209.06341 (pp. 3, 25).
- It interprets its failure mode through the student's own methods preprint, Zhu and Bertsimas (2025), *Overfitting in Adaptive Robust Optimization* (S623, not carded) (pp. 24, 26).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + IO; AP: energy, partner OCP}: The paper enters through a partner's investment.
  - OCP Group plans a US$7 billion green-ammonia facility producing one million metric tons a year from solar and wind over 25 years (p. 1).
  - The stated central challenge is meeting the minimum-load requirements of the Haber–Bosch (HB) process under renewable intermittency and uncertainty, where shutdowns and restarts are costly (pp. 1–2).
  - Two decisions are posed: capacities that stay feasible through long periods of low renewable output, and hourly flows under forecast error (p. 2).
  - The gap is located in the literature (p. 3). The closest model plans with perfect foresight over one historical year, handles uncertainty only by inflating the HB minimum load, and omits hot-idling and shutdowns. Clustering-based scenario reduction smooths away prolonged low-generation events.
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - **Strategic model**: a MILO that minimizes the levelized cost of ammonia (LCOA), with binaries for HB hot-idling and shutdowns. It is strengthened by an LO warm start and bilinear cuts (pp. 2, 7–9).
  - **Scenario reduction** (pp. 9–15):
    - Days are clustered by k-means, and representative years are chosen by a MILO that matches the cluster distribution in Wasserstein distance.
    - Inside each cluster, a robust problem is solved, and its worst-case perturbation becomes a synthetic adversarial trajectory that is imposed hour by hour.
    - This sits between constraint-wise robustness, which is too conservative, and S307's constraint aggregation, which is too weak for hourly feasibility (pp. 12–14).
  - **Operational model**: a rolling-horizon model with stage-wise affine decision rules over an ellipsoid-box set of forecast errors. It is compared with nominal and static robust policies (pp. 15–19).
  - **Against S307**: the aggregated robust constraint is replaced by hourly constraints on a worst-case trajectory. The CLT-inspired set and the k-means clusters stay (pp. 3, 11–14).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - **Data**: 25 years of hourly solar and wind capacity factors from satellite and reanalysis sources (PVGIS; ECMWF Reanalysis v5) (pp. 2, 25). They are split into 15 years for planning and 10 for out-of-sample evaluation, with leap days dropped (p. 4).
  - **Plant model** (pp. 4–7): seven capacity components (the air-separation unit is assumed large enough), hourly electricity and mass flows, storage dynamics and degradation. The parameters are in Table 6 (p. 27). LCOA is linear because OCP's contracts fix production at 1 MT a year (p. 5).
  - **Binaries** for hot-idling, shutdown and restart, with at most N_shutdowns shutdowns a year and an L-hour transition (pp. 7–8). The experiments set these to 10 shutdowns and 24 hours (p. 20).
  - **Uncertainty sets**:
    - Strategic: the CLT-inspired polyhedral set of S307, with hourly bounds ρσ, a CLT aggregate bound Γσ√|D_c| and a smoothness bound Δσ_Δ (eqs. (6a)–(6d), p. 11). Its one-sided version is read as the Bertsimas–Sim budget set (pp. 11–12).
    - Operational: an ellipsoid-box set on forecast errors, with covariance Σ and radius Ω (eq. (18), p. 18).
  - **Assumptions the paper admits**:
    - Perfect foresight brings look-ahead bias, though it is "standard in the green ammonia literature" (p. 9).
    - Non-consecutive representative years break the storage links between years (p. 11).
    - The distribution-free guarantee assumes errors independent across time and source, which is reasonable if the forecast already captures the correlations (p. 28).
  - **Forecasts in the experiments** are the realized values plus Gaussian noise whose standard deviation grows with √t (p. 23).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - **Warm start from the nested model.** Fixing all binaries at zero recovers the LO, so the LO solution is feasible for the MILO and gives an upper bound at once (p. 9).
  - **Bilinear cuts** (2v′)–(2x′) are logically equivalent to the indicator constraints and tighten the LO relaxation. But they turn the model into a mixed-integer quadratically constrained problem (p. 9).
  - **Representative years**: a MILO minimizes the 1-Wasserstein distance between the cluster distribution of the n chosen years and that of all years (eqs. (5a)–(5f), pp. 10–11).
  - **Separation.** Capacities are nonnegative and fixed before operations, so the adversary's problem in the aggregated constraint does not depend on them (eq. (10), p. 13).
    - Proposition 3.1 gives the counterpart with the minimizer û. It also gives a conservative closed-form approximation built on the CLT bound, which is exact when that bound binds at every hour (p. 13).
  - **Proposition 3.2**: under the budget set, F_full ⊆ F_one ⊆ F_agg, so the one-scenario formulation lies between the other two. The proof uses the sign û ≤ 0, and the paper notes that the chain need not hold for the CLT set (p. 14).
  - **Diversifying degenerate worst cases**: fix the optimal value ζ*, sample uniformly within the bounds, and project each sample onto the optimal face by a quadratic program (pp. 14–15).
  - **Stage-wise affine rules** cut the number of rule coefficients from O(T²) to O(T). Grid import is left as implicit recourse: the minimum import that restores the energy balance (p. 17).
  - **Set choice by class** (p. 18):
    - With constraint-wise separable sets, static and adaptive robust values coincide (Marandi–den Hertog), so a set coupled across time is needed.
    - Budget sets keep linearity and suit mixed-integer models; ellipsoids give SOCPs with stronger guarantees. The ellipsoid is used because the operational model is continuous; with binaries it would lead to mixed-integer SOCPs.
  - **Proposition 4.1** gives the AARC by dualizing the box and taking the ellipsoid's support function. The result is a second-order-cone constraint with α, β ≥ 0 (pp. 18–19; proof in B.2, p. 29).
  - **Guarantees for calibrating Ω** (pp. 27–29):
    - the Gaussian quantile (Lemma B.1);
    - a truncated-Gaussian extension, proved by conditioning (Proposition B.2);
    - a distribution-free sub-Gaussian bound, Ω = √(2 ln(1/ε)), extended to bounded support (Lemma B.3, Proposition B.4).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - **Solver and machine**: Gurobi 11.0.0 on MIT SuperCloud, 32 threads, 192 GB RAM, Intel Xeon Platinum 8260; a 0.01% gap tolerance and a 72-hour limit (p. 20).
  - **Study 1** (a one-year perfect-foresight instance, CAPEX only, pp. 19–22):
    - A cold start found no feasible solution in 10.6 hours and kept a 21.1% gap after 72 hours, so warm starts were used from then on, starting from a baseline CAPEX of $7.212B (p. 20).
    - Hot-idling was never selected: the warm start was proved optimal (p. 20).
    - For the hot-idle model, three cuts reduced the root gap from 21.2% to 0.01% and proved optimality at the root; without cuts the model converged in 6.8 hours (pp. 20–21).
    - For the shutdown and full models, the cuts tightened the root but slowed the search. After 72 hours, the no-cut variants had the smaller gaps: 2.56% and 2.28%, against 3.95% and 5.22% (Table 2, p. 21).
    - Shutdowns gave a 0.73% CAPEX reduction ($7.159B, still at a 3.95% gap) (pp. 20–21).
    - The sensitivity analysis moves parameters only in directions favourable to hot-idling. Only a higher minimum load helps: −0.27% at 30% and −3.25% at 60% (Table 3, pp. 21–22).
  - **Study 2** (perfect forecasts, no hot-idle or shutdown features, n = 5 representative years, pp. 22–23):
    - Capacities are computed for perfect foresight, CLT, budget and aggregation (Table 4, p. 22). The aggregation plan has no solar and relies almost entirely on wind. LCOA values are withheld for confidentiality (p. 22).
    - A 10-year out-of-sample rolling-horizon simulation follows, with a 72-hour prediction and a 6-hour control horizon. The perfect-foresight, CLT and budget plans stay feasible, at 2876, 3052 and 3032 t/day. The aggregation plan violates the HB minimum load "even under perfect forecasts" (p. 23).
  - **Study 3** (CLT capacities, one unseen year, 12-hour prediction and 6-hour control horizons, pp. 23–24):
    - Nominal, static robust and adaptive robust policies are run at Ω = 0, 1, 2 and 3 (Table 5, p. 23).
    - The nominal policy produces the most but draws 59.3 MW from the grid on average. Static Ω = 1 cuts grid use by 84% for 7.3% less ammonia (p. 24).
    - The adaptive policy produces more than the static one at every Ω. But it fails (an infeasibility that grid import cannot repair) in 41.7% of intervals at Ω = 1, falling to 0.3% at Ω = 3 (pp. 23–24).
  - **Card's reading of Table 5**:
    - Production and grid figures exclude failed intervals (p. 23). Adaptive production at Ω = 1 is therefore averaged over the 58.3% of intervals that did not fail.

- **D6 Positioning**: Two literatures (p. 3).
  - **Green-ammonia optimization.**
    - Salmon and Bañares-Alcántara (2023) is the closest model, and it is criticized concretely: perfect foresight over one year, uncertainty handled through an inflated minimum load, and no hot-idling or shutdowns.
    - Wang (2025) is a two-stage stochastic MIO with shutdowns; Yu et al. (2023) is a robust mixed-integer linear-fractional model with box sets. Both rely on few scenarios or simple sets and do not use multi-decade data.
  - **Scenario reduction by clustering**: centroids suppress low-generation events (Teichgraeber and Brandt 2019, p. 3).
  - **The group's own prior device is scoped, not attacked.** Constraint aggregation is introduced "While effective in that setting" and then called "less suitable" here (p. 3). The experiments go on to show it failing (p. 23).
  - **The RO toolkit** is cited to his own and his co-authors' papers: Bertsimas–Sim (2004), Bertsimas–den Hertog–Pauphilet (2021), Bertsimas–Na–Stellato–Wang (2024), Marandi–den Hertog (2018), plus Ben-Tal et al. (2004) (pp. 11–12, 17–18, 25).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract opens with the central challenge in one sentence. It closes with a trade-off that includes the negative half: adaptive policies "provide weaker protection against realizations outside the uncertainty set" (p. 1). Contribution 3 repeats that caveat (p. 4).
  - Findings are hedged to what the gaps allow. Shutdown flexibility helps, "although its magnitude cannot be precisely quantified" (p. 21), and hot-idling gives "no measurable benefit" (p. 20).
  - The failure mode is given a name, "brittle failures", and is read as overfitting, an interpretation taken from the group's own preprint (p. 24).
  - Limitations get their own subsection (§2.7, p. 9).
  - Each design choice gets a one-sentence reason: k = 20 (p. 10), stage-wise rules (p. 17), ellipsoid against budget set (p. 18).
  - The conclusion restates the results and makes no new claims (p. 24).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - **Three contributions** (pp. 3–4): the MILO with flexible HB operation and its strengthening; the robust scenario-reduction framework; and the adaptive rolling-horizon model, compared with nominal and static policies.
  - **Limits are concrete and mostly printed**:
    - perfect-foresight bias (p. 9);
    - hot-idling is worthless at the base parameters (p. 20);
    - the cuts hurt the harder models (pp. 20–21);
    - the size of the shutdown benefit is uncertain (p. 21);
    - the hierarchy of Proposition 3.2 holds only for the budget set (p. 14);
    - adaptive policies fail brittly, and since the rolling horizon already adapts, the reported gains are only the increment of affine recourse beyond model predictive control (p. 24).
  - **Not discussed**: the LCOA cost of robustness (withheld, p. 22); a combined run of the MILO with scenario reduction; field use by OCP. No future-work list is given (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 9, 20–21). Warm start, exact solve and an honest gap.
  - The warm start is the nested LO (all binaries at zero), which gives a valid upper bound.
  - Solver version, machine, threads, gap tolerance and time limit are printed. Gaps are reported at the root, at 24 hours and at 72 hours, and the cold-start failure is printed.
  - The claim is narrowed to what the gap allows (p. 21).
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 22–24).
  - The duel is not against an incumbent heuristic. It is against two model choices: perfect foresight (the literature's standard) and constraint aggregation (the group's own S307 device). Both are judged out of sample by feasibility and production.
  - No certificate is possible for the operational policies, and the scenario-reduced models' solve times are absent.
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 5, 13, 18–19). Devices that keep the class are chosen deliberately:
  - the owner's contract fixes production, which makes LCOA linear (p. 5);
  - the aggregated adversary separates from the capacities (p. 13);
  - budget sets are kept for mixed-integer models, and ellipsoids are used only in the continuous operational model, to avoid MISOCPs (p. 18);
  - the AARC is a second-order-cone constraint (p. 19).
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 9, 20–21). The bilinear cuts take the MILO into a mixed-integer quadratically constrained class, and the measured cost is printed: tighter roots, but larger final gaps on the shutdown and full models (Table 2, p. 21). The class change is measured as the stop rule asks, but the device is not changed; the paper reports the cost rather than resolving it.
- Method 3: evidence ✅ (pp. 23–24, 27–29).
  - The knob Ω comes with solution-independent probability bounds for calibrating it (Appendix B.1).
  - Its curve (production, grid use and failure rate at Ω = 0–3) is printed with its bad case, 41.7% brittle failures at Ω = 1.
  - A recommendation follows: the static policy is the most reliable (p. 24).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 15, 22). The strategic knobs (ρ, Γ, Δ) are not reported for the capacity results, and the cost of robustness (LCOA) is withheld. No cost curve reaches the reader.
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 5, 22–24). The decision is the owner's (OCP's capacity plan); the owner's contract terms enter the model (p. 5); the owner's engineers are co-authors (p. 1). Decisions are judged by out-of-sample feasibility, production and grid use, not by forecast accuracy.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 22). The baselines are the literature's perfect-foresight model and the group's own earlier device, not OCP's current plan. Costs are withheld, and there is no field test.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 24–25). One partner, a programme of papers: S307, with Cory-Wright and Digalakis, then this paper with a new student. The student's own methods preprint (Zhu and Bertsimas 2025) supplies the interpretation of the failure mode.
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted):
  - **Heuristic 2, self-applied.** The c30 card noted that S307's robust plan holds energy balance only in aggregate, and that S307 does not discuss hourly feasibility (card c30, S307 pp. 22–23). This paper tests exactly that point on the same partner's next problem and shows that the aggregation plan cannot be operated (pp. 3, 12–13, 22–23). It is an answer by a new paper with a new student, with no external critic named.
  - **Heuristic 4, evidence.** Negative results appear in the body and in the abstract: hot-idling gives no benefit, the cuts hurt, adaptive policies are brittle, and aggregation fails (pp. 1, 20–24).
  - **Tension T4, evidence**, with both sides measured in one table. The cuts strengthen the relaxation (root gaps fall from about 21% to 0.01–5%), yet on the harder models they lose to the weaker formulation in gap after 72 hours (Table 2, p. 21).
  - **Tension T1.** There is no overclaim of *optimal*: the best incumbent keeps its 3.95% gap in the text (p. 21).
  - **Tension T6**, mostly resolved toward the caveat: the abstract and the contributions carry the downside (pp. 1, 4). One residue is Table 5's averaging over successful intervals only (card's reading, p. 23).
- New-pattern candidate: **the worst case of a robust counterpart as an adversarial scenario**. When the adversary separates from the decisions, solve once for the worst-case perturbation and impose it as a synthetic scenario in every original constraint. This lies between constraint-wise and aggregated robustness, with a feasible-set hierarchy proved where the set is one-sided (pp. 13–14).
- New-pattern candidate: **self-revision within a partner programme**. Re-test the group's own earlier device out of sample on the partner's next problem, limit the earlier claim to its setting, and replace the device (pp. 3, 12–14, 23).
- New-pattern candidate: **diversifying a degenerate worst case**: fix the optimal value, sample, and project (pp. 14–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) When a MILO adds optional operating modes to an LP, warm-start it with the LP solution (binaries at zero). That solution is feasible by construction and gives an upper bound before any branching (p. 9).
- (D4) Try the bilinear form of indicator constraints, but measure it: report root and late gaps for each family of cuts, because a stronger relaxation can slow the search (pp. 9, 20–21).
- (D4) Choose representative periods with a small MILO that minimizes the Wasserstein distance between the cluster histogram of the selected periods and that of the full data (pp. 10–11).
- (D4) If the robust adversary does not depend on the first-stage decisions (nonnegative capacities multiplying the uncertain factors), compute its worst case once and use it as a scenario in every hourly constraint. Where the set is one-sided, prove the inclusion F_full ⊆ F_one ⊆ F_agg (pp. 13–14).
- (D4) To get diverse worst-case scenarios from a degenerate LP, fix the optimal value and project random samples from the box onto that optimal face with a quadratic objective (pp. 14–15).
- (D4) Before adopting adaptive rules, check whether the uncertainty set is constraint-wise separable. If it is, adaptivity adds nothing (Marandi–den Hertog), so pick a set coupled across time (p. 18).
- (D5) Report a robustness knob's curve with three columns, for static and adaptive policies side by side: the objective, the recourse used (grid import) and the rate of unrecoverable failures (Table 5, pp. 23–24). State how failed intervals are treated in the averages (p. 23).
- (D5) Evaluate capacity plans by simulating operations on held-out years with a rolling-horizon model, so that hourly infeasibility shows up even under perfect forecasts (pp. 22–23).
