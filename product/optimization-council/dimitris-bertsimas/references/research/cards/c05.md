# Paper cards · batch c05 (core)

Batch intent: See how Bertsimas worked in 1998–2000, just before the robust-optimization era, across four domains at once: a continuous-LP algorithm for fluid scheduling of queueing networks (LO+SD, S156), an applied MIP deployment at an investment firm (IO+AP, S101), dependent randomized rounding of LP relaxations (IO, S121) and a survey of moment problems solved by convex and semidefinite optimization (CP+SD+AP+IO, S007). Three questions: (a) how he enters a problem owned by another tradition (optimal control, portfolio practice, approximation algorithms, probability and finance) and which device puts it in a solvable class (Method 1); (b) whether results carry bounds, certificates or gaps, and how solver limits are reported (Method 4, Tension T1); (c) whether this pre-2004 record already shows the later *one knob, priced* and *judge against the owner's rule* moves (Methods 3 and 5).
Focus dimensions: D1–D8 (all; core batch), read with team.json `card_dimensions`: D1 also names the domain codes; D3 = model and data; D4 = formulation and analysis; D5 = computational and empirical evaluation. Extra weight on D4 (the reformulation device) and D5 (what is measured and what is not).

### S156 · A new algorithm for state-constrained separated continuous linear programs (SIAM Journal on Control and Optimization 1998, DOI 10.1137/s0363012995292664)
**Read level**: full — §§1–9 with all proofs (pp. 1–32) read; the reference list (pp. 32–34) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Xiaodong Luo (first author; the problem class comes from his 1995 MIT ORC PhD thesis, ref. [32], pp. 2, 33) · **Position in trajectory**: the stochastic-networks period, at its deterministic end: a fluid (continuous-time LP) model for scheduling job shops and multiclass queueing networks. Two years later S007 cites it as the policy-generating half of a methodology that pairs fluid-control policies with SDP lower bounds (S007 p. 18). Received 4 October 1995, accepted in revised form February 1998; partly funded by his NSF Presidential Young Investigator award with matching funds from Draper Laboratory (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry (LO+SD)**: The entry is a gap between solver classes: "significant progress" in large-scale finite-dimensional and semi-infinite LP against "little progress" for LP in infinite-dimensional spaces, although such LPs model manufacturing and communication systems (abstract, p. 1). The authors take Anderson's separated continuous LP (SCLP, introduced to model job-shop scheduling, p. 2) and enlarge it to the state-constrained class SCSCLP: minimize ∫(c'u + g'y) dt subject to ∫₀ᵗ Gu ds + Ey(t) = a(t), Hu ≤ b(t), Fy ≤ h(t), u ≥ 0, with applications in communications, manufacturing and urban traffic control (p. 2). The problem is stated as: an algorithm for SCSCLP under Assumption 1, plus duality and solution-structure results as byproducts (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: enlarge a well-studied class by the constraint type the applications need (here state constraints Fy ≤ h and a state cost g'y) and show that the old class is a subclass, so earlier results become special cases (p. 2, p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: For a fixed partition P the discretized problem DP(P) is an LP, so the difficulty is the unknown partition (p. 8). Pullan's and Philpott–Craddock's algorithms alternate "improve the control" and "improve the partition"; this one "improves both the control and partition at the same time" (p. 8). The device is the substitution v̂(tᵢ) = (tᵢ − tᵢ₋₁)û(tᵢ₋₁+), which makes the breakpoints tᵢ decision variables and turns DP(P) into a quadratic program with polyhedral constraints, QP(|P|), generally nonconvex (pp. 8–9). Only KKT points are needed, found by Frank–Wolfe or matrix-splitting methods (pp. 3, 10). Algorithm A (pp. 22–23): (1) a KKT point of QP(|Pₖ|); (2) PURIFY: merge adjacent intervals whenever inequality (11) shows the merge lowers the cost (Lemma 3, p. 11; p. 12); (3) double the breakpoints; (4) compute the duality gap from the LP AP(P) and stop when it vanishes (or falls below β); (5) move to a strictly improving solution. Kept from Pullan: discretization and a dual-related second discretization ("a close variation", p. 12). Changed: simultaneous improvement of control and partition; removal of redundant intervals; a descent construction with about twice rather than three times as many control pieces (pp. 17–18).
  - Transferable technique: when a problem is an LP once a discrete structure is fixed, turn that structure into continuous variables by a change of variables, accept the nonconvex QP that results, and ask only for KKT points plus a separate global-optimality test (pp. 8–10, 22).
- **D3 Model & data**: A deterministic continuous-time LP with bounded measurable u, y, η (p. 4). Assumption 1: a(t), h(t) continuous; a, c, h piecewise linear; b, g piecewise constant; the problem feasible and bounded below (p. 4). No boundedness of the solution set is assumed (abstract, p. 1). The authors name the core difficulty: u(t) may be unbounded and an optimal solution may not exist, which "perhaps is the key difficulty" for conventional duality proofs (p. 10). Bounded u is needed for Lemma 1 (p. 9), and the structure theorem needs a bounded solution set and E = I (Theorem 6, p. 29). Test data: a reentrant line (Kumar) with n stations and 5 classes per station, arrival rate 1 into class 1 only, and randomly generated mean service times, holding costs and initial queues (p. 31).
- **D4 Formulation & analysis**:
  - Weak duality by integration by parts; requiring π(t) to be of finite variation is what makes it valid (Proposition 1, pp. 4–5). Complementary slackness characterizes strong duality (Corollary 1, p. 5).
  - Zero-length intervals with v̂ ≠ 0 encode Dirac impulses in u. A τ-splitting construction gives sequences of proper solutions whose values converge, so V(SCSCLP) ≤ V(QP(|P|)) for every P (Lemmas 1–2, pp. 9–10).
  - A sandwich of discretizations: V(AP(P)) = V(AP*(P)) ≤ V(SCSCLP*) ≤ V(SCSCLP) ≤ V(DP(Q)) for all partitions P, Q (Theorem 2, p. 17). It rests on mapping solutions of the dual discretization to piecewise-linear dual functions (Theorem 1, pp. 15–17). A QP solution whose value meets the AP(P) value is optimal (Corollary 3, p. 17).
  - Descent by "doubling of breakpoints": an ε-patch of the current solution with an AP(P) solution changes the cost by δ + o(ε) with δ < 0 (Theorem 3, pp. 18–22); the first Frank–Wolfe iterate bounds the current duality gap (Theorem 4, p. 22).
  - Finite convergence (Theorem 5, p. 29): positive-length intervals map injectively to generalized extreme points of finitely many linear systems (resolution theorem; Lemmas 5–7, pp. 24–29; Corollary 4, p. 29), and the KKT points of each QP take finitely many objective values (Luo–Tseng; Lemma 8, p. 29). No rate or complexity bound is given.
  - Byproducts: no duality gap and a piecewise-linear optimal dual (Theorem 7, pp. 29–30); a piecewise-constant optimal control when the solution set is bounded and E = I (Theorem 6, p. 29).
  - Transferable technique: prove strong duality "algorithmically": design a finitely terminating primal algorithm whose stopping test is a primal–dual equality, and read the duality theorem off its termination (abstract, p. 1; pp. 23–24, 29).
- **D5 Computational & empirical evaluation**: C code on a Sparc 10/41 that calls the academic LOQO 1.08 for the LP and QP subproblems; four modules, one of which builds a dual feasible solution from the primal partition as a lower bound (pp. 30–31). One instance family, the reentrant line: G the incidence matrix of a line digraph, H block-diagonal with mean service times, F = −I, c = 0, b = 1, h = 0, g random (p. 31). Table 9.1 lists, per iteration, the objective, the number of control pieces, the dual objective and the time: after 3 iterations the primal value is 5963.6674 against a dual value of 5963.2700, with 29 pieces and 2,436.61 s (p. 32); that is a relative gap of about 7×10⁻⁵ (my arithmetic). The text does not say which instance size Table 9.1 uses. Fig. 5 plots time against 5–25 stations at precision 10⁻⁴, labelled "close to quadratic"; 25 stations give 250 continuous variables (pp. 31–32). Limit reported: time grows "almost exponentially with the accuracy requirement" (p. 32). No baseline is run: neither Pullan's nor Philpott–Craddock's algorithm.
  - Transferable technique: print the dual bound next to the primal value in every iteration row, so each row is its own certificate (Table 9.1, p. 32).
- **D6 Positioning**: The lineage is Bellman's continuous LP (p. 1) and Anderson's SCLP (p. 2), followed by a survey: Lehman, Drews, Hartberger, Segers; the simplex-like algorithms of Perold and Anstreicher, "still incomplete"; Ilyutovich via Pontryagin's maximum principle; the interior-point method of Ito–Kelley–Sachs; Anderson–Nash; Pullan's series, credited as "the state of the art"; Philpott–Craddock (p. 2). Criticism is by restatement of requirements, not by attack: Pullan needs the maximal interval length to tend to zero and his descent triples the pieces; Anderson–Nash need all extreme points (pp. 18, 24). The new dual reduces to Pullan's alternative dual for SCLP (p. 4). Own prior work cited: Avram–Bertsimas–Ricard on fluid control of multiclass queueing networks (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract opens with a field contrast (progress vs. little progress) and then lists six claims (p. 1). The introduction has a contribution paragraph, a section roadmap and a reading instruction: "first read sections 2 and 6" (pp. 2–3). An informal argument precedes the convergence proof (p. 23). The paper is theorem-dense; Figs. 1–3 illustrate solution structure and constructions, and Fig. 5 is the only data plot (pp. 7, 19, 26, 32). Headline and caveat sit apart: the abstract calls the algorithm "efficient" and "effective in solving large-scale realistic problems (with several hundred continuous variables)" (p. 1), while the body reports growth almost exponential in the accuracy and a largest instance of 250 continuous variables (pp. 31–32), an early instance of Tension T6. The closing sentence hedges with "We believe" (p. 32).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution at the level of theory and algorithm: a new problem class, a finitely convergent algorithm, no duality gap without boundedness assumptions, the structure of optimal solutions, and a computational illustration (pp. 2–3, 29–32). Limits stated: exact solution is much harder than approximation (time almost exponential in accuracy, p. 32); Assumption 1 restricts the data to piecewise-linear or piecewise-constant functions (p. 4); the structure theorem needs a bounded solution set and E = I (p. 29). Limits not stated [card-writer's reading]: no rate or complexity bound; a single random instance family; no comparison with existing algorithms. Future: exploit the special structure of the intermediate QPs (p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the owning traditions are optimal control and continuous LP (Bellman, p. 1; Pontryagin's principle in Ilyutovich, p. 2). The device rewrites the infinite-dimensional LP as a sequence of finite QPs solved with an off-the-shelf solver (LOQO) (pp. 8, 30), and the dual is read for certificates and solution structure (pp. 15–17, 29–30). It departs from Method 1's *kept in the solver class*: the reformulation leaves LP for a nonconvex QP and compensates with an LP-dual global-optimality test (pp. 8–10, 17), not with a class-keeping device. The other half of Method 1's stop rule holds: solve times are measured at the application scale (Fig. 5, p. 32).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (certificate part only, pre-MIO era): the algorithm stops on a primal–dual gap, and every reported iteration carries its dual bound (pp. 22–23, 32). There is no duel with an incumbent: no other algorithm is run.
- Method 6 ✅ weak evidence (step 3, thesis lineage): the paper is the journal version of a student's thesis problem class (p. 2; ref. [32], p. 33), funded by a PYI award with industry matching funds (p. 1).
- New-pattern candidate: *duality as the byproduct of an algorithm*: strong duality and solution structure follow from the termination of a constructive algorithm, explicitly "even when there is no optimal solution" (abstract, p. 1; p. 24).
- New-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device* (also S121, S007): the duality and piecewise-constant-structure results of the SCLP literature are recovered and extended as consequences of Algorithm A (pp. 3, 29–30).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Enlarge a studied class by the constraint type your application needs and show that the old class is a special case (p. 2, p. 4).
- [D2] Turn an unknown discretization into decision variables by a change of variables (v = Δt·u); settle for KKT points of the resulting nonconvex QP and certify global optimality with a dual discretization (pp. 8–10, 17).
- [D2] Keep the discretization lean: merge intervals whenever a closed-form test (inequality (11)) shows that the merge lowers the cost, so convergence does not depend on mesh refinement (pp. 10–12, 24).
- [D4] Represent impulses (Dirac controls) as zero-length intervals carrying mass and approximate them by τ-splitting sequences, instead of excluding them by assumption (pp. 9–10).
- [D4] Bound the number of pieces by an injective map from pieces to extreme points of finitely many linear systems (pp. 24–29).
- [D5] Report primal value, dual bound, model size (pieces) and time per iteration in one table (p. 32).

**Quotes**:
- "We prove algorithmically the absence of a duality gap for this class of problems without any boundedness assumptions on the solution set." (p. 1)
- "It is our experience that (SCSCLP) is easier to approximate than to solve exactly." (p. 32)

### S101 · Portfolio construction through mixed-integer programming at Grantham, Mayo, Van Otterloo and Company (Interfaces 1999, DOI 10.1287/inte.29.1.49)
**Read level**: full — the whole paper, including the appendix formulation and the testimonial by Jeremy Grantham (pp. 1–18). Figures 1–6 survive only as captions in the text file; the plots themselves were not available.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Christopher Darnell and Robert Soucy, both of GMO (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the finance strand {AP} that SKILL.md places in 1995–2001; an applied write-up of a deployment that began in October 1996 (p. 9), published in an Interfaces special issue (acknowledgments, p. 17). It predates his robust-optimization papers and is the only paper in this batch with a live partner and money at stake.

- **D1 Problem & entry (IO+AP)**: The entry is a practice complication that the Markowitz quadratic-programming theory "does not address": many names and many trading tickets raise custodial fees and transaction costs (p. 2). The owner's crisis made it urgent: composite portfolios of 1,000–1,500 securities against an industry norm near 200, eroding client confidence ("Several key clients were threatening to leave the firm"), strained operations and rising costs (pp. 4–5). The existing fix, a postprocessing step that drops small positions and trades, was rejected for two reasons: it removes small-capitalization stocks that GMO forecasts will outperform, and it destroys control over return, risk, allocation and cost targets, which clients demand even if they do not care about global optimality (p. 5). The problem is stated as: build a portfolio close to a target in security and sector exposure, with the same liquidity, turnover and expected return, controlled frictional costs, and fewer names and tickets, for single portfolios and for portfolios of up to 50 subportfolios (pp. 2, 4, 6, 8). MIP is chosen because the counts are integer (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: before modelling, write down why the owner's current shortcut (here, thresholding small positions) fails in the owner's own terms (strategy and accountability), so the exact model answers a stated business objection (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: One MIP replaces a two-step process (a QP target, then thresholding). The objective sums seven penalized terms: L1 distance to the target, sector-exposure deviation, a names indicator, a tickets indicator, minus expected return, a piecewise-linear convex illiquidity cost and a piecewise-linear convex transaction cost (pp. 6–8). The target itself is still built by QP or by a closed-form rule (p. 6), so risk enters only through the target. For several subportfolios the same model optimizes all of them jointly, which lets subportfolios cross trades with each other instead of trading in the market (p. 8). One model runs in two modes: tracking mode and performance-enhancing mode (pp. 10, 13).
  - Transferable technique: run one model in two modes for two owner goals by changing only the penalties (pp. 10, 13).
- **D3 Model & data**: A mixed-integer linear program: binaries y_names(i) and y_tickets(i), auxiliary variables for the absolute values, and segment variables for the piecewise-linear costs (appendix, pp. 17–18); the number of variables is typically 8–10 times the number of securities (p. 9). Data: GMO's current and target weights, forecast returns a(i) from multifactor models, sector memberships, an illiquidity index lq(i) and a volume vol(i) per stock (pp. 3, 6–8). Monthly rebalancing (p. 6). The penalties are not derived: "We choose penalties heuristically after extensive experimentation", using thousands of 20-year historical simulations and raising a penalty whenever a portfolio characteristic looks unsatisfactory (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation & analysis**: No theorems. Formulation devices (pp. 17–18): absolute values linearized by paired inequalities; the illiquidity and transaction-cost functions, called "piecewise linear and convex", modelled by bounded segment variables without binaries (pp. 7–8, 17); the link w_f(i) ≤ y_names(i) strengthened to w_f(i) ≤ aᵢ·w_max·y_names(i), with constants that depend on market capitalization, because "The resulting relaxation is stronger" (pp. 17–18). Solver engineering (p. 10): (1) strengthen the formulation to improve relaxation bounds; (2) set node selection and branching priorities from the problem structure — branch first on the largest target positions, try "keep" first for large positions and "eliminate" first for small ones; (3) tune CPLEX's stopping criterion and the allowed suboptimality.
  - Transferable technique: derive branching priorities and branch directions from the target solution you are tracking (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: tighten big-M links with problem-specific caps (maximum weight times a size-dependent constant) (pp. 17–18).
- **D5 Computational & empirical evaluation**: Implementation: about 10,000 lines of FORTRAN 90 (TRGTOPT) around the CPLEX 4.0 callable library, on a DEC AlphaServer 4100 5/466 with four CPUs used independently and 2 GB of memory (p. 9). Solve times: in October 1996 a 1,500-security single portfolio could take 15 CPU hours; after the three changes such problems take "a few minutes" (pp. 9–10). Table 1 gives average and maximum sizes (up to 6,546 rows and 51,532 columns) and 0.4–4.0 CPU minutes per problem (p. 9). The baselines are the owner's target portfolio and the benchmark index (MSCI UK for the UK example, p. 10).
  - UK historical simulation, 1977–1997, tracking mode: about 55% of the target's securities (80/146), 60% fewer tickets, turnover "virtually identical" (pp. 10–13).
  - US small-growth historical simulation, 1982–1997, performance-enhancing mode: 2.0 times the benchmark against 1.6 times for the target, 55% fewer names, 80% fewer tickets (p. 13).
  - Live: 11 portfolios worth $8.158 billion, October 1996 to January 1997, with an average of 48.7% fewer names and 79.3% fewer tickets (p. 13; Table 2, p. 16). Both averages are unweighted means of the 11 rows (my arithmetic). Table 2's market-value column is headed "$ billion", but its entries (4,308 … 88) sum to 8,158, which matches $8.158 billion, so they are in $ million (p. 16).
  - Not reported: optimality gaps, or the suboptimality tolerance actually used (p. 10).
  - [Card-writer's reading] The performance-enhancing result was obtained "by adjusting the various penalties" (p. 13) over the same history used to judge it, and the penalties were tuned by thousands of historical runs (p. 8); no hold-out period is described. The authors flag only that these results are "only in simulation" (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: report the deployment's solve-time history (15 hours to minutes) together with the changes that produced it (pp. 9–10).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper cites one reference, Markowitz (1959) (pp. 2, 18). Novelty is claimed against practice and literature together: "we are not aware of any use of mixed-integer-programming methods in the construction of portfolios in practice", and the problem "has not been described previously in the academic literature" (p. 2); optimizing several subportfolios at once is "not a problem that has been addressed by others" (p. 4). No academic work on cardinality-constrained portfolios is cited.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Interfaces genre: the firm and its crisis first (pp. 2–6), then model, implementation, results and impact; pull quotes (pp. 2, 3, 5); an abstract with six numbered benefits (p. 1); a closing testimonial letter from the founder (p. 18). Claims are partly hedged ("If this result continues to hold ... and we expect that it will", p. 17) and partly promotional ("This free lunch", p. 16). Headline and caveat sit apart: "globally optimize the portfolio" (p. 6), while the implementation tunes the allowed suboptimality and reports no gaps (p. 10), a 1999 instance of Tension T6.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution at the application level: an MIP portfolio-construction process used for 11 portfolios and for two new small-cap funds worth $1.1 billion a year after launch; about $4 million a year saved through fewer tickets; no new trading staff in two years; fewer settlement errors (pp. 13, 16). Limits stated: the return improvement is seen only in simulation (p. 13); there are no savings in variable costs from fewer positions, only avoided rate increases (p. 16); the reduction in execution cost is "difficult to quantify" (p. 16). Future: extend to the large-stock US funds and to goals beyond tracking (pp. 16–17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: an owner (GMO), a decision (the final portfolio), data (targets, forecasts, liquidity), the current rule (QP target plus thresholding, rejected in the owner's terms, p. 5), decision metrics (names, tickets, turnover, cumulative return relative to the benchmark), historical simulation before live deployment, and one applied paper per deployment (pp. 5–16). Own run times are reported, including the bad initial 15 hours (pp. 9–10).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the model stays a linear MIP, with absolute values and convex costs linearized, solved by a commercial solver at the application scale with measured times (pp. 9, 17–18); the formulation was strengthened to improve the LP bounds (pp. 10, 17–18). For Tension T4 this is a 1999 data point where practice matched his later stated emphasis on strong formulations.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: step 1 holds (one joint MIP replaces a greedy postprocessing step, pp. 5–6), but the incumbent heuristic is rejected by argument rather than dueled numerically (p. 5), and no certificate or gap is reported while suboptimality tolerances were tuned (p. 10), consistent with Tension T1.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pre-2004): the trade-offs are governed by seven penalties set "heuristically" through thousands of simulation runs (p. 8), not by one interpretable knob with a published price curve.
- New-pattern candidate: *historical back-simulation as the adoption gate*: simulate the method over two decades of the owner's own history to convince top management before deployment (p. 10), then report live results separately (p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate: *structure-informed branching from the target solution* (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] State the business objection to the owner's current shortcut before proposing the exact model (p. 5).
- [D2] Run one model in two modes for two owner goals (tracking vs. performance-enhancing) by changing only the penalties (pp. 10, 13).
- [D4] Model convex piecewise-linear costs with bounded segment variables and keep binaries for the counts only (pp. 7–8, 17).
- [D4] Strengthen big-M links with problem-specific caps (pp. 17–18).
- [D4] Set branching priorities and directions from the structure of the target solution (p. 10).
- [D5] Keep simulated results (backtests) and implemented results (live portfolios) in separate sections and tables (pp. 10–16).

**Quotes**:
- "Since the number of positions and trades in the composite portfolio is an inherently integer quantity, we decided that we would use mixed-integer-programming methods to globally optimize the portfolio." (p. 6)
- "Prior to implementing the mixed-integer-programming approach, we performed extensive historical simulations. We used these to convince top management at GMO of the effectiveness of the method." (p. 10)

### S121 · On dependent randomized rounding algorithms (Operations Research Letters 1999, DOI 10.1016/s0167-6377(99)00010-3)
**Read level**: full — the whole paper (pp. 1–10), including the tightness example and the reference list. The text file drops the ligatures fi/ff/fl (e.g. `dened` for *defined*), which does not affect reading.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Chungpiaw Teo (National University of Singapore) and Rakesh Vohra (Northwestern) (p. 1); SKILL.md lists Teo in his IO student lineage [04 A.4] · **Position in trajectory**: the LP-relaxation approximation strand {IO} that SKILL.md places in 1985–1995 (this paper was received in January 1996 and revised in September 1998, p. 1). It continues his rounding papers with Vohra (set covering, ref. [2]) and with Teo and Vohra (multicut, IPCO 1995, ref. [1]) (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem & entry (IO)**: The entry is a design gap in a known technique. Randomized rounding (Raghavan–Thompson) rounds each variable independently, with probability xᵢ or with a nonlinear f(xᵢ) (Goemans–Williamson; Bertsimas–Vohra) (pp. 1–2), and "Few structural results of the fractional optimum solution have been used" (p. 2). A survey by Grötschel and Lovász is quoted to say that rounding "appropriately" is not sufficiently analyzed (p. 1); Goemans–Williamson's max-cut algorithm is the one example where the geometry is exploited and variables are rounded dependently (p. 2). The problem is stated as: a class of dependent rounding schemes for "variants of the min-cut type" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Couple all variables through one random number. Draw a single U ~ Uniform[0, 1] and round every variable by the same threshold (s–t cut, p. 3); use U on one side of a bipartition and 1 − U on the other (sign-balanced Boolean quadratic problems, p. 4); use sequential thresholds Uᵣ (uncapacitated lot-sizing, p. 5); lay a comb of points {U, 1 + U, …, k − 1 + U} over consecutive intervals of lengths yⱼ (k-median on a cycle, p. 6); restrict U to [1/2, 1] (feasible cut, p. 8); split the variables randomly into sets A and B before thresholding with U and 1 − U (min-k-sat, p. 9). Changed from classical randomized rounding: dependence among the variables. Kept: the two-step template, solve the LP and then randomize (p. 1). The claimed advantage is that the analysis "becomes extremely simple" (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: couple the rounding of all variables through one shared random threshold (or U and 1 − U across a bipartition) so that joint probabilities reproduce the LP's own min/max terms (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Model & data**: 0–1 integer programs with nonnegative costs and their LP relaxations; graphs undirected unless stated (p. 2). Lot-sizing: no backlogging, zero lead time and a cost condition on the c_{i,j}; "For ease of exposition" the proof covers only a special case of the condition, and "The argument can be adapted" to the general one (p. 5); the LP is first augmented with ordering inequalities w_{i,j} ≤ w_{i,k} for j > k (p. 5). Min-k-sat: at most k literals per clause; clauses containing a literal both negated and unnegated are removed in preprocessing (p. 9). No data.
- **D4 Formulation & analysis**:
  - Integrality by expectation: Z_LP ≤ Z_IP ≤ E(Z_H) = Z_LP, because the expected cut indicator of edge (u, v) is P(min ≤ U ≤ max) = |y(u) − y(v)|, exactly the LP's term (Theorem 1, p. 3). For directed cuts, LP duality with max-flow then yields a probabilistic proof of the max-flow–min-cut theorem, and precedence constraints y(u) ≤ y(v) survive because threshold rounding preserves order (p. 3).
  - Matching the LP's nonlinear terms: with U and 1 − U, P(xᵢxⱼ = 1) equals min(xᵢ, xⱼ) or max(0, xᵢ + xⱼ − 1), the values the LP takes at optimality (Theorem 2, p. 4).
  - An exchange argument justifies adding valid ordering inequalities; induction then shows P(yᵢ = 1) = ȳᵢ (Theorem 3, pp. 5–6). Systematic sampling uses the geometry of the cycle (Theorem 4, pp. 6–7).
  - Approximation: E(x(u, v)) ≤ 2|y(u) − y(v)| by cases on where 1/2 falls (Theorem 5, p. 8); E(Z_H) ≤ 2(1 − 1/2ᵏ)Z_LP by a union bound plus the probability 2/2ᵏ that a clause's literals split favourably (Theorem 6, p. 9).
  - Tightness: a formula with all 2ᵏ sign patterns has Z_IP = 2(1 − 1/2ᵏ)Z_LP, so the bound equals the LP's integrality gap (pp. 9–10).
  - No running-time analysis and no derandomization (p. 2).

### S007 · Moment problems and semidefinite optimization (Handbook of Semidefinite Programming 2000, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4615-4381-7_16)
**Read level**: full — the whole 38-page manuscript (pp. 1–38; references skimmed). **Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Ioana Popescu (INSEAD) and Jay Sethuraman (Columbia) (p. 1), whom SKILL.md lists in his CP and SD student lineages [04 A.4] · **Position in trajectory**: the moments-and-SDP period {CP} of 1995–2001. A survey chapter that packages four strands of his group: SDP relaxations of achievable regions (Bertsimas; Bertsimas–Niño-Mora, p. 7), optimal probability inequalities (Bertsimas–Popescu, p. 20), option-price bounds (Bertsimas–Popescu, p. 26) and the covariance reading of SDP relaxations (Bertsimas–Ye, p. 33), plus new computations (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem & entry (CP+SD+AP+IO)**: Entered by questions, one list per field where moments arise (pp. 2–3). Probability: are the Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff inequalities "best possible", can they be generalized to several variables, and is there a general optimization-based theory? Finance: the best bounds on a derivative's price from the first two moments of the underlying, with no model of price dynamics, plus the inverse problems from observed option prices. Stochastic optimization: designing optimal scheduling policies for multiclass queueing networks is EXPTIME-hard, so can strong lower bounds be found by exploiting the fact that performance vectors are moments? Discrete optimization: is there a general way to generate near-optimal integer solutions from an SDP relaxation? The abstract repeats the four as questions (p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: open a survey with one list of concrete questions per field in which the common object (here, moments) appears (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: "The key connection" is the feasible moment sequence (Definition 1, p. 4): moment feasibility is a semidefinite condition (Hankel matrices in one dimension, Theorem 1, pp. 4–5; for first and second moments, the matrix [[1, M'], [M, Γ]] ⪰ 0, Theorem 2, pp. 5–6). From it the chapter derives: (i) PSD constraints on queue-length moments added to the achievable-region LP (pp. 9, 15); (ii) the tight bound on P(X ∈ S) as an LP over measures whose dual ranges over polynomials g ≥ χ_S and is solved through separation (pp. 21–23); (iii) option prices bounded as sup E[φ(X)] under moment constraints (pp. 27–29); (iv) an SDP solution Y read as a covariance matrix, sampled from N(0, Y) and rounded (pp. 33–34).
  - Transferable technique: turn the fact that the variables are moments into a PSD constraint ([[1, M'], [M, Γ]] ⪰ 0) and add it to any relaxation whose variables are expectations (pp. 5–6, 15).
- **D3 Model & data**: Moment information (n, k, Ω). Strong duality needs the moment vector to be interior to the set of feasible moment vectors; the boundary case is handled by restricting the support (Theorem 8, pp. 22–23). Γ is assumed to have full rank, said to lose no generality (p. 24). A membership oracle for S is assumed (p. 21). Queueing model: an open network with Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, Markovian routing with I − P invertible, mutual independence, steady state under a stable policy, and preemptive nonidling admissible policies; only lower bounds are sought, not policies (pp. 7–8). Finance: only no-arbitrage (a martingale measure), with a zero interest rate assumed without loss of generality (pp. 26–27).
- **D4 Formulation & analysis**:
  - Lift and project for queueing: equilibrium relations become constraints on auxiliary performance variables, further linear and PSD constraints bound them, and the result is projected back, framed explicitly as lift-and-project in the Lovász–Schrijver sense (p. 9). The linear constraints come from projection, definitional and lower-bound identities (Theorem 3, pp. 10–11) and from the flow conservation law L⁻ = L⁺ applied to every aggregated S-queue, proved by a sample-path up-crossing argument (Theorems 4–5, Corollaries 1–2, pp. 12–14). The PSD constraints follow from Theorem 2 (Theorem 6, p. 15).
  - LP duality over measures: weak duality from χ_S ≤ g (Theorem 7, p. 22); strong duality and complementary slackness from Isii and Smith, where the optimal dual polynomial touches χ_S on the support of a discrete extremal distribution (Theorem 8, p. 22); optimization is equivalent to separation (Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver, pp. 23, 29).
  - Explicit bounds as convex programs: a multivariate Markov bound from n convex problems (Theorem 9, p. 23) and a multivariate Chebyshev bound 1/(1 + d²), with d² the squared Mahalanobis distance from the mean to the convex set S (Theorem 10, pp. 24–25); its one-sided closed form improves even the scalar Chebyshev inequality (p. 25). These results are stated with proofs deferred to the group's papers (pp. 23–25).
  - Complexity boundary: the (n, 2, R₊ⁿ) problem is NP-hard through co-positivity (Murty–Kabadi), and the (n, k, Rⁿ) problem is NP-hard through a reduction from 3SAT, attributed to Bertsimas–Popescu without proof (p. 26). The file reads "k > 3"; the OCR renders ≥ as > elsewhere and the separation problem is named 3SEP, so this file cannot settle whether the threshold is k ≥ 3 or k ≥ 4 (SKILL.md, Method 1 step 5, says k ≥ 4 from the 2005 paper).
  - Polynomial-time separation when φ and the moment functions are quadratic or piecewise linear over polynomially many convex sets: test whether Ā = Σ yᵢAᵢ − A is PSD, take a negative eigenvector as the violated point if not, else solve convex QPs (Theorem 11, Algorithms A–B, pp. 29–31).
  - Lo's tight upper bound on a call price is re-proved through the dual: the optimal dual quadratic is tangent to the payoff line x − k (Theorem 12, pp. 31–32).
  - Max s–t cut: x = sign(X) with X ~ N(0, Y); Sheppard's orthant formula of 1900 gives E[x̂ᵢx̂ⱼ] = (2/π) arcsin(yᵢⱼ), feasibility follows from E[(x̂_s + x̂_t)²] = 0, and Goemans–Williamson's inequality gives E[Z_H] ≥ 0.878 Z_SD (Proposition 1, Theorem 13, pp. 33–35).
  - Transferable technique: certify a moment bound with a dual polynomial that dominates the indicator (or the payoff), and read the worst-case distribution off the points where it touches (pp. 22, 31–32).
  - Transferable technique: prove a separation problem polynomial by a PSD test whose failure gives a negative-eigenvector witness, followed by convex QPs (pp. 29–30).
- **D5 Computational & empirical evaluation**: Two queueing examples compare the LP relaxation, the SDP relaxation and a simulated heuristic policy derived from fluid control (pp. 16–18). (i) A two-station network (Fig. 1) with a symmetric arrival rate varied from 0.1 to 1.18, minimizing the expected number in system; the SDP has 283 variables and 259 constraints; solver SDPA (Fujisawa–Kojima–Nakata); each instance under a minute on a Pentium II; the comparison policy is LBFS-B, with the threshold B chosen optimally by simulation (pp. 16–17). (ii) A single station with four classes and mean service times 0.05, 0.1, 0.2 and 0.4, with second-moment terms in the objective (without them the LP is exact); an SDP of 234 variables and 220 constraints, each under two minutes; a simulated dynamic priority policy (p. 18). Findings as stated: in (i) the SDP improves the LP by 55%–60%, more at higher traffic intensity, and is close to the simulated value of LBFS-B; in (ii) it improves the LP "often by an order of magnitude" (p. 18). Failure reported: in certain cases SDPA could not reach the desired accuracy, and the best primal and dual feasible values are reported instead (p. 16). Not reported: confidence intervals for the simulations. Table values are unreadable in this file (pp. 17, 19).
  - Transferable technique: judge a relaxation by the gap it certifies for a concrete heuristic policy (bound against simulated policy cost), not only by its improvement over a weaker relaxation (pp. 17–18).
