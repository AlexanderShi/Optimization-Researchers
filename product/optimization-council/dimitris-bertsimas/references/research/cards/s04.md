### S177 · On the large deviations behavior of acyclic networks of queues (The Annals of Applied Probability 1998, DOI 10.1214/aoap/1028903373)
Read level: **partial**. Read: pp. 1–14 (abstract, introduction, preliminaries, network model, Assumptions B–C, §4 statements and the queue-length step); pp. 15–16 and 20–23 (departure-process set-up, most likely path, Theorem 5.4, the Varadhan step); pp. 30–31 (Theorem 6.4); pp. 35–42 (§7 statements, the §8 tandem example, §9 conclusions, appendix). Not read: the proofs on pp. 17–19, 24–29 and 32–35. File p. 1 titles the paper "…acyclic networks of G/G/1 queues".

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis (Boston University) and John N. Tsitsiklis (MIT) (p. 1). Paschalidis is a student co-advised with Tsitsiklis, PhD 1996 (04-mentorship §A.2). · **Position in trajectory**: SD period. Received December 1994, revised November 1997 (p. 1); funded by the PYI award with Draper matching funds, ARO and NSF (p. 1); same author trio as S187.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD}: Packet networks must keep loss and delay probabilities very small, so the paper studies tails (pp. 1–2). Single-queue large-deviations (LD) results exist. Network bounds are not known to be tight, and the departure process resists exact analysis (p. 2). The entry is a gap in the closest prior work [16]. Its G/D/1 departure result rests on technical assumptions about the arrival process. The departure process must satisfy those same assumptions before it can feed the next queue, and [16] does not check this (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: Put LD Assumptions B and C on external arrivals and services (pp. 8–9). Then prove that the three operations of a single-class acyclic network preserve them: passing through a FCFS G/G/1 queue, superposing independent streams and deterministic splitting. Induct node by node, then compose with single-queue results for waiting time and queue length (pp. 3, 7, 9). Changes from [16]: continuous time, G/G/1 instead of G/D/1, and self-contained assumptions instead of [14]'s technical result (pp. 2–3). The most likely path to each rare event is characterized along the way (pp. 1, 3, 20–21).
- **D3 Model and data**: A directed acyclic graph in which two directed paths meet in at most one node; single class, FCFS, infinite buffers (p. 6). Interarrival times are stationary and possibly dependent within each external stream; service times are iid per node and independent across nodes; routing fractions are deterministic (p. 6). A stationary process of waiting times and queue lengths is assumed, which "contains an implicit stability assumption" (p. 7). Assumption B gives a one-sided LDP for arrivals and a Gärtner–Ellis LDP for services, with strictly convex rate functions; strict convexity is admitted to be there to avoid differentiability issues (pp. 8–9). Assumption C gives sample-path lower bounds that mild mixing guarantees (pp. 8–9). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): The waiting-time LDP (Theorem 4.1) is taken from the literature: "This result is not new" (p. 10). The queue-length tail comes from a distributional-law relation between the arrival's queue length and the waiting plus service time of an earlier customer (Eq. 28, p. 13). That relation holds in the LD setting without the renewal assumption (p. 12). For departures (§5), an upper bound and a matching lower bound are proved by sample-path arguments rather than the Lindley algebra (p. 16). Theorem 5.4 gives the departure rate function in terms of the arrival and service rate functions (p. 21; symbols partly garbled), and the most likely path is read off the optimizer of the underlying problem (pp. 20–21). A one-sided Varadhan lemma recovers the log-MGF as a convex dual, so the induction can continue (p. 22). M/M/1 departures reproduce Burke's theorem in the LD regime (p. 4). Palm and stationary versions of a point process have the same LD behaviour (Theorem 6.4, pp. 30–31); this is used for superposition.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: One worked example in Matlab: two tandem queues with deterministic servers, fed by two-state Markov-modulated deterministic arrivals with mean interarrival time 0.16. Results: θ₁* = −9.47 for the first queue, departures smoother than arrivals, and no large queue at the second queue (pp. 38–40). No simulation check.
- **D6 Positioning** (brief): It extends [16]'s inductive approach while saying it is "not very clear to us" how [16] can be applied inductively (p. 2). It records two related works that reached the authors after submission (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: an LD calculus for acyclic networks with explicit most likely paths, and a Palm-versus-stationary LD equivalence offered as "of independent interest" (pp. 3, 40). Stated limits: single class, acyclic graphs, paths meeting at most once (p. 6). Open problems: feedback, several traffic types, and choosing scheduling policies in the LD regime (p. 40).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: optimization is the analytic language here, not a model handed to a solver. Each rate function is an infimum over paths of empirical rates; the minimizer is the most likely path, and convex duality (Varadhan) returns the log-MGF that the next node needs (pp. 20–22). There is no LP, conic or MIO class and no computation beyond one example. This bears on Inner Tension T3: a probability paper whose key steps are optimization.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: written with a co-advised student and his co-adviser (p. 1; 04-mentorship §A.2).
- new-pattern candidate: *Closure calculus for decomposition*. The three network operations map the assumption class into itself, so each queue is analysed alone (pp. 3, 9, 22). Departures, superposition and thinning play the same role in his 2015 robust queueing paper (S119, card c15). That paper also has a Burke-type theorem, as this one does (p. 4).
- new-pattern candidate: *most likely path as an optimizer*: the path that realizes the rare event is the solution of the variational problem behind the rate function (pp. 1, 20–21, 37–38); recurs in S187.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To analyse a network inductively, pick an assumption class for input processes and prove that every network operation (departure, superposition, splitting) maps the class into itself. Then one single-node theorem plus induction covers every node (pp. 3, 9).
- Before chaining someone else's single-node result, check that its output satisfies the conditions it requires of its input; say so in print if it does not (p. 2).
- Present a rare-event result together with its most likely path: the empirical arrival and service rates over the busy period that cause it, drawn as a figure (pp. 20–21, Fig. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "our approach provides a calculus of acyclic networks since we will be able to determine the large deviations behavior of each individual queue inductively" (p. 9)
- "It is an interesting open problem to derive similar results for network models that have feedback and accommodate more than one type of traffic." (p. 40)

### S214 · From valid inequalities to heuristics: A unified view of primal-dual approximation algorithms in covering problems (Operations Research 1998, DOI 10.1287/opre.46.4.503)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–11, every section with its proofs and the open problems; the references (pp. 11–12) were skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chung-Piaw Teo (MIT, on a graduate scholarship from the National University of Singapore, p. 11; PhD 1996 {IO}, 04-mentorship §B.6) · **Position in trajectory**: the LP-relaxation approximation strand {IO}. Received May 1994, revised July 1995 and February 1996, accepted February 1996, printed July–August 1998 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+LO}: Discrete optimization has two camps (p. 1). Polyhedral combinatorics adds valid inequalities, but which class is better is not clear a priori and is judged by computational experience. Approximation algorithms are judged by the worst case. Primal-dual analyses "appear to be problem specific" and give no insight into design (p. 1). The entry: a technique to design approximation algorithms "when better formulations are available" (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea**: A generic primal-dual algorithm for 0–1 covering problems (pp. 2–3). At each stage it adds a valid inequality for the current instance and sets its dual y_r = min c_i/a_i over positive coefficients. It then fixes the minimizing variable to 1, updates the costs and right-hand side and recurses. A reverse-deletion step at the end leaves a minimal solution. The ratio is the *strength* λ of the inequalities used: the maximum of a·w/b over minimal integral solutions w (Definition 1, p. 3). Kept from Goemans–Williamson: the primal-dual scheme and reverse deletion. Changed: the inequality becomes a free design input whose strength is the guarantee (pp. 1–3).
- **D3 Model and data**: min cx, Ax ≥ b, x ∈ {0,1}ⁿ with A, c ≥ 0; minimization with a nonnegative objective (pp. 1–2). A formulation is reducible if fixing a variable leaves an instance of the same class; then strength can be computed on the original instance (Definition 2, p. 4). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Theorem 1: Z_H ≤ λ Σ y_r b_r ≤ λ Z_D, hence ≤ λ·IZ, and ≤ λ times the LP value when the added inequalities are redundant for the LP. The proof is backward induction over the stages using minimality (pp. 3–4). Theorem 2 is the tightness result: a cost vector built from the extreme minimal solution forces Z_H ≥ λ(1−ε)·IZ (p. 4). In the geometric picture, the best inequality gives the thinnest slab between ax = b and ax = λb that holds all minimal solutions (pp. 3–4). Applications by strength calculations:
  - multicut formulation of the MST: strength 1, which proves integrality and recovers Kruskal; cut formulation: 2(1−1/n) (p. 5)
  - Hall–Hochbaum's row-sum bound for general covering (Theorem 5, p. 5)
  - vertex feedback set: 4 log|V| via Erdős–Pósa (pp. 5–6)
  - uncrossable functions: 2(1−1/l), with a new self-contained proof (Theorem 8, pp. 6–7)
  - contra-polymatroids: strength 1 (integral), and k for the intersection of k (Theorems 9–10, p. 8)
  - consecutive-ones and related set-covering matrices (Theorems 11–15, pp. 8–9)
  - a multiphase version with a harmonic factor (Theorem 16, pp. 9–10); weakly supermodular cut covering (Theorem 17, p. 10)
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none (no computational section).
- **D6 Positioning** (brief): Builds on Goemans–Williamson and Williamson et al. and separates its strength notion from Goemans's (p. 3). Claims the best worst-case bound known "in most cases" (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing** (brief): Two numbered contributions, one of which concedes the efficiency loss (p. 2). A geometric figure precedes the tightness theorem (pp. 3–4). The paper closes with three numbered open problems (p. 11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution: one design rule (find valid inequalities of low strength) that recovers many known bounds with simpler proofs (pp. 2, 11). Limits:
  - the algorithms are "not as efficient as the known ones" (p. 2)
  - constructing the minimal sets for uncrossable and weakly supermodular functions is left open (pp. 7, 10)
  - open problems: least-strength inequalities, coordinating the choice across stages in a "dynamic programming-like" way, and strength under instance restrictions such as planarity (p. 11)
  - the fractional-covering use in stochastic scheduling is deferred to Teo's thesis (p. 11)

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (IO form): the formulation (the valid inequalities) is designed first. The algorithm and its guarantee follow from the dual variables y_r the algorithm builds (pp. 1–4). The boundary is printed as a tightness theorem and open problems (pp. 4, 11). It sits on the strong-formulation side of Tension T4 (a stronger inequality gives a better ratio, pp. 3–4). It also shows the Research Taste line "geometry first, then algebra and algorithms" (slab picture, pp. 3–4).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: a student paper of the IO lineage (Teo; p. 11; 04-mentorship §B.6).
- new-pattern candidate: *subsume the incumbent as a special case*. Recovered as strength calculations: Kruskal; the Hall–Hochbaum and Bertsimas–Vohra row-sum bound; Bar-Yehuda et al.'s 4 log|V|; Williamson et al.'s 2-approximation; Goemans et al.'s cut-covering bounds (pp. 5–11).
- new-pattern candidate: *bound, constructive heuristic, provable ratio, tightness instance* (Theorems 1–2; Remark 3: maximum strength at the first stage makes each application's bound essentially tight; pp. 3–4).
- new-pattern candidate: *From-X-to-Y titles naming the transplant* ("From valid inequalities to heuristics", p. 1; a 1998 instance, earlier than those in card c18).
- new-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device*: integrality of the multicut and constrained contra-polymatroid polyhedra, Kruskal's algorithm, and optimality on consecutive-ones matrices (pp. 5, 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn approximation-algorithm design into inequality design. Define the strength of a valid inequality against minimal solutions and prove ratio ≤ maximum strength by induction over the primal-dual stages. Each new problem then needs only a strength calculation (pp. 3–5).
- Show the bound is not an artefact of the analysis. Build a cost vector from the extreme minimal solution so that the algorithm attains λ(1−ε) (p. 4).
- Use reducibility, under which fixing a variable keeps the instance in its class, so a property checked once holds at every stage. Enlarge the class (weakly supermodular rather than proper functions) when that is what restores reducibility (pp. 4, 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Theorem 1 reduces the construction of good approximation algorithms to the design of valid inequalities of small strength." (p. 4)
- "Though the algorithms constructed by this framework are not as efficient as the known ones, the analysis of the algorithms is considerably simpler." (p. 2)

### H010 · Optimization of multiclass queueing networks with changeover times via the achievable region approach: Part II, the multi-station case (Mathematics of Operations Research 1999, DOI 10.1287/moor.24.2.331)
Read level: **partial**. Read: pp. 1–12 (abstract, introduction, model, Theorems 1–3 with proofs, Corollaries 1–2, the statement of Theorem 4); p. 16 (Corollary 3 with remarks, Proposition 1); pp. 20–30 (Theorems 5–8, Table 3, the Klimov bound, numerical results, policies, the Palm-calculus appendix). Not read: pp. 13–15 and 17–19 (the proof of Theorem 4, Proposition 2 and the convex-constraint derivations).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: José Niño-Mora, then at the MIT ORC as a PhD student and postdoc (p. 30) · **Position in trajectory**: the SW1 achievable-region programme; companion of S212 (Part I) (p. 2). Received 2 December 1996, revised September and November 1998 (p. 1). The computations were done by Jay Sethuraman (p. 30). DSpace catalogues his 1999 thesis as "Scheduling multiclass queueing networks and job shops using fluid and semidefinite relaxations" (04-mentorship §A.1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+LO+CP}: Most multi-station multiclass queueing networks (MQNETs) have no exact performance analysis, and optimizing them appears intractable (Papadimitriou–Tsitsiklis). The workable objective is therefore computable bounds that measure a heuristic's suboptimality gap (p. 1). Most network models omit changeover times (p. 1). The entry is the limits of the three existing tools (p. 3):
  - conservation laws exclude changeovers and multiple stations
  - work-decomposition laws exist only for single servers
  - potential functions are "algebraic in nature" and give no physical insight

  The stated aim is to "support the thesis" that the approach solves hard problems (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: Lift, bound and project. Equilibrium relations become constraints on auxiliary conditional moments (Table 1, p. 5); PSD constraints are added, and the result is projected back onto mean queue lengths (p. 5). The plan is credited to Lovász–Schrijver's lift-and-project (p. 5). New constraint families (pp. 6–21):
  - projection constraints (Theorem 1) and lower bounds (Theorem 2)
  - flow conservation L⁻ = L⁺ for every aggregated S-queue (Theorem 3)
  - network work-decomposition laws (Theorem 4), giving workload bounds stronger than those of Bertsimas–Paschalidis–Tsitsiklis 1994 (Corollary 3, p. 16)
  - convex changeover constraints (Theorem 5) and PSD moment constraints (Theorem 6)

  The bounds are LPs, SDPs and a convex program, tabulated with their sizes (Table 3, p. 22).
- **D3 Model and data**: M single-server stations and N classes; Poisson external arrivals, exponential services, Markovian routing with I−P invertible; general changeover-time distributions; mutual independence (p. 4). Policy classes: dynamic, static, stable, nonidling. Service may be preempted, but a changeover, once started, must finish (p. 4). The system is assumed to be in steady state under a stable policy; ρ < 1 at each station is necessary but not sufficient for stability (pp. 4–5). The flow-conservation constraints need only a finite first moment of the total queue length, weaker than the earlier papers' assumption (Remark 1, p. 11). Costs are linear; second-moment costs go to an SDP (pp. 21, 23). Synthetic instances only.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Theorem 3 applies L⁻ = L⁺ to the S-queue and converts the Palm probabilities into time-stationary ones with Papangelou's theorem via stochastic intensities (pp. 9–10).
  - The potential-function method generated higher-order constraints recursively; Corollary 1 gives closed formulas for all of them (pp. 10–11).
  - In the single-server case the workload bounds, together with one equation, describe the performance region exactly, through the work conservation laws of Bertsimas–Niño-Mora 1996 (Remark 2, p. 16).
  - The PSD condition [1 z'; z Z] ⪰ 0 is imposed on first and second moments (Theorem 6, pp. 20–21).
  - Klimov's one-pass adaptive greedy computes a bound in N steps; LP duality proves it valid (Theorem 7, pp. 22–23).
  - The paper claims the first relaxation of the second-moment performance region (p. 23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: "Limited" experiments (p. 23), run by Sethuraman (p. 30); no solver, machine or run time is given.
  - Two-station re-entrant line with changeovers: Z_CONVEX against the best of the 4 simulated priority policies. The gap widens with load (ρ = 0.9, CH = 0: 3.05 vs 17.12), and the bound "seems also weak" (Table 4, p. 24).
  - Rybko–Stolyar network: Z_LP2 and Z_SD2 against LBFS with a threshold tuned by simulation (ρ = 0.983: 80.766, 81.093, 85.643). Both bounds are strong, and the SDP gain is not significant (Table 5, p. 25).
  - One station with four classes, objective Σ x_i + y_ii: the SDP bound rises far above the LP bound (ρ = 0.975: 725.855 vs 89.332) but stays below the simulated optimal policy's 1166.362. The SDP is therefore not exact (Table 6, pp. 25–26).

  The §10 policies are not tested.
- **D7 Writing** (brief): Numbered contributions appear in the abstract and the introduction (pp. 1, 3). Table 3 lists every bound with its program class and size (p. 22). There is no concluding section: the paper ends with §10 and an appendix (pp. 26–30).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the physical origin of the potential-function constraints with closed formulas for them, network work-decomposition laws, the first second-moment relaxation (an SDP), an N-step bound and two policies (pp. 1, 3). Printed limits:
  - Z_CONVEX is weak in heavy traffic (p. 24), and the SDP is not exact (p. 26)
  - unlike the single-station case, the relaxations yield no optimal policy (p. 26)
  - the stability of the index policy is unresolved (p. 26)
  - the finite-buffer LP is not a formal bound unless constraints are dropped (p. 27)

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: SW1 in practice. Stochastic scheduling is written as LP, SDP and convex programs over a relaxed achievable region (pp. 5, 21–22). The dual is read for Klimov's indices, which give both the N-step bound and an index policy (pp. 22–23, 26). The boundary is printed (pp. 24, 26–27). The paper also partly answers the 1995 survey's two open problems, which 01-publications §2 SW1 records as "followed only in part": it computes SDP relaxations on three small networks and proposes two network policies, though the policies are untested (pp. 25–28).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the bound is set against the incumbent heuristic (best simulated priority rule, tuned LBFS, a known optimal policy) in place of a solver certificate. No solver versions, run times or replication counts are given (pp. 23–26).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: one student carries the programme through his PhD and postdoc, and a second runs the computations (p. 30). The work is split into Part I and Part II papers (p. 2).
- new-pattern candidate: *physical origin of an algebraic device*. The potential-function constraints turn out to be the flow-conservation law L⁻ = L⁺, which also yields closed formulas (pp. 3, 8, 10–11); recurs in S212.
- new-pattern candidate: *PSD moment-matrix strengthening carried across domains* (Theorem 6 and Table 6; pp. 20–21, 25–26).
- new-pattern candidate: *policy read off a relaxation*: Klimov indices of a fictitious pooled server, and a randomized decentralized rule from the z variables of a truncated LP (pp. 26–28); untested.
- new-pattern candidate: *staged companion papers from one model* (Part I single station, Part II multi-station; p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write conservation laws at Palm epochs, then convert each Palm expectation into a time-stationary one with Papangelou's formula. All constraints then live on one set of variables (pp. 9–10).
- When a family of constraints was found algebraically, look for the physical law it encodes. The law can give closed formulas for the whole hierarchy (pp. 3, 10–11).
- Add the PSD condition [1 z'; z Z] ⪰ 0 whenever the variables are the first and second moments of one random vector (Theorem 6, p. 20).
- Test a relaxation on an instance whose optimal policy is known. Non-exactness is then shown, not assumed (Table 6, pp. 25–26).

**Quotes**:
- "Our objective in this paper is to support the thesis that the achievable region approach is an effective tool for solving hard performance optimization problems." (p. 3)
- "Also, since we know that the simulated policy is optimal, we can also conclude that the semidefinite relaxation we consider is not exact." (p. 26)

### S130 · Asymptotically optimal algorithms for job shop scheduling and packet routing (Journal of Algorithms 1999, DOI 10.1006/jagm.1999.1047)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–23 read. Most displayed formulas (the theorem bounds, the congestion definitions and the LP's sums) are lost in the extraction, so the bounds below come from the abstract and the prose.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: David Gamarnik (IBM T. J. Watson Research Center, p. 1). His MIT PhD (1997) was co-advised by Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis (04-mentorship §A.2). · **Position in trajectory**: the SD–IO bridge; NSF grant DMI-9610486 (p. 1); the job-shop algorithms were implemented by Leon Hsu (p. 23).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+SD+LO}: Job-shop makespan minimization is NP-hard and "notoriously difficult"; a 10×10 instance stayed unsolved for over 20 years (p. 2). Packet routing with fixed paths is a job shop; choosing the paths adds routing decisions (p. 2). The entry joins two communities (pp. 2–3): fluid control of multiclass queueing networks (his own line with Avram–Ricard and Luo) and LP-rounding approximation algorithms. The regime studied is many jobs of each type.
- **D2 Key idea**:
  - The fluid relaxation replaces discrete jobs by continuous flow and lets machines share effort. It is solved in closed form: its optimal makespan equals the maximum congestion C_max, a lower bound on the true makespan (Proposition 2, pp. 7–8).
  - A "synchronization algorithm" mimics the fluid's proportional effort. In each window of length R + U_max, every machine processes exactly a_i jobs of each type, and R is chosen to balance the error terms (pp. 8–12).
  - For path selection, a multicommodity-flow LP gives the lower bound and the per-edge allocations. Packets left over at the end are routed one by one (pp. 17–19).
- **D3 Model and data**: Deterministic instances: J machines, I job types, integer processing times, n_i jobs of type i present at time 0, no arrivals. Processing is nonpreemptive, one task per machine at a time, stages in order (pp. 2, 4–5). Packets take unit time per edge, and one packet crosses an edge at a time (p. 2). The paper makes no probabilistic assumptions (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - The makespan is at most C_max plus a lower-order term whose constant depends on processing times but not on the number of jobs. Hence the algorithm is asymptotically optimal (abstract, p. 1; Theorem 1, p. 10). The term reads as O(√C_max) in the garbled renderings "O(c)" (p. 1) and "O(Jc*)" (p. 17).
  - The proof tags one job and bounds its delay over at most J_max stages (pp. 10–11).
  - With proportional job counts the bound is C_max + O(1) (abstract, p. 1; p. 13).
  - The bound is compared with Sevast'janov's geometric algorithm, analytically only (Proposition 3, pp. 13–15).
  - The routing results follow: Theorem 2 (p. 17); Proposition 4 and Theorem 3 (pp. 19–22).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Three variants were run on the Muth–Thompson 10×10 instance with N jobs of each type, N ≤ 50 and N ≤ 2,500: synchronization, the proportional-case modification and a "variable Ω" version in which machines do not idle (Figs. 1–2, pp. 13–15). The plotted values are lost in the extraction. The text reports the variable-Ω version within 1% of the lower bound for N ≥ 500 (p. 13). No run times, no other computed baselines, and no packet-routing test.
- **D6 Positioning** (brief): The closest prior work, Sevast'janov's algorithm, is called more complex, and the new bound is argued stronger on many instances (pp. 3, 13–15). The paper contrasts itself with Karp's probabilistic asymptotic optimality (p. 4).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution: algorithms asymptotically optimal on every instance with many jobs, read as showing that "the dynamic and not the combinatorial character" dominates at scale (p. 22). Limits:
  - the additive term depends on the processing times (p. 4)
  - the routing algorithm's last step is "inefficient" (p. 19)
  - the abstract says "within 1% of optimality even for moderately sized problems" (p. 1); the body shows this only for N ≥ 500 jobs per type, and only for the variable-Ω variant, which has no theorem (p. 13)

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence {IO+SD}: a combinatorial problem owned by scheduling is relaxed into a fluid control problem, a continuous LP solved in closed form. For routing it becomes a polynomial-size multicommodity LP. The schedule is built from the relaxation (pp. 6–8, 17–19).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: quality is reported as distance to the relaxation bound, not certified by an exact solve. The best-performing variant is not the one with the theorem, and the incumbent (Sevast'janov) is compared through bounds only (pp. 13–15).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: a co-advised SD-lineage student keeps working with him after graduating (p. 1; 04-mentorship §A.2).
