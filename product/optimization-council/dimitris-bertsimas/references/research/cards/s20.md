### S290 · Compressed sensing: A discrete optimization approach (Machine Learning 113(9) 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10994-024-06577-0; arXiv 2306.04647)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–39 (abstract, §§1–7, Algorithms 1–2, Tables 1–10, declarations and author contributions). Two passages were skimmed: the SOS-certificate construction in the proof of Theorem 5 (pp. 15–17) and the block-inversion cost analysis (pp. 19–20). References pp. 40–44 were consulted for selected entries. The file is arXiv v3 of 12 July 2024 (p. 1). First-page check: title and authors Bertsimas and Johnson match (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nicholas A. G. Johnson (MIT ORC, corresponding; equal contribution, p. 1). By the contribution statement, Johnson did the implementation, data, simulations and first draft (p. 39). · **Position in trajectory**: the sparse-ML line *under a modern optimization lens* (SW4), carried to compressed sensing. The branch and bound follows the group's sparse-plus-low-rank work ([46], p. 20) and its backbone method ([48], p. 21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO CP}: The problem is to minimize ‖x‖₀ subject to ‖Ax − b‖² ≤ ε. It is attacked almost only by ℓ1 convex approximations or greedy heuristics, whose guarantees need strong statistical assumptions; RIP, for one, cannot be verified on real data (pp. 4–5). Integer optimization "has been given little attention", and the earlier MILPs need big-M values and cover only ε = 0 (p. 4). Entry: add a ridge term (1/γ)‖x‖² and solve the discrete problem exactly (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Binaries z model the support, and the perspective constraint x_i² ≤ z_i θ_i gives an exact MISOCP (Theorem 3, p. 10). When γ ≥ max_{x∈X} ‖Wx‖∞², its continuous relaxation is (weighted) Basis Pursuit Denoising (Theorem 4, p. 11), so the field's standard convex method becomes the relaxation of the exact model. An SOS/SDP hierarchy gives stronger bounds (Theorem 5, p. 15). A custom B&B takes lower bounds from the BPD-type relaxation and upper bounds from greedy column selection (pp. 17–22).
- **D3 Model and data**: A ∈ R^{m×n}, ε > 0, and γ > 0 set to √n by default or cross-validated (p. 2); coordinate weights W generalize the model (p. 3). The authors concede three computational limits: γ0 requires solving the hard problem (p. 8), λε in Theorem 2 "cannot be computed easily" (p. 9), and the SDP needs PSD matrices of size C(2n+d, d) (p. 17). Data:
  - Gaussian synthetic data: m = 100, σ = 10, ε = α‖b‖², 100 trials per setting (p. 24);
  - 100 MIT-BIH ECG records, with a K-SVD dictionary (1024 × 2000) and a 40 × 1024 Bernoulli sensing matrix (pp. 31–32);
  - del.icio.us multi-label data: about 16,000 pages, 983 labels, 100 Hadamard rows (p. 35).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Theorem 1: for γ ≥ γ0 = ‖x̃‖² (x̃ a minimum-norm ℓ0 optimum) both problems share an optimum, by cardinality counting (pp. 7–8). Theorem 2 bounds the sparsity loss below γ0 through the ridge solution and KKT (pp. 8–9). Theorem 4 solves the separable inner problem in closed form (pp. 11–12). Theorem 5 maps any dual-feasible BPD point to a degree-1 SOS certificate of equal value (pp. 15–17). The B&B uses most-fractional branching, least-lower-bound node selection and feasibility cuts Σ_{i∈I0} z_i ≥ 1, with finite δ-optimality (Theorem 6, via Theorem 21 of [46], p. 20). Its two speed-ups, a backbone on the BPD support and early stopping, give up the guarantee by the authors' own account (pp. 21–23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia 1.5.2/JuMP 0.21.7 with Gurobi 9.0.3 and Mosek 9.3 (γ = √n, d = 1) on MIT Supercloud, code on GitHub (p. 23). Against OMP and greedily rounded BPD and IRWL1, with 10-minute caps (5 for ECG): 2.71/16.62/6.04% sparser (p. 24); an SOS bound 8.92% above SOC for n ≤ 50 (pp. 29–30); a dominating ECG frontier (p. 34); higher multi-label accuracy (p. 36). Mean time sits at the 600 s cap for n ≥ 400, against ≤ 4 s for the baselines (Table 3, p. 27), and no gaps or certified counts are reported (my reading of §6).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a formulation, relaxations and an algorithm. Gains are stated against both the average and the best benchmark (pp. 1, 3). The limit is in the abstract and the conclusion: run time grows by orders of magnitude, so the method suits applications where runtime is not critical (pp. 2, 38). Future work: valid inequalities, a more scalable B&B, and fast methods for the SDP bound (p. 38). The claim that the custom B&B beats Gurobi's MISOCP (pp. 13–14) is not tested in §6 (my reading).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–4, 10–17, 27): a problem owned by signal processing and statistics is written exactly as a MISOCP through a class-keeping device, the perspective reformulation. The incumbent convex method is read off as its relaxation (Theorem 4), and the boundary is printed (SDP size, p. 17; run times, p. 27).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 18–27): the incumbent heuristics serve both as components and as opponents. BPD's support is the backbone and greedy rounding gives the upper bound. Solver versions and caps are stated (step 3). Step 4 is missing, however: the abstract promises "certifiable optimality" (p. 1), yet the experiments use a backbone that drops the guarantee and caps that bind at n ≥ 400, and no gap is reported (Tension T1).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 7–9, 34, 38): γ is a knob tied to sparsity by Theorems 1–2, and on ECG it traces a sparsity–error frontier (p. 34). The price of exactness is paid in computation and printed up front. The bound is data-dependent and not computable (p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate: *recover the incumbent convex surrogate as the exact model's relaxation* (pp. 10–14, 18). BPD becomes the perspective relaxation of the MISOCP. This justifies it as a bound and lets it be reused inside the B&B. The move is close to Heuristic 1, which finds the robust problem a heuristic solves.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Add a small ridge term to an ℓ0 objective. Prove a finite γ0 above which the regularized and original problems share an optimum, and bound the loss in sparsity below it (Theorems 1–2, pp. 7–9).
- Write the support with the perspective constraint x_i² ≤ z_i θ_i, then prove that its relaxation equals the field's standard convex method under a parameter condition. You gain a familiar lower bound and a ready subproblem solver (pp. 10–14, 18).
- Turn every infeasible node into a cut Σ_{i∈I0} z_i ≥ 1 that prunes all its descendants (pp. 18, 20).
- Apply your own post-processing (greedy rounding) to the baselines and report results before and after it. Report gains against both the average baseline and the best baseline in each experiment (pp. 1, 24–25).

**Quotes**:
- "This improved accuracy comes at the cost of an increase in computation time by several orders of magnitude." (p. 2)
- "though Basis Pursuit Denoising is typically motivated as a convex approximation to CS in the presence of noise, it can alternatively be understood as the natural convex relaxation of the mixed integer second order cone problem given by (12)" (p. 11)

### S331 · Holistic deep learning (Machine Learning 113(1) 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10994-023-06482-y; arXiv 2110.15829)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17 (abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1–6, captions and panel values of Figures 1–3). The Appendix A tables (pp. 23–26) were skimmed; references (pp. 18–22) were consulted only for the group's own entries. The file is arXiv v5 of 20 March 2023 (p. 1), earlier than the 2024 journal print. First-page check: title and authors Bertsimas, Villalobos Carballo, Boussioux, Li, A. Paskov and I. Paskov match (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Kimberly Villalobos Carballo, Léonard Boussioux, Michael Lingzhi Li, Alex Paskov, Ivan Paskov (all MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a cohort paper. It bundles the group's own components into deep learning (pp. 3–8, 16): robust training (Bertsimas et al. 2021); stable training (Bertsimas & Paskov 2020; Bertsimas, Dunn & Paskov 2022); and Optimal Policy Trees (Amram, Dunn & Zhuo 2022).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML RO}: Networks used in high-stakes decisions have three weaknesses: they are vulnerable to input perturbations, unstable across train–validation splits, and over-parametrized. The three were "almost exclusively studied in isolation", with little known about their trade-offs (p. 2). Entry: one loss function that imposes robustness, stability and sparsity together (pp. 2, 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Minimize loss (4) (p. 6). It adds a sigmoid-gated ℓ0 penalty λΣσ(βs_j) to the stability form θ + (1/a)Σ[robust cross-entropy − θ]⁺.
  - Robustness: a first-order linearization of the network inside the ℓ∞ ball adds ρ‖∇ₓ…‖₁ to each logit difference (eqs. 5–7, pp. 6–7).
  - Stability: the loss is taken on the worst training subset of size a (pp. 8–9).
  - Sparsity: Savarese et al.'s deterministic sigmoid approximation of ℓ0, run over several rounds of re-initialization (pp. 7–8).

  Everything is trained by gradient descent (p. 7). An Optimal Policy Tree then prescribes a loss from data-set meta-features (pp. 16–17).
- **D3 Model and data**: Feed-forward ReLU classifiers: 2 × 128 for tabular data, AlexNet for images (pp. 5, 11). The authors concede that the robust term (7) "is not always an upper bound" of the adversarial loss; they rely on earlier experiments for small ρ (p. 7). Data: 45 UCI sets (up to 245,056 rows and 856 features), MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR10 (p. 10). Each set has a fixed 20% test set and 10 random 80/20 train–validation splits (p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The one optimization device is the stability term. The inner max over binary z with Σz = a is linear in z, so z can range over its convex hull. LP duality and the closed form u_n = [L_n − θ]⁺ then give the unconstrained loss θ + (1/a)Σ[L_n − θ]⁺ (pp. 8–9). There are no theorems.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Eight loss combinations (Table 1, p. 10) are scored on natural accuracy, PGD ℓ∞ adversarial accuracy, worst accuracy over 10 splits and % zero weights (pp. 9–10), with hyperparameters chosen on validation (p. 11), against the nominal network only (my reading of §4). Gains concentrate where nominal accuracy is 70–90% (p. 12); HDL is 16.2× slower (Table 5, p. 14); a policy tree is trained on 40 data sets and tested on 5 (pp. 16–17). The tables also show costs the text does not discuss: CIFAR10 natural accuracy 70.1 → 69.3; on MNIST, adding sparsity to robustness lowers adversarial accuracy (78.4 → 74.7; HDL 76.0), and Sparse (42.6) and Stable+Sparse (27.7) fall below the nominal 49.6 (Tables 3–4, p. 14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a framework, ablations, a SHAP analysis and a prescriptive recommender (pp. 2–3, 17). Limits appear only inside the results: sparsity "generally hurts accuracy and stability" (p. 16), and the linearization caveat (p. 7). There is no limitations or future-work section (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 6, 11–17): three properties, each with one knob (ρ, a, and λ with β̄), and the trade-offs are measured (multi-objective rank p. 13, SHAP p. 15, slowdown p. 14). But there is no knob–property bound or price curve. The user receives a prescribed loss rather than a curve (pp. 16–17).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 8–9): step 4 (dualize) keeps the worst-case-subset problem in a class that SGD can train, not in an LP, conic or MIO class.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3–8, 16): the group's earlier components are reused and combined by a cohort of students.
- New-pattern candidate: *prescriptive tree as method selector* (pp. 16–17). A policy tree learned on data-set meta-features recommends which loss to train. It is scored out of sample against the nominal method and against the best method for each data set (Table 6, p. 17).
- New-pattern candidate: *first-order surrogate for an MIO-born model class* (pp. 7–8). The ℓ0 penalty that the group elsewhere solves exactly by MIO becomes a sigmoid-gated penalty trained by gradient descent at network scale. The same move appears in S477.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Make a loss over the worst training subset trainable by SGD. Relax the binary inner max to its convex hull, dualize it, and eliminate the slacks in closed form to get θ + (1/a)Σ[ℓ_n − θ]⁺ (pp. 8–9).
- Run the full factorial of loss components (2³ = 8 models). Choose each model's configuration by its average validation rank across all metrics, and report test ranks by difficulty band of the data sets (pp. 10–13).
- Split each reported slowdown into cost per batch times number of iterations (Table 5, p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "Even though the expression in Eq. (7) is not always an upper bound of Eq. (5) for an arbitrary value of ρ" (p. 7)
- "it is often possible to simultaneously improve robustness, stability, and sparsity without sacrificing performance on natural accuracy" (p. 3)

### S348 · Pareto adaptive robust optimality via a Fourier–Motzkin elimination lens (Mathematical Programming 205(1) 2024, DOI 10.1007/s10107-023-01983-z; arXiv 2012.04419)
Read level: **partial**: the main text, pp. 1–22, was read in full, with Example 2 (p. 10) and the bilinear reformulations (p. 17) skimmed. The appendices were read only in part: A.1 on FME (pp. 24–25), Lemma 10 and Corollary 5 (p. 29), Appendix B (pp. 29–31), and the proof of Theorem 1 (C.4, pp. 34–35). The other proofs (C.1–C.3 and C.5–C.15, pp. 30–41) were not read. The file is arXiv v3 of 5 May 2022, dated 30 April 2022 (p. 1). First-page check: title and authors Bertsimas, ten Eikelder, den Hertog and Trichakis match (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: S. C. M. ten Eikelder (Tilburg), Dick den Hertog (Amsterdam), Nikolaos Trichakis (MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the RO programme (SW3), now on adaptive RO. Two co-authors tie it to that programme: den Hertog, his co-author on the RO book (SKILL lineage), and Trichakis, whose 2014 paper with Iancu introduced Pareto robust optimality (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO LO; IO only as testbed}: RO problems usually have many worst-case optimal solutions, and these differ in non-worst-case scenarios (p. 1). Iancu and Trichakis's PRO settles the choice for static RO. For two-stage adaptive RO, existing approaches apply PRO after imposing linear decision rules (LDRs), which "fails to produce solutions that cannot be dominated" (p. 2). Entry: a radiation-therapy toy example in which the PARO first-stage set {20 ≤ x ≤ 30} is a strict subset of the PRO set {20 ≤ x ≤ 40} (pp. 2–3, Table 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: PARO is defined for pairs (x, y(·)) and for each stage separately (Definitions 6–8, pp. 6–7). The here-and-now x matters most, because y can be re-solved once z is known (p. 7). Fourier–Motzkin elimination (FME) of the adaptive variables turns the ARO into a static RO with a convex piecewise-linear (PWL) objective (Lemma 1, p. 8). PARO for x is then equivalent to PRO for that static problem (Lemma 2, p. 9). FME serves as a proof lens here, not as an algorithm (pp. 4, 11).
- **D3 Model and data**: Two-stage linear ARO with fixed, continuous recourse; A(z), r(z) and c(z) are affine in z; U is compact and convex with nonempty relative interior; x may be integer (pp. 5–6). The authors admit three restrictions: the treatment covers only two-stage linear models (p. 4); several constructive results need d = 0 (no adaptive variables in the objective), "a limiting assumption" (p. 11); and Lemma 5's uniqueness condition is "quite restrictive" (p. 13). Data: 250 random facility-location instances (40 sites, 20 demand points, a budgeted demand set with Γ = 200) and 1,000 smaller instances in Appendix B (pp. 19–20, 29).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Existence of a PARO x (Theorem 1, p. 9): minimize ∫_U f(x, z) dP(z) over the worst-case optimal set with a strictly positive P (e.g. Gaussian); dominated convergence gives continuity, and any minimizer is PRO (Lemma 12, pp. 34–35). A counterexample shows compactness cannot be dropped (p. 9). A PWL PARO rule exists, proved once by FME and once by basic LP solutions (Lemma 3, Theorem 2, p. 11). With d = 0, a PRO solution of the static problem under a rule structure feasible for every feasible x is PARO; this covers hybrid, block and simplex (LDR) uncertainty (Theorem 3, Corollary 2, pp. 11–12). Lemma 4 checks whether a rule is a PARO extension (p. 12). The general method appends a second column-and-constraint generation (C&CG) stage to Zeng and Zhao's, with bilinear subproblems solved by alternating LPs from LDR-based and random starts (Algorithm 2, Lemma 7, pp. 14–17). Boundary printed: full FME only up to about 20 adaptive variables (p. 9), ARO NP-hard (p. 14), existence proofs not constructive (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi 9.0 dual simplex on an i7-8565U with 16 GB (p. 19). ARO takes ≤ 6 C&CG iterations (251 s on average) and the PARO stage ≤ 6 (78 s) (p. 20). x_PARO differs from x_ARO in 31% of instances (median ℓ1 differences 3–4, Table 2). On those instances the median gains over ARO, PRO and PRO with LDR recourse are 0.54–1.83% at the nominal scenario, rising to 19.1% in the scenario of maximal difference (Table 3, p. 20). Some gains are negative, and ARO is the first solution found (pp. 20–21); Appendix B agrees (p. 30).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: the concept, existence results, constructive methods, and FME proofs that recover the Bertsimas–Goyal and Zhen et al. results on the optimality of LDRs (p. 4). Limits: the heuristic bilinear solves can stop without a PARO solution, or falsely conclude that a solution is ARO (p. 17). Algorithm 2 sometimes cannot improve the ARO solution, when that solution is already PARO or is unique (p. 19). Future work: tractable algorithms for larger instances and more general classes (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1–3, 19–21): the property here is efficiency in non-worst-case scenarios at an unchanged worst case. PARO "can only yield performance benefits, without any associated drawbacks" (p. 2), so its price is zero and its gain is measured. The paper answers the SKILL's own correction to Method 3, that budgeted robust optima need not be Pareto efficient (Iancu–Trichakis, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2013.1753). It does so in the critic's frame and with the critic as co-author (Heuristic 2).
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 8–11, 14, 24–25): the reformulation device (FME turns ARO into a static PWL RO), the class kept (LDRs suffice under simplex uncertainty, Corollary 2(iii)), and the boundary printed (about 20 adaptive variables; NP-hardness).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 14, 17–20): there is no MIO duel, but the rule against calling uncertified results optimal holds. Heuristically solved stages are labelled "(approximate) PARO", the risk of false termination is stated, and several starting points reduce it.
- New-pattern candidate: *Pareto refinement of an optimal set* (pp. 2–3, 14–16). Optimize a secondary criterion, here performance in non-worst-case scenarios, over the set of worst-case optimal solutions. Existence comes from theory, and an extra C&CG stage computes it. The same move appears in S401 (Pareto-optimal model sequences).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove that an optimal set contains a non-dominated element, minimize the integral of the objective against a strictly positive measure over that set; continuity follows from dominated convergence (pp. 34–35).
- Use FME as a proof device: eliminate the adaptive variables and track which original constraints produce each bound. Structural results on decision rules then follow from the eliminated system (pp. 8, 24–25, 30).
- Report improvement in three kinds of scenario: the nominal one, a random sample, and the scenario that maximizes the difference between two solutions, found with the same subproblem (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "From a practical standpoint, this means that implementing PARO solutions can only yield performance benefits, without any associated drawbacks." (p. 2)
- "The methods used in the existence proofs of Section 3 are not computationally tractable, i.e., they provide little guidance for finding PARO solutions in practice." (p. 11)

### S351 · M3H: multimodal multitask machine learning for healthcare (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2404.18975)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–23 (abstract, §§1–7, Table 1; Figs. 1, 2 and 5 survive only as text fragments). References (pp. 24–30) were consulted for selected entries. The supplemental figures and table cited on pp. 9 and 14 are not in the file.

First-page check: the title matches, but p. 1 has no author block and no arXiv stamp. The abstract matches abstracts.json for S351 word for word, and works.json lists D Bertsimas and Y Ma (from Scholar, DBLP and his homepage). Authorship therefore rests on the index, not on the file (see problems).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yu Ma (per works.json; not in the file) · **Position in trajectory**: the multimodal health-AI programme (2019–2026 period). It reuses the HAIM-MIMIC-MM data set of Soenksen, Ma, …, Bertsimas (2022) (pp. 12, 29).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML AP}: Medical models are built for single diseases and single tasks, so they share no knowledge. Multitask work picks task combinations from prior medical knowledge (pp. 4–5). Entry: one framework spanning diagnoses, operations and phenotyping, and binary, multiclass, regression and clustering tasks. It adds an explainability score over the outcome space (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Frozen pretrained embeddings (ClinicalBERT for notes; Densenet121-res224-chex for images) feed, in turn, modality networks, a contrastive shared module, task networks and a cross-task attention layer (pp. 6–7). The query comes from task tokens, and the weights are softmax(I + α·M): the identity favours learning from the task itself, α (set to 0.1) controls exploration of other tasks (pp. 11, 15). Clustering is an autoencoder followed by K-means with 15 clusters (pp. 9–10). The loss is a weighted sum with all weights set to 1 (p. 12). The TIM score averages the gain to task i from adding task j over subsets of the other tasks (p. 19).
- **D3 Model and data**: HAIM-MIMIC-MM has 12,586 samples from 5,178 hospital stays, with 4 modalities and 11 sources (pp. 12–13). Tasks: 40 diagnoses grouped into 16 departments, 8-class chest pathology, length of stay, 48-h mortality, hospital-acquired infection, and 15 phenotypes (pp. 13–15). The data are split 80/20 at patient level, with 5-fold CV over batch size and learning rate (pp. 15–16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a for proofs. Task combinations come from a greedy forward search that keeps the top 3 and stops at size 3 (p. 16). The TIM sum over subsets is approximated by sampling subsets or capping their size (pp. 19–20). The TIM and attention formulas are garbled in the text layer (pp. 11, 19).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: The best multitask model is compared with the best single-task model on the same test set, with ±SD from 5 bootstraps of 90% of the test set (p. 16). Gains are 1–41.2% in diagnosis, 3.3–12.4% in operations and 62.7% in phenotyping (pp. 17–18): chest pathology AUROC 0.770 → 0.788, length-of-stay R² 0.563 → 0.581, silhouette 0.413 → 0.671 (Fig. 5, p. 19). The text does not say whether task combinations were chosen on validation or test scores (p. 16), and there are no external baselines, run times or deployment results (my reading).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The claimed contributions are the first such framework, TIM and the attention mechanism (pp. 2–3). The abstract calls the framework "production ready" (p. 1); the basis given is that it was "packaged and tested on standard PCs" (p. 22). The limitations section is concrete (p. 23): missing modalities and tasks; robustness to data perturbation (via DRO); task selection as a combinatorial problem (Pareto-optimal disease combinations); and a link to predict-then-optimize.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · contradiction ✗ (pp. 1, 16–19, 22–23): the paper scores predictions (AUROC, R², silhouette) against single-task models, not decisions against a hospital's current rule. Cost and operational gains are argued, not measured (pp. 1, 22). Decisions are left to future predict-then-optimize work (p. 23). This is the Research Taste warning sign "prediction accuracy with no decision value".
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 16, 23): the choice of which tasks to train jointly is made greedily (top 3, up to size 3). The authors themselves name it a nonlinear combinatorial problem for future work. It is the kind of greedy step Method 4 would replace by one joint optimization.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 5, 12, 29): the HAIM programme continues, with the same data asset and a student of the health lineage (Ma).
- New-pattern candidate: *explain the outcome space, not the features* (pp. 5, 19–20). The TIM score averages the marginal gain from adding a task over subsets of the other tasks, and is shown as an asymmetric task-by-task heatmap.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure task interaction as the average change in a source task's score when a target task is added, over subsets of the other tasks. Cap the cost by sampling subsets or limiting their size, and report the asymmetric matrix of ranks (pp. 19–20).
- Bias cross-task attention toward the task's own representation with softmax(I + α·M), and tune α as a single exploration knob (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "multitask training can improve the performance of single-task models if the tasks are judiciously chosen" (p. 3)
- "designing what tasks to include for a multi-disease setting is a nonlinear, combinatorial problem that can be challenged by the curse of dimensionality as the number of possible combinations explodes" (p. 23)

### S393 · The ROAD to precision medicine (npj Digital Medicine 7(1) 307, 2024, DOI 10.1038/s41746-024-01291-6; arXiv 2311.01681)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–23 (abstract, §§1–4, Algorithm 1, Tables 1–3; figures by caption only). References (pp. 23–26) were not read. The file is arXiv v1 of 3 November 2023, on a "Noname manuscript" journal template (p. 1). First-page check: title and authors Bertsimas, Koulouras and Margonis match (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Angelos Koulouras (MIT), Georgios Antonios Margonis (MIT and Memorial Sloan Kettering, corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the health programme of the 2019–2026 period, with a clinical partner. It follows an earlier GIST policy tree by the same group (Bertsimas et al. 2023, pp. 21–22) and uses Optimal Policy Trees (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA AP; ML, IO for matching}: Evidence-based medicine fits the average patient. Methods for heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE) meet four obstacles (pp. 2–3):
  - unobserved confounding in observational data;
  - outputs that are risk strata or feature importances, not patient subgroups;
  - validation by significance tests, which cannot show how many patients would be over- or under-treated;
  - one-variable-at-a-time subgroup analysis in RCTs.

  Entry: emulate an RCT within prognostic strata, correct for the residual confounding, then prescribe with trees (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: The R.O.A.D. pipeline (Algorithm 1, p. 9) runs in four stages.
  - Stratify and match: train a baseline-risk model on untreated patients, bucket everyone by predicted risk, and match treated to untreated patients within each bucket by an MIO (pp. 5–6).
  - Correct: fit outcome models on each arm. If the treated arm's mean predicted risk exceeds the untreated arm's, read the excess as the minimum unobserved confounding. Then up-weight treated patients without recurrence (cost-sensitive weight ρ) until the sign flips (pp. 6–7).
  - Prescribe: train an OPT on the two predicted outcomes (p. 7).
  - Validate: score sensitivity and specificity in external cohorts (p. 8), and tune ρ on them (p. 10).
- **D3 Model and data**: The outcome is binary recurrence. The identifying assumption, stated by the authors, is that the treatment does not increase risk: imatinib "is not detrimental" (p. 4). The sensitivity metric assumes that the drug would have prevented the recurrences (p. 8). Data:
  - GIST: 536 MSK patients, 117 treated, matched down to 164 (82/82) (pp. 11–12); validation on 557 Polish registry patients from the pre-imatinib era (p. 14).
  - Sarcoma: an MSK RCT with 164 patients (77 brachytherapy), with baseline risk from the MSK nomogram; validation on 936 MSK patients without radiotherapy (pp. 16, 18).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The matching MIO (1) assigns each treated patient to a distinct untreated patient in the same bucket and minimizes the sum of ‖x_i − x_j‖² (p. 6). My reading: this is a bipartite assignment problem, solvable as an LP. The OPT objective is min over τ of Σ[1{τ=0}h₀ + 1{τ=1}h₁(·, ρ)] (pp. 7–8). There are no theorems. Absolute and relative risk reductions are read in each tree node (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: For GIST, ρ from 1.5 to 3.25 moves sensitivity over 84–94% and specificity over 77–52% (Table 2, p. 14). The chosen ρ = 2.25 gives 89% and 70% in the Polish cohort, NPV 93% (pp. 14–15), against 82% sensitivity for the SSG XVIII/AIO trial criteria and 63% specificity for PERSIST-5 at similar sensitivity (p. 15). For sarcoma: 90% and 14%, sparing about 15% of patients from radiotherapy (pp. 18–19). Results are stable across tree hyperparameters (pp. 13, 20), and the trees repeat 98.7% of the earlier OPT's recommendations to treat (p. 22). My reading of pp. 13–14: ρ was tuned "in a validation cohort" and the final figures are reported in that same cohort, with no separate test cohort.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a method and two clinical demonstrations. The claims are large: the framework "has the potential to revolutionize medical research" (p. 4). Limits are stated inside the text:
  - residual unobserved confounding may remain after weighting (pp. 7, 20);
  - the variables the trees use may be surrogates, not causes (p. 21);
  - without rebalancing, the trees found no subgroups in the RCT data (p. 22).

  There is no limitations section, and no future work is listed (pp. 20–23).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 8, 14–15, 18–19): recommendations, not predictions, are scored, in external cohorts and against the current rule: trial eligibility criteria for GIST, radiotherapy for all for sarcoma. The output is a tree clinicians can read (Research Taste, mark 5). The caveat is the reuse of the tuning cohort noted in D5.
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 10, 13–14): one knob, ρ, traces a sensitivity–specificity curve (Table 2). The authors print why they stop at ρ = 2.25: they accepted 6% less specificity for 4% more sensitivity, but would not give up a further 18% for 5%. They then hand the choice of metric to the end user (p. 14).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 5–6): an MIO appears only as the matching step, cited to the IO textbook (Bertsimas & Weismantel 2005). ML models and OPTs carry the work, not a formulation in the solver class.
- New-pattern candidate: *calibrate unobserved confounding with a known sign* (pp. 6–7, 13, 20). Because the treatment is known not to harm, the treated-minus-untreated gap in predicted risk is a lower bound on confounding, and a loss weight cancels it.
- New-pattern candidate: *validate prescriptions in a never-treated external cohort* (pp. 8, 14, 18). Sensitivity and specificity do not depend on prevalence, and a cohort from before the treatment existed shows the disease's natural course.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Match treated to untreated patients separately within strata of predicted baseline risk, so that the matching balances prognosis and not only raw covariates (pp. 5–6).
- When the direction of a treatment's effect is known, fit outcome models per arm on the matched cohort. Raise the weight ρ on treated non-events until the arm means cross, then tune ρ further on validation sensitivity and specificity (pp. 6–7, 10).
- Before any subgroup analysis of an RCT, stratify by baseline risk to detect imbalance within strata, and rebalance by oversampling. Here the trees found nothing without rebalancing (pp. 16, 22).

**Quotes**:
- "the essence of precision medicine is to find the best match between the available treatments and patient subgroups and not the discovery of causal relationships" (p. 21)
- "we attempted to train counterfactual models and OPTs despite the distribution imbalances, but the trees could not identify any patient subgroups with HTE" (p. 22)

### S401 · Towards stable machine learning model retraining via slowly varying sequences (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2403.19871)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–11 (abstract, §§1–6, Tables 1–3, Figure 1) and Appendices A–D (pp. 14–21; the figures in D by caption only). References (pp. 12–13) were consulted for the self-citations. The file is arXiv v5 of 4 February 2025 (p. 1). First-page check: title and authors Bertsimas, Digalakis Jr, Ma and Paschalidis match (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Vassilis Digalakis Jr (HEC Paris), Yu Ma (MIT), Phevos Paschalidis (Harvard) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: continues the group's stability line, from stable trees (Bertsimas & Digalakis 2023) to slowly varying regression under sparsity (OR 2024) (p. 12). This paper carries it to the retraining of production ML in a hospital (p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML DA; IO as formulation; AP health}: Retraining optimizes predictive power greedily at each batch. The resulting shifts in model structure and insights erode the trust of expert users (pp. 1–2). Related work decides when to retrain, not how to keep successive models consistent (p. 2). Entry: choose the whole sequence of models jointly, trading accuracy against stability (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Problem (1) minimizes the summed distances d(f_b, f_{b+1}) between consecutive models, subject to L̂_P(f_b) ≤ (1 + α)·best_b in each batch (pp. 3–4). This ε-constraint form is chosen to control *the price of stability* (p. 4). In practice each batch has a finite set of pre-trained, bootstrapped candidates. The selection MIO (5) has products of binaries, but Theorem 3.1 reduces it to a shortest path in a layered graph (p. 5). To retrain, build a new full sequence and deploy its last model; the paper calls this "stability by process" (pp. 3, 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: Nested batches D_1 ⊆ … ⊆ D_B and a bounded loss. The authors assume uniform generalization bounds (Assumption 2.1) and a stability loss that does not depend on the data (Remark 2.2) (p. 3). Distances (p. 8):
  - ‖β_i − β_j‖² for regressions;
  - optimal matching of tree paths, solved as an LP;
  - spectral distances for networks;
  - distances between feature importances or SHAP values.

  Data (p. 9):
  - a Connecticut hospital system: 168,815 patients and 865,954 patient-days, with mortality-or-hospice prediction scored by AUC;
  - Yearbook photos (37,921, 1930–2013) as FaceNet embeddings;
  - HuffPost headlines (about 200,000, 2012–2018) as text embeddings.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Solutions of (1) are weakly Pareto optimal, unique ones Pareto optimal, and complementary problems certify Pareto optimality (Theorems 2.4–2.6, p. 4; proofs p. 14). A Pareto excess-risk bound follows from Súkeník & Lampert (Lemma 2.7, p. 4), with a linear-regression instance (eq. 4, p. 5). The shortest-path reduction deletes candidates outside the tolerance (p. 14). Given a common initial model, inter-sequence stability is at most ε + ε′ by the triangle inequality (Theorem 3.4, pp. 7, 14). As printed, the bound constants in eqs. (3)–(4) (p. 5) and (7)–(8) (p. 14) differ (my reading).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: On synthetic linear regression, the restricted formulation (10 or 100 candidates per batch) is 1–3% behind the full frontier for α ≤ 0.03 and fails at larger α (pp. 5–7). The main comparison runs LogReg, CART, XGBoost and an MLP at α ∈ {0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1} against a greedy best-validation sequence, scored by AUC on a held-out final period (Python 3.7, Xeon E5-2690, p. 10); headline: 2% less accuracy for 30% more stability (p. 10). The tables print bad cases the text summarizes away. The text says realized deterioration is "always significantly smaller" than the tolerance, yet Table 2 shows 15.01% for the MLP on Healthcare at α = 0.1, and in Table 3 SVML's first inter-sequence loss is worse than greedy's (0.561 vs 0.476) (pp. 10–11). My reading: the healthcare test period (September 2021 – December 2022) runs past the stated data end (May 2022) (p. 9), no run times are reported, and the claimed deployment (pp. 1–2, 9) has no production outcomes.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a framework, guarantees, a polynomial algorithm and three case studies (pp. 1–2). Limits: the restricted formulation misses Pareto-optimal solutions at higher tolerances (p. 7), and distances between networks of different architectures are left open (p. 8). Future work: anticipated, quantifiable distribution shifts and explicit retraining costs (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3–4, 6, 10–11): the paper names the property to price (*the price of stability*, p. 4) and gives it one knob, the multiplicative tolerance α. It proves Pareto properties and publishes the curve (Figure 1, Tables 1–2). The bad cases are in the tables, although the text summarizes them away.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 5, 14): a class-keeping device. A selection MIO with products of binaries is solved exactly as a shortest path, which gives a polynomial certificate on the restricted problem.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 9–11): the baseline is the owner's current rule. The hospital retrains greedily, uses XGBoost in production, and works on a 6-month window, to be shortened to 3–4 months. But the scores are AUC and model distance, not decisions, and the claimed deployment has no reported outcome.
- New-pattern candidate: *Pareto refinement of an optimal set* (p. 4). The ε-constraint form, plus uniqueness or complementary problems, yields Pareto-optimal sequences (as in S348).
- New-pattern candidate: *optimize the whole sequence, deploy the last* (pp. 3, 7). Stability comes from re-solving the whole history, not from a proximal step away from the deployed model.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Pre-train a pool of bootstrapped candidates for each period, then pick one per period by a shortest path over a layered graph. Edge weights are model distances; candidates outside the accuracy tolerance are deleted (Theorem 3.1, pp. 5, 14).
- State the tolerance as a multiplicative factor on each period's best loss. Vary it to recover the frontier, and certify Pareto optimality by uniqueness or by complementary problems (pp. 4–6).
- Check that stability in the distance you optimized (XGBoost Gain importance) carries over to an independent one (SHAP) (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "Our approach thus relies on imposing stability by process." (p. 3)
- "On average, across all datasets and model types, a 2% reduction in (realized) predictive power results in a 30% improvement in model stability." (p. 10)

### S477 · Deep Trees for (Un)structured Data: Tractability, Performance, and Interpretability (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2410.21595)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–27 (abstract, §§1–6, Algorithms 1–3, Tables 1–16, declarations and author contributions; figures by caption only). References (pp. 27–28) were consulted for the self-citations. The file is arXiv v1 of 28 October 2024 (p. 1). First-page check: title and authors Bertsimas, Everest, Gu, Peroni and Stoumpou match (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Lisa Everest, Jiayi Gu, Matthew Peroni, Vasiliki Stoumpou (MIT ORC) (p. 1). By the contribution statement, Bertsimas "conceived, supported, and supervised the presented work and edited the paper", and Peroni developed DeepTree (p. 27). · **Position in trajectory**: the Optimal Trees programme (SW4: OCT, hyperplane trees, the 2019 book), moved to soft trees trained by gradient methods, for images (pp. 2–3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML HS}: Trees are interpretable but not expressive enough for nonlinear or unstructured data, while deep networks remain black boxes (pp. 1–2). The paper presents gradient training of its trees as more efficient than Optimal Classification Trees, and as scaling to greater depth and larger feature spaces (p. 2). Entry: soft trees with richer split functions, plus a way of growing them that scales (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: A Generalized Soft Tree (GST) routes samples through sigmoid gates and has linear leaves (eq. 3, p. 4). A split function is either a hyperplane or a single layer of convolution, ReLU, max-pooling and a linear map (pp. 5–7). Two penalties shape the routing: a split penalty spreads samples evenly over leaves, and a sample penalty concentrates each sample on few leaves (pp. 4–5). DeepTree grows a full tree step by step (pp. 7–9):
  - it splits the eligible leaf with the largest leaf loss;
  - it trains the new depth-1 subtree with the rest of the tree frozen, as a warm start;
  - it retrains the whole tree every few steps.
- **D3 Model and data**: Classification with cross-entropy (MSE for regression) (p. 4). Hyperparameters are set by Bayesian optimization with cross-validation (pp. 10–11). Data (pp. 11–18):
  - MIMIC-IV mortality: 70,887 ICU patients, 14 risk factors (p. 11);
  - MNIST and Fashion-MNIST;
  - CIFAR-10;
  - Celeb-A gender: 60,000 balanced images, downsampled to 109 × 89.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a for theorems. The case for the greedy split rule is twofold (p. 8). Computationally, trying every leaf would mean training hundreds to thousands of depth-1 subtrees per step, at O(1) s each. On risk, linear leaves let a new split reproduce its parent leaf exactly, so a poorly chosen leaf is unlikely to cause overfitting.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Results average 5 trees per depth from 4 to 10, and grown trees add 8–32 leaves (pp. 11–12). The best GST on validation beats CART, RF and XGBoost, tuned by CV or grid search, on MNIST (98.87 vs 97.74), Fashion-MNIST (92.21 vs 90.81), CIFAR-10 (68.64 vs 55.41) and Celeb-A (97.00 vs 96.45) (pp. 13–19). It loses on MIMIC-IV (74.00 vs RF 74.70 and XGBoost 75.06, Table 4, p. 12) and stays far from the cited 99.5% on CIFAR-10 (pp. 15, 17). Interpretability is measured by a (k, α)-fraction; with the penalty, accuracy falls at depth 8 (67.54 → 66.14, p. 21), and the Celeb-A nodes shown were chosen "after inspection" (p. 22). No run times are reported (my reading); code is available on request (p. 27).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a model class, a growing algorithm, regularizers and three interpretability tools (pp. 2, 26). The limits are stated plainly: the trees are not as interpretable as traditional trees (p. 26), they struggle with shapes and patterns, which explains the gap on CIFAR-10 (p. 25), and the sample penalty's benefit fades with depth (p. 20). Future work: combine the regularizer with DeepTree for more interpretable GSTs (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 7–9, 11–19): the exact MIO tree with a certificate gives way to first-order training and a greedy split rule that "may not be optimal" (p. 8). The claim changes to match: *tractable*, not *optimal*. This is the path of Heuristic 3 and Tension T1. Step 5 survives: the trees duel the incumbents CART, RF and XGBoost at their tuned best, and the MIMIC-IV loss is printed.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 27): a new cohort extends the Optimal Trees programme ([BD17], [BD19], [BDW21]). It builds on OCT, on the 2019 book's equivalence between hyperplane trees and networks, and on trees with polynomial leaves.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 19–21): one interpretability property, leaf-weight concentration, gets a knob (α_S) and a metric, and its accuracy cost is reported by depth. There is no curve and no bound.
- New-pattern candidate: *first-order surrogate for an MIO-born model class* (pp. 2–4, 7–9). A tree class born as an MIO (OCT) is relaxed to sigmoid routing and trained by backpropagation, which takes it to images and to depth 10. S331's sigmoid ℓ0 penalty is the same move.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Grow a soft tree from a shallow full tree (Algorithms 1–3, pp. 7–9). Among leaves with enough weight, split the one with the largest unnormalized leaf loss. Train the new depth-1 subtree with the rest of the tree frozen, insert it, and retrain everything every few steps.
- Measure how interpretable soft routing is with a (k, α)-fraction: the share of samples whose top k leaves carry at least α of their weight. Report it next to accuracy, with and without the penalty (pp. 19–21).
- To show where a leaf budget is best spent, compare grown shallow trees with full deeper trees at matched leaf counts (Figs. 1–2, pp. 15, 17).

**Quotes**:
- "While this approach to selecting the leaf to split on may not be optimal" (p. 8)
- "highlighting how they struggle to learn more abstract concepts, such as shapes or patterns, which likely explains the gap in performance between these trees and deep neural networks on challenging image datasets like CIFAR-10" (p. 25)
