### S008 · From predictive to prescriptive analytics (Management Science 2020, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2018.3253; arXiv 1402.5481)
Read level: **full** (arXiv v4): main text pp. 1–32 (Sections 1–7) in full; references pp. 32–36 skimmed; supplement Section 8 (ERM, pp. 37–42) in full; Sections 9–12 (mixing extensions and proofs, pp. 42–53) skimmed for the proof structure; Sections 13–14 (experiment parameters, feature construction, pp. 53–57) read. Figures are extracted as marker soup, so values are taken from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Nathan Kallus (Cornell, p. 1); an NSF Graduate Research Fellowship is acknowledged (p. 32), presumably Kallus's [inferred] · **Position in trajectory**: the anchor of SW5 and of Method 5 in SKILL.md; DA programme. This file is v4; that v1–v2 were Kallus's single-author preprint comes from SKILL.md SW5 and is not visible in the file.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA ML AP}: In OR/MS applications the data are "predominantly" observations of auxiliary covariates X, not of the uncertain quantity Y (abstract, p. 1). Stochastic optimization and SAA use iid data on Y only, and ML predicts Y from X but does not address optimal decisions (p. 2). The problem is conditional stochastic optimization, min over z of E[c(z;Y) | X = x], with the joint distribution unknown and only data S_N available (eq. 2, p. 2). The entry point is a stated gap: it "is not clear how to go from a good prediction to a good decision" (p. 2). Plugging in a point prediction ignores the residual uncertainty (eq. 10, p. 6), and the bootstrap uncertainty of the prediction is "the wrong uncertainty" (footnote 1, p. 7). Two extensions follow: decisions that change Y, as prices do (pp. 3–4, 12–15), and censored demand (pp. 24–25). The motivating application is replenishment at a media distributor (pp. 4, 23).
- **D2 Key idea**: Read each local-learning predictor as a weight function w_{N,i}(x) over past observations and solve the re-weighted SAA, min over z of Σ_i w_{N,i}(x) c(z; y_i) (eq. 3, p. 3; eq. 11, p. 10). The weight families (pp. 10–12):
  - kNN (eq. 12), Nadaraya–Watson and recursive kernels (eqs. 13–14);
  - local linear weights truncated at zero (LOESS*, eq. 16), so that the problem stays convex;
  - CART leaf co-membership (eq. 17), which compiles the prescription into a tree "with the decisions that are truly decisions" (p. 12);
  - RF leaf co-membership averaged over the trees (eq. 18), the method chosen for the application (p. 12).
  - What is kept and what changes: the optimization problem differs from SAA "only in the weights" (p. 16); the output is a decision, not a prediction. When decisions affect Y, the historical decision z1 is added to the features and, under ignorability, the problem becomes a conditional one with no counterfactuals (Theorem 1, eq. 20, p. 14). A unitless metric P, the coefficient of prescriptiveness, measures how far a policy moves from the data-poor SAA decision toward perfect foresight (eq. 22, p. 21). ERM over linear decision rules is developed as a side route and judged limited for constrained problems (pp. 3, 37–38).
- **D3 Model and data**: Decision z in Z ⊂ R^dz, cost c(z; y), covariates X; data (x_i, y_i), or (x_i, y_i, z_i) when decisions affect Y (pp. 1–3, 13). Assumptions adopted:
  - existence, equicontinuity of c in z, and Z closed and either bounded or with a coercivity condition (Assumptions 3–5, pp. 18–19);
  - iid sampling in the main text, called "a strong assumption" that is "often only a modeling approximation"; α-, β- and ρ-mixing processes (ARMA, GARCH, Markov chains) are covered in supplement Section 9 (pp. 18, 42–44);
  - for decision-dependent uncertainty, a decomposition of z and ignorability (Assumptions 1–2, pp. 13–14), the latter argued to be "particularly defensible" because historical managerial decisions were made from observable quantities (p. 14);
  - for censoring, demand and censoring threshold conditionally independent given X, no shared atoms, bounded costs (Theorem 11, p. 25).
  - Real data: four years of sales and inventory records of a vendor that ships about 1 billion units a year to over 50,000 retailers, restricted to video media at European retailers (pp. 4, 23, 25), plus harvested IMDb, Rotten Tomatoes and Google Trends data and Google geocoding (pp. 26–28); d_x = 91 features (p. 28). Synthetic data: a two-stage shipment-planning LP whose covariates follow a 3-dimensional ARMA(2,2) process and whose demands follow a factor model (pp. 5–7, 53–54).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Proof devices:
  - Tractability (Theorem 2, p. 16; proof p. 52): with nonnegative weights, convex Z and c convex in z, an ε-optimal solution takes time polynomial in the effective sample size N0 (the number of positive weights), via epigraph variables, subgradient cuts and the ellipsoid method (Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver).
  - When decisions affect Y the problem is non-convex in general: solve once per value of a discrete z1 (Theorem 3, p. 17), or discretize at a cost exponential in d_z1 (Theorem 4, p. 17; ball-cover proof, p. 52); with CART weights an exact procedure enumerates the leaves reachable from x (p. 17).
  - Asymptotic optimality and consistency for the kNN, kernel, recursive-kernel, local-linear and LOESS* weights (Theorems 5–9, p. 19); the decision-dependent version for kernel and local-linear weights (Theorem 10, p. 20). Proof skeleton (pp. 44–51): a.s. pointwise convergence of the weighted conditional cost for each fixed z, from universal strong laws (Walk 2010) or uniform kernel rates (Hansen 2008); extension to all z through a countable dense set, equicontinuity and weak convergence of the weighted conditional distribution (Lemma 7, p. 47); uniform convergence on compacts (Lemma 5, p. 45); eventual coercivity confines the argmins to a compact set, which yields convergence of values and of argmins (Lemma 6, pp. 45–47). LOESS* equals LOESS eventually because the weights become nonnegative (p. 50).
  - No result for CART and RF ("we do not have firm theoretical results", p. 19), although RF is the method chosen for, and applied in, the real case (pp. 12, 29).
  - Censoring: a conditional Kaplan–Meier transformation of the weights (eq. 24, p. 24). Theorem 11 (p. 25) covers weights (12)–(16), not RF; its proof uses Beran (1981) and the portmanteau lemma (p. 51).
  - ERM side (pp. 37–42): tractability (Theorem 12, p. 38); out-of-sample bounds through a multivariate Rademacher complexity and a vector-valued extension of the Ledoux–Talagrand comparison lemma (Theorem 13, Lemma 1, pp. 39–40); complexity bounds for norm-ball classes (Lemmas 2–3, p. 41).
  - Duality read for structure: in the capacitated problem (23), Lagrangian duality turns capacity into a per-unit cost, so each order is a conditional quantile; but the multiplier depends on all conditional distributions at once, which is why quantile regression alone does not solve it (p. 24).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Synthetic (pp. 6–7, 15–16, 22–23, 54–55): true risk against N and against d_x with added noise dimensions. SAA beats the covariate methods for N below about 64; the RF prescription is no worse than SAA at small N and better at large N; CART and RF are largely unaffected by irrelevant dimensions (p. 7). With pricing, SAA drives the price to infinity and earns 0, and the point-prediction decision plateaus (p. 15). P approaches 0.46 in the example (average R² = 0.16, p. 22); as R² is varied from 0 to 1, the point-prediction decision is very negative at R² = 0 and beats most methods at R² = 1 (pp. 22–23). Inconsistencies left in v4: §1.1 calls the instance 5 warehouses (p. 6), the supplement 4 (p. 53; Fig. 11, p. 54); the text speaks of "both" examples (pp. 6–7) and of two different problems (p. 12) while one example is shown.
  - Real case (pp. 28–31): an RF of 500 trees, with separate forests for weeks 1–35 on the shelf and one for the steady state (pp. 28–29). Prediction R² = 0.67 for new releases, compared with a same-as-last-week heuristic (p. 29). Rolling backtest over 150 weeks (19 Dec 2011–9 Nov 2014), retraining each week on past data only (p. 30). Because sales are censored, scoring uses capacities at a quarter of nominal, "necessary just for a counterfactual scoring" (p. 30).
  - Baselines: perfect foresight; SAA++, SAA made specific to weeks on the market "to make it a fair comparison"; an RF point-prediction policy scaled to capacity (p. 31). Result: P = 0.88 averaged over just under 20,000 locations; P of 0.85–0.90 in four cities against 0.40–0.57 for the point-prediction policy, so that "we nearly double the improvement"; not better on every day (Fig. 9, pp. 30–31); P across locations in Fig. 10 (p. 30).
  - Not reported: solvers, run times or computing environment; the vendor's own replenishment rule as a baseline; any field deployment.
- **D6 Positioning**: Against data-driven OR (SAA, stochastic approximation, robust SAA, data-driven DRO, RO and data-driven RO, the exploration–exploitation literature), all of which assume iid data on Y only (pp. 7–8). Against ML, which predicts but does not decide (p. 2). ERM decision rules (Rudin–Vahn 2014; Kao et al. 2009) are generalized to multivariate decisions but judged inapplicable to constrained ones (pp. 8–9, 38). Kernel weighting for conditional stochastic optimization (Hanasusanto–Kuhn 2013; Hannah et al. 2010) is credited but said to lack the link to the ML methods used in practice and a rigorous asymptotic analysis (p. 9). Two self-citations are listed as submitted (data-driven RO, robust SAA) and a third has no venue (pricing from observational data) (p. 33). Predecessors are restated and extended, not attacked.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract opens with the ML–OR/MS combination and a "departure" from prior data-driven optimization "reflecting our practical experience" (p. 1), and ends on one headline number (88%), in the shape of Heuristic 9. Contributions are lettered a)–g) (pp. 3–4); the synthetic duel sits in §1.1, before the literature review (pp. 5–7); P is introduced by analogy with R² (p. 21). Hedging on P: "(eventually) bounded between 0 (not prescriptive) and 1" in the introduction (p. 4), "bounded above by 1" and possibly negative in the body (pp. 21–22). Headline against caveat (Tension T6): the abstract says the methods are proved "asymptotically optimal under mild conditions" (p. 1) and the one-paragraph conclusion calls the approach "asymptotically optimal" (p. 32), while the body excludes CART and RF, the methods actually used (p. 19). The vendor stays anonymous and some figures are on relative scales (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a framework (ML-derived weights), tractability and asymptotic theory for several weight families, a decision metric P and one real application. Limits stated in the body: no theory for CART and RF (p. 19); the discretization for decision-dependent uncertainty works "only for small dz1" (p. 17); ERM rules cannot handle constraints (p. 38); ignorability is needed (p. 14); the censored-scoring workaround (p. 30). No future directions: the conclusion claims only "a potential for substantial impact" (p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 10–12, 21, 29, 31). Decisions, not predictions, are scored (P, p. 21), and the prediction R² of 0.67 is called secondary to "the performance of the prescription" (p. 29); learning happens inside the decision through ML weights (pp. 10–12); the naive rule is strengthened before the duel (SAA++, p. 31).
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 30–31). The baseline is a constructed data-poor rule, not the vendor's current replenishment rule, and the evaluation is a retrospective backtest of "what we would have done" (p. 30), not a field test.
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 16–17, 24). The class-keeping device is the weighting: the problem inherits SAA's complexity (Theorem 2, p. 16); the boundary is printed (decision-dependent uncertainty is non-convex; exponential discretization, p. 17); Lagrangian duality explains the coupling in the real problem (p. 24).
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (p. 2). The why-now argument is the explosion of data and advances in ML, stated without any figure.
- Heuristic 6: evidence (pp. 26–28, 56–57): the data asset is built (harvested IMDb, Rotten Tomatoes and Google Trends data; clustered plots and actor communities as features).
- New-pattern candidate: **synthetic duel of both schools up front**: a known-truth example in §1.1 where both incumbent routes (SAA and point prediction) plateau above the full-information optimum while the proposal converges (pp. 5–7; again with pricing, p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: **an identification assumption defended by the decision process that generated the data** (ignorability, p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To turn a predictive method into a prescriptive one, extract its implicit conditional distribution as weights on past observations and keep the downstream optimization problem unchanged (eqs. 3, 12–18, pp. 3, 10–12); truncate negative weights (LOESS*) to keep convexity without losing the asymptotics (p. 11).
- When the decision moves the uncertainty (prices), add the historical decision as a feature and argue ignorability from how past decisions were made (pp. 13–14); with tree weights, enumerate the leaves reachable from x (p. 17).
- For censored demand, transform the weights by a conditional Kaplan–Meier estimator (eq. 24, p. 24); when scoring on censored history, shrink capacities so censoring rarely binds, and say that this is for scoring only (p. 30).
- Report a unitless decision metric, P = 1 − (R(policy) − R(perfect foresight)) / (R(SAA) − R(perfect foresight)) on held-out data, and show its distribution across sites (eq. 22, p. 21; Fig. 10, p. 30).
- Evaluate with a rolling origin: retrain each period on the past only and score the next period (p. 30); strengthen the naive baseline before the duel (SAA++, p. 31).
- Asymptotic optimality of a data-driven decision rule: pointwise convergence of the estimated conditional cost, then a dense countable set plus equicontinuity, then uniform convergence on compacts, then coercivity to trap the argmins (Lemmas 5–7, pp. 45–47).

- "Considering the uncertainty associated with new releases, we feel that this is a positive result, but at the same time what truly matters is the performance of the prescription in the problem." (p. 29)

### S054 · COVID-19 mortality risk assessment: An international multi-center study (PLoS ONE 2020, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0243262)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13 (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, limitations, conclusions, acknowledgments, author contributions, references). The supporting information (S1 File: site-level tables, ROC curves, and S2–S6 Text on imputation, XGBoost training, other ML methods, Optuna tuning and SHAP) is not in the file: not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: 18 named authors including Bertsimas, plus the Hellenic COVID-19 Study Group: Mingardi, Orfanoudaki, Stellato and Wiberg ("contributed equally"), Lukin, Nohadani (Benefits Science Technologies), Dal Bello (MIT Physics of Living Systems) and partners at the Seville university hospital, Hartford HealthCare, HM Hospitals and ASST Cremona (p. 1); Bertsimas is first and corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the 2019–2026 health-care period; part of the group's COVID-19 work, with the tool hosted at covidanalytics.io (received 20 July, published 9 December 2020, p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP ML}: Clinical need: identify hospitalized COVID-19 patients at high risk of death early, to triage and allocate resources (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Gap: earlier ML scores were built mostly on Chinese hospitals, while the virus may have mutated; the one large international model "lacks comprehensive patient data" (p. 2). The problem is stated as binary classification of in-hospital death from data at admission (p. 4). Entry point: partner data from hospitals in Italy, Spain, Greece and the US during the first wave (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea**: An XGBoost classifier, "a leading ML method" (p. 2), on 22 admission features; SHAP for global and per-patient explanations (pp. 4, 6–9); an online calculator (p. 8). There is no optimization model, and the group's own optimal trees do not appear in the paper body; other ML methods are compared only in the supplement (S3 Table, S4 Text, p. 4; not read). - **D3 Model and data**: 3,927 patients from six centers comprising 33 hospitals (abstract, p. 1). Derivation: 3,062 (Cremona 1,441, HM Hospitals 1,390, Hartford affiliates 231); validation: 865 (Seville 219, Hellenic CSG 323, Hartford Hospital 323) (p. 5). Study dates between 1 February and 15 May (Table 1, p. 3). - **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for optimization. Analysis devices: an 85/15 train–test split with the same mortality prevalence; seven hyperparameters tuned by K-fold cross-validated AUC with Optuna (p. 4); SHAP values that sum to the patient's risk (p. 6); per-cohort thresholds at 80% sensitivity (p. 5); 95% confidence intervals (p. 5); survivor vs non-survivor comparisons by two-sided t-tests, binary variables included (Table 2 note, p. 4); Python 3.7 (p. 4).

Prospective validation is under way at Cremona (emergency room) and Seville (EHR integration) (p. 10); no outcome of that use is reported.

### S061 · Sparse high-dimensional regression: Exact scalable algorithms and phase transitions (The Annals of Statistics 2020, DOI 10.1214/18-aos1804; arXiv 1709.10029)
Read level: **full** (arXiv v1, 28 Sep 2017): pp. 1–22, all sections; references skimmed. Figure axes are extracted as marker soup, so values come from captions, Tables 1–3 and the prose. The published AoS version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Bart Van Parys (MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1), supported by a Swiss National Science Foundation Early Post.Mobility fellowship (p. 20) · **Position in trajectory**: the ML programme's next step after best subset selection (AoS 2016, SW4), which it extends from n and p in the 1000s to the 100,000s (pp. 2, 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO; CP for the relaxation}: Problem (1): least squares with a Tikhonov (ridge) term and the constraint ‖w‖₀ ≤ k (p. 1). Entries:
  - The exact problem is NP-hard and was "branded hopeless" for decades, so the literature turned to heuristics (iterative thresholding, Lasso, Elastic Net) whose recovery theory makes strong assumptions on the data (pp. 2–3).
  - The group's own big-M MIO (Bertsimas–King–Mazumder 2016) reached n and p in the 1000s, but the data-dependent constant M "affects the strength" of the formulation, and building the model becomes burdensome as p grows (pp. 2–3).
  - Phase transitions of exact sparse regression could not be checked empirically for lack of exact scalable algorithms (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea**: Take the dual (kernel) view. Eliminate w in closed form, so that (1) becomes a pure binary problem in the support s whose objective is convex in s, built from "micro kernels" K_j = X_j X_jᵀ (Theorem 1, eqs. 10–11, p. 6); no big-M constant is needed (p. 4). Solve it by outer approximation (Duran–Grossmann) inside a single branch-and-bound tree with lazy-constraint callbacks (pp. 9–10), with cuts evaluated through the matrix inversion lemma (pp. 10–11) and a warm start from an SOCP relaxation (pp. 8–9, 11–12). What is kept: the exact ℓ0 problem and a commercial MIO solver (Gurobi) as the engine. What changes: the variables (support only), the formulation (convex integer instead of big-M), the algorithm (tailored cutting planes). Then the exact solver is used as an instrument to observe phase transitions in accuracy, false alarms and solve time (pp. 4–5, 14–18). Nonlinear features enter as kernels of transformed columns (Corollary 1, p. 19).
- **D3 Model and data**: Only synthetic data. Y = X w_true + E; rows of X iid N(0, Σ) with Σ_ij = ρ^|i−j|; k nonzero coefficients drawn from ±1; Gaussian noise scaled to a target √SNR (p. 12). Nominal √SNR = 20; k ∈ {10, 15, 20}, p ∈ {5,000, 10,000, 15,000}, √SNR ∈ {3, 7, 20} (Table 2, p. 16); ρ = 0.1 in the timing table (p. 13), ρ = 0 for the transitions (p. 14). Assumptions admitted for convenience: k is known in the timing and accuracy experiments, so that accuracy and false alarms can be studied apart (p. 13); cross-validation over k = 1–20 only in the false-alarm experiment (p. 16); γ and k "must ultimately be determined from the data" (p. 1). The fast cut evaluation relies on n much larger than k, argued to be a statistical necessity (p. 11). The quoted Gamarnik–Zadik threshold assumes binary coefficients, k → ∞ and σ²/k → 0 (Theorem 5, p. 14). Every regime tested has √SNR ≥ 3 (Table 2, p. 16), so the low-SNR check that SKILL.md lists among Method 4's critic-informed checks is not addressed in this version.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Proof devices:
  - Lemma 1 (pp. 5–6): the ridge loss is c = ½ Yᵀ(I + γXXᵀ)⁻¹Y, by the normal equations and the matrix inversion lemma, and is SDP-representable in the kernel matrix by a Schur complement.
  - Theorem 1 (p. 6): split (1) into an outer problem over the support and an inner ridge problem solved in closed form; the result is a convex integer problem. The penalized variant follows the same way (eq. 13, p. 7).
  - Dual reading: Vapnik's dual of ridge regression (Theorem 2, p. 7) shows the formulation is "a subset selection problem in the space of kernels instead of regressors" (p. 8).
  - Theorem 3 (pp. 8–9): the continuous relaxation, a large SDO if done naively, becomes a small SOCP by swapping min and max (Sion's theorem) and writing the sum of the k largest terms α*ᵀK_jα* by LP duality.
  - Algorithm 1 and Theorem 4 (pp. 9–10): outer approximation terminates finitely with the exact solution (a corollary of Fletcher–Leyffer); in the worst case exponentially many cuts are needed, each requiring an MIO solve (p. 10).
  - Cut and gradient evaluation in O(k³ + nk) instead of O(n³), through the k × k capacitance matrix (eq. 20, Algorithm 2, pp. 10–11).
  - Warm start: the k largest entries of α*ᵀK_jα* from the SOCP solution (eq. 21, pp. 11–12); Lasso or the 2016 first-order heuristic "could have been used equally well" (p. 12).
  - No theory for the observed speed: "a hard theoretical picture is not yet available" (pp. 13–14).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia, Gurobi 6.5, an Intel Xeon E5-2690 at 2.90 GHz under CentOS 6.7 (p. 12).
  - Scalability (Table 1, p. 13): n ∈ {10k, 20k, 100k}, p ∈ {50k, 100k, 200k}, k ∈ {10, 20, 30}, √SNR = 20, ρ = 0.1. Exact solve times of 15.6–528.7 s against 69.5–988.0 s for the Lasso (GLMNet, R package 1.9-5, p. 21), exact faster in every cell; the n = 100k, p = 200k cells are NA because building X and Y threw a memory exception (p. 13). Accuracy is not reported because both methods recover the support in this regime (p. 13). For comparability the Lasso solution is the least regularized point on the path with exactly k nonzeros (p. 13), and the paper concedes that GLMNet computes a whole path (p. 13).
  - Phase transitions (Figure 1, pp. 14–16): p = 2,000, k = 10, √SNR = 20, 50 data sets, error bars of one standard deviation; full recovery at n_t = 120 for the exact method against about 180 for the Lasso (theory: n1 = 152); n0 ≈ 50 (p. 16). Time limit 15 min, after which "the best solution found up to that point" is taken (p. 14). Under cross-validated k, the Lasso keeps a high false-alarm rate (p. 16).
  - Parameter study (§4.4, pp. 16–18): 20 replications, limits of 10 min (Figs. 2–3) and 1 min (Fig. 4), again with best-found solutions; n/k drives the transition, p matters little, and lower SNR moves it later (p. 17).
  - Nonlinear illustration (pp. 19–20): 20 regressors × 8 transforms = 160 features, medians of 5 runs, cross-validation over k = 1–10 at about 10 s per k. With a = 0, (A%, F%) = (100, 0) from n = 200; with a = 1, F% stays at 17–28 (Table 3, p. 20).
  - Gaps at the time limit are never reported; no real data set is used.
- **D6 Positioning**: Respectful toward the convex-proxy literature ("a truly impressive amount of high-quality work", p. 2) while stressing its strong assumptions, poor support recovery and shrinkage bias (pp. 2–3). Claims two orders of magnitude over its own 2016 MIO and over leaps and bounds (pp. 4, 13). Imports the dual view from kernel learning (pp. 3, 7) and outer approximation from MINLP (Duran–Grossmann 1986; Fletcher–Leyffer 1994; pp. 9–10), which ties the work to the MINLP guest team's literature. Takes phase-transition context from Donoho–Tanner, Wainwright and Gamarnik–Zadik, the last "motivated by the results of the present paper" (pp. 3–4). Claims the complexity transition was not reported before, hedged that it "has been allude to before" (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract follows Heuristic 9: a "novel" reformulation, the scale (n and p "in the 100,000s", "two orders of magnitude"), a reversal of "traditional complexity theory", and a verdict that the exact approach is "a superior alternative" to heuristics (p. 1). The reversal sentence recurs almost verbatim in the introduction, §4.3, §4.5 and the conclusion (pp. 4–5, 16, 19, 20). Strong verbs: it "impeaches the primary selling point of heuristic approaches" (p. 3), a "potentially game changing speed up" (p. 13), "refute the widely held belief" (p. 13). Hedges: "provide evidence" (p. 1), "strong empirical evidence" (p. 4), the nonlinear method "serves only as an illustration" (p. 20). "Faster than Lasso" is in the abstract (p. 1); the admission about whole-path timing is in the body (p. 13): Tension T6. v1 typos (e.g. "statically meaningful", p. 4; "Off course", p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a formulation, an algorithm and a computational phenomenon; the statistical theory is borrowed (Theorem 5, p. 14). Limits stated: worst-case exponentially many cuts (p. 10); no explanation of the speed (pp. 13–14); a region n0 < n < n_t where the exact estimator is "statistically relevant but computationally not feasible" (p. 16); memory limits (p. 13); interactions of features are invisible to the nonlinear extension (p. 20). Future: nonlinear feature discovery as "a fierce and more disciplined competitor" to "black box" approaches such as neural networks (pp. 5, 20).

- New-pattern candidate: **eliminate the continuous part in closed form and cut on the binary support** (a big-M-free convex integer program solved by single-tree outer approximation; pp. 5–11).
- New-pattern candidate: **exact solver as a scientific instrument**: once the exact problem is solvable at scale, use it to observe phenomena heuristics cannot show, and map hardness against the statistical regime (solve time against n/k and SNR; pp. 4, 14–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If the continuous part of a mixed problem has a closed-form inner solution, substitute it to get a pure-binary convex objective and drop the big-M constants (Lemma 1, Theorem 1, pp. 5–6).
- Run outer approximation in one branch-and-bound tree with lazy-constraint callbacks rather than re-solving an MIO per cut (p. 10).
- Make each cut cheap: compute the loss and its gradient through the matrix inversion lemma in O(k³ + nk) (eq. 20, Algorithm 2, pp. 10–11).
- Warm-start from the continuous relaxation's dual: pick the k largest scores α*ᵀK_jα* (eq. 21, pp. 11–12); keep the relaxation small by an inner LP dual (sum of k largest) after a minimax swap (Theorem 3, pp. 8–9).
- Phase-transition protocol: known truth, accuracy A% and false-alarm F%, n varied with one parameter at a time moved from nominal values, 20–50 replications with error bars (pp. 12, 14–18).
- When timing against a path algorithm, choose a comparable path point (least regularized with exactly k nonzeros) and disclose the asymmetry (p. 13).
