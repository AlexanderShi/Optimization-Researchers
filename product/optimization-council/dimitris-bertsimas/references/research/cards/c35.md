### S683 · Detection and Localization of Subdural Hematoma Using Deep Learning on Computed Tomography (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2512.09393)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–9 of arXiv v1.
- All text read: abstract, "Research in context" box, methods, results, discussion, Tables 1–2, figure captions, contributors, declarations, data sharing, acknowledgments and the generative-AI statement. References (pp. 7–8) scanned for positioning.

- The supplement is not in the file: not read.
- Title and authors confirmed on p. 1; Bertsimas is last and corresponding author.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vasiliki Stoumpou (MIT ORC), Rohan Kumar (MIT ORC; Boston University), Bernard Burman (MIT), Diego Ojeda (UConn School of Medicine) and Tapan Mehta (UConn; Hartford HealthCare) (p. 1). Contributors' statement: concept and design by all six; data analysis and drafting by VS, RK and BB; supervision by TM and DB (p. 6). · **Position in trajectory**: The health-care-at-scale period. Hartford HealthCare supplies data, compute and clinician co-authors (pp. 1, 3, 7). The group's HAIM multimodal framework (ref. 19, with Bertsimas) is cited as the motivation for fusion (p. 2; ref. p. 8). Of this batch, the paper with the least optimization content.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP (health care) + ML; no optimization model}:
  - Entry: an application gap. SDH incidence is rising, and delayed intervention worsens outcomes (pp. 1–2).
  - The FDA-cleared commercial tools (Rapid SDH, Viz Subdural) fall short (p. 2):
    - they report detection, not segmentation accuracy;
    - they focus on bleeds above a volume threshold (about 1 mL);
    - they do not disclose models, training data or fusion strategies.
  - Academic SDH work is 2D, acute-only or small (U-Nets on fewer than 110 scans) (p. 2). ChatGPT-4V reaches about 60% detection accuracy (p. 3).
  - Problem as stated: a "multimodal" pipeline that detects and localizes SDH, is "transparent", and is evaluated at a scale of thousands of studies (abstract, p. 1; "Research in context", p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Three model families, fused by averaging predicted probabilities (pp. 3–4, Figure 1):
    - an XGBoost model on clinical variables;
    - an ensemble of 3D CNNs on CT volumes;
    - a 2D hybrid segmentation network (a U-Net-type branch with spatial and channel attention, plus a Swin Transformer branch), whose probability maps feed a second family of 3D CNNs.
  - Ensemble members are chosen by greedy forward selection on validation AUC, restarted from each of the top individual models (p. 5).
  - "Interpretability" means voxel-level probability maps, i.e. localization by a black-box ensemble, not an interpretable model form (pp. 1, 3, 5–6).
  - What changed vs the nearest work: tabular data fused with two imaging models, at more than 25,000 studies (p. 2). What did not: the components are standard deep-learning parts (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Hartford HealthCare: 15,183 adult patients, 2016–2023, one observation per non-contrast head CT (p. 3). Exclusions (age under 18, brain tumour, craniectomy for other reasons) leave 25,316 studies, 3,774 with confirmed SDH (p. 3). The abstract says 25,315 studies and 2015–2024 (p. 1).
  - Tabular features: demographics, anticoagulant and antiplatelet use, aPTT, INR, platelets and Charlson components. Each lab is the last value before imaging plus a two-year exponentially weighted average (p. 3).
  - Images: windowed to 0–130 HU, skull stripped by thresholding and connected components. Every study is cut to 15 slices (down-sampled, or central slices duplicated), a number said to balance information and computation (p. 3).
  - Segmentation labels: 335 studies annotated manually under physician supervision at 256×256; two random augmentations each give over 12,000 slices; 100 all-zero control studies are added (p. 4).
  - Split 64/16/20 at patient level, to prevent leakage (p. 3).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a: no optimization model and no proofs.
  - The one algorithmic device beyond training is the multi-start greedy forward selection of ensemble members (p. 5).
  - Operating thresholds come from Youden's J on the validation set (Table 1 caption, p. 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Test AUC (primary metric; Table 1, p. 6; p. 5):
    - tabular 0.751 [0.732–0.770];
    - CNN ensemble 0.922 [0.909–0.934];
    - Seg-CNN ensemble 0.926 [0.912–0.938];
    - overall ensemble 0.9407 [0.930–0.951], with sensitivity 0.815, specificity 0.947 and F1 0.774.
  - Brier score 0.050 for the ensemble vs 0.113 for the tabular model (Figure 2 legend, p. 6).
  - Segmentation (Table 2, p. 5): Dice 0.768 on the validation set, against 0.692 (vanilla Swin U-Net), 0.720 (frozen layers), 0.741 (attention U-Net) and 0.754 (single-output fusion). Retrained elsewhere: 0.92–0.93 on CVC-ClinicDB polyps, and 0.83 vs a U-Net's 0.76 on a Kaggle brain-tumour set (p. 5). No test-set Dice is reported.
  - Baselines are the authors' own components and architecture variants. Radiologists and the commercial tools are not run on the same scans; Rapid SDH enters only through its vendor-reported 92% sensitivity (p. 2).
  - No decision, triage-time or patient-outcome metric. The "time to surgery" gain is a projection (p. 6).
  - Successes only: Figure 3 shows "correctly segmented" slices (pp. 5, 9). The claim of validation "across the entire spectrum" of hematoma presentations (p. 6) has no size-stratified result in the file.
  - Internal inconsistencies [my reading]: the ensemble is "eight models" listed as 1 + 5 + 4 (p. 4), then "all ten models" (p. 5), then "all 9" (Table 1, p. 6). Table 1's CNN groups are "4-model" ensembles, but §2.2.5 lists five CNNs on CT (pp. 4, 6).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Against the commercial products on transparency and scope, not on accuracy (pp. 2, 6).
  - Against academic SDH work on modality and sample size (p. 2).
  - General-purpose vision-language models serve as a foil for domain-specific training (p. 3).
  - The only self-citation is HAIM (ref. 19, p. 8). No optimization literature is cited.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Clinical-journal format: a structured abstract with confidence intervals (p. 1) and a Lancet-style "Research in context" box (evidence before, added value, implications; p. 2).
  - Statements: contributors (p. 6); data sharing, competing interests (none) and generative AI (ChatGPT for grammar) (p. 7).
  - A "To our knowledge ... first" claim rests on scale (p. 2).
  - The limitations paragraph is specific: annotation volume, scanners and populations, external validation (p. 6).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution level: an application pipeline with empirical results; no optimization method or theory.
  - Limits (p. 6): few annotated scans; the need for diverse scanners and populations, for semi- or weakly supervised labelling, and for external validation across institutions.
  - Future: prospective, real-time deployment as decision support inside EMR systems such as EPIC (p. 6).
  - Data cannot leave the health system; all analyses ran in Hartford HealthCare's secure environment (p. 7).

**Method links**:

  - Steps 1 and 6 are present: a partner owns the decision (Hartford's clinicians), and deployment into the EMR is the stated next step (pp. 1, 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split at the patient level, not the study level, when patients contribute several scans, so that repeat studies cannot leak across training, validation and test (p. 3).

- When selecting ensemble members greedily, restart the forward selection from each of the top models rather than only the best, and report the selection order (p. 5).
- Report calibration (reliability curve and Brier score) beside AUC when clinicians will read the output as a probability, and fix operating thresholds on validation data (p. 6).

### S684 · Sparse Multiple Kernel Learning: Alternating Best Response and Semidefinite Relaxations (Transactions on Machine Learning Research 2025, arXiv 2511.21890)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–27 of arXiv v2 (the TMLR 11/2025 version).
- Body pp. 1–20 read in full.
- Appendix A (kernel families, p. 21) and Appendices B–D (proofs and the dual derivation, pp. 21–25) read in full.
- References pp. 25–27 scanned.
- Title and authors confirmed on p. 1.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Caio de Próspero Iglesias and Nicholas A. G. Johnson (both MIT) (p. 1); Bertsimas is listed first (alphabetical order). · **Position in trajectory**: A 2025 entry of the ML-under-a-modern-optimization-lens programme. Its template is an exact cardinality constraint plus an ℓ2 penalty "for robustness" (abstract, p. 1; "to encourage robustness", p. 2), backed by the group's regularization–robustification result (Bertsimas & Copenhaver 2018, p. 4). Reviewed on OpenReview (p. 1). The first author of S688 is the same student.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO + CP}:
  - Entry: the field's convex surrogate. Multiple kernel learning (MKL) restricts kernel weights to the simplex, ‖β‖1 = 1, "typically motivated" as a convex surrogate for ℓ0 (p. 2).
  - Imposing cardinality directly "produces an NP hard problem", so the literature uses ℓ1, ℓp, or smooth nonconvex penalties (pp. 3–4).
  - Problem (4): minimize over β in the simplex with ‖β‖0 ≤ k0 the maximum over the SVM dual α, plus λ‖β‖² (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *Alternating best response* (Algorithm 1, p. 7):
    - the α-step is a standard kernel-SVM dual, solved by LIBSVM's SMO (p. 5);
    - the β-step, after completing the square, is the Euclidean projection of w = d/(4λ) onto the sparse simplex. GSSP (Kyrillidis et al. 2013) solves it exactly in quasilinear time (pp. 5–6);
    - the start is a random k0-sparse vector or a relaxation's solution; the run stops after T iterations or when the objective stalls for M iterations (pp. 6–7).
  - *Exact MISDP*: dualize the α-problem (a pseudo-inverse form, eq. 10, p. 8); replace it by an LMI through a generalized Schur complement (eq. 11, Proposition 4, p. 9); model the support with binaries z and a perspective constraint β_i² ≤ z_i ω_i (eq. 13, pp. 9–10).
  - *Relaxation ladder*:
    - the full SDP, with z ∈ [0,1]^q (Theorem 5, p. 10);
    - "sparse SDP" approximations that impose ℓ×ℓ principal minors, only those touching the border row (p. 11);
    - ℓ = 2 gives an SOC relaxation, strengthened by random unit vectors (eqs. 16–17, pp. 11–12); ℓ = 3 gives the 3×3-SDP (eq. 18, p. 12);
    - simultaneously diagonalizable kernels turn the full SDP into an SOCP (Theorem 6, p. 11), an assumption the authors call strong (p. 11).
  - What changed: the ℓ1 surrogate gives way to an explicit cardinality constraint. What did not: the SVM solver and the open-source benchmarks. The MISDP is never solved: a custom branch-and-bound is sketched (p. 10) and left to future work (p. 20).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Binary classification with q prespecified kernels combined convexly; C, λ and k0 tuned by 10-fold cross-validation over a grid (p. 14).
  - Ten UCI data sets, 150–4,601 examples; iris, wine and heart are converted to binary tasks. One 80/20 split with a fixed seed; features standardized on training statistics (p. 13; Table 1).
  - Ten base kernels: linear; polynomial of degree 2, 3, 5; three RBF; two sigmoid; one Laplacian. Each is symmetrized and given 10⁻⁶I (p. 13).
  - Theorem 3 assumes positive definite kernels and a support that stops changing (p. 8).
  - Tractability devices admitted: the random start, and the sparse-SDP and SOC relaxations for large n (pp. 11, 16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Convergence of Algorithm 1:
    - accumulation points exist, by compactness (Bolzano–Weierstrass; Proposition 1);
    - a convergent sequence ends at a mutual best response, by Berge's maximum theorem (Proposition 2) (p. 7; proofs pp. 21–22);
    - cycling is possible (p. 8);
    - linear convergence holds if the support stabilizes and C²nk0[max λ1]² < 2λ min λn (Theorem 3, p. 8). Proof: both best-response maps are Lipschitz (strong concavity in α, 2λ-strong convexity in β), so their composition is a contraction (Banach) (pp. 22–23).
  - Cost per iteration O(qn² + q log q + nI) (p. 8).
  - Exactness rests on strong duality (Slater; affine constraints, p. 8), the generalized Schur complement (p. 9; proof p. 24) and the perspective reformulation (Günlük–Linderoth, p. 9). Relaxation validity: map any feasible point to a relaxation-feasible point with the same value (Theorem 5 proof, p. 24).
  - Counting device: since the inner block is PSD for every feasible β, only the minors touching the first row matter, which cuts C(n+1, ℓ) constraints to C(n, ℓ−1) (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Julia 1.10.1 and Python 3.10.14; MOSEK 10.1.31 for SDPs; LIBSVM v0.8.0; MKLpy 0.6; MIT Supercloud (Intel Xeon Platinum 8260); code released (p. 12). Four questions, Q1–Q4 (pp. 12–13).
  - Q1, accuracy and runtime (Tables 2–4, pp. 14–15):
    - random-start Algorithm 1 matches or beats the best of EasyMKL, AverageMKL and CKA on all ten sets, strictly on eight; +3.34 points on average (my recomputation from Table 2 agrees);
    - it selects 1–5 kernels;
    - training times are comparable, and it is fastest on spambase.
  - Q2, warm starts (Table 5, p. 16):
    - uneven effects: parkinsons 89.7 → 94.9, ionosphere 93.0 → 94.4, haberman 67.7 → 66.1;
    - memory limits leave four sets without a full-SDP start;
    - the 4.05-point headline takes, per data set, the better of the two warm-started variants on test accuracy (p. 16) [my reading: a choice made on the test set].
  - Q3, certificates (Tables 6–7, pp. 17–18):
    - global optimality certified on wine and haberman; gaps fall from 44.3% to 2.4% (parkinsons) and from 111.2% to 51.6% (ionosphere);
    - Table 7's values equal (F − f_LB)/f_LB, not the (F − f_LB)/F printed on p. 17 [my recomputation from Table 6], so the printed gaps are the larger of the two;
    - the four sets without a full SDP have only SOC or 3×3 bounds (spambase: 0.20 against 759.10; Table 6, p. 17). The summary's "below 52%" (p. 20) therefore covers six of the ten sets;
    - the body says warm starts "consistently reduce" the objective (p. 17), but Table 6 shows increases on breastcancer (70.86 → 83.23) and mammographic (1092.47 → 1173.24).
  - Q4, q = 50 kernels (Tables 8–10, pp. 18–19):
    - mean accuracy 90.3%, against 80.6 (EasyMKL), 79.5 (AverageMKL) and 67.7 (CKA);
    - about 1.9 kernels selected;
    - on spambase it trains in 25.37 s, while among the baselines only CKA finishes (822.97 s);
    - the C grid changes to {1, 2, 5, 10} (p. 18).
  - Failures reported: memory limits (Tables 5–6), a loose SOC bound, and possible cycling (p. 8). Every result comes from one split; there is no variance across splits or seeds.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Lineage (pp. 3–4): MKL as an SDP (Lanckriet et al. 2004); Bach et al. 2004; Sonnenburg et al. 2006; SimpleMKL (2008). Sparsity by ℓ1, ℓp or group penalties (Bach 2008; Koltchinskii–Yuan 2010; Kloft et al. 2011), and by smooth nonconvex surrogates (Subrahmanya–Shin 2010; Xue–Song 2019).
  - Critique by restatement, with his own result as the warrant: Lasso-type penalties give "robust estimators but not sparse estimators" (Bertsimas & Copenhaver 2018) (p. 4).
  - The relaxation devices are credited to Blekherman–Dey–Molinaro–Sun 2022 and Song–Parrilo 2023 (p. 11).
  - Benchmarks are chosen for being open source and reproducible (MKLpy) (pp. 4–5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract leads with the contrast (surrogate vs explicit cardinality) and two headline numbers, 3.34 and 4.05 points (p. 1).
  - Four numbered questions are answered in order in a summary section (pp. 12–13, 20).
  - Hedging drifts between body and summary:
    - warm starts "occasionally yield improvements" with heterogeneous effect (p. 16), but "systematic benefits when computationally feasible" in the summary (p. 20);
    - the abstract's "in several cases" globally optimal (p. 1) becomes "On two datasets" in the summary (p. 20).
  - Code release framed as bridging theory and practice (p. 12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution levels: a formulation; an algorithm with convergence results; an exact MISDP with a relaxation hierarchy; an empirical study; code (pp. 2–3, 12).
  - Limits, all concrete: SDP relaxations exhaust memory as n grows (pp. 11, 16–17); convergence is not guaranteed (p. 8); simultaneous diagonalizability rarely holds (p. 11).
  - Future: a custom branch-and-bound on the SDP relaxation, to reach global optimality; regression (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 2, 8–12, 16–17): an ML-owned problem is written exactly as a MISDP, and dualization removes the inner maximization. But the exact model lives in a class with no off-the-shelf solver at this scale, so it serves only for bounds and warm starts. The boundary is printed as memory limits (Tables 5–6).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 1, 12–20):
  - Present: explicit cardinality replaces the surrogate (step 1); versions and machine are stated (step 3); a certificate or gap is given per data set (step 4); the duel is against the incumbents' reference implementations (step 5); the paper says where it wins most (ionosphere, heart, parkinsons) (step 6).
  - Changed: the warm start runs the other way (relaxation → heuristic); nothing is solved exactly; gaps exceed 40% on several sets.
  - Tensions T1 and T6: the 4.05 headline is chosen on test results, and the "consistently reduce" claim is contradicted by Table 6.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (weak) (pp. 3–4): the NP-hardness belief behind the ℓ1 surrogate is reopened. But tractability comes from a cheap heuristic plus relaxations, not from a solver speed-up; no "why now" figure is given.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (weak) (pp. 1–2, 4, 12): the sparse-ML programme is carried by a new student, under the programme's template (sparsity enforced exactly, robustness by ℓ2), with released code; the same student leads S688.
- Heuristics (not counted): Heuristic 1 ✅: ℓ1 is read as robustness rather than sparsity, and the ℓ2 term is added "to encourage robustness" (pp. 2, 4).
- new-pattern candidate: **relaxation-seeded heuristic**: the relaxation's support starts the heuristic even when its bound is useless. On spambase the SOC bound is 0.2 against an objective above 700, yet accuracy rises from 90.9% to 93.9% (pp. 16–17).
- new-pattern candidate: **relaxation ladder priced in memory**: SOC with basis vectors, SOC with random vectors, 3×3-SDP, full SDP. Each step trades bound strength against memory, shown side by side in Table 6 (pp. 11–12, 17).
- new-pattern candidate: **binaries-plus-ridge template carried to a new learning model**: explicit ℓ0, an ℓ2 term for robustness, a perspective reformulation and a Boolean relaxation (pp. 2, 9–10). Compare the matrix version in c26 S250.

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a min–max learning problem with a cardinality constraint on the outer variable, check whether each block's best response can be solved exactly (here an SVM dual and a sparse-simplex projection). Then alternate exact best responses and prove three things: accumulation points exist; limits are mutual best responses; once the support stabilizes, a contraction condition gives linear convergence (pp. 5–8, 21–23).
- To turn an inner maximum over an SVM-type dual into one minimization without bilinear terms: dualize, then replace the pseudo-inverse quadratic by a Schur-complement LMI (eqs. 10–11, pp. 8–9, 24).
- Model the support of a nonnegative weight vector with binaries and the rotated-cone constraint β_i² ≤ z_i ω_i; the ℓ2 penalty becomes λΣω_i (p. 9).
- When the full SDP is too large, impose only the ℓ×ℓ principal minors that contain the border row of the Schur matrix, because the inner block is PSD by construction. Strengthen the ℓ = 2 SOC version with random directions x, using θ·xᵀK(β)x ≥ (xᵀγ)² (pp. 11–12).
- Fix the evaluation as numbered questions (accuracy and runtime, warm starts, optimality gap, scalability) and answer them in order in a summary (pp. 12–13, 20).
- Recompute every headline number and gap from your own tables before submission. Here the gap denominator, the "consistently" claim and the test-set maximum in the 4.05 headline disagree with the tables (pp. 16–18).

### S688 · Prescribe-then-Select: Adaptive Policy Selection for Contextual Stochastic Optimization (Transactions on Machine Learning Research 2025, arXiv 2509.08194)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–22 of arXiv v2 (the file prints "Published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research (01/2026)").
- Body pp. 1–13 read in full; references pp. 14–16 scanned.
- Appendices A–F read in full: algorithms (p. 17), data generation and parameters (pp. 18–19), illustrative trees (p. 20), runtimes (p. 21) and the OPT formulation (p. 22).
- Figures 1–7 are images. Only captions are in the text layer, so curve values were not read; numerical claims about them come from the prose.
- Title and authors confirmed on p. 1.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Caio de Próspero Iglesias (MIT Sloan) and Kimberly Villalobos Carballo (NYU Tandon) (p. 1); Bertsimas is last. · **Position in trajectory**: The prescriptive-analytics (DA) programme, extended by a student and a former co-author:
- the candidate policies include the weights of Bertsimas & Kallus 2020 (pp. 5–6);
- the selector is Optimal Policy Trees (Amram, Dunn & Zhuo 2022), run with Interpretable AI's software (pp. 6, 10);
- the piecewise data design follows Amram et al. 2022, Zhou et al. 2023 and Optimal Prescriptive Trees (Bertsimas, Dunn & Mundru 2019) (p. 10);
- Carballo co-authored the group's kernel paper on multistage stochastic optimization (ref., p. 14).

Reviewed on OpenReview; code public (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + SD (contextual stochastic optimization); ML as the selector; AP (newsvendor, shipment planning) as testbeds}:
  - Entry: heterogeneity nobody exploits. Policies from different paradigms (SAA, point prediction, predictive–prescriptive weights) win in different regions of the covariate space, "with no single policy uniformly dominating" (p. 1; pp. 4, 11).
  - Gaps in the rivals:
    - parametric decision rules cannot guarantee hard or integer constraints (p. 2);
    - predict-then-optimize can misalign the training loss with the decision cost (p. 2);
    - end-to-end training is costly and has slower regret rates (Hu, Kallus & Mao 2022) (p. 2);
    - RL mixture-of-experts is online, unconstrained and trained on surrogate objectives (p. 3);
    - the only combination method in CSO, Cui et al. 2025, averages policies, which breaks feasibility under hard constraints (p. 3).
  - Problem: formalize policy selection in CSO with hard feasibility constraints (contribution 1, p. 2; eq. 1, p. 4; eq. 5, p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *Prescribe*: a library of feasible candidate policies. SAA (eq. 2); point prediction with kNN, a random forest or a neural network (eq. 3); predictive–prescriptive weighted SAA with kNN or forest weights (eq. 4) (pp. 5–6).
  - *Select*: each candidate policy is a "treatment"; an Optimal Policy Tree assigns one policy to each region of covariate space. Selecting rather than averaging keeps every decision feasible (pp. 6–7).
  - *Pipeline* (pp. 7–8; Algorithms 1–2, p. 17):
    - K-fold cross-fitting builds a held-out cost table of realized costs c(π^m(x_i), y_i);
    - R seeds per fold give K×R trees;
    - inference takes a majority vote;
    - the candidate policies are refit on all training data.

- It builds on two group papers with de Moor, den Hertog, Koukouvinos and Zhen, both on Optimization Online in 2023 (pp. 2, 6, 11, 17, 20): "A novel algorithm for a broad class of nonconvex optimization problems" ((a)) and "Cone product reformulation for global optimization" ((b)).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP (polynomial optimization, SDP) + RO (as the device); the global-optimization ground of the MINLP team}:
  - Problem (1): minimize a degree-d polynomial subject to polynomial constraints and a set X = {Ax ≤ b, h(x) ≤ 0} with convex h (pp. 1–2).
  - Entry: the owning tradition's limits (p. 2):
    - SOS needs large SDPs, and a hierarchy of them to converge; it "may or may not provide an optimality guarantee", and handles only linear or polynomial constraints;
    - Lasserre's hierarchy needs high-order relaxations;
    - RLT is presented as a special case of SOS;
    - BARON is the general-purpose alternative.
  - Entry from his own line: RPT handles sums of linear times convex (SLC) functions. So the question is whether every polynomial has an SLC decomposition, and which decomposition is best (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *Existence*: every polynomial on [0,1]^n has SLC decompositions:
    - degree 3: Σ x_i p_i + Σ (1 − x_i) q_i + β, with convex quadratics (Theorem 1, p. 4);
    - degree 4: pairwise products of bound factors times convex quadratics (Theorem 4, pp. 9–10);
    - degree d: products of d − 2 bound factors times convex quadratics (Theorem 6, pp. 14–15);
    - an alternative with convex factors of degree d − 1 (Theorem 8, pp. 23–24).
  - *Best decomposition* (pp. 5–7, 11–13):
    - the valid decompositions form a set: coefficient-matching equalities plus LMIs;
    - after RPT (multiply and divide by the linear factor, linearize U = xxᵀ, add products of constraints) the relaxation is linear in the coefficients and convex in (x, U);
    - the authors "view the problem as a robust optimization problem", with the coefficients as uncertain parameters, and solve the min over (x, U) of the max over coefficients (eq. 3, p. 6);
    - the inner max is dualized with the robust-optimization book's Theorem 13.1 into a single SDP (Theorem 2, pp. 6–7; Theorem 5, pp. 12–13).
  - *Embedding*: the bound is used at every node of the spatial branch-and-bound of (a) (pp. 1, 17).
  - What changed vs SOS: the largest LMI is (n + 1)×(n + 1) whatever the degree, while SOS needs an LMI of dimension C(n + d/2, d/2); general convex constraints are allowed (pp. 3, 15–16). What did not: the RPT machinery and the branch-and-bound scheme of (a).
- **D3 Model and data**:

- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Existence proofs by construction: match coefficients, add α‖x‖² with α taken from row sums so that each Hessian is diagonally dominant (hence PSD by Gershgorin), then cancel the additions with linear terms (Theorems 1, 4, 6, 8; pp. 4, 10, 14–15, 23–24).
  - Tightest decomposition by robust-counterpart duality: the inner max over a spectrahedral coefficient set becomes the LMIs (4a)–(4l) and (8a)–(8o) (Theorems 2, 5; pp. 6–7, 12–13).
  - Theorem 3: adding other linear constraints as factors cannot improve the best decomposition. Each linear constraint is a combination of x_i and 1 − x_i, so the new term folds back into the class (p. 8).
  - Size accounting:
    - the largest LMI has O(n²) entries, against at least O(n^d) for SOS (p. 3);

  - Weaker alternatives, priced:
    - Gershgorin linear inequalities instead of LMIs give "much worse bounds" (p. 8; Theorem 7, pp. 22–23);
    - the degree d − 1 decomposition cannot be optimized, so its bounds are loose (p. 15);
    - an adversarial cutting-set scheme (Mutapcic–Boyd) is noted as an option (Remark 4, p. 13).

    - Lasserre's hierarchy and RLT as special cases of SOS (p. 2, citing Parrilo).
  - [my reading] The bound is a min–max (eq. 3), not a max–min. Every valid decomposition reproduces the polynomial when U = xxᵀ, so the min–max is still a valid lower bound, at least as tight as any single decomposition. The paper does not discuss the order.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To learn treatment rules from records confounded by severity:
  1. fit an outcome model on the less aggressive arm and score the more aggressive arm;
  2. bucket both arms on the predicted outcome;
  3. match within buckets on history;
  4. drop the worst-matched pairs before training (pp. 7–8).
- When training a binary step-up policy, weight the more aggressive class higher, so that residual confounding pushes the rule toward caution (p. 8).
- Translate each learned split into a clinical sentence and check it against known medicine, such as HbA1c near 8–9% and BMI near the 25 and 30 cut-offs (pp. 10–17).
- Validate a guideline on a second health system from a later period (here 2020–2024 against 1998–2014) (pp. 2, 9).
- Evaluation protocol, by contrast with this paper and following S079: do not filter the cohort on the outcome of the baseline decision (p. 6). Report the outcome model's out-of-sample error, intervals, the agreement rate and the all-case effect next to the disagreement-set effect (p. 10; cf. [card S079, pp. 5–6]).
