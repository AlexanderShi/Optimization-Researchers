### S025 · A new generation of vehicle routing research: robust algorithms, addressing uncertainty (Operations Research 1996, DOI 10.1287/opre.44.2.286)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–19. Sections 1–8 and the acknowledgment were read; the reference list (pp. 17–19) was skimmed. Theorem constants come from the prose where the displayed formulas are garbled.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: David Simchi-Levi (Northwestern University, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a survey received March 1993, revised twice and accepted August 1995 (p. 1). It gathers two programmes:
- his 1988–1993 routing line: a priori optimization with Jaillet and Odoni (DOI 10.1287/opre.38.6.1019, cited p. 10); the dynamic traveling repairman with van Ryzin (S091, card c02); Bertsimas–Xu; Bertsimas–Howell; Bertsimas–Chervi–Peterson (S089, card c03) (pp. 10–17);
- Simchi-Levi's probabilistic analysis of VRP heuristics, with Bramel, Bienstock and Li (pp. 2–9).

[inferred from the cited works] §§1–5 are Simchi-Levi's line and §§6–7 Bertsimas's. Funding: ONR, NSF and his PYI award with Draper matching funds (p. 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + AP (vehicle routing); IO as the engine}: The paper enters from the field's evaluation habit. VRP heuristics are "analyzed empirically" on standard test problems, and, following Fisher (1995), they lack robustness: they are "patched up" case by case and end up "extremely sensitive to changes in the data" (p. 1). Its remedy is worst-case and probabilistic analysis, which yields "robust" algorithms, meaning algorithms that do not depend on the environment or on data variability (pp. 1–2). A second entry is that the classical view is static and deterministic, while distribution, inventory resupply and repair have strong stochastic and dynamic parts (p. 2). Classical models "have little to say" once time and randomness enter: randomness destroys combinatorial structure, and dependent travel times break queueing assumptions (p. 13, after Psaraftis 1988).
  - Transferable: open a methods paper by naming the field's evaluation habit (heuristics tuned to benchmark sets) and the specific failure it causes (sensitivity to data changes), then offer a different kind of evidence (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Analysis first, algorithms second.
  - The asymptotic optimal cost is dominated by radial (depot) travel: 2nE(d)/q with equal demands (Theorem 3, p. 3), and 2nγE(d) with unsplit demands, γ being the bin-packing constant (Theorem 6, p. 4).
  - That structure condemns a whole class. Every route-first/cluster-second heuristic converges to the next-fit constant γ^NF, so none is asymptotically optimal. UOP(α) is the best of the class and is still 33.3% above optimum for uniform [0,1] demands (Theorems 7–8, p. 5).
  - The asymptotically optimal solution built in the proof becomes an algorithm. The location-based heuristic (LBH) solves a capacitated concentrator-location problem, with the setup cost 2d_j of a simple tour and the insertion cost as connection cost, by Lagrangian relaxation. It is asymptotically optimal (pp. 6–7, Theorem 10). Fisher–Jaikumar's generalized-assignment heuristic is the special case in which the seeds are chosen first rather than jointly (p. 7).
  - For stochastic demand, a priori optimization: one sequence through all potential customers, updated by simple rules. Strategy A keeps the fixed order; strategy B skips customers with no demand. This replaces daily re-optimization (p. 10).
  - For dynamic demand (DTRP), queueing is combined with geometric probability. In light traffic the problem is a median location problem (Theorem 16, p. 14); in heavy traffic arrivals are batched into TSP tours (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: when a proof builds an asymptotically optimal feasible solution (the upper bound), make that construction the algorithm and solve its combinatorial core exactly (LBH, pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Model and data**: Assumptions by model:
  - Deterministic VRP: symmetric distances that obey the triangle inequality (p. 2); i.i.d. planar locations with compact support; i.i.d. demands independent of location (Theorems 3 and 6, pp. 3–4). Time windows drawn from a distribution with a continuous density (Theorem 11, pp. 7–8).
  - SVRP: known, independent, discrete demand distributions, with no demand above capacity (p. 11).
  - DTRP: renewal arrivals at rate λ; i.i.d. locations with a continuous density on a bounded region of area A; m identical vehicles at constant speed; i.i.d. service times with two finite moments. Vehicles are first uncapacitated, then limited to q demands per tour (p. 14).

  All results are asymptotic and almost sure. The discussion "remains at an intuitive level", with proofs left to two handbook chapters (p. 2; references pp. 18–19). No data sets.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Devices reported (proofs not given here):
  - Upper and lower bounds that converge to one constant, with a grid upper bound whose cells shrink more slowly than n grows (pp. 3–4).
  - Subadditive processes (Kingman) for the scheduling constant (p. 7), and Rhee–Talagrand martingale inequalities for bin packing (p. 5).
  - Worst-case ratios with tight instances: OP(α) and UOP(α) (Theorems 1–2 and 4–5, pp. 3–4). The cyclic a priori heuristic is within 1 + α (+O(1/n)) of re-optimization when every customer has demand (Theorem 13, p. 11). The space-filling-curve heuristic is within O(log n), and a matching bad example exists (Theorem 14, p. 12).
  - Theorem 12: the LP relaxation of the set-partitioning formulation is asymptotically tight. This shows why column generation works, while "other classical formulations" can have diverging LP and IP values (pp. 8–9).
  - The expected length of an a priori sequence can be evaluated in polynomial time (the bound reads "O(Kn7)" in the OCR). The expectation of re-optimization is intractable: (K + 1)^n demand patterns, each requiring a VRP solve (p. 11).
  - DTRP: lower bounds valid for all stable policies, from geometric probability, queueing and optimization, with upper bounds from specific policies (p. 15). Consequences:
    - stability iff ρ < 1, independent of geometry and speed (Theorem 17, p. 14);
    - system time grows like 1/(1−ρ)², the "inverse square" (pp. 14, 16);
    - doubling the fleet cuts system time fourfold (p. 14);
    - TSP policies are within a factor of about 1.8 of the bound, and the slack is conjectured to sit in the lower bound (p. 15);
    - the capacitated case adds radial travel to the stability condition (Theorem 18, p. 16).
  - Transferable: rule out a heuristic class at once by mapping its asymptotic cost onto a simpler problem's known constant (route-first/cluster-second ↔ next-fit bin packing, p. 5).
  - Transferable: when a policy class is hard to optimize, bound all stable policies from below, exhibit a simple policy that attains the same order, and say where you think the remaining constant comes from (p. 15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a for the survey itself: no new experiments. Others' evidence is reported in one line each:
  - LBH "tested empirically" (p. 7); the time-window algorithm "works very well" on standard test problems (p. 8).
  - A hierarchical distribution design comes within 35% of a lower bound at 100 customers and within 16% at 500 (p. 9).
  - Exact SVRP solved up to 50 nodes by Gendreau, Laporte and Seguin (p. 12); "extensive computational results" for the a priori strategies (p. 12).
  - DTRP heuristic constants estimated by simulation (p. 15); limiting behaviour said to appear already at moderate sizes (p. 17).

  No instances, solvers or run times are given.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Two traditions are opposed by name: the empirical heuristic tradition (Fisher 1995; p. 1), and stochastic-programming treatments of random demand that solve only small problems, from which the a priori approach is "completely different" (p. 10). The paper continues the probabilistic analysis of combinatorial optimization (Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley, Haimovich–Rinnooy Kan, Rhee–Talagrand, Karmarkar; pp. 3–5) and the a priori line of Jaillet and of Bertsimas–Jaillet–Odoni (p. 10). It answers Psaraftis's (1988) remark that routing and congestion theory had not been combined (p. 13). Dynamic routing is placed inside a wider family of dynamic transportation models, one reference per area, including his own dynamic air traffic work with Vranas and Odoni (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Filed under "Survey, Expository & Tutorial" (p. 1). Each theorem is followed by a paragraph that explains it "intuitively" (pp. 4, 6; "explained as follows", p. 3). There are "Reflections" subsections (§6.4, p. 12; §7.4, p. 17) and a four-point summary whose first point opens "We believe" (p. 17). A referee's qualification is printed inside the summary: travel-time variation and crew scheduling complicate VRPs in practice. It is followed by "We hope" for an impact on practice (p. 17). Practice examples lead the uncertainty sections: UPS, bank cash collection, post offices, hot meals, utility repair, car haulers, LTL consolidation (pp. 10–11, 13). [inferred] The title brands the work as a programme ("a new generation"), which compares with Heuristic 9's programme brand. Hedges: "we believe", "we conjecture" (p. 15), "we hope" (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a synthesis with an agenda, theory → insight → algorithms. The summary states three benefits of analysis (p. 17). Limits written:
  - proofs omitted (p. 2);
  - the hierarchical design may converge slowly in practice (p. 9);
  - heavy-traffic optimality of the TSP policies only conjectured (p. 15);
  - the referee's caveat on practice (p. 17);
  - a priori optimization recommended where computing is scarce (pp. 12, 17).

  No list of future work. On the RO-origin question raised in note 01: "robust" here means performance that does not depend on the data environment, shown by worst-case and probabilistic analysis (p. 1). There is no uncertainty set and no robust counterpart. The 1996 title shows the concern, not the 2004 device.

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 6–8, 11, 14–16). Problems owned by other traditions (empirical VRP heuristics, stochastic programming, queueing) are re-entered through combinatorial optimization plus probability:
  - the VRP is recast as concentrator location and solved by Lagrangian relaxation (pp. 6–7);
  - the set-partitioning LP is proved asymptotically exact (p. 8);
  - the boundary is printed: re-optimization's expected cost is intractable, a priori costs are computable (p. 11).

  The device is asymptotic analysis, not a formulation kept in a solver class.
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 11–12, 14–16). Two precursors of "the price of X":
  - The price of fairness: spatially unbiased and biased DTRP policies, a distinction the paper ties to "fairness" (p. 14), have closed-form bounds, and the gap vanishes for uniform demand (pp. 14–15).
  - The price of simplicity: a priori strategies against re-optimization, with the nearest-neighbour-started cyclic heuristic asymptotically equal to re-optimization in the capacitated case, "particularly important since nearest-neighbor tours are widely used in practice" (p. 12).

  Set size n is one knob that trades travel cost against waiting time (p. 16). No curve is published, and there is no solution-independent probability bound.
- Method 2: variant ⚠, a mirror image (pp. 10, 12, 17). Method choice is indexed to the computing available. In 1996 scarce computing argues for a priori routes over re-optimization; after 2014 abundant computing argues for exact MIO.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 10–13, 17). Practice supplies the settings and the objective: waiting time matters more than distance, for five stated reasons (p. 13). Operational reasons justify a priori routes: computing resources, redesign time, driver confusion, regular service by the same driver (p. 10). But no owner's current rule, data set or decision metric is used.
- Tension T3: evidence of the practised side (pp. 3–17): almost-sure asymptotics and queueing theory carry the whole paper.
- New-pattern candidate: **bound, constructive policy, provable ratio, tightness check** (pp. 3–5, 11–12, 14–16; with S091 and S052 in card c02).
- New-pattern candidate: **static methods adapted to dynamic problems**: "static vehicle routing methods when properly adapted can yield optimal or near-optimal policies" for dynamic problems (p. 17; with S091, card c02).
- New-pattern candidate: **price of simplicity** (a priori against re-optimization, pp. 11–12; with S089, card c03).
- New-pattern candidate: **asymptotic optimal structure turned into an algorithm** (pp. 6–7; summary point 2, p. 17).
- New-pattern candidate: **structural theorem explains computational success** (Theorem 12, pp. 8–9; with S015 in this batch).
- New-pattern candidate: **method choice indexed to available computation** (pp. 10, 12, 17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn the asymptotically optimal upper-bound construction of a proof into the algorithm, and solve its combinatorial core exactly: capacitated concentrator location by Lagrangian relaxation (pp. 6–7).
- Rule out a whole heuristic class by showing that its asymptotic cost converges to a weaker problem's constant (γ^NF against γ), and name the best member of the class (p. 5).
- Compare a non-adaptive policy with full re-optimization twice: by a worst-case ratio, and by an almost-sure asymptotic ratio (Theorems 13–15, pp. 11–12).
- Split a stochastic system by regime: a location problem in light traffic, TSP batching in heavy traffic. Then give the stability condition and scaling laws (pp. 14–16).
- When an objective trades two costs, expose the one parameter that moves along the trade-off (set size n, p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "new algorithms that are more robust, i.e., algorithms that are independent of the specific environment and the variability in the data" (p. 1)
- "A priori optimization in VRPs is an attractive policy in the absence of intensive computational power." (p. 17)

### S289 · On the worst case complexity of potential reduction algorithms for linear programming (Mathematical Programming 1997, DOI 10.1007/bf02614620)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13, the whole paper including the acknowledgements and references. Formulas are partly garbled, so the proof was followed through the prose and the surviving equations.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xiaodong Luo (MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1; also first author of S156, card c05) · **Position in trajectory**: an LO research paper of the textbook years (the LO textbook with Tsitsiklis appeared the same year, per team.json). Received July 1995, revised July 1996 (p. 1); PYI-funded with Draper matching funds (p. 1). The first author thanks Robert Freund for "stimulating his interest in interior point algorithms". Anstreicher, Todd and Yinyu Ye (spelled "Yinyue Ye") are thanked for comments on an earlier version (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {LO}: Potential-reduction interior-point methods (Ye; Freund; Gonzaga–Todd) combine the best known O(√n L) iteration bound with line searches that make them practical. The paper calls this "a rare combination" (p. 2). It asks four numbered questions (p. 2):
  1. Is the bound inherent in the algorithm, or an artefact of weak proof methods?
  2. Do line searches improve it?
  3. Does network structure help?
  4. Is the bound inherent in linear programming itself?

  The paper answers the first three negatively for this class (pp. 1, 3).
  - Transferable: when an algorithm's bound has stood for years, ask in print whether it belongs to the algorithm or to the proof, and construct the instance that decides it (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: No new algorithm; the contribution is a lower-bound instance. The instance is a network LP with 2n variables in two chains of equalities x_i = x_{i+1}, with b = 0 and c = e (p. 6). On it, Ye's algorithm with exact line searches needs Ω((√n/ν) log(1/ε)) primal steps for potential parameter q = 2n + ν√n (Theorem 3, pp. 11–12). Only primal steps are counted, for two reasons: they dominate the work per iteration, since they solve a linear system (p. 6), and dual steps leave the duality gap unchanged here (p. 12).
- **D3 Model and data**: The setting:
  - Standard-form LP and its dual (p. 2).
  - The Tanabe–Todd–Ye primal-dual potential G(x, s) = q log x's − Σ log x_j − Σ log s_j (p. 3).
  - Ye's Algorithm A with parameters 0 < γ < 1, α ≥ 0, q ≥ n: a primal step when ‖u‖ ≥ γ, a dual step otherwise (p. 4). Freund's and Gonzaga–Todd's dual-step variants are on p. 5.
  - The known upper bound: for q = n + √n and γ = 0.22, the potential falls by 0.02 per iteration (Theorem 2, p. 5).

  The instance is deliberately degenerate: optimal value 0, a unique primal optimum at zero, every dual feasible point optimal (p. 6). The bound holds from one specified start: the two chains in ratio 1 + ω*, s^0 = e, y^0 = 0 (pp. 9, 11). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Proof skeleton:
  1. Symmetry: the projection matrix is the same at every iteration, and the iterates take only two values, r^k and t^k (Proposition 1, pp. 7–8).
  2. The step chosen by exact line search depends only on the ratio r^k/t^k and satisfies g(r/t) = g(t/r). The minimizer is unique, by a second-derivative argument (Proposition 2, pp. 8–9).
  3. Fixed point: choose the starting ratio so that one step maps r/t to t/r. The same step α* then repeats forever (strategy, p. 9). The explicit solution is ω* = ν/√n and α* = (2 + ν/√n)/(1 + ν/√n)² (Proposition 3, p. 10).
  4. Induction on k (p. 11). Each primal step cuts the gap only by the factor 1/(1 + ν/√n), starting from a gap of 2n + ν√n, which gives the step count (p. 12).

  Corollaries (p. 12): q = 2n + Θ(1) gives Ω(n log(1/ε)); q = 2n + Θ(√n) gives Ω(√n log(1/ε)), which matches the upper bound; a larger ν weakens the bound. The bound extends to Freund's and Gonzaga–Todd's methods. It may not cover Kojima–Mizuno–Yoshise, Nesterov's long-step method, Ye's projective method or Ye et al. (p. 12). A reviewer supplied a "substantial simplification" of the proof of Proposition 3 (p. 13).
  - Transferable: to show an iteration bound is tight, build an instance whose iterates stay in a two-value symmetric family. Prove that the exact line-search step depends only on their ratio and is symmetric in it. Then start at the ratio that the step maps to its reciprocal, so the same step repeats (pp. 7–11).
  - Transferable: set b = 0 so the gap equals c'x and only primal steps move it. A bound on primal steps is then a bound on work (pp. 6, 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none (pure theory; no runs and no solve times).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Inside interior-point complexity theory:
  - Upper bounds: Karmarkar's O(nL); path-following O(√n L) (Renegar, Gonzaga), whose small steps are not "attractive for a practical implementation"; Ye's potential reduction as the practical route to the best bound (p. 2).
  - Earlier lower bounds: short-step Θ(√n L); Anstreicher's worst-case Karmarkar step; Kaliski–Ye's Ω(n) on one constraint; Powell's Ω(n) for discretized semi-infinite programs; Todd and Todd–Ye on primal-dual methods (pp. 2–3).
  - For this council, three guest members appear. Ye's algorithm is the object of study, and Ye commented on a draft (pp. 4, 13). Nesterov's long-step method is named as possibly escaping the bound (p. 12). Powell's Karmarkar lower bound is cited (p. 3).

  This shared complexity-first ground softens the "Ye, Nesterov" disagreement line of SKILL.md's Roundtable Card.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract calls the question "a significant open question" and promises "some negative answers" (p. 1). Numbered questions are answered by number (pp. 2–3). Question 4 is called "rather deep" and tied to the existence of a strongly polynomial LP algorithm (p. 2). Three reasons (a)–(c) argue that the lower bound is strong (p. 6). The paper runs 13 pages with no figures. Hedging is precise: the algorithms the example may not cover are listed by reference number (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a tightness result for one class of interior-point algorithms. Limits written:
  - one instance and one starting point (Theorem 3, p. 11);
  - other potential-reduction algorithms possibly not covered (p. 12);
  - the case q = n + ν√n with ν = Ω(√n) left open (p. 12).

  Future work: "a general lower bound for all potential reduction algorithms" (p. 12). Question 4, whether O(√n L) is inherent in LP, is not addressed (pp. 2–3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 6, 11–12). In 1997 tractability meant complexity class: the paper prints where an algorithm's guarantee ends (a tight Ω(√n log(1/ε))) and reports no solve time at all. This is the far end of the drift that Method 1's Limitations record, from complexity class to solve time [02 C2]. By the later Research Taste it falls under warning sign 2 ("polynomial time without solve times"). The motive is still practical: the class matters because line searches make it implementable (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: **numbered open questions answered in order** (pp. 2–3; with S015 in this batch).
- New-pattern candidate: **tightness instance by symmetry and a forced repeating step** (pp. 7–11). This is related to c02's tightness candidates, but here the target is another author's upper bound.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a tight lower-bound instance from symmetry plus a fixed point of the line-search map, so that exact line searches cannot help (pp. 7–11).
- Count only the expensive step type, and argue explicitly why that count bounds the total work (p. 6).
- List, by reference number, the algorithms an example may fail to cover, instead of claiming generality (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "is the bound inherent in the algorithm or is it because of the weakness of our proof methods?" (p. 2)
- "In the theory and practice of linear programming this is a rare combination." (p. 2)

### S009 · Optimal control of execution costs (Journal of Financial Markets 1998, DOI 10.1016/s1386-4181(97)00012-8)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–47. Read: the text (pp. 1–44), Tables 1–6 and Appendix A (pp. 44–47). Skimmed: the reference list (pp. 47–50). Inline symbols are font-substituted and were read from context.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Andrew W. Lo (MIT Sloan; corresponding author, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the opening of his finance line with Lo. Two companion working papers are cited: an empirical analysis of best execution (Bertsimas, Hummel & Lo 1998) and a static-optimization alternative to dynamic programming under constraints (Bertsimas & Lo 1998) (pp. 33, 48). Robert Ferstenberg is credited with "stimulating our interest" (p. 43). The paper was presented to practitioner audiences, including the NYSE Conference on Best Execution and the Society of Quantitative Analysts (p. 44). Funding: MIT's Laboratory for Financial Engineering, a Sloan fellowship, NSF and his PYI (p. 44). The finance strand continues with the GMO deployment (S101, card c05).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + AP (finance and execution)}: The paper enters from a gap in practice and regulation:
  - Execution costs are large. A "paper" Value Line portfolio beat the market by almost 20% a year, while the actual fund beat it by 2.5% (Perold, p. 2).
  - Institutions split blocks into multi-day "packages": only 20% of package value is completed within a day, and over 53% is spread over four days or more (Chan–Lakonishok, p. 3).
  - The law requires "best execution", yet "no clear definition of best execution exists" (Macey–O'Hara, p. 2).

  The authors define best execution as the solution of a stochastic dynamic program (p. 3). They note that the dynamic nature of execution was recognized only implicitly by Perold and by practitioners (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when practice or law relies on an undefined performance notion, propose one operational definition as the optimum of an explicit model, and present it as one definition, not the definition (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Minimize E[Σ P_t S_t] subject to Σ S_t = S̄ over T periods. The law of motion for prices includes the trade's own impact, and the Bellman equation is solved over the state (P_{t−1}, X_t, W_t) (pp. 6–7). Results:
  1. Linear permanent impact with an arithmetic random walk makes the naive equal split S̄/T optimal (pp. 8–9).
  2. A serially correlated information variable X_t adds a linear correction to the naive term. It can then be optimal to trade into expected price rises (pp. 10–12, 16).
  3. The LPT law keeps a linear policy (plus a constant) and a quadratic value function. LPT has a geometric no-impact price and a temporary impact linear in trade size as a percentage of price. The cost splits exactly into Perold's paper cost plus the implementation shortfall (pp. 20–22).
  4. General laws of motion are solved numerically, by discretized DP or by approximate DP with least-squares quadratic value functions, the latter taken from Bertsekas (1995) (pp. 25–30).
  5. A portfolio version: a non-diagonal impact matrix couples the stocks (pp. 30–33).
  6. Constraints, with one closed form under no-sales constraints when information multiplies impact (pp. 33–37).

  The optimal value function doubles as a price benchmark for principal-bid and block trades (pp. 4, 43).
- **D3 Model and data**: Modelling choices:
  - Discrete time with 30-minute periods (T = 13 per NYSE day), chosen over continuous time for closed forms and because empirical models must be discretized anyway (p. 5, fn. 6).
  - A buy programme; selling is symmetric (p. 4, fn. 4).
  - A risk-neutral expected-cost objective (p. 41).
  - Partial equilibrium: the law of motion is unaffected by the investor's trades (p. 42).
  - The order type (limit or market) is subsumed (p. 38), and parameters are treated as known (p. 39).

  The authors flag the §2 dynamics as empirically implausible: negative prices, permanent impact, impact that falls with price (pp. 7, 17–19). They adopt LPT "for convenience" and to keep prices non-negative (p. 20). Data: none. All parameters are calibrated: impact of $500,000, or 5%, on a 100,000-share block; 2% daily volatility; one tick of noise (pp. 12–13, 22). Estimation on real data is deferred to Bertsimas, Hummel & Lo (1998) (pp. 6, 20, 32, 42–43).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Devices:
  - Bellman recursions with closed-form coefficient recursions, proved by induction on k (Appendix A, pp. 44–47; e.g. a_k = (h/2)(1 + 1/(k + 1)), p. 45).
  - Each coefficient is interpreted. Why X_t > 0 raises current purchases when ρ > 0 (pp. 11–12). Why the impact cost does not vanish as T → ∞: it tends to hS̄²/2 (p. 9).
  - Cost accounting for discretized DP: memory O(KJN); 800,000 evaluations per stage and 16 million in all, about 16 s at 10^−6 s each; a factor of 1,000 more for finer grids (p. 28).
  - Approximate DP is exact when the value functions are quadratic. About its approximation error, "very little is known" (p. 29).
  - Why constraints break DP: with no-sales constraints the value function becomes piecewise quadratic with 3^k pieces, 3,486,784,401 at T = 20 (p. 35).
  - Under multiplicative impact the policy has a threshold structure (pp. 36–37): wait for the last period if θ ≤ 1/2; the naive split if θ = 1; front-load if θ > 1.
  - Transferable: before choosing a numerical DP method, count the state × control points per stage and the total evaluations, and price them in seconds (p. 28).
  - Transferable: show why a constraint destroys a closed form by counting the pieces of the value function it creates (p. 35).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Simulation only.

- **D6 Literature positioning**: Dynamic optimization in finance is old (Merton, Samuelson), but its use "in defining best execution is novel" (p. 3). The model builds on:

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 9–10, 21–22, 27–29, 33, 35). Formulation first, for a notion that law and practice leave undefined (p. 3). The model is specified so that it stays linear-quadratic: a linear policy and a quadratic value function (pp. 9–10, 21–22). - Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 3–4, 13–25, 43). The baseline is the practitioners' rule, "a naive (and not uncommon) strategy" (p. 3), and the decision metric is execution cost in cents/share against it (Tables 1–4). The value function is offered to dealers as a pricing benchmark (pp. 4, 43). Against: the evidence is simulated with calibrated parameters only, with no owner's data and no field test (pp. 6, 20, 32).
- Heuristic 1: evidence ✅, in DP form (pp. 4, 9–10, 43). **Transferable techniques**:
- Find the model under which the practitioners' rule is exactly optimal, then change one assumption at a time and show how the optimal policy departs from the rule (pp. 4, 9–12, 20–22).

- Split the objective into an uncontrollable "paper" term and a controllable impact term. Use the split twice: to connect to a practitioners' metric (implementation shortfall), and to show that almost all outcome variance is uncontrollable, which supports a risk-neutral objective (pp. 21, 39–41).

### S015 · The air traffic flow management problem with enroute capacities (Operations Research 1998, DOI 10.1287/opre.46.3.406)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17. Read: the text (pp. 1–13) and Appendices A–C (pp. 13–17). Skimmed: the reference list (p. 17). The OCR of the formulas and of the facet proofs in Appendix C is partly garbled; their steps were followed at the level of structure.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Sarah Stock Patterson (MIT, p. 1; his PhD student, thesis 1997 per note 04) · **Position in trajectory**: received August 1994, accepted May 1996 (p. 1). This is the second step of the air-traffic line after Vranas, Bertsimas & Odoni (1994a,b; S036 in card c02), whose formulation it supersedes (pp. 2, 7). Odoni is thanked for "inspiring our work in this area" (p. 17). Funding: Draper Laboratory, the FAA and his PYI (p. 17). SKILL.md's Research Trajectory row for 1995–2001 lists moments, finance and the LO textbook but omits this air-traffic IO line, which note 01 does list.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + AP (air transportation)}: The paper enters from an application, led by a dollar figure. Delays cost U.S. airlines about $3 billion a year, against reported industry losses of $2–2.5 billion, and 700–1,100 flights a day are delayed by 15 minutes or more (p. 1). Current practice:
  - the FAA uses a computerized first-come, first-served ground-holding procedure, refined by controllers' experience (p. 1);
  - rerouting is left to controllers' experience "and not through a formal optimization model" (p. 9).

  The modelling gap:
  - single- and multi-airport ground-holding models (SAGHP, MAGHP) ignore en-route sector capacities (p. 2);
  - Lindsay et al.'s concurrent integer program has weak LP relaxations that need branch and bound (p. 2);
  - Helme's space-time flow model is untested (p. 2);
  - rerouting (TFMRP) had not been addressed (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A 0-1 integer program over cumulative variables w_{fjt} = 1 if flight f has reached sector j by time t. The text calls this definition "critical" (p. 4). With it:
  - sector, airport (turnaround) and time connectivity become pairwise precedence inequalities (5)–(7) (p. 5);
  - the requirement that a flight reach each sector at some time telescopes to fixing the last variable at 1, which removes variables and constraints (pp. 4, 7);
  - ground and air delays, and hence the objective, are linear in w (pp. 4–5).

  An "Important Remark" says that this choice of variables, not the linearly equivalent "at time t" variables of S036, makes the formulation strong (p. 5). Specialized to MAGHP, the formulation's LP bound dominates the earlier ones (Proposition 1, p. 7). Extensions need no new machinery (pp. 8–10):
  - runway configurations that couple arrival and departure capacities, folded in without new variables (p. 8);
  - hub connectivity, through assignment variables;
  - banks of flights, through first- and last-arrival variables;
  - rerouting, through path or sector variables.
  - Transferable: in time-indexed scheduling or flow IPs, use "by time t" cumulative binaries. Precedence becomes w_{f,j',t+l} ≤ w_{f,j,t}; monotonicity becomes w_{t−1} ≤ w_t; the assignment equality becomes a fixed parameter (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Model and data**: The model:
  - Capacities are deterministic and static per period: airport departures, airport arrivals and sectors. Schedules, sector traversal times, turnaround times and per-flight ground and air delay costs are known (pp. 3–4).
  - Time is discrete, in 5- or 15-minute periods. Time windows T_f^j bound lateness: D = 6, half an hour, in the U.S.-scale sizing (pp. 4, 6).
  - Size is estimated before computing, for a U.S. instance with 20 airports, 200 sectors, 10,000 flights and 168 periods: at most 300,000 variables and 688,320 constraints, against 120,000 and 234,720 for MAGHP (p. 6).

  The data:
  - the ground-holding data sets of Vranas et al. (1994a), with departure data generated to fit (p. 11);
  - a 4-airport network with three hypothetical sectors around LaGuardia: 200 flights from the January 1993 OAG, and 1,000 flights from Draper's pseudo-OAG generator (p. 12);
  - two real instances, also used at MITRE: 278 flights, 10 airports and 178 sectors over 7 h; 1,002 flights, 18 airports and 305 sectors over 8 h (p. 12). Of these, "Nothing used in these datasets was generated or hypothesized" (p. 13).

  Determinism is stated as a limit (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Results and devices:
  - NP-hardness with unit capacities, by reduction from job-shop scheduling: aircraft ↔ jobs, airports and sectors ↔ processors (Theorem 1, pp. 7–8).
  - Polyhedral comparison by inclusion: the integer feasible sets coincide while the LP relaxations nest, so Z_VBO ≤ Z_TP ≤ Z_MAGHP (Proposition 1, p. 7; proof in Appendix A, pp. 13–14).
  - Theorem 2: P_TFMP and P_MAGHP are not integral, even with uniform costs. A two-flight counterexample has a determinant-2 submatrix and a fractional optimum (Appendix B, pp. 14–15).
  - The connectivity inequalities are facets; the capacity inequalities are not (p. 10).
  - The facet proofs count affinely independent points (Appendix C, pp. 15–17). They cover MAGHP only, under simplifying assumptions: equal windows, zero turnaround, unit capacities (p. 15). The TFMP half is "similar, but more algebraically involved" and is not given (p. 15). The appendices call the result Theorem 1a and 1b (OCR "la", "lb"), while the text calls it Theorem 2 (pp. 14–15).

  Facets were sought to explain the computations: "in order to obtain a deeper understanding of why this formulation performs so well computationally" (p. 10).
  - Transferable: prove LP-bound dominance over rival formulations by mapping each to a common variable space and showing that the relaxations nest (Proposition 1, p. 7; Appendix A, pp. 13–14).
  - Transferable: refute integrality with the smallest counterexample (two flights; a 4×4 submatrix of determinant 2), then explain good computational behaviour through facets (pp. 10, 14–15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: GAMS with CPLEXMIP 2.1 on a Sun SPARCstation 10 model 41 (p. 11). Five research questions open the section (p. 11) and are answered point by point (p. 13).
  - **MAGHP**: 1,000 and 3,000 flights at connectivity 0.2–0.8. Tests are run at the "infeasibility border": capacities just above infeasibility, found by trial and error and judged the practically relevant and hardest regime (p. 11). Metrics: CPU seconds, % of flights non-integral, objective value (Tables I–VI, pp. 11–12).
    - Non-integral LP solutions occurred only at 1,000 flights and 40% connectivity: 4% and 2.2% of flights, at two capacity levels (pp. 11–12).
    - Tables III–IV reproduce Vranas et al.'s "Previous Results". Their 3,000-flight non-integrality column reads "not given", and the earlier model had no departure data (p. 11). So this is a comparison with published numbers, not a rerun.
    - The earlier LP "was solved faster" for 3,000 flights (p. 11). The total time to an integral solution was lower here (p. 12).
  - **TFMP**: 200 flights in 234 s and 1,000 flights in 436 s, all integral (p. 12; Table VII, p. 13).
    - Table VII's objective values are not monotone in sector capacity (12/12/12: 244,225; 10/12/12: 24,225; p. 13). This is a typo in the source or the OCR; it cannot be resolved from the text.
  - **Real instances**: 18,733 variables and 43,226 constraints solved in 1,141 s; 69,497 variables and 151,662 constraints in 29,920 s; both integral (p. 13).
  - Limits reported: larger runs were prevented by the lack of real data and by SPARCstation memory (p. 13).
  - [inferred] 29,920 s (about 8.3 h) exceeds the instance's own 8-hour horizon, while the models are "intended to be used on-line and solved repeatedly during a day" (p. 2). The paper nonetheless calls the times "reasonable" (p. 13).
  - Delay costs are never compared with the FAA's first-come, first-served rule (none of Tables I–VII, pp. 11–13).
  - Transferable: test at the infeasibility border of the capacity parameters, and report the share of fractional variables next to CPU time for every instance (pp. 11–13; S036 in card c02 used the same border).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: A taxonomy places all prior work, much of it from Odoni's group (p. 2): SAGHP → MAGHP → TFMP → TFMRP, crossed with deterministic/stochastic and static/dynamic. The paper supersedes the authors' own earlier MAGHP formulation (Vranas, Bertsimas & Odoni 1994a): its LP bound is stronger, and rounding heuristics are no longer needed (pp. 2, 7). The concurrent rival is read in a balanced way: Lindsay et al.'s relaxations are weak, yet their strengthening techniques cut times (p. 2). Helme's model is criticized speculatively ("it is expected that there will be severe dimensionality problems", p. 2). The authority invoked is polyhedral combinatorics (p. 2). Lindsay later supplied data for the experiments (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract lists contributions (a)–(e); its opening sentences reappear almost verbatim as the introduction's first paragraph (p. 1).

  - An "Important Remark" paragraph explains the variable choice (p. 5).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions (a)–(e) (p. 1): a strong formulation (some constraints are facets), NP-hardness, extensions, LP-bound dominance, large-scale computation.
  - Limits written: the model is deterministic and static. Before deployment it would need:

    - dynamic updating, since en-route delays are issued only once a flight is airborne;

  - Also written: the rerouting formulations are untested (p. 10); other facets were not sought (p. 11); size was limited by data and memory (p. 13).

  - Future work: other capacitated flow systems, such as manufacturing and ground transportation (p. 13).
