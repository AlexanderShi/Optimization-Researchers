### S829 · A central limit theorem for the exodic tree and exodic matching in geometrical probability (MIT Sloan School working paper WP# 3313-MS 1991, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–18 (abstract, §§1–5, Figures 2–5, references). The proofs of §3 (pp. 5–13) were read for their structure; most displayed formulas there, including the variance constants and the stated rate of deviation from normality, are garbled. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and van Ryzin match (pp. 1–2); the file is working paper WP# 3313-MS of June 27, 1991 (p. 1), the item listed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Garrett van Ryzin (MIT ORC, p. 2; his student, see card S195 in s01) · **Position in trajectory**: the sequel to S195 (ref. [3], p. 18), whose Crofton-method analysis of the exodic tree and matching it carries from expected lengths to distributions (p. 5); funded by NSF grants, the UPS Foundation and his PYI award with Draper matching funds (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO}: For n uniform points in a region of R^d, the lengths of the TSP, MST, matching, Steiner tree and Held–Karp bound obey laws of large numbers (Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley; Steele's subadditive functionals), and a central limit theorem for them is "a long open conjecture" (abstract, p. 2; p. 3). Rhee and Talagrand's Gaussian tail bounds for the planar TSP are the cited evidence for it (p. 3). The entry: prove the CLT not for the optimal tree but for the tree and matching built by a natural heuristic, Gilbert's exodic heuristic (pp. 2–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Apply Crofton's method to the generating function f(n, a, s) of the exodic tree's length in a ball of volume a. Enlarge the ball by δa and condition on whether the new point falls in the thin shell (events E1, E2); this gives a differential–difference equation in a and n (eqs. 1–3, pp. 6–7). The exodic order makes the shell point's edge the distance R_{n−1}(a) to the nearest inner point, and given R_{n−1}(a) > r the other n − 1 points are uniform in a ball of volume a − A(r) (eq. 4, Figure 1, pp. 7–8). An exponential trial solution, matched power by power in n, yields the mean, the variance and the normal limit (pp. 10–12). The matching follows "after identical calculations" (Theorem 4, pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Model and data**: n i.i.d. uniform points in a d-dimensional ball of volume a centred at the origin, with n → ∞ for fixed d (pp. 5–6). The exodic heuristic sorts the points by distance from the centre and joins each to its nearest predecessor; the matching starts from the outermost point (p. 4). Events of probability o(δa) are dropped (p. 6). No data; simulation in the plane (d = 2) only (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Crofton's perturbation (pp. 5–7); Lemma 2, the joint density of the tree length and the shell distance by a Taylor expansion in A(r) (pp. 6–8); Lemma 3, asymptotics of the transforms needed by Watson's lemma (pp. 9–10); Bender–Orszag techniques for linear differential–difference equations (p. 10); the moment generating function of the normalized length tends to that of N(0, 1) (p. 12). The authors stress that the variance is computed exactly and state a rate of deviation from normality (p. 12; the exponent is garbled).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Simulation for d = 2 (pp. 13–17). The sample mean of L_n/√n approaches 1/√2 ≈ 0.707 for n from 64 to 32,000, with sample sizes chosen from a ±10% width of the 95% confidence interval (355 trees at n = 64, 100 at n = 32,000) (Figure 2, p. 13). The sample standard deviation, with 240 samples per point, is called close to the asymptotic 0.152 for n > 1000 (Figure 3, pp. 13, 16). A Q–Q plot and a histogram of 1,000 trees with n = 1,000 support normality, but their sample standard deviation is 0.222, and the text says the asymptotic 0.152 is "not a particularly good estimate" at that size (Figures 4–5, pp. 16–17). The text does not reconcile this with the closeness read from Figure 3 (my reading).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contribution: the first CLT "in this class of problems", in every dimension, with an explicit variance (their claim, pp. 2–3, 12). Limits: the theorem covers heuristics, not the optimal MST, matching or TSP, so the conjecture itself stays open (pp. 2–3); the conclusion claims only "additional insight" (p. 17); no future directions are stated (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13–17): the honest-gap habit in probabilistic form: the asymptotic prediction is checked against simulation, and the size at which it is poor (n = 1,000: 0.222 against 0.152) is printed in the body, as Heuristic 4 asks.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 5, 18): the second paper of a programme line carried with the same student (S195 → S829).
- New-pattern candidate: *check the asymptotics by simulation at small n* (pp. 13–17).
- New-pattern candidate: *settle an open conjecture first for a tractable surrogate* (pp. 2–4): the CLT for the optimum stays open, so the authors prove it for a heuristic whose sorting order makes Crofton's recursion exact.
- Tension T3 (practice side): pure geometric probability, no optimization model.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Crofton's method on a generating function: perturb the region's volume, condition on where one extra point lands, and obtain a differential–difference equation for the transform of the length (pp. 5–7).
- Solve that equation asymptotically with an exponential trial solution and match powers of n to read off the mean and variance constants (pp. 10–12).
- Set the simulation's sample size per n from a target confidence-interval width, and add a Q–Q plot at one fixed n to test the shape as well as the moments (pp. 13, 16).

**Quotes**:
- "It has been a long open conjecture that a central limit theorem holds for the length of these problems." (p. 2)
- "Although for n = 1000 the asymptotic standard deviation of 0.152 is not a particularly good estimate, Figure 4 clearly shows that the data follow closely a normal distribution." (p. 16)

### S203 · Deducing queueing from transactional data: the queue inference engine, revisited (Operations Research 1992, DOI 10.1287/opre.40.3.s217)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–12 (abstract, §§1–5, Figures 1–4, references). The algorithm's formulas (Steps 0–6, p. 3), most proof algebra (pp. 4–10) and every complexity exponent except the on-line algorithm's O(n) are garbled; claims about them rest on the prose. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas (MIT) and Servi (GTE Laboratories) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: L. D. Servi (GTE Laboratories, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received April 1990, accepted July 1991 (p. 1); an inference-from-data paper with an industrial co-author, supported by MIT's International Financial Services Center and Leaders for Manufacturing programme (p. 12). His homepage files it in the folder *Machine Learning under a Modern Optimization Lens* (folder name in the works.json URL; metadata, not the text).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD; AP (banking, telecommunications) as motivation}: Larson's 1989 problem: infer transient queue lengths from nothing but the start and end times of each service (p. 1). ATMs log these data but cannot see their queues; mobile radio, checkout counters, traffic lights, pay telephones and network nodes are alike, and the algorithm of §1 "has been applied" to ethernet transmission data (Gawlick 1990) (p. 1). The entry: Larson's two observations (busy periods can be identified from the log; under Poisson arrivals each customer's arrival time is uniform on a known interval) and his two algorithms (p. 1), which the paper speeds up and generalizes.
- **D2 Core idea**: Given that n customers arrived by the end of a busy period, Poisson arrival times are uniform order statistics (eq. 1, p. 2), and each observed service start bounds an arrival time from above. The posterior of the number present at time t is therefore a ratio of integrals over ordered simplices, which an iterated-integral identity (Lemma 1, p. 4) turns into two triangular recursions (h_jk, f_jk; Steps 1–4, p. 3). Steps 5–6 give the queue-length distribution and bounds on waiting-time moments (pp. 3, 6–7). The same algorithm runs for a time-varying Poisson process after the time change y = Λ(t) (Theorem 4, p. 8). An O(n) on-line recursion updates the estimate after each departure, likened to Kalman filtering (pp. 1, 9–10). Renewal arrivals get a formula that is "the basis of the algorithm" (Theorem 5, pp. 8–9), evaluated only for Erlang-2, with Mathematica (p. 11).
- **D3 Model and data**: Poisson arrivals with an unknown rate that is constant within a busy period and may change between busy periods (p. 2); service times, discipline and number of servers arbitrary, the only assumption being that all servers are busy whenever customers queue (p. 1). The time-varying case needs Λ(t) to be known (p. 8); the renewal case needs a known interarrival density (p. 8). Data: synthetic, regularly spaced departure epochs t_i = i/n (pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Lemma 1 by induction (p. 4); Propositions 1–2 justify the recursions by coefficient matching through Taylor's theorem (pp. 4–5); Theorem 1 gives the queue-length distribution (pp. 5–6). Between departures N(t) is binomial given its values at the neighbouring departures, so E[Q(t)] is piecewise linear; Larson had proved this too, but the authors' proof "more easily generalizes" to time-varying rates (p. 6). Theorem 2 bounds waiting-time moments by integration by parts (pp. 6–7); Theorem 3 is the order-statistic density for a non-homogeneous Poisson process (p. 7); Theorem 6 proves the on-line recursion (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: §1.2's algorithm in a straightforward implementation on a SUN 3 workstation, with n² memory, handles up to n = 99 departures per busy period "reliably in less than 40 seconds"; most practical busy periods have fewer than 100 departures, by their account (p. 10). Figures: estimates for n = 99 and the average queue as a function of n (pp. 10–11); exponential against Erlang-2 interarrivals for n = 5, λ = 10 (p. 11); λ = 10 against 100 for Erlang-2 (pp. 11–12). The abstract promises results on "speed and accuracy" (p. 1), but there is no comparison with Larson's algorithms and no test against simulated true queues (pp. 10–12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A faster exact algorithm, waiting times, time-varying and renewal arrivals, and an on-line version (pp. 1–2). Limits printed: "Our methods in the previous sections critically depend on the Poisson assumption" (p. 8); λ(t) must be known (p. 8); estimates beyond the last departure need the service distribution and the number of servers (p. 10). The paper ends with its computational section, with no conclusions or future work (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 9): the model is shaped by data an operator already holds (Method 5, step 3), and the on-line version is motivated by real-time control of service (p. 9). But the output is an estimate, not a decision; there is no owner's rule and no field data in the evaluation.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 7–8): a class-keeping device outside optimization: the time change keeps the time-varying case inside the same algorithm "without adding any complexity" (p. 7), and the price, printed, is that λ(t) must be known (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device* (p. 6): Larson's piecewise linearity re-proved by a binomial argument that carries over to time-varying rates.
- New-pattern candidate: *infer the unobserved state from the data the system already logs* (pp. 1–2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When only event times are logged, condition on the total count: Poisson arrival times become uniform order statistics, the logged events become ordering constraints, and the resulting simplex integrals can be computed by recursion (pp. 2–5).
- Reduce a non-homogeneous Poisson model to the homogeneous one by the time change y = Λ(t) and reuse the algorithm unchanged (pp. 7–8).
- Turn a batch posterior computation into an O(n) update after each event (pp. 9–10).

**Quotes**:
- "In this analysis, no assumptions are required regarding the service time distribution: it could be state-dependent, dependent on the time of day, with or without correlations." (p. 1)
- "This algorithm is similar to Kalman filtering in structure in that the current estimates for future queue lengths are updated dynamically in real time." (p. 1)

### S102 · Further results on the probabilistic traveling salesman problem (European Journal of Operational Research 1993, DOI 10.1016/0377-2217(93)90145-d)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–28 (abstract, §§1–8, figure captions; reference list skimmed). Many displayed formulas are garbled, including the bound of Proposition 1 and the rates in Theorems 15–18. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas (Sloan) and Howell (MIT Department of Mathematics) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Louis H. Howell (MIT Department of Mathematics, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received June 1990, revised March 1991 (p. 1); a sequel to his 1988 thesis on probabilistic combinatorial optimization (ref. [5]) and to the PMST paper S148 (ref. [6]), with thanks to his adviser Odoni and to Jaillet (p. 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + SD; AP (routing) as motivation}: Jaillet's PTSP (1985): find an a priori tour through n points of minimal expected length when only a random subset is present and the present points are visited in the a priori order (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Reasons not to re-optimize every day: limited resources and time, and the regularity and personalization of service (the same driver for the same customer), with meals-on-wheels, forklifts and mail delivery as examples; Odoni first formulated the generic problem for the mail case (p. 2). The entry: Jaillet's lower bound held only for prime n, an "unnatural distinction" (p. 5); the paper sharpens the bounds and compares the a priori strategy with re-optimization from several viewpoints (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Jaillet's closed form for the expected length of a given tour (eqs. 2–3, p. 3) is the evaluation engine, and the problem is attacked from several sides:
  - a cycle-structure argument proves a strengthened form of Jaillet's conjecture for odd n (Proposition 1, pp. 5–7);
  - LP duality turns a lower bound into a transportation problem (Proposition 2, pp. 7–8);
  - a tour induced by a tree costs at most twice the tree in the PMST sense, which gives an MST heuristic within 2d of optimal for an MST of diameter d (Proposition 6, Theorems 7 and 10, pp. 11–13);
  - sorting heuristics keep their order on every subset, so the space-filling-curve tour is O(log n) from re-optimization in the worst case and within about 25% of it for uniform random points (Theorems 16–17, pp. 2, 17–18);
  - the nearest-neighbour tour is poor even on average (Theorem 18, pp. 18–19);
  - local search with O(n²) sweeps (2-p-opt, 1-shift) for computation (pp. 20–24).
- **D3 Model and data**: n known points present independently with probabilities p_i, often all equal to p (p. 3); some results hold under correlated presence (Proposition 6, p. 11; Theorem 16, p. 18). Symmetry and the triangle inequality are assumed where needed (pp. 6–8, 11); distinct costs, "not a truly restrictive assumption", for Proposition 9 (p. 12). Probabilistic models: uniform points in the unit square (p. 18); costs uniform on (0, 1) with p_1 = 1 (pp. 18–19). Data: 10 random 100-node instances averaged, and single instances of 150 and 5,000 nodes (pp. 20–25).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: the symmetric cycle structure of an optimal tour for odd n (pp. 5–7); dual node potentials (pp. 7–8); f(p), the minimum over tours of finitely many analytic functions, is continuous, decreasing and piecewise convex (Proposition 5, p. 9); induced tours and an MST exchange argument (Lemma 8, pp. 11–13); a solvable special case, d(i, j) = u_i + v_j, in which all tours have the same expected length (Proposition 11, pp. 13–14); Jensen's inequality and a lemma of Rosenkrantz, Stearns and Lewis for a lower bound on re-optimization (pp. 14–15); Theorem 15 bounds how far any constant-guarantee TSP heuristic can be from the PTSP optimum (p. 17; the rate is garbled); order statistics for the nearest-neighbour analysis (p. 19).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Random tour, angular sorting, space-filling curve, 2-p-opt and 1-shift against 2-opt and 3-opt TSP tours, on 10 averaged 100-node problems, with the BHH constant 0.765 as the reference line (Figure 8, pp. 24–26); n = 25–200 (Figure 9, p. 25); TSP tours at n = 100 against 500 (Figure 10, p. 26). Findings: 1-shift is "usually optimal" for small p, a conclusion based on convergence to the same tour from every start (p. 25); TSP tours look good at n = 100 but degrade at n = 500, where the poor behaviour that small instances mask appears (p. 26). Limits printed: running time and O(n²) memory become excessive beyond a few hundred nodes (p. 25). No run times and no certified optima are reported.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Sharper bounds, a solvable special case, links to the PMST, worst-case and probabilistic comparisons with re-optimization, and computational advice: the space-filling curve, then 3-opt if p is large, then 1-shift (pp. 1, 25). Open: a constant-guarantee heuristic for the PTSP under the triangle inequality (p. 17); even n in Proposition 1 (p. 7); the structure of optimal tours as p → 0 (p. 25). The conclusion names two motives for probabilistic combinatorial optimization: realism, and the robustness of deterministic optima when instances change (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 20, 24–26): the incumbent heuristic is a warm start (space-filling-curve tours seed 2-p-opt and 1-shift), and the incumbent practice (good TSP tours) is dueled at growing n until its weakness shows (Figure 10, p. 26). There is no exact solve and no certificate; "usually optimal" rests on restarts converging to one tour (p. 25), an early instance of Tension T1.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 7–8): the dual of an LP yields a bound for a stochastic combinatorial problem, "a mathematical programming rather than a combinatorial approach" (p. 7), but the problem itself is not written in a solver class.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 14–18, 24–26): the price of a priori routing (regularity of service, p. 2) is measured against re-optimization: a worst-case ratio of O(log n), about 25% for random points, and curves in p. It is never framed as the price of a property, and p is a parameter of the model, not a knob.
- New-pattern candidate: *a priori solution with a cheap update rule, priced against re-solving* (pp. 2, 14–18).
- New-pattern candidate: *distribution-free guarantee from a heuristic that ignores the probabilities* (pp. 18, 20): the space-filling-curve bound "does not depend on the probabilities" and holds under dependence (p. 18), so the heuristic can be used even when the probabilities "are not accurately known" (p. 20).
- New-pattern candidate: *robustness of deterministic optima to instance changes as a stated motive* (p. 27), about a decade before his RO programme. Whether it led there is not shown; the Domain Map records why he entered RO as unknown.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove a lower bound for a stochastic combinatorial problem by subtracting node potentials from the costs and dualizing: the best such bound is an LP, here a transportation problem (pp. 7–8).
- For a priori problems, prefer heuristics whose output on a subset equals their full output restricted to that subset (sorting, space-filling curves); worst-case and probabilistic guarantees for the deterministic problem then transfer, whatever the presence distribution (pp. 17–18).
- For local search on an expected-value objective, keep auxiliary partial-sum matrices so that a whole neighbourhood sweep costs O(n²), and choose the neighbourhood that is a small change under the stochastic objective (moving one node rather than reversing a section) (pp. 21–24).
- Before concluding that an incumbent is good enough, rerun the comparison at larger n to see whether a small-instance regime masks its failure (p. 26).

**Quotes**:
- "If we experiment with larger problems, though, it becomes apparent that the expected poor behavior is simply masked by the transition into the low-p regime described above." (p. 26)
- "The second motivation is interest in investigating the robustness (with respect to optimality) of optimal solutions to deterministic problems, when the instances for which these problems have been solved are modified." (p. 27)

### S293 · Optimization of polling systems and dynamic vehicle routing problems on networks (MIT Operations Research Center working paper OR 283-93 1993, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–38 (abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1–3, references). Some formulas and Table 3's header are garbled; Table 3's columns were identified from the observations on p. 36 and checked by arithmetic against its own entries. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas (Sloan and ORC) and Xu (MIT Center for Transportation Studies) match (pp. 1, 3).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Haiping Xu (MIT Center for Transportation Studies, p. 3; thanked among his PhD students in S580, p. 49) · **Position in trajectory**: December 1993, PYI-funded with Draper matching funds (pp. 1, 3); the primary paper behind the polling section of the achievable-region surveys (S580 §3, pp. 23–29; S185, card c03).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO; CP for the bound; AP (transportation, computing, communication, production) as motivation}: Sequence one server among N queues with switch-over times so as to minimize weighted mean waiting (pp. 5, 7). The polling literature "almost exclusively addresses the performance of specific policies rather than the optimal design" (p. 7), and no paper proposes a policy together with a performance guarantee (p. 9). In transportation the server is a vehicle, and static policies may be "the only realistic policy" because the vehicle does not see the network's state (p. 8); the work extends the Bertsimas–van Ryzin Euclidean DVR line "using drastically different techniques" (p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea**: Bound first, policy second (p. 9). Variables m_ij, the average number of server moves from station i to j per unit time, enter a convex program with flow conservation and the stability condition Σ d_ij m_ij ≤ 1 − ρ; its optimum bounds every static policy (Theorem 1, pp. 14–16). The bound's solution is then turned into policies by a "closeness condition": random routing with probabilities from the normalized m_ij, and routing tables from a small integer program that minimizes the largest gap between integer switch-over counts and their targets, with connectivity relaxed and the table length kept between N and L_max (pp. 23–26).
- **D3 Model and data**: Poisson arrivals, general service times with known first two moments, linear waiting costs, asymmetric switch-over times; stable iff ρ < 1 (p. 7). Policies are non-preemptive, non-anticipative and stable, FIFO within a class (pp. 10–11). The key assumption, stated: the server's behaviour is independent of a tagged customer's arrival, true for static policies only (pp. 16–17). Test systems: Boxma, Levy and Weststrate's three-station example (ρ = 0.84) and a four-station example built so that a known routing table is optimal (pp. 28, 34).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: each station is an M/G/1 queue with vacations, analysed by residual times, PASTA and Little's law (Proposition 1, pp. 11–14); the switch-over-free optimum, a strict-priority rule, gives a second bound (pp. 14–15); v⁽²⁾ ≥ v² and flow conservation give the convex program (pp. 15–16); a forward-recurrence argument strengthens it for homogeneous exhaustive systems (Theorem 2, pp. 17–19); dropping flow conservation and solving the KKT conditions gives closed forms (Theorem 3, pp. 19–21); a best-a-priori-location argument bounds all policies, dynamic ones included (Theorem 4, pp. 21–23). Tightness: the exhaustive cyclic policy attains the bound in symmetric systems (Theorem 5, p. 27), and random routing is within a factor of 2, approaching 2 (Theorem 6, pp. 27–28). Heavy traffic: m_ij → 0 as ρ → 1, which motivates the conjecture that the static bound also holds for dynamic policies (p. 17).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Simulation with 10 replications of 10⁶ time units, the first 50,000 discarded (p. 28). On Boxma et al.'s example their table 121213 is 3.5% above the bound and the paper's 14-entry table 2.2% (p. 30). Over d from 0.01 to 1000 the average ratios to the bound are 1.044 (Boxma et al.), 1.026 (the paper's table), 1.054 (cyclic), 1.117 (random) and 1.012 (a most-loaded-queue dynamic policy), whose ratio falls below 1 for small d, where the static bound does not apply (Table 1, p. 31). With asymmetric switch-over times the table is within 2% of the bound (pp. 32–34). On the four-station example the table sits at the bound, "as we constructed the arrival rates so that the policy will be optimal" (p. 36). Not reported: the convex and integer solvers and their run times.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Bounds for static policies and for all policies, routing tables within 3% of the bound on the cases studied, and a map of when dynamic policies pay (pp. 36–37). Limits: the heavy-traffic conjecture is unproven; the constructed example favours the table; the random policies were not optimized (p. 36). As extracted, Table 3's last row gives 1.002 for the dynamic policy against 1.004 for the table, which does not bear out the prose claim that in very heavy traffic "the static policy is actually better" (p. 36; my reading). Open problem: techniques for "provably near optimal dynamic policies" (p. 37).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 7–9, 14–21, 31): a problem the queueing literature analyses policy by policy is written as a convex program over flow-like variables, "a network flow problem with a side constraint" in the survey's words (S580 p. 26); KKT multipliers give closed forms (pp. 20–21); the boundary is printed: the static bound fails for dynamic policies at small d (p. 31), and the heavy-traffic extension is labelled a conjecture (pp. 17, 37).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 23–31): bound, policy and gap: every policy is reported as a ratio to the relaxation's bound, and the incumbent (Boxma et al.'s heuristic table) is dueled on its own example. Integer programming builds the policy, but no warm start, solver, time limit or IP optimality is reported.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (p. 3; S580 p. 49): a student paper inside the achievable-region programme, later consolidated in the sole-author surveys.

- **D2 Core idea**: At each decision time, re-solve a compact pure 0-1 program over departure and arrival time slots, with the implemented decisions fixed and the flight sets updated (I1, pp. 11–14). A second form assumes infinite departure capacities, gives airborne aircraft priority and precomputes the arrival "excesses" (I2, pp. 13–17). Extensions keep the size: cancellations with "exactly the same number of variables and of constraints" (p. 19); a polytope of departure–arrival capacity trade-offs with "exactly the same (number of) variables" and slightly more constraints (pp. 19–21); hub connections and en-route speeding (pp. 21–23). No theorems.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: The runs solve the LP relaxation, not the IP, because the two values are very close for identical cost functions in the static work, and "rounding mistakes are expected" (p. 31). Most-probable and worst-case forecasting are compared with a static random-selection policy over probability pairs (Tables 5–6, pp. 35–38). The formulations are "a method rather than a mere bag of tricks" (p. 42). - **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + LO + IO + CP}: Stochastic and dynamic optimization lacks "a unified method of attack": each problem gets "its own somewhat adhoc approach", and how close a solution is to optimal is often unclear (p. 5). Mathematical programming, by contrast, has a few formulations and an a priori plan of attack that yields optimal solutions or guarantees (p. 5). The paper sets a research program: (1) a unified approach that gives a feasible solution and a guarantee and "should not fundamentally change when the problem changes"; (2) a complexity classification of the classical problems (p. 5). The map: indexable systems in P; restless bandits PSPACE-hard; polling NP-hard (it contains the TSP) and PSPACE-hard; multiclass queueing and loss networks EXPTIME-hard (pp. 6–7).
- **D2 Core idea**: Characterize the achievable region of performance vectors and optimize over it, through relaxations that tighten as valid inequalities do in integer programming (Figures 2–3, pp. 7–9), under the thesis that better formulations lead to deeper understanding and better solution methods (abstract, p. 3; p. 8). Three routes to policies (pp. 9–10): an adaptive greedy algorithm whose dual variables give indices (indexable systems, §2); integer programming on the relaxation's solution (polling, §3); and linear control of a deterministic fluid model, then simulation against the bound (§6). The third rests on the thesis that the difficulty is "primarily the dynamic and combinatorial character of the problem and only secondarily the stochastic character" (pp. 10, 45).
- **D3 Model and data**: Markov bandits and branching bandits under nonidling, nonpreemptive, nonanticipative policies (pp. 10, 18–19); polling under static policies (pp. 23–25); open networks with Poisson arrivals, exponential services and Markovian policies that satisfy Assumption A, adopted "For technical reasons" (p. 30), phase-type services said to be an easy extension (p. 38); loss networks with Poisson arrivals and exponential holding times (pp. 41–42); a deterministic three-class fluid network (pp. 44–45). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: extended polymatroids whose vertices are the priority rules (Theorem 1, p. 14); the adaptive greedy algorithm runs in O(n³), and its dual variables sum to generalized Gittins indices (pp. 15–16); index decomposition (Theorem 3, p. 17); conservation laws imply the region (Theorems 4–5, p. 18). Polling: the convex bound and closed forms (Theorems 6–7, pp. 25–26). Networks: the potential-function method, proved in full "since it is instructive" (Theorem 8, pp. 31–32); the nonparametric version with O(R²) variables and constraints instead of R variables and O(2^R) constraints (Theorem 9, pp. 34–35); a quadratic-size reformulation of the extended polymatroid, which proves a polynomial-formulation conjecture for this case (Theorem 10, pp. 35–37); higher-order constraints generated with Maple, and PSD strengthening (pp. 38–40). Loss networks: Theorems 11–12 (pp. 42–44). Fluid control: Pontryagin's principle gives threshold policies (Theorem 13, p. 46).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: No new computation. §§3.3 and 4.5 summarize the cohort's simulations (pp. 28–29, 40–41); the loss-network comparison with Kelly's bounds rests on "very limited computational experience" (p. 44). The summary restates S293's conjecture that the static polling bound holds for dynamic policies and widens it with "or for large changeover costs" (p. 29), where S293 states heavy traffic only (S293 p. 37).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A five-step method: variables; relaxations; an exact solution or a bound; policies built by integer programming; fluid control plus simulation, compared with the bounds for a suboptimality guarantee (pp. 47–48). A complexity reading: the higher the order of relaxation needed for exactness, the harder the problem (p. 48). Open problems: systematic policies from relaxations; bounds on heuristics' distance from relaxations; higher-order and piecewise-linear potentials; an efficient algorithm for the continuous LP (p. 48). Limits: the stochastic threshold curve is only conjectured from the fluid solution (pp. 46–47), and the partition of the state space "is combinatorially explosive" (p. 47).

### S129 · The distributional Little's law and its applications (Operations Research 1995, DOI 10.1287/opre.43.2.298)

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Daisuke Nakazato (MIT, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received March 1991, revised January, June and October 1992, accepted March 1993 (p. 1); the queueing strand {SD} that S336 (card in s02) later extended into a unified method for overtake-free systems. - **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD}: Little's law and its extensions relate means, not distributions; to find the distributions of L and W, researchers exploit each system's particular structure (p. 1). Haji and Newell proved a distributional law under FIFO, and Keilson and Servi treated Poisson arrivals (p. 1). The entry: show that this more restrictive law can derive performance measures across systems "in a unified way" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: L has the distribution of the number of equilibrium-renewal arrivals during W (Theorem 1, pp. 2–3). Two proofs: a first-principles one, with customers numbered backward from a random time, and a new matrix proof for mixed generalized Erlang (MGE) arrivals, which generalizes Burke's theorem on pre-transition and post-departure probabilities and yields a closed-form kernel (Theorems 3–4, pp. 4–7). - **D3 Model and data**: Stationary arrivals one at a time; no blocking, balking or reneging; FIFO; later arrivals do not affect earlier customers' times (Theorem 1, p. 2). Renewal arrivals from p. 3 on, and MGE arrivals for the second proof, a class that "can approximate any renewal arrival process arbitrarily closely" (pp. 4–5). Batch arrivals are excluded because they would need geometric batch sizes, "somewhat restrictive" (p. 3); the law fails for the number in a multi-server system, where customers overtake (p. 4). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: L ≥ n iff the n-th most recent arrival is still present (pp. 2–3); renewal generating-function kernels and contour integrals (pp. 3–4); uniformization of the phase process and PASTA (pp. 5–6); flow balance of up and down jumps (Theorem 3, p. 6); a rank-one inverse identity (p. 5); expansions of the kernel in log z and of the renewal function's transform as s → 0 (pp. 8–9); singularity matching for the inverse problem (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a (worked transform examples only, e.g. Erlang-2 arrivals, pp. 8–9).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: By the authors' account most results "were known in the literature"; what is new is the unifying derivation (p. 2), and the asymptotic relations (19)–(20) are new "to the best of our knowledge" (p. 8). Limits: heavy-traffic validity and neglected o(1) terms (pp. 8, 10); overtaking and batches lie outside the law (pp. 3–4). Closing claim: for overtake-free systems, the laws for the system and the queue together suffice to "fully characterize the queueing system" (p. 12). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1–4, 12): the formulation-first instinct in probabilistic form: one law to unify derivations that were system-specific, with the class boundary printed (pp. 3–4). There is no optimization, so this is the probability side of Tension T3, as card S336 found for its sequel.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To relate a count and a time in steady state, number customers backward from a random observation time: the count is at least n exactly when the n-th most recent arrival is still waiting (pp. 2–3).
