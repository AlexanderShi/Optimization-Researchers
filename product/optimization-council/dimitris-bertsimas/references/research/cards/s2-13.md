- Is there one knob, with a curve handed to the decision-maker (Method 3)?
- Which lineage and which partner carry the work (Method 6)?

### S238 · Selecting children with vesicoureteral reflux who are most likely to benefit from antibiotic prophylaxis: application of machine learning to RIVUR (The Journal of Urology 2021, DOI 10.1097/ju.0000000000001445)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–10 of the journal print. Read: the abstract, introduction, Materials and Methods, Results, Tables 1–3, Discussion, the appendix table of risk groups A–D, the references and the two invited editorial comments. Figures 1–4 keep only their captions. First-page check: the title and the authors, Bertsimas among them, match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Li (MIT ORC); Carlos Estrada, Caleb Nelson and Hsin-Hsiao Scott Wang (Boston Children's Hospital, Advanced Analytics Group of Pediatric Urology) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the 2019–2026 health-care period.
- The byline is ordered by group. The contribution note names Wang "first and lead author", and Bertsimas, Estrada and Nelson "senior co-last authors"; Li carried out the analysis (p. 1).
- The MIT side brings optimal tree imputation and OCT (refs 11–12, p. 2).
- The work was presented at the AUA annual meeting in May 2019 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + DA; AP (pediatric urology)}:
  - Continuous antibiotic prophylaxis (CAP) halves recurrent UTI (rUTI) in children with VUR (RIVUR) (pp. 1–2).
  - But the trial was underpowered for long-term outcomes such as scarring, and growth and resistance effects are debated. So "more selective use" beats one size for all (p. 2).
  - Clinicians already personalize CAP by index-UTI severity and VUR grade, based on experience and without transparent criteria (p. 2).
  - Problem: predict each child's rUTI risk with and without CAP, and treat those whose risk drops most (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - One optimal classification tree is fitted per trial arm (CAP, placebo). The difference of the two predicted probabilities is the child's CAP benefit, and CAP is assigned above a risk-reduction cutoff (pp. 2–4).
  - A decision table maps the four arm-risk combinations to decisions. A predicted *increase* under CAP is read as an artifact of unmeasured confounders, and such children are handled like group B, with no CAP (group D, p. 8).
  - Validation on the held-out 20%: children whose random assignment matched the recommendation ("compliers") are compared with those whose did not ("defiers"). A Neyman repeated-sampling test is applied to the null that the compliers' ATE is at most the defiers' (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - All 607 RIVUR children: 19 US centers; randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled; age 2 months to 6 years; grade I–IV VUR; 2-year follow-up (p. 2). The arms are 302 CAP and 305 placebo; 91% are female and the median age is 12 months (p. 3).
  - Predictors are limited to what is known after the index UTI and before CAP assignment (p. 2).
  - Missing values are imputed by optimal tree imputation (p. 2). Creatinine, a split variable of the final tree (Table 3, p. 6), is missing in 110 (placebo) and 115 (CAP) children (p. 4).
  - The split is 20% test, with a 4:1 train/validation split within each arm and fivefold CV; the software is Julia 0.6.0 and SAS 9.4 (pp. 2–3).
  - Limits the authors admit: the age range, female predominance and a low rUTI rate; the exclusion of congenital anomalies; a small cohort; BBD not assessable; inter-observer variability of the VUR grade (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a as a formulation; OCT is used as a tool. Devices: the per-arm risk difference (p. 2) and the complier/defier contrast (p. 3).
  - My reading: with arms of near-equal size (302 vs 305, p. 3), compliers and defiers share the same covariate mix in expectation. So their gap compares the rule with its opposite, and their mean is random assignment, which is the claim made on p. 7.
  - Neither treating every child (the trial's finding, pp. 1–2) nor clinicians' current selection (p. 2) is scored on the held-out set.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - The rUTI model has an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI 0.74–0.87) (p. 3).
  - The abstract claims that with a 10% cutoff, treating 40% instead of everyone reaches the minimal population rUTI (p. 1). The 40% does not appear in the body, where the population curve against random assignment (Fig. 4) is described only qualitatively (pp. 5, 7).
  - Held out: 51 compliers and 70 defiers have rUTI of 7.5% and 19.4%, p = 0.037 (p. 1) or 0.04 (p. 7). My reading: 7.5% of 51 and 19.4% of 70 are not whole-patient counts, and the denominators are not explained.
  - Table 3 lists risk reductions per leaf from 0% to 51.85% (p. 6).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - The contribution is an interpretable subgroup map for selective CAP that needs at most 7 clinical variables (9 in all) (p. 8; Table 3, p. 6).
  - The limits are specific (generalizability, size, BBD, VUR grading), and the model is called imperfect. Future work: more data and ureteral diameter (p. 8).
  - The two invited editorial comments speak for their writers, not the authors. DeMarco asks whether creatinine is signal or "statistical noise" (p. 9). Hannick notes that the AUC rests on an arbitrary high/low cutoff, and that the algorithm is cumbersome and not yet directly applicable (pp. 9–10).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2–3, 5, 7): decisions, not only predictions, are scored on held-out randomized data, and the population trade-off is drawn (Fig. 4). But the owner's current rules (treat all, or clinicians' experience-based selection, pp. 1–2) are not the scored baseline; the comparison is compliers against defiers.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 4–5, 7): one knob, the risk-reduction cutoff, traces the share treated against population rUTI and is set against random assignment. It is left to families and physicians (an 18% reduction may or may not be "worthwhile", p. 5). There is no bound, and the curve is shown only as a figure.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–2): the group's tools (OCT, optimal imputation) enter a clinical team's trial reanalysis, published in a clinical journal with Bertsimas as co-senior author.
- New-pattern candidate: concordance split on held-out randomized data (pp. 2–3, 7).
- New-pattern candidate: analytics partner inside a clinical team's study (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To score a treatment rule on trial data, split the held-out patients by whether their random arm agreed with the rule. With equal arms, the outcome gap estimates the rule against its opposite, and the mean of the two groups is random assignment (pp. 2–3, 7; the equal-arm argument is my reading).
- Fit one model per trial arm and read the treatment benefit as the difference of the two predicted risks. Send children with a predicted harm (negative benefit) to no treatment, and say why (pp. 2, 8).
- Give the cutoff as a knob with a population curve (share treated against outcome) next to random allocation, not as a single operating point (pp. 4–5, 7).

**Quotes**:
- "If the model assigned CAP treatment would not bring any benefit compared to baseline, then one expects the ATE of the compliers would be the same as defiers." (p. 3)
- "It is important to recognize that this prediction model is imperfect, primarily due to the inherent size limitation." (p. 8)

### S247 · Near-optimal nonlinear regression trees (Operations Research Letters 2021, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2021.01.002)
Read level: **full, preprint version**: file pp. 1–16, the Elsevier preprint "submitted to Operation Research Letters January 4, 2021" (p. 1), cited as [S247 preprint p. N]. The printed ORL pages 201–206 were not available. Formulas (1)–(6) are partly garbled, so claims about them rest on the prose. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Dunn and Wang match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jack Dunn and Yuchen Wang (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the Optimal Trees programme: OCT 2017, the 2019 book and Dunn's 2018 thesis (refs [2]–[4], pp. 1–2). It carries regression trees from linear leaves (ORT-LH) to polynomial ones.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; the engine is nonconvex first-order optimization, not MIO}:
  - CART is greedy and only locally optimal (p. 1), and constant leaves limit regression trees (p. 2).
  - The usual repairs fall short (p. 2):
    - fitting linear leaves after a constant tree gives trees larger than needed;
    - splitting on the most significant variable is not the optimal split;
    - stepwise two-variable splits do not generalize to hyperplanes.
  - ORT, ORT-L and ORT-LH improve out-of-sample R² over CART by 1.4%, 21.2% and 23.4% on 60 UCI sets. MARS predicts polynomials of split values, but greedily (p. 2).
  - The entry is to combine MARS with ORT-LH (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Hyperplane splits, with a leaf prediction that sums products of |aᵀx − b| along the root-to-leaf path (eq. 1, p. 4).
  - The identity |aᵀx − b|·1{aᵀx < b} = max(b − aᵀx, 0) rewrites the tree as products of hinge functions. The whole tree becomes one unconstrained problem: squared loss plus a ridge penalty on (a, b, f), "motivated by robustness considerations" (Bertsimas–Copenhaver, ref. [13]) (eqs. 3–5, p. 6).
  - Algorithm 1: the start is the best of M random (a, b) draws, each with leaf coefficients f fitted by ridge regression. Each iteration takes an Adam step on (a, b) and then solves the ridge problem for f exactly, which the authors found experimentally better than joint updates (pp. 6–8).
  - Table 1 classifies tree methods by split (univariate/multivariate), leaf (constant/linear/polynomial) and solution (greedy/optimal). NNRT fills the multivariate–polynomial–"near-optimal" cell (p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Features are rescaled to [0, 1] (p. 3). There are two knobs, depth D and penalty λ, tuned on validation data (Algorithm 2, pp. 7–8).
  - Synthetic data: random hyperplane trees with polynomial leaves, for D ∈ {2, 3, 4}, n ∈ {10,000; 100,000} and m ∈ {10, 50, 250}. Five trees per setting, λ = 0 and one random start (pp. 7, 9).
  - Real data: 40 UCI regression sets, with 50/25/25 splits repeated five times (pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - There is no convergence or optimality analysis. The abstract's "converges to the global optimal" (p. 1) rests on recovering synthetic ground truths (R² 0.996–0.999, Table 2, p. 9).
  - My reading: hinge products are only piecewise differentiable, although the paper calls the objective differentiable (pp. 3, 7). No bound or gap is computed, so "near-optimal" is a name, not a certificate.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: 40 UCI sets, depths 1–12 and 50 random starts (p. 11). The baselines:
    - MARS (R earth), trimmed to the same depth with its penalty tuned;
    - random forests (R);
    - XGBoost (η = 0.1);
    - ORT-LH (OptimalTrees, autotuned).
  - NNRT is written in Julia and was run on the MIT Engaging cluster with 4 cores and 16 GB (p. 11).
  - Mean out-of-sample R² is 0.753 for NNRT, against 0.74 (XGBoost, ORT-LH), 0.736 (RF) and 0.71 (MARS) (Table 4, p. 12).
  - Pairwise wins: 34–6 over MARS, 24–16 over RF, 23–15 with 2 ties over XGBoost, and 28–12 over ORT-LH (Table 5, p. 13).
  - Synthetic runs take 20–910 s (Tables 2–3, pp. 9–10); no real-data run times are given.
  - My reading of the reporting:
    - the text ranks XGBoost 2.8 and ORT-LH 3.0, while Table 4 gives the reverse (pp. 11–12);
    - the text says "NNRTs at depth three outperform all other methods at depth twelve" (p. 12), but Table 6 shows a tie at 0.74 with XGBoost and ORT-LH (p. 13);
    - some means lie outside their intervals (e.g., RF on abalone, p. 12);
    - references [21]–[23] on comparing classifiers statistically are listed (p. 16), but no test is reported.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - The contribution is a new formulation of regression trees, a gradient method for it and an R² edge on 40 sets (pp. 3, 14).
  - The stated limits are two sentences: multivariate splits are harder to interpret than univariate ones, and training is much slower than XGBoost on larger sets (p. 13). No future work is stated (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 3, 6–9, 11): it follows step 1 (one joint model for all splits and leaves) and step 5 (a duel with the greedy incumbent's standard implementation at matched depth, its penalty tuned). But nothing is solved by MIO and no certificate or gap is reported. The warm start is random rather than the incumbent, and global optimality is claimed from ground-truth recovery (Tension T1).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 6): formulation first, with the whole tree written as one optimization problem. But the class is a nonconvex unconstrained NLP trained by Adam, not LP, conic or MIO. The ridge term follows Heuristic 1 (robustness behind regularization, p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: Fill the missing corner (Table 1, p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: first-order surrogate for an MIO-born model class (pp. 3, 6–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Rewrite an indicator-times-distance term as a hinge, |aᵀx − b|·1{aᵀx < b} = max(b − aᵀx, 0). The tree's prediction then becomes one piecewise-smooth function of its split parameters (p. 6).
- Alternate first-order steps on the hard block with an exact closed-form solve on the easy block (ridge for the leaf coefficients given the splits) (pp. 6–8).
- Before the real data, show recovery of synthetic ground truths across (depth, n, m). Add a second synthetic test, with a linear truth fitted by the polynomial model, to show that the regularizer suppresses complexity that is not needed (pp. 9–10).
- Tabulate accuracy against maximum depth for every method, so readers see how shallow the new model can be (Table 6, pp. 12–13).

**Quotes**:
- "These experimental results suggest that NNRTs recovered the ground truth and converged to an optimal solution." (p. 9)
- "Comparing to CART or MARS which use univariate splits, NNRTs use multivariate splits which are harder to interpret." (p. 13)

### S276 · Prediction of neutropenic events in chemotherapy patients: A machine learning approach (JCO Clinical Cancer Informatics 2021, DOI 10.1200/cci.21.00046)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–8 of the journal print. Read: the abstract, introduction, Materials and Methods, Results, Tables 1–4, Figures 1–2 (captions and axis values), Discussion, affiliations, support, disclosures and references. The Data Supplement (temporal split, implementation details) is not in the file. First-page check: the title and the authors, Bertsimas last, match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Holly Wiberg (MIT ORC); Peter Yu, Pat Montanaro, Jeff Mather, Suzi Birz and Michelle Schneider (Hartford HealthCare) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the Hartford HealthCare partnership of the health-care period.
- The work is supported by a grant from Hartford HealthCare, and Bertsimas is the corresponding author (p. 7).
- The method is the group's own tools: optimal feature selection (OFS, ref. 12) and MedImpute (ref. 10) (pp. 2–3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP (oncology); DA in the threshold analysis}:
  - Severe and febrile neutropenia (SN-FN) endanger chemotherapy patients. ASCO recommends G-CSF prophylaxis when SN-FN risk exceeds 20% (p. 1).
  - Existing models are logistic regression or uninterpretable ML (p. 1).
  - The gaps it fills (p. 1):
    - risk at any cycle, not only the first;
    - a broad range of cancers and drugs;
    - only discrete EMR fields, so the model can run directly off the record.
- **D2 Core idea**: An end-to-end pipeline from EMR extraction to implementation (pp. 1–4):
  - curated discrete EMR features;
  - MedImpute for missing values;
  - six classifiers compared on performance and interpretability;
  - a 20-feature OFS model as the final sparse additive score;
  - a cost-based alert threshold that minimizes C_FP·#FP + C_FN·#FN and depends only on the cost ratio (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Hartford HealthCare's seven acute-care hospitals; chemotherapy encounters from May 2016 to October 2019. Leukemia is excluded, and each cycle start is one observation (pp. 1–2).
  - The outcome is SN (ANC < 500) or FN (ANC < 1,000 with fever > 101°F) within 28 days (p. 2).
  - There are 107 features; those missing in more than 40% of encounters are dropped (p. 2).
  - The 80/20 split is by patient, and the two sets are imputed separately. Tuning is by 5-fold CV; the temporal split appears only in the supplement (p. 2).
  - The data hold 17,513 encounters, 2,806 patients and 449 events (2.6%) (p. 4).
  - Limits the authors admit: a retrospective design with no causation (p. 6), and structured fields only, which omits relative dose intensity and qualitative assessments for the sake of portability (p. 6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a as a formulation; OFS is used as a tool. The device is the cost-ratio threshold (pp. 3–4). My reading: the cost-minimizing threshold is computed "on the test set" (p. 4), so the reported operating point (specificity 95.7%, sensitivity 42.9%) is chosen in-sample.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Test AUCs run from 0.789 to 0.869 and average precision from 0.080 to 0.148, against a no-skill precision of 0.026 (Table 3, p. 4).
  - OFS20 reaches AUC 0.865, second to RF's 0.869, with the highest average precision (0.148). LR reaches 0.858, XGB 0.819, the group's OCT 0.805 and CART 0.789 (p. 4).
  - Threshold example for non-small-cell lung cancer: G-CSF costs $2,580 and a hospitalization $21,822.50 (ratio 8.5), which gives τ = 0.16 (p. 4).
  - Across cost ratios the optimal threshold starts to fall above a ratio of 5: it is 16% for ratios 5–10 and 8% for 15–30 (p. 4). The guidelines use 20% (ASCO) and 10–20% (NCCN) (p. 6).
  - The comparison with Lyman et al. (AUC 0.81) is by citation, not on the same data (p. 5).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - The contribution is a 20-feature interpretable score that runs off the EMR, with a threshold framework each site can adjust (pp. 5–7).
  - The stated limits are specific (pp. 6–7):
    - it is retrospective;
    - the benefits of true positives and negatives are not modelled;
    - nonfinancial costs (alarm fatigue, quality of life) are not measured.
  - External and prospective validation is hoped for (p. 7). The data cannot be shared, by IRB rule (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 3–4, 6): the owner (Hartford HealthCare) and its decision (G-CSF, EMR alerts) are named, and the guideline threshold of 20% is the reference. But the guideline rule is not scored on the same cost metric, and the main evaluation is AUC and average precision, not decisions.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 3–5): the cost ratio is one knob, and the curve of threshold against ratio (Fig. 2) goes to the institution "for tailoring algorithms to individual hospital needs" (p. 1). There is no bound.
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (p. 4): the group's own optimal tree loses (OCT 0.805 against LR 0.858), and the paper says so, reading it as a sign of weak nonlinear interactions. This follows step 6 (say where the method wins) and Heuristic 4 (negative results in the body). Taste mark 5: OFS20 is chosen over the slightly higher-AUC random forest for interpretability (p. 4).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 7): a partner-funded student project, published in a clinical journal and built on the group's tools.
- New-pattern candidate: cost-ratio threshold curve for a risk score (pp. 3–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a risk score into a decision by minimizing C_FP·FP(τ) + C_FN·FN(τ). Only the cost ratio matters, so publish τ* as a function of C_FN/C_FP and mark the guideline threshold on it (pp. 3–4, 6). Choose τ on validation data, not on the test set (my reading).
- When a patient contributes many encounters, split train and test by patient and impute the two sets separately (p. 2).
- Restrict inputs to discrete EMR fields so other sites can run the model directly off their records, and say what this leaves out (pp. 2, 6–7).
- With 2.6% positives, report average precision next to AUC, with the no-skill precision (0.026) as the reference (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "Overall, the strength of the linear models suggests that nonlinear feature interactions are not highly significant in this prediction problem." (p. 4)
- "The optimal threshold is determined purely by the cost ratio." (p. 4)

### S420 · Optimizing influenza vaccine composition: A machine learning approach (Naval Research Logistics 2021, DOI 10.1002/nav.21974)
Read level: **full, AM version**: file pp. 1–31, the MIT Open Access author's final manuscript (p. 1), accepted but not copyedited (p. 2); cited as [S420 AM p. N].
- File p. 1 is the cover, and the running numbers are file − 1.
- File pp. 28–31 hold figure files (tree image names and the text of the tensor figure). The trees of Figure 3 are images and were not read.
- First-page check: the title and the authors Bandi and Bertsimas match (pp. 1–2).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Hari Bandi (MIT ORC); Bertsimas is the corresponding author (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the DA and health line. It reuses the group's tensor completion (Bertsimas–Pawlowski 2019, built on Farias–Li) and its optimal regression trees (ORTs) (Bertsimas–Dunn 2019) (pp. 3–4, 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + RO + ML; AP (public health)}:
  - WHO, FDA and EMA committees reformulate the vaccine every year, six to eight months ahead. Emerging strains are hard to predict: strains co-circulate and compete, and ferret antibodies differ from human ones (p. 3).
  - Earlier OR models leave a gap (pp. 5–6). Wu–Wein–Perelson (a stochastic DP), Kornish–Keeney (optimal stopping), Cho and Özaltın et al. (bilevel) all model the committee's choice between keeping a strain and switching. None identifies emerging strains from data, prescribes by region or quantifies morbidity and mortality.
- **D2 Core idea**: Three layers (pp. 3–4):
  - (1) Low-rank completion of the tensor of circulation rates (country × virus × season), with a drift constraint ‖S^t − S^{t−1}‖_F ≤ λ on the latent core. U and V come from SVDs of the tensor unfoldings and the cores {S^t} from one SOCP (Algorithm 1, pp. 9–11).
  - (2) ORTs predict morbidity and mortality from the antigenic distance between vaccine strain and circulating viruses, weighted by circulation rates, one distance per subtype (pp. 11–13).
  - (3) A robust choice of one strain per subtype minimizes the worst-case weighted distance over Y = U S Vᵀ with ‖S − Ŝ‖_F² ≤ λ. A minimax swap (Lemma 3.1, citing von Neumann 1928) turns it into an SOCP, and the chosen strain is the argmin at the worst case Y* (pp. 13–14).
  - The nominal model takes last season's core S^{T−1} as the forecast (p. 13).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The GISRS tensor covers 1987–2018 (T = 32), m = 1,206 viruses and n ≈ 132 countries, and about 88% of its entries are missing (pp. 3, 8–9).
  - Hemagglutination-inhibition distances d_uv = log(max_u h_uv / h_uv) are available for 1,377 viruses × 82 vaccine strains (p. 9).
  - Morbidity and mortality estimates come from CDC, ECDC, GISRS and Iuliano et al.; past compositions for 1987–2018 come from IRD (p. 9).
  - The rank r and λ are chosen by cross-validation (p. 11), and the ORT parameters on a holdout set (p. 11).
  - The assumption admitted: the latent weights "do not change drastically across consecutive flu seasons" (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Both the drift-constrained completion step (problem 4, pp. 10–11) and the robust choice (problem 8, p. 14) are SOCPs, and the problem is called "both practically and theoretically tractable" (p. 6).
  - My reading: von Neumann's theorem needs a convex strategy set, and the paper states the swap as Lemma 3.1 for binary z without this step (pp. 13–14).
    - Over the vertices z ∈ {0,1}^q the swap gives only max_Y min_v ≤ min_v max_Y.
    - The inequality can be strict: two strains with costs y and 1 − y, y ∈ [0, 1], give 1/2 against 1.
    - So SOCP (8) is the value of the randomized (mixed-strain) problem, a lower bound on P_i, and the strain it returns is the best response to the worst case, not necessarily the robust strain.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - A retrospective study from 2009–10 to 2018–19 compares predicted morbidity and mortality under the robust composition with those under WHO (Northern Hemisphere), CDC/FDA (USA and four states) and EMA (Europe) compositions. The outcome predictor is ORTs trained in 20-year moving windows on weighted distances to the observed circulation (pp. 15–16; Tables 1–7, pp. 19–22).
  - It claims lower morbidity and mortality: 11–14% and 8–11% against WHO, 8–10% and 6–9% against FDA, and 10–12% and 9–11% against EMA (p. 2).
  - At strain level, the text counts the robust strain closer in 3–6 of "ten" seasons and identical in 2–5 (pp. 16–17); the tables list nine seasons (pp. 24–27). No run times are given.

### S163 · Two-stage sample robust optimization (Operations Research 2022, DOI 10.1287/opre.2020.2096; arXiv 1907.07142)
Read level: **full, arXiv version**: pp. 1–55 of arXiv 1907.07142v4 (3 Nov 2020), in the INFORMS template; cited as [S163 arXiv p. N]. Its history line gives submission on 11 Feb 2018, three revisions and acceptance on 1 Nov 2020 (p. 1).
- Read: §§1–6 (pp. 1–30) and Appendix D (pp. 52–55).
- Appendices A–C (pp. 33–51), the proofs behind Lemmas 1–3, were read for their statements and structure only.
- First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Shtern and Sturt match (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Shimrit Shtern (Technion) and Bradley Sturt (University of Illinois at Chicago) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Sturt's sample-robust-optimization line in the DA programme. It is the two-stage companion of the multistage paper (Bertsimas et al. 2018 = S113, card c2-06), and it builds on that paper's equivalence between SRO and type-∞ Wasserstein DRO and its asymptotic optimality (pp. 4, 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + DA; SD (two-stage stochastic LP); LO as engine}:
  - SAA is an LP whose size grows linearly in N, and it is asymptotically optimal. But with few data its cost estimate is optimistically biased (p. 2).
  - Without relatively complete recourse, SAA can need impractically many data points before its first-stage decisions become feasible (p. 2).
  - Wasserstein DRO helps out of sample, but two-stage robust and distributionally robust problems are NP-hard (pp. 2–3).
  - The entry is the group's own earlier limit: the multistage SRO paper approximated SRO by linear or piecewise-linear rules but "does not analyze the tightness of these approximations" (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - SRO averages, over the N data points, the worst-case second-stage cost over a ball of radius ε_N around each point, intersected with a polyhedral superset Ξ of the support (p. 6). At ε_N = 0 it is SAA (Remark 1, p. 7).
  - The multi-policy approximation (MP) gives each ball its own linear decision rule (p. 9).
  - Theorem 1: MP bounds SRO from above and is never worse than a single policy. The best local rule can be picked for each realization, so the second stage need not be non-anticipative (pp. 10–11).
  - Proposition 1: with linear decision rules, MP is an LP (ℓ1, ℓ∞) or an SOCP (ℓ2) with O(N) variables and constraints (p. 11).
  - Theorem 2: as ε_N → 0, the costs of MP and SRO converge almost surely to the stochastic optimum, and accumulation points of their decisions are optimal (p. 12).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The model is a two-stage stochastic LP with uncertainty in the right-hand side, h(ξ) = h⁰ + Hξ. The data are i.i.d., and only a polyhedral superset Ξ of the support is known (p. 6).
  - Conditions [A2]–[A3] are the usual SAA conditions: standard, but not verifiable in practice (p. 12).
  - [A4] is the opaque one: some first-stage decision is feasible for linear rules in every small ball. A sufficient form can be checked by one RO problem (Prop. 2, p. 13), and [A4] is equivalent to [A5] and [A6] (Lemma 1, pp. 14–15). In the authors' words, "despite our efforts" no counterexample was found, and the question is left open (pp. 12–13, 16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - The proof of Theorem 2 is a sandwich (pp. 16–21):
    - liminf ≥ v*: SRO and MP bound SAA from above, and SAA converges (King–Wets, Robinson);
    - limsup ≤ v*: move the first-stage decision a step λ toward x* (Lemma 2) and bound the cost of the local rules (Lemma 3), using the finite set of recourse matrices from [A6];
    - convergence of the decisions follows from Robinson's epiconvergence result.
  - The appendices use radii of polyhedra and Hoffman's lemma (pp. 34–38).
  - Appendix D reviews finite-sample guarantees from type-∞ Wasserstein concentration (Trillos–Slepčev), which give a rate for ε_N. Its Example 3 is a case where SRO is feasible for large N and SAA is not (pp. 52–55).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Two synthetic problems; M = 100 training sets per N from 16 to 512; a test set of 10⁴ points; v* estimated by SAA on 10⁵ points (pp. 22–23).
  - Inventory network (n = 10, no relatively complete recourse; ε_N = 10N^{−1/10} or 20N^{−1/10}) (pp. 24–26):
    - MP is far more often feasible than SAA;
    - MP's optimality gap nearly vanishes at N = 512, while SP's grows;
    - the DRO baselines are forced to x = (K, …, K).
  - MP and SP take 0.4–1.5 s per data point and SAA 1–2 ms (p. 24).
  - Medical scheduling (n = 8, complete recourse) (Table 1, p. 28):
    - MP matches the event-wise DRO approximation (Wass SW) and SAA in cost;
    - MP and Wass SW overestimate cost by 5–10%, while SAA underestimates it in 50–80% of training sets;
    - at N = 512, MP takes 9.96 s and the SDP approximation (Wass SDP) 323 s.
  - My reading: no solver, version or machine is named. The gaps in Figure 3 average only over the training sets whose decision was feasible on the whole test set (p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - The contribution is a simple, tractable scheme that is provably asymptotically optimal, also without relatively complete recourse (pp. 3, 28–29).
  - The stated limits:
    - whether [A4] is equivalent to feasibility is open (p. 16);
    - the constants of the ε_N rate depend on the unknown distribution (p. 53);
    - the radius "can be determined, for example, by performing k-fold cross-validation" (p. 24), but in the experiments it was set by the rate.
  - Future work: other DRO settings, which will probably need sets that shrink with N (pp. 29–30).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a two-stage robust problem, give each data point's uncertainty set its own recourse rule. Overlapping rules still give a valid upper bound, because the second stage has nothing left to anticipate (Theorem 1, pp. 10–11).
- Prove the asymptotic optimality of a data-driven approximation by a sandwich. The lower bound comes from SAA's known consistency, the upper bound from a feasible perturbation of the true optimum (pp. 20–21).
- For an opaque assumption, give a sufficient condition that is cheap to check (one RO feasibility problem, Prop. 2), then an exact characterization (Lemma 1). Test both on an example where the simple check fails (Example 2) (pp. 13–15).
- Report a "prediction error", the gap between the in-sample optimal value and the out-of-sample cost, to show whether a method's value is a reliable upper bound (pp. 23, 25, 28).
