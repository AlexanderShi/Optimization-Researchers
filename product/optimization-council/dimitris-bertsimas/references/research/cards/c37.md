- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO and CP as formulation devices; RO for the reading of the regularizer}:
  - **Problem**: matrix completion (MC) with fully observed side information that "depends linearly on the true underlying matrix" (abstract, p. 1).
  - **Motivation**: recommendation systems (the Netflix Prize), signal processing, system identification and image denoising (pp. 1–2).
  - **Entry point**: an assumption in prior work. Existing methods for MC with side information make "additional structural restrictions on the reconstructed matrix", through an explicit parametrization (p. 2). Here the side information is instead the label of a regression whose features are the unobserved matrix. The model follows predictive low-rank kernel learning (Bach and Jordan 2005), which assumed a fully observed input (p. 2).
  - **Statement**: Problem (1) minimizes Σ_Ω (X_ij − A_ij)² + λ‖Y − Xα‖²_F + γ‖X‖_* subject to rank(X) ≤ k (p. 2). It is "most natural" for noisy side information, as opposed to noiseless or graph side information (p. 2). The paper claims that (1) "has not previously been directly studied" (p. 3).
  - **Not present**: no owner, decision, belief about intractability or *why now* (the skill's Method 2 term); the case is generality and flexibility (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - **Key insight**: model the column space of X with an orthogonal projection matrix P. Partial minimization over α gives ordinary least squares, and the projection term λTr(Yᵀ(I − X(XᵀX)†Xᵀ)Y) then becomes λTr(Yᵀ(I − P)Y). This holds under the bilinear constraint (I − P)X = 0 with P idempotent and tr P ≤ k (pp. 9, 11–12).
  - **Reformulation**: factor X = UVᵀ and replace γ‖X‖_* by (γ/2)(‖U‖²_F + ‖V‖²_F), using Mazumder, Hastie and Tibshirani's identity (Prop. 6, p. 13). P is stored as MMᵀ with M of size n×k, so it costs n·k variables, not n² (pp. 13, 23).
  - **Splitting**: a copy Z = U and an indicator of the projection set (problem (10), p. 16) make every ADMM block closed-form:
    - U and V: row-separable k×k ridge solves, multithreaded (Props. 8–9, pp. 17–19);
    - P: a rank-k truncated SVD, computed without forming the n×n matrix (Prop. 10, pp. 20–22);
    - Z: a Woodbury formula (Prop. 11, pp. 22–23).
  - **Algorithm 1** updates (U, P) together, then (V, Z), then takes dual ascent steps (pp. 23–24).
  - **What changes** relative to the group's earlier mixed-projection papers: strong SDP relaxations and certifiably optimal small instances give way to scalable feasible solutions for large instances (pp. 6–7).
  - **What stays**: the projection matrix as "the matrix generalization of using binary variables" (p. 6); the matrix perspective relaxation (p. 14); the UVᵀ factorization standard in MC (p. 5).
  - **Why this route**: optimizing over n×m (and n×n) matrices "can rapidly become prohibitively costly" (p. 12).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - **Problem class**: nonconvex, with a rank constraint. The reduced objective f(X) is "in general neither convex nor concave and is non-smooth" (Prop. 2, p. 9).
  - **Data model**: A has low rank and Y = Aα + N (p. 3).
  - **Unreported settings**: λ and γ "can either take a default value or can be cross-validated" (p. 3). The values used in §6 are not stated, nor is the tolerance ε (pp. 27–29; searched the whole file).
  - **Tractability moves the authors admit**:
    - The algorithm solves (8), which relaxes P ∈ P_min(k, rank(UVᵀ)) to P ∈ P_k. This can be strictly looser "in certain pathological cases" (k = n = m, Ω = ∅) (p. 14).
    - The SDP relaxation needs a bound M on the spectral norm of an optimal X, taken from the objective value of X = 0 (pp. 14–15).
    - Theorem 13 assumes bounded dual iterates with summable successive differences, and an eigenvector condition that "can only be verified upon termination" (pp. 24, 27).
  - **Synthetic data**: U, V and β uniform on [0, 1], Gaussian noise with σ = 2, 90% of entries missing, Y = Aβ + N (p. 29). The data are thus generated from the model's own linear side-information assumption [inferred: a well-specified regime; misspecification is not tested].
  - **Real data**: Netflix Prize ratings with six TMDB movie features (budget, revenue, popularity, average vote, vote count, runtime), in two filtered datasets (pp. 39–40).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - (i) **Regularization as robustness**: (1) equals a min–max problem against a perturbation Δ bounded in spectral norm, by nuclear/spectral norm duality. The worst case is Δ̃ = γUVᵀ (Prop. 1, pp. 8–9). The authors call the result "not surprising given the duality of norms" (p. 9).
  - (ii) **Nonconvexity proof**: a closed-form partial minimization over α (least squares with a pseudo-inverse), then a proof that f is neither convex nor concave. The proof restricts f to a line in a two-dimensional instance and shows two chord violations (Prop. 2, pp. 9–10). My recomputation confirms f(−1) = f(0) = 2, f(−0.5) = 2 + √2/2 and f(−4) = f(3) = 5.04; the text writes fX(0.5) for fX(−0.5) (p. 10).
  - (iii) **Cheaper evaluation**: a truncated SVD cuts the cost of evaluating f from O(m²n + m³ + n²d) to O(kn(m + d)) (Prop. 3, pp. 10–11).
  - (iv) **Exactness**:
    - of the projection reformulation, by uniqueness of the orthogonal projection onto a subspace (Prop. 4, pp. 12–13);
    - of the factorized form, via Prop. 6 (Prop. 5, pp. 13–14).
  - (v) **SDP relaxation (9)**: built from the matrix perspective and the convex hull of P_k, and proved valid with the generalized Schur complement (Prop. 7, pp. 15–16). Eigenvector disjunctions are named as a way to tighten it (p. 16).
  - (vi) **Closed forms with operation counts**:
    - a Ky Fan-type bound for the P-step (pp. 20–21);
    - a truncated SVD of C̄ = F₁F₂ᵀ through a custom matrix-vector product, in O(k²n + knd) (pp. 21–22);
    - Woodbury for Z (pp. 22–23);
    - per-iteration complexity O(k²n + knd + (k³(n+m) + k²nm)/w) (Prop. 12, p. 24).
  - (vii) **Theorem 13** (pp. 24–27), using a technique of Xu, Yin, Wen and Zhang (2012):
    - Strong convexity of the U-, V- and Z-blocks gives a sufficient decrease (21)–(23).
    - The augmented Lagrangian is bounded below by completing squares (24).
    - Limit points satisfy the KKT system (27a–f) if the eigenvector condition holds.
    - The authors call it "an a posteriori convergence result", and they turn the condition into a computable "dual residual" P₂ − P₁P₂ (p. 27).
    - [My check: on p. 25 the dual-step term should be −(1/ρ₁)‖Φ_t − Φ_{t+1}‖², not −ρ₁‖·‖², and the Ψ term in (24) should carry a minus sign. Neither changes the argument, because summability is assumed.]
  - **Not given**: no approximation guarantee, optimality gap or complexity class for (1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Replace a projection term built from a pseudo-inverse, X(XᵀX)†Xᵀ, by a projection-matrix variable P with (I − P)X = 0 and tr P ≤ k. Store P = MMᵀ, so that it costs n×k memory rather than n² (pp. 11–13, 23).
- (D2) Split with a copy Z = U and an indicator of the nonconvex set, so that every ADMM block has a closed form: row-separable k×k ridge solves, a truncated SVD for the projection block, and Woodbury for the coupling block (pp. 16–23).
- (D4) Compute the truncated SVD of a matrix C = F₁F₂ᵀ without forming C: implement the matrix-vector product through the factors, in O(n(d + k)) per product (pp. 21–22).
- (D4) Bound the spectral norm of any optimal solution by the objective value of the trivial solution X = 0 divided by γ. This gives the big-M-like constant that a perspective relaxation needs (pp. 14–15).
- (D4) For a nonconvex ADMM, prove the a posteriori result (bounded duals and summable dual steps imply KKT at limit points under a checkable condition). Then turn the condition into a computable dual residual and plot it next to the primal residuals (pp. 24–28).
- (D4) Prove that an objective is neither convex nor concave by restricting it to a line in a two-dimensional instance and exhibiting one chord violation of each kind (Prop. 2, pp. 9–10).
- (D5) Time each block of the algorithm in every sweep, and check the empirical scaling against that block's complexity count (Figs. 4, 7, 10, 13; pp. 32–44).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + IO + DA}:
  - **Motivation**: wildfire costs, given in dollars: more than $70 billion a year, $149 billion for the 2018 California fires, $150 billion for Los Angeles in January 2025. Ground sensors and satellites are limited (p. 2).
  - **Entry point**: an application gap. Drones detect smoke and flames reliably, but "most studies in this domain assume that drones follow predefined flight paths". Route planning for early discovery "remains largely unaddressed" (p. 2). Drone-routing work is mostly post-detection; detection work treats sensor placement or onboard image analysis, without active routing (p. 2).
  - **Stated problem**: deploy limited numbers of ground sensors, charging stations and drones on an N × M grid over a discrete horizon, to minimize "the expected detection delay E[∆t] over the ignition distribution". Deployment is guided by a burn-risk field r_it ∈ [0, 1], from simulation or from ML (p. 3).
  - **Form of the contribution**: a benchmark (formalism, library, semi-synthetic dataset and implemented strategies; pp. 2–3), addressed to two communities: ML researchers who test risk models and OR researchers who test routing strategies (p. 4).
  - **Not present**: no agency or decision owner is named. Engagement with "public agencies and field responders" is a future aim (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - **Key idea**: couple ML risk maps to optimization-based deployment, and score both by simulated detection (p. 4; Fig. 1 caption, p. 4).
  - **Placement strategies**:
    - GaussianCov extends Church and ReVelle's maximal covering location problem. A charging station covers cells indirectly, through the drones it serves. Its coverage K_i is the probability that a drone doing a 2-D Brownian motion from the station visits cell i. K_i is estimated by repeated 3×3 uniform convolutions and capped at 1 (pp. 6–7, 15).
    - Random placement is the baseline (p. 7).
  - **Routing strategies**:
    - MaxCov is a rolling-horizon, risk-weighted maximum unique coverage MILP. It is re-solved every T_reev steps, with the risk updated from drone observations (p. 7; App. E.2, pp. 16–17).
    - UniCov is the same model under a uniform risk map, described as "an ablation of Max-Coverage" (pp. 7–8).
    - Brownian is a random walk (p. 7).
  - **Scalability**: the area is split into operational zones of charging stations within battery range (p. 6; App. C, pp. 13–14).
  - **What changes** relative to the related work: routing that is risk-weighted and adaptive, with recharging and return to base. The reviewed work assumes uniform importance, known fire locations or single missions (p. 3).
  - **What stays**: classical max-coverage location and MILP routing, solved by a commercial solver (pp. 6, 8).
  - **Objective mismatch**: the optimized objective is risk coverage, not the stated E[∆t]. Routing that minimizes expected detection time is listed as future work (pp. 3, 10).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - **Model**: a grid I (N × M) and a horizon T. Fires ignite and spread, and a fire counts as detected when it enters a device's sensing radius (p. 3).
    - Variables: binary placement variables x^g_i and x^c_i on feasible subsets; binary flight and charging variables a_its and c_its; coverage θ_it; battery b_ts (Table 1, p. 4).
    - Drone rules: one-cell moves after rescaling by the cruising speed; a communication radius R to a charging station; an endurance D_max, renewed at a station (pp. 3–5).
    - Spatial exclusion radii between devices (p. 3; App. E.1, p. 15).
    - Risk is lowered in cells that drones monitor (p. 5; Fig. 6 caption, p. 22).
  - **Data**:
    - Sim2Real-Fire provides over a million simulated trajectories and 1,000 real fires with five modalities, but its ignition points are synthetic and uniform (p. 5).
    - These are matched to 2.17 million historical ignitions from FPA FOD: within 150 m, on any calendar day, in regions at least 15 km wide. The result is 7,746 scenarios on 49 layouts of 30–60 km (p. 5).
    - Scenarios are tagged by size (large means a final burn radius above 20 km), spread speed, historical match, seasonal match and layout. Each gets a random 1–12 h delay (p. 5).
  - **Test set**: the 12 layouts with at least 80% matches, 474 scenarios (p. 8).
  - **Risk maps**:
    - The USDA Forest Service static burn-probability (BP) map. The text gives it a 30 m resolution (p. 7), while ref. [12] and Table 9 cite a 270-m product (pp. 11, 20).
    - A mock "ground-truth" dynamic map, obtained by averaging the simulated fire propagation per layout (p. 7). The paper frames it as an ideal-conditions upper bound (pp. 7–8). [Inferred: if those averages include the 474 test scenarios, the map embeds the test fires.]
  - **Resources per scenario**: 8 ground sensors, 2 drones and 2 charging stations (p. 8).
  - **Drone parameters**: the specification cited is the Mugin EV350: 130 km range, 120 min, 24 m/s, 80 km transmission (p. 7). The runs use 600 m/min, a 1 h battery and a 50 km transmission range (Table 6, p. 14); the speed is defined as an effective scanning rate (p. 5).
  - **Simplifications the authors admit** (p. 9): no communication failures, terrain constraints or execution uncertainty; full observability and reliable risk updates; no effect of wind or temperature on drones.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - **Formulation devices**:
    - (i) Max-coverage placement with indirect, partial coverage, linear in fractional coverage variables φ_i. Existing sensors can be fixed through variable values (App. E.1, p. 15). The model is solved "efficiently using linear programming" (p. 15).
    - (ii) The routing MILP (2a)–(2n), with constraints for fly-or-charge, station capacity, one-cell moves, battery recursion and monotone first-time coverage, plus a terminal "no-suicide" constraint: at the horizon's end, a drone must have enough battery to reach the nearest station (App. E.2, p. 16). Start conditions (3)–(5) are added (p. 17).
    - (iii) A rolling horizon: solve over H = 10 steps, execute T_reev = 5, update the risk, re-solve (pp. 16–17; Fig. 3 caption, p. 17; Table 6, p. 14).
    - (iv) A greedy decomposition into zones (Algorithm 1, pp. 13–14).
    - (v) Optional extensions: partial detection by drones, and a dispersion reward linearized "using auxiliary variables and big-M constraints" (p. 17).
  - **Analysis**: n/a. There are no bounds, gaps, optimality or complexity results. For the MILPs, no time limit, MIP gap or certificate is reported (pp. 8, 16–17).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - **Setup**: Julia 1.11.3, JuMP 1.26.0 and Gurobi 12.0.3, on an Intel Xeon Platinum 8260 cluster with 32 CPUs and 125 GB per job (p. 8).
  - **Runtime**: under 30 s of computation per hour of operation for every strategy, and under 2 s for placement (p. 8). MaxCov's mean routing execution time is 20.3 (SD 9.2; units not stated) in Table 7 (p. 19).
  - **Metrics**: share of fires detected within 12 h, mean detection time, fire size at detection, share of detections by device, map explored, distance travelled, distance to stations, routing time (pp. 5–6; Table 5, p. 14).
  - **Results** (Tables 2–3, p. 9):
    - With the BP map, Uniform Coverage detects as many fires or more: 93% vs 91% overall with random sensors, 97% vs 96% with GaussianCov, and 100% of fast fires. MaxCov misses fires: 73% vs 100% in the Fast Big category with GaussianCov (pp. 8–9).
    - With the ground-truth map, MaxCov is faster overall at similar detection rates: 2.49 h vs 3.74 h with random sensors, 3.20 h vs 3.41 h with GaussianCov (p. 9).
    - With GaussianCov placement, however, the oracle map leaves MaxCov's overall time and detection rate unchanged: 3.16 h and 96% with BP, 3.20 h and 95% with the oracle (Tables 2–3, p. 9).
    - GaussianCov placement beats Random in detection rate "in nearly all fire types" (pp. 8–9). Over the 30 rate cells of Tables 2–3 it is higher in 20, equal in 7 and lower in 3, all three under the ground-truth map.
    - Drones account for 71.5–100% of detections, with two drones against eight sensors and two stations (p. 9; Tables 7–8, p. 19). Brownian is worst (p. 9).
  - **Baselines**: only the authors' own ablations and random strategies. None of the reviewed literature methods is run: probabilistic path planning (PPP), the risk-specific UAV patrol path (RSUPP), makespan models or particle swarm optimization (PSO) (p. 3).
  - **Dispersion and tests**: SDs are printed and are often larger than the means. There are no confidence intervals or tests, yet the abstract and conclusion say "significantly" (pp. 1, 9). [My rough unpaired check from Table 3, treating scenarios as independent: the GaussianCov gap between MaxCov and UniCov, 0.21 h, is about 0.7 standard errors; the gap with random sensors, 1.25 h, is about 4.]
  - **Per category** under the ground-truth map, MaxCov is faster in 4 of the 8 size × speed cells and UniCov in the other 4 (Table 3, p. 9). The claim that MaxCov "consistently outperforms" UniCov in detection time (p. 8) therefore holds for the Overall column only.
  - **Metric definition**: mean detection time is taken over detected fires only in Table 5 (p. 14), but over all scenarios on p. 5. With the former, a strategy that misses fires can show a faster mean, e.g. 73% detected at 2.00 h (Table 2, p. 9) [inferred].
  - **Coverage of the test**: 474 of the 7,746 scenarios (p. 8).
  - **Release**: code on GitHub and data on Hugging Face (p. 10); licences tabulated (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Related work** (p. 3):
    - MILPs that jointly place watchtowers or balloons with single battery-constrained drone missions, without explicit recharging (De la Fuente et al., EJOR 2024);

    - Gaussian-mixture risk partitioning (RSUPP);

- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - **Contribution level**: infrastructure (formalism, open library, semi-synthetic dataset, baseline strategies) plus one empirical finding: "the bottleneck for drone-based wildfire detection systems lies in developing accurate, temporally dynamic burn probability maps rather than routing optimization alone" (p. 9).
  - **Limits stated specifically** (p. 9): communication failures, terrain and execution uncertainty are abstracted away; observability is full and risk updates reliable; environmental effects on drones are ignored. The authors argue that the hourly rolling horizon could absorb such effects.
  - **Future work** (p. 10): reinforcement learning; weather-conditioned dynamic risk maps; routing that minimizes expected detection time; heterogeneous fleets; predictions coupled to real-time decisions; datasets from historical fire propagation; analyses of policy and hardware.
  - **Open challenges** (p. 10): detecting small fires; better risk-map prediction.
  - **Unstated** [inferred]: MILP optimality gaps; literature baselines; tests on the 7,272 scenarios outside the high-match subset; statistical tests; whether the ground-truth map is out of sample.

**Method links**:
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 2–4, 7–9, 14).
  - Decisions are scored, not predictions (steps 4–5). Risk maps are judged by their impact on detection with routing held fixed (p. 4), and the finding is about which routing policy suits which quality of map (pp. 8–9).
  - But there is no owner, data holder or current rule. The baselines are random and uniform ablations. The predefined flight paths that "most studies" assume (p. 2) are not benchmarked. Agencies and responders are a future audience (p. 9), and there is no field test (step 6).
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 6, 8, 13–17).
  - Routing and placement are written as MILP and LP coverage models solved by Gurobi through JuMP. A rolling horizon and a zone decomposition keep them tractable, and times are measured at the operating cadence: under 30 s per hour of operation (p. 8).
  - This is the class-keeping, measured-time part of the method, applied to an application. There is no dual reading and no printed boundary.
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 3, 7–9, 16–19).
  - The exact MILP is the engine, but no time limit, gap or certificate is reported.
  - The opponents are uninformed ablations, not the incumbent heuristics reviewed on p. 3.
  - The headline "significantly outperforms" (pp. 1, 9) has no test behind it and holds for the Overall column only (Table 3, p. 9): Tension T6.
- Heuristics and taste (not counted):
  - Heuristic 6 evidence: a field that lacks the data to decide gets a data asset (7,746 scenarios, openly released), and prediction is split from selection, with risk maps tested under fixed routing and routing under a shared map (pp. 4–5, 10).
  - Taste mark 1 consistent: the benchmark faces practice, and agencies are named as future users (p. 9).
  - Methods 2, 3 and 6 are not engaged in the text.
- New-pattern candidates:
  - **decision-coupled benchmark: score predictive models by the decisions they induce, in one open library for ML and OR** (p. 4);
  - **perfect-information input as an upper bound that separates prediction error from policy error** (pp. 7–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Model the indirect coverage of a facility that serves mobile agents (here a charging station) by the visit probability of a random walk from the facility. Compute it by repeated 3×3 convolutions, cap it at 1, and use it in a max-coverage location model (pp. 6–7, 15).
- (D4) In rolling-horizon routing with energy limits, add a terminal "no-suicide" constraint (battery at the end of the horizon ≥ distance to the nearest station), so that every truncated plan stays recoverable (p. 16).
- (D5) Build an ablation of an optimization policy by giving it a uniform input (here a uniform risk map). The difference between the two runs isolates the value of the input information (p. 8).
- (D5) Run the operational predictor next to an oracle input built from the simulator, to bound what better prediction could buy, and report the two tables side by side (pp. 7–9).
- (D5) Add a random delay to ignition times (here 1–12 h), so that policies cannot exploit a fixed onset time (p. 5).
- (D5) Tag benchmark scenarios by difficulty attributes (size, spread rate, historical and seasonal match) and report results per category (pp. 5, 9).
