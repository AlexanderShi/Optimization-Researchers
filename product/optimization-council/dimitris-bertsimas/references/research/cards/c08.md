### S031 · Shortfall as a risk measure: properties, optimization and applications (Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control 2004, DOI 10.1016/s0165-1889(03)00109-x)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–29 (body pp. 1–27, acknowledgements p. 28, references pp. 28–29). Figures 1–7 read from their captions and the surrounding text; plotted values not read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Geoffrey J. Lauprete (MIT Operations Research Center), Alexander Samarov (MIT Sloan and University of Massachusetts, Lowell) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the finance strand (AP) of the 1995–2005 moments period. It borrows the moment-bound technique of the 1999 Bertsimas–Popescu working paper (the S018 line) for its Table 1 (p. 7; reference p. 28). It reaches, independently, the same sample LP as Uryasev–Rockafellar's CVaR (pp. 4, 15–16). Its cardinality-constrained index-tracking MIP (§6.4) is an early case of an integer formulation chosen where finance would solve a quadratic problem [inferred link to SW4].

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + LO; IO in §6.4; CP in §3.1.3; DA precursor in §5.1}: The entry is a gap between theory and practice in risk measurement. Standard deviation dominates, and mean-variance "is the industry standard" (p. 1). It is exactly right only under quadratic utility or elliptical returns, and both fail: satiation and increasing absolute risk aversion; asymmetry from options; contagion in down markets (p. 2). Downside measures exist but are "not widely used" because of computational difficulty and weak portfolio theory (p. 2). VaR, the industry's quantile measure, ignores how large tail losses are and is not coherent (p. 2). The stated goal is a methodology to define, measure, analyse and optimize risk that is computationally tractable (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: before proposing a replacement, name the incumbent, the assumptions under which it is exactly right, and the observed facts that break them.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Define risk as the quantity whose minimization is equivalent to non-dominance for every risk-averse investor. By the Levy–Kroll theorem (Theorem 1, p. 5), second-order stochastic dominance compares conditional tail means. Hence shortfall s_α(x) = μ'x − E[R'x | R'x ≤ q_α(R'x)] (eq. (1), p. 5). Three facts carry the paper. Shortfall is proportional to the standard deviation under normal or elliptical returns (Prop. 1, p. 6). It averages VaR over levels below α, so s_α ≥ VaR_α (Prop. 2, p. 7). It is convex in x through the check-function form s_α(x) = (1/α) min_c E[ρ_α(R'x − c)] (eq. (6), pp. 9–10), whereas VaR is not (p. 10). The sample problem written with order statistics, (18), becomes an LP with n + T + 1 variables and 2T + 2 constraints (Theorem 3, pp. 15–16). The mean-risk portfolio framework is kept: frontier, two-fund separation, beta (§4). What changes is the risk functional, so that it is convex and LP-representable.
  - Transferable: derive the objective from a dominance or axiomatic characterization (here non-dominance for all concave utilities, p. 5). The choice is then not ad hoc, and the field's standard results can be re-derived for it.
- **D3 Model and data**: Single-period portfolio, fixed target mean, budget constraint. The joint return distribution is continuous with finite mean (p. 5), and linear constraints such as x ≥ 0 are optional (p. 15). The estimator (10) sorts sample portfolio returns and makes no distributional assumption (p. 11). For αT < 1 the paper points to extreme-value theory (p. 12). The gradient formula needs a continuous positive density (Prop. 3(c), p. 10). The Hessian formula is stated "Under appropriate conditions" without proof (p. 11). §5.1 replaces Σ by T samples from N(0, Σ) and recovers mean–standard deviation only as T → ∞, "only an approximation" for finite T (p. 17). Data: simulated normal assets and simulated skewed assets (pp. 18–20); 17 asset-class indices, monthly, January 1994–September 2001 (p. 22); daily CRSP returns of 96 S&P 100 stocks, 1996–1999, via WRDS (pp. 25–26).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - LP duality removes the order statistics. The sum of the K smallest components of v is the LP min{vᵀz : Σz = K, 0 ≤ z ≤ 1}, (21). Its dual (22) turns the exponentially many subset constraints of (19) into the compact LP (20) (Theorem 3, pp. 15–16). The check-function form yields the same LP a second way (p. 16).
  - The optimal bounds on VaR and shortfall given mean and standard deviation (Table 1) are attained by some distribution. They are stated as Theorem 2 with no proof in the paper; the reader is pointed to Bertsimas–Popescu 1999 (pp. 7–8). Table 2 turns them into a model-risk ratio: at α = 1% the normal model can understate shortfall by a factor of 3.7 (9.9499 against 2.6652, pp. 7–8).
  - Portfolio theory: the frontier is convex (Prop. 4, p. 12); with a riskless asset it is a ray (Prop. 5, p. 13); shortfall beta in CAPM form (Prop. 6, p. 14). All three follow from Lagrangean stationarity with the gradient (7). Beta is constant in α iff s_α(x) = g(α)b(x) (p. 14).
  - Mean-variance as an LP by sampling, (25) (p. 17). Cardinality-constrained tracking: the MIQP (26) becomes a linear MIP (27) through the normal-model identity between standard deviation and shortfall (pp. 24–25).
  - Transferable: when an objective involves order statistics (the worst K of T outcomes), write the K-sum as the LP {Σz = K, 0 ≤ z ≤ 1} and dualize. The exponential family of subset constraints becomes O(T) linear constraints (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: quantify the model risk of a parametric assumption as the ratio of the distribution-free worst case (with the same moments) to the parametric value, tabulated by risk level (Table 2, p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Two questions are posed first: how different the allocations are, and whether mean-variance problems, including those with cardinality constraints, can be solved as linear ones (p. 18).
  - (a) Normal data, 3 assets, 100 replications at T = 50, 500 and 2000, α from 2% to 50%, with medians and 10%/90% bands (Fig. 1, pp. 18–19). The two methods agree above α ≈ 15%. Below that, shortfall weights are more volatile, since only the αT lowest order statistics are used (p. 19).
  - (b) Three synthetic assets with the same mean and standard deviation and zero correlation. They differ in skew: a lognormal stock, a stock with a call overlay (left-skewed) and a stock with a put overlay (right-skewed), priced by Black–Scholes. Mean-variance is therefore "blind" and splits equally (p. 20). Shortfall moves weight from B to C (p. 21). Its portfolios dominate in the tails, while MV dominates in the mid-range (Fig. 4, p. 21).
  - (c) 17 asset classes. The frontiers are almost identical at α = 16.7%. At α = 2.5%, MV is slightly better in mean-variance space and slightly worse in mean-shortfall space (p. 23). Everything in (a)–(c) is in-sample; there is no out-of-sample test.
  - (d) Tracking an equally weighted S&P 100 with M = 96 down to 40 stocks. T = 100–1000 samples are drawn from the estimated N(r̄, Σ), with weights between 0.3% and 10%. CPLEX is used, with no version, machine or gap given, and stopped after five feasible solutions because "running times for exact optimality can be excessive" (p. 26). Tracking error runs from 0 (M = 96) to 1.1% per month; run times are 0.7–519.3 s (Tables 3–4, p. 27). Insight (b), that the tracking error "converges to the solution of the mean-variance optimization with cardinality constraints as T increases" (p. 27), is not checked against any MIQP value in the tables.
  - Transferable: build synthetic test assets the baseline cannot tell apart (equal first two moments, zero correlation, skew set by option overlays). Any difference in allocation is then due to the new criterion (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Shortfall is presented as a variation of the mean excess function and TailVaR (p. 3). The body credits independent work openly. The LP is Uryasev–Rockafellar's for CVaR (pp. 4, 15–16). The gradient (7) "has been also independently obtained by Tasche (2000) and Scaillet (2000)" (p. 10). Propositions 5 and 6 carry the label "(Tasche, 2000)" (pp. 13–14). Yet the contributions list says the paper will "prove a two-fund separation theorem" and define a "new" risk-level-specific beta (p. 3). Coherence is handled by re-statement, not rejection. Shortfall is TailVaR plus the mean, so it fails translation invariance and positivity only through that adjustment. For a fixed mean it gives the same allocation as TailVaR (pp. 8–9).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A five-item contribution list keyed to sections (pp. 3–4). Hedging varies across the headline claims.
  - The abstract is hedged: the approach "might have advantages over mean-variance" (p. 1).
  - The introduction is stronger. It promises results "suggesting" that the shortfall frontier in mean–standard-deviation space "outperforms" the mean-variance one, and calls the computation "stable" (p. 4).
  - The body reports otherwise: MV is slightly better in that space (p. 23), and shortfall weights are more volatile at small α (p. 19).
  - A result the authors cannot test is explained by belief: "We feel that the closeness of the two optimal portfolios is an indication that the joint return of these asset classes obeys a joint multivariate normal distribution." (p. 23)
  - The conclusion ends on a conviction, not on limits (p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution has four parts. Conceptual: shortfall derived from stochastic dominance. Structural: its relations to standard deviation, VaR, LPM and coherence, plus frontier, separation and beta. Computational: an LP for the sample problem and a linear MIP for cardinality constraints. Empirical: illustrations on simulated and real data. Limits stated in the body: volatile estimates at small α (p. 19); extreme-value extrapolation needed for αT < 1 (p. 12); finite-T sampling only approximates mean–standard deviation (p. 17); exact MIP optimality too slow (p. 26). Limits not stated:
  - The §6.4 experiment samples from an estimated Σ (pp. 25–26), so it does not exercise the advertised advantage of skipping covariance estimation (pp. 17–18).
  - The MIP has no gap and no reference solution.
  - There is no out-of-sample test.

  The only future direction is to "consider more closely the notion of shortfall in real world environments" (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a risk question owned by financial economics is rewritten so that it stays in the LP class (Theorem 3, pp. 15–16), and a quadratic MIP becomes a linear MIP (27) (pp. 24–25). In the paper's words, the LP "replaces sorting ... with an appropriately chosen linear program" (p. 15).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: solver maturity drives the formulation. Linear MIP codes have been available "for decades", quadratic ones "only recently" (p. 18), and MIQP packages "have appeared very recently" (p. 24). But the argument is unquantified and points away from the newer class, the reverse of the 2016 MIQO argument of SW4.
- Method 4 ✗ contradiction: the MIP is stopped after five feasible solutions with no gap or bound. The claim that the best of them is "either the best or very close to the best solution" carries no number (p. 26). This is a 2004 instance of Tension T1.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the baseline is the industry's rule (mean-variance, p. 1). The asset-allocation data were supplied by Chris Darnell, and the work was partly funded by Merrill Lynch and General Motors (acknowledgements, p. 28). But the judging uses in-sample frontiers, and the introduction's outperformance claim (p. 4) is not what §6.3 reports (p. 23).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the moment-bound device of the 1999 Bertsimas–Popescu working paper is reused with other coauthors, in finance, for Theorem 2 (p. 7; reference p. 28).
- new-pattern candidate: **LP duality collapses subset / order-statistic constraints** (the K-sum LP and its dual, pp. 15–16). [inferred] This may be the same device as SW3's budgeted robust counterpart; that paper is not read in this batch.
- new-pattern candidate: **assume only what can be estimated, worst-case the rest**. Table 1 bounds VaR and shortfall "even if the distribution of returns are unknown", from mean and standard deviation alone, and Table 2 prices the normality assumption (pp. 7–8). The pattern recurs in S169 and S018.
- new-pattern candidate: **tight-or-nothing: every bound certified by an extremal distribution, exact or asymptotic**. The Table 1 bounds are "best possible in the sense that there exist probability distributions that attain them" (p. 7). The construction is not given in this paper; S169 and S018 give theirs.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the sum of the K smallest (or largest) of T values as the LP {Σz = K, 0 ≤ z ≤ 1} and dualize; 2^T subset constraints become n + T + 1 variables and 2T + 2 constraints (Theorem 3, pp. 15–16).
- Express a tail functional through the quantile-regression check function, s_α(x) = (1/α) min_c E[ρ_α(R'x − c)]. This gives convexity at once and a second route to the same LP (pp. 9–10, 16).
- Motivate a new objective as the exact criterion of a dominance theorem for a whole utility class (Levy–Kroll), not as a modelling preference (pp. 4–5).
- Tabulate model risk as worst-case over parametric value per risk level (Table 2: up to 3.7 at α = 1%, pp. 7–8).
- Design synthetic assets that the baseline cannot distinguish (same mean and standard deviation, zero correlation, skew via call and put overlays), so differences are attributable to the new criterion (p. 20).
- Report allocations over 100 replications as median and 10%/90% bands against the risk-level knob (Figs. 1–5, pp. 19–24).

**Quotes**:
- "This formulation replaces sorting, usually required for computing quantile-based quantities, with an appropriately chosen linear program." (p. 15)
- "The reason for this is that running times for exact optimality can be excessive." (p. 26)

### S045 · Solving convex programs by random walks (Journal of the ACM 2004, DOI 10.1145/1008731.1008733)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17 (body pp. 1–16, references pp. 16–17; no appendices).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Santosh Vempala (MIT Mathematics Department, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a theoretical-computer-science paper (CP, with LO as a special case) from the moments-and-convexity period. It was received December 2001 and revised January 2004 (p. 17). It is the batch's only paper on the algorithm side alone, in the oracle model of Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver. It cites his LP textbook for the bounding-cube assumption (pp. 2, 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP; LO as the m-inequality case, §4.3}: Convex minimization over a set given by a separation oracle "represents a frontier of polynomial-time solvability" (p. 1). The Ellipsoid method (Khachiyan; Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver) solves it, and Vaidya improved the complexity (pp. 1–2). The entry has two parts. One is a tool transfer: sampling by random walks, which had been central to polynomial-time algorithms for estimating volumes and computing the permanent (p. 2). The other is a question: can optimization use only a membership oracle? GLS answer it only for "centered" sets, with an algorithm that "is intricate" (p. 2). The paper solves K1 ∩ K2, with a separation oracle for K1 and a membership oracle for K2. This problem is "not known to be solvable using the Ellipsoid method or Vaidya’s algorithm" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open with a precise frontier (what is polynomial with which oracle), then name the one case existing methods cannot handle.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A cutting-plane method that queries an approximate centroid. Keep a polytope P ⊇ K, starting from the bounding cube. Query the average z of N random points of P, and cut P by the halfspace through z given by the oracle. Repeat at most 2nL times, then declare K empty (algorithm, p. 3). Levin's 1965 exact-centroid method "is computationally intractable" (footnote 1, p. 3). The change is to replace the exact centroid by a sample mean that random walks make computable. Kept from the ellipsoid tradition: the separation-oracle model and cuts through the query point. Changed: the query point (a sampled average, not an ellipsoid centre) and the enclosing body (a polytope, not an ellipsoid). Optimization reduces to feasibility by bisection on the level of a quasi-convex objective (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a classical method is optimal in iteration count but needs an intractable object (an exact centroid), ask whether a randomized estimate of that object keeps the guarantee, and prove how much error the guarantee tolerates.
- **D3 Model and data**: K is closed and bounded (p. 4). It lies in an axis-aligned cube of width R and, if nonempty, contains a cube of width r; L = log(R/r) (pp. 2–3). Access is by separation oracle (§§2–5) or by membership oracle with a known interior ball (§6). "High probability" means probability above any fixed constant (p. 9). Lemma 5 needs only pairwise independence of the samples (p. 10). The paper does not deal with "the technical difficulties caused by near-uniform sampling of nearly independent points" and refers them to Kannan–Lovász–Simonovits (p. 14). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: The proof skeleton has four layers.
  - (1) A geometric lemma. Grünbaum's 1/e theorem is generalized to halfspaces through a point at distance t from the centroid: 1/e − t (Theorem 3, p. 6). The proof goes through Lemma 4: the one-dimensional marginal density of an isotropic convex body is below 1 (pp. 6–8). That lemma is shown by Brunn–Minkowski symmetrization and by moving mass away from the centroid, which can only increase the moment of inertia (Claim 1, p. 7), until the extremal body is a cone. The cone is computed explicitly, and a "tedious (but routine) computation" is avoided by a second use of the claim (p. 8).
  - (2) A sampling lemma. E‖z‖² = n/N, so N = O(n) samples put z near the centroid, and every cut removes at least 1/3 of the volume (Lemma 5, pp. 9–10).
  - (3) A volume count: at most n·log_{3/2}(R/r) volume drops, hence at most 2nL iterations with high probability (Theorem 6, p. 10). This matches Vaidya and is best possible (Yudin–Nemirovski), against O(n²L) for the ellipsoid method (p. 3). For LPs, logconcave tails (Prékopa) and a union bound over the m constraints give N = O(log² m) (Lemma 7, Cor. 8, pp. 10–11).
  - (4) The cost of sampling. Hit-and-run mixing (Lovász, Theorem 9, p. 12) and near-isotropy from O(n log² n) samples (Rudelson, improving Kannan–Lovász–Simonovits's O(n²); Lemma 10, Cor. 11, pp. 12–13) give O*(n⁴) walk steps per iteration (Theorem 12, p. 14). A multipoint walk keeps isotropy implicitly; this is proved by a conductance comparison (Theorem 13, pp. 14–15). The membership-oracle case uses a chain-of-bodies rounding (Theorem 14, pp. 15–16).
  - Table I compares total complexity: O(nLT + n⁷L) here against O(nLT + n^3.38 L) for Vaidya and O(n²LT + n⁴L) for the ellipsoid method (p. 4).
  - Transferable: prove a perturbation-robust version of the classical lemma your method relies on (1/e becomes 1/e − t), so that a cheap estimated query point inherits the classical guarantee (p. 6).
  - Transferable: for polyhedral special cases, union-bound over the finitely many hyperplanes the oracle can return, cutting the per-iteration sample size from O(n) to O(log² m) (pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none. There is no implementation or experiment. The algorithm "seems to merit empirical evaluation" (p. 16), and practical sampling "might be much faster than the known worst-case bounds" (p. 4).
- **D6 Positioning**: Simpler than the Ellipsoid-based methods of Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver and than Vaidya's. It improves on the Ellipsoid method's O(n²L) oracle calls and matches Vaidya's O(nL) (p. 3). It calls itself "simple" (pp. 1–2) and the GLS membership algorithm intricate ("quite nice but intricate", p. 15). It concedes early that "the overall running time can be higher than that of previous algorithms" (p. 2; Table I, p. 4). It credits Kannan–Mount–Tayur 1995 as an earlier application of random walks to optimization (p. 2) and Levin for the centroid idea (p. 3). Footnote 2: whether Vaidya's algorithm extends to the membership problem is not known (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A three-sentence abstract that states no theorem (p. 1). The comparison table comes early, losing rows included (end of §2, p. 4). Proof intuitions sit in parentheses ("moving mass away from the center of gravity can only increase the moment of inertia", p. 7). Hedges point to the future: faster sampling methods "might well" come and would improve the algorithm directly (p. 4). The paper ends on "Extensions", not on a summary (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical and algorithmic:
  - an oracle-optimal randomized cutting-plane method;
  - an algorithm for K1 ∩ K2 with mixed oracles (Theorem 14, p. 15);
  - a generalized Grünbaum theorem that "might be of interest in other settings" (p. 2).

  The limits are explicit: higher total arithmetic complexity than Vaidya (Table I, p. 4); sampling technicalities not handled (p. 14); no experiments (p. 16). Future directions: an interior-point-like method that averages random points of the improving set; empirical evaluation; faster-mixing walks (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: here "tractable" means the 2004 complexity-class sense. The selling points are an oracle-optimal O(nL) count and polynomial time. No solve time is given, and the running time is admitted to be higher (pp. 2, 4, 16). This is evidence for the drift recorded under Method 1's limitations (complexity class in 2004, solve time in 2019). It is also the Taste warning sign "polynomial time without solve times", which the authors flag themselves (p. 16).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: an idea set aside as "computationally intractable" (Levin's exact centroid, footnote 1, p. 3) becomes polynomial once a new tool exists: random-walk sampling of convex bodies (pp. 2, 4). The "why now" is a tool change, but it sits in a footnote and is unquantified.
- new-pattern candidate: **separation problem = the nominal problem or oracle (GLS optimization–separation equivalence) as the tractability argument**. The whole paper works in the oracle model (pp. 1–3, 15). The pattern recurs in S169 (p. 12) and S018 (pp. 7, 16–17, 20, 23).
- new-pattern candidate: **perturbation-robust version of a classical theorem, so that a sampled estimate suffices** (Theorem 3, p. 6). Single paper.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Generalize the classical lemma behind a method to an approximate version (Grünbaum 1/e becomes 1/e − t at distance t from the centroid), so that sampling can replace an intractable exact computation (Theorem 3, p. 6).
- Prove an extremal-body lemma by symmetrization plus the fact that moving mass away from the centroid can only increase the moment of inertia, reducing to a cone that can be computed (Lemma 4 and Claim 1, pp. 6–8).
- For polyhedra, union-bound over the m constraint hyperplanes the oracle can return: O(log² m) samples per iteration instead of O(n) (Lemma 7, Cor. 8, pp. 10–11).
- Note when only pairwise independence is needed (a variance argument) to relax the sampling requirement (p. 10). Discard the samples used to choose the query point, to avoid correlation with later samples (p. 14).
- Put a complexity table with the rows you lose next to the algorithm (Table I, p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "Its complexity is optimal in terms of the number of oracle queries, but the overall running time can be higher than that of previous algorithms" (p. 2)
- "The algorithms seems to merit empirical evaluation; in practice, it might be possible to sample more efficiently than the known worst-case bounds." (p. 16)

### S169 · Probabilistic Combinatorial Optimization: Moments, Semidefinite Programming, and Asymptotic Bounds (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2004, DOI 10.1137/s1052623403430610)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–25 (body pp. 1–24, references pp. 24–25; no appendices).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Karthik Natarajan (Singapore-MIT Alliance) and Chung-Piaw Teo (NUS Business School) (p. 1). The work list gives only "D Bertsimas, K Natarajan"; file p. 1 lists Teo as third author. · **Position in trajectory**: it joins his first period, probabilistic analysis of combinatorial problems (it cites Avram–Bertsimas 1992 on the spanning-tree constant, p. 24), with the moments-and-SDP line. It is "building on the work of Bertsimas and Popescu [4]" (p. 3), which it cites as "to appear" (p. 24). Received June 2003, accepted March 2004 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP + SD + IO; AP: PERT tardiness}: The expected optimal value of a 0-1 problem with random objective coefficients is hard to evaluate, and even bounds are "quite challenging" (p. 2). The classical probabilistic analysis assumes independence. The entry attacks that assumption on estimation grounds: the number of estimates needed to capture dependence "grows exponentially", so the paper optimizes over all dependence structures that match marginal moments (Remark, p. 3). The application that forces it is project management: expected completion time when means and variability are known but correlations are not (p. 2). Problem (1.3) asks for the tight upper bound, the sup over the distribution class of E[Z*max(c)] (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Generalize Meilijson–Nadas (complete marginals, longest path) to marginal moment information, for any 0-1 problem (p. 3). Interchanging sup and min gives the bound (3.2), Z ≤ min_d {Z*max(d) + Σ_i sup E[c_i − d_i]⁺} (p. 6). It was known to be tight only when the worst-case marginal does not depend on d (Klein Haneveld), so not with second moments (pp. 6–7). The main result is that it is tight for any marginal moment information (Theorem 3.1, p. 8). Each inner problem is univariate, and univariate nonnegativity is exactly an SDP (Prop. 3.3, p. 11). So the bound can be computed whenever the nominal problem can be solved (Theorem 3.4, p. 12). Kept: the Meilijson–Nadas convex reformulation. Changed: the information model (from known marginals to moments) and the claim (from a valid bound to a tight one).
  - Transferable: when a hard multivariate certificate is needed (nonnegativity of a multivariate polynomial over a product set), check whether the information is only marginal. If it is, dualize coordinate by coordinate to reach univariate certificates, which are exact (pp. 7–11).
- **D3 Model and data**: Random objective coefficients with support Ω_i and given moments of piecewise-polynomial functions; no cross moments (pp. 2–3); E|c_i| < ∞ (p. 3). Strong duality needs the moment vector inside the moment space, which can be checked coordinate by coordinate (p. 5).
  - Theorem 3.4 needs a polynomially solvable nominal problem (p. 12); Theorem 3.5 needs a compact LP description of its convex hull (pp. 12–13).
  - The asymptotics need (A) a common continuous F, without independence; (B) feasible solutions of equal cardinality; (C) each element in equally many solutions (pp. 17–18). In addition, F(c) = ρ_α c^α near 0 (p. 19).
  - §5.2 assumes common first two moments and (D), a partition of the ground set into disjoint feasible solutions (pp. 22–23).

  Data: two PERT networks from Birge–Maddox, with ranges and first two moments (three in Example 1) (Tables 4.1, 4.3, pp. 16–17).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Primal over distributions with moment constraints, and its semi-infinite dual, (2.1)–(2.3), with Isii duality (Theorem 2.1, p. 5). The direct dual has one multivariate nonnegativity constraint per feasible solution. SOS gives only a sufficient condition, and tractable necessary conditions are unknown. So (2.3) "is difficult to solve even for simple fik(·), regardless of the difficulty of the nominal problem" (p. 5).
  - Theorem 3.1 is proved by a minimality argument (pp. 8–10). Choose each univariate constant minimal. The minimal constant of a separable multivariate function is then the sum of the univariate minima, because the univariate minimizers combine into one point of Ω (p. 9). Summing the constraints over i ∈ B_p and i ∉ B_p yields a feasible point of the original dual (pp. 9–10).
  - Polynomial time (Theorem 3.4, p. 12). The SDP blocks have polynomial size, and the remaining constraint t ≥ Z*max(d) is separated by solving the nominal problem with objective d; this is "a general cutting plane algorithm" (p. 12). A compact SDP (3.17) follows by substituting the LP dual of a compact convex-hull description (pp. 12–13).
  - Asymptotics: KKT conditions for the convex problem (5.3), with uniform dual weights λ_p = 1/P justified by symmetry (C), give Z*max = K1·E[c | c ≥ F⁻¹(1 − K1/n)] (Theorem 5.1, pp. 18–19). Explicit integrals then give the scaling n^{(α−1)/2α} (Theorem 5.3, p. 20).
  - A negative result. Under first two moments and (D), cyclic unions of m disjoint feasible solutions define a distribution that matches the moments and has optimal value 0 in every realization, so Z*min = 0 (p. 23). The construction takes m = (μ²/(μ² + σ²))·P̃ without discussing integrality (p. 23).
  - Transferable: to prove that an uncertainty-aware version of a 0-1 problem is polynomial, show that its only hard constraint is separated by one call to the nominal solver, then invoke the equivalence of separation and optimization (p. 12).
  - Transferable: for highly symmetric combinatorial families, guess uniform dual multipliers and verify KKT; closed-form worst-case values follow (pp. 18–19).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Two PERT networks reused from Birge–Maddox: 10 activities on 3 paths, and 29 activities on 14 paths, five due dates each. They were solved with SeDuMi 1.03 on a Pentium II 550 MHz under Windows 2000, in "less than a minute" in total (pp. 15–17). The metric is the bound value against the values reported in [5]; Birge–Maddox's method was not rerun (Tables 4.2 and 4.4, pp. 16–17). The SDP bounds are lower (tighter) at every due date except one tie (0.73 at T = 85, Example 2, p. 17). The third moment was added "to check the value of the extra information on the upper bound" (p. 15). It tightens Example 1 further: 20.29 → 20.10 at T = 0, and 0.47 → 0.18 at T = 25 (p. 16). No simulation checks the bounds against particular distributions. Asymptotic constants are set beside the independent-model literature (Walkup, Aldous's π²/6, Donath, Frieze's ζ(3); Tables 5.2–5.3, pp. 21–23). No failure case is reported.
  - Transferable: take a competitor's own published test instances, then add one level of information they could not handle. This shows correctness and the value of the extension on the same instances (pp. 15–16).
- **D6 Positioning**: Generalizes Meilijson–Nadas and Weiss (pp. 2–3). It settles a question the PERT literature had left open: tightness "has not been known to hold in general", even for the longest path (p. 3, citing [5, p. 844]). The correction of Birge–Maddox is direct but phrased as a consequence of the theorem: "In light of Theorem 3.1, it is clear that in [5] a heuristic method is used to calculate the bound." (p. 16). The "lack of precision" of their linearization is offered as an advantage of the SDP approach (p. 15). The paper cites Lasserre and Parrilo for the multivariate SOS route (p. 5) and sidesteps that route by exploiting univariate structure (pp. 10–11). Its constants are set beside the independence results, not against them, with the closeness noted where it appears (spanning tree, "surprisingly close", p. 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A contribution list (a)–(d), with the headline asymptotic table up front (Table 1.1, pp. 3–4). The obstacle is stated plainly before the fix: "Unfortunately, this formulation has exponentially many constraints in general." (p. 4; again p. 5). - **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: a tightness theorem; polynomial-time computability inherited from the nominal problem; a compact SDP. Asymptotic constants without independence: for the assignment problem 0.5 for every N, against π²/6 under independence (p. 21); 1 for spanning tree and TSP, against ζ(3) ≈ 1.202 for the spanning tree under independence (p. 22). And a PERT application. The limits appear in the body, not in the conclusion:

### S018 · Optimal inequalities in probability theory: A convex optimization approach (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2005, DOI 10.1137/s1052623401399903)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–25 (body pp. 1–24, references pp. 24–25; no appendices). The formula extraction is broken in Prop. 3.1, Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 4.3 (pp. 7–16). Statements and proof skeletons are readable; the exact index sets of (3.4)–(3.9) and (4.2) were not checked.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ioana Popescu (INSEAD; her 1999 MIT PhD thesis is reference [33], p. 25) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: SKILL.md Signature Work SW2. Received December 2001, accepted June 2004 (p. 1). S031 cites it as a 1999 MIT Operations Research Center working paper under a slightly different title (S031 p. 28). S169 cites it as "to appear" (S169 p. 24) and reuses its univariate SDP characterization (S169 p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP; SD (applied probability)}: The paper opens on classical probability, not on an application, with three numbered questions (p. 1):
  - are the Markov, Chebyshev and Chernoff bounds best possible?
  - can they be generalized to several variables, and when can they be computed?
  - is there a general optimization-based theory for them?

  The gap is taken from the community's own critics (pp. 3–4). Diaconis: "the theory [of moment problems] is not up to the demands of applications". Kemperman: a deep study of algorithms has been rare. Smith: the bounds lack simple closed forms. In the authors' own words, Smith's computational procedure "is far from a formal algorithm". The (n, k, Ω)-bound problem asks for the sup and inf of P(X ∈ S) over all distributions on Ω with given moments up to order k (p. 2). A bound is tight if it equals the sup, even when it is only approached in the limit (Def. 1.2, p. 2).
  - Transferable: state the gap in the critics' own quoted words, then state the paper's goals as answers to them: complexity, algorithms, closed forms (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Write the bound problem as an LP over measures, (2.1), and dualize it into an LP over polynomials g ≥ χ_S on Ω, (2.2) (pp. 5–6). Weak duality makes every dual solution a valid bound. Strong duality, for an interior moment vector, makes the optimum tight (Theorems 2.1–2.2, p. 6). In one dimension, nonnegativity of a polynomial on an interval is exactly a PSD condition, so the tight bound is a single SDP (Prop. 3.1, Theorem 3.2, pp. 7–12). In several dimensions, Putinar's representation gives a convergent sequence of SDP relaxations (Theorem 4.3, Cor. 4.4, pp. 15–16). For k = 1 and k = 2 the problem reduces to convex optimization over S (Theorems 5.1 and 6.1, pp. 17–21). Kept from Isii, Karlin and Smith: the duality theory. Added: algorithms, a complexity classification and closed forms (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Model and data**: A random vector on Ω ⊆ Rⁿ with given moments up to order k; S and Ω are semialgebraic (pp. 2, 14–15). For the complexity results the data are rational, and "efficient" means polynomial in the data and log(1/ε), within ε of the tight bound (p. 4). Strong duality needs the moment vector inside the moment space (Theorem 2.2, p. 6). Theorem 4.3 needs Putinar's compactness conditions, e.g. a bounded polyhedron or a known radius M (p. 14). The (n, 1, Rⁿ₊) case uses means only; the (n, 2, Rⁿ) case uses the mean and an invertible covariance (p. 20). If only upper bounds on moments are known, the dual gains sign constraints (p. 6). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - (i) Univariate SDPs. Nonnegativity on R follows from factoring into real roots of even multiplicity and conjugate pairs, so the polynomial is a sum of squares with a PSD Gram matrix. Rays and intervals reduce to R by the substitutions x = t², x = at²/(1 + t²), x = a ± t² and x = a + (b − a)t²/(1 + t²) (Prop. 3.1, pp. 7–10), "inspired by Nesterov" (p. 7).
  - (ii) Closed forms for k ≤ 3 (Table 2, p. 13). The one-sided k = 2 bound C²/(C² + δ²) is "always stronger" than Chebyshev's C²/δ² (p. 23). The k = 3 proofs are deferred: "The proof of the theorem is given in Popescu [33]." (p. 12).
  - (iii) Multivariate. Sum of squares is equivalent to a PSD Gram matrix (Prop. 4.2, p. 14). With Putinar this gives ZD(d) → ZD, but the sizes "are not bounded by a polynomial in n, even for fixed k" (p. 16).
  - (iv) Hardness. The separation problems for (n, k, Rⁿ₊) with k ≥ 2 and for (n, 2k, Rⁿ) with k ≥ 2 are NP-hard, by reduction from matrix copositivity (Props. 4.5–4.6, p. 17). Optimization–separation equivalence and strong duality carry the hardness to the bound problem (p. 16). On Rⁿ, odd top-order moments "can be disregarded as they will not improve the bound" (p. 16).
  - (v) k = 1. An LP dual, a scaling argument and a minimax exchange give min(1, 1/inf_{x∈S} max_i x_i/M_i), that is, n convex problems (Theorem 5.1 and (5.2), pp. 17–18). Extremal two-point distributions are constructed when the bound is attained, and sequences when it is not (Theorem 5.2, pp. 19–20).
  - (vi) Unions of convex sets. Separation reduces to two convex minimizations: over Ω, to test g ≥ 0, and over S, to test g ≥ 1 (Algorithm A, p. 20). For k = 2 a PSD test comes first and yields a violated point if it fails (Algorithm B, p. 23).
  - (vii) k = 2 on a convex set. Marshall–Olkin's bound 1/(1 + d²) is reread through gauge duality. When Γ⁻¹M_δ ≥ 0, KKT gives a closed form (Theorem 6.1, Props. 6.3–6.4, pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: map a problem family onto one landscape table (polynomial cases with their theorem, NP-hard cases with their reduction; Table 1, p. 5). Prove hardness on the separation problem rather than on the optimization problem (pp. 16–17).
  - Transferable: for one-dimensional interval constraints, reduce every case to nonnegativity on R by a rational substitution, so one PSD lemma serves six cases (pp. 7–10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none. There are no computations, timings or numerical examples. The results are theorems, algorithms and closed-form tables (Tables 1–2, pp. 5, 13).
- **D6 Positioning**: A long historical section (pp. 3–4) runs from Chebyshev and Markov through Karlin–Studden, Marshall–Olkin and Isii to Smith and Shapiro, with Shohat–Tamarkin's remark that "the problem of moments lay dormant for more than 20 years" (p. 3). The duality theory is taken as given (Isii, Karlin, Shapiro; p. 6). **Transferable techniques**:
- Turn the question whether an inequality is best possible into an LP over measures with moment constraints and its dual over polynomials. Weak duality gives validity; strong duality (interior moment vector) gives tightness (pp. 5–6).
