### S658 · Nonlinear formulations and improved randomized approximation algorithms for multiway and multicut problems (Sloan School of Management, MIT, working paper 1995; no DOI)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–25 of Sloan working paper WP# 3838-95-MSA, June 1995. These are the cover (p. 1), the abstract, §§1–6 and the references (pp. 2–25); pp. 4–5 repeat pp. 2–3. First-page check: the title and the authors D. Bertsimas, C. Teo and R. Vohra match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chungpiaw Teo, an MIT ORC graduate student on a National University of Singapore scholarship, and Rakesh Vohra (Ohio State) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**:
- The work belongs to his mid-1990s approximation-algorithm line (IO). A preliminary version appeared at IPCO 1995 (p. 2).
- Funding is his NSF Presidential Young Investigator award with Draper matching funds (p. 2). 04-mentorship.md ties that award to his 1990s students Niño-Mora, Gamarnik and Paschalidis.
- S256 in this batch is Teo's later journal paper with the same rounding device. It cites Teo's 1996 thesis on approximation algorithms via LP relaxations (S256, p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO; LO as the relaxation engine}: The prior state is set out on pp. 2–3:
  - Multiway cut is NP-hard for k ≥ 3, even on planar graphs (Dalhaus et al.).
  - Polyhedral work (Chopra–Rao, Cunningham, Chopra–Owen) supplies valid inequalities and an extended formulation tighter than all earlier ones.
  - On approximation, Dalhaus et al. give a 2(1 − 1/k) algorithm, and Garg–Vazirani–Yannakakis an O(log |V(H)|) bound for multicut.

  The entry: derive all these formulations from one exact nonlinear model, then analyse the LP relaxations by rounding (contribution list, p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Let y_j(u) be the probability that node u ends up in terminal j's component.
  - Edge (u, v) is then cut with probability 1 − Σ_j y_j(u)y_j(v), and the nonlinear program (NLF) built on this is exact (Theorem 1, p. 6).
  - Linearizing the products gives Chopra–Owen's EF1, a polynomial-size EF2 and a new ℓ1-type EF3 (pp. 7–9).
  - Eliminating x turns EF2 into a stable-set relaxation on a derived graph I(G). Stable-set facets (cliques, odd cycles) then become multiway-cut inequalities (pp. 13–17).
  - Rounding: one uniform U is drawn for all nodes, and y_j(u) is set to 1 when U ≤ y_j(u). The most expensive of the k cuts is discarded (pp. 18–19).
- **D3 Model and data**: An undirected graph with nonnegative edge weights and k terminals. Nonnegativity is assumed from p. 8 on, which makes EF1 = EF2. For multicut there is a demand graph H with independence number α(H) (pp. 3, 8, 22). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Formulations:
    - NLF is exact: sample a cut from its optimum; the expected cost equals the objective, so it cannot beat the minimum cut (p. 6).
    - EF2 = EF3 by explicit maps between feasible points (Theorem 3, pp. 8–9).
    - Projection yields path, tree, odd-wheel and bipartite inequalities (Theorem 4, pp. 9–11).
    - Fractional extreme points give a gap of 16/15, and a family tends to 10/9 (pp. 11–13). Boolean-quadric inequalities do not cut the point off (p. 13).
    - The stable-set reduction makes maximum independent set on k-partite graphs NP-hard as a corollary (p. 15).
  - Polynomial cases: (IND) is solvable in polynomial time and is integral when I(G) is perfect (Theorems 6–7, p. 17). This gives trees (Chopra–Owen, Erdős–Székely) and graphs whose demand nodes meet every cycle of length ≥ 4 (Theorems 8–9, pp. 17–18). Lemma 10 is only sketched (p. 18).
  - Rounding: an edge falls in the cut around S_j with probability |y_j(u) − y_j(v)|, so E[Z_H] ≤ 2(1 − 1/k) Z_EF3 (Theorem 11, p. 19). The 1/k is restored from the drop-the-largest-cut step. Further results:
    - for k = 2, a randomized proof of max-flow min-cut (p. 19);
    - deterministic versions by conditioning, or by a minimum cut over threshold sets (p. 20);
    - for k = 4, a relaxation that matches Dalhaus et al.'s 4/3 (pp. 20–21).
  - Multicut:
    - an exact nonlinear model (Theorem 13, p. 21);
    - linearization recovers the classical (M1) (p. 22);
    - a stronger (M2), solvable by the ellipsoid method (Theorem 14, p. 23);
    - a 2α(H)(1 − 1/|V(H)|) bound relative to (M2) (Theorem 15, pp. 23–24; fraction restored).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none. The only computations mentioned are Chopra and Owen's (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: formulations, polynomially solvable cases and bounds (concluding remarks, p. 24). Limits stated:
  - The 2(1 − 1/k) bound is "a slight improvement" over Dalhaus et al.; it gains by being relative to the LP rather than the integer optimum (pp. 3, 20).
  - The multicut bound beats Garg et al. on dense demand graphs but is weaker on sparse ones (p. 3).

  No future work is stated (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ {IO; LO as engine}: a problem owned by approximation algorithms and polyhedral combinatorics is written exactly (NLF, p. 6). Its linearizations are LPs solvable in polynomial time (pp. 7, 17). The boundary is printed twice: as explicit integrality gaps (16/15, tending to 10/9, pp. 11–13) and as the graph classes where the LP is integral (pp. 17–18). This is the "LP-relaxation rounding" entry of the Domain Map's IO row.
- New-pattern candidate: *exact nonlinear (probabilistic) formulation as a generator of LP relaxations*: linearize the products of an exact model, recovering the known formulations and deriving stronger ones (pp. 3, 6–9, 21–22). In this batch also S213 (0–1 IP as quadratic equalities) and S500 (support as polynomial inequalities).
- New-pattern candidate: *dependent randomized rounding with one shared threshold* (pp. 3, 18–20). S256, also with Teo, uses the same device.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) For an exact continuous model of a partition problem, make the assignment variables probabilities and write the probability of each cost event (here 1 − Σ_j y_j(u)y_j(v)). Prove exactness by sampling a solution from the optimum, whose expected cost equals the objective (Theorem 1, p. 6).
- (D2) Generate relaxations by linearizing that model's products in more than one way: a product variable z, or the ℓ1 bound |y_j(u) − y_j(v)|. Check the approach by first recovering the published formulation (pp. 7–9).
- (D4) Round with one shared uniform threshold instead of independent coins. Each edge is then cut with probability equal to an ℓ1 term already in the LP, so the expected cost is bounded by the LP value. Drop the most expensive part to gain a (1 − 1/k) factor (pp. 18–19).
- (D4) Quantify a formulation's weakness with an explicit fractional extreme point and with a scalable family whose gap has a limit (16/15, then 10/9) (pp. 11–13).

**Quotes**:
- "Compared with traditional randomized rounding, our algorithm introduces dependencies in the rounding process." (p. 3)
- "The route through the nonlinear characterization offers additional insights into the above formulation." (p. 22)

### S181 · Stability conditions for multiclass fluid queueing networks (IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control 1996, DOI 10.1109/9.543999)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–14 of the journal print (abstract, §§I–VI, references, author biographies). Displayed equations are garbled by OCR, so the arguments are followed from the prose. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Gamarnik and Tsitsiklis match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: David Gamarnik, an MIT ORC PhD candidate (p. 14), and John N. Tsitsiklis (LIDS and ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**:
- SD period. The manuscript was received in February 1995 and revised in February 1996. Funding: the PYI award with Draper matching funds and an ARO grant (p. 1).
- 04-mentorship.md lists Gamarnik's PhD (1997), co-advised with Tsitsiklis.
- 03-process-evidence.md records an authors' correction (IEEE TAC 42(1):128, 1997, DOI 10.1109/TAC.1997.553697). Its content was not read, so this card cannot say which result it touches.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD; LO as the test}: The state of the question on p. 1:
  - The load condition ρ < e is necessary and sufficient for stability in single-class and acyclic multiclass networks. In multiclass networks with feedback, priority policies and even FIFO can be unstable.
  - Lyapunov-function work (quadratic, piecewise linear convex) gives ever sharper sufficient conditions, each stated as LP feasibility.
  - The exact stability region is open. So is whether piecewise linear (or any convex) Lyapunov functions can ever be exact.

  Fluid stability implies stochastic stability (Dai), with a partial converse (Meyn), so the fluid model is the target (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Look at trajectories, not test functions.
  - Any stable trajectory of a two-station network splits into phases of four types, according to which station is busy (Proposition 1, pp. 2–3).
  - The total time τ_j and the work τ_k^j spent in each phase type become LP variables, tied together by work conservation, feasibility and boundary conditions. The resulting LP bounds the emptying time (the strong busy period) of every stable work-conserving policy (Proposition 2, pp. 3–5).
  - Setting Q(0) = 0 gives LP[0]. The network is stable under all work-conserving policies if and only if ρ < e and LP[0] has only the zero solution (Theorem 3, p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: A deterministic fluid model (α, μ, P, C) with n classes and J stations (pp. 1–2):
  - the routing matrix is substochastic with spectral radius below one;
  - allocations are work-conserving;
  - stability means emptying in finite time (Chen; Rybko–Stolyar).

  The exact result is for J = 2 (pp. 2–7). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Sufficiency is by contradiction (Theorem 1, p. 5). If LP[0] has only the zero solution, LP[Q(0)] is bounded, with value Z. Follow an unstable policy up to time Z and then switch to a stable one; this violates Proposition 2.
  - Necessity is constructive (Theorem 2, pp. 6–7). From a nonzero solution, build Q(0) and a piecewise-linear allocation that returns to Q(0): a periodic unstable trajectory. Load contradictions come from multiplying by CM⁻¹[I − P′]⁻¹ (p. 6).
  - Duality, in three steps:
    - Down–Meyn's piecewise-linear Lyapunov LP is feasible iff its dual has only the zero solution (Lemma 5, p. 9).
    - That dual is simplified (Lemmas 6–7) and shown equivalent to LP[0] (Lemmas 8–9, pp. 9–12).
    - Hence piecewise-linear Lyapunov functions are exact for two stations (Corollary 1, p. 12).
  - Special case: two stations with a single class at one of them are stable under the load condition. This generalizes three-class examples of Kumar and of Down–Meyn (Theorem 4, pp. 7–8).
  - For J stations, decomposing by emptying times gives an LP G[0] with nJ variables and 2n + J(J − 1) constraints. The test is sufficient only (Theorem 10, p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none. The test is called "efficiently solvable" (p. 13), but no instance is solved.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: an exact LP test for two stations, its equivalence with Lyapunov LPs, and sufficient conditions for J > 2 (pp. 1, 13). DLP[2] is recommended as the most economical test, with half the variables of LP[0] (p. 12). Limits (p. 13):
  - Necessity for J > 2 is conjectured, not proved.
  - Fluid–stochastic equivalence is "not fully proven (although highly suspected)", so no iff result for stochastic networks follows.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ {SD; LO}: a question owned by the Lyapunov-function and stochastic-process tradition becomes an LP feasibility test (Theorem 3, p. 7). The dual is read to settle the rival method's power (Corollary 1, p. 12). The boundary is printed: J > 2 sufficient only and conjectured necessary; transfer from fluid to stochastic networks unproven (p. 13). This adds an LP stability device to the SD row, which the Domain Map voices from co-authored papers only.
- New-pattern candidate: *exact region as the condition that a homogeneous LP has only the zero solution, with the converse by an explicit periodic counterexample built from a nonzero solution* (pp. 5–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To turn a dynamic property into an LP, decompose an arbitrary trajectory into phases by which constraints are active. Aggregate time and work per phase into variables, and write conservation and boundary conditions as linear constraints. The LP then bounds a performance quantity over all policies (pp. 2–5).
- (D4) Prove an iff with the same LP. Only the zero solution gives boundedness and a contradiction; a nonzero solution is turned into an explicit periodic counterexample (pp. 5–7).
- (D4) To compare with a rival certificate, dualize the rival's LP and show that its dual and your LP relax each other. The equivalence then carries your exactness over to the rival method (pp. 8–12).

**Quotes**:
- "Using linear programming duality, we show that for two-station systems the Lyapunov function approach is equivalent to ours and therefore characterizes stability exactly." (p. 1)
- "new sufficient conditions for multiclass networks with more than two stations that we believe are necessary, although we were unable to establish necessity" (p. 1)

### S280 · Multiclass queueing systems in heavy traffic: an asymptotic approach based on distributional and conservation laws (Operations Research 1997, DOI 10.1287/opre.45.3.470)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–18 of the journal print (abstract, introduction, related work, §§1–5, references). This is a scan: formulas are garbled, and on pp. 15–17 table columns interleave with the prose. Table values are used only where a row is legible. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Mourtzinou match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Georgia Mourtzinou (MIT) (p. 1). Her 1995 MIT PhD thesis, "An Axiomatic Approach to Queueing Systems", is cited (p. 18) · **Position in trajectory**:
- Received October 1993, revised January and June 1995, accepted July 1995 (p. 1).
- The multiclass sequel of two papers (pp. 2, 18): Bertsimas–Nakazato on the distributional Little's law (1995) and Bertsimas–Mourtzinou (Adv. Appl. Prob. 1996).
- Acknowledged funding: the PYI award and NSF DDM-9014751 (p. 18). 04-mentorship.md records the latter as a grant with Odoni as PI.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD}: The systems are multiclass single-server queues with general renewal arrivals (p. 1): ΣGI/G/1 under FIFO, under preemptive and nonpreemptive priorities, and gated polling with changeover times. What existed (pp. 1–2):
  - Brownian heavy-traffic limit theorems for FIFO and for priorities (Iglehart–Whitt; Fendick–Saksena–Whitt; Whitt; Reiman–Simon).
  - Conservation laws that give the performance of priority policies, but only for Poisson arrivals.
  - Polling solved for Poisson arrivals (Sarkar–Zangwill), with a Brownian approach only for two stations.
- **D2 Core idea**: Three steps.
  - Extend the distributional law (number in system = arrivals during a system time) to several classes (Theorem 2, p. 3).
  - Add conservation laws and the server's state to get a complete system: 4N equations in 4N unknowns for FIFO (Theorem 4 and p. 7).
  - Solve it asymptotically, with renewal-kernel expansions as z → 1 and t → ∞ (Theorem 3, p. 5).

  The outputs:
  - waiting-time transforms and means under FIFO (Theorem 6, Proposition 1, p. 8);
  - the conservation-law set function b(A) in heavy traffic for three priority systems (Theorem 8, p. 10);
  - gated-polling mean waits from an N × N linear system (Theorem 9, pp. 13–15).

  The paper states this as a programme (p. 1): define the random variables, derive the laws that relate them, and treat heavy traffic as solving those equations asymptotically ("physics tradition").
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Overtake-free systems (Assumptions A.1–A.4, pp. 2–3): customers enter and leave one at a time, FIFO holds within the system, later arrivals do not affect earlier customers, and the renewal arrival streams are independent.
  - Interarrival or service times are nonarithmetic (p. 8).
  - Priority results are asymptotically exact only for class subsets whose load tends to 1; for the others they are approximations (p. 11).
  - The N-class nonpreemptive case needs a common service distribution (Table I, p. 9; p. 16).
  - The results are exact for Poisson arrivals at any load (pp. 5, 15, 18).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Random-observer and reverse-time numbering arguments for the distributional law (pp. 3–4); Little's law at the server (p. 6).
  - Smith's renewal asymptotics for the kernels, checked against numerical Laplace inversion with two algorithms (pp. 5–6).
  - Device for priorities: b(A) is the same for every work-conserving, nonanticipative policy that favours A. It can therefore be evaluated under FIFO inside A and inside E − A (p. 9).
  - Polling: a heavy-traffic decomposition of the mean waits that generalizes Fuhrmann–Cooper (Proposition 2, pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: The closed forms (DL) are compared with simulation ("Act.") and with the traditional heavy-traffic formulas (HT) on seven setups (Tables II–XII, pp. 15–18): Erlang/M/1, H2/M/1, three-class FIFO, two- and four-class priority, and ten- and two-node polling.
  - DL is usually closer, but HT is better for H2/M/1 with c² > 1 (Table III, p. 15).
  - DL errors are large at low load. In one Erlang/M/1 queue at ρ = 0.4, DL gives 0.042 against a simulated 0.234 (−82%), and HT gives 0.417 (+78%) (Table II, p. 15).
  - Polling deviations stay within about 2.2% (Table XII, p. 18).
  - Simulation run lengths and confidence intervals are not reported.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: an analysis method plus closed forms for three classes of systems (pp. 1, 17–18). Limits stated in §5.7 (pp. 17–18):
  - Accuracy grows with the waiting time, so the method is best for polling, then priority, then FIFO.
  - It is better the closer the arrivals are to Poisson.

  No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ {SD}: formulation first, but the formulation is a complete equation system derived from general laws and solved asymptotically, not a problem kept in an optimization class (pp. 1, 7). The conservation-law set function b(·) is the polymatroid object behind the achievable-region work (SW1); here it serves as an analysis device for priority policies (p. 9).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (steps 5–6 only): the closed forms are dueled against the incumbent approximation (HT), with simulation as ground truth. The losing regimes are printed: c² > 1 and low load (pp. 15–16). There is no certificate step.
- Heuristic 4 (not a Method) · evidence ✅: the regimes where the method loses are in the body (Tables II–III, p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *laws-first analysis*: derive a complete equation system from laws valid at every load, then solve it asymptotically (pp. 1, 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *evaluate a policy-invariant quantity under the most tractable admissible policy* (p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Before approximating, count the unknowns and the laws. Add laws (Little's law at the server, conservation) until the system is square, and only then expand asymptotically (pp. 6–7).
- (D4) If a quantity is invariant across a class of policies, compute it under the most tractable member of the class, here FIFO within the priority groups (p. 9).
- (D5) Report simulation, your approximation and the incumbent approximation side by side, with percentage deviations across traffic intensities, and say where the incumbent wins (Tables II–IV, pp. 15–16).

**Quotes**:
- "Heavy traffic, then, is nothing more than solving the equations that describe the system asymptotically." (p. 1)
- "It is instructive to note that for c2 < 1 or c2 ~ 1 our method slightly outperforms the HT, although things are reversed when c2 > 1." (p. 15)

### S213 · A new algebraic geometry algorithm for integer programming (Management Science 2000, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.46.7.999.12033)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–10 of the journal print (abstract, §§1–6, 13 examples, references). The text layer drops relational symbols (=, ≥, ∈), so formulas are read from context. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Perakis and Tayur match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Georgia Perakis (MIT Sloan) and Sridhar Tayur (Carnegie Mellon GSIA) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**:
- Received April 1998; the paper "was with the authors 11 months for 2 revisions" (p. 10).
- 01-publications.md lists it among the IO anchors, before *Optimization over Integers* (with Weismantel, 2005). The IO domain scope in team.json names algebraic and geometric methods.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO}: Any 0–1 IP is a system of quadratic equalities: Ax = b together with x_j² = x_j (pp. 1–2). Conti–Traverso's algebraic approach works with toric ideals for all right-hand sides. This one fixes b, as Thomas–Weismantel (1997) do, and is motivated by 0–1 problems. It relates to Pitassi's Nullstellensatz proofs of infeasibility and to Lovász (p. 1). Five contributions are listed (pp. 1–2):
  1. enumeration and exact counting of solutions;
  2. optimization of general IPs;
  3. sensitivity analysis;
  4. an infeasibility certificate ("strong duality");
  5. the inverse problem: an IP for a given set of points.
- **D2 Core idea**: Algorithm A computes a reduced Groebner basis of the ideal ⟨Ax − b, x_j² − x_j⟩ in lex order.
  - G = {1} certifies infeasibility. Otherwise the elimination ideals give a triangular system, solved by back-substitution with the elimination and extension theorems (pp. 3–4).
  - The number of solutions equals the number of standard monomials (Theorem 2, p. 6).
  - Optimization: add y − c′x. The roots of the resulting polynomial in y are the feasible costs (p. 8).
  - Sensitivity: keep b_i as a variable and read the value function off the basis (pp. 9–10).
  - General IPs: binary expansion, or the polynomial x(x − 1)⋯(x − U); inequalities via binary slack variables (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**: Integer data. Over the complex numbers, an algebraically closed field, the variety equals the 0–1 feasible set (pp. 2–3). Variables are bounded, either explicitly or by Papadimitriou–Steiglitz's bound (p. 9). No data sets.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Correctness follows from the elimination and extension theorems and the Nullstellensatz (Theorem 1, pp. 3–4).
  - Structure of the reduced basis (Properties 1–3, pp. 5–6): a unique solution gives elements x_i − a_i; the basis has a triangular partition; its binomial and square-free groups speed up enumeration.
  - Basis elements read as logical relations: one element "implies that in all solutions exactly one of x5 and x6 is equal to 1" (p. 4); by the element in x3 and x4, both variables "should always have the same value in any feasible solution" (pp. 4–5).
  - The infeasibility certificate is read as Farkas's lemma for IPs (§3.3, p. 7).
  - No complexity bound for computing the basis is given.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Own C implementation of Buchberger's algorithm on a personal computer (p. 4), plus 13 small examples. The computational report is qualitative: "several integer programming problems of up to 100 variables", faster "when the problem is either infeasible or it has very few solutions", and one 100-variable chain example (p. 10). There are no timings and no comparison with branch and bound or a commercial solver. The comparison with branch and bound is an argument on one family, where LP-based branch and bound needs exponentially many subproblems (Example 9, p. 8).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: an algorithm with several by-products (conclusions, p. 10). The only limit stated is its niche: infeasible problems or problems with few solutions (pp. 1, 10). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ {IO}: formulation first (the 0–1 IP as a polynomial system, p. 1), but the target is an algebraic object, not an LP, conic or MIO class. Tractability is not measured: no solve times, no complexity bound, only "up to 100 variables" (p. 10). In Method 1's terms the paper leaves the class without measured solve times, which the stop rule forbids.
- Research Taste mark 7 (not a Method) · evidence ✅: the infeasibility certificate (G = {1}) is presented as the integer analogue of Farkas's lemma (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *exact nonlinear (polynomial) formulation as a generator of relaxations and certificates* (pp. 1, 3–4, 7); with S658 and S500 in this batch.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Write binary constraints as x_j² − x_j = 0 next to Ax − b. A lex-order reduced Groebner basis is then triangular, and back-substitution enumerates all solutions (pp. 3–5).
- (D2) To get every feasible objective value, add y − c′x to the ideal and eliminate down to a polynomial in y. To get the value function, keep a right-hand side b_i as a variable (pp. 8–10).
- (D4) Read the basis elements as logical implications (exactly one of two; equal pairs) to expose the structure of the feasible set (pp. 4–5).

**Quotes**:
- "Our results are inspired from the observation that we can view any 0–1 IP as a system of quadratic equalities." (p. 1)
- "In preliminary computational work, we have observed that Algorithm A is computationally faster, when the problem is either infeasible or it has very few solutions." (p. 10)

### S147 · Probabilistic service level guarantees in make-to-stock manufacturing systems (Operations Research 2001, DOI 10.1287/opre.49.1.119.11183)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–15 of the journal print (abstract, §§1–11, references). Greek letters are dropped, and Tables 2–5 lose their decimal points ("4316" for 43.16), so no table entries are quoted; the prose percentages are used instead. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Paschalidis match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis (Boston University) (p. 1). 04-mentorship.md lists his 1996 PhD, co-advised by Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis · **Position in trajectory**:
- Received February 1999, accepted September 1999 (p. 1).
- The paper applies large-deviations results from three papers by Bertsimas, Paschalidis and Tsitsiklis (1998a, 1998b, 1999) and from Paschalidis's thesis to manufacturing (pp. 7, 14–15).
- Funding: NSF DMI-9610486 and the MIT–Singapore alliance (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD; AP (manufacturing)}: Multiclass make-to-stock production with backlogging: decide when to idle and which product to make (p. 1). Two entry points (p. 2):
  - Linear backorder costs are standard in the literature but cannot be estimated from data, whereas inventory-cost slopes can. Service is therefore stated as a bound ε_j on the stockout probability of each class.
  - Demand and production are autocorrelated (sales events, weather, machine breakdowns). The paper claims that distributional information, not just two moments, is critical.

  The problem: minimize expected inventory cost subject to P(stockout_j) ≤ ε_j (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Split the policy in two (pp. 2–3).
  - Sequencing comes from a deterministic fluid control problem solved by Pontryagin's principle (Proposition 5.1, pp. 5–6). With linear costs, work on the backlogged class with the largest b_j: a priority policy. With quadratic costs, work on the largest c_j|x_j|: generalized longest queue first (GLQF).
  - Idling uses a base-stock hedging point per class (p. 6). Substituting L = w − x turns the system into a make-to-order queue, so a stockout probability equals an overflow probability (pp. 4, 7).
  - The hedging points: large-deviations decay rates θ*_j (Theorems 7.1–7.3, pp. 7–9) and a refined asymptotic P ≈ α_j e^{−θ*_j w_j}, with α_j matched to E[L_j]. These give w_j = −log(ε_j/α_j)/θ*_j (eqs. (33)–(35), p. 9).
  - The priority order minimizes an analytic approximation of expected inventory cost over the m! orderings (§9, pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Model and data**: Discrete time slots; stationary demand and production processes with mild mixing, which covers renewal and Markov-modulated processes (pp. 2–3). Stability requires E[ΣD_j] < E[B] (p. 3). Admitted limits:
  - The linear and quadratic costs serve only to derive the sequencing structure (p. 3).
  - The stockout constraint controls only the fraction of stockouts, not waiting times, and can be unfair (p. 3).
  - The policies are not optimal for an objective of expected inventory plus backorder cost, whose optimum is unknown (p. 7).
  - GLQF asymptotics exist only for two classes (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Gärtner–Ellis large deviations (pp. 3–4). In the single-class M/M/1 case the asymptotic is exact (θ* = μ − λ), so the hedging point is exact (p. 5).
  - The multiclass decay rates come from an optimal-control formulation of the most likely overflow path. It gives a tight lower bound, which a large-deviations upper bound matches (Remark 2, p. 8).
  - Theorem 7.2 reduces each decay rate to the largest root of a convex function (p. 8).
  - E[L_j] comes from simulation or from the Krämer–Langenbach-Belz GI/G/1 approximation (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Two synthetic examples, with bursty Markov-modulated demand and a facility that alternates between ON and OFF (broken down): three classes under priority (five cost and service cases), and two classes under GLQF (pp. 11–14).
  - The predicted best ordering matched the simulated best in all five cases, and the inventory-cost approximations were within 5.5–9% (p. 11).
  - Analytic hedging points were mostly within 3% of the simulated ones; two cases were at 3.8% and 7.52%, partly because of the integer grid of the simulation (p. 12).
  - Achieved stockout probabilities had the order of magnitude of the targets (pp. 12–13).
  - There is no competing policy as a baseline, e.g. base-stock with two-moment safety stocks, or Wein's heavy-traffic policy.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: probabilistic QoS constraints, autocorrelated processes, and a combined fluid and large-deviations method (p. 2; §11, p. 13). Limits:
  - the search over m! orderings, with a heuristic search suggested (p. 11);
  - E[L] needs simulation or an approximation (pp. 9, 12).

  Proposed implementation: estimate Markov-modulated models online from observations and update the policy (p. 12). The conclusion claims a "substantial performance loss" from ignoring distributional information (p. 13). The paper shows this only through the ordering examples (Cases 4–5, p. 11), not against a two-moment policy.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: step 3, shape the model around the data that exist. Backorder costs are dropped because they cannot be estimated, inventory-cost slopes are kept because they can (p. 2), and online estimation is proposed (p. 12). But there is no owner's current rule as a baseline and no real data: the examples are synthetic (pp. 11–14).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: the property is the service level, the knob is ε_j per class, and the price is the hedging point (safety stock). The price comes from an asymptotic formula that is exact only in the M/M/1 case (pp. 5, 9). The curve is tabulated as hedging point and achieved stockout probability for ε between 0.15 and 10⁻⁴ (Tables 2–5, pp. 12–14). There is no solution-independent bound.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ {SD}: a rare-event probability is computed as the value of a deterministic optimal-control problem whose optimal path is the most likely overflow scenario. It reduces to the largest root of a convex function (p. 8). This is an optimization lens on probability, but not an LP, conic or MIO formulation.
- New-pattern candidate: *fluid model for the policy's structure, rare-event asymptotics for its parameters* (pp. 2–3, 5–9).
- New-pattern candidate: *replace an unmeasurable penalty by a probabilistic constraint with a per-class knob* (pp. 2–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Under a base-stock policy, substitute L = w − x to turn a make-to-stock system into a make-to-order queue. The stockout probability becomes a buffer-overflow probability with known asymptotics (pp. 4, 7).
- (D2) Decide the structure of a stochastic control policy (which class to serve) in the fluid limit, and set its safety parameters (when to idle) with rare-event asymptotics (pp. 3, 5–9).
- (D4) Calibrate the prefactor of an exponential tail approximation by matching its mean to E[L], an idea the paper takes from Abate, Choudhury and Whitt. Then invert it for the threshold that meets a target probability (p. 9).
- (D5) Validate analytic thresholds against simulated ones over a range of target probabilities, and separate the error from the simulation grid from the error of the method (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "The assumption of linear backorder costs, that is often made in the literature, appears to serve analytical tractability rather than an accurate representation of reality." (p. 2)
- "The important conclusion is that the optimal priority ordering may depend on subtle distributional differences between classes that cannot be captured by just the first few moments." (p. 11)

### S256 · Multistage lot sizing problems via randomized rounding (Operations Research 2001, DOI 10.1287/opre.49.4.599.11222)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–10 of the journal print (abstract, §§1–5, references). Formulas are partly garbled; the proofs of Theorems 5–7 and Proposition 2 were followed in outline. First-page check: the title and the authors Teo and Bertsimas match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chung-Piaw Teo, first author (National University of Singapore) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**:
- The journal version of Teo and Bertsimas, IPCO 1996 (references, p. 10). The same page cites Teo's 1996 MIT thesis, "Constructing approximation algorithms via linear programming relaxations".
- Received January 1999, revised March 2000, accepted June 2000 (p. 1).
- 04-mentorship.md marks it as a student-first paper. S658 in this batch is the same student's 1995 working paper with the same rounding device.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO; AP (inventory and distribution)}: Multistage lot sizing over an acyclic assembly or distribution network, with constant demand, fixed ordering costs, echelon holding costs and no stockouts; the policies are stationary and nested (p. 1). The entry has two parts:
  - Roundy's 94% (fixed base) and 98% (variable base) power-of-two guarantees rest on the EOQ-type structure of the relaxation's optimal solution. They fail when the input is merely feasible (p. 4).
  - Lu and Posner's 1 + ε schemes do not help in the fixed-base model, and the complexity of the optimal policy is open (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Write T_i = 2^{p_i} z_i T_L and round all items with one random Y on [1, 2): round down if z_i < Y, otherwise up.
  - The distribution of Y is chosen so that E[T°_i] and E[1/T°_i] are at most 1.06 times T_i and 1/T_i. Any feasible solution then rounds to a nested power-of-two policy (Algorithm A, Theorem 1, p. 4).
  - A log-uniform Y recasts Roundy's 98% algorithm (Algorithm B, Theorem 2, pp. 4–5).
  - The rounding preserves the order of the T_i, so the greedy (Edmonds) maximizer of a submodular ordering cost stays optimal. This gives 1.06 and 1.02 under submodular costs (Theorem 4, p. 5).
  - With resource constraints: 1.44 (Algorithm C, Theorem 5), and 1.06 with a single resource (Algorithm D, Theorem 6) (p. 6).
  - Joint replenishment with a fixed base uses a stronger relaxation, with costs interpolated piecewise linearly between integer intervals. Its optimal intervals are integral, and it lower-bounds every dynamic policy (Propositions 1–2, pp. 7–8). Randomizing between power-of-two and 3·2^p roundings (p = 0.7, q = 0.3) then gives 1.043, i.e. 95.8% (Algorithm E, Theorem 7, pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**: The assembly rate is infinite, so lead time is zero and the zero-ordering property holds. The holding cost at a node is no larger than the sum at its predecessors. The base period T_L is fixed or optimized (pp. 1–2). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Linearity of expectation with a tuned threshold distribution (p. 4).
  - Derandomization: once the z_i are sorted there are at most n + 1 distinct outcomes, so O(n log n) time (p. 4).
  - Order preservation keeps the polymatroid greedy solution optimal (p. 5).
  - Convexity and Jensen-type bounds show that the joint-replenishment relaxation lower-bounds dynamic policies (pp. 7–8, followed in outline).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none. The paper does not discuss "the details of solving these relaxations efficiently in practice" (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: simpler proofs of known bounds, extensions to submodular and constrained problems, and a stronger formulation that yields the 95.8% joint-replenishment bound against dynamic policies (p. 3; §5, p. 9). Limit (Remark 2, p. 9): intervals with T*_i = 2 lose in the rounding, which also rules out intervals of 5T_L and above. For the one-warehouse multiretailer improvements the paper points to Teo–Bertsimas (1996) and Teo's thesis (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ {IO as engine; AP}: a problem owned by inventory theory (Roundy's power-of-two policies) is handled through a convex relaxation and rounding. The improvement comes from a stronger formulation, as the conclusion says (p. 9). The boundary is printed: no extension to 5T_L and beyond (p. 9). This supports the Domain Map's IO row (LP-relaxation rounding; strong formulations).
- New-pattern candidate: *dependent randomized rounding with one shared threshold*, here with a threshold distribution tuned to balance two cost terms (pp. 4–5, 8–9). This is the second instance, after S658.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) For costs of the form K/T + HT, choose the threshold distribution F so that rounding inflates T and 1/T by the same factor. One common draw for all items keeps the policy nested (p. 4).
- (D4) Derandomize a single-threshold rounding by enumerating the at most n + 1 breakpoints of the sorted fractional parts (p. 4).
- (D4) When a cost is a maximum over a polymatroid, use an order-preserving rounding, so that the same greedy vertex stays optimal before and after rounding (p. 5).
- (D2) To beat a classical bound, first strengthen the relaxation (interpolate the cost between integer intervals). Then randomize between two rounding classes and tune the mixing probability (pp. 7–9).

**Quotes**:
- "We perform a randomized rounding analysis and show that the dependence on the structure of the optimal solution can be removed for the 1.06 bound to hold." (p. 4)
- "This difficulty also precludes the possibility of extending the technique to include order intervals of type 5TL and above." (p. 9)

### S500 · Geometry, moments, and semidefinite optimization (Proceedings of the 15th International Symposium on MTNS 2002; no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–15 of the manuscript dated May 2002 (abstract, §§1–6, Tables 1–6, references). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Caramanis match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Constantine Caramanis (MIT LIDS, NSF predoctoral fellowship) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**:
- The moments programme (SW2) carried to PDEs, with support from the SMA–MIT alliance (p. 1).
- It builds on two works cited on p. 15 (refs [2] and [3]): Bertsimas–Popescu's optimal inequalities, and Caramanis's June 2001 MIT Master's thesis on solving linear PDEs by semidefinite optimization.
- 01-publications.md lists the later journal paper (Bertsimas & Caramanis, Math. Program. 108, 2006) among the CP anchors.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP; SD (probability bounds) and PDE bounds as applications}: Geometric facts about the feasible set (connected, compact, convex, discrete) decide how hard a problem is, yet they "are rarely exploited" (p. 1). A moment relaxation, max f(m) s.t. m ∈ P, ignores two facts: m must be a moment sequence, and its distribution has a known support K. The paper uses P ∩ V(K) instead (p. 2). Two applications (pp. 1–2): bounds on linear functionals of linear PDEs without a mesh, and optimal probability inequalities.
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - By Haviland's theorem, a sequence is a moment sequence on Ω iff the moment functional is nonnegative on every polynomial nonnegative on Ω. Putinar's representation on compact semialgebraic sets turns this into PSD localizing matrices, one per defining polynomial (Theorems 1–3, pp. 3–4). Truncation gives a hierarchy (p. 4).
  - PDEs (pp. 5–8): write the weak form with monomial test functions and the adjoint operator, which gives linear equalities in the interior and boundary moments of u. Add PSD constraints for u − u0 ≥ 0, with u0 as a decision variable, then minimize and maximize the linear functional.
  - Probability (pp. 12–13): the dual of the (n, k, Ω) bound needs a polynomial that is nonnegative on Ω and at least 1 on S. Putinar certificates of degree 2r give a hierarchy of SDPs.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Linear differential operators with polynomial coefficients; a separable class of test functions; solutions with compact support or exponentially decaying tails (pp. 5–6).
  - K is compact basic semialgebraic and satisfies Putinar's condition (p. 4); the support is known a priori (pp. 1–2).
  - Test cases: a modified Bessel equation on [0, 1] and the Helmholtz equation on [0, 1]² with f = 3e^{x+y} (pp. 9–11); uniform distributions on [−1, 3] and on a disk of radius 2 (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Haviland's and Putinar's theorems, and Theorem 3's characterization by localizing matrices (pp. 3–4).
  - The boundary is printed (p. 4): truncated matrices give necessary conditions only, and unless P = NP the certificate degree must be exponential in the worst case. Hence a trade-off between complexity and tightness.
  - The adjoint comes from integration by parts or Stokes's formula and is "easily automated" (pp. 6, 10).
  - The max and min of u are bounded on one side only; the other side is "only an approximation" (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Solvers: SeDuMi, SDPA, SOSTOOLS, and an LP code the paper calls "AMPLE" (pp. 9, 11, 13).
  - Bessel: the moment bounds are tight to four decimals at N = 24 (Table 2, p. 10). The approximations of the min and max stay near −0.31 and 0.50–0.51 for N = 20–40, against true values of −0.347 and 0.583, and they are not monotone in N (Table 1, p. 10).
  - Helmholtz: the LP bounds are loose (the upper bound on m0,0 is +∞), while the SDP bounds are tight at N = 10–20 (Tables 3–4, pp. 11–12). Solve times are under 0.5 s, 3–5 s and 1–3 min for N = 5, 10 and 20 (p. 12). The exact moments are never printed for comparison.
  - SDPA showed numerical instabilities (p. 11).
  - Probability: support knowledge lowers the bounds by "20 to 30 percent in some cases", but at three moments it changes nothing (0.4640 in both columns) (Tables 5–6, p. 14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: two applications and a framework claimed to be general (§6, p. 15). Limits:
  - the complexity of the hierarchy (p. 4);
  - one-sided bounds on the max and min (p. 8);
  - numerical instability (p. 11);
  - an effect of geometry that "may be more pronounced in some set ups, than in others" (p. 14).

  Future: "many more applications" (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ {CP}: questions from probability and from PDEs become SDPs (pp. 2–8, 12–13). The worst case is read from the dual, as polynomial nonnegativity (p. 13). The boundary is printed: truncation is only necessary, the degree is exponential unless P = NP (p. 4), and the max/min bounds are one-sided (p. 8). This matches the Domain Map's CP row ("probability questions as convex programs; worst case from the dual; NP-hardness mapped").
- New-pattern candidate: *exact nonlinear (polynomial) formulation as a generator of relaxations* (pp. 2–4); with S658 and S213 in this batch.
- New-pattern candidate: *use cheap structural information before buying more data*: support constraints at low moment order instead of more moments (p. 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Bound a linear functional of a linear PDE without a mesh. Use the weak form with monomial test functions and get the adjoint by integration by parts; this gives linear equalities in moments. Then add PSD moment constraints for u − u0 ≥ 0, with u0 as a variable (pp. 5–8).
- (D2) Before adding higher-order moment constraints, add the localizing matrices of the support. They are cheaper and can tighten the bound more (p. 14).
- (D5) Report the LP-only bounds and the SDP bounds side by side to isolate the value of the geometry constraints (Tables 3–4, p. 12).
