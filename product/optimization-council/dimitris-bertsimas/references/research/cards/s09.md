### S065 · Tractable stochastic analysis in high dimensions via robust optimization (Mathematical Programming 134(1) 2012, DOI 10.1007/s10107-012-0567-2)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–23 (introduction, building blocks, queueing section with Theorems 2–4 and the RQNA experiments), pp. 23–36 (auctions: literature, robust LP (37)–(43), Theorem 5, Proposition 4, Algorithm 4, ROM-Si experiments; Algorithms 2–3 on pp. 28–29 skimmed), the option set-up pp. 36–38 and pp. 44–46 (option results, concluding remarks) read; the option formulations pp. 38–44 not read (they summarise the working paper published as S171); references pp. 46–48 skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chaithanya Bandi (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the programme statement of the robust-probability line ("The research program surveyed here", p. 4), received 16 November 2011 and accepted 1 June 2012 (p. 1); it surveys six 2011–2012 working papers of Bandi with Bertsimas, Youssef and Chen (refs [2]–[7], p. 46).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+SD+AP}: probability theory "has not been developed with computational tractability as an objective when the dimension increases" (abstract, p. 1); the open cases named are queueing networks with general distributions, multi-item auctions with budget constraints, network information theory and multi-asset options with frictions (p. 3), with Kingman quoted against treating arrival times as random variables (p. 3). Entry: replace the Kolmogorov axioms and random variables by uncertainty sets built from limit laws (pp. 1, 5).
- **D2 Key idea**: take the conclusions of probability as primitives: the CLT set |ΣXᵢ − nμ| ≤ Γσ√n (eq. 1, p. 5), stable-law sets with n^{1/α} for heavy tails (eq. 5, p. 7) and Shannon typical sets for known densities (Proposition 1, Corollary 1, pp. 8–10); then compute a performance measure as the max or min of f over the set (p. 6). Three applications: closed-form worst-case waiting times (Theorem 2, p. 12) and set families closed under passing through a queue (Theorem 3, an analogue of Burke's theorem), superposition and thinning, so that a network decomposes node by node (Theorem 4, pp. 14–18); incentive compatibility, individual rationality and budget feasibility as robust LP constraints with a mechanism ROM (eqs. 37–42, Theorem 5, pp. 26–29); options by ε-arbitrage with ℓ1 replication error, which gives LPs (p. 36).
- **D3 Model and data**: Γ is "a small numerical constant 2 or 3 that is selected adaptively to make a good fit empirically" (p. 5); the queue sets hold for k ≤ k0, typically n − 30 (p. 11), and equal tail indices αa = αs are assumed (p. 11; the asymmetric case is deferred to [6], p. 18); in RQNA the service-variability parameter is a function fitted by linear regression to simulated single-queue waiting times (p. 19); auction valuations lie in i.i.d., correlated or normal-typical polyhedral sets (p. 26). "We do not provide all the mathematical proofs" (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: the Lindley recursion plus the set bounds reduce the worst case to a one-dimensional concave maximization solved in closed form (eqs. 17–21, pp. 12–13), with the same dependence on λ/(1 − ρ) and on the variability terms as Kingman's bound (eqs. 22–25, pp. 13–14); the auction LP is dualized (eq. 43, p. 27); its bilinear subproblems are NP-hard for general sets but have an extreme-point optimum, polynomial when the set has polynomially many extreme points (Proposition 4, p. 30), and are solved by generalized Benders decomposition (Algorithm 4, p. 30).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: on Kuehn's nine-queue network RQNA's sojourn-time errors relative to simulation stay within ±3.9%, against 5.8–23.7% for Whitt's QNA (Table 1, p. 20); random 10–30-node networks keep errors below 6.2% with feedback and below 8.5% with mixed arrival laws (Tables 3–6, pp. 21–22); ROM-Si earns 27–103% more than Myerson when the distribution family is wrong (Table 7, pp. 33–34) but 17.6–58% less when the true mean is two or three times the assumed one (Table 9, p. 35); option Tables 10–11 (p. 45) repeat the numbers of S171's Tables 3 and 5; no solver, machine or run time is given (searched).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a programme claim, tractable methods in three fields that "go beyond the current state of the art of stochastic analysis" (p. 46); admitted limits: proofs left to the working papers (p. 4), auction results "mixed" when the mean is misspecified (p. 35), the largest option errors for American puts (p. 45). Future: the programme "will also advance optimization as it will reveal new optimization problems" (p. 46).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: problems owned by probability, economics and finance are written as LP or bilinear programs over uncertainty sets, and the auction LP's dual is written out (pp. 6, 26–27, 36); the boundary is printed: bilinear subproblems NP-hard for general sets (p. 30).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: presented as a research programme, carried by Bandi with Youssef and Chen in six working papers within two years (pp. 4, 46).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the why-now is historical (probability was built for foundations, optimization for computation since Dantzig, with practical rather than polynomial efficiency as the test) instead of a measured solver figure (pp. 3–4, 45–46).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the knob Γ is fitted to data (p. 5) or, in RQNA, to simulation (p. 19), not priced as a protection–cost curve.
- Taste mark 6 ✅ evidence: Dantzig's "final test of any theory" is quoted as the programme's guide (p. 4).
- Tension T3 evidence: probability distributions are called "a choice" (p. 46), while limit laws, typical sets and simulation-fitted parameters carry probability back in (pp. 5–10, 19).
- new-pattern candidate: *limit laws as primitives*: uncertainty sets from the CLT, stable laws and typical sets replace distributions (pp. 5–10; also S171 p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a probabilistic performance question into a max or min over a set built from a limit theorem, then check that the robust answer has the classical bound's functional form (Kingman) (pp. 5–6, 13–14).
- For networks, prove that the set family is closed under the network operations (passing through a node, superposition, thinning), so the network decomposes into single nodes (pp. 14–18).
- Write incentive constraints (IC, IR, budget feasibility) as robust constraints over the valuation set and dualize once (pp. 26–27).
- Test robustness to misspecification twice: fix mean and standard deviation and swap the distribution family, then sweep the total variation distance; report where the robust design loses (Tables 7–9, Fig. 2, pp. 33–35).

**Quotes**:
- "The key building block in our approach is that rather than assuming as primitives the axioms of probability theory (Kolmogorov axioms and the notion of a random variable), we assume as primitives the conclusions of probability theory, namely its limit laws." (p. 5)
- "Modeling stochastic phenomena with probability theory is a choice, that is probability distributions are not inherent to the problem." (p. 46)

### S232 · An integer optimization approach to associative classification (Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 25, pp. 269–277, 2012; no DOI or arXiv id)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–8 read in full; references p. 9 skimmed. The longer MIT DSpace version with further results (ref. [6], p. 9) was not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Allison Chang, Cynthia Rudin (MIT Operations Research Center); author order alphabetical (footnote 1, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an early MIO-for-ML paper funded by NSF IIS-1053407 (p. 1), before the best-subset and optimal-trees line; it calls itself "among the first to use MIO methods for machine learning" (p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+IO}: accurate classifiers such as SVMs are not interpretable, while decision trees are interpretable "but not specifically optimized to achieve the highest in-sample accuracy" (p. 1); rule learning faces a combinatorial explosion of rules and L! orders of L rules (p. 2), which associative classifiers (CBA, CMAR, CPAR) handle by heuristic search or by ordering rules with an interestingness measure (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: two MIOs (ORC). RuleGen mines rules on the frontier of maximal support s and minimal coverage sX, which contains the best rule for many interestingness measures (Table 2, pp. 3–4); a ranking MIO orders the rules into a decision list that maximizes regularized training accuracy, with both null rules included so that the default class is learned (pp. 4–6). Interpretability enters as two regularizers: small rule bodies, and a short list through the rank r* of the higher null rule (pp. 1, 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: binary transactions tᵢ ∈ {0,1}^d and binary classes, the multi-class case said to extend "trivially" (p. 2); the objective is 0–1 accuracy, claimed robust to outliers (p. 6); weights are set so regularization never costs accuracy (R_gen,x = 0.1/n, R_gen,b = 0.1/(nd), R_rank = 1/L), with minimum coverage 0.05 and five iterations per frontier point (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: xᵢ stays continuous because constraints (1)–(2) force it to 0 or 1 (p. 3); the frontier is traced by lowering an upper bound on coverage, with no-good cuts (6) excluding rule bodies already found (p. 4); the ranking MIO uses assignment variables and big-M-type links, adding (11), (12) and (23) only to tighten the LP relaxation (p. 6); no theory beyond the formulation's validity.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: 11 public data sets, 3-fold cross-validation, against logistic regression, SVM (RBF, C = 1), CART, C4.5, random forests and AdaBoost in R 2.15.0; ORC in AMPL 11.210 with Gurobi; ranking times up to 21,505.7 s, the solver generally stopped before proving optimality and no gaps reported (p. 7); by Table 4, ORC has the highest or tied-highest test accuracy on 4 of 11 sets and the lowest on SPECT (p. 7); a nine-rule Tic-Tac-Toe list against 21 CART and 36 C4.5 leaves (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: accuracy "on par" with top methods and no forced trade-off between accuracy and interpretability (pp. 7–8); limits: more computation ("willing to take more time", p. 2), uncertified rankings (p. 7), interpretability "necessarily subjective" (p. 1); future: optimization-based rule mining, interpretable classifiers and new regularizers (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: rule mining and list ranking, the domain of data-mining heuristics, are written exactly as MIOs; formulation strength is named as the key to solvability, and LP-tightening constraints are flagged as such (pp. 2–3, 6).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: a joint MIO replaces heuristic mining and measure-based ordering and duels six standard classifiers out of sample (pp. 2, 6–7), but without warm starts and without certificates or gaps; the paper argues that early termination "does not imply that we do not have an optimum" (p. 7; Tension T1).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the why-now is asserted ("tremendous progress in MIO hardware and software over the last two decades") without a figure (p. 2).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: interpretability gets two knobs, but they are set so that they never cost accuracy, and the paper claims there is no trade-off instead of pricing one (pp. 7–8).
- new-pattern candidate: *one frontier for a family of criteria*: optimize the two primitive statistics that many scores are monotone in, so a single frontier holds every score's optimum (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a family of scores is monotone in two primitive statistics, trace their efficient frontier once (bound one, maximize the other, add no-good cuts for ties) instead of optimizing each score (pp. 3–4).
- Keep binary only the structural choices; let indicators that the constraints force to 0/1 stay continuous (p. 3).
- Add redundant valid inequalities that tighten the LP relaxation, and say which constraints are redundant (p. 6).
- Show interpretability on a data set whose true rule people know (Tic-Tac-Toe's three in a row), next to the model sizes of the interpretable competitors (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Our ability to solve an MIO problem depends critically on the strength of the formulation, which is related to the geometry of the set of feasible solutions." (p. 2)
- "We generally terminated the solver before (7) solved to provable optimality." (p. 7)

### S159 · A hybrid approach to beam angle optimization in intensity-modulated radiation therapy (Computers & Operations Research 40(9) 2013, DOI 10.1016/j.cor.2012.06.009)
Read level: **full**: the article-in-press version, pp. 1–11, all text and Tables 1–4; references p. 11 skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Valentina Cacchiani (University of Bologna, corresponding author), David Craft (Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School), Omid Nohadani (Purdue) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a health-care application from the RO period that uses simulated annealing as a working tool; it cites Bertsimas, Nohadani and Teo's robust SA and nonconvex robust optimization papers (refs [3]–[4], p. 11); Thomas Bortfeld is thanked (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {HS+LO+AP}: in IMRT planning, planners pick beam angles "on the basis of experience and intuition" and software then optimizes the intensities (abstract, p. 1); angle choice relies on trial and error (p. 2), and published methods (simulated annealing, genetic algorithms, MIP, score functions) need a candidate angle set (p. 2). Entry: choose angles and intensities together over every angle the accelerator can deliver (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Key idea**: a hybrid method (HM) alternating K_GD gradient steps and K_SA annealing steps over the angle vector; for fixed angles the intensities come from an LP solved to optimality by CPLEX, and the gradient comes from that LP's dual and sensitivity analysis (after Craft [6]) (pp. 4–5). The annealing uses Gaussian moves and adaptive cooling T0·exp(−c·l^{1/d}) (p. 5). Changed: no candidate set; kept: Hong et al.'s clinical model (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Model and data**: co-planar beams (p. 2); a nonlinear clinical model with max/mean, min/mean and ramp objectives, and dose bounds and weights set by physicians (eqs. 1–13, pp. 3–4); dose matrices computed every Δ degrees (2° for the phantom, 5° for the real case) and linearly interpolated (pp. 2–3, 6–7); beamlet-based, without multileaf-collimator delivery (p. 2).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: no convergence theory; steps shrink tenfold until the objective improves or a threshold set by machine accuracy is reached (p. 4); for a coarse dose grid a thresholded rule moves each angle by 2°, 1° or the machine minimum (p. 4); the MILP comparator selects n of m candidate angles with binaries, a cardinality constraint and links x_j ≤ I·c_k (eqs. 15–17, p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: a 2D pancreas phantom (1,192 voxels, five angles of 16 beamlets), 40 runs of 1,800 s with CPLEX 9.0 on a 1.6 GHz Intel T2300 with 1 GB: about 18% better than the equispaced start, gradient descent alone worst (p. 6), the average run 2.4% and the worst 4.2% from the best (p. 7); the MILP on 36 candidate angles is optimal in 2,236 s but 5% worse than HM's best, and on 72 candidates optimal only after 123,823 s and 2.8% worse (p. 7); one MGH 3D pancreas case (five angles of 112 beamlets, CPLEX 11.0, 80,000 s): HM 11.8–20.7% better than equispaced angles, pure annealing 7.3–15.3% (Table 2, p. 8); at 30 min the stomach instance gains 0.0% (Table 4, p. 10). Parameters are the best of several settings tried (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: 4–7% better than pure annealing and above 10% better than equispaced angles (p. 11); limits: case-specific thresholds (p. 4), no collimator implementation (pp. 2, 11), long runs; future: faster runs that allow several starting sets, and direct aperture optimization (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the baseline is the planners' own rule, equispaced angles, "a common approach" (p. 4), on a hospital partner's case with physician-set parameters (pp. 4, 7).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the roles are reversed: the heuristic over continuous angles is the method and the exact MILP over a candidate grid is the beaten opponent (p. 7); no certificate or gap is reported for the heuristic.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the LP stays the inner engine and its dual serves as a gradient oracle for an outer nonconvex search, instead of a reformulation that keeps the whole problem in class (p. 4).
- Heuristic 3 ✅ evidence: the exact model stalls (34 h for 72 candidates, p. 7), and local search with restarts runs over the structural variables, the angles (pp. 4–7).
- Tension T2 evidence: simulated annealing serves as a working tool (pp. 4–5).
- new-pattern candidate: *heuristic outer search over an LP engine*: continuous design variables moved by dual-based gradients and annealing, each candidate scored by an exact LP solve (pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For design variables that enter an LP's data, get the gradient of the LP optimum from its dual solution and the chain rule through the data matrix, and use it inside a local-plus-annealing search (p. 4).
- Tie step sizes and stopping thresholds to the actuator's physical resolution, so the search never proposes designs the machine cannot deliver (pp. 4–5).
- Price a discretization: solve the exact MILP on candidate grids of increasing resolution to optimality and compare it with the continuous-space method (p. 7).
- Report anytime results (30 min, 1 h, 5 h) next to the full run (Table 4, p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "The proposed method presents a main difference from previous works: it does not require to have on input a set of candidate beam angles." (p. 1)
- "We wish to mention that we tried several different parameter settings, and we describe in the next section the choice that experimentally turns out to be the best one." (p. 5)

### S211 · Learning preferences under noise and loss aversion: An optimization approach (Operations Research 61(5) 2013, DOI 10.1287/opre.2013.1209)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 2–10 (printed pp. 1190–1198) read in full, references pp. 10–11 skimmed; file p. 1 is the publisher's cover sheet. Formula symbols are partly garbled in the extraction (parentheses as 4 and 5, commas as 1, points as 0); prose intact.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Allison O'Hair (MIT Sloan lecturer per the author notes, p. 11) · **Position in trajectory**: robust and integer optimization applied to elicitation for a personalized dieting application (p. 8); received November 2012, accepted June 2013 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+IO+DA}: preference learning spans economics, machine learning and OR, multicriteria decision making and marketing (p. 2); earlier methods pick one utility although several fit the data, while people answer inconsistently and are loss averse (pp. 2–3); adaptive questionnaires suffer from response errors in early questions (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: (a) one binary flip variable per answered question, with a cap on the number of flips, makes the utility set self-correcting (pp. 4–5); (b) the next question is the pair whose hyperplane passes closest to the analytic center of this mixed-integer set, a variant of Toubia et al. for a fixed item list (pp. 5–6); (c) the remaining set U_k becomes the uncertainty set of a max–min recommendation problem (p. 7); (d) CVaR over hit-and-run samples of U_k tunes the conservatism (pp. 7–8). A "feel strongly" answer removes the flip option for that question (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: linear utility u′x with u ∈ [−1, 1]^n (p. 3); a strictness margin of 0.1 (pp. 4–5); at most 10% of the answers may flip (p. 5); for CVaR the binaries are fixed at their analytic-center values (p. 7); recommendations by enumeration over the items (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: big-M flip constraints (p. 4) and big-M slack selection inside the log-barrier analytic-center problem, solved by Newton's method from an interior point (eqs. 1a–1o, p. 5); question choice by enumerating O(n²) pairs (p. 6); the max–min cannot be dualized as in Bertsimas–Sim because the inner set is mixed-integer, so items are enumerated (p. 7); the CVaR problem becomes an LP by dualizing the inner minimization (eq. 5, p. 8); hit-and-run mixes in polynomial time (Lovász–Vempala) (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: simulated users only: 10 planted utility vectors, 200 runs per user and noise level, Gaussian response noise, 10 questions, 102 dinner recipes with 186 ingredient attributes (pp. 8–9); score: the mean true rank of the five recommended recipes and its CVaR at 5% and 20%, against Toubia et al.'s analytic center (AC) and a self-correcting AC (pp. 9–10); the robust version is dominated on average but least variable, AC is best for noise σ ≤ 0.2 and CVaR for 0.2 < σ ≤ 0.5 (pp. 9–10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: one contribution each to robust optimization, marketing and risk modelling (p. 3); limits: robust solutions too conservative (p. 7), parameter values "representative" rather than cross-validated (p. 9), no evaluation on real users' answers (pp. 8–9); no future work stated (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the CVaR level is a conservatism knob motivated by the Bertsimas–Sim trade-off (p. 7), and the flip cap is a budget on data errors (p. 5); there is no solution-independent bound, and the curves run over noise, not over the knob (pp. 9–10).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the CVaR recommendation stays an LP by dualizing the inner problem (p. 8), and the boundary is printed: the max–min over a mixed-integer set is not dualizable, so items are enumerated (p. 7).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: recommendations are scored by the true rank of what is recommended, a decision metric, against the marketing incumbent (p. 9), though with simulated users (p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate: *budgeted data errors*: binary flips with a cap make the learned set robust to a fixed number of wrong answers, the budget idea applied to data (p. 5).
- new-pattern candidate: *the elicited feasible set as the uncertainty set* of the downstream decision (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Cap the number of data points allowed to be wrong (Σφᵢ ≤ Γk) instead of penalizing errors in the objective (p. 5).
- Keep the whole set of models consistent with the data and optimize the decision against it, instead of collapsing it to a point estimate (p. 7).
- When the robust counterpart is not dualizable, fix the binaries at a reference point, sample the remaining polytope uniformly (hit-and-run) and optimize the sample CVaR as an LP (pp. 7–8).
- Evaluate a recommender with planted true utilities and report the mean and the tail (CVaR) of the true rank of what it recommends, across noise levels (pp. 8–10).

**Quotes**:
- "this would minimize the number of response errors we assume happen, instead of the number of response errors that actually happen." (p. 5)
- "Although the robust optimization approach provides a loss averse method, it tends to produce solutions that may be too conservative." (p. 7)

### S315 · An accelerated first-order method for solving SOS relaxations of unconstrained polynomial optimization problems (Optimization Methods and Software 28(3) 2013, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2012.656114)
Read level: **full**, of the March 2011 preprint titled "An Accelerated First-Order Method for Solving Unconstrained Polynomial Optimization Problems" (p. 1): pp. 1–19 read in full, references pp. 20–22 skimmed; not compared with the published version.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Robert M. Freund (MIT Sloan), Xu Andy Sun (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the moment and SDP line {CP} turned to algorithms; it cites Bertsimas's moment-problem papers as uses of polynomial optimization (refs [4], [5], [7], pp. 1, 20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP}: the SDP hierarchy for unconstrained polynomial optimization has a matrix variable of order O(n^m × n^m), and interior-point software (GloptiPoly, SOSTOOLS) is limited by this growth (p. 2); accelerated first-order methods had not been applied to the class (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: run an accelerated first-order method (AFOM, of Auslender–Teboulle and Nesterov type) on Lu's reformulation, the squared distance to the affine set minimized over the cone (pp. 6–7), and make each iteration cheap: the basic moment matrices are mutually orthogonal, so AA* is diagonal and the projection onto the affine set costs O(N²) instead of O(M²N²) (Lemmas 1–3, pp. 8–12). Changed: scheme (2.14) is used although worst-case analysis prefers (2.15), because (2.14) converged faster in their runs (p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: even degree 2m; p(x) − γ* is assumed SOS, so the relaxation is exact and strong duality holds (pp. 5, 7); planted instances p(x) = Σᵢ(qᵢ(x) − qᵢ(x*))² + γ* around a random x* ∈ [−1, 1]^n, so γ* is known; dense quartics and sextics with about 10% density (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: an O(1/ε) iteration bound quoted from [1] and [15] (Theorem 1, p. 7); Lemma 1 via the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury identity (pp. 8–9); a closed-form projection from the normal equations (Lemma 2, pp. 9–11); divisors bounded for numerical stability (Remark 2, p. 11); O(N²) operations per projection (Lemma 3, p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: AFOM in Matlab R2008 against SeDuMi 1.1 on a 2.5 GHz Core 2 Duo laptop with 3 GB, SeDuMi's stopping rule adapted (marginally more conservative for AFOM), ε = 10⁻⁴, the gap measured to the planted γ* (pp. 12–14); AFOM reaches n = 30 for quartics and n = 16 for sextics, where the interior-point method fails beyond n = 15 and n = 9 for lack of memory (Tables 1–2, p. 15); the interior-point method is faster on small instances, AFOM from n ≥ 12 (quartics) and n ≥ 8 (sextics); interior-point gaps are slightly better on quartics, and AFOM varies more (pp. 14, 17); instances per size fall from 100 to 10–15 at large n (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithmic contribution plus an unexplained "curious observation": the ū_k sequence converges much faster than the ũ_k sequence the theory covers (pp. 17–18); the paper concedes that augmented-Lagrangian Newton-CG methods solve quartics with n = 100 to high precision, while claiming for AFOM a global rate without regularity conditions (p. 19); future: AFOM as a first phase, then a low-memory second-order method (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the algorithm, not the model, is redesigned: SDP structure is exploited inside a first-order method (pp. 8–12), a co-authored counter-example to the skill's remark that his record treats solvers as black boxes.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the reopening rests on a tool change (accelerated first-order methods) and is shown at about twice the interior-point scale (p. 2), without a quantified speed-up figure.
- Heuristic 4 ✅ evidence: negative results stay in the body: the interior-point method faster on small instances and more accurate on quartics, AFOM's variability, an unexplained phenomenon, a competitor at n = 100 (pp. 14, 17–19).
- new-pattern candidate: *choose the reformulation by measured convergence, not worst-case complexity* (p. 6), the practical-tractability stance also stated in S065 (p. 4) and S178 (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before running a first-order method on an SDP, check whether the constraint matrices have disjoint supports; if so, AA* is diagonal and the affine projection is closed-form (pp. 8–12).
- Plant the optimum: build test instances as sums of squares around a random point, so the exact optimal value, and hence the true gap, is known (pp. 12–13).
- Against an interior-point code, reuse its stopping rule and state in which direction your adaptation is more conservative (pp. 13–14).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+AP+LO}: Black–Scholes has no closed form for American and barrier options, simulation scales badly with dimension, geometric Brownian motion is empirically violated, and frictions rule out exact replication (p. 1); a fitted stochastic process is a model of reality, not reality (p. 1), and ε-arbitrage by dynamic programming is intractable in high dimension (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: model returns by polyhedral sets that impose CLT behaviour deterministically (eqs. 1–7, pp. 3–4) and choose the self-financing portfolio with the smallest worst-case ℓ1 replication error, Bertsimas–Kogan–Lo's ε-arbitrage with ℓ1 in place of ℓ2 (pp. 2, 4); the price is the portfolio's initial value (p. 4). - **D4 Formulation and analysis**: split the uncertainty set at the payoff's kink and dualize each piece (Bertsimas–Sim) to get an LP with 16T + 4 variables and 4T + 4 constraints for a European call (pp. 4–5); sizes from O(T) to O(M·T²) across option types (Table 1, p. 2); robust put–call parity within the two replication errors (Theorem 2.2, p. 4); buy-and-hold replication is optimal without position limits (Proposition 6.1), and deep-in-the-money prices are closed-form for Γ in an interval (Propositions 6.2–6.3) (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: MSFT 18-week European calls (price errors at most 0.018), MSFT 25-week American puts (errors up to 0.225; a cubic Γ(K) cuts replication errors by about half) and 8-week 1/100 DJIA index calls (Tables 2–5, pp. 9–11); average out-of-sample errors of 11.2–15.3% for Asian, lookback and Asian-index options (Table 6, p. 11); 50–500 assets priced within three minutes (Tables 7–9, p. 11); calibration on random strike subsets, test on the rest (p. 10); no competing pricing method run, and no solver, machine or data dates given (searched).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: tractability (polynomial scaling) and flexibility (risk aversion, frictions, option portfolios) (pp. 2, 12); limits: the largest errors for American puts, from the extra worst-case exercise layer (p. 12); Proposition 6.1 lost under position limits (p. 7); no future work stated (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: pricing, owned by stochastic calculus and dynamic programming, is written as LPs whose size grows polynomially, and the error norm and the correlation norm are chosen to keep them linear (pp. 2, 4–5, 7).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: Γ is the knob, but it is read off market prices as an implied risk aversion per strike (p. 8), a data-calibrated knob, with no protection-versus-cost curve.
- new-pattern candidate: *limit laws as primitives* (p. 3; with S065 pp. 5–10).
- new-pattern candidate: *implied knob*: invert the model to the robustness parameter that reproduces each observed price, as with implied volatility, and fit a curve across instruments (pp. 8, 10).

**Transferable techniques**:

- Handle a piecewise-linear payoff inside a robust constraint by splitting the uncertainty set at the kink and dualizing each piece (pp. 4–5).
- Calibrate a robustness parameter like implied volatility: invert it per instrument, fit a low-order curve in a market variable, and test it on held-out instruments (pp. 8, 10–11).
