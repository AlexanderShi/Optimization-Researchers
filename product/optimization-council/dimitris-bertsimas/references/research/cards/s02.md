### S220 · Transient and busy period analysis of the GI/G/1 queue: The method of stages (Queueing Systems 1992, DOI 10.1007/bf01159205)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–32, all sections. Many displayed formulas are OCR-garbled (e.g. Theorem 2's formula, p. 14), so formulas are described, not reproduced.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Daisuke Nakazato (MIT ORC, p. 1; his PhD student, 1990, per 04-mentorship.md) · **Position in trajectory**: Received June 1990 (p. 1). Queueing strand of 1985–1995 {SD}. The MGE stage device reappears in S336 (S336 p. 6).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD}: Transient and busy-period behaviour "have long been considered as very difficult problems" (p. 1). Yet they matter when a system opens, closes or gets a new control, or converges too slowly for steady state to be informative: manufacturing start-ups, and airport runways with time-varying demand (pp. 1–2). The gap: exact results existed only for M/M/1 (Bessel functions; Abate–Whitt), so the field had turned to numerics, namely Grassmann's randomization and integration of the Kolmogorov equations (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace general distributions by mixed generalized Erlang (MGE) stages, a class dense in all distributions (p. 2). MGE_L/MGE_M/1 then becomes a Markov chain on (number in system, arrival stage, service stage) (p. 3).
  - The homogeneous part is solved by separation of variables with tensor algebra, and the boundary-plus-initial ("compensation") part by linear algebra. The transform is a sum of M geometric terms, and its M coefficients come from an M×M system solved in closed form (pp. 5–6, 11–13).
  - The busy period follows from a new "queue build up time" matrix whose eigenvalues are given by the same M roots (pp. 13–16).
  - The transforms are then inverted numerically (§6).
- **D3 Model and data**: MGE stages have nonnegative branching probabilities and real rates, so they carry a probabilistic meaning (p. 3). FCFS and ρ < 1.
  - The system starts empty. The approach works in principle for any initial state, but "the algebra required makes the explicit derivation very hard" (p. 5).
  - Distinct roots are assumed, as a convenience, not a necessity (p. 9).
  - For the waiting time, Assumption 1 starts the arrival process in its stationary phase. It is needed only for closed forms, not for a numerical solution (pp. 18, 21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1, the queue-length transform (p. 6), rests on four steps: eigenvector matching (p. 7); a characteristic-polynomial claim using det(I + B) = 1 + trace(B) for rank-one B (p. 8); Rouché's theorem for the M roots (p. 9); and a Vandermonde system solved by Cramer's rule (pp. 11–12).
  - Checks: the generating function at z = 1 equals 1/s, and the result is symmetric in the roots (p. 13).
  - Theorem 2, the busy period (p. 14), uses a rank-one inverse update (p. 15) and a spectral decomposition (p. 16).
  - Theorem 3 gives the waiting time (p. 21); its s → 0 limit recovers the steady-state Lindley solution (p. 24).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Mathematica on a Macintosh II, with built-in root finding (pp. 24–25). Inversion uses Platzman–Ammons–Bartholdi (FFT, support truncated at E[Bp] + 3σ) and Hosono's method; the paper notes that inverting a probability transform numerically is #P-complete (p. 24). The only validation is against exact M/M/1 values, for ρ from 0.1 to 0.99. In Table 1 (ρ = 0.75) Hosono matches the exact values while PAB deviates (pp. 25–26). An MGE3/MGE2/1 example is illustrated; the largest run was MGE20/MGE20/1 (pp. 25–31). No timings are reported.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An analytic result (closed-form transforms) plus a numerical procedure. Stated limits: empty initial state, distinct roots, Assumption 1, and benchmarks only for M/M/1 (pp. 5, 9, 18, 25). Named next step: a sequel that poses GI/G/1 as a Hilbert problem (p. 2). The introduction promises concluding remarks (p. 3), but the file ends with §6 and the references (pp. 24–32).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 2–3, 5–6). The representation (MGE stages) is chosen so that a known machinery, Markov chains plus transforms, applies exactly, and the paper says where explicit tractability ends: the initial condition (p. 5). But the class kept is analytic, not LP, conic or MIO, and no optimization appears. This is the probability side of Tension T3.
- Heuristic 4: weak evidence (p. 25). The lack of benchmarks beyond M/M/1 is stated in the body.
- New-pattern candidate: **dense tractable class as the universality device**. MGE stages are dense in all distributions, so exact analysis for MGE is presented as general (p. 2; also S336 p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To analyse a queue with general distributions exactly, approximate them by a dense phase class whose stages have a probabilistic meaning (MGE), exploit the Markov structure, and state the price: the number of stages (pp. 2–3).
- Split the Kolmogorov equations into a homogeneous part, solved by a geometric (separation-of-variables) ansatz, and a boundary/initial "compensation" part, solved as a small linear system (pp. 5–6).
- Check closed forms by a normalization identity (generating function at z = 1 equals 1/s) and by recovering the known steady-state limit (pp. 13, 24).
- Validate a numerical inversion on the one exactly solvable case across the whole load range before illustrating new cases (pp. 25–26).

**Quotes**:
- "Transient and busy period analysis in queueing models have long been considered as very difficult problems." (p. 1)
- "Unfortunately, we did not have any other numerical results to compare with except the ones for the M/M/1 queue" (p. 25)

### S126 · On central limit theorems in geometrical probability (The Annals of Applied Probability 1993, DOI 10.1214/aoap/1177005271)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–14. Some display formulas are garbled, including the exact rate exponents (pp. 3, 11–12).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Florin Avram (Northeastern University, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Received July 1991 (p. 1). Probabilistic analysis of combinatorial problems {SD}, companion to their MST-constant paper (ref. [2], p. 13). The editor, J. M. Steele, is thanked (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD; the objects are combinatorial optimization graphs}: Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley and Steele proved laws of large numbers for TSP, matching, MST and related problems on random points. Central limit theorems were believed but unproved, because the dependence among edges "seems to be local" (p. 1). The entry is to make this folklore precise for three graphs of computational geometry: the kth-nearest-neighbour graph, the Voronoi diagram and the Delaunay triangulation (pp. 1–2). The Delaunay triangulation contains the MST (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Find an event A_n with P(A_n) → 1 under which the configuration is m-dependent across a subdivision into O(n/log n) subcubes, each holding between 1 and O(log n) points. Then apply Baldi–Rinott's dependency-graph CLT, which also gives rates (pp. 4–5). This replaces Bickel–Breiman's long fourth-moment proof by a simple conditioning argument (pp. 2–3). The approach was inspired by Ramey's unsuccessful thesis attempt at the MST (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**: Poisson process of intensity n on [0,1]² (p. 3), with points in general position with probability 1 (p. 2). The subdivision uses m = ⌊(n/(c log n))^{1/2}⌋ with c > 5/4 (p. 5). The usual model of n uniform i.i.d. points is not covered (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Lemma 2 shows P(A_n) → 1 (p. 6).
  - Proposition 3, "the heart" of the development, gives a fixed dependence radius by geometric arguments: R = 4 for the nearest-neighbour graph, R = 6 for Voronoi and Delaunay (pp. 6–7).
  - Proposition 4 bounds the length within a subcube (pp. 7–8). Proposition 5 lower-bounds Var[L_n] by planting a positive-probability local configuration and conditioning (pp. 8–10).
  - Theorem 6 gives the CLT with a polylogarithmic factor over n^{1/4} (exponents garbled), removing the conditioning via P(A_n) → 1 (pp. 10–11).
  - Remarks: λ = O(log n) is forced by the method, and the log factors are conjectured to be artefacts (p. 11). The same device gives rates for Groeneboom's and Hsing's convex-hull CLTs (pp. 11–12), and only a lower bound on the variance is needed (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a (no computation).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory (probability). The boundary is printed: for the MST the event is known but its probability cannot be shown to tend to 1, and for TSP and matching no such event has been found (pp. 4–5). Future work: d dimensions, to which kNN and Delaunay generalize, and the uniform Euclidean model (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 4–5, 13). Only step 5 is present: the paper prints exactly where its device stops (MST, TSP, matching; Poisson model only). There is no formulation in a solver class. This is the probability side of Tension T3.
- New-pattern candidate: **condition on a high-probability event that makes dependence local, then apply a dependency-graph CLT with rates** (pp. 4–6, 10–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove a CLT for a sum whose dependence is "local", find a high-probability event under which dependence has a fixed finite range. Condition on it, apply Baldi–Rinott, and remove the conditioning with P(A_n) → 1; the rate then picks up the failure probability (pp. 4–6, 10–11).
- Lower-bound a variance by planting many disjoint positive-probability local configurations and using Var ≥ E[Var | F] (pp. 8–10).
- Choose the subdivision scale just large enough for the good event (λ ≈ c log n), and say which part of the rate comes from that choice (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "we succeeded in proving CLTs for the length of three graphs" (p. 1)
- "In contrast, for the MST we know the event, but we cannot show its probability tends to 1." (p. 5)

### S659 · Decomposition algorithms for analyzing transient phenomena in multi-class queuing networks in air transportation (Sloan School working paper 1993; version read: Operations Research 43(6) 1995, DOI 10.1287/opre.43.6.995)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17. Version read: OR, the Operations Research 1995 print, not the 1993 Sloan working paper. The print gives the same title and authors and says the paper was received January 1993 (p. 1). Formulas and the Appendix pseudocode are partly garbled.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael D. Peterson (McKinsey, p. 1; PhD 1992, co-advised with Odoni per 04-mentorship.md) and Amedeo R. Odoni (his own PhD adviser) · **Position in trajectory**: Network sequel to the single-airport model (S135; their 1992 working paper, p. 1) {SD AP}. Accepted November 1994 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + AP (air transportation)}: Hub-and-spoke "banks" compress arrivals and propagate delay through the network (p. 1). The single-airport study "ignored the potentially important phenomenon of interaction between airports" (p. 1), and transient network analysis existed only as diffusion approximations or special cases (p. 1). The intended use is a strategic planning tool for hub connectivity and schedule slack. It is set against American Airlines' simulation, which is built on historical flight-time distributions (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Decomposition. The exact single-airport recursion is run at each node, period by period, and the resulting delays are fed forward into downstream arrival schedules (p. 4).
  - Algorithm 1 updates each aircraft's delay deterministically: the expected wait, minus slack (eq. (19), p. 4).
  - Algorithm 2 passes two moments. It fits a mixed (atom plus exponential) waiting-time density, gives each aircraft probabilities of c extra periods of delay, and treats arrival counts as sums of Bernoullis, assumed independent and normal (pp. 5–9).
  - The paper calls itself "similar in spirit (though far different in content)" to Whitt's QNA (p. 1).
- **D3 Model and data**: Landing runways act as one server. Demand is deterministic and time-varying over Δt periods, and capacity follows a semi-Markov process, Markov in the computations (pp. 2–3). Each itinerary leg carries a slack (p. 4). Approximations admitted for tractability: update by expected values (p. 6), independent contributions (p. 8), normality despite skewness early in the day (p. 9), and a network collapsed to its hubs (400+ nodes to 5–6, p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Complexity: moment recursions cost O(S²K²Qmax) per airport (p. 3); Algorithm 1 costs O(NS²K³Qmax) and Algorithm 2 O(RNS²K³Qmax) (pp. 5, 9).
  - Speed-ups: restarting only every m periods cuts iterations from 3,240 to 360 at K = 80, m = 10 (p. 5), and a linked-list structure re-hangs aircraft records in O(V) (pp. 4–5).
  - Two-moment fit, eqs. (24)–(25), falls back to a pure exponential when the squared coefficient of variation is below 1 (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: A two-hub network with the test cases of Table I; case 1 is built to resemble DFW in March 1989 (pp. 9–10).
  - Benchmark: a reduced-form terminating simulation (10,000 replications, about 1% relative error) that propagates realized rather than expected delays, implemented in C on an Ultrix DECsystem 5900 (p. 11).
  - Results: agreement in case 1; in case 2, a 0–30% discrepancy (deviation measures 6.11 and 5.69 for Algorithms 1 and 2), and similar in case 3 (pp. 11–12).
  - Run times: about 10 and 30 minutes for the two algorithms against 5 minutes for the simulation (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Modelling and algorithms plus policy insight. Hub isolation protects the other hubs but worsens the isolated one, and slack involves trade-offs (pp. 13–15). Limits are printed in the abstract and the body: delay propagation is underestimated in heavy traffic, and the results are "partly discouraging" (pp. 1, 13). Future work: capture tail delays without enumerating sample paths (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 9–15). An application-led model with DFW-like data and policy what-ifs (connectivity, slack). But the benchmark is the authors' own simulation, not the owner's current rule, and there is no field test.
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 13–15). Two operational knobs, connectivity p and slack, with their trade-offs read off: "The carrier trades off the benefit of limiting the scope of propagation against the cost of a higher scale of delay" (p. 15). No bound and no price curve.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (p. 1). A student paper with the co-adviser (Peterson with Odoni), part of a thesis turned into two journal papers (S135, S659).
- Heuristic 4: evidence (pp. 1, 13). The negative accuracy result is in the abstract.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Decompose a transient network: run the exact single-node recursion node by node and period by period, passing delay information downstream. Benchmark against a simulation that differs only in the approximation under test (realized vs expected updates), so that the gap isolates that approximation (pp. 4, 11–13).
- When only two moments are available, fit a mixed atom-plus-exponential distribution suggested by simulated histograms, with a fallback when the moment condition fails (pp. 6–7).
- When a recursion restarts every period, restart it only every m periods, where m is the minimum time between an aircraft's stops, and report the iterations saved (p. 5).
- Collapse a large network to the nodes whose delays matter (the hubs) and treat the others as fixed (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "Thus, the results are at least partly discouraging for the approximation procedures." (p. 13)
- "Case 1 suggests that for networks of airports like DFW, waiting times are probably not high enough to create significant network effects on a frequent basis" (p. 13)

### S135 · Models and algorithms for transient queueing congestion at airports (Management Science 1995, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.41.8.1279)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17. OCR noise in the running heads and some equations.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael D. Peterson (Indiana University, p. 1; PhD 1992, co-advised with Odoni per 04-mentorship.md) and Amedeo R. Odoni · **Position in trajectory**: Received October 1992; the closing line, OCR-garbled, reports 7 months with the authors for 2 revisions (p. 17). The single-airport model behind S659 {SD AP}.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + AP (air transportation)}: Delays at hub airports. The paper opens with the share of Americans dissatisfied with deregulation (17% to 36%), ground delays of 2,000 hours a day in 1986, 21 airports above 20,000 hours in 1990, and banks of up to 40 flights within 30 minutes (p. 1).
  - The gap: steady-state analysis misleads under time-varying demand (Odoni–Roth; Green–Kolesar), phase-type Kolmogorov approaches have huge state spaces, and diffusion approximations do not fit airports (p. 2).
  - Three features force a new model: time-varying arrivals, weather-dependent capacity and interdependent service times (pp. 2–3).
  - Stated aim: a planning tool that assesses the expected delays of a proposed daily schedule (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Treat demand as deterministic and time-varying over 15-minute intervals, and capacity as a Markov or semi-Markov weather process. Each interval is then a deterministic fluid step, and moments follow from a backward recursion over (period, capacity state, age, queue length) (pp. 3–5). The semi-Markov process is recast as a Markov one on an age-augmented state (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions: Assumption 1 (known arrivals per interval) and Assumption 2 (S capacity states with arbitrary discrete holding times) (pp. 3–4). Service is deterministic within an interval, aircraft-type separations are not modelled, and the model is "strategic" rather than operational (p. 4).
  - Data: the DFW schedule for March 1989 (Figure 1, p. 7) and six capacity states from engineered performance standards (EPS), with the top state corrected from 95 to about 115 per hour using UNISYS observations (pp. 7–8). Historical capacity data did not exist, so the capacity history was rebuilt from 8 years of NOAA hourly weather (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Theorems 1–3 and Corollary 4 give recursions for the first and second moments of queue length and waiting time. The waiting time at the boundary is split into the part inside the next interval and the part after it (pp. 4–5). Theorem 5: memory O(SKMQmax) and time O(S²K²MQmax), without the factor M in the Markov case (p. 6). Full distributions would cost a factor Qmax ≈ 200 more (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Markov vs semi-Markov: χ² tests reject geometric holding times, yet the occupancy probabilities match (Table 2). The Markov and semi-Markov waiting times coincide, at 5 minutes against about 2 hours on a DEC-3100 (pp. 8–10).
  - Arrival assumption: deterministic arrivals are compared with time-varying Poisson arrivals in 10,000 simulated days (pp. 10–11).
  - Field validation: against DOT on-time statistics, with outliers trimmed. The predicted queueing delay is about half of the total lateness (250 vs 540 minutes) and the peak timing matches; the authors judge the comparison of limited use (pp. 11–12).
  - Policy runs: the starting-state effect (a factor of about 3), Markov vs i.i.d. capacity, interference when Delta's banks follow American's (Table 3; "mixed" evidence), and demand smoothing (pp. 12–16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Model, algorithm and policy insight. With a cap L on arrivals per 15 minutes, moderate smoothing (L = 28) cuts the average delay from 6.05 to 3.29 minutes by moving 7.23% of flights, and severe smoothing (L = 20) cuts it to 2.43 minutes by moving 17.37% (Table 4, p. 16). More than two-thirds of the gain comes from the moderate step (p. 16). Limits printed: the validation data are unfit for the purpose (pp. 11–12), service dependence is not modelled (p. 4), and upstream delays are deferred to the network follow-up (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 1–2, 7–8, 11–16). Real data from the airport authority, DOT, OAG and NOAA; the status-quo schedule is the baseline; policies are scored in decision units (minutes of delay, share of flights displaced); and the evaluation's limits are stated. There is no field test (step 6 is missing).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 15–16). One knob (the cap L), a priced curve (delay vs flights displaced) with a knee ("diminishing returns"), handed to policy makers. But the curve is computed by the model, with no solution-independent bound.
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 4, 10). The model class is chosen by measured run time: semi-Markov is dropped for Markov after the outputs prove insensitive, in the spirit of Method 1's stop rule on measured solve times. The class here is a recursion, not LP or MIO.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (p. 1). A student paper with the co-adviser (Peterson with Odoni).
- Heuristic 4: evidence (pp. 10, 12). The rejected χ² tests and the weak DOT validation are printed in the body.
- New-pattern candidate: **choose the model class by measured run time after showing the decision outputs are insensitive** (pp. 8–10). S659 keeps the Markov formulation for the same reason (S659 p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When one model feature is expensive (semi-Markov holding times), test its fit (χ²), then test whether the decision outputs change. If they do not, adopt the cheaper model and report both run times (pp. 8–10).
- Before validating against imperfect field data, say what the data measure and what they do not. Trim outliers by a stated rule, report the aggregate gap (250 vs 540 minutes), and say what matches (peak timing) (pp. 11–12).
- Price a policy knob with a two-column table, benefit (average delay) against cost (share of flights moved), at two settings, and read off the knee (Table 4, p. 16).
- When the uncertainty process is persistent, report results conditional on the starting state: averaging hides a factor of about 3 (pp. 12–13).

**Quotes**:
- "On balance, Figure 6 is of limited usefulness." (p. 12)
- "smoothing policies exhibit diminishing returns and increasing costs." (p. 16)

### S237 · Branching bandits and Klimov's problem: Achievable region and side constraints (IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control 1995, DOI 10.1109/9.478231)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13. Formulas partly garbled; p. 13 holds the author biographies.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis (student, co-advised with Tsitsiklis per 04-mentorship.md) and John N. Tsitsiklis · **Position in trajectory**: Received April 1994 (p. 1). Part of the achievable-region programme (SW1) {SD LO IO}.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + LO (+IO: extended polymatroids)}: Klimov's problem (a multiclass M/GI/1 queue with Bernoulli feedback) and Weiss's branching bandits, with linear side constraints such as fairness (p. 1). The gap is twofold (p. 2). Polyhedral characterizations had exponentially many constraints, so side-constrained problems led to exponential algorithms. And the side-constraint results of Nain–Ross and Makowski–Shwartz gave structure but no usable algorithm for several constraints.
- **D2 Core idea**: A "philosophically very different" route from the bandit-index view. Characterize the achievable region of mean-queue-length vectors as a polyhedron, so that control becomes an LP (p. 1). Then give a "parsimonious" lifted description, with O(R²) variables and constraints, whose linear projection is exactly that region (pp. 2, 7–8). Optimal side-constrained policies randomize among at most L + 1 priority rules, found in polynomial time (pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Model and data**: Nonpreemptive service; arbitrary service and offspring distributions, independent across services (pp. 2–3). Assumption A: the spectral radius of E[N] is below 1, and the tails are of exponential type. The tail condition is "much stronger than needed" and is relaxed at the end by moment-matched approximations and continuity (pp. 3, 11–12). M/GI/1 is the most general setting for Klimov's problem: a G/GI/1 counterexample breaks the conservation laws (p. 3).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Squaring the one-step evolution equation and taking stationary expectations gives the 2^R − 1 conservation inequalities (Theorem 3.2, p. 5). With auxiliary variables I_ji = E[x_j N_i] it gives the O(R²) lifted equalities (Theorem 4.1, pp. 7–8).
  - Extreme points are exactly the priority policies, by an adjacency argument plus perturbation (pp. 5–6). Points on infinite edges correspond to almost-priority policies (p. 7). The region equals the projection of the lift (Theorems 4.2, 5.4; pp. 8–10).
  - The convex-combination algorithm finds an exit point by LP and a facet by LP sensitivity analysis, then recurses over R stages (pp. 10–11).
  - Appendix: the lift has extreme points that no policy attains (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory and algorithm. Open problems: networks with general distributions, and whether every extended polymatroid is the projection of a polynomial-size polyhedron (pp. 11–12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 1–3, 7–12). The owning tradition and its tool are named (bandit indices). The variables are performance vectors. The class is kept (LP, also with side constraints) and made polynomial by a lifted formulation. The boundary is printed: the G/GI/1 counterexample, networks left open, and extreme points of the lift that no policy attains.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (p. 1). A co-advised student paper with Tsitsiklis in the achievable-region lineage.
- New-pattern candidate: **lift-and-project to a polynomial-size extended formulation** of an exponentially described region (pp. 7–8, 11–12).
- New-pattern candidate: **unify-then-extend** (pp. 1–3, 10–11). Klimov's problem becomes a special case of branching bandits, the exponential characterizations of Tsoucas and of Bertsimas–Niño-Mora are recovered, and the potential-function method is extended to general distributions.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To get linear constraints on a stochastic system's performance vector, square the one-step evolution equation and take stationary expectations. Each subset-priority rule makes one inequality tight (p. 5).
- Lift with auxiliary second-moment variables (I_ji = E[x_j N_i]) to replace exponentially many constraints by O(R²) of them, then check whether the lift's extreme points are achievable (pp. 7–8, 12).
- To realize an LP optimum as a policy, decompose it into at most L + 1 extreme points (Carathéodory plus a basic optimal solution). Read the extreme points as priority rules and randomize among them per busy period (pp. 6, 10).
- Relax a technical tail assumption by approximating with exponential-type variables of the same means and variances, when the constraints depend only on those moments (pp. 11–12).

**Quotes**:
- "We are thus able to convert a difficult stochastic control problem to one of optimizing a linear cost function over the achievable region, and this is a linear programming problem." (p. 1)
- "as it turns out, this is much more complicated than it might have appeared at first sight." (p. 2)

### S181 · Stability conditions for multiclass fluid queueing networks (IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control 1996, DOI 10.1109/9.543999)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–12 and 14 read. File page 13 (printed page 1630) has no text, so §V's sufficient-condition theorem for J stations, any concluding section and references [1]–[9] were not read. The LP constraint displays are garbled throughout.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: David Gamarnik (student, co-advised with Tsitsiklis per 04-mentorship.md) and John N. Tsitsiklis · **Position in trajectory**: Received February 1995 (p. 1). SD stability strand. 04-mentorship.md records an authors' correction in IEEE TAC 1997; it was not read.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + LO}: Multiclass networks with feedback can be unstable under priority and even FIFO policies although every station's load is below one (p. 1). The exact stability region was open. It was also unknown whether convex Lyapunov functions, quadratic or piecewise linear, can certify that region exactly (p. 1). The object is the fluid model, justified by Dai's fluid-limit theorem (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Instead of searching for Lyapunov functions, decompose every feasible trajectory of a two-station fluid network into four phase types (which stations are busy, which empty), and sum time and work per phase type into LP variables (pp. 2–4). LP[Q(0)] bounds the strong busy period; minimizing instead gives a lower bound on the emptying time (pp. 3–5). Stability holds if and only if LP[0] has only the zero solution (Theorems 1–3, pp. 5–7).
- **D3 Model and data**: Fluid model (α, μ, P, C) with substochastic routing of spectral radius below 1, and work-conserving policies (p. 2). Stability means emptying in finite time (Chen; Rybko–Stolyar), and the load condition ρ < e is assumed, being necessary (p. 2).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Sufficiency: if LP[0] admits only zero, LP[Q(0)] is bounded, and an unstable policy gives a contradiction (p. 5).
  - Necessity: any nonzero solution of LP[0] is turned into a work-conserving periodic trajectory with Q(τ) = Q(0) (pp. 6–7).
  - Theorem 4: two-station networks with one class at station 2 are stable under the load condition, generalizing a three-class example of Kumar and Down–Meyn (p. 7).
  - §IV: the dual of Down–Meyn's Lyapunov LP is a relaxation of LP[0], and through Lemmas 5–7 the two are equivalent. Piecewise linear convex Lyapunov functions are therefore exact for two stations; a reduced LP has half as many variables (pp. 8–12).
  - §V starts a J-station decomposition by "emptying times" (p. 12); its statement is on the missing p. 13.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory, exact for two stations. For J > 2 the new sufficient conditions are conjectured necessary, "although we were unable to establish necessity" (p. 1). That the fluid result holds for stochastic networks is only "suspected" (p. 1). The author biography (p. 14) lists INFORMS's Erlang Prize 1996 and SIAM's Best Publication Award in Optimization 1996, without naming the works.

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 1–12). The owning tradition (Lyapunov stability analysis) and its tool are named. The variables range over a describable set (time and work per trajectory phase), and stability becomes an LP test. Dualization reads the rival certificate, the Lyapunov LP, as the dual relaxation (step 4). The boundary is printed: J > 2 is sufficient only.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (p. 1). A co-advised student paper with Tsitsiklis.
- New-pattern candidate: **trajectory decomposition into phase-type LP variables, with a periodic-trajectory construction for necessity** (pp. 2–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn stability under every policy into an LP. Classify states by which resources are busy, sum time and work per phase type over a whole trajectory, and write conservation per phase; an LP whose only feasible solution is zero then certifies stability (pp. 2–5).
- Prove necessity by converting a nonzero LP solution into an explicit adversarial, periodic policy (pp. 6–7).
- When a rival method certifies by an LP (here the Lyapunov LP), take its dual and compare it with your own LP to settle exactness (p. 8).
- Use the homogeneous LP (zero right-hand side) for qualitative questions and the inhomogeneous one for quantitative bounds, here upper and lower bounds on the busy period (pp. 3–5).

**Quotes**:
- "Using linear programming duality, we show that for two-station systems the Lyapunov function approach is equivalent to ours and therefore characterizes stability exactly." (p. 1)
- "although we were unable to establish necessity." (p. 1)

### S336 · A unified method to analyze overtake free queueing systems (Advances in Applied Probability 1996, DOI 10.2307/1428073)
Read level: **partial**. Read: pp. 1–20 (§§1–4, and §5's set-up and Theorem 8), pp. 24–25 (§6's set-up) and pp. 33–34 (end of §6, and the §7 conclusions). Skimmed for theorem headings only: pp. 21–23 (vacation applications) and pp. 26–33 (priority-queue derivations). Not read: pp. 34–38 (appendices, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Georgia Mourtzinou (MIT ORC, p. 1; his PhD student, 1995, per 04-mentorship.md) · **Position in trajectory**: Received November 1992, revised December 1995 (p. 1). Queueing strand {SD}. Extends the MGE vector law of Bertsimas–Nakazato (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD}: The paper asks for the laws of queueing theory, by analogy with Maxwell's equations. Queueing theory solves applications case by case, without a set of laws that leads to complete solutions (pp. 1–2). Little's law lacks second-moment information, so H = λG is the candidate (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Derive the Haji–Newell distributional laws (L =d N*(S)) from H = λG (pp. 4–5) and apply them to both the queue and the system in overtake-free systems. Then solve a 4×4 system of transform relations (L–S, Q–W, S = W + X, and L–Q) for all four distributions (pp. 2–3, 12, 15). There are two versions: an asymptotic method for arbitrary renewal arrivals in heavy traffic, and an exact method for MGE arrivals.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions A: customers arrive and leave one at a time, with no blocking, balking or reneging; they leave in FIFO order; later arrivals do not affect earlier customers' times (p. 4).
  - Overtake-free means that A holds for both the queue and the queue plus server (p. 8).
  - Heavy-traffic results need L, Q, S, W → ∞, which excludes D/D/1 (pp. 9, 11). Exact results need MGE arrivals, though PH works too (p. 15).
  - The GI/G/∞ result treats service-time classes as independent, which is an approximation (p. 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Asymptotics: Theorem 3 derives the kernel asymptotics by Smith's method, expanding in s, splitting into partial fractions and expanding around z = 1 (pp. 9–10). Theorem 4 gives the asymptotic laws (p. 10).
  - Asymptotic PASTA: E[L−] and E[L] differ, asymptotically, only through the arrival process's coefficient of variation (p. 11).
  - Theorem 5: GI/G/1 heavy-traffic transforms. They are exact for Poisson arrivals (the P-K formula), and W is asymptotically exponential (pp. 12–13).
  - Theorem 6: GI/G/∞ via mixtures of deterministic services (pp. 13–14).
  - Theorem 7: MGE/G/1 queue and waiting time, without Hilbert factorization (pp. 16–18).
  - Theorem 8: generalized vacations (pp. 19–20). Priority queues are handled via effective service times (pp. 24–25).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A methodological unification. The boundary is printed: overtake-free systems are "a small subset" of those met in applications, and laws for systems with overtaking (GI/G/s, networks) are the open problem (pp. 33–34).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 1–3, 12, 15, 33–34). The formulation-first instinct appears in probabilistic form: find the right set of laws, so that one method solves a whole class, and say where the class ends. There is no optimization; this is the probability side of Tension T3.
- New-pattern candidate: **unify-then-extend** (pp. 1–3, 18–19). The P-K formula, PASTA, the Fuhrmann–Cooper decomposition and the Keilson–Servi results are recovered as special cases, then generalized.
- New-pattern candidate: **dense tractable class as the universality device** (p. 6). MGE is described as "the simplest class of distributions that is dense in the space of all distributions".

**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive a distributional law from H = λG by choosing an indicator process h(t) and per-customer quantities g_j so that both sides become probabilities of the event of interest (pp. 4–5).
- Write a "method" as a fixed sequence of transform relations: the law linking customers and time for the system and for the queue, S = W + X, and a system-specific L–Q link. Solve the resulting 4×4 system, and reuse it across models (pp. 12, 15).
- For heavy traffic, expand the renewal kernel around s → 0 and z → 1 and keep the leading exponential. Check that the answer becomes exact in the Poisson case (pp. 9–13).

**Quotes**:
- "What are the laws of queueing theory?" (p. 2)
- "The major open problem is to identify queueing laws for systems that allow overtaking, which lead to a complete solution." (p. 34)

### S435 · Improved randomized approximation algorithms for lot-sizing problems (IPCO 1996, DOI 10.1007/3-540-61310-2_27)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–15. The file has no abstract and no concluding section: it opens with §1 and ends with §5 and the references. Several formulas are garbled; the OCR renders √2 as "v~".
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chung-Piaw Teo (MIT Sloan and ORC, p. 1; his PhD student, 1996, per 04-mentorship.md) · **Position in trajectory**: IPCO 1996. LP and convex-relaxation approximation strand {IO}. A journal follow-up is Teo & Bertsimas 2001 in Operations Research (per 04-mentorship.md; not read).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO, with an operations-management application}: Multi-product lot sizing over an acyclic product network, restricted to stationary, nested, power-of-two policies (pp. 1–2). The incumbent is Roundy's (and Maxwell–Muckstadt's) deterministic rounding of a convex relaxation: 98% effective with a variable base period and 94% with a fixed one, tight, and reused across extensions (pp. 2–3). The entry: re-derive and improve these bounds by randomized rounding (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Write each relaxed value as T_i = 2^{p_i} z_i T_L and round it up or down by comparing z_i with one shared random Y. Y's distribution F is designed so that the expected ratios of the setup term (K/T) and the holding term (HT) are equal, and the shared Y preserves the nesting order (pp. 4–5).
  - Derandomize by trying the at most n + 1 breakpoints (O(n log n)); in the 98% case this reproduces Roundy's algorithm (pp. 5–6).
  - Unify the 94% and 98% bounds by allowing p base periods (Algorithm C); p = 2 already gives 97% (pp. 6–7).
  - Improve the fixed-base bound with a stronger relaxation, piecewise-linear over {T_L, 2T_L, 3T_L}, and a mixture of the 2^p and 3·2^p grids. Algorithm D gives 1.043, and Algorithm E gives 1.031 when every T_i* ≥ √2·T_L (pp. 7–10).
- **D3 Model and data**: Constant demand, infinite production rate, echelon holding costs and ordering costs, under nested stationary policies (p. 1). The relaxation is convex and solvable in polynomial time: O(n log n) on trees and O(n) on stars (p. 2).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - The expected-cost bounds compute E[T^R]/T and T·E[1/T^R] under F. Theorem 1 gives 1.06 (3√2/4, attained at y = √2) and Theorem 2 gives about 1.02 (pp. 4–6). Theorem 3 bounds other cost terms (91.8%; p. 6).
  - Lemma 5: optimal relaxed values below 3T_L can be taken in {T_L, 2T_L, 3T_L} (p. 8). Theorem 6 (1.043) follows by case analysis over two ranges of w (pp. 9–10).
  - One-warehouse multi-retailer (OWMR) problem: six stronger relaxations (T_0 ≥ 6T_L, or T_0 = kT_L for k = 1, …, 5), each solvable in O(n), give an O(n) 96.9% algorithm (Theorem 11, pp. 11–13).
  - Extensions: capacitated versions, submodular ordering costs, and resource constraints. For the last, Roundy's 1.44 bound is generalized to submodular joint costs by scaling with √2 before Algorithm B (Theorem 12) (pp. 13–14).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a (no computation).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic guarantees. The stated reason for their reach is that the proof does not use the structure of the optimal solution, so extensions come "almost effortlessly" (pp. 3, 13). No limits or open questions are stated, since there is no concluding section.

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 1–2, 7, 11–12). Formulation first in the IO sense: a convex relaxation, strengthened exactly where rounding loses (a piecewise linearization over the discrete points; six case relaxations). But the deliverable is a rounding guarantee, not a model kept in a solver class. The paper is also evidence for the strong-formulation side of Tension T4 (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: **designed-distribution randomized rounding, derandomized by breakpoint enumeration, recovering the incumbent's deterministic rule** (pp. 3–6).
- New-pattern candidate: **unify-then-extend** (pp. 3, 6–7). Roundy's 98% algorithm is recovered as a derandomization, and the 94% and 98% bounds become endpoints of one family.

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a cost K/T + HT, choose the rounding distribution so that the two expected ratios, E[T^R]/T and T·E[1/T^R], are equal, then maximize over the fractional position. One shared random threshold preserves monotone constraints (T_i ≥ T_j) (pp. 4–5).
- Derandomize a threshold rounding by sorting the fractional parts: there are only n + 1 distinct outcomes, so try them all (p. 5).
- Strengthen the relaxation where the guarantee is lost. Linearize the objective over the few discrete values that small variables can take, and mix two rounding grids (2^p and 3·2^p) with tuned probabilities (pp. 7–9).
- Split the problem into a few cases (T_0 ≥ 6T_L, or T_0 = kT_L), solve a stronger relaxation for each, and take the best (pp. 11–13).

**Quotes**:
- "Our proof is simple and unlike the original deterministic rounding does not depend on the structure of the optimal solution." (p. 3)
- "Roundy's 98% algorithm can be obtained by derandomizing our algorithm." (p. 3)
