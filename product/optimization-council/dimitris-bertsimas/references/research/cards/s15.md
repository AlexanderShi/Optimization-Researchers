### S136 · A scalable algorithm for sparse portfolio selection (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2022, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2021.1127; arXiv 1811.00138)
Read level: **full** (arXiv v5). Parts read: pp. 1–40 and 44–47, namely the abstract, §§1–5 with the in-body proofs, the references, Appendix A (proofs of Corollaries 2 and 4), the Appendix B text with Tables 13–15, and Appendix C (in-out pseudocode). The instance-wise Tables 10–12 (pp. 41–43) were scanned, not read row by row. Figures 3–4 are captions only in the text layer (p. 34). First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Ryan Cory-Wright match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ryan Cory-Wright (MIT ORC, p. 1). 04-mentorship.md lists him as Bertsimas's PhD student (2022) and this paper as a thesis-topic paper, an inference made there. · **Position in trajectory**:
- The paper carries the ridge-regularized outer-approximation line from regression to constrained portfolios. It joins Bertsimas and Van Parys (2020; S061, card c21) in imposing a ridge (p. 7). For the general setting of its reformulation's tightness and of the perspective-cut link, it points to Bertsimas, Cory-Wright and Pauphilet (2019; S175, card c23) (pp. 6, 17).
- The cardinality motivation goes back to his 1999 asset-manager implementation (S101, card c05; p. 2).
- Dates: arXiv v5 is of 28 March 2021. The History line gives first submission in November 2020 and revision in March 2021 (p. 1), and the acknowledgments also thank referees of previous versions (p. 35).
- The index also lists S725, a 2018 item by Cory-Wright titled *A scalable algorithm for sparse and robust portfolios*, with no open text. Its relation to this paper was not checked.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO CP; AP (finance) as testbed; ML via the regression equivalence}: The problem is Markowitz mean-variance selection with a cardinality bound and general linear constraints. Minimum-investment constraints are handled by an extension of the reformulation (pp. 1–2, 15). It is NP-hard even without the linear constraints (Gao and Li 2013, p. 2).
  - The entry point is a scale gap. No certifiably optimal method had been shown to converge in practical time beyond 400 securities (abstract, p. 1), whereas real-world instances are defined as 500 ≤ n ≤ 3,200: an index at least the size of the S&P 500 (p. 2 and footnote 1). Table 1 lists each rival's largest solved instance as reported by its authors, 100 to 400 securities (p. 4).
  - The diagnosis has three parts. Big-M relaxations are weak (footnote 4, p. 6). Outer approximation (OA) had lost to branch-and-bound on this problem for lack of informative subgradients (p. 6). And every perspective approach assumes the problem must not be modified (p. 7).
  - Sparsity itself has two motivations: monitoring costs per position, and investors' suspicion of managers who track an index while charging active fees. The paper cites the 1999 implementation for both (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Change the model first, then the cut.
  - Add a ridge term (1/(2γ))‖x‖₂² (Problem (4), p. 3). Rewrite portfolio selection as constrained sparse regression, using a Cholesky factor of Σ and completing the square (pp. 8–9).
  - Replace x by Diag(z)x but leave the ridge term unscaled. Dualizing the inner QP then makes f(z) a maximum of functions linear in z, hence convex, and gives a subgradient from one QP (Theorem 1, Corollary 1, pp. 9–12).
  - Run OA inside one branch-and-bound tree through lazy-constraint callbacks (pp. 10, 13). Three additions:
    - a discrete first-order warm start taken from the best-subset paper (Algorithm 2, pp. 18–19);
    - the bound of the SOCP (perspective) relaxation in the stopping test (Theorem 2, Algorithm 3, pp. 20, 23);
    - in-out root cuts (p. 14).
  - Kept from earlier work: the Duran–Grossmann and Fletcher–Leyffer OA scheme, the perspective relaxation and the in-out method (pp. 3, 14). The inner dual re-derives Frangioni and Gentile's perspective cuts, which here run inside CPLEX or Gurobi without a tailored branch-and-bound (§3.5, p. 17).
- **D3 Model and data**: The model is long-only and fully invested, with ‖x‖₀ ≤ k, l ≤ Ax ≤ u, optional minimum investment, and Σ positive semidefinite of rank r (pp. 2, 8, 15).
  - Three choices are made for tractability:
    - the ridge term, which leaves the problem NP-hard (p. 3, footnote 2). It is read as robustification only by citation: Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage and DeMiguel et al.'s 1/N result (pp. 3–4);
    - Lipschitz continuity of f in z for the heuristic, which holds when the duals are bounded (pp. 12, 18);
    - diagonal-dominance extraction when Σ is diagonally dominant (p. 15).
  - Data:
    - OR-library port1–5, with n from 31 to 225 (p. 26);
    - the Frangioni–Gentile 200+, 300+ and 400+ sets, 10 instances each, run with diagonal matrices that Frangioni et al. made public (pp. 27–28);
    - daily closes of the S&P 500, Russell 1000 and Wilshire 5000 from 3 January 2007 to 29 December 2017 (Yahoo! Finance), rescaled to a one-month holding period, with low-rank SVD covariance estimates (p. 30).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Theorem 1 holds by strong duality at each fixed z. The inner problem is a convex QP with linear constraints, so the Abadie constraint qualification holds and no extra assumption is needed; z_i² = z_i at binaries finishes the argument (pp. 11–12). OA then terminates within C(n, k) ≤ (en/k)^k iterations (p. 13).
  - Theorem 2: the convex-hull relaxation equals one SOCP, which is dual to the perspective relaxation. The proof chains the Sion–Kakutani minimax theorem, LP duality over the capped simplex, and SOC duality (pp. 20–21). The duality gap shrinks as γ decreases and reaches 0 at a finite γ (p. 21).
  - Corollaries 4–5 use the KKT conditions for max-k norms. Corollary 4 gives a sufficient condition for the SOCP bound to be exact, with the support read off the top k entries of w* (p. 21; proof pp. 38–39). Corollary 5 gives a closed form for diagonal Σ (pp. 22–23).
  - Propositions 1–2 use textbook sensitivity analysis, citing his own LP textbook among others (pp. 16–17). A regularized optimum is 1/(2γ)-optimal for the unregularized problem, and the optimal support stays fixed for γ beyond some γ₀.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Julia 1.1 with JuMP 0.18.5, CPLEX 12.8.0 for the masters and Mosek 9.0 for the subproblems, on a MacBook Pro (2.9 GHz i9, 16 GB), one thread, default parameters, code on GitHub (p. 24). The opponents are CPLEX on the big-M and MISOCO formulations of the regularized problem, with limits of 300 s, 3,600 s or 600 s.
  - Results:
    - Unconstrained OR-library instances take at most 0.17 s; big-M needs more than 300 s on port2–5 (Table 3, p. 26).
    - On the Frangioni–Gentile 400+ set with k = 6, the method averages 54.47 s, against 531.7 s for CPLEX, which left 8 of 10 instances unsolved (Table 6, p. 29).
    - On the index data, constraint-free runs take under 1 s. The unsolved Wilshire runs with a minimum-return constraint end at gaps of at most 1.85%, where CPLEX often finds no feasible solution (Table 9, p. 33).
  - Failures are printed. Port4 with k = 10 and a minimum-return constraint is solved by no method within an hour, and CPLEX's MISOCO is faster on several minimum-return instances (Table 4, pp. 25–26). Without diagonal extraction, plain Algorithm 3 hits the 600 s limit on all 50 runs of the 200+ set (Table 13, p. 44).
  - There is no out-of-sample evaluation of the portfolios.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic and theoretical: the regularized model and the OA algorithm (p. 3), and the SOCP bound with its tightness condition (p. 1). There is also software (p. 24).
  - Stated limits:
    - The regularized problem is still NP-hard (footnote 2, p. 3), and OA's worst case is exponential in k (p. 13).
    - Algorithm 1 is ill-suited to fixed time budgets (p. 18).
    - SDO-based diagonal extraction needs prohibitive memory once n is in the thousands (p. 15).
    - Root cuts can hurt on easy problems (footnote 7, p. 14; p. 28).
    - Comparisons with published methods are imperfect, because of different machines and unavailable code (pp. 4, 29, 35).
    - The speed without constraints is partly due to the data (p. 35).
    - Restarts are not explored (p. 14).
  - There is no conclusion section and no future direction: the paper ends with the four-point summary of §5.5 (pp. 33–35).
  - Card-writer's reading:
    - Big-M is benchmarked on the ridge problem, where the paper itself says it cannot exploit the regularizer (p. 27).
    - The index runs use covariance estimates of rank 50 to 1,000 and do not print n (pp. 30–33).
    - On the Frangioni–Gentile data, γ = 1000/n makes the ridge negligible (p. 28). The gains there therefore come from OA with diagonal extraction, not from the ridge.

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 6–7, 9–13, 17, 20–21).
  - The model is redesigned (a ridge term; z kept off the regularizer) so that a commercial MILP solver's lazy callbacks apply.
  - The dual is read three ways: for cuts, for the support (Corollary 4), and to re-derive the rival perspective cuts (§3.5).
  - The boundary is printed: still NP-hard, a worst case of C(n, k) iterations, port4 unsolved.
  - Bears on Tension T4 and the IO caution about weak big-M. Like S061 and S175, the paper leaves big-M because its relaxations are weak (footnote 4, p. 6), and shows it failing from n = 85 on (Table 3, p. 26), though only on the regularized problem (p. 27).
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 4, 6, 10).
  - The written-off thing is a method, not a problem. OA had lost to branch-and-bound here, and the paper grants the reason: uninformative subgradients (p. 6).
  - The change it names is a new dual subgradient plus lazy callbacks, which are now standard in MILO solvers (pp. 6, 10).
  - The *why now* number is a scale ratio over rivals' self-reported instances (Table 1, p. 4), not a solver × hardware figure.
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 16–17, 31–34). One knob, γ, carries a solution-independent price: the regularized optimum is 1/(2γ)-optimal (Proposition 1). The support is stable in γ, and the paper publishes allocations, runtime and cuts against γ (Figures 2–4, pp. 32–34). As in S175, the priced property is tractability. The robustness reading of γ is cited, not measured (pp. 3–4).
- Method 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 18–20, 24–35).
  - The warm start is the group's own discrete first-order heuristic, with five random starts and L = 10 (pp. 19–20).
  - The solve is exact, with certificates or printed gaps under stated limits, solver versions, machine and threads (pp. 24–33). Unsolved runs are reported with their gaps, not called optimal (Tables 5, 7–9, pp. 27, 31–33), so Tension T1 is not triggered.
  - Variant: the opponent is the commercial solver on two formulations, not an incumbent heuristic. Published rivals enter only through their self-reported times (pp. 4, 29).
- Heuristic 4: evidence ✅. Failures printed in the body:
  - port4 with k = 10 and a minimum-return constraint is solved by no method (p. 25);
  - CPLEX's MISOCO is faster on several minimum-return instances (Table 4, p. 26);
  - CPLEX's own heuristics find better incumbents than Algorithm 2 (p. 28);
  - every plain run without diagonal extraction on the 200+ set fails (p. 44);
  - Zheng et al.'s tighter diagonals slow the method in 95% of instances (p. 40).
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 4, 18). Practice sets the class and the scale: index sizes, monitoring costs and rebalancing time budgets. But the portfolios are never scored as decisions. There is no out-of-sample risk or return and no asset manager's rule as a baseline. Finance is the testbed (AP), not the judge.
- Method 6: evidence, weak (pp. 6–7, 35).
  - One device passes between students and across problem classes: from S061 (with Van Parys) to S175 and this paper (with Cory-Wright, whose thesis line 04-mentorship.md infers it to be).
  - The in-out code came from Jean Pauphilet (p. 35). 04-mentorship.md names Pauphilet as Cory-Wright's informal second adviser (Heuristic 8).
- Tension T6 (headline vs caveat): evidence. The introduction says problems with up to 3,200 securities are solved to certified optimality in hundreds or thousands of seconds (p. 3). The body carries the caveats:
  - Table 9 stops at 600 s, with gaps on some minimum-return runs (p. 33);
  - the speed partly depends on the data (p. 35);
  - n is not printed for the indices (p. 30).
- New-pattern candidate: **regularize the model for tractability, then price and undo the regularization**.
  - A small ridge term makes f convex in z and the relaxation near-exact. Proposition 1 prices it at 1/(2γ), and an unregularized QP on the fixed support polishes the answer (pp. 3, 7, 16–17, 21).
  - The pattern recurs in S061 (card c21: ridge with closed-form elimination) and S175 (card c23: *re-read a modelling convention as a hidden, tunable modelling choice*). This card is the third.
- New-pattern candidate: **exact equivalence to a sibling problem the group already solves**. Portfolio selection equals constrained sparse regression through a Cholesky factor (pp. 8–9). The equivalence imports the regression OA machinery (S061) and the best-subset warm start (S010, card c16; p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- On/off logic with a strongly convex term: write Diag(z)x, leave the ridge term unscaled, dualize the inner QP and use z_i² = z_i. Then f(z) is convex in z, and the subgradient −(γ/2)w_i*² comes free with each subproblem (pp. 9–12).
- Solve each subproblem only on the k active indices, and fill the inactive dual entries in closed form (w_i = max(·, 0)). This gives a full cut, and the strongest cut for those duals (p. 13).
- Price a regularized surrogate before using it:
  - bound its suboptimality for the original problem (1/(2γ));
  - polish by re-solving the unregularized QP on the support found (pp. 3, 16–17);
  - use support stability to tune γ after one certified solve (p. 31).
- Stabilize in-out root cuts at the exact optimum of the cheap SOCP relaxation. Cap them at 200 root cuts, 20 per node and at most 50 nodes (p. 14; Algorithm 4, p. 47). Stop on the larger of the OA and SOCP bounds (p. 23).
- Replace no-good feasibility cuts by implied constraints on z where they exist. For a minimum-return constraint, require some z_i = 1 among the assets with µ_i ≥ r (pp. 14–15, 25). When many constraints bind, copy x into the master instead (p. 15).
- When rivals' code is unavailable, do two things:
  - tabulate each rival's largest solved instance, with solver, constraints and data as reported by its authors, and say why the comparison is imperfect (pp. 4–5);
  - benchmark against the commercial solver that has absorbed the rivals' techniques (footnote 3, p. 4; p. 25).
