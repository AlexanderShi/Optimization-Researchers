### S325 · Personalized prescription of ACEI/ARBs for hypertensive COVID-19 patients (Health Care Management Science 2021, DOI 10.1007/s10729-021-09545-5)
Read level: **full**, medRxiv preprint (posted 4 November 2020): pp. 1–17, all sections and references. The Appendix and Supplemental Tables S3–S5 cited in the text (pp. 4, 6, 8) are not in the file and were not read. The HCMS print was not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors** (p. 1):
- Alison Borenstein and Luca Mingardi (MIT Sloan).
- Omid Nohadani (Benefits Science Technologies).
- Agni Orfanoudaki and Holly Wiberg (MIT ORC), marked as equal contributors.
- Bartolomeo Stellato (Princeton ORFE).
- Pankaj Sarin and Dirk J. Varelmann (Brigham and Women's Hospital).
- Vicente Estrada, Carlos Macaya and Iván J. Núñez Gil (Hospital Clínico San Carlos, Madrid).

**Position in trajectory**: the COVID-19 period of the healthcare-analytics programme {DA, AP}. It reuses the ensemble voting framework of the group's coronary artery disease and antihypertensive papers [7, 8] (pp. 3, 15). A tool is published at covidanalytics.io (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA, ML, AP}:
  - Evidence on ACE inhibitors and ARBs for hypertensive COVID-19 patients was inconclusive. Early fears of harm through ACE2 gave way to studies showing no harm or lower mortality, so "there is no consensus" on their use (pp. 1–2).
  - RCT evidence was scarce, and causal-inference methods estimate average effects (p. 2). "Regress and Compare" is criticized for model-specific prediction errors and biases (p. 3).
  - The question is asked per patient: does this drug class lower in-hospital mortality or morbidity (p. 2)?
- **D2 Core idea**: The paper adapts the group's ensemble form of regress-and-compare [8] (p. 3):
  - Match treated and untreated patients.
  - Train six classifiers per arm: RF, CART, OCT, gradient-boosted trees, QDA and Gaussian naive Bayes.
  - Each classifier votes for treatment when predicted risk falls by at least 5% in relative terms. The majority wins, and ties go to the side whose voters have the higher average AUC (p. 6).
  - Five evaluation metrics stand in for the missing counterfactual (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Cohort: 3,643 hypertensive inpatients from 38 hospitals in five countries. The derivation cohort is Spanish (HOPE registry, HM Hospitals); the validation cohort comes from Italy, Germany, Ecuador and Brigham and Women's (pp. 1, 3–5).
  - Variables: 29 features, imputed with MICE; features present in fewer than 70% of rows are dropped (p. 4).
  - Outcome: in-hospital death or morbidity (sepsis, acute renal failure, heart failure, embolic event) (p. 4).
  - Matching: the data are observational, so cardinality matching [59] is run on pre-treatment and co-treatment features. Balance was targeted at SMD < 0.10 and reached < 0.05, leaving 1,920 matched derivation cases (pp. 4–6, 8).
  - Admitted simplifications: the drug's effect is taken in isolation from co-treatments, lab values are binary with preset cut-offs, and the results "are not causal" (p. 14).
  - Unexplained gap: the derivation arm counts (1,043 + 1,663 = 2,706; p. 8 and Table 2, p. 10) do not add up to the 2,842 stated on p. 3. The text read does not explain it.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for proofs or formulations. The optimization content is outsourced to cardinality matching and OCT (p. 6). The paper's own design consists of:
  - the decision rule: the 5% relative-reduction threshold, the vote and its AUC tie-break (p. 6);
  - the metric definitions (pp. 7–8):
    - PE: mean predicted risk under the prescription minus the observed rate, computed only on patients whose recommendation differs from their care;
    - CPE: PE rescaled by the new site's event rate over the training rate;
    - PR: one learner scores both the given and the prescribed treatment, and the range over the six learners is reported;
    - agreement rate and prescription AUC.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Software: Python 3.6.3 and Julia 1.2.0, with scikit-learn, XGBoost and Interpretable AI (p. 6).
  - Arm models: average AUC 0.819 (with drug) and 0.800 (without) on test, 0.744 and 0.731 on validation (Table 3, p. 11).
  - Validation prescriptions: PE −1.0%, CPE −0.5%, PR from −0.7% to −4.5%, agreement 48.2%, prescription AUC 0.774. The share of patients recommended the drug rises from 35.0% to 46.8% (pp. 8–9; Table 5, p. 11).
  - Test set: PE is +0.008, a worsening. The text calls it a slight increase but prints "(-0.8%)" (p. 9 vs Table 5, p. 11).
  - The 5% threshold was chosen in an appendix that is not in the file (p. 6). No field test is reported.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution, at application level: a multi-country dataset, per-arm risk models, a personalized rule and an online tool (pp. 3, 11).
  - Specific stated limits (pp. 13–14):
    - the treatment is taken in isolation;
    - data from February–May 2020 carry timing confounders (hospital congestion, changing protocols);
    - lab values use binary cut-offs;
    - the results are not causal;
    - the outcome cannot register harms such as birth defects, so the rule's higher prescription rate for women cannot be checked against them.
  - Future work: N treatments under the same voting scheme once data allow, integration into EMR systems, and testing at other hospitals (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 7–9, 13–14).
  - Prescriptions are scored against the standard of care, on exactly the patients where the two differ.
  - Agreement is reported but declared not to be a target (p. 8).
  - Validation is on other countries.
  - What the evaluation cannot show is printed: the results are not causal, and harms outside the outcome are missed.
  - Variant: the "owner's rule" is shifting practice rather than a rule (p. 8), the counterfactuals come from the same models that prescribe (p. 7), and no field test is reported.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 3, 11). The ensemble-prescription framework is reused from two earlier papers of the group [7, 8], two ORC co-authors are marked as equal contributors, and a public interface ships with the paper.
- Taste mark 5: variant ⚠ (p. 6). The prescribing rule includes black boxes (RF, XGBoost), explained afterwards with SHAP rather than built to be interpretable.
- Pattern pool:
  - **Counterfactual replay against the historical decision-maker** (p. 7; Table 5, p. 11);
  - **Ship a public tool with the paper** (p. 11);
  - **external validation at a partner hospital abroad** (pp. 3–4, 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Score a prescription rule under several evaluators (p. 7; Table 5, p. 11):
  - the ensemble's own estimate (PE);
  - the same estimate rescaled to a new site's event rate (CPE);
  - each single learner used as ground truth for both arms, reporting the min–max range (PR).
- Compute benefits only on patients whose recommendation differs from the care they received. Report agreement separately and state that it is not a target (pp. 7–8).
- Recommend a treatment only when it cuts predicted risk by at least a set relative amount (here 5%), so that near-ties do not drive prescriptions (p. 6).
- Split the derivation and validation cohorts by country rather than at random (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "While the agreement rate is informative, the goal of the prescriptive algorithm is to improve upon the current standard of care and thus a high agreement rate is not necessarily desirable." (p. 8)
- "Finally, we note that these results are not causal and do not isolate the effect of ACEI/ARBs in patient outcomes." (p. 14)

### S354 · Algorithmic insurance (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv:2106.00839)
Read level: **partial**, arXiv v3 (30 March 2026).
- Read: the main text, pp. 1–33, in full (§§1–8: the model, every theorem and proposition statement with its discussion, the case study and the discussion); e-companion p. 38 (overview), pp. 49–51 (proof of Theorem 1) and pp. 56–60 (EC.3 validation of the functional form, EC.4 case-study costs, EC.6 notation).
- Not read: the EC.1 lemmas (pp. 38–49) and the remaining proofs (pp. 51–56). References were skimmed, and the 2021 v1 was not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Agni Orfanoudaki (Saïd Business School, Oxford; p. 1). She is an MIT ORC co-author in S325 (p. 1).

**Position in trajectory**: an ML-risk line with a former ORC co-author {DA, ML, AP}. First posted in 2021, it is still an arXiv preprint in its 2026 v3 (p. 1). The reference list (pp. 33–37) cites none of Bertsimas's own papers; the only co-author paper cited is Feizi, Orfanoudaki et al. 2023 (p. 34).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA, ML, AP}:
  - AI errors create liability (a missed cancer costs millions, a false positive thousands). Insurers cannot price this risk, and developers lack a framework that links system design to liability (pp. 1–2).
  - The entry point is an operational choice that nobody prices: the classification threshold, usually set to maximize accuracy (pp. 3, 14).
  - The problem is stated as CVaR minimization of scenario losses over the threshold, followed by contract design (pp. 3, 10–11).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - A one-parameter trigonometric calibration curve (k for discrimination, α for class balance) makes the error rates closed-form functions of the threshold, so the CVaR-optimal threshold has closed forms.
  - The accuracy-optimal practice is then priced against that optimum.
  - The paper proposes a dual-limit liability contract that mandates the CVaR-optimal threshold as a condition of coverage. This turns insurance into a governance mechanism (pp. 8–11, 18–19).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: a binary classifier whose scores are uniform on [0, 1] and treated as calibrated (p. 8). α is defined as the class-0 share (pp. 8, 60) and k ∈ (0, π/2] (p. 8). There are J cost scenarios with per-instance false-positive and false-negative costs (p. 10).
  - Assumptions adopted for tractability or by convention:
    - data and costs stay stable within a contract period, adopted "to ensure analytical tractability" (p. 32);
    - costs come from scenario estimates, not claims history (p. 7);
    - premiums are based on CVaR (pp. 7, 18);
    - no ties among scenario losses (pp. 11, 51);
    - the tail set is unchanged by small perturbations (p. 12);
    - drift is exponential (p. 22);
    - interpretability acts multiplicatively (p. 23).
  - Apparent inconsistencies in v3:
    - The case study sets α to the cancer prevalence (0.008 and 0.15; pp. 25–26; 0.8% and 15% in Table EC.3, p. 59), yet Table 1 labels α "non-cancer rate" (p. 27) and the definition makes it the class-0 share.
    - The diagnostic k = 1.745 exceeds the stated maximum π/2 (pp. 8, 27).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (statements read; one proof read):
  - The base program is Rockafellar–Uryasev's CVaR formulation (pp. 10–11).
  - Theorem 1 (p. 11) gives three closed-form regimes for a fixed tail set; the global optimum comes from enumerating self-consistent tail sets. Its proof (pp. 49–51) combines:
    - convexity of τ ↦ CVaR (Lemma EC.8);
    - first-order conditions on each branch (EC.14–EC.17);
    - mutual exclusivity of the parameter regions (Lemma EC.7).
  - The prose describing the regimes (pp. 11–12) does not follow Theorem 1's case numbering.
  - Further results:
    - the optimal threshold moves monotonically with the cost ratio but can respond non-monotonically to β (Propositions 1–2, pp. 12–13);
    - the accuracy-optimal threshold solves P(Y = 1 | τ) = 1/2, whatever the costs (Lemma 1, p. 14);
    - the threshold gap is linear in the deviation of the tail-cost ratio (Proposition 3), and the CVaR penalty is bounded quadratically in the gap by curvature constants (Proposition 4) (pp. 15–16);
    - insurance value splits into risk transfer, capital relief and calibration gain (Theorem 2, pp. 19–20);
    - limits of the value function, an optimal contract length under drift and a first-order condition for interpretability (Propositions 5–7, pp. 21–24).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Functional-form check: the curve is fitted to calibration curves of logistic regression, random forests and gradient boosting on synthetic scikit-learn data with α ∈ [0.40, 0.60]. There are 120 fits, with median R² 0.879 (EC.3, pp. 56–57). The main text instead says 80 combinations and includes neural networks (p. 9).
  - Mammography case study: no algorithm-specific claims exist, so parameters come from the literature and costs from four-tier multipliers (pp. 25–26, 57–59). Claim-emergence rates are 2%/20% (false positives/false negatives) in screening and 3%/35% in diagnosis (p. 26).
  - Cost of accuracy thresholds, in extra CVaR: 22.0% without claim friction and 2.9% with it in screening; 73.7% and 1,313% in diagnosis (Table 1, p. 27).
  - Insurance value: about 15% of CVaR in screening, 14–16% in diagnosis, and up to 64.6% for accuracy-maximizing diagnostic firms (pp. 28–29). Optimal contract lengths fall from 33.8 to 16.4 months as drift increases (p. 30).
  - No real classifier, image data or claims data enter the case study.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: modelling and theory with a stylized case study, which the paper calls "the first quantitative framework" for algorithmic liability (p. 33).
  - Stated limits (p. 32): stationarity within contract periods, binary classification only, scenario-based costs, simplified threshold enforceability, a single-organization view.
  - Future work (p. 32): regression, RL and generative AI; robust optimization against ambiguity in scenario probabilities and costs; market-level and multi-agent dynamics.
  - The surrogate was validated only on near-balanced data (α 0.40–0.60, p. 57), while the case study runs at α = 0.008 and 0.15.

**Method links**:
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 15–17, 27–29).
  - What fits: a property the decision-maker wants (low tail liability), one knob (the threshold, with β as risk aversion), proved gap and penalty bounds, a price table, and a hand-off of the knob to the insurer, who mandates it.
  - What differs: the price computed is the cost of ignoring the property (accuracy-maximizing practice), not of buying it, and the bounds' constants depend on the optimum and on the stylized model.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 14–17, 25–30). The threshold is judged by the liability its decisions create, not by accuracy, against practice's rule (accuracy maximization). But there is no owner, partner data or field test: all parameters come from the literature.
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 8–11). Tractability comes from choosing a surrogate model whose error rates integrate in closed form, plus the CVaR linear-programming form, not from keeping a solver class. The boundary is printed (p. 32).
- Heuristic 7 (text only): evidence (p. 1). The manuscript stays on arXiv across five years and three versions.
- Pattern pool: **analyzable surrogate of the practical learner** (pp. 8–10; EC.3 pp. 56–57).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Choose a one-parameter surrogate of a learner's calibration curve whose error rates integrate in closed form. Before relying on it, fit it by least squares to real learners' empirical calibration curves and report R² by regime (p. 9; pp. 56–57).
- To optimize a threshold under CVaR: use the Rockafellar–Uryasev form, solve the first-order conditions branch by branch for a fixed tail set, then enumerate tail sets to find a self-consistent optimum (p. 11; pp. 49–51).
- Measure practice against the optimum with two metrics, a decision gap (∆τ) and a relative risk penalty (∆CVaR). Bound the penalty quadratically in the gap using curvature constants (pp. 15–16).
- Decompose an intervention's value into components (risk transfer, capital relief, calibration gain) and report each one by firm type (Theorem 2, pp. 19–20; Figure 5, p. 29).
- Model claim friction as emergence rates that differ by error type and scale the costs. Report results with and without friction (pp. 18, 26–27).

**Quotes**:
- "Unlike traditional insurance that merely transfers risk, algorithmic insurance can function as both a financial instrument and an operational governance mechanism" (p. 1)
- "Guided by current algorithmic insurance offerings, we adopt the assumption of stationarity within contract periods to ensure analytical tractability." (p. 32)

### S366 · Frequency estimation in data streams: Learning the optimal hashing scheme (IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering 2021, DOI 10.1109/tkde.2021.3103819)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v2 (arXiv:2007.09261v2, 2 June 2021, TKDE format): pp. 1–15, including Appendix A (proof of Theorem 1, p. 13), Appendix B (flowcharts, pp. 13–14), Appendix C (notation, p. 15) and the author biographies. References were skimmed. The axes of Figures 7–8 are garbled in the extraction, so plotted values are taken from the text (p. 12). The TKDE print was not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Vassilis Digalakis Jr., an MIT ORC PhD candidate advised by Bertsimas (p. 15).

**Position in trajectory**: learning-augmented algorithms, from the years of ML under a modern optimization lens {IO, ML}. It cites the group's "voice of optimization" and online-MIO papers as related work (pp. 2, 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO, ML}:
  - The task is streaming frequency estimation in sublinear space. The paper assumes a prefix of the stream has been observed and that elements carry features (pp. 1, 3).
  - Count-Min relies on random hashing. The learned Count-Min of Hsu et al. gives predicted heavy hitters their own buckets, but "remains heuristic" (pp. 2–4).
  - The entry point is to replace oblivious random hashing by an optimized, data-dependent hashing scheme (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Assign the prefix's elements to buckets so as to minimize λ·(estimation error) + (1 − λ)·(feature-similarity error). An element's estimate is the average frequency of its bucket.
  - Train a classifier from features to buckets, so that unseen elements can also be estimated. The hashing scheme is a hash table plus this classifier (pp. 4, 7).
  - An adaptive extension counts the distinct elements in each bucket with a Bloom filter (p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Prefix frequencies stand in for the stream's (p. 4).
  - The objective deliberately weights elements uniformly rather than by frequency, so that rare elements are also estimated well (p. 4).
  - The big-M constant is set to M ≥ the largest prefix frequency (p. 5).
  - Synthetic data: G Gaussian feature groups (p = 2), group arrival probability proportional to 1/g, and a fraction g0 of each group allowed in the prefix (pp. 7–8).
  - Real data: the AOL query log, with 21 million queries (3.8 million unique) over 90 days in 2006; day 1 serves as the prefix (pp. 9, 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1 turns the nonlinear binary problem into an exact MILO with O(n²b) variables and constraints (p. 5). The device (p. 13): multiply by the bucket counts, linearize error×assignment with big-M and assignment×assignment with McCormick inequalities.
  - At case-study scale this would need about 10^11 variables (p. 5). The paper therefore uses block coordinate descent with incremental bucket statistics, at O(n²b) per iteration and with no global guarantee (pp. 5–6).
  - For λ = 1 the problem is a one-dimensional k-median problem, solved exactly by dynamic programming in O(nb) time. That solution also warm-starts BCD (pp. 5–6).
  - Unlike Count-Min, which comes with a probabilistic error bound (p. 3), the method offers no error guarantee.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Set-up: Python 3 on a Xeon E5-2690 with Gurobi. Only the 2016 manual is cited, and no version, time limit or gap is given (pp. 8, 15). Synthetic experiments are repeated ten times, real ones five times (pp. 8, 11).
  - Synthetic findings (p. 9):
    - at G = 6 the MILO improves only slightly on BCD;
    - beyond G = 10, BCD falls behind the exact DP;
    - classifier results "heavily depend on the data generating process".
  - AOL baselines: Count-Min, and learned Count-Min given an ideal heavy-hitter oracle, both at their best hyperparameters (p. 10).
  - AOL results: one to two orders of magnitude lower average error, and 45–90% lower frequency-weighted error. For example, the average error is about 29 against 479 at 120 KB (p. 12; Figure 7a, p. 12). As memory grows, the baselines approach opt-hash on the weighted metric (pp. 11–12).
  - Gaps in the protocol:
    - the real-data runs use BCD at λ = 1, although DP is exact for that case (p. 11 vs pp. 5–6);
    - memory is counted at 4 bytes per bucket (p. 11), and the classifier's size is not mentioned;
    - the Bloom-filter extension is never evaluated (pp. 7–12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: algorithmic (MILO, BCD, DP warm start, learned hashing of unseen elements) and empirical. Interpretability through feature importances is also claimed (pp. 2–3, 12).
  - The conclusion summarizes, with no limitations or future-work paragraph (pp. 12–13).
  - Limits visible in the text: BCD degrades beyond G = 10; results depend on the data-generating process; error grows over time for every method; the advantage narrows as memory grows (pp. 9, 11–12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 4–6, 13). An object owned by the streaming-algorithms tradition, the random sketch, is rewritten as an exact MILO. The boundary is printed as a size count (10^11), next to the exactly solvable case λ = 1.
- Method 4: evidence ✅, with a variant (pp. 6, 8–12).
  - Evidence: the DP optimum warm-starts BCD, exact MILO and DP runs on small instances measure the heuristic, the incumbent is dueled in its strongest form (ideal oracle, best hyperparameters), and the paper says where the method wins (low memory).
  - Variant: no certificate or gap at scale, and no solver version or limits.
- Heuristic 3 (text only): evidence (p. 5). A size count justifies the switch to a local-search heuristic, with MIO kept for small instances.
- Heuristic 5 (text only): variant (p. 7). A classifier learns the optimizer's output (bucket codes) to extend it to unseen elements, not to replace a repeated solve.
- Pattern pool:
  - **solution-as-label** (p. 7);
  - **exact optimum on a small solvable instance as the certificate for a heuristic** (pp. 8–9);
  - **Size-count intractability verdict licensing a tailored heuristic** (p. 5);
  - **optimistic upper bound for the incumbent** (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To linearize an objective built on bucket averages (p. 13):
  - multiply each error inequality by the bucket count;
  - replace error×assignment terms by big-M constraints, with M the largest observed value;
  - replace assignment×assignment terms by McCormick inequalities.
- Solve the special case that has an exact polynomial algorithm (here λ = 1, by DP), and use it both as warm start and as yardstick for the general heuristic (pp. 6, 9).
- Turn an optimal assignment of the observed items into labels for a classifier on features, so the scheme extends to unseen items (p. 7).
- Give the baseline an ideal oracle (the true heavy hitters of the test period) and its best hyperparameters, so that a win is conservative (p. 10).
- Report one uniformly weighted and one frequency-weighted error metric, and say which method wins on each (pp. 11–12).

**Quotes**:
- "In general, however, their approach remains heuristic, does not guarantee optimal performance, and possibly throws away information by taking hard, binary decisions." (p. 4)
- "Nevertheless, solving a mixed integer linear optimization problem of that size can still be prohibitive in the applications we consider." (p. 5)

### S386 · A robust optimization approach to deep learning (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv:2112.09279)
Read level: **partial**, arXiv v2 (6 April 2023), retitled "Robust Upper Bounds for Adversarial Training" on p. 1; the running head keeps the listed title (p. 3).
- Read: pp. 1–24 in full (every main-text derivation, including the proofs of Theorem 5, Corollary 6 and Theorem 7, plus the experiments and conclusions) and Appendix C on CNNs (pp. 35–36).
- Not read: Appendix A, the proofs of Lemmas 1–4 (pp. 28–30), and Appendix B, the generalization to L layers (pp. 30–35). References were skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Xavier Boix (MIT Brain and Cognitive Sciences), Kimberly Villalobos Carballo (MIT ORC) and Dick den Hertog (Amsterdam Business School) (p. 1). The work was funded in part by Fujitsu Laboratories (p. 24).

**Position in trajectory**: the robust-optimization-for-ML line {RO, ML}. It follows robust classification of SVMs, logistic regression and trees (S104), and is written with the co-author of the 2022 RO book (pp. 3–5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO, ML}:
  - Adversarial training either relies on heuristics that fail against larger or new attacks, or minimizes upper bounds from layer-by-layer convex relaxations whose gaps propagate (pp. 2–3).
  - The entry point: ML has not used "the more advanced tools" of RO (p. 3). Two obstacles stand in the way: networks are neither convex nor concave in the input, and a robust counterpart must keep training scalable (p. 5).
  - The problem: minimize a closed-form upper bound of the adversarial loss over an Lp ball (pp. 6–7).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - The loss is bounded by per-class worst-case logit differences (Lemma 1, p. 7).
  - aRUB linearizes the logits, not the loss, and adds a dual-norm penalty for each class (eqs. 9–11, p. 8).
  - RUB splits each ReLU layer into convex and concave parts and applies conjugate duality layer by layer, until the inner maximum is linear in the input. With an L1 ball the dual becomes a maximum over 2M points, which gives a provable, closed-form bound that can be trained by backpropagation (pp. 10–15).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Networks: feed-forward with ReLU activations (p. 6).
  - Losses must be translation-invariant and monotone (Assumptions 3.1–3.2). Softmax cross-entropy, multiclass hinge and 0–1 loss qualify; squared and absolute error do not (pp. 6–7).
  - The bound is provable only for L1 balls (p. 14).
  - The derivation is shown for two layers, with L layers left to Appendix B (p. 11).
  - The free variables t are fixed at the sign pattern; any t in [0, 1] keeps the bound valid (p. 15).
  - Memory grows with the input dimension (pp. 3, 19).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Lemmas 2–3 extend conjugate (Fenchel) duality to compositions of functions (p. 10). Lemma 4 gives the conjugates of the ReLU pieces (p. 11).
  - Theorem 5 is a sup-inf over s and t whose objective is linear in the first layer (p. 12).
  - Corollary 6 applies the dual norm (p. 13); setting s = t to the sign pattern recovers aRUB (p. 13).
  - Theorem 7 combines the min-max inequality with L1/L∞ duality to obtain a maximum over 2M vertices (p. 14).
  - aRUB itself is not guaranteed to be a bound, so its validity is checked empirically (p. 8; Table 1, p. 10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Data and attacks: 46 UCI data sets plus MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR; foolbox PGD and FGM attacks at default parameters (p. 15).
  - Protocol: hyperparameters are chosen per defense, attack and radius on validation data, L1 radii are scaled by √m, and over 40,000 networks were trained (p. 16).
  - Baselines: PGD-L∞, FGSM-L∞, COAP-L∞, a first-order Baseline and Nominal training (pp. 16, 20).
  - Who wins depends on the radius: aRUB and PGD lead at small ρ, PGD in a middle range and RUB at large ρ. CIFAR is the exception, where "all methods perform very poorly overall" (pp. 16, 21).
  - Certified L1 accuracy for RUB: 86.02% on Fashion-MNIST and 97.11% on MNIST at ρ = 2.8, and 45.66% on CIFAR at ρ = 5.54 (p. 22).
  - Throughput: RUB is faster than PGD on UCI data and slower than aRUB on vision data (Tables 2 and 6, pp. 18, 22).
  - aRUB was an upper bound of the PGD loss in 79–99% of the checks (Table 1, p. 10).
  - Code is released (p. 24). RUB is not run on CNNs (p. 35).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: methodological, namely two training losses, one of them provable, with a broad benchmark.
  - Stated limits: guarantees only for L1, memory for high-dimensional inputs, CIFAR performance, and aRUB valid only empirically (pp. 10, 14, 21–22).
  - The abstract's claim for RUB at large perturbations meets a CIFAR exception in the body (pp. 1, 21).
  - Future work: other norms, how bound tightness changes across layers (p. 23), and RUB for CNNs (p. 35).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 5, 10–15, 35). Duality turns the robust problem into a counterpart, but the "class" kept is the training pipeline: a closed-form, unconstrained loss trainable by backpropagation, stated as requirement (ii) (p. 5). The boundary is printed: L1 only, memory for 2M points, no CNN bound.
- Method 4: evidence ✅ for the duel (pp. 15–22).
  - Named incumbents are run on 49 data sets under equal per-attack validation tuning.
  - The paper says which method wins in which radius regime, including the losing CIFAR case, and reports speeds.
  - Variant: there is no MIO, so the "certificate" is a lower bound on adversarial accuracy, presented with the caveat that it does not show higher accuracy (p. 22).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 4, 16, 21). ρ is the knob, and the accuracy-by-radius tables are the price curve. At ρ = 0 robust training raises natural accuracy, a negative price that the paper says was also observed for SVMs and trees (p. 4). There is no bound tying the knob to a probability.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 3–5, 24). The RO-for-ML programme is carried from robust classification to deep networks, with a co-author of the RO book and an ORC co-author. The released code (p. 24) bears on the skill's reproducibility checkpoint (Workflow F).
- Pattern pool: **score rivals with your own certificate** (new; pp. 22–23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If a loss depends only on logit differences and is monotone in them, bound the adversarial loss by plugging the per-class worst-case differences into the nominal loss (Lemma 1, p. 7).
- Split each ReLU layer into convex and concave parts, then apply convex and concave conjugates (Lemmas 2–4) to push the inner maximization down one layer at a time (pp. 10–12).
- Choose the uncertainty norm whose dual norm is a maximum over finitely many points (L1 → L∞), which removes the auxiliary maximization variables (p. 14).
- When a training surrogate is only approximately a bound, report how often it upper-bounds the strongest attack's loss across all networks trained (Table 1, p. 10).
- Scale radii across norms so that all balls contain the same L2 ball, and tune every defense separately for each attack type and radius (p. 16).
- Certify every competitor with your bound, re-selecting hyperparameters for the bound, and say that a better certificate does not mean better accuracy (p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "This expression may not always be an upper bound of the adversarial loss (Eq. (2)); however, we observe across a variety of experiments that usually it is indeed an upper bound (see Table 1)." (p. 8)
- "While these results are only lower bounds for the adversarial accuracy and we cannot claim a better accuracy for RUB than for the rest of the methods" (p. 22)

### S421 · The price of diversity (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv:2107.03900)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (3 July 2021, "Submitted to Operations Research"): pp. 1–33 read in full, references pp. 33–36 skimmed. The extraction drops the ε glyph (it prints "-demographic parity", e.g. pp. 14, 26), and the figures' plotted values are taken only from the text.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Hari Bandi (MIT ORC) (p. 1).

**Position in trajectory**: a "price of" manuscript {ML, IO, AP}. Its reference list (pp. 33–36) cites the group's OCT and robust-classification work but not the price-of-fairness paper that SKILL.md lists under Method 3. The flip budget comes from robust classification (S104) and is turned from min-max into min-min (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML, IO, AP}:
  - Historical selection data (bar passage, parole risk, lending) carry systemic bias. Models trained on such data reinforce the bias and can amplify it through feedback loops. Dropping protected attributes fails because other features correlate with them (pp. 1–3).
  - The existing fairness approaches (pre-processing, post-processing, Lagrangian) each have drawbacks (pp. 3–4), and demographic parity alone does not select the most meritorious people within each group (p. 7).
  - The problem: reach ε-demographic parity while bounding the change in merit (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - A "min-min" MIO flips exactly ⌈τ_w n_w⌉ and ⌈τ_b n_b⌉ labels in the two groups while fitting logistic regression or a soft-margin SVM. Constraints keep the mean z-score of each merit attribute among positives within δ of its original value (pp. 8–10).
  - The budgets τ_w and τ_b follow in closed form from the target gap ε, with the total number of positives held fixed (pp. 14–15).
  - An OCT trained on the flip decisions turns them into readable rules for decision makers (pp. 27–33).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Two groups per protected attribute (p. 8). Diversity means demographic parity; merit means the mean z-scores of chosen attributes (pp. 7, 9).
  - The α-bias statistic is said not to prove bias (p. 7). Covariates are standardized separately within each group (p. 15).
  - Data (pp. 17, 21, 24):
    - LSAC law students: 20,738 rows, 11 features; α = 0.3034 by race, 0.0211 by gender.
    - COMPAS: the COMPAS label itself is the outcome; α = 0.29.
    - Taiwanese credit default: α = 0.033 by gender.
  - ε is set to 0.01 or 0.001 (pp. 18, 24).
  - Not stated: the δ used, the train/test split, and dataset sizes beyond LSAC (pp. 16–28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - The z·β products are linearized with big-M, and the merit ratios by cross-multiplying (p. 10).
  - Logistic regression becomes a convex MINLO, solved by outer approximation with lazy-constraint callbacks. The nonlinear step is a plain logistic regression on the flipped labels, and finite convergence is cited from Fletcher–Leyffer (pp. 10–12).
  - The SVM becomes an MIQO for an off-the-shelf solver (p. 14).
  - τ comes from two linear equations (pp. 14–15).
  - No theorem bounds the price.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Tables 1–3 give merit means by group for the data, SVM, DP-SVM, LR and DP-LR (pp. 18, 21, 24). Figures 2, 4 and 5 plot price curves of merit change against ε.
  - The price is small, for example under 0.15 SD for COMPAS, and negative for gender in LSAC (pp. 19–21, 24, 26).
  - Out-of-sample AUC: DP-SVM is at least SVM's on all sets, while DP-LR falls below LR on law students (0.856 vs 0.867) (Table 4, p. 26).
  - The OCT rules reach 0.80–0.94 accuracy (Table 5, p. 28).
  - Missing from the protocol: only glmnet for Julia v1.5 is named (p. 12); there is no solver version, run time or gap; and none of the fairness methods reviewed is run as a baseline.
  - Caption slips: Table 3 says race for a gender study (p. 24), and Figure 8 names the law dataset for credit data (p. 31).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: methodological (a label-flip MIO with merit constraints, plus an OCT tool for implementation), with three case studies. The headline is that the price of diversity is low and sometimes negative (pp. 1, 5, 33).
  - The conclusion states no limitations and no future work (p. 33).
  - Limits visible in the text: only two groups and only demographic parity, a fixed ε, an unreported δ, and merit proxies taken as given.

**Method links**:
- Method 3: evidence ✅, with a variant (pp. 16–26, 27–33).
  - Evidence: the property (diversity without loss of merit) is framed for human decision makers (pp. 5, 33); one knob (ε, with δ as the merit tolerance) is used; price curves are published, including negative prices; and the decision makers get OCT rules.
  - Variant: there is no solution-independent bound, and the knob is fixed rather than set from data, which is exactly the skill's correction to Method 3.
- Method 1: evidence ✅, with a variant (pp. 3–4, 8–14). A problem owned by the fairness-in-ML literature is written as an exact MIO (a convex MINLO solved by outer approximation, and an MIQO). Variant: no solve times or scale boundary are printed.
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 12, 26). Only vanilla LR and SVM are dueled, although incumbent fairness methods are reviewed (pp. 3–4). Global optimality is claimed for outer approximation (p. 12), but no certificate or gap is reported.
- Taste mark 5: evidence (pp. 27–33). Decisions about people come with interpretable rules.
- Pattern pool:
  - **explain the optimizer with an interpretable model** (pp. 27–33);
  - **adversary's budget turned into a repair budget** (new: robust classification's min-max label flips become min-min; pp. 4, 8–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a robust model's adversarial budget into a repair budget: flip a fixed number of labels per group to help the fit, solving jointly with the classifier (pp. 4, 8–9).
- Derive per-group flip budgets in closed form from the target parity gap, holding the total number of positives fixed (eq. 9, pp. 14–15).
- Write merit constraints as bounds on the mean z-score of those selected, and cross-multiply to make them linear (p. 10).
- Solve a convex MINLO by outer approximation with lazy cuts, where the subproblem for fixed binaries is the standard learner on modified labels (pp. 10–12).
- Fit a shallow three-class OCT (flip up, flip down, no change) on the optimizer's flips, and report its accuracy with the residual ε and δ (Table 5, p. 28).
