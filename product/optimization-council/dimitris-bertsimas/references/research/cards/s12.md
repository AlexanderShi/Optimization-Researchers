### S313 · A scalable algorithm for two-stage adaptive linear optimization (arXiv preprint 2018, arXiv 1807.02812)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–23 (body pp. 1–20, App. A proof pp. 20–21, App. B data pp. 21–22; references p. 23 skimmed).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Shimrit Shtern (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position**: RO line; builds on his own adaptive-optimization papers with de Ruiter (2016, duality) and Dunning (2016, AMIO partitions) (p. 3); the list gives no journal version.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + IO; AP test cases}: The problem is two-stage adaptive LO (1) with a mixed-integer first stage, continuous recourse and a polytope U (p. 2). The entry is a measured failure of the incumbent. Zeng–Zhao's column-and-constraint generation (CCG) scales, but it can return infeasible first-stage decisions when only Assumption 2 holds (some feasible first stage exists) rather than relatively complete recourse (Assumption 1). Zeng–Zhao acknowledge that a feasibility oracle is needed but provide none (pp. 2–3). Table 1 measures the failure: CCG is feasible in 69% (location-transportation), 66% (lot-sizing) and 0/3 (unit commitment) of cases. His own AMIO is feasible but slow (46% terminated, 0.5% gap) (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Duality Driven Benders Decomposition (DDBD) keeps CCG's loop but uses two oracles (pp. 9–10). Inside the loop runs a fast inexact one, F1: alternating maximization on the bilinear infeasibility problem (21), started at CCG's complementarity maximizer (Algorithm 3). A slow exact one, F2, is called only when the loop stops (Algorithm 2, step 4). F2 ("Dual Basis Cuts") partitions the dual uncertainty set W of the Bertsimas–de Ruiter dual (22) by optimal LP bases. Each cell gets an affine λ(w), made an LP by the robust counterpart (pp. 11–13).
- **D3 Model and data**: Fixed recourse B; uncertainty enters the constraints linearly through Cu; U = {Du ≤ d, u ≥ 0}; the problem is bounded (p. 2). Test sets are small (pp. 15, 17, 19, 21):
  - random location-transportation instances, N = L = 10, budget γ = 0.5, with capacity covering every demand in U;
  - lot-sizing, N = 5;
  - IEEE 14-bus unit commitment, with reserves dropped and 5% demand fluctuation.
  Admitted: for location-transportation, "the affine decision rule is often optimal or close to optimal" (p. 15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - CCG and its limits:
    - Prop. 1 restates CCG's complementarity oracle as the big-M MIO (9), valid only for feasible x (pp. 5–6).
    - Lemma 1 and Prop. 2 give finite termination under Assumption 1, by vertex counting and a Hausdorff–Lipschitz limit (p. 7; App. A pp. 20–21).
    - Prop. 3 shows the oracle underestimates Z for infeasible x, and its maximizer need not be a vertex (pp. 7–8).
    - An exact complementarity feasibility oracle (Corollary 1) does not solve in 20–30 min (p. 8).
  - DDBD:
    - Theorem 1: finite termination; the proof is omitted as similar to Prop. 2 (p. 9).
    - Prop. 4: F1 returns a vertex, with value at least CCG's (p. 11).
    - Prop. 5: an unbounded index-0 cell certifies infeasibility (p. 12).
    - Theorem 2: Dual Basis Cuts (DBC) terminates finitely, by counting bases, with K ≤ (d^{2^{d+l}} − 1)/(d − 1) (p. 14).
- **D5 Evaluation**: Julia v0.4.2 and Gurobi v6.5.1 on one core of a 32-processor AMD64 with 246.4 GB; stop at UL gap 10⁻³ or 1000 s; opponents CCG, AMIO and the authors' DBC (pp. 14–15). DDBD was always feasible, against 69% and 66% for CCG (Tables 2–3, pp. 16, 18). On unit commitment, AMIO and DBC returned nothing and CCG was always infeasible. DDBD's verification took 12,443 s against 375 s to optimality at T = 24 (p. 19; Table 4, p. 20). Admitted: DDBD is slower to low accuracy (p. 16), and on lot-sizing verification takes about as long as reaching optimality (6.32 s to verify vs 7.1 s to reach optimality, on average), "the cost of ensuring feasibility" (pp. 17–18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm with finite-convergence proofs (pp. 19–20). Limits printed: F1 carries no guarantee (p. 10), F2 dominates the run time at scale (p. 19), and the instances are small. The conclusion states no future directions (pp. 19–20).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 3, 9, 14–20): the incumbents (CCG, and his own AMIO) are the opponents. Solver version, machine, time limit and gap are stated, and failure rates and termination reasons are printed. The *find fast, certify later* split sits inside the algorithm: F1 in the loop, exact F2 at the end.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 8, 11–12): the dual is read for structure. In (22) x does not affect feasibility, and λ(w) is piecewise linear and independent of x, which makes an exact infeasibility certificate possible. Each cell stays an LP. The boundary is printed: the complementarity feasibility MIO does not solve.
- New-pattern candidate: a fast inexact oracle inside the loop and a slow exact oracle only at termination (pp. 9–11, 19–20).
- New-pattern candidate: constraint generation as the scaling engine (CCG/Benders loop, pp. 4–9).
- New-pattern candidate: an introduction table that measures the incumbent's failure rate before the method appears (Table 1, p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure the incumbent's failure rate on the standard test problems and put that table in the introduction, before your method (Table 1, p. 3).
- Split an expensive oracle in two: a cheap heuristic one inside the loop and an exact one called once at termination. Then report how often the exact one changed the answer; on unit commitment it never did (p. 19).
- Certify infeasibility of x by maximizing over the dual recession cone intersected with a box ((20)–(21)). This turns "unbounded dual" into a bounded bilinear problem (p. 10).
- Report time to optimality and time to verify separately (Table 3, p. 18; Table 4, p. 20).

**Quotes**:
- "This implies that the DDBD is much more scalable in terms of high accuracy solutions, but requires more time for low accuracy solutions." (p. 16)
- "We found that, for this problem and under this choice of parameters (as well as many others), the affine decision rule is often optimal or close to optimal." (p. 15)

### D017 · Optimal Explanations of Linear Models (CoRR 2019, arXiv 1907.04669)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–12 (body pp. 1–8, references pp. 9–10, App. A–B proofs pp. 10–11, App. C algorithm p. 12).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Arthur Delarue, Patrick Jaillet, Sébastien Martin (MIT, p. 1). Page 1 prints the first author as "Dimitris Bersimas", with the Sloan address dbertsim@mit.edu · **Position**: an 8-page 2019 preprint whose §3.3 is titled "The Price of Interpretability" (p. 5). SKILL.md Method 3 cites a separate arXiv 1907.03419 on the same theme; the two were not compared here.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO}: Interpretability is ill-defined and is usually replaced by proxies such as sparsity or integer coefficients (p. 1). Practitioners sometimes prefer more complex models they can explain, and EU law grants a right to explanation, not to sparse models (p. 1). The problem: decompose a fitted linear model into a sequence of simple steps, and measure how interpretable the best such sequence is (pp. 1–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: A coordinate path changes one coefficient per step, starting from 0. It explains β if it ends at β. The path loss Lα = Σ αk c(βk) weighs the costs of the intermediate models, and a model's loss is the loss of its best explanation (eq. (2), Definition 1, pp. 2–5). Setting αk = γ^k gives one knob γ, which trades incrementality against complexity (pp. 4–5). The Pareto front ("price of interpretability") is computed by weighted sums over path length K (Prop. 1, p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**: Linear models with a cost c(β) such as MSE (p. 2); Theorem 1 assumes c is bounded and nonnegative (p. 5). Coherence axioms (a)–(b) restrict losses to monotone functions of the cost sequence (p. 4). Data: a toy age-from-height-and-weight example with ρ = 0.9 (p. 2); a 100-row, 6-feature job-prestige set (p. 7); California test scores 1998–1999 (p. 7); Porto bike sharing, 731 days and 18 features (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Path length recovers sparsity: Lc(β) = the number of nonzeros (p. 3).
  - Theorem 1: as γ → ∞, models are ranked by complexity and then cost; as γ → 0, greedy-built models are the most interpretable (p. 5; proofs by bounding geometric sums, pp. 10–11).
  - Computation: (7) is a convex MIO with SOS-1 constraints on β_{k+1} − β_k, solvable by Gurobi or CPLEX "for small problems" (p. 6).
  - Local improvement separates the hard outer choice of indices i from the easy inner convex QP over the step values δ, resampling q steps at a time (eq. (10), pp. 6–7; Algorithm 1, p. 12).
- **D5 Evaluation**: The exact K = 10 path on the 100×6 set took 5.078 s. Local improvement took 0.004 s with a 0.02% gap (q = 1) and 0.019 s at the optimum (q = 2) (Table 2, p. 7). No solver version, machine or limit is stated. On California, four steps take the MSE from 0.122 to 0.097 (optimum 0.095), compared with stagewise and direct paths (Fig. 3, pp. 7–8). On bike sharing, the expected cost is 0.145 against 0.150 for a LASSO sequence (p. 8). All comparisons are in-sample.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: definitions, two results, an exact MIO and a heuristic (p. 2). Limits:
  - The weighted-sum method misses part of the front (dashed line, Fig. 2, p. 6).
  - Exact solves work only for small problems (p. 6).
  Next: other step functions (integer steps as in SLIM, tree splits) and human-in-the-loop selection (p. 8). There is no conclusion section, and the California model is said to "predict the number of trips" (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 4–6): the property (interpretability) gets one knob (γ). A theorem fixes what the knob's limits mean, and the price curve is printed with its bad region: the front is discontinuous and part of it is unreachable.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 2–3, 6): a concept owned by ML is written as an optimization problem, the incumbent proxy (sparsity) appears as a special case, and the exact problem is a convex MIO.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 6–8): the exact MIO certifies the heuristic on a small real instance, and greedy stagewise is the opponent. But the comparison is in-sample only, with no solver version, machine or limit.
- New-pattern candidate: recover the field's incumbent proxies as the two limits of a one-parameter family (sparsity at γ → ∞, greedy at γ → 0; pp. 3, 5).
- New-pattern candidate: inner-easy/outer-hard decomposition (a convex inner problem over δ, local search over the indices; p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- State coherence axioms first, then a one-parameter family that satisfies them. Then prove that the family's limits recover the field's two incumbent proxies (pp. 4–5).
- Use the exact MIO as a referee on a small real instance to certify a heuristic's gap before running the heuristic at scale (Table 2, p. 7).
- When the decision-maker's preferred sparsity is unknown, weight the path's steps by the probability that each level is chosen (αk = pk), so the path minimizes expected cost (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Recent EU legislation [8] guarantees citizens a “right to explanation,” not a right to be affected only by sparse models." (p. 1)
- "The coordinate path is willing to make some suboptimal steps in preparation for very cost-improving steps." (p. 7)

### S174 · Personalized treatment for coronary artery disease patients: a machine learning approach (Health Care Management Science 2020, DOI 10.1007/s10729-020-09522-4)
Read level: **full**: body pp. 1–32 (introduction, data, censoring, classification and regression models, ML4CAD, evaluation framework, results, discussion and limitations). References pp. 32–37 and the population tables (p. 38) were not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Agni Orfanoudaki (MIT ORC), Rory B. Weiner (MGH Cardiology) (p. 1) · **Position**: health-care prescriptive analytics (2019–2026 line). It reuses the group's OCT/ORT and optimization-based imputation (pp. 9, 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML + AP (health care)}: CAD guidelines use general risk factors but give no patient-specific way to choose among drugs, PCI and CABG, and subpopulations lack trials (p. 2). Two obstacles are named: counterfactual outcomes are unobserved, and EMR assignment is biased (p. 2). The goal is to choose the primary treatment that maximizes time to an adverse event (TAE) (pp. 5–6). Data: Boston Medical Center EMR for 1.1 million patients, 1982–2016, of whom 21,460 are included (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**: ML4CAD runs in five steps (pp. 19–20):
  1. Impute missing covariates.
  2. Impute TAE for censored patients by k-NN.
  3. Fit one regression model per method (ORT, CART, RF, linear, boosted trees) and per treatment.
  4. The methods vote for the treatment with the largest predicted TAE; ties are broken by R².
  5. Report the agreeing methods' average prediction.
  Unlike regress-and-compare, several models vote (pp. 4, 20). The evaluation lets each ML model in turn act as oracle for the observed treatment (the PR metric, pp. 22–23).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Five options (Table 2, p. 10); p = 31 covariates at diagnosis (pp. 7–9, 13); covariates more than 50% missing are dropped (p. 9).
  - 13,679 censored patients (62.9%) get k-NN TAE (k = 50, chosen by the downstream classifier's AUC). They stay in both training and testing (pp. 10–11, 13).
  - Separate models per treatment group avoid bias from current prescribing (p. 19).
  - Admitted: no randomization, no socioeconomic or preference data, an unrepresentative population (p. 31).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): No theorems. Prescription effectiveness (PE) is the estimated TAE under the recommendation minus the observed TAE (eqs. (3)–(4)). The prescription-robustness (PR) grid pairs estimation models with ground-truth models (eqs. (5)–(6)). An R²-like accuracy is computed where ML4CAD agrees with the physician (eqs. (7)–(8)), plus the degree of agreement among models (pp. 22–24). The censoring imputation is validated by artificially censoring known outcomes on [1, 3650] days: R² = 0.81 (p. 11).
- **D5 Evaluation**: 75/25 split; cross-validated depth and minimum bucket (pp. 13, 17). AUC: RF 84.29%, OCT 81.54%, boosted trees 81.43%, LR 80.83%, CART 73.33% (Table 4, p. 14). RF has the best R² for all five treatments (Table 6, p. 18). ML4CAD raises TAE by 24.11% (4.56 → 5.66 years) on PE. The Table 8 entries range from 0.485 to 1.283 years (p. 25). It agrees with physicians in 28.24% of cases and cuts PCI from 18.84% to 6.04% (p. 28). No OCT/ORT solver, gap or time is reported.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: a censoring method, interpretable predictors, a prescription algorithm, an evaluation framework and an online app (pp. 6, 20–21). Stated limits: observational data, estimated counterfactuals only, an unrepresentative population, coarse treatment categories, and disagreement with the standard of care in most cases (pp. 31–32). The paper calls ML4CAD's estimates "the most optimistic" (p. 25). Next: censoring by optimization rather than heuristics, and a clinical trial (p. 32). Slip: p. 14 calls OCT superior to "all other ML methods", while Table 4 on the same page puts RF ahead on AUC and accuracy.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 19–26, 31–32): decisions are scored against the hospital's standard of care and robustness is checked across model-based ground truths. Limits are listed, and a trial is named as the field test. But the decision score uses the voting models' own (maximal) estimates, and learning and deciding are not joint.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 13–14, 18–19): OCT and ORT beat greedy CART (AUC 81.54% vs 73.33%; ORT ahead in four of five tasks). But RF leads everywhere, and p. 14 still claims superiority over all (Tension T6). No certificates, solver or time limits are reported.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (pp. 1, 9, 13, 20–21): a student carries the programme's tools (OCT/ORT, optimization-based imputation) into a partner hospital's data, with a clinical coauthor and a physician-facing web app.
- New-pattern candidate: a grid of model-based ground truths for counterfactual evaluation (pp. 22–25).
- New-pattern candidate: validate censored-outcome imputation by censoring known outcomes artificially (p. 11).
- New-pattern candidate: a clinician-facing web app released with the paper (pp. 20–21; also S189).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When counterfactuals are unobservable, score the policy under every pair of estimation model and ground-truth model, and print the whole table (Table 8, p. 25).
- Validate a censoring imputation by re-censoring complete records at random times and scoring the imputation against the known outcome (p. 11).
- Fit outcome models per treatment group so the historical assignment is not learned as a feature (p. 19).
- Report the number of agreeing models per patient as a confidence signal (Table 10, p. 28).

**Quotes**:
- "Our approach does not guarantee optimality for the treatment selection problem." (p. 26)
- "The ultimate validation of our algorithm would be the realization of a clinical trial." (p. 32)

### S189 · Machine learning provides evidence that stroke risk is not linear: The non-linear Framingham stroke risk score (PLoS ONE 2020, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0232414)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–18 (article body pp. 1–17; author contributions p. 18). References pp. 18–20 were not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Agni Orfanoudaki (first and corresponding author), Emma Chesley and Christian Cadisch (MIT ORC); Barry Stein, Amre Nouh and Mark J. Alberts (Hartford HealthCare). Bertsimas is last author (p. 1) · **Position**: an application of OCT (SW4) to clinical risk scores, under a Hartford HealthCare grant (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP (health care)}: The Framingham stroke scores (FSRS and the revised R-FSRS) rest on Cox-type models that assume linear, additive risk factors; interaction terms inflate the feature space (p. 2). The entry is to replace the additive score with an interpretable nonlinear one built from Optimal Classification Trees, and to validate it externally (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Each Framingham offspring examination is one observation (18,793 visits from 4,385 people), but all of a person's visits go to the same side of the split (p. 3). MedImpute fills missing values (pp. 3, 6). OCT (depth ≤ 8, minimum bucket 20) is selected by iterating with a path-by-path review against the medical literature (pp. 3, 7). The score is validated on 17,527 BMC patients (p. 3) and delivered as an adaptive questionnaire app (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Participants are stroke-free, over 40 and uncensored within 10 years (p. 3). Covariates are the R-FSRS factors plus medication, ECG findings and procedures (Table 2, p. 5); the outcome is 10-year stroke (p. 5). The derivation cohort (FHS) is Caucasian; the validation cohort comes from hospital EHRs and is sicker (pp. 15–16). Admitted: the data are not i.i.d., and causality is not shown (pp. 16–17).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): n/a; there are no theorems. Evaluation: 10-fold CV; AUC with bootstrap 95% CIs over five random partitions; sensitivity and specificity at 0.5; Hosmer–Lemeshow χ² (p. 7). The FD2 sample (R-FSRS's own development data) sits entirely inside FD1's test set (p. 7).
- **D5 Evaluation**: Baselines: R-FSRS by sex, L1 logistic regression, CART, RF and XGBoost (p. 7).
  - FD1 test AUC: N-SRS 0.8743, R-FSRS 0.7374, RF 0.8829, XGBoost 0.8846 (Table 3, p. 9).
  - BMC AUC: 0.7403 vs 0.6491, with RF 0.7482 and XGBoost 0.7501 (Table 4, p. 11).
  - Calibration χ²: 1.96 vs 8.05 on FD1 (p. 9); 7.12 vs 36.66 on BMC (p. 11).
  - Julia 1.0 and R 3.5; code on GitHub (pp. 2, 7).
  - The abstract's validation AUCs (75.29% vs 65.93%) do not match Table 4 (0.7403 vs 0.6491) (pp. 1, 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a validated interpretable score with 23 risk profiles and new nonlinear findings on T-wave abnormality and hematocrit (pp. 1, 13–15). Stated limits: a Caucasian derivation cohort and a sicker validation cohort; no comparison with CHADS2 or CHA2DS2-VASc; prospective validation still "under evaluation" (pp. 15–17). Next: thresholds on N-SRS that warrant treatment (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (pp. 2–4, 11, 17): the baseline is the field's current tool (R-FSRS), the score is tested on an external partner population, and a prospective trial is planned. But the score is predictive (AUC, calibration), not a decision.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 7–9, 11, 13): OCT beats greedy CART (0.8743 vs 0.7981) but is matched, not beaten, by RF and XGBoost. The paper calls them "equivalent" on pp. 9 and 11, yet on p. 8 calls N-SRS superior to other established ML methods (Tension T6). No MIO gap, solver or time limit is given.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 18): a student-led clinical line funded by the partner (the grant covered student stipends). The author-contribution list credits Bertsimas with conceptualization, methodology, funding, resources, supervision and review, not the original draft.
- New-pattern candidate: one row per visit, with the split made by person (pp. 3, 13).
- New-pattern candidate: a clinician-facing app shipped with the paper (pp. 7–8, 15; also S174).

**Transferable techniques**:
- With a longitudinal cohort, enlarge the training set by treating each visit as a row, but keep all of a person's visits on one side of the split (pp. 3, 13).
- Put the incumbent score's own development sample wholly inside your test set, so that both scores are compared out of sample (p. 7).
- Review every tree path against the clinical literature during model selection, alongside the hyperparameter search (pp. 3, 7).

**Quotes**:
- "We strictly require that observations from the same individual belong in at most one of these two sets, avoiding potential natural boosts in the downstream performance." (p. 13)
- "We also acknowledge prospective validation of this model would outperform validation of blinded data sets" (p. 17)

### S254 · Scalable holistic linear regression (Operations Research Letters 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2020.02.008)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13 (arXiv:1902.03272v2, 4 Mar 2020, labelled "Preprint submitted to Operation Research Letters"; not the ORL print).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position**: continues the holistic-regression programme of Bertsimas & King (OR 2016) (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO}: In the 2016 holistic MIO, sparsity, group sparsity and pairwise collinearity are explicit constraints. Significance and multicollinearity are checked afterwards by bootstrap and condition number, and the resulting cutting-plane loop does not scale beyond n in the low 100s (pp. 1, 3). Two gaps are named: significance is tested after fitting, and VIF or condition-number constraints are only sufficient signs of multicollinearity, not necessary ones (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two devices, both added to one MIQO:
  - Significance becomes a-priori big-M constraints (7)–(8) built on the asymptotic normality of least squares. They are enforced lazily at integer-feasible solutions (pp. 4–5).
  - Multicollinearity is read off the eigenvectors of XᵀX with eigenvalues below ε (Theorem 2). Each relation becomes a cut Σ_{i∈Supp(a)} z_i ≤ |Supp(a)| − 1 from a minimum-support vector in their span; an MIO (11) finds these vectors and is re-solved until it is infeasible (Algorithm 1, pp. 6, 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**: Errors are i.i.d. with mean 0 and variance σ², and XᵀX/n → Q is invertible (Theorem 1, after Eicker 1963). Significance is tested "when n large enough" (p. 4). Relations follow Definition 1, with an intercept added (p. 5); the algorithm ignores the perturbation b of Theorem 2 (p. 8). Data: synthetic designs with planted 3–10-variable relations (p. 10), and five UCI sets described as "randomly selected" (n 568–9,358, p ≤ 125) (pp. 10, 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Theorem 2 is a weak equivalence between ε-relations and vectors near the small-eigenvalue span, proved in the eigenbasis (pp. 6–7). The tightest big-M for (12), M = 1/√m, comes from a norm inequality (p. 9).
- **D5 Evaluation**:
  - Algorithm 1 alone (n = 1000): 100% accuracy throughout, but a false-positive rate up to 24% and 5,940.56 s at p = 1000 with ten relations (Table 1, p. 10).
  - Full framework against Lasso, the 2016 bootstrap method, Tamura's MISDO (SCIP 6.0.0 + SCIP-SDP 3.1.1) and Chung's MIQO. Setup: Julia 1.0 and Gurobi 8.0 on an i7-5820k with 16 GB; 60/20/20 split (p. 11).
  - Results: every selected variable is significant and all relations are avoided, in 50–725 s. Lasso has lower test loss on four of five sets, using more variables. MISDO returns nothing on Air Quality and Crime, and the bootstrap method runs 39,870–60,000 s (Table 3, p. 12; p. 13).
  - No optimality gaps are reported.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: the significance formulation, the eigenvector theory, and scaling from the low 100s to about 10,000 (pp. 2, 12). There is no limitations section. Visible limits: asymptotic significance (p. 4), false positives at p = 1000 (p. 10) and Lasso's lower loss (p. 12). The largest n tested is 9,358, against the abstract's "10,000s" (pp. 1, 12). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 4–5, 8–9): statistics' post-hoc checks become a-priori constraints of one MIQO, through lazy significance constraints and eigenspace cuts, so the solver class is kept.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (pp. 1–3): an explicit continuation ("we continue the research program initiated in [1]"), carried by a new student, with the programme's aim restated (regression "more of an art than science").
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 11–13): the incumbents are dueled with solver versions and machine stated. But there are no gaps or certificates, and the win is reframed from test loss to sparsity and significance.
- Also Heuristic 1: the Γ‖β‖₁ term "models robustness", citing Bertsimas–Copenhaver (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: constraint generation as the scaling engine (lazy significance constraints; iterated multicollinearity cuts) (pp. 5, 9).
- New-pattern candidate: turn a post-hoc statistical test into an a-priori lazy constraint (pp. 2, 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a post-fit diagnostic (significance, multicollinearity) into a lazy constraint checked only at integer-feasible incumbents (p. 5).
- To exclude a family of bad supports, compute the near-null eigenspace once, then generate minimum-support vectors in it with a small MIO; each vector gives the strongest cut (pp. 8–9).
- Derive the tightest big-M from norm inequalities instead of guessing it (p. 9).

**Quotes**:

### S288 · Certifiably optimal sparse inverse covariance estimation (Mathematical Programming 2020, DOI 10.1007/s10107-019-01419-7)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–23, the whole body (§4.2–4.3 subproblem algorithms skimmed); App. A.1 assumptions (p. 24); App. C (pp. 29–30). The proofs (pp. 24–29), App. B, the App. D figures (pp. 30–33) and the references were not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jourdain Lamperski, Jean Pauphilet (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position**: carries the best-subset line (SW4) to precision matrices, as cited on p. 4.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO + CP}: Precision matrices need sparsity: Σ is singular when n < p, and sparse models are readable (pp. 1–2). The incumbent ℓ1 surrogate (graphical lasso) inherits Lasso's bias (pp. 2, 5), and SCAD and MCP give no optimality guarantee (p. 5). The paper solves the cardinality-constrained MLE (3) itself, claiming the first provably exact approach (p. 4). It argues that ℓ1 methods mainly buy robustness (pp. 2, 6–7).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Binary supports Z, with a convex inner covariance-selection problem h(Z) (pp. 7–8).
  - Since h is piecewise constant, a regularizer (big-M or ridge) smooths it. By duality (Theorem 2), h̃ becomes a maximum of functions linear in Z, with explicit subgradients (pp. 8–9).
  - Outer approximation with lazy callbacks, warm-started by Glasso or MB, gives a feasible Z and a lower bound at every iteration (Algorithm 3.1, pp. 9–10).
  - Cuts are reused over a decreasing grid of k (p. 11). Big-M is recast as "a smoothing technique", with ridge as the alternative (p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: Gaussian data and the empirical covariance; the diagonal is excluded from the count (pp. 1, 4, 7). The regularizer satisfies A1–A3 (p. 24). Ridge gives an upper approximation; big-M is exact when M is valid (p. 8). Data:
  - synthetic precision matrices following [3], with condition number p and n = p (p. 16);
  - a breast-cancer gene-expression set: 133 patients, 113 genes kept after t-test screening (pp. 20–21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Theorem 1.A/B: norm regularization equals a min–max problem with noise on Σ, proved by dual norms (p. 6).
  - Theorem 2: the dual of the inner problem gives convexity in Z and the cut (7) (pp. 8–9; proof not read).
  - Finite convergence follows from generalized Benders theory (p. 10).
  - Parameter scales M0 and γ0 come from norm bounds on Θ* (p. 11).
- **D5 Evaluation**:
  - Subproblem solvers: block coordinate descent on the primal is usually best; BFGS is 50–100× slower and does not finish in 12 h for p > 1000 (pp. 14–15).
  - Synthetic p = 200, tuned on validation likelihood (Table 1, p. 17): k* = 199 vs 796 for MB and Glasso; FDR 0.049 vs 0.68–0.75; test −LL 141 vs 157–162; 200–350 s vs 1–4 s. Under BIC tuning (Table 2): k* 194 vs 276 and 542. MB stays the most accurate (p. 17).
  - Gaps: stop at 10⁻⁴ or 5 min. The large M or γ needed make gaps exceed 100%, so most runs report none (pp. 16–17).
  - Certification appears only in App. C: p ≤ 200, M = 0.5 set from the ground truth, on 4 Xeon E5-2690 v4 CPUs (pp. 29–30).
  - Breast cancer: MCC 0.460 vs CLIME's 0.506, but sparser (Table 4, pp. 21–22).
  - The master MIO solver and its version are not named in the parts read.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions as listed (pp. 2–3, 23). Stated limits: certificates are costly in the 1000s (p. 3); time grows steeply with M and γ (p. 16); big-M is harder to scale than ridge (p. 20). Extensions add support, degree, hub and tree constraints on Z (pp. 22–23). The Summary gives no future-work paragraph (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 6–10): a statistics estimator is written exactly as a binary problem over supports, and dualizing the inner problem yields the cuts. The robust reading of ℓ1 (Theorem 1) is Heuristic 1 in full text.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 3, 10, 16–17, 29–30): there is an incumbent warm start (MB, Glasso), a lower bound at every iteration and a stated time limit. But the title's *certifiably optimal* is backed by certificates only on small instances, while the headline runs carry no gaps (Tension T1). The paper says so openly.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (p. 4): the "why now" is asserted (decades of MIO progress) without a rebuilt figure.
- New-pattern candidate: constraint generation as the scaling engine (outer approximation with lazy callbacks; cuts reused across k) (pp. 9–11).
- New-pattern candidate: big-M read as a regularizer that smooths the value function, with ridge as the alternative (pp. 7–8, 20, 23); bears on Tension T4.
- New-pattern candidate: inner-easy/outer-hard decomposition (the combinatorial part separated from the SDP part, pp. 5–6, 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a big-M is hard to choose, treat it as a regularizer, run ridge on the same cut machinery, and report which one scales (pp. 7–8, 20).
- Reuse every outer-approximation cut across the hyperparameter grid by solving for decreasing k; the cuts do not depend on k (p. 11).
- Derive starting regularization scales from a priori norm bounds on the optimum (p. 11).
- State where gaps become uninformative and which experiments therefore carry none (pp. 16–17).

**Quotes**:

- "To avoid such unrealistic assumptions, robustness and sparsity should, in our opinion, be considered as two distinct properties and be treated as such." (p. 7)

### S301 · Fast exact matrix completion: A unified optimization framework for matrix completion (Journal of Machine Learning Research 2020, arXiv 1910.09092)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–6 (introduction, reformulation, Prop. 1) and pp. 11–26 (fastImpute, the theory statements, complexity, all experiments, conclusion). Not read: §3 projImpute (pp. 6–10), App. A proofs (pp. 31–43) and the references.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position**: follows Bertsimas & Li (2018), *Interpretable matrix completion* (pp. 3, 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML}: Low-rank matrix completion, with and without side information. Nuclear-norm relaxations and Burer–Monteiro gradient methods dominate, and guarantees for the factored methods often need special regularizers (p. 2). Inductive methods mostly assume perfectly predictive side information (p. 3). The entry is a unified formulation in which the plain problem is the case B = I (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Write X = U Sᵀ Bᵀ with ‖S‖ = 1 and add a Tikhonov term on U, justified as robustness via Bertsimas–Copenhaver. U is eliminated in closed form row by row, and descent runs on S only (Prop. 1, p. 5). Each step is a gradient move on the sphere with Nesterov acceleration. fastImpute samples n0 rows and m0 columns per step (Algorithm 2, pp. 11–12). What changes from Burer–Monteiro: U is a function of S, not a second gradient block (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**: The analysis covers only 1/γ = 0 and B = I, with Gaussian noise added to the gradient. It assumes row sampling at rate r > Ck/m and Assumptions 1–2 (submatrix full rank), which exclude spiky matrices (pp. 13–16). Uniform sampling costs an extra log n factor (pp. 14, 17). Data: uniform [0,1] factors with 20–95% of entries missing (pp. 19, 22–23); Netflix (471,268 × 14,538) with 59 TMDB features (p. 21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Theorem 1: the sampled objective and gradient concentrate (adapted from Bertsimas–Li 2018) (pp. 12–13).
  - Lemma 3: with 1/γ = 0 the gradient is orthogonal to S, so the method is plain SGD (p. 15).
  - Theorem 3: the objective is strict-saddle with no spurious local minima. With Fang et al.'s SGD result, this gives δ-closeness to a global minimum in poly(1/δ) iterations (Corollaries 2–3, pp. 14–17; proofs not read).
  - Cost per step: O((pk + k³) n log n / α) (p. 18).
- **D5 Evaluation**:
  - Protocol: fastImpute untuned (tmax = 50, θ = π/64, γ = 10⁶); every baseline tuned (IMC, softImpute-ALS, softImpute-SVD, MFSGD, GROUSE). Hardware is only "a server with 16 CPU cores"; ten runs per setting, and a 20-hour cap shown as N/A (pp. 19–20, 23, 25).
  - Large synthetic settings: about 10% of the baselines' time and "∼ 40 − 70%" lower MAPE. Table 4 ranges from −15% (GROUSE) to −95% (SISVD) (pp. 24–25). With 20–50% of entries missing, IMC and softImpute are more accurate (pp. 20, 25).
  - Netflix: 2,934 s and 20.7% MAPE vs IMC's 7,605 s and 21.0%; without side information, 2,098 s and 13.8% vs softImpute-ALS's 38,256 s and 20.1% (pp. 22, 26).
  - The implementations are in different languages (pp. 19, 23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: the separable reformulation, the algorithm and its guarantee, and the scaling results (pp. 3–4, 26). Limits stated in passing: cubic cost in k, the O(nm) fill step dominates at large sizes (p. 18), and rows with fewer than k entries need regularization (p. 17). The guarantee is proved for B = I and extended to general B by assertion (p. 13). The conclusion states no limits or future work (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 2, 5): formulation first (eliminate a block; unify the side-information and plain problems), but the engine is a custom stochastic first-order method, not a solver class.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 19–26): the incumbents are dueled on known-truth synthetic data and on Netflix, with tuned baselines, an untuned own method and the cap printed. But there is no certificate: *exact* names a recovery guarantee under assumptions.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (pp. 3, 5, 17): continues Bertsimas–Li 2018 with the same student, and uses the robustness–regularization equivalence (Heuristic 1) to justify the ℓ2 term.
- New-pattern candidate: inner-easy/outer-hard elimination (pp. 2, 5).
- New-pattern candidate: tune the baselines, not your own method, and say so (pp. 19, 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Eliminate the block that has a closed-form optimum, normalize the remaining block to remove scaling invariance, and descend only on that block (pp. 2, 4–5).
- Prove global convergence in two steps: show the objective is strict-saddle with no spurious local minima, then cite a theorem that SGD escapes saddles (pp. 13–15).
- Leave your own method untuned while tuning every baseline, and state it (pp. 19, 23).
- Label comparisons with methods built for another setting (online algorithms) as scaling-only (p. 24).

**Quotes**:
- "We explicitly stress here that no parameter tuning is done on fastImpute as we intend to show that the algorithm is not parameter sensitive." (p. 19)
- "We note that online matrix completion is usually used in different settings than offline algorithms and such comparison is only to note the favorable scaling behavior of fastImpute." (p. 24)

### S302 · On polyhedral and second-order cone decompositions of semidefinite optimization problems (Operations Research Letters 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2019.12.003)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–9 (arXiv:1910.03143v2, 2 Dec 2019, labelled "Preprint submitted to Operations Research Letters"; not the ORL print).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ryan Cory-Wright (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position**: a CP methods letter alongside the group's 2019 decomposition work, citing Bertsimas–Cory-Wright–Pauphilet 2019 and Berk–Bertsimas 2019 (p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP; ML applications}: SDO is polynomial by interior-point methods (IPMs) but memory-bound in practice: MOSEK cannot solve constrained instances with n > 250 on a laptop. The paper calls this theory–practice gap "optimization folklore" (p. 1). Kelley's cutting-plane method for SDO is exponentially slow in the worst case and performed poorly in an earlier LP-initialized implementation (p. 1). The entry: bound Kelley's iterations by the initial region's diameter, trace the poor performance to the LP start, and start from an SOC approximation instead (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Start from the SOC relaxation that imposes all 2×2 minors (3), not the LP diagonal-dominance one (2). Refine with trailing-eigenvalue cuts (Prop. 5) or new nuclear-norm cuts (Props. 6–7) (pp. 2–4; Algorithm 1, p. 5). The nuclear-norm cuts keep the problem in its original space instead of lifting it by Schur complements (pp. 4, 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: Assumption 1 is a trace bound tr(X) ≤ T, natural in sparse PCA, or imposed as a big-M. The method needs an exact separation oracle and Lipschitz cuts (p. 5). Remark 2 offers a strongly convex regularizer instead, and strong duality is not needed (pp. 5–6). Data: Pitprops, communities and arrhythmia (UCI), plus a 2,130-stock Wilshire 5000 correlation matrix (p. 6); synthetic rank-10 matrices with half the entries observed (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Diameter bounds: 2nT for the LP approximation, T for the SOC one (Props. 1, 3). Via the eigenvalue–trace bound, λmin ≥ T/n − nT for LP against 2T/n − T for SOC (Props. 2, 4) (pp. 2–3). Theorem 1: Kelley's method needs at most (LT/ε + 1)^{n²} iterations, by a ball-packing volume argument "essentially due to [16]"; the bound depends on the initial region through T (p. 5).
- **D5 Evaluation**: Julia 1.0, MOSEK 9.0 and JuMP 0.18.5 on one Xeon E5-2690 v4 with 32 GB and 12 threads (p. 6).
  - Sparse PCA bound gaps, measured against Berk–Bertsimas feasible solutions (60 s limit): SOC 6.60/0.83/3.01/0.48%; with 20 cuts 1.11/0.75/1.54%; SDO 1.09/0.75/n/a (Tables 2–3, pp. 6–7).
  - The SDO takes 233.6 s on communities against 2.8 s for SOC, and cannot run on arrhythmia within 32 GB (p. 6).
  - Nuclear-norm minimization: faster than MOSEK for n ≥ 100; MOSEK runs out of memory at n ≥ 250; n = 500 solves in 1,245 s (five instances; pp. 7–8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: a diagnosis through the worst-case bound, an initialization choice, a new cut class and two applications (pp. 1–2). Limits: n² SOC constraints are too many above about n = 2000, so a weaker aggregated relaxation is used for Wilshire (p. 6); the SVD costs Ω(n³) (p. 8). Next: an inexact sketching oracle (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (pp. 1, 5, 8): an inherited belief (Kelley's method is too slow for SDO; complexity theory as the test) is re-measured with today's solver, and conceded only for IPMs. But the "why now" is a diagnosis through the bound's constant, not a solver × hardware figure.
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 2–4, 6–8): the SDO is kept in the SOC-plus-linear class by outer approximation and cuts, and the nuclear norm is handled in the original space. The boundary is printed (n > 2000; SVD cost).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (pp. 6–8): bound gaps are reported per data set against the best feasible solution, and the incumbent IPM is dueled on time and memory, with n/a printed where it fails.
- New-pattern candidate: diagnose an incumbent algorithm's practical failure through the constant in its worst-case bound, then fix the initialization (pp. 1–2, 5).
- New-pattern candidate: constraint generation as the scaling engine (pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Read a slow method's worst-case bound for the constant that depends on your design choice (here the initial diameter), and change that choice (pp. 1–2, 5).
- Compare relaxations by their worst-case violation of the dropped constraint (λmin bounds via the eigenvalue–trace inequality), not only by tightness on instances (pp. 2–3).
- Replace a lifted reformulation with a semi-infinite one in the original space, whose most-violated cut is a cheap linear-algebra step (an SVD) (pp. 3–4, 7).

**Quotes**:
- "it is optimization folklore that there is a gap between SDOs theoretical and practical tractability." (p. 1)
- "while the traditional complexity theory accurately describes the performance of IPMs, it is too conservative with respect to cutting-plane methods." (p. 8)
