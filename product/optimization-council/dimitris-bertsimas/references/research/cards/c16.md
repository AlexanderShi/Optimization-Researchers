### S010 · Best subset selection via a modern optimization lens (The Annals of Statistics 2016, DOI 10.1214/15-aos1388; arXiv 1507.03133)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:1507.03133v1): main text pp. 1–42 in full; appendices A–D (pp. 48–63) read, proofs followed but not re-derived line by line; references (pp. 43–47) skimmed. The AoS print was not read. This file's speed-up figure is 200 billion (pp. 1, 8); the skill cites about 450 billion for "AoS 2016, p. 819".
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Angela King (MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1), Rahul Mazumder (MIT Sloan and ORC; "A major part of the work was performed when R.M. was at Columbia University", p. 42) · **Position in trajectory**: the skill's SW4. It replaces the group's own tailored branch-and-bound of 2009 ([3], Bertsimas–Shioda; card S040 in c12), now called "less scalable than the methods presented in this paper" (p. 5), with a general MIO solver, and it is paired with Bertsimas–Mazumder 2014 (least quantile regression) as joint evidence that MIO suits statistics (p. 41).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO; HS as a heuristic warm start}: The entry is an inherited intractability verdict. Best subset selection (1) is NP-hard, leaps in R does not scale beyond p = 30, and the problem "has been widely dismissed as being intractable by the greater statistical community" (p. 2); optimal solutions of (1) and of its Lagrangian form (4) are "deemed as intractable" (p. 5). The rivals' gaps: Lasso's good behaviour rests on restricted-eigenvalue or compatibility conditions that are hard to verify, and under noise and correlation it shrinks coefficients and brings in noise variables (p. 3); nonconvex penalties (MCP, SCAD, Sparsenet) come with no guarantee of closeness to the global minimum (p. 4). He keeps the cardinality-constrained form because k fixes the sparsity exactly and (1) has better statistical properties than (4) according to [48] (pp. 4–5), and extends it to polyhedral side constraints Aβ ≤ b and the least-absolute-deviation loss (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A discrete first-order method (gradient step, then hard thresholding to the k largest entries) finds good solutions in seconds. These warm-start a MIQO solver that improves them and supplies lower bounds, so the method gives "a guarantee on its suboptimality even if we terminate the algorithm early" (abstract, p. 1; contributions 1–2, p. 6). What changes relative to the closest classical methods: a general MIO solver with data-derived bounds replaces tailored enumerators (leaps and bounds, Furnival–Wilson 1974; Bienstock 1996; Bertsimas–Shioda 2009; p. 5), and the thresholding algorithm covers any smooth convex loss under a cardinality constraint without the coherence or RIP assumptions of iterative hard thresholding (p. 18). What stays: the classical estimator (1) itself. Why now: the MIO speed-up (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Linear model, columns standardized to zero mean and unit ℓ2 norm (p. 2). Class: MIQO with binaries z, by big-M (5) or by SOS-1 (8), plus bounded variants (9)–(11) with constants MU, Mℓ, Mζ (pp. 9–13). Assumptions adopted for tractability: valid bounds on β and Xβ. They are provable from coherence and restricted eigenvalues (Theorem 2.1 needs µ[k−1] < 1; p. 15) or from convex QPs with an upper bound UB (pp. 16–17); otherwise they come from a warm start, MU := τ‖β̂_hyb‖∞ with τ ∈ {1.1, 1.5, 2}, offered as "excellent parameter specifications in practice" but not provable (p. 17). For the algorithm: g convex with ℓ-Lipschitz gradient and L > ℓ (pp. 18, 22); Proposition 6(e) needs bounded k-sparse level sets, which for least squares means every k-column submatrix has full rank (p. 22). Data: Gaussian designs with Σij = ρ^|i−j| or Σ = I and four β0 patterns (Examples 1–4), SNR = var(x′β0)/σ² (p. 26); Diabetes with second-order interactions (p = 64, random subsample n = 350; p. 26); Leukemia reduced to the 1,000 features most correlated with the response out of more than 3,000 (n = 72), with a semi-synthetic response of five unit coefficients at SNR = 7 (pp. 26–27); double-exponential errors for the LAD study (p. 39).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Convex hull of the big-M set = {‖β‖∞ ≤ MU, ‖β‖1 ≤ MU·k}; hence constrained Lasso (7) is a weaker relaxation than the root-node relaxation (6), with Z3 ≤ Z2 ≤ Z1 (pp. 9–10). "The choice of MU affects the strength of the formulation" (p. 9); the SOS-1 formulation (8) avoids big-M altogether (p. 10).
  - Two equivalent bounded MIQOs chosen by the size of the quadratic form: (9), with a p×p form, for n > p; (10), with ζ = Xβ and an n×n form, for p ≫ n (pp. 12–13).
  - Bounds from compressed-sensing quantities through (1,1) operator norms (Propositions 1–2, Theorem 2.1, pp. 14–15; proofs pp. 49–52). The authors note that Theorem 2.1 has "a purely computational motivation" yet involves the same coherence and restricted-eigenvalue quantities as ℓ1 theory (p. 15). Alternatively, bounds come from 2p and 2n convex QPs (max/min βi or ⟨xi, β⟩ subject to loss ≤ UB), each solved through one scalar multiplier, all in parallel (pp. 16–17, 48–49), or from warm starts (p. 17).
  - Discrete first-order method: hard thresholding is the exact projection onto the ℓ0 ball (Proposition 3, p. 19; proof p. 55); the descent-lemma upper bound QL (Proposition 4, p. 19) turns each step into Hk(β − ∇g(β)/L) (Algorithm 1, p. 20); Algorithm 2 adds a line search (p. 20).
  - Convergence: sufficient decrease (L − ℓ)/2·‖βm+1 − βm‖² (Proposition 6(a), p. 22; proof pp. 52–53); the support settles after finitely many iterations when αk > 0, by contradiction with (48) (pp. 22, 53); fixed-point uniqueness and global minimizers as stationary points (Propositions 7–8, p. 23; proofs p. 55); an O(1/ε) rate to ε-stationarity by telescoping (Theorem 3.1, p. 23; proof p. 56); a linear rate once the support settles, if g is strongly convex on it (p. 23).
  - Polishing on the active set (p. 24); Nesterov smoothing of the LAD loss with continuation τ ← 0.8τ (p. 25); a bounding-box formulation (37) with ℓ1 balls around an incumbent in β or Xβ (p. 34); a k-path solved by callbacks that add the cut Σz ≤ k (pp. 31–32).
  - No new statistical theory: Appendix C reviews known risk bounds for (1) and for the thresholded Lasso (pp. 56–58).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Gurobi 5.5 through Matlab and Python 2.7, the first-order code in Matlab 2012b; an 8-core Xeon (1.80 GHz, 16 GB) for n > p and a 16-core Xeon (3.1 GHz, 128 GB) for p ≫ n (p. 27).
  - Upper bounds: three arms on the same instances (Algorithm 2 with 50 random starts; MIO cold; MIO warm), scored by relative accuracy (falg − f*)/f* and time to best within 500 s (Tables 1–2, pp. 27–28, 33); Leukemia runs of 4,000 s (p. 32; Figure 5, p. 33).
  - Certificates: gap curves, warm vs cold (Figures 2–3, pp. 11, 29). On Diabetes (n = 350, p = 64) optima appear in seconds and certificates take 10–20 minutes (p. 11).
  - Statistics: Lasso (glmnet 1.7.3), Sparsenet, stepwise (Stats 3.0.2) and two debiased-Lasso variants, each tuned on a held-out validation set. Scored by prediction error ‖Xβ̂ − Xβ0‖²/‖Xβ0‖² and number of nonzeros, averaged over ten instances (pp. 29–30; Appendix D.2, pp. 58–63); LAD-Lasso for the LAD loss (p. 40). The MIO k-path stops each k at a 1% gap or 15 minutes, at default solver settings although tuning gave 2–4× speed-ups (pp. 31–32).
  - Failures and limits printed in the body: lower bounds "evolve" slowly for p ≫ n (p. 34); more correlation means longer runs (p. 35); boxes in Xβ make the problem harder (Figure 7, p. 36); Lasso gives the best predictive model at the smallest SNR of Example 2 and is marginally better at small SNR in Examples 2–3 (pp. 38–39); Sparsenet is marginally better in a few instances (p. 30); for LAD, MIO and the first-order method "behave similarly" (p. 40).
  - Not stated [my reading]: which §2.3 bounds (provable or warm-start) the certified runs used (p. 28; Figure 3 caption, p. 29); the MIO time limit in the p ≫ n statistical study, whose grid of ten k values in [3, 2k0] includes the true k0 (p. 37). The headline certified scale, "n in the 1000s and p in the 100s" (pp. 1, 6), has no table of certification times in this version: what is shown is Diabetes (p = 64), and runs at n = 1000, p = 50 were solved "to provable optimality" but "We do not report the experiments for brevity" (pp. 30–31). No code release is mentioned (text searched for `code`, `software` and `available`).
- **D6 Positioning**: Respectful toward Lasso ("a substantial amount of impressive work", p. 3), followed by a list of its documented shortcomings, citing the statistics literature itself for the gap between best subset and Lasso (pp. 3–4). Nonconvex-penalty methods are credited as statistically useful but uncertified; the opponents include Sparsenet, a method of coauthor Mazumder (pp. 4, 29–30). Optimization precedents: leaps and bounds (p ≤ 30), Bienstock 1996 and his own 2009 branch-and-bound, called less scalable (p. 5); Blumensath–Davies iterative hard thresholding as the closest algorithm (p. 18). The speed-up is taken from Bixby 2012 and Nemhauser 2013 (pp. 7–8); theory from Raskutti–Wainwright–Yu and Zhang–Wainwright–Jordan backs the empirical gap (pp. 57–58).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract opens on the speed-up figure, then the method, then the scale numbers (p. 1). The word "astonishing" appears four times (pp. 1, 7, 7, 41) and "to the best of our knowledge" three times, once with a first-time claim (pp. 2, 5, 6). Tractability is redefined twice as solve time at application scale (pp. 2, 5), and the old belief is conceded as justified when formed (p. 8). A motivating bound-evolution figure comes before the formal development (Figure 2, pp. 10–11), and the results prose hedges ("seems", "marginally", pp. 30, 38–39). Headline vs table [my reading]: the debiased Lasso is called "inferior to MIO by orders of magnitude" (p. 60) and MIO's models "orders of magnitude sparser" (Table 5 caption, p. 61), but the tabulated ratios are at most about 3.5 for prediction error (Tables 6 and 8, pp. 62–63) and about 2 for model size (Tables 5 and 7, pp. 61–62).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Four stated contributions: a certified MIO with side constraints and the LAD loss; a discrete first-order framework with convergence theory; computation at the stated scales; an empirical study of best-subset statistics (p. 6). Limits stated: hours to certify for p ≫ n (p. 6), slow lower bounds (p. 34), "higher, but still reasonable, computational times" (p. 41). Future work: the statistical properties of sub-optimal solutions of (1) (p. 58). [my reading] The promise that MIO "always provides a guarantee on its sub-optimality even if the algorithm is terminated early" (p. 41) holds for the bounded problem (11). It carries over to (1) only with the provable bounds of §2.3.1–2.3.2 (p. 17), and the box runs certify optimality only "inside that bounding box" (p. 34).

**Method links**:

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Characterize the convex hull of your MIO's constraint set to show that the rival's convex estimator is a weaker relaxation of your root node; print the ordering of bounds (Z3 ≤ Z2 ≤ Z1, pp. 9–10).
- (D4) Tighten big-M constants from the data: with the warm start's objective as UB, solve max/min βi (and ⟨xi, β⟩) subject to loss ≤ UB; each is a convex QP with a one-multiplier closed form, and all run in parallel (pp. 16–17, 48–49).
- (D4) Between equivalent MIO formulations, choose by the dimension of the quadratic form: p×p for n > p, n×n through ζ = Xβ for p ≫ n (p. 13).
- (D4) Discrete projected gradient for cardinality constraints: hard-threshold the gradient step; prove sufficient decrease with L > ℓ, finite support identification by contradiction, and an O(1/ε) stationarity rate by telescoping; then polish on the active set (pp. 18–24, 52–56).
- (D4) For a non-smooth loss, smooth it (Nesterov) and run a continuation τ ← 0.8τ, warm-starting each stage (p. 25).
- (D5) Report three arms on the same instances (heuristic alone, MIO cold, MIO warm) with relative accuracy to the best known value and time to best within a fixed limit (Tables 1–2, pp. 28, 33); plot upper bound, lower bound and gap over time (Figures 2–3, pp. 11, 29).
- (D5) Solve the whole k-path in one model with solver callbacks that add Σz ≤ k as k decreases, with a per-k stop rule (1% gap or 15 minutes) (pp. 31–32).
- (D5) Run at default solver settings and disclose the tuning speed-up you forgo (2–4×), so that times read as tractability evidence, not benchmarks (p. 32).
- (D5) When global bounds stall, add ℓ1 balls around an incumbent and certify inside them; vary the radius and report that the optimum does not move (pp. 34–36, 59).
- (D5) Score sparse estimators by prediction error relative to the true signal and by support size at validation-tuned complexity, over grids of SNR and correlation ρ (pp. 26, 30).

### S076 · Fairness and collaboration in network air traffic flow management: An optimization approach (Transportation Science 2016, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2014.0567)
Read level: **full**, 2011 working-paper version: pp. 1–36 in full, including the appendix proof (pp. 35–36); references (pp. 37–38) skimmed. The file is titled "A Proposal for Network Air Traffic Flow Management Incorporating Fairness and Airline Collaboration" and dated April 25, 2011 (p. 1): same authors, same two-stage models and the same headline of under 30 minutes for up to 25 airports (p. 1). The published abstract in abstracts.json is reworded (for example, Stage 1 is called "a natural generalization of RBS"); the TS print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shubham Gupta (MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1); funded by NSF Grant EFRI-0735905 (p. 1); data from Lincoln Labs (p. 35) · **Position in trajectory**: a late item in his air-transportation line. It adapts the Bertsimas–Stock-Patterson model (OR 1998) and cites the group's line from Vranas–Bertsimas–Odoni (1994) to Bertsimas–Lulli–Odoni (2008) (pp. 2, 5); a co-authored paper by Barnhart, Bertsimas, Caramanis and Fearing, introduced with "Simultaneously", treats fairness another way (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + IO}: The entry is a theory–practice gap: ATFM research "has not been fully implemented in practice", for two diagnosed reasons. Network models take a central decision-maker's view without the airlines, and the fairness notions of Collaborative Decision-Making (CDM) work at a single airport and ignore network effects (p. 1; literature pp. 2–3). Ration-by-Schedule (RBS) cannot simply be applied airport by airport: that may break time, sector and flight connectivities, and with the maximum delay that "any tractable optimization model" needs, an RBS-feasible schedule may not exist (p. 3). Evidence on real data: the efficiency-only TFMP schedule for 14 July 2004 has 915 reversals and 1,492 periods of overtaking, against a maximum of about 9,500 reversals (p. 11). Statement: two stages, fairness controls inside a network model, then airline slot trading across airports (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Fairness is preservation of the scheduled arrival order, the RBS principle. For each reversible pair of flights at an airport or sector, a reversal counts when the later-scheduled flight arrives first, and overtaking counts the periods by which it leads; both enter the delay-cost objective with weights λ (pp. 3–4, 11–16). Stage II lets airlines submit Vossen–Ball "at-least, at-most" offers across airports, one binary per offer, under one of two objectives: maximize trades, or equalize trades across airlines (pp. 20–22). Kept: the Bertsimas–Stock-Patterson model and its "by" variables (p. 5), the total-delay cost expression adapted from Bertsimas–Lulli–Odoni, here with super-linear coefficients (pp. 8–9), and the Vossen–Ball offer structure (p. 20). Why this design: one variable per pair keeps the model close to TFMP in size (p. 19); RBS becomes a special case (p. 17); airport reversals are primary and sector reversals secondary, on stakeholder grounds (pp. 17–18).
- **D3 Model and data**: A binary time-indexed network model with deterministic airport-departure, airport-arrival and sector capacities per 15-minute period (pp. 6, 10). No cancellations (p. 5). Flight connectivities are hard constraints, kept because they "enable stronger polyhedral structure" although hub banks make practice differ (Remark 1, pp. 10–11). The maximum delay M is the stated tractability assumption, set to 6 periods (90 minutes) (pp. 3, 24–25). Data: six days from 2004–2006, 55 major U.S. airports, the five largest airlines, 4,590–5,844 flights a day (Table 4, pp. 23–24). Trajectories are fixed by randomized shortest paths on a 10 × 10 grid of 100 sectors, and connections come from tail numbers (pp. 23–24). Capacities are realized operations plus 20% slack, since true capacities are unknown (p. 24). Airline offers are generated by a rule that maximizes each airline's on-time arrivals (p. 31). [my reading] The authors say M "has no consequences on the fairness properties" (pp. 24–25), but M also defines which pairs count as reversible (Definitions 2.1–2.2, p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - One-sided linearizations (6) and (11) are proved correct only when the objective minimizes the indicator (Theorems 1–2, pp. 13, 15).
  - Objective-independent replacements: (7a)–(7d) describe the convex hull of the four feasible points of the overtaking truth table (Table 1, Figure 2, pp. 12–14); (12a)–(12b) model a reversal with one binary per pair (p. 16).
  - Proposition 1: the per-pair reversal polyhedron is integral because its matrix is totally unimodular. The proof uses two standard TU facts cited from Optimization over Integers and a row-partition argument over two-row blocks; without its last column the matrix is a network matrix (pp. 35–36).
  - RBS is the special case of a high λ whenever RBS is feasible (Remark 2, p. 17). An equalizing objective |dw − γ| spreads reversals or trades across airlines (pp. 18–19, 21–22).
  - Size bounds per model and a U.S.-scale example: 300,000–600,000 variables and 780,000–1,380,000 constraints (Tables 2–3, p. 19).
  - [my reading] Integrality is shown for each pair's added constraints alone, not for their intersection with the TFMP constraints, so the "theoretical evidence" of strength (p. 1) is local.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: CPLEX 11.0 through AMPL on a laptop with 2 GB of RAM; instances of about 300,000 variables and 800,000 constraints (p. 25).
  - Baselines: the day's actual delays and the efficiency-only TFMP, which cuts delays by 23% on average but leaves 500–1,000 reversals (Table 5, p. 25).
  - Fairness grids: TFMP-Overtake and TFMP-Reversal with λ = 100 at 0, 5, 15, 25 and 30 airports, reporting solve time, reversals, overtaking, delay and % increase over TFMP (Tables 6–7, pp. 26, 28). A λ sweep {0, 1, 10, 100, 1000} at 25 airports gives fewer than 100 reversals for at most 10% more delay cost, and 10–30 reversals for 10–40% more (Figure 5, p. 27).
  - Further studies: sector reversals in 5 north-eastern sectors and 10 airports, +13% delay cost for λs = 10 vs +3% for λs = 1 (Table 8, Figure 6, pp. 29–30); super-linear vs linear costs, about ±2% in reversals and at most 1% in delay (Figure 7, p. 31); trading in under a minute, equal trades per airline under Objective 2, and an ablation against single-airport trading (Tables 9–10, Figure 8, pp. 32–34).
  - Failures reported: a breakdown at 30 airports and above, put down to memory (p. 28); 3,600 s entries in the 30-airport rows, although no time limit is stated (pp. 26, 28); solutions within 20% of optimal in under 15 minutes (pp. 28–29); single-airport trading beats network trading when local offers dominate (p. 33).
  - Not stated: how the k airports with fairness are chosen (Tables 6–7, pp. 26, 28), which day Table 10 uses (p. 34), and the gaps of the 3,600 s runs (pp. 26, 28).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Odoni's 1987 formulation and an ATFM network literature that is largely the group's own (Vranas et al. 1994; Bertsimas–Stock 1998, 2000; Bertsimas–Lulli–Odoni 2008), faulted collectively for taking "the point of view of a single decision-maker" (p. 2). CDM and RBS (Ball et al.; Wambsganss) and Vossen–Ball slot trading are credited but confined to one airport (p. 3). The closest rival is the simultaneous Barnhart–Bertsimas–Caramanis–Fearing paper, co-authored by Bertsimas: this paper claims an exact model of overtaking against "an approximate method", a reversal metric, network effects and Stage II, and then closes that "both papers contribute" from distinct perspectives (pp. 4–5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper calls itself a "proposal" (title, p. 1; the word is on 23 lines). The abstract opens on the theory–practice gap with "In our opinion" (p. 1), and opinion markers ("We feel", "we believe") recur through to the conclusion (pp. 4, 18, 23, 24, 28, 29, 34). Worked examples and remarks justify each modelling choice (Examples 2.1–2.2, 3.1–3.2, 6.1; Remarks 1–3; pp. 8–11, 17–19, 25, 31–32). The headline "less than 30 minutes for up to 25 airports" appears in the abstract, the contributions and the summary (pp. 1, 4, 34). Headline vs tables [my computation]:
  - "consistently less than 30 minutes for up to 25 airports" (p. 28) does not hold for 4 of the 12 runs at 25 airports: 3,073 s and 1,983 s in Table 6 (p. 26), 3,600 s and 2,292 s in Table 7 (p. 28). The "on average" wording holds (means 1,221 s and 1,585 s; pp. 26, 28).
  - The two delay-cost summaries look crossed. The Overtake text says 13–41% with an average of 24.5% (p. 26), while Table 6 gives 13.2–40.3% with mean 24.6%. The Reversal text says 13–40%, again with an average of 24.5% (pp. 27–28), while Table 7 gives 13.1–40.8% with mean 23.2%.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Stated contributions: a two-stage proposal for network ATFM with fairness and collaboration, results on national-scale real data, and polyhedral evidence of strength (p. 4); the conclusion claims "practical tractability in real-time settings" (p. 34). Limits stated: memory at 30 or more airports (p. 28); M ≥ 10 means millions of variables (p. 24); capacities are inferred (p. 24); sector-reversal control costs about 13% and is therefore made secondary (pp. 29, 34). Limits unstated [my reading]: capacities are deterministic and the stochastic argument is qualitative (p. 18); offers are synthetic (p. 31); there is no field test or stakeholder evaluation; the 23% gain over actual delays depends on the assumed 20% capacity slack (pp. 24–25). The conclusion lists no future work (p. 34).

**Method links**:
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 14, 16, 26–30). The price of fairness is published as curves: reversals against % delay increase over λ ∈ {0, 1, 10, 100, 1000}, with a knee (under 100 reversals for at most 10%, p. 27); a second knob, the number of airports with fairness (Tables 6–7, pp. 26, 28); bad cases printed (sector reversals +13%, breakdown at 30 airports; pp. 28–29). **Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Model order-based fairness with cumulative "by" binaries (w = 1 if arrived by t): a reversal or a period of overtaking becomes a linear inequality on w_f′ − w_f (pp. 5, 12, 15).
- (D4) For each indicator, write the truth table of feasible 0/1 points, take its convex hull to get objective-independent constraints, and check integrality with a TU row-partition argument (Table 1, Figure 2, pp. 12–16, 35–36).
