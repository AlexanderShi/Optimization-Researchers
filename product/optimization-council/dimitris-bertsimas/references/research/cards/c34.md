### S527 · The ROAD to clinical trial emulation (Research Square 2025, DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-5576146/v1; arXiv 2412.03528)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–27 of arXiv v1 (arXiv:2412.03528v1 [stat.AP], 4 Dec 2024), the arXiv id the work list gives. The Research Square posting named as the venue was not compared with it.
- Body pp. 1–21 read in full, and references pp. 22–23.
- Figures 1–6 (risk distributions, the two trees, Kaplan–Meier curves) and supplementary eFigures S1–S7 (pp. 24–27) are images. Only their captions and the text's descriptions were read, so the trees' splits are known from the text (p. 13) and not from the figures.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Angelos Koulouras and Carol Gao (MIT), Hiroshi Nagata, Junki Mizusawa and Yukihide Kanemitsu (National Cancer Center Hospital, Tokyo), and Georgios Antonios Margonis (MIT and Memorial Sloan Kettering; corresponding author) (p. 1). Kanemitsu and Mizusawa are authors of the JCOG0603 trial report the paper emulates (ref. list, p. 22). Koulouras is a Bertsimas PhD student [04 B.2, B.5]. Bertsimas is first author; there is no contribution statement. · **Position in trajectory**: the second R.O.A.D. paper. It modifies the framework of Bertsimas, Koulouras & Margonis (npj Digital Medicine 2024; pp. 2–3, 22) and cites the group's imatinib paper (Lancet Oncology 2024; pp. 19, 22). S682 in this batch adapts the same pipeline to cardiology. The acronym R.O.A.D. is not expanded in the file.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML; IO as the matching engine; AP (surgical oncology) as the testbed}:
  - *Entry*: an admitted limit of the dominant method. Target-trial emulation cannot remove unobserved confounding, and the paper quotes Hernán and Robins saying so (pp. 2, 18–19). At the same time, regulators push real-world evidence: the EMA (2019), the 21st Century Cures Act and NICE's 2022 framework (p. 2). A review counts 168 emulation studies in 2020–22 (p. 2).
  - *The predecessor's limit*: R.O.A.D.'s upweighting of treated patients with good outcomes suits high-efficacy treatments. For low or moderate efficacy, how much to adjust "is unclear ... unless the target trial is specified" (p. 3).
  - *Problem*: from a target RCT's published summaries (arm outcome rates and covariate means), build an observational cohort and counterfactual models that reproduce them; then find subgroups with heterogeneous effects (pp. 3–4).
  - *Testbed*: colorectal liver metastases, emulating JCOG0603 (hepatectomy plus mFOLFOX vs hepatectomy alone, 300 patients) (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *Four steps* (Algorithm 1, p. 9):
    1. Apply the trial's eligibility criteria (p. 4).
    2. Train an "X-ray" baseline-risk model on untreated patients, cut risk buckets, and select matched pairs by an integer program. The program minimizes the gaps between both arms' mean baseline risk and the trial's control rate, and between each reported covariate mean and the trial's (pp. 4–6).
    3. Train one counterfactual model per arm. Retrain the treated-arm model with a cost-sensitive weight ρ until its mean prediction equals the trial's treated-arm rate (pp. 6–7).
    4. Fit an Optimal Policy Tree (Amram, Dunn & Zhuo 2022) on the counterfactuals, optionally with rewards shrunk to encode "first, do no harm" (pp. 7–8).
  - *Validation*: node-level confounding checks with two external prognostic scores (p. 8), and a blind replication in the RCT cohort run by the trial's own group (pp. 10, 20).
  - *What changed vs R.O.A.D.*: the matching now targets the trial's prognosis and covariate means, not only treated–untreated comparability. The weight is tuned to an external anchor (the trial's arm rates) instead of being set by judgment (p. 3).
  - *What did not change*: risk-bucket matching, per-arm models and OPT (pp. 2–3, 6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - *Observational data*: 779 trial-eligible patients from the International Genetic Consortium for Colorectal Liver Metastasis (IGCLM), resected 2002–2019; 639 had surgery alone and 140 adjuvant chemotherapy (pp. 4, 10). The emulated cohort has 216 patients, 108 per arm (p. 11).
  - *Anchor data*: only the trial's published summaries. These are 5-year DFS of 38.7% in the control arm (39% by Kaplan–Meier) and 49.5% (about 49%) in the chemotherapy arm, plus four reported prognostic covariates (nodal status, four or more metastases, a metastasis of 5 cm or more, laterality) (pp. 4, 6, 10–11).
  - *Endpoint*: RFS in the observational data vs DFS in the trial. Only 13 of the trial's 176 events were secondary cancers, and the paper calls the difference "a strength rather than a limitation" (p. 3).
  - *Simplification admitted*: the methods are written for binary recurrence "for simplicity" (p. 4), with a classification loss "such as the cross-entropy loss" (p. 7), while the results use random survival forests (p. 12).
  - *Modelling assumptions, as the paper states them*:
    - The remaining gap between the predicted treated-arm outcome and the trial's rate is attributed to unobserved confounding or prognostic-model error, and is removed by ρ (pp. 6–7, 19).
    - Balanced observed prognosis inside tree nodes is taken as "reasonable evidence" that unobserved confounding is absent too (p. 8).
    - Trial covariates that were not reported (disease-free interval, tumour location) are treated as unobserved confounders and absorbed by weight tuning (p. 11).
  - *Notation slips*: the matching constraints write sums where means are meant, and they set the mean recurrence probability w equal to µ0, which the text calls the DFS rate (pp. 4–5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - *Matching integer program* (Problem (1), pp. 5–6). Binary z_ij pairs treated patient i with untreated patient j.
    - The objective sums the absolute gaps between µ0 and each arm's mean baseline risk, the absolute gaps between each arm's covariate means and the trial's, and the pair distances Σ z_ij‖x_i − x_j‖².
    - Constraints: each patient is matched at most once; n_k^sel pairs per risk bucket; no pairs across buckets.
    - [inferred] The per-bucket counts fix the total number of pairs, so the ratios have constant denominators. Problem (1) is then a linear MIO once the absolute values are linearized. The paper names no solver, solve time or gap.
  - *Cost-sensitive counterfactuals* (eq. (2), p. 7): the loss on treated patients without recurrence is multiplied by ρ (Elkan 2001). The authors "expect" the mean treated-arm prediction to rise with ρ ≥ 1 and tune ρ until it equals µ1. Monotonicity is asserted, not proved (p. 7).
  - *Constrained rewards* (pp. 7–8): the subgroup-effect constraints "are not directly part of the OPT implementation". They are approximated by setting the no-chemotherapy reward to 0.78 of the chemotherapy reward wherever it is higher. 0.78 "was the largest constraint we could apply while still allowing the OPT to split" (p. 8).
  - *Tree selection*: hyperparameters are tuned, and the final tree is chosen "partly" by concordance with the counterfactual models' preferred treatment. A minimum node size guards against overfitting (p. 7).
  - *Confounding test*: within each node, external risk scores are compared between arms with p-values (Tables 2–3, pp. 14–15).
  - *Validation statistics*: Kaplan–Meier medians and log-rank tests, judged on three criteria: direction, size and significance (pp. 10, 14–15).
  - *No theorems*: "we establish the theoretical foundation of our framework by using an example" (p. 3).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Matching*: the emulated cohort's predicted baseline 5-year RFS is 35%, against the trial's 38.7–39%, with balanced covariates (Table 1, pp. 11–12). The untreated group's Kaplan–Meier 5-year RFS rises from 27% before matching to 34% after (p. 18).
  - *Confounding before weighting*:
    - In the emulated cohort, surgery alone shows the better 5-year RFS (38% vs 34%; p = 0.016) (p. 11).
    - The chemotherapy group's rate is called "11% lower" than the trial's 49% (p. 11), although 34% vs 49% is 15 points as printed.
    - The predicted benefit is 6%, against the trial's 11% (p. 19).
  - *Weights*: 1.5 for treated patients without recurrence, and 1.25 for untreated patients without recurrence (pp. 11–12). The second weight is not described in the Methods, which adjust the treated arm only (pp. 6–7).
  - *Counterfactual model*: random survival forests with a C-index of 0.88 in sample; 3-fold cross-validation gives 0.68 for untreated and 0.61 for treated patients (p. 12). The Discussion gives 0.65 cross-validated and argues that the in-sample fit, the model's ability to "memorize" the data, suffices for emulation (p. 18).
  - *Subgroups*: OPT 1 has five leaves, three of which (186 of 216 patients) are recommended chemotherapy. OPT 2 has three leaves, two of which (182 of 216) are recommended (p. 13). The two trees agree on 177 of 186 patients, 95.2% (p. 15).
  - *Confounding check*: every node-level p-value for the MSK CRS and JHH GAME scores exceeds 0.05; the smallest are 0.052 and 0.070. The text reads this as "no confounding ... whether observed or unobserved" (pp. 14–15).
  - *External validation in JCOG0603*, run by the trial group (p. 15):
    - OPT 1, patients recommended chemotherapy: median RFS improves by 22.2 months in the emulated cohort (10.9 vs 33.1; p = 0.001) and by 24 months in the RCT cohort (16.8 vs 40.8; p = 0.05).
    - OPT 2, the same group: 21.7 months (p < 0.001) vs 20.4 months (p = 0.01).
    - Patients advised against chemotherapy show no benefit in either cohort (p = 0.24 and 0.43 for OPT 1; 0.11 and 0.94 for OPT 2).
    - The share without benefit is 14% vs 15% (OPT 1) and 16% vs 17% (OPT 2) (p. 20).
  - *Failures reported*: two OPT 1 nodes recommended chemotherapy but showed no actual benefit on internal and external validation. The paper calls this "not problematic", because those nodes would not be used (p. 13).
  - *Not done*: no other emulation method (propensity matching, IPW, doubly robust estimation) is run on the same data; there is no sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding; the matching program's solve is not reported; there is no code or data statement (pp. 1–23).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper frames itself against Hernán and Robins and quotes their admissions that emulation cannot overcome confounding (pp. 2, 18–19).
  - Classical adjustments (propensity-score matching and its ML variants, stratification, IPW, g-estimation, doubly robust methods) are called "ineffective against unobserved (unmeasured) confounding" (p. 2).
  - Own lineage: "extending the approach we pioneered in the R.O.A.D. framework" (p. 6). OPTs are "optimal decision trees, developed by our team" (p. 1), citing Amram, Dunn & Zhuo (2022).
  - Subgroup analysis: positioned against one-variable-at-a-time subgroups (Kent et al. 2018) (p. 19).
  - External prognostic models: MSK CRS (Fong et al. 1999), and the JHH GAME score, whose 2018 paper has the corresponding author Margonis as first author (pp. 8, 23).
  - No sensitivity-analysis method for unmeasured confounding appears in the references (pp. 22–23).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - *Headline claim*: the abstract says it is the first framework "to successfully address both observed and unobserved confounding", a challenge since "the 1950s" (p. 1). The claim recurs as "the first work that transforms observational data into a target RCT cohort" (p. 3).
  - *Limitations reframed as strengths*: RFS instead of DFS (p. 3); the low cross-validated C-index, answered by the in-sample fit (p. 18).
  - *Objections answered by rhetorical questions*: "How, then, did we successfully emulate the trial's baseline DFS?" (p. 18); "how can we even know if it is present" (p. 19).
  - *Hedges*: "Pending further validations by other researchers" and "a bold claim" (p. 20).
  - *Draft-state signs*:
    - Duplicated paragraphs (pp. 10, 13).
    - Disease-free-interval thresholds of 12 and 12.5 months for the same split (p. 13).
    - "median OS" in an RFS comparison (p. 15).
    - The Figure 6 caption says "advised adjuvant chemotherapy" for the group advised against it (p. 17).
    - The constrained rewards are cited as Section 2.4 instead of 2.5 (p. 20).
    - Algorithm 1 has no Step 4 (p. 9).
  - *No limitations section* (Discussion, pp. 16–21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - *Contribution levels*: a methodological pipeline (four "innovations", pp. 17–18), one clinical application with external validation, and a clinical finding: about 15% of trial-eligible patients do not benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy (p. 20).
  - *Limits the paper states*: emulation leans on in-sample fit (p. 18); unreported trial covariates cannot be matched (p. 11); a suitable trial must exist (pp. 4, 20).
  - *Limits it does not state*:
    - The identification assumption that the residual gap is confounding.
    - Small nodes behind the non-significant confounding checks.
    - That many trees were fitted and two were chosen.
  - *Future and impact*: larger emulated samples, genomic covariates, wider access to trial-like data, fairness (pp. 20–21). OPTs are said to make "hypothetical trials" unnecessary (pp. 19–20).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: decisions (who receives adjuvant chemotherapy) are judged against the strongest rule available, the positive trial's treat-everyone result (p. 20). The check is a blind one by the trial's owners, with the models frozen (p. 20): step 6 of the method in a strong form. But the yardstick is a published RCT, not an owner's current rule. No decision metric (such as P or expected RFS under the policy) is computed, and the trees are built and selected on in-sample counterfactuals (pp. 7, 18).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: a problem owned by epidemiology (target-trial emulation) is restated as an integer program that matches published trial moments (pp. 5–6), and an identification gap becomes a calibration target (p. 7). But the solver class and solve times are not reported, so the method's stop rule (class left unmeasured) is open.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: two one-dimensional knobs carry properties. ρ sets the size of the confounding correction, and the reward shrink of 0.78 encodes "do no harm" (pp. 7–8, 11–12). Both are set from a data anchor or at an extreme value; neither is shown as a curve, and no bad case is printed.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the trees are called optimal with no solver, gap or time. Two trees are picked from many by concordance, and they are not duelled against a greedy or causal-ML alternative (pp. 1, 7, 13). Tension T1.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: programme continuity (R.O.A.D. 2024 → this paper → S682), clinical partners as coauthors, and a regulatory framing (pp. 2–3, 19–20).
- Heuristic 6 ✅ (weak): the IGCLM consortium is the data asset the method runs on (pp. 4, 10).
- new-pattern candidate: ROAD pipeline: risk-bucket matching, per-arm counterfactuals, outcome-class reweighting, policy tree (pp. 3–9; also S682 pp. 3–4).
- new-pattern candidate: calibrate a bias knob to a published trial's arm outcomes (pp. 6–7, 11–12, 19).
- new-pattern candidate: frozen models handed to the data owner for blind validation (pp. 1, 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To emulate a published RCT, turn its Table 1 into an assignment integer program. Penalize the gaps between each arm's mean predicted baseline risk and the control arm's event rate, and between each reported covariate mean and the trial's, with one-to-one matches inside risk buckets and a pair-distance term (pp. 5–6). Report the solver and solve time, which this paper omits.
- Calibrate a cost-sensitive weight on one outcome class until the model's mean counterfactual equals an external anchor. Report the gap before calibration as the size of the bias being corrected (here 6% vs 11%) (pp. 6–7, 11–12, 19).
- Hand frozen models to whoever holds the validation data and ask for subgroup Kaplan–Meier curves back, with no refitting after the results arrive. State the three success criteria in advance: direction, effect size and significance (pp. 10, 20).
- Check covariate balance inside every subgroup with prognostic scores trained elsewhere, so nothing leaks (pp. 8, 14–15). Report the p-values, but do not read non-significance in small nodes as proof that confounding is absent.
- To encode an asymmetric principle such as "do no harm", shrink the rewards of the competing option, and report the strongest shrink that still lets the tree split (p. 8).
- Fit a second tree on different splits and report the overlap of recommendations (95.2%) and whether the share without benefit replicates (pp. 15, 20).

**Quotes**:
- "To our knowledge, this is the first framework to successfully address both observed and unobserved confounding" (p. 1)
- "The answer lies in our model’s in-sample C-index, which was excellent at 0.88, indicating the model’s ability to “memorize” the data within the study sample." (p. 18)

### S621 · Early Warning Index for Patient Deteriorations in Hospitals (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2512.14683)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–31 of arXiv v1 (arXiv:2512.14683v1 [cs.LG], 16 Dec 2025).
- Body pp. 1–26 read in full; references pp. 27–31 scanned.
- Figures 1–5 (threshold curves, SHAP plots, pipeline, dashboard) are images; their captions and the text's descriptions were read.
- Appendix Table 1 (the green and yellow medication lists) and Appendix Figure 1 (calibration), cited on pp. 12 and 21, are not in the file and were not read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yu Ma (University of Wisconsin–Madison), Kimberly Villalobos Carballo (NYU), Gagan Singh, Jeff Mather, Dan Kombert and Howard Haronian (Hartford HealthCare), and Michal Laskowski (Holistic Hospital Optimization) (p. 1). By the contribution statement (p. 26):
- Ma led model development, experiments, code, analysis and writing.
- Laskowski wrote code and implemented the model in the hospital system.
- "D.B. directed the overall project, from concept and research to implementation, and edited the manuscript".

· **Position in trajectory**: the Hartford HealthCare hospital-operations line, after the group's inpatient-flow paper (Bertsimas, Pauphilet, Stevens & Tandon, MSOM 2022; cited pp. 4, 27). Ma (PhD 2025) and Villalobos Carballo (PhD 2024) are his former PhD students [04 B.6]. Holistic Hospital Optimization is the company the research notes record Bertsimas as founding in 2022 [02 E5; 06 §1]. The paper itself says only that the pipeline "is developed by a proprietary firm" (p. 21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP (hospital operations); DA only through the threshold design}:
  - *Entry from the partner*: a large U.S. hospital system that embeds predictive models in its EHR found that existing scores (the EPIC deterioration index, NEWS) "were not adopted widely in practice". Their performance is low because their inputs come from a single source, so predictions contradict clinicians' judgement (p. 3).
  - *Problem*: every day, predict for each non-ICU inpatient the probability of an aggregated deterioration event in the next 24 hours: emergency-response-team dispatch, ICU admission or death. All data up to that day may be used (pp. 3, 9).
  - *Operational framing*: deterioration is tied to nurse scheduling, ICU beds and readmissions (pp. 4–5). Acute "track-and-trigger" systems leave no planning time; stable deterioration does (p. 5).
  - *Second entry*: systems for high-stakes settings "should not be purely quantitative"; clinicians' private information must enter through a human-in-the-loop process (pp. 3–4, 6–7).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *One multimodal patient-day vector* of 1,478 features (pp. 11–12):
    - Tabular data: demographics, social behaviour, administrative status, and operational context such as ward census and day of week.
    - Daily summaries of vitals and labs: average, peak, minimum, last value and trend.
    - Text embeddings of medications and diagnoses.
  - *Text device*: high-cardinality categories are treated as text. They are embedded by tiny-ClinicalBERT (dimension 312) with a task prompt prefixed, then averaged per day, instead of being one-hot or top-k encoded (pp. 6, 10–11).
  - *Caregiver-centric clock*: data are cut at 11:59 pm and predictions published at 8 am, before rounds (pp. 9, 21–22).
  - *Human in the loop*:
    - SHAP is run on large-error cases, the cases are reviewed with clinicians, and features are changed: DNR orders, and "green" and "yellow" medication classes (pp. 12, 15–16).
    - Alert thresholds are iterated with physicians in live use (p. 16).
  - *What changed vs the incumbents*: more modalities and a daily caregiver-centric clock.
  - *What did not change*: standard learners (random forest; gradient-boosted trees, with an XGBoost citation; TabNet) and post-hoc attribution (pp. 14, 18).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - *Cohort*: 18,633 patients at one large U.S. hospital, 2021-01-01 to 2023-04-01, giving 110,900 patient-admission-days (pp. 9–10). Excluded: patients in the ICU, stays under 24 hours, dispatch times outside the admission window, and missing admission dates.
  - *Outcome*: the union of four team-dispatch types (RRT, cardiac alert, anesthesia STAT, difficult airway), ICU admission and death within 24 hours (pp. 12–13). Unrecorded events count as negatives (p. 13).
  - *Admitted*: the outcomes are surrogates for deterioration severity and are endogenous; ICU admission depends on bed availability and on human decisions. The model is therefore not an estimate of dispatch or admission decisions (p. 13).
  - *Missing values*: the last value in the same stay, otherwise 0 (p. 11).
  - *No embedding alignment* (e.g., contrastive learning), to keep features explainable (p. 12).
  - *Chronological 70/15/15 split* (pp. 13–14):
    - Training to 2022-08-07.
    - Validation 2022-08-13 to 2022-11-29.
    - Test 2022-11-29 to 2023-04-01.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for proofs.
  - The learning problem is stated as empirical risk minimization over a parametric family, with hyperparameters chosen on the validation set (p. 14). The grids: 20, 50 or 100 estimators and depth 3 or 4 for the tree ensembles; dimensions 16 or 32 and 3 or 5 steps for TabNet. The final model is refitted on training plus validation data.
  - The aggregated outcome is written as a sum of three indicators, although it is defined as their union (p. 13).
  - No optimization model is used for the decision; the thresholds are set by deliberation (pp. 15–16).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Test AUROC by modality subset and learner* (Table 1, p. 17):
    - Best: gradient-boosted trees on all three modalities, 0.798.
    - Best single modality: time series, 0.768. Language alone: 0.660.
    - TabNet is weakest (0.605–0.708).
    - One negative: random forest on tabular plus language (0.733) scores below tabular alone (0.738) (p. 17).
  - *Inconsistencies*:
    - The abstract reports 0.796 (p. 2), Table 1 0.798.
    - The text's "4.7%" gain over the best single modality (p. 17) does not follow from the table: 0.768 → 0.798 is about 3.9% relative.
  - *Thresholds* (Figure 1, p. 18): at 0.06, sensitivity is 0.846, specificity 0.557 and precision 0.154; high-risk alerts fire at 0.12. The dashboard uses 3% for yellow and 12% for red, plus day-over-day rises of 1.5% and 6% (p. 22), so the medium-risk cut differs from p. 18.
  - *SHAP* (Figures 2–3, pp. 18–20): ward census and hospital admissions push risk up, as do next-day surgery, DNR orders, RASS below 0 and an average heart rate above 105.
  - *Calibration*: said to be checked in Appendix Figure 1 (p. 21), which is not in the file.
  - *Missing*:
    - No confidence intervals.
    - No head-to-head with the EPIC index, NEWS or clinicians on the same patients.
    - No outcome data from the deployment (pp. 17–25).
    - No code.
  - *Compute*: Python 3.8.11 on a server with 32 GB RAM and an Intel Xeon CPU (p. 16).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Operations-management work on the ICU and patient flow is used to argue that deterioration forecasts are an operational input: KC & Terwiesch 2012; Kim et al. 2015 and 2024; Chan et al. 2012; Ouyang et al. 2020 (pp. 4–5).
  - Early-warning scores (NEWS, APACHE III, the EPIC index) are criticized for single-modality inputs (pp. 5–6); multimodal AI (Acosta et al. 2022) is the model (p. 6).
  - Human-in-the-loop papers from Management Science (ride hailing, medication, investment, hotel pricing, text messaging) frame the refinement process (p. 7).
  - *Own work*: inpatient flow (Bertsimas et al. 2022) (pp. 4, 27). The pipeline "largely follows the one in [18]" (p. 21), a numeric reference that no entry of this author-year reference list resolves.
  - *Citation slips*: KC & Terwiesch (2012), an econometric ICU-flow paper, is cited for pre-trained language models (p. 10); "Na 2023" is left unformatted (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - *Format*: a structured abstract (Problem definition / Methodology / Results and Managerial implications) in an INFORMS "Article submitted to" template (p. 2), M&SOM style [inferred].
  - *Contribution phrasing*: "two key steps that had not been previously explored or discussed" (p. 7); "To the best of our knowledge, there is limited work" (p. 4).
  - *Deployment wording drifts*:
    - "currently used as a triage tool" (abstract, p. 2).
    - "currently under testing" (p. 21).
    - "Once fully implemented" (p. 24).
    - The dashboard processes about 1,500 patients across "the seven satellite locations" (p. 22), while expansion to seven hospitals is "anticipated" (p. 24).
  - *Unmeasured managerial claims*: "Concrete outcomes ... include measurable reductions in the length of hospital stays and ICU admission rates" (p. 24). The same paper says operational effects "cannot be straightforwardly evaluated" (p. 25).
  - *Typos*: "class of cleaners", "hold-one-out validation set" and "inference event" (p. 14); "S0upercomputing" (p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - *Contribution level*: an applied ML system and its deployment process: pipeline, dashboard, human-in-the-loop refinement (pp. 7–8, 20–22).
  - *Limits, concrete* (p. 25):
    - ICU patients are excluded.
    - No images or clinical notes are used, because of processing delays and identifiable information.
    - Operational effects cannot be evaluated, because of endogenous factors.
    - A 24-hour cadence does not match shorter shifts.
  - *Future*: predictions every 6 or 8 hours (pp. 25–26); expansion to satellite hospitals and other systems; retraining every 6 months (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (with partial adherence):
  - Present: steps 1 and 6. There is an owner with its data, a dashboard in use or testing, and thresholds set with the users (pp. 3, 16, 21–22).
  - Absent: the incumbent rule (the EPIC deterioration index and NEWS) is named as the reason for the project (p. 3) but never scored on the same patients. The only baselines are the model's own modality subsets (p. 17). The decision is not scored at all; operational effects "cannot be straightforwardly evaluated" (p. 25).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: a partner hospital, an author from his 2022 company, and the inpatient-flow pipeline reused (pp. 1, 21–22). No competing-interest statement appears (p. 26); cf. SKILL.md integrity rule 5.

### S623 · Overfitting in Adaptive Robust Optimization (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2509.16451)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–5 of arXiv v3 (arXiv:2509.16451v3 [math.OC], 19 Nov 2025), a paper for the NeurIPS 2025 MLxOR workshop (p. 1).
- Figure 1 is an image; its caption was read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Karl Zhu (MIT Operations Research Center), first author (p. 1), named among his students in a 2025 group post [04 B.8]; no contribution statement. · **Position in trajectory**: the RO line in 2025. It builds on three of the group's own papers: probabilistic guarantees (Bertsimas, den Hertog & Pauphilet, SIOPT 2021), uncertainty coupling (Bertsimas, Na, Stellato & Wang, IJOO 2024), and robustification–regularization equivalence (Bertsimas & Copenhaver, EJOR 2018) (pp. 2–3, 5). Five of its ten references are Bertsimas papers (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO; ML as the lens}:
  - *Entry*: an "under-discussed drawback" of ARO. Adaptivity makes constraints that did not depend on u (e.g., non-negativity) depend on it. Adaptive policies then fail outside U, where static ones often stay feasible (pp. 1–2).
  - *Framing*: brittleness is overfitting: better performance inside the set, worse generalization outside it (pp. 1–2).
  - *Setting*: a robust LP with right-hand-side uncertainty b_i + d_iᵀu and affine rules x(u) = z + Vu (p. 1).
  - *Premise for evaluation*: a good set should not contain all realizations, so simulations should sample from an underlying distribution, not from U (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Give each constraint its own uncertainty set, sized by how critical it is (p. 3):
    - Hard constraints get sets that cover the support, i.e. a deterministic guarantee.
    - Soft constraints, those with a backup such as grid imports, get probabilistic sets.
  - Read each robust counterpart as a norm bound on the adaptive coefficients. The guarantee 1 − ε then acts as a regularization parameter, and ε → 0 gives V = 0, the static policy (p. 4).
  - *What changed*: one set size per constraint instead of one U.
  - *What did not*: the affine decision rule and the standard robust-counterpart machinery (pp. 1, 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - *Toy*: two periods with a renewable forecast of 2 units each, grid imports at cost 100, and the budget set {‖u‖∞ ≤ ρ, ‖u‖₁ ≤ Γ} (p. 2).
  - *Probabilistic guarantees*, recalled from ref. [9] (p. 3): Gaussian u, where the chance constraint is equivalent to an ellipsoidal counterpart; or independent zero-mean u with support [−1, 1], for ball-box and budget sets.
  - *Deterministic guarantees*: bounded supports [L_k, U_k] or semi-bounded ones (p. 3).
  - *Data*: none; the toy is solved by hand.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - *Toy solutions* (p. 2):
    - Nominal: objective 4, with x = y = 1 and s = 0.
    - Static robust with (ρ, Γ) = (1, 1): objective 2. This is equivalent to a box, because static RO "in general cannot exploit this coupling" (ref. [7]).
    - Restricted affine rules x_i(u_i), y_i(u_i): objective 3 with y_i = 1 + u_i, which is infeasible (a negative flow) for u_1 < −1.
    - Enlarging only the non-negativity constraints' set to (2, 2): x_i = y_i = 1 + u_i/2, still objective 3, and feasible for all u ≥ −2, the physical lower bound.
  - *Recalled robust counterparts* (p. 3):
    - Gaussian: the chance constraint is equivalent to an SOC constraint with the quantile ρ_{1−ε}.
    - Ball-box: counterpart (3), with guarantee 1 − exp(−ρ²/2), so ρ = √(2 ln(1/ε)).
    - Budget: the bound is garbled in the text layer. The stated choice reads Γ = √(2 ln(1/ε))·√p once the lost radical is restored [inferred from the ball-box line, where the radical is rendered as "p"].
  - *Proposition 1 and Corollary 1* (p. 3): the LP counterpart for box support, a_iᵀz + Uᵀβ − Lᵀα ≤ b_i with β − α = Vᵀa_i − d_i and α, β ≥ 0; for semi-bounded supports, set α_k or β_k to 0. Both are stated without proof ("We state the results directly below", citing ref. [10]).

  - Reference [5] is garbled: an author listed twice, "Mcgill Ca" as an author, and no venue or year (p. 5).

  - Claims are proportionate: "This view motivates a principled approach" (p. 1); "Our analysis suggests several lessons" (p. 4).

  - *Limits not discussed* (pp. 2–4):

**Method links**:

### S624 · Global nonconvex optimization with integer variables (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2508.07018)

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Danique de Moor and Thodoris Koukouvinos (MIT Operations Research Center) and Demetrios Kriezis (MIT) (p. 1); no contribution statement. · **Position in trajectory**: a sequel to two of the group's papers: RPT-BB for continuous nonconvex problems (Bertsimas, de Moor, den Hertog, Koukouvinos & Zhen, Optimization Online 2023a) and the cone-product reformulation (2023b) (pp. 1–2, 20). SKILL.md's Latest section lists the latter as IJOC 2026 (DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2023.0345). All instances come from 2023a (p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + CP; AP (Dutch dike heightening) as the one real-world test}:
  - *Entry*: nonconvexities (economies of scale, concave revenues, start-up and ramping costs) combine with integer choices. Integer variables rule out gradient-based local search, and fractional solutions weaken convex relaxations (pp. 1–2).
  - *Own method's boundary*: RPT-BB handles continuous problems whose nonconvexity is a sum of linear-times-convex (SLC) terms. By Hartman (1959) and Weierstrass approximation, that covers approximately any continuous nonconvex problem (p. 2). Integer domains are the gap.
  - *Problem (1)*: min f_0(x) s.t. f_k(x) ≤ 0, x ∈ X, with SLC functions f_k (2) and a compact X of linear, equality and convex constraints over binary, integer and continuous blocks (pp. 3–4).
  - *Examples*: a mixed-integer nonconvex QP, with Max-Cut (p. 4), and dike heightening (p. 5).
  - *Incumbents*: BARON (Sahinidis 1996) and SCIP (Achterberg 2009) (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *Three steps* (pp. 5–9):
    1. Relax integrality.
    2. Apply RPT to get a convex relaxation: multiply constraints pairwise, write the convex factors in perspective form, and linearize xxᵀ by X.
    3. Branch with a hybrid rule.
  - *Integer structure enters the relaxation for free*: McCormick inequalities for pairs of binaries "naturally emerge", and X_ii = x_i is added for binaries (p. 6).
  - *Hybrid branching* (pp. 7–9):
    - If the maximum integrality violation Δ exceeds δ, branch on the most fractional integer variable.
    - Otherwise branch on the hyperplane given by the top eigenvector of X*_C − x*_C x*_Cᵀ (Anstreicher 2022).
    - Every branching inequality is multiplied with the existing constraints in the child.
  - *What changed vs RPT-BB*: integer branching, binary cuts and the threshold rule. *What did not*: the RPT relaxation and eigenvector branching (pp. 2, 5).
  - *Alternative left open*: apply RPT directly to the mixed-integer problem and let SCIP branch on the integers (pp. 18–19).

  - *Cohort*: low-to-intermediate risk (STS < 3·5) severe aortic stenosis, treated with TAVR or SAVR between September 2016 and June 2023 (p. 3). Excluded: prior aortic interventions, concomitant procedures, and aortic regurgitation unrelated to stenosis.
  - *Sources* (p. 3):
    - The STS Adult Cardiac Surgery Database (SAVR) and the TVT Registry (TAVR), plus echocardiography and CTA.
    - Mortality by linkage to the Connecticut Death Registry by name and date of birth (deaths January 2015 – May 2024), which misses out-of-state deaths.
  - *Hartford*: 1,352 patients, restricted to the 372 with CTA (151 SAVR, 221 TAVR). Remaining gaps were filled by KNN imputation, and the matched cohort has 236 patients (pp. 4–5).
  - *St. Vincent's*: 305 patients (36 SAVR, 269 TAVR). All CTA variables are imputed by KNN models fitted at Hartford (p. 4).
  - *Outcome*: y ∈ {0, 1}, death within 5 years (p. 3); censoring is handled by the survival models (p. 3).
  - *Admitted*:
    - Matching lowers or keeps the SMD of 22 of 39 features, and "not all covariates are perfectly aligned" (p. 5).
    - After matching, mortality is 6·78% for SAVR and 8·47% for TAVR. The remaining gap is attributed to residual and unobserved confounding plus genuine heterogeneity (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for proofs.
  - *Formulations*: the OPT objective (1) (p. 4); the weighted set (2) (p. 4).
  - *Sensitivity and specificity redefined for prescriptions* (eqs. (3)–(4), p. 4): the share of bad-outcome patients whose treatment the policy changes, and the share of good-outcome patients whose treatment it keeps.
  - *Not given*: the OPT's solver, depth, run time or optimality; the implementation is not named (pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Leaf table* (Table 2, p. 6):
    - In Hartford the prescribed arm has the lower observed mortality in all five leaves (p. 5). This is an in-sample statement about the training cohort.
    - At St. Vincent's the SAVR counts per leaf are 1, 2, 2, 23 and 8. Node 7 reverses (TAVR 23·3% against SAVR 0·0% with 2 patients); the paper explains this by imbalance and by current practice (pp. 5–6).
  - *Discrimination* (Table 3, Panel A, p. 7), with bootstrap CIs:
    - Hartford, labelled "train": sensitivity 0·78, specificity 0·52, concordance 0·50.
    - St. Vincent's: 0·55, 0·38 and 0·39.
  - *Estimated improvement in 5-year mortality over real-world decisions* (Panel B, p. 7):
    - OPT: +20·2% at Hartford and +13·8% at St. Vincent's.
    - All-SAVR: −1·0% and +4·6%. All-TAVR: +1·4% and +1·5%. Random: +0·1% and +3·1%.
    - The evaluator is the set of counterfactual models trained on the matched Hartford cohort (p. 6).
  - *Model-free leaf analysis* (Panel C, pp. 6–7): +55·45% and +43·42%. For patients where the policy disagrees with practice, it imputes the observed mean outcome of leaf-mates who received the recommended treatment, "following our prior TAVR methodology".
  - *Numbers drift*: the abstract gives 0·77 and 20·3% (p. 1); Table 3 and the Discussion give 0·78 and 20·2% (p. 7).
  - *Caveats the text does not draw*:
    - The Hartford metrics are computed on the training cohort used to fit and select the tree (pp. 4–5, 7).
    - At St. Vincent's, the CTA split variables (annulus and sinus of Valsalva diameters, p. 8) are imputed (p. 4). The Limitations call this "substantial missing CTA measurements" (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence, with corrections:
  - Present: an owner (Hartford's heart team), a decision and data. The current rule, real-world prescriptions, is the baseline, alongside naive policies (Table 3, p. 7). Decisions are scored by estimated mortality and by a model-free leaf check, on an external hospital too. A field test is named as the next step (p. 2).
  - Corrections: the scorer is the same family of counterfactual models, the Hartford figures are in-sample, and the external CTA values are imputed. So the method's demand to say what the evaluation cannot show is only partly met (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Score a prescriptive policy with two outcome-conditioned agreement metrics plus overall concordance (p. 4):
  - Sensitivity: the share of bad-outcome patients whose treatment the policy changes.
  - Specificity: the share of good-outcome patients whose treatment it keeps.
- Report evaluation at three levels (pp. 6–7):
  1. Model-based improvement over real-world decisions.
  2. The same improvement for the naive policies (all-A, all-B, random), with bootstrap CIs.
  3. A model-free leaf analysis that imputes outcomes from leaf-mates who actually received the recommended treatment.
- Print a leaf table with counts and observed outcomes per arm for every cohort, so readers can see the small cells behind external claims (Table 2, p. 6).
- Upweight the two discordant outcome classes (better arm with a bad outcome, worse arm with a good outcome), so per-arm counterfactual models do not become optimistic for one arm only. Tune the weight together with tree complexity (p. 4).
- When a feature is missing at the external site, say so where the external results are reported, not only in the Limitations. This paper imputes the very CTA variables its tree splits on (pp. 4, 7–8).
