### S456 · Should We Relax Stability in Matching Markets? (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2509.14475)
Read level: **full**: all 21 pages, including Appendix A.1 (assumptions, lemma, theorem and proof, pp. 18–20) and the references (pp. 20–21). Figures 1–8 did not extract (captions only), so values are taken from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Carol Gao (Operations Research Center, MIT, p. 1); Theodore Papalexopoulos and Felipe Cordera are thanked (p. 18) · **Position in trajectory**: 2019–2026 period. The paper continues a Boston Public Schools optimization line from Papalexopoulos's 2022 MIT PhD thesis (cited pp. 2–4, 6, 13; reference p. 20). This text does not say whether Bertsimas supervised that thesis.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO LO AP}:
  - The incumbent: centralized school and residency matches use Deferred Acceptance (DA), which guarantees stability, i.e. no blocking pair (p. 2). The paper names two limits. DA optimizes stability only. It also has "little variability": by the Rural Hospitals Theorem every stable matching matches the same applicants (p. 2).
  - The application numbers: transportation is more than 10% of Boston Public Schools (BPS) spending and has risen by more than 24 million (17%) since 2018, while total travel differs by no more than 0.13% across all stable solutions (Papalexopoulos 2022). Unmatched students go to a later round that breaks stability anyway (p. 2).
  - In residency matches with couples, stability has no unique definition, and a stable match may not exist (pp. 2–3).
  - The entry is a field's axiom treated as a soft constraint: trade the number of blocking pairs against other objectives, posed as the title's question (pp. 1, 3).
  - The scale problem comes from the predecessor: the exact MIO with a Rural Hospitals presolve scales only in the fully stable case (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Key idea**: Algorithm 1 (p. 10) has three steps.
  1. Run DA.
  2. Recover by inverse LP a cost vector b* that makes DA's solution optimal for the LP relaxation of an assignment problem with at least N matches (eqs. 9–18, pp. 6–7).
  3. Solve a multi-objective assignment IP: minimize λ1·b*ᵀx minus rank-share rewards for applicant and program preferences, subject to the assignment constraints, at least N matches, and either a travel budget T (eqs. 19–24, pp. 7–8) or at least T couples placed together (eqs. 25–35, pp. 8–9).

  The rank-share terms steer any deviation from the stable solution "in the direction that maximizes both" sides' preferences, so it incurs few blocking pairs (p. 8). What changes: the explicit blocking-pair model, about 300,000 binaries and 100 million constraints at BPS scale, is replaced by a learned linear cost that "implicitly optimizes for stability" (pp. 1, 6). What is kept: the assignment constraints, with DA as anchor and baseline.
- **D3 Model and data**: The model has applicants I, programs J, capacities C_j, utilities p_ij and q_ij, and valid pairs S. A blocking pair is defined by conditions (a1/a2) and (b1/b2) (pp. 4–5). All data are synthetic.
  - School choice: 92 schools and 16,255 students "constructed based on the BPS 2017 Transportation Challenge dataset" (p. 10). Student utility (eq. 36) mixes distance, the School Quality Framework score and Uniform(0,1) noise. There are five student types φ ∈ {0, .25, .5, .75, 1}, and each student ranks 2–9 schools, giving 95,076 admissible pairs. School utility (eq. 37) uses µ = 0.75 (pp. 10–11). [Reader's note: as printed, distance enters both utilities with a positive sign, and no transformation is stated (pp. 10–11).]
  - Residency: 92 hospitals and 16,255 residents, 2,000 of them in 1,000 couples (p. 15). "For tractability", partners share the same set of acceptable hospitals (p. 8).
  - The appendix theorem rests on two assumptions (pp. 18–19). Assumption 1: the error of the inverse cost is i.i.d. sub-Gaussian. The paper calls this most reasonable for a small blocking-pair budget B and weaker as B grows (p. 18). Assumption 2: optimal assignments are Lipschitz in the cost vector.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Devices:
  - an exact MIO "allowing no more than B number of blocking pairs", built from indicator logic for unmatched, under capacity, applicant unhappy and program unhappy (constraints 1–8, pp. 5–6);
  - inverse optimization from LP strong duality: dual feasibility, complementary slackness and equal objectives, plus a normalization Σb = 1 and a regularizer toward an initial vector b̄ (after Ahuja–Orlin) to rule out trivial cost vectors (eqs. 14–18, p. 7);
  - rank-share preference weights |P<(i,j)|/|P(i)| ∈ [0,1) used as linear rewards (p. 8);
  - the standard product linearization for couples (eqs. 26–29, p. 9);
  - Lemma 1: the number of blocking pairs is Lipschitz in ℓ1 with constant (p_max + 2C_max)/2, proved by counting the pairs one reassignment can change (pp. 18–19);
  - Theorem 1: an exponential tail bound on the excess blocking pairs of the inverse method over the exact one, chaining Lemma 1, Assumption 2 and sub-Gaussian norm concentration (pp. 19–20).

  [Reader's notes:
  1. Assumption 2 asks a map from all of R^|S| into the finite set of 0–1 assignments to be Lipschitz. Such a map is continuous on a connected space and therefore constant, so as stated the assumption cannot hold whenever the optimal assignment depends on the cost (p. 19).
  2. The proof identifies the inverse-method solution with x(b_inv), although problem (19)–(24) also carries preference terms and the travel constraint (pp. 8, 19).
  3. As extracted, the sign of v_j in complementary-slackness constraint (16) differs from (15) (p. 7).]
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - BPS base case (pp. 11–14):
    - DA matches 15,466 students and leaves 789 unmatched, with 3.21 miles of travel on average (p. 11).
    - With the number matched fixed at DA's, a 5% cut in average travel costs 0.7% blocking pairs and a 10% cut costs 3% (p. 11). The λ's are tuned by grid search for each T (footnote 2, p. 11). The deviation in the offer-rank distribution is "marginal when small numbers of blocking pairs are allowed" (p. 11).
    - With travel held at DA's level, matching 100 more students costs 1.8% blocking pairs and 200 more costs 3.6% (pp. 12–13).
    - Across 15 homogeneous preference problems, a 5% blocking-pair budget yields travel cuts of 3% to 20%. The gain is smallest when students rank by distance, a bad case the paper explains (p. 13).
    - Starting from a travel-minimizing stable solution instead of DA gives indistinguishable curves. This is shown on a different instance, because the base case's stable solution is unique (pp. 13–14, footnote 3).
  - Exact vs inverse on 200 students and 7 schools: the exact MIO reaches fewer blocking pairs for the same travel cut. The gap is at most 1 point and shrinks above 10%. The inverse method takes seconds per weight set and minutes at full size, while the exact method "fails to find an optimal solution or even a feasible solution in hours" (p. 15).
  - Residency: DA places 549 of 1,000 couples together. A 5% budget raises this to nearly 60% (+9%) and a 10% budget to 67% (+23%) (pp. 15–16). The offer-rank distribution stays similar (p. 17).
  - Not reported (searched, not found): solver, version, hardware, time limits, the λ grid, code, variation across random preference draws, and the number of students involved in blocking pairs.
  - [Reader's arithmetic: in the base case, 0.7%, 3% and 5% of 95,076 admissible pairs are about 670, 2,850 and 4,750 blocking pairs. Matching 100 or 200 more students costs about 1,710 or 3,420 blocking pairs, roughly 17 per extra student matched.]
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Against the DA tradition (Roth 1986, 2008; Abdulkadiroğlu et al. 2005a, b), with its "almost religious devotion to stability" (p. 2).
  - Credits optimization inside stability: the stable admissions polytope (Baïou–Balinski 2000), MIO preprocessing (Delorme et al. 2019), correlated lotteries and community cohesion (Ashlagi–Shi 2014; Shi 2015), and distributional goals (Bodoh-Creed 2020) (p. 3).
  - Papalexopoulos (2022) is the immediate predecessor, with a presolve that scales only the fully stable case (pp. 3–4, 6).
  - Couples: Kojima et al. (2013), McDermid–Manlove (2010) and Biró–Klijn (2013), plus Manlove et al. (2017), who show that minimizing blocking pairs is NP-hard and hard to approximate (pp. 2–3).
  - Inverse optimization: Ahuja–Orlin (2001) and Chan et al. (2025) (p. 7).
  - Incentives (strategy-proofness, a main reason DA is used) are not discussed; "incentives" appears only in the title of Kojima et al. (p. 21).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - A question title, answered in the conclusion's last sentence: relaxing stability is "not only viable, but in many contexts, desirable" (p. 17). The contributions are bold-labelled bullets (pp. 3–4).
  - Headline against body (Tension T6). The contributions promise "up to more than 20%" less travel "with as low as 5% of blocking pairs" (p. 4). That is the best of 15 preference variants; the base case gives 5% at 0.7% and 10% at 3% (pp. 11, 13). The "small percentage" of blocking pairs is measured against the 95,076 admissible pairs (p. 11), not against students. The run time is given as "in seconds" (p. 4) and as "in minutes" at realistic size (p. 15).
  - Concessions sit in the body ("Admittedly", p. 15), and the theorem's own text weakens its assumption (p. 18).
  - v1 slips: w_ij is defined with r_ij (p. 5); constraint (4) has y_i for y_j (p. 5); the domains of y and z are swapped (p. 15); |Q(i)| appears where Q(j) is defined (pp. 8–9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: an algorithmic device and computational evidence for policy, with one probabilistic bound under strong assumptions (pp. 10–20).
  - Limits stated: the exact method is better on small instances (p. 15); Assumption 1 weakens as B grows (p. 18); couples must share acceptable sets (p. 8); the data are synthetic (pp. 10, 15).
  - Not discussed: strategic reporting under the new mechanism, who bears the blocking pairs, and whether a district or match operator would accept the trade.
  - No future-work section (p. 17); other objectives such as racial integration "can also be easily incorporated" (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 3: evidence ✅ (pp. 3–4, 11–13, 15–16). The property priced is the incumbent's axiom. Its relaxation becomes a curve: blocking pairs against travel, matches and couples placed together. The bad case is printed and explained (gains as low as 3% when students rank by distance, p. 13). The choice goes to the decision-makers: policy-makers "may choose the weights they would like to place on stability and other objectives" (pp. 3–4).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 11, 18–19). The knob the decision-maker cares about, B, is not set directly; the curve is traced through T and a grid search on λ (footnote 2, p. 11). The bound is not a knob–property bound like Γ's: it bounds the heuristic's excess over the exact curve, under an assumption that cannot hold as stated (D4).
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 5–7, 15). The exact formulation is written down (constraints 1–8) and its size printed (p. 6). Structure is read off the LP dual (eqs. 14–18), and the boundary is shown: at scale the exact model finds no feasible solution in hours (p. 15).
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 6–8). The device keeps the class by giving up exactness. The dual yields a surrogate cost that encodes stability only "implicitly", and an extra modelling assumption is made "for tractability" (p. 8).
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 10, 15). DA, the incumbent, is both anchor and opponent. No solver, time limit, certificate or gap is reported, and the exact model's failure "in hours" is undocumented (Tension T1).
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 4, 6, 15). There is no why-now figure. The intractability verdict is inherited from a 2022 thesis by counting variables and constraints, then confirmed by a run whose parameters are not given. The response is a surrogate, not a re-measurement with today's solvers (cf. Taste warning sign 8).
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 2–4, 11–17). The baseline is the mechanism that BPS and residency matches use. Outcomes are decision metrics: travel, number matched, couples together, offer ranks. The trade-off is handed to policy-makers rather than fixed by the authors (pp. 3–4), in the direction of SKILL.md's stakeholder correction.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 10, 15). Preferences are simulated; there is no owner involvement or field test, and incentives are not examined.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ [inferred] (pp. 1, 3–4, 18). A new ORC student carries a Boston Public Schools line forward from a predecessor thesis's stated limit; compare Heuristic 7 (retry with the next student).
- Heuristic 3: evidence (pp. 6, 15). The exact model stalls on size; the paper switches to a tailored heuristic, keeps the MIO for a small instance, and concedes the gap.
- Heuristic 1: variant (pp. 1, 6–7). The paper recovers the objective behind the incumbent's output, but by inverse optimization of one stable solution, not by finding the robust problem a heuristic solves.
- New-pattern candidate: **own predecessor system's limit as the entry point**. The predecessor thesis's MIO "is challenging to scale ... when stability is relaxed" (p. 4). All four works in this batch share this entry.
- New-pattern candidate: **Size-count intractability verdict licensing a tailored heuristic**: about 300,000 binaries and 100 million constraints at BPS scale (p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: **exact optimum on a small solvable instance as the certificate for a heuristic**: 200 students and 7 schools (p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: **Inverse optimization of a rival's decisions**: the cost vector that makes DA's stable output optimal (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To relax a hard combinatorial property at scale, first recover by inverse LP a cost vector that makes the incumbent's property-satisfying solution optimal. Use dual feasibility, complementary slackness and strong duality, plus a normalization and a regularizer toward a non-trivial starting vector. Then optimize the new objectives with that cost as a proxy (eqs. 14–24, pp. 7–8).
- Reward deviations by rank shares |P<(i,j)|/|P(i)| ∈ [0,1), so that moves away from the stable solution go up both sides' preference lists (p. 8).
- Show a policy trade-off three ways: a curve at fixed volume, a curve that varies the volume (students matched) at fixed cost, and the distribution of offer ranks. The change is then visible beyond one scalar (pp. 11–13, 17).
- Stress the curve across preference regimes (5 × 3 homogeneous problems) and explain the worst regime (p. 13). Swap the starting stable solution to show the curve does not depend on the anchor (pp. 13–14).
- Benchmark the heuristic against the exact MIO on a sub-instance small enough to solve, at matched values of the other objective (p. 15).
- Count the effect of one reassignment on the number of violated pairs to get an ℓ1 Lipschitz constant for a combinatorial metric (Lemma 1, pp. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "However, the literature is characterized by an almost religious devotion to stability." (p. 2)
- "Admittedly, the exact method is able to achieve a smaller percentage of blocking pairs for the same amount of travel reduction." (p. 15)

### S457 · Multimodal prescriptive deep learning (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2501.14152)
Read level: **full**: all text on pp. 1–21 (methods, experiments, discussion, conclusions, references, Appendix A.1). Pages 22–31 are tree figures whose content did not extract; their captions were read. Tables 1–10 extracted cleanly.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Lisa Everest and Vasiliki Stoumpou (Operations Research Center, MIT, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the 2019–2026 multimodal-AI and prescriptive line. It builds on the group's prescriptive analytics (Bertsimas–Kallus 2020), Optimal Prescriptive Trees (2019), OCT (2017) and HAIM (Soenksen et al. 2022) (pp. 1–3). Optimal Policy Trees (Amram et al. 2022) supply the training objective (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA ML AP}:
  - The entry: ML and DL mostly target prediction accuracy, and the paper claims DL has "underutilized potential" for decisions (p. 1).
  - The problem: from observational data {(x_i, y_i, t_i)}, learn a policy τ(x) that picks the outcome-optimizing treatment. It must cover four treatment scenarios (binary, multiple discrete, single continuous, multiple continuous) and both structured and unstructured inputs (p. 4).
  - Gaps claimed:
    - Regress & Compare, including the group's own 2020 extension, uses "classical machine learning methods" without neural networks. It suffers from few samples per treatment and from treatment-assignment bias, and it is a black box (p. 2).
    - Prescriptive and policy trees are "limited to learning outcome functions that can be modeled by trees of relatively small depth" (p. 3).
    - Causal methods estimate effects, not policies (p. 3).
    - Earlier prescriptive networks handle only binary or discrete treatments (p. 3).
  - The headline novelty: "the first prescriptive method to handle multimodal data" (pp. 1, 4, 17).
- **D2 Key idea**: A four-step pipeline (p. 4):
  1. Embeddings. Structured features are normalized or one-hot encoded; trees keep the original values. Notes go through Clinical Longformer (768-d), are reduced by PCA to 32-d, and the modalities are concatenated (p. 5).
  2. A counterfactual reward matrix Γ, from a direct method or a doubly robust estimator (eq. 1). Continuous doses are discretized (pp. 5–6).
  3. A feedforward network with a softmax over treatments. It is trained to minimize the probability-weighted reward (eq. 4), the softened form of the Optimal Policy Trees objective (eq. 2) "proposed by Amram et al. (2022)" (pp. 6–7). The prescription is the most probable treatment (p. 7).
  4. Mirrored OCTs: Optimal Classification Trees fitted to the network's prescriptions as class labels (pp. 7–8).

  What is kept from the policy-tree line: the reward-matrix objective and the counterfactual step. What changes: the policy class (a network) and the optimizer (Adam on a relaxed objective, p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**: Six real datasets:
  - TAVR: 2,127 patients with radiology reports; the choice is between two valves, to minimize pacemaker implantation (p. 10).
  - Liver trauma: 722 patients from electronic medical records; surgical vs non-surgical treatment; outcome mortality (p. 10).
  - Diabetes: 58,200 type-2 patients at Boston Medical Center, 1999–2014; drug combinations; outcome HbA1c (p. 11).
  - Groceries: The Complete Journey data, 2,500 households, one product (strawberries), 97,295 transactions, prices from $2 to $5 in steps of $0.50, objective revenue; missing prices are imputed by a mode rule (p. 12).
  - Splenic injuries: 35,954 rows, three treatment options (p. 12).
  - REBOA: 9,998 patients; missing values filled by the group's Optimal Imputation with kNN (p. 12).

  Rewards are doubly robust for most datasets. Groceries uses the direct method because price "does not seem" correlated with the covariates (pp. 6, 12; Table 3, p. 12). Data are split 50/50, because the test half needs its own counterfactual estimator (p. 8). The sources and ethics approvals of the TAVR, liver, spleen and REBOA data are not stated (searched, not found).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No theorems (n/a for proofs). Devices:
  - a doubly robust reward matrix (eq. 1, p. 6);
  - a softmax relaxation of an indicator objective, so backpropagation applies (eqs. 2–4, pp. 6–7);
  - knowledge distillation into OCTs (p. 7);
  - four evaluation metrics:
    - relative outcome improvement Ī against the historical treatment, with both arms drawn from the reward matrix (eq. 5, pp. 9–10);
    - realism D̄: the mean absolute difference between prescribed and actual treatments, with discrete treatments ordered by severity (eqs. 6–7, pp. 13–14);
    - coverage N̄ of the treatment space (eq. 8, pp. 14–15);
    - the standard deviation of each sample's prescriptions across 25 models (pp. 15–16).

  [Reader's note: as extracted, eq. (5) takes the absolute value of the difference, which cannot produce the negative improvements of Table 4 (p. 13); the reported values must use signed differences.] The gap between objectives (2) and (4) is not analysed.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol: 5 random splits × 5 models = 25 per method; tuning on 15% of the training half; early stopping; Adam (p. 9).
  - Unstructured data (Table 1, p. 11). Multimodal PNNs beat tabular-only PNNs under both test estimators: TAVR 8.66 vs 3.41 under the tabular estimator and 32.15 vs 20.50 under tabular and notes; liver 32.50 vs 16.34 and 40.30 vs 25.32. Standard deviations are large (e.g. ±6.80, ±14.70). Mirrored OCTs score higher than PNNs on both datasets, which the paper calls "dataset-specific" (pp. 10–11). OCT training accuracy is 75.7–87.7% (Table 2, p. 11).
  - Structured data (Table 4, p. 13), against Regress & Compare (XGBoost, p. 11), Causal Forest and Optimal Policy Trees:
    - PNN is best on diabetes (4.01 vs 2.50), groceries (110.88 vs 106.58) and spleen (20.47 vs 16.41).
    - Optimal Policy Trees are slightly ahead on REBOA (10.79 vs 10.22).
    - Causal Forest is negative on spleen and REBOA, and Regress & Compare on REBOA (−17.80 ± 27.91).
    - Mirrored OCT training accuracy is 91.5–98.0% (Table 3, p. 12).
  - Realism, coverage and stability (Tables 5–10, pp. 14–16). On groceries, PNN is the least realistic (D̄ 1.4386) and the least stable (0.9332), and on diabetes it covers only 16.62% of the treatments; the text still calls these "comparable" (pp. 14, 16). In TAVR, 62% (multimodal) and 45% (tabular) of PNN prescriptions switch valves (p. 14).
  - Controls are proposed but not tested: thresholds or penalties on switches, dropout, and per-treatment probability thresholds (pp. 14–15).
  - Not reported (searched, not found): run times (despite "without requiring large computational resources", p. 17), network sizes, software, baseline implementations, and statistical tests behind "significantly".
  - [Reader's arithmetic: the "1.38% on average" interpretability cost (p. 4) is the mean of PNN minus Mirrored OCT over Table 4: 0.08, 0.66, 4.75 and 0.01 points. One dataset (spleen, 20.47 → 15.72) carries almost all of it.]
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Builds on, and criticizes, the group's own line. Regress & Compare as extended by Bertsimas–Kallus (2020) does "not take advantage of neural networks" (p. 2). Optimal Prescriptive Trees and Optimal Policy Trees keep interpretability but "struggle with learning more complicated functional forms" (pp. 2–3).
  - Other prior art (pp. 2–3):
    - personalization trees (Kallus 2017);
    - causal trees and forests (Athey–Imbens 2016; Wager–Athey 2018);
    - causal boosting and MARS (Powers et al. 2018);
    - offline policy learning (Zhou–Athey–Wager 2023);
    - prescriptive networks for binary treatments trained by MIP (Patil et al. 2024);
    - prescriptive ReLU networks (Sun–Tsiourvas 2023);
    - JANOS (Bergman et al. 2022).
  - Differences claimed: more treatment scenarios and a different training objective (p. 3).
  - Patil et al.'s reference entry calls their models "prescriptive networks (PNNs)" (p. 19); the text does not address the shared name.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Claims and intensifiers: priority "to the best of our knowledge" (pp. 1, 4, 17); "remarkably" (pp. 4, 13); "significantly" with no test (pp. 1, 10, 17).
  - Headline against body (Tension T6). The abstract's 32% and "over 40%" are the multimodal PNN under the tabular-and-notes estimator (Table 1, p. 11). The abstract does say "estimated", and the body admits that the estimator's modality changes the size of the gain (p. 10).
  - The authors say candidly that one favourable result went against their expectation (p. 14). Realism, stability and interpretability get separate sections (pp. 13–17).
  - Draft traces: "Force Appendix above figures" appears five times (p. 21); the Patil et al. reference carries that paper's whole abstract (p. 19); the trees are available only as images and through a Google Drive link (pp. 21–31).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: a framework, an empirical comparison, and interpretability by distillation (pp. 4, 17).
  - Limits admitted in passing: Mirrored OCTs cannot fully capture the PNNs (p. 16); note embeddings are not interpretable (pp. 16–17); results depend on the estimator's modality (p. 10).
  - Not discussed: the bias of evaluating on estimated counterfactuals, the absence of any prospective or field evaluation, and data provenance.
  - No future-work section (p. 17); the controls of pp. 14–15 read as future options.

**Method links**:
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 9, 13–14). The target is prescription, not prediction. Prescriptions are scored against the treatment actually given, and realism is measured as distance from current practice.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 8–10, 12). The improvements are model-estimated outcomes on the test half, not observed or field-tested. The owner's current rule (Method 5's baseline) is here only the observed historical treatment, not a stated clinical policy, and no partner or decision owner is named.
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 6–7, 9, 11, 13). The duel with incumbents (Regress & Compare, Causal Forest, Optimal Policy Trees) on shared splits is kept, but the exact solve is dropped: the policy objective is relaxed and trained by Adam, with no certificate, timing or implementation details. The group's own optimal trees now play the capacity-limited baseline (p. 3; Tension T2).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 4, 13, 21). A price of interpretability is reported, but as one averaged number at a fixed tree depth (7), not as a curve over a knob. The bad case (spleen) is in the table but not discussed.
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 6–7). The exact policy objective is written first, then relaxed so that it stays in the trainer's class of differentiable losses: the deep-learning analogue of class keeping. The relaxation gap is not examined.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 1–3, 7, 12). The paper assembles the group's components (prescriptive analytics, OCT, optimal prescriptive trees, Optimal Imputation, HAIM-style multimodal embeddings) with two new ORC students.
- Taste mark 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 7, 16–17). Interpretability is recovered after the fact from a black-box prescriber, and only partly for multimodal inputs.
- New-pattern candidate: **own predecessor system's limit as the entry point**: the group's trees are "limited to ... relatively small depth" (p. 3), and its Regress & Compare has no neural networks (p. 2).

- New-pattern candidate: **distil the black-box prescriber into an optimal tree**: Mirrored OCTs (pp. 4, 7, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a discrete prescription objective Σ_i Σ_t 1{τ(x_i) = t}·Γ_it into a trainable loss by replacing the indicator with softmax probabilities, then prescribe the argmax (eqs. 2–4, pp. 6–7).
- When the test set must be scored on counterfactuals, split 50/50 so that the test half can carry its own reward estimator (p. 8). With several modalities, report the gain under estimators fitted on each modality (pp. 9–10).
- Recover interpretability by training an optimal classification tree on the black-box policy's prescriptions. Report the tree's fidelity (training accuracy) next to its outcome score (pp. 7, 11–12).
- Audit prescriptions beyond the headline gain: mean absolute distance from the actual treatments, the share of the treatment space used, and the per-sample standard deviation across 5 splits × 5 seeds (eqs. 6–8, pp. 13–16).
- For tabular data with text, embed the notes with a domain language model, reduce them by PCA to a size suited to n, and concatenate the result with the tabular features (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "We find that deep learning has tremendous and underutilized potential in the area of decision-making." (p. 1)

### S523 · Adaptive forests for classification (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2510.22991)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–21, including Appendices A–C (pp. 18–21) and the code-availability line (p. 21). Figure 1 is an image; its caption was read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yubing Cui (Operations Research Center, MIT, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the optimal-trees line, OCT (2017) → Optimal Policy Trees (Amram, Dunn, Zhuo 2022) → OP2T (Bertsimas–Peroni 2024) → Adaptive Forests. It runs on Interpretable AI's software (pp. 13, 20), the company SKILL.md lists under Method 6 (Interpretable AI, 2018).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO DA}:
  - The entry: RF and XGBoost aggregate "equally weighted CART trees" (abstract, p. 1). Weighted-RF variants weight trees by out-of-bag accuracy but apply the same weights to every input (pp. 1, 4).
  - The question: can the weights be unequal and adapted to each input (pp. 1–2)? A panel of specialist doctors, whose opinions should be weighted by patient, motivates it (pp. 2–3, Figure 1).
  - The task is binary and multi-class classification. AF(x) predicts argmax_k Σ_j w_{l(x),j}·f_jk(x): an Optimal Policy Tree routes x to a leaf l that carries a weight vector from a finite candidate set W in the simplex (p. 2). Scoring is by AUC and one-vs-rest AUC (p. 4).
  - The immediate predecessor's limit is the entry for the second contribution: OP2T "is limited to a finite set of pre-defined weights" (p. 7).
- **D2 Key idea**: Read ensemble weights as treatments and the ensemble rule as a prescription. An Optimal Policy Tree assigns a weight vector per leaf; rewards are computed directly from the labels and the base predictions, so no counterfactual estimation is needed (OP2T, pp. 6–7, 12). Then grow the set of treatments:
  - MIOs generate new weight vectors that minimize 0–1 loss on the OPT data or within a leaf. Exploration constraints keep a new vector at least min_gap from all candidates; exploitation constraints keep it within max_gap of a previously selected vector (pp. 9–10).
  - A further MIO keeps the k most promising candidates (eq. 3, p. 11).
  - The OPT is retrained, for at most 10 iterations or until its structure stabilizes (pp. 8, 20–21).

  What is kept: RF-style randomized CART base learners (p. 20). What changes: the aggregation (input-dependent) and the candidate weights (optimized). Base trees are capped at 50 (binary) and 100 (multi-class), against up to 1,000 for the benchmarks (pp. 7, 13).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Data partition (Table 1, p. 9): 20% test; 12% validation, for the choice of configuration; 40.8% to train the CARTs; 27.2% to train the OPT.
  - CARTs are trained on random subsamples of instances and features, with ratios drawn from [0.5, 0.9] (p. 20). The OPT has a maximum depth, a minimum leaf size and a split penalty λ ≤ 0 (p. 6).
  - Configuration choices are picked on validation in the first iteration (pp. 11–13, 20):
    - six reward functions (hard, soft, thresholded soft, Euclidean, KL, negative cross-entropy);
    - OPT inputs with or without the base learners' predictions;
    - four ways to drop "deterministic" instances on which all trees agree (pp. 12–13).
  - The weight set is initialized by uniform sampling of the simplex or by structured warm starts (p. 18).
  - Data: 14 binary and 7 multi-class sets from UCI and the Boston Medical Center diabetes data; n from 74 to 5,000, p from 4 to 45, K up to 12 (pp. 13–15).
  - Stated heuristic assumption: a weight vector that was never chosen is unlikely to have useful neighbours (p. 10).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No theorems (n/a for proofs). Devices:
  - weight selection under 0–1 loss as an MIO with big-M (M = 2) and a strict-inequality ε (eqs. 4a–4f, p. 18);
  - multi-class correctness through per-class comparison binaries b(i, q), joined by an AND linearization B(i) (eqs. 5a–5i, pp. 18–19);
  - a nonconvex quadratic exploration constraint ‖w − w′‖₂ ≥ min_gap, flagged as needing solver settings (p. 10);
  - exploitation through binary slacks with a big-M of 10, at most all but one slack active (p. 10);
  - a top-k selection MIO with leaf-to-weight assignment variables (eq. 3, p. 11);
  - the deterministic-instance filter, justified because no convex weighting can change those instances' predictions (pp. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Treat a model-selection or ensembling choice as a prescription. A policy tree routes each input to a weight vector, and rewards are computed from the labels and base predictions, so no counterfactual estimation is needed (pp. 6–7, 11–12).
- Grow a finite action set by optimization. Solve for a new action that minimizes loss on the current leaf or the whole set; force it at least min_gap away from the existing actions and within max_gap of one already used; then keep only the top k with a selection MIO (pp. 9–11).
- Write the event that the weighted ensemble predicts y correctly as an MIO with per-class comparison binaries and an AND linearization; big-M = 2 suffices because probabilities lie in [0, 1] (pp. 18–19).
- Before learning a weighting policy, drop the training instances whose outcome no admissible action can change, i.e. those on which all base learners fall on the same side of 0.5 (pp. 12–13).
- Report robustness as top-two counts next to averages and wins, and show how sensitive the results are to the base learners' depth (pp. 13–15).
