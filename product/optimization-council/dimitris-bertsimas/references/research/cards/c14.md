### S114 · Equitable and efficient coordination in traffic flow management (Transportation Science 2012, DOI 10.1287/trsc.1110.0393)
**Read level**: full. All 19 file pages of the published INFORMS courtesy copy were read, including the acknowledgments and the reference list. In Tables 10–15 the text layer renders the decimal point as "0" (for example "1208" for 12.8). The table figures below are decoded on that rule and cross-checked against the prose wherever the prose repeats them (p. 15–18). Greek symbols are also lost in the text layer; this card writes λ for TODA's trade-off weight and α for RBS-EP's exponential base.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Cynthia Barnhart (MIT Civil and Environmental Engineering), Constantine Caramanis (UT Austin, ECE), Douglas Fearing (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The paper sits where two of his lines cross. The first is the air-transportation line: Vranas–Bertsimas–Odoni 1994 (the first integer program for the multi-airport ground-holding problem), Bertsimas–Patterson 1998 (whose variables are this paper's foundation) and Bertsimas–Lulli–Odoni 2011 (p. 4, p. 9). The second is the fairness line of Bertsimas–Farias–Trichakis 2011, *The price of fairness*, whose bound the paper says its results agree with (p. 4, p. 15). The paper was received in June 2009, revised in March 2010 and March 2011, and accepted in August 2011 (p. 1). It was funded by NASA NextGen and NSF EFRI. The acknowledgments thank Amedeo Odoni, Bertsimas's own PhD adviser (SKILL.md, Academic Lineage), "for being a supportive and enthusiastic advisor", and Metron Aviation staff for their expertise (p. 19). S044 is its sibling: the same fairness-versus-efficiency question in another application.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Domains: IO + AP. The entry is a conflict inside current FAA practice.
  - Each traffic flow management (TFM) program allocates arrival slots by ration-by-schedule (RBS). A program is either a ground delay program (GDP) at an airport or an airspace flow program (AFP) at a flow-constrained area (FCA). RBS keeps a first-scheduled, first-served (FSFS) order, the industry's accepted standard of fairness (p. 1–2).
  - When programs overlap, one flight can receive conflicting controlled departure times. The FAA resolves the conflict with one of two heuristics: *precedence RBS*, the Flight Schedule Monitor default that gives the GDP priority, or *exemption RBS*, enabled in March 2009 (p. 2–3).
  - It matters in practice: in 2007 at least one GDP and one AFP were active on about 40% of days (Table 1, p. 2), and TFM accounts for about 30% of all delays (p. 2).
  - A four-flight example (Tables 2–6) shows that, once programs conflict, FSFS at every resource and minimum total delay cannot both hold (p. 3).
  - The literature has split in two (p. 3–4). Single-resource models handle equity and collaboration, run fast, and were adopted. Network models aim at computational efficiency and gained less traction. The paper restricts the network problem to the coordination of conflicting programs in order to bridge the two (p. 4).
  - The problem is stated as three requirements on a formulation (p. 5): it must reduce to RBS for a single resource; it must give a bicriterion trade-off curve between delay and fairness; it must compare favourably with current practice.
- **D2 Core idea & design**: Generalize the accepted fairness rule instead of replacing it.
  - **The metric.** Time-order deviation (TOD) charges a flight only for delay beyond its *maximum expected delay*: the largest delay that its scheduled position would receive at any of its controlled resources (§2.3, p. 7–8). The metric is derived from five properties that explain why RBS is accepted (§2.2, p. 6).
  - **The models.** Two integer programs on the Bertsimas–Patterson time-indexed variables trade delay against fairness:
    - TODA, a convex approximation of TOD with weight λ (§3.3, p. 10–11);
    - RBS-EP, which adds no variables or constraints and changes only the objective coefficients. Each interval of delay beyond the flight's worst independent-RBS delay d^RBS_MAX(f) is penalized exponentially, with base α (§3.4, p. 11).
  - **What is kept and what is changed.** Kept from Bertsimas–Patterson: the variables and the capacity structure (p. 9). Changed: a fairness term is added, only controlled resources are modelled, delay is ground delay only, and aircraft-connectivity constraints are dropped (p. 11–12).
  - **Why.** Integration: the models use the same inputs as the existing TFM programs and output slot assignments, which is stage 1 of the collaborative decision-making (CDM) process (p. 12).
- **D3 Model & data**: A deterministic, multiresource ground-holding model.
  - Time is discretized into 5- or 15-minute intervals. Capacities b_rt are set for controlled resources only. Each flight's controlled flight plan is a sequence of steps, each with an earliest start time and a processing time (§3.1, p. 8–9).
  - Choices admitted as simplifications or as matching practice:
    - equality in step precedence (4) forbids airborne delay (p. 9);
    - aircraft connectivity is omitted as "an explicit modeling choice", and its impact is called an open question (p. 12);
    - the intra-day dynamics of the programs are not modelled (p. 12);
    - each flight's delay is capped at d^RBS_MAX(f) + 15 or 30 minutes (p. 14).
  - Data. A clear-weather schedule (23 April 2007) from Flight Schedule Monitor is overlaid with the programs of 10 representative 2007 days that had both GDPs and AFPs (p. 13). Historical scenarios cut all arrival capacities by 7.5%, to offset the lighter schedule; hypothetical ones also cut FCA capacities by 25% (p. 13). FCAA08 is replaced by FCAA06 because the data carry only sector entry and exit times (p. 13).
  - Scenario size: 1,572–5,191 flights, with 4.3%–15.7% of flights in conflicting programs (Table 10, p. 13, decoded).
- **D4 Formulation & analysis**:
  - **Foundation** (p. 9–10). Cumulative binaries y_fit = 1 if step i of flight f has started by time t. Constraints: monotonicity (1), completion (2), earliest start (3), step precedence (4), and capacity through y_fit − y_fi(t−p) (5). The start time is s(f,i) = T − Σ_t y_fit, and the objective is total delay (6).
  - **Resource-ordered view for TODA** (p. 10–11). Variables u_rjt = 1 if j tasks of resource r have started by time t. They carry monotonicity (9)–(10) and task order (11), and a counting constraint (12) links them to the flight variables. The time of the j-th controlled arrival at a resource then becomes linear, and so does the expected delay ED(f,i) (13).
  - **Non-convexity handled by approximation** (p. 10–11). The max over steps in the maximum expected delay (7) is replaced by an average over I_MAX(f), the steps that independent RBS predicts to be most delayed (14). The resulting term equals TOD when that prediction is right and otherwise overstates it (15).
  - **RBS-EP** (p. 11). Coefficients c_ft (16) make the marginal cost of each extra interval beyond d^RBS_MAX(f) grow by the factor α; the objective is (17).
  - **Solution devices** (p. 14, p. 18):
    - the delay window, without which some instances would have "well over a million" binaries;
    - greedy integer rounding of the LP relaxation, ordering flights by their relaxed first-step start times, so that a good feasible schedule exists right after the root node;
    - a CPLEX relative gap of 0.01%.
  - **No theorems.** The properties of TOD, and the claim that TOD is zero exactly when the schedule equals the RBS allocations (independent resources), are argued in prose (p. 8). The only complexity remark is that the general TFM scheduling problem is NP-hard, so the incumbents' inefficiency is "not surprising" (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational & empirical evaluation**:
  - **Design.** 40 instances: 10 days × {historical, hypothetical} × {precedence, exemption RBS} (p. 15). The baselines are the FAA's two current rules, replayed in the historical order in which programs were reported (p. 13).
  - **Levelling** (§4.1, p. 12). Precedence RBS may exceed AFP capacities. The overruns of its compressed schedule are therefore given to the optimization models as raised capacities. The authors still say the field is tilted toward precedence RBS.
  - **Operating points** (p. 15–16):
    - RBS-EP (Fair, 30): the smallest α whose schedule is at least as fair, by TOD, as the incumbent's;
    - RBS-EP (1.001, 15): α fixed at 1.001, with a 15-minute window.
  - **Results** (Tables 11–14, decoded; prose p. 15–17):
    - Against exemption RBS, historical days: delay falls 4.0% and 4.5% (7,339 → 7,046 and 7,008 hours, p. 17), with 1.6% unfair delay against 9.3%.
    - Against exemption RBS, hypothetical days: delay falls 8.8%–9.6%.
    - Against precedence RBS: 0.4%–2.5% on historical days and 5.3%–6.4% on hypothetical days (p. 17).
    - Over the 20 scenarios of each baseline, RBS-EP (1.001, 15) allocates 1.8% unfair delay against 1.5% for precedence RBS, and 2.2% against 6.5% for exemption RBS (p. 15–16).
  - **Trade-off curves** (Figs. 5–6, p. 14). On 7/16/2007, RBS-EP tracks TODA's approximate frontier but cannot reach its low-unfairness end. TODA is intractable on the complex scenarios; it was compared with RBS-EP only on smaller constructed scenarios "outside of the ones shown here" (p. 14).
  - **Failure reported** (Figs. 7–8, p. 17).
    - The problem: at α = 1.001 the delay distribution has a longer tail. Many flights are delayed at least 2.5 hours, more than the maximum under exemption RBS, and the authors call this "likely a significant issue".
    - The fix: α = 2.001 shrinks the tail, at a cost of 1,429 → 1,443 hours (historical) and 1,527 → 1,581 hours (hypothetical).
  - **Run time** (Table 15, p. 18). Under 10 s of CPLEX time per instance (largest decoded value 9.03 s). The 15-minute window is about 40% faster. The machine is a PC with dual Xeon 3220 quad-core processors and 16 GB of RAM, running Ubuntu 8.04 and CPLEX 11.2 through Java.
  - **Money** (p. 17–18). An attribution chain starts from the Joint Economic Committee's $25.7 billion:
    - 62.3% of delay is direct impact;
    - about 30% of that is due to ground holding;
    - 13% of it falls on days with both GDPs and AFPs;
    - so a 1% reduction is worth just over $10 million a year.

    The chain ends at $25–$50 million a year, and two reasons why it underestimates are stated (p. 18). The estimate is computed against exemption RBS, because the extra FCA capacity created by precedence RBS has downstream costs that are hard to evaluate (p. 16).
  - **Not reported**: TODA solve times as numbers; per-flight unfairness beyond the delay distributions of Figs. 7–8; any field trial.
  - **A detail to check against the PDF** (decoded): the prose names 6/19, 6/27, 7/18 and 7/27 as the days on which exemption RBS is most unfair (p. 16), but Table 12 also shows 13.0% unfair delay on 5/2/2007.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues Bertsimas's own models: Vranas–Bertsimas–Odoni 1994, Bertsimas–Patterson 1998 and Bertsimas–Lulli–Odoni 2011. Bertsimas–Gupta (2011, working paper) is cited as related work (p. 4). Lulli–Odoni (2007), on inequities in network formulations, is the "critical backdrop" (p. 4). TOD is presented as a generalization of the exemption-bias measure of Vossen et al. (2003), with two named differences (p. 7). The network school, his own earlier models included, is said to have gained less traction "Arguably" because it failed on equity, collaboration and tractability (p. 4). Current practice is credited first, with RBS's three salient features (p. 4), and only then shown to fail on constructed examples (p. 5–6).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract ends on a dollar figure (p. 1), the introduction opens with the economy-wide cost of delay (p. 1), and four contributions are numbered (p. 4). Design requirements are listed before any model (p. 5). Every concept comes with a small worked example: the four-flight conflict (p. 3, p. 5–6) and a TOD calculation in which 60% of one flight's delay is unfair (p. 7–8). Hedging is moderate ("we believe", p. 4 and p. 6; "Arguably", p. 4). Weaknesses are stated in the body: the tilted comparison (p. 12), the intractable TODA (p. 14) and the heavy tail (p. 17). There are no theorems; the paper consists of a model, a metric and computation.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a model, an algorithm and an application: a fairness metric for the multiresource setting, two integer programs, and evidence from historical scenarios (p. 4, p. 18). The paper frames itself as "a bridge" toward national-scale TFM (p. 5) and the RBS-EP model as the "first step in an ongoing sequence of practical enhancements" to the FAA's procedures (p. 18–19; the phrase itself is on p. 19). The limits are concrete:
  - aircraft connectivity and the intra-day dynamics of programs are left as open questions (p. 12);
  - TODA is not tractable at scale, and RBS-EP cannot reach the low-unfairness end of the frontier (p. 14);
  - the tail at low α (p. 17);
  - the savings estimate rests on an attribution chain (p. 17–18).

  No deployment is reported.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence.
  - The owner is the FAA. Its current rules (precedence and exemption RBS in Flight Schedule Monitor) are the baselines, replayed in historical order, and the data come from the owner's own decision-support tool (p. 2–3, p. 12–13).
  - The metrics score decisions: total delay, unfair delay and dollars (p. 15–18).
  - The paper reports its own costs: the tail and the run times (p. 17–18).
  - Step 6 (field test) is absent.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence, with a ⚠ variant.
  - Evidence: the property is fairness, defined from the stakeholders' own FSFS standard. It gets one knob (α in RBS-EP, λ in TODA), a trade-off curve, and a printed bad case: the tail and the unreachable low-unfairness end (p. 14–17).
  - Variant: this paper has no solution-independent bound. The knob is a penalty base, and the reported operating point is chosen to match the incumbent's fairness (p. 15). The theoretical bound is left to the 2011 price-of-fairness paper (p. 4, p. 15).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence for step 3 (a class-keeping device): RBS-EP puts fairness into the objective coefficients of the nominal time-indexed model, with "no new variables or constraints" (p. 11), so the fair model is as large, and as fast, as the nominal one. TODA linearizes an order statistic (p. 10–11). There is no dual reading (step 4 is absent).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the incumbent's allocation bounds each flight's delay window, and LP rounding supplies the incumbent at the root node. The reported 0.01% gap therefore refers to the restricted problem; the suboptimality of the restriction is acknowledged (p. 14, p. 18). The duel with the incumbent is held at matched capacity (p. 12).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence for step 3: a programme lineage (1994 → 1998 → 2011 → this paper), with Odoni thanked as advisor (p. 4, p. 19).
- Heuristic 4 ✅: the negative results sit in the body: the tilt, the heavy tail, the intractable TODA (p. 12, p. 14, p. 17).
- Tension T6 (headline vs caveat) ✅: the $25–$50 million headline (p. 1) uses the exemption-RBS baseline; against precedence RBS the historical gains are 0.4%–2.5% (Table 11, decoded; choice explained p. 16).
- Roundtable Card, blind spot *fair benchmarking* ✗ contradicted here: the comparison with the incumbent is explicitly levelled, and the remaining tilt is stated (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidates:
  - (a) *adoption-compatible design*: keep the incumbent's inputs, outputs and accepted fairness standard, and require the model to reduce to the current rule in the simple case (p. 4–5, p. 11–12);
  - (b) *dominate the incumbent at its own fairness level, then price the relaxation* (p. 15–17);
  - (c) *level the field and say which way it still tilts* (p. 12);
  - (d) *axioms, then metric*: list the properties that make the accepted rule acceptable, then build a metric that satisfies them and reduces to the rule (p. 6–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] When stakeholders already accept a fairness rule, write down why they accept it as properties. Then build a metric that satisfies them, extends to the harder setting and is zero exactly on the accepted rule in the simple case (p. 6–8).
- [D4] To use *the time of the j-th event at resource r* in a time-indexed integer program, add cumulative ordered indicators u_rjt. Link them to the entity-indexed indicators with a single count-equality constraint per resource and period (constraints (9)–(12), p. 10–11).
- [D4] Replace a nonconvex max over components by an average over the components that a cheap heuristic predicts to be maximal. The approximation errs on the conservative side: it overstates the metric unless the prediction is right (p. 10–11).
- [D4] In time-indexed scheduling models, encode a fairness or priority preference purely through objective coefficients on start times (here exponential beyond a fair threshold). No variables or constraints are added, so the model keeps its size and speed (p. 11).
- [D4] Use the incumbent's allocation to bound each variable's domain (here d^RBS_MAX(f) + 15 or 30 minutes); this keeps the binaries from exceeding a million (p. 14).
- [D4] When a decision is due within a minute, round the LP relaxation greedily (here by relaxed start times), so that a feasible solution exists right after the root node (p. 14).
- [D5] When the incumbent violates constraints, feed its violations to your method as relaxed capacities, and say which way the field still tilts (p. 12).
- [D5] Report two operating points: the smallest knob value that is at least as fair as the incumbent (dominance), and a fixed near-efficient value. Then show the tail of the distribution and how the knob fixes it (p. 15–17).
- [D5] Turn delay reductions into money through an explicit attribution chain, and list how it underestimates (p. 17–18).

### S044 · Fairness, efficiency, and flexibility in organ allocation for kidney transplantation (Operations Research 2013, DOI 10.1287/opre.1120.1138)
**Read level**: full, of the preprint. The text file is the 24 January 2011 preprint (31 pages; its title has no serial comma). It was read in full, including the list of acronyms, the SRTR disclaimer and the references. The published version (Oper. Res. 61(1):73–87) was not read. Its abstract (abstracts.json) differs: it speaks of "several case studies" and adds case studies that "pursue the design of policies targeted specifically at remedying criticisms leveled at the recent point system proposed by U.S. policy makers". The preprint contains no such case study. Pages from this card are preprint pages; cite them as [S044 preprint p. N].

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vivek F. Farias (MIT Sloan and ORC), Nikolaos Trichakis (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The fairness programme with Farias and Trichakis, after *The price of fairness* (Oper. Res. 2011). Trichakis's 2011 thesis, *Fairness in operations: from theory to practice*, was co-advised by Bertsimas and Farias [04 T15]. The CV listed this paper as *Flexibility, Fairness and Efficiency in Kidney Transplantation*, submitted to Oper. Res. in 2011 [03, table of retitled manuscripts]. S114 is its sibling. Note 06 records a 2025 MSOM Society award for responsible research on organ allocation [06]; this batch did not check whether the award concerns this paper.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Domains: DA + AP, with LO as the engine. The entry is a live policy redesign.
  - **The legal frame.** US law treats deceased-donor kidneys as national resources, to be allocated by a priority method over waiting lists without any money transfers (NOTA 1984; p. 2–3). The OPTN Final Rule asks policies to balance medical efficiency and equity without discrimination (p. 7).
  - **Why redesign.** The point system in use for more than 20 years had become inconsistent with the Final Rule as patients accumulated waiting-time points (p. 8–9).
  - **The committee's work.** The OPTN Kidney Transplantation Committee (KTC) chose four criteria: LYFT, dialysis time, donor profile index and CPRA. It tried more than 28 scoring rules by simulation and published a proposal in 2008: KAS = 0.8 LYFT(1−DPI) + 0.8 DT·DPI + 0.2 DT + 0.04 CPRA (p. 9–10).
  - **The paper's challenge.** Of five design challenges (fairness constraints, efficiency, prioritization criteria, simplicity, implementation; p. 3–4), the paper takes "implementation": how to weight the given criteria so as to maximize life-years subject to fairness (p. 4).
  - **The question** asked of the KTC proposal: is there another scoring rule "of the same simple format that dominates the proposed one, i.e., is equally or more fair and more efficient?" (p. 10). Finding weights is hard because simulating a single scoring rule "takes hours" (p. 11).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: Three steps (Procedure 1, p. 15).
  - (i) **Hindsight LP.** Solve the LP of a planner who knows every eligible patient–organ pair: maximize total LYFT subject to matching constraints and linear fairness constraints Ax ≤ b (problem (1), p. 14).
  - (ii) **Duality.** Price the fairness constraints by their optimal duals y. The problem becomes a matching problem with adjusted values c = LYFT − (yᵀA) (problem (2), p. 14).
  - (iii) **Regression.** The policy must act online and rank by the chosen criteria, so regress c on the score components to get the weights (p. 15).

  What is kept: the point-system form, the criteria and the KTC's fairness profile. What changes: only the weights (p. 19–20). The justification is an analogy with Talluri–van Ryzin bid-price controls, for which asymptotic optimality is known: the fairness duals play the role of bid prices, and "one can then derive a similar result" (p. 15–16; footnote 4).
- **D3 Model & data**:
  - **Design model.** It assumes complete ex post information on patients (registration, evolution of status, compatibility) and organs over a period (p. 12), and an eligibility set C (p. 12–13). Fairness constraints are admissible if they are linear in the assignment x: group shares of transplants, or average LYFT or average dialysis time among recipients (p. 13).
  - **Explicit simplifications** (p. 14): the planner has foresight, and patients "accept all organs offered to them". A fractional x is read as a randomized policy.
  - **Evaluation model.** Evaluation uses the SRTR simulator (KPSAM). Its acceptance model is exogenous and fitted to history; it "ignores endogeneity" but reproduces how policymakers simulate (p. 5, p. 18–19). LYFT comes from SRTR survival models (p. 18).
  - **Data.** SRTR data for 2008: the first six months for design, the last six for evaluation (p. 19).
  - **Score components.** LYFT, dialysis time (piecewise linear, three pieces) and CPRA. DPI "proves superfluous" (p. 19, notes 5–6).
  - **Fairness inputs.** Lower bounds at the KTC policy's simulated recipient shares by race, age, dialysis time, blood type, diagnosis, CPRA band and the 11 UNOS regions (p. 20; constraints (3)).
- **D4 Formulation & analysis**:
  - An LP with bipartite matching constraints (1) (p. 14).
  - LP duality turns the fairness side constraints into per-pair adjustments and leaves a matching problem (2) (p. 14).
  - An ordinary least-squares projection of the dual-adjusted values onto the interpretable components gives the scoring rule (p. 15).
  - The preprint has no theorem or proof; near-optimality rests on the bid-price analogy (p. 15–16).
  - [inferred] The lower bounds cover every cell of each partition at the KTC's own shares, which sum to 100% (p. 20); in the design LP they therefore act as equalities.
- **D5 Computational & empirical evaluation**:
  - **Benchmark.** The KTC's 2008 proposal, simulated with the KPSAM, survival models and SRTR data that the KTC itself uses (p. 18–19).
  - **Out-of-sample results** (Table 1, p. 22):
    - transplants: 5,746 (KTC) vs 5,796 (designed policy);
    - net LYFT: 34,290 vs 37,092, an increase of 8.2% (p. 21);
    - average LYFT: 5.95 vs 6.4.
  - **The designed rule** (p. 21): KAS = LYFT + g(DT) + 0.12 CPRA. For dialysis time, g awards 0.55 points per year up to 5 years, 1 per year up to 10, and 0.25 beyond.
  - **Fairness profile.** The text calls it "almost identical" (p. 21), and Fig. 2 compares the 11 regions (p. 23). Read off Table 1, though, several groups fall below the KTC shares that served as lower bounds (p. 22):
    - African-American: 35.7% → 33.3%;
    - age 34–49: 26.4% → 24.8%;
    - hypertension: 21.6% → 19.2%;
    - age 18–34: 5% → 4.2%.
  - **Side effect on equity** (p. 24). Under the designed policy, recipients with under 5 years of dialysis gain 9.7% more LYFT than the average recipient (14.2% under the KTC policy). Those with more than 5 years gain 13.4% less than the average (19.4% under the KTC policy).
  - **Sensitivity** (p. 24–27).
    - The knob: a uniform slack s on the dialysis-time constraints or on the CPRA constraints.
    - The result: LYFT rises to about 44,300 years, 30% above the KTC policy, more steeply for dialysis time (Figs. 3–4).
    - The KTC's operating point is marked on the same axes, and such relaxed policies "might not be implementable" (p. 25).
  - **Not reported**: run times and sizes of the LP and the regression (number of eligible pairs), the regression fit, the number of simulation replications or their variability, other years or splits.
- **D6 Positioning**: Earlier OR work on organ allocation is credited with designing near-optimal dynamic policies "from the ground up" (p. 5–6):
  - simulation (Ruth et al. 1985);
  - stochastic assignment (Righter 1989; David–Yechiali 1995);
  - a fluid model with fairness (Zenios et al. 2000);
  - patient choice and mechanism design (Su–Zenios 2004–2006).

  This paper instead stays within the "precise constraints imposed by current practice" (p. 6). It contrasts its method with approaches that need "simplifications, clustering or grouping" of patients and organs (p. 16). The KTC is treated as the client, not as a target: its proposal is the benchmark, and the question is "whether this is the best we can do" (p. 10). The bid-price lineage is Talluri–van Ryzin 1998 (p. 15–16).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The headline numbers (8% and 30%) appear in the abstract and in the numbered contributions (p. 1, p. 4–5). "Critically" introduces the 8% twice (p. 4, p. 28). Fig. 1 draws the method as a black box from inputs to weights (p. 11). The policy context (law, rule, committee process) fills about seven pages before the method (p. 2–11). The far end of the trade-off curve is qualified (p. 25). Section 5 is titled "Discussion and Future Directions" but names no future direction (p. 25, p. 28). There are no theorems; the bid-price argument is a discussion section (p. 15–16).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a policy-design method, one designed policy and a trade-off tool (p. 28). The claimed merits (p. 16–17):
  - it scales to detailed data;
  - policymakers only choose the components and the fairness properties;
  - it extends to other organs;
  - redesign is quick if criteria change or the Office for Civil Rights does not approve them.

  The limits are mostly left implicit: near-optimality only by analogy (p. 16); fairness only "approximately satisfied" (p. 11), with some groups' shares dropping out of sample (Table 1, p. 22); exogenous acceptance behaviour (p. 5); a single year of data. No future direction is given (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ strong evidence.
  - The owner is the OPTN/UNOS KTC, and the current rule is its 2008 proposal, with the 20-year-old policy as background (p. 8–9).
  - Scoring uses the owner's own simulator, models and data, on a held-out half-year (p. 5, p. 18–22).
  - The metrics score decisions: life-years, transplants and recipient shares.
  - Step 6 (field test) is absent.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence, with a ⚠ variant.
  - Evidence: the price of fairness is drawn as a curve over one knob, the uniform slack s. The incumbent's operating point is marked, and the far end is flagged as possibly not implementable (p. 24–27).

### S340 · A comparison of Monte Carlo tree search and mathematical optimization for large scale dynamic resource allocation (arXiv preprint 2014, arXiv:1405.5498)
**Read level**: full. The file is arXiv v1 (21 May 2014; "Preprint submitted to Elsevier May 22, 2014", p. 1), 37 pages. All text, algorithms, tables and figure captions were read, as was the reference list. The scatter glyphs of the box plots (p. 20–30) carry no text and were skipped. Whether a journal version exists was not checked (no search in this batch).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. Daniel Griffith and Robert Moss (MIT Lincoln Laboratory), Vishal Gupta and Velibor V. Mišić (MIT ORC), Mykel J. Kochenderfer (Stanford, Aeronautics and Astronautics) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: The work was sponsored by ASD(R&E) under an Air Force contract (p. 1). It is a methods comparison where three of his lines meet (p. 33): his dynamic resource allocation framework (Bertsimas–Gupta–Lulli, EJOR 2013, ref. [12]), his fluid models of queueing networks (Avram–Bertsimas–Ricard 1995, ref. [5]) and air traffic flow management (ref. [13]). Wildfire returns as an application in his 2026 preprints on sensor placement and drone routing [06 §8].

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Domains: SD + HS + IO, with AP as the testbed. The entry is a question between two communities.
  - **The gap.** AI and OR each have a successful framework for dynamic stochastic optimization: Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS) and mathematical optimization (MO). But "the relative merits of these two approaches are not well understood" (p. 1).
  - **The case against assuming MCTS transfers.** MCTS has succeeded in games whose states evolve deterministically and whose branching is small (at most 81 in the Go instances cited). The dynamic resource allocation (DRA) instance here branches about 230 million ways (p. 2).
  - **The OR side.** Re-optimization (model predictive control, fluid approximations, certainty-equivalent control) works well in practice and sometimes carries guarantees, while MCTS has little computational record on DRAs (p. 3).
  - **The testbed.** Tactical wildfire management, chosen for its importance ($3.5 billion of federal spending in 2013) and its difficulty (p. 3). Earlier wildfire OR (Hu–Ntaimo; Ntaimo et al.) addresses initial attack and simulation, not the tactical DRA (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: Adapt each school's flagship method to one MDP and compare them under fixed budgets.
  - **MCTS.** Double progressive widening with a UCB exploration bonus (Algorithm 1, p. 7–9), plus two DRA-specific changes:
    - action generation that mutates or recombines actions already in the tree, chosen by tournament selection on Q (Algorithm 2, p. 9–10);
    - a strong rollout policy, the Floyd–Warshall (FW) heuristic, which weights each cell by Σ_y R(y)/D(x, y) (p. 10–11).
  - **MO.** A deterministic, "smoothed" fluid version of the dynamics, with continuous fuel and fire intensity, written as a mixed-integer linear program. It is re-solved at each decision step, and the first allocation is applied (p. 11–14). From the fluid literature (control of queueing networks, revenue management) it keeps the idea of continuous deterministic rates (p. 11–12). What is new is a linear dynamical system for intensity with binary fuel switches (p. 4, p. 13).
- **D3 Model & data**:
  - **The MDP** (p. 5–6). A grid in which each cell has a burning flag and a fuel level. Fuel burns deterministically (1); ignition from burning neighbours (probability P(x, y)) and suppression (probability Q(x), independent attempts) are stochastic (2)–(3). Extinguished cells can reignite, and rewards R(x) are negative.
  - **Simplifications** (p. 5): resources are identical and travel time is ignored. The model is "simplified" on purpose, to make the difference between the methods clearer.
  - **MO calibration** (p. 14–15):
    - the big-M bounds Ī_t(x) come from a recursion with no suppression and unit transmission;
    - initial fuel is F0 = δ + Σ Ī_t;
    - ζ = P, ζ̃ = Q, δ = 0.1.
  - **Data**: synthetic only (Tables 2–3, p. 17–19); there are no real fire data.
    - Grid 1: a linear reward gradient across the grid, P = 0.06, Q = 0.8, k ∈ {8, 12, 16, 20, 30}.
    - Grid 2: an exponential reward profile with parameter λ, fire started in the middle, P = 0.02.
    - Initial fires are random, with up to 845 burning cells.
- **D4 Formulation & analysis**:
  - **The MIO** (5a)–(5j) (p. 13–14). Binaries A_t(x, i) put team i on cell x, and binaries z_t(x) flag fuel below δ. The intensity dynamics are written as an inequality that binds at an optimum. The big-M terms are Ī_t(x)·ζ̃ and (F0(x) + Σ F0(y))·z.
  - **A relaxation policy** (p. 14): make A continuous, score each cell by v(x) = Σ_i A_0(x, i), and assign the teams to the |I| highest-scoring cells.
  - **The only analysis** is the count of the branching factor via Stirling's approximation (6) (p. 26). There is no theorem on either method's performance.
  - **Statistics**: additive-effects models with backward stepwise deletion from second-order interactions, and ANOVA (p. 20, p. 23, p. 27, p. 31).
  - **An ambiguity**: p. 14 says the relaxation strategy is followed in §5, whereas p. 20 and p. 23 speak of the integer program being solved to optimality, or not, within the limit.
  - [inferred] Ī_t(x) adds up the neighbours' bounds each period (p. 15), so the big-M constants grow with the horizon. The strength of the formulation is not discussed.
- **D5 Computational & empirical evaluation**:
  - **Setup** (p. 16):
    - a custom C++ MCTS, and Gurobi 5.0 for MO;
    - 2.2 GHz cores, 4 GB RAM, single-threaded;
    - 60 s per decision for both methods by default (Table 1);
    - 256 simulations per configuration (p. 20, p. 26, p. 29);
    - two straw men: random suppression and the FW heuristic.
  - **MCTS tuning** (Grid 1, k = 20, 4 teams, 10 s; Fig. 5, Table 4, p. 19–22). The exploration bonus matters little with the FW rollout. Depth interacts with progressive widening. Algorithm 2 helps mainly with the weak rollout.
  - **State space** (k = 8–30; Figs. 6–8, Table 5, p. 20–25).
    - Gains over FW: MO +2.22 and +3.80 points (8 and 4 teams); MCTS +0.83–0.87 and +2.74–3.03 points at 60–120 s.
    - Extra MCTS time (60, 90, 120 s) changes little (Fig. 8).
    - MO's per-decision time has a long upper tail, in which the integer program is "typically not solved to optimality" (Fig. 6, p. 23).
  - **Action branching** (2–32 teams; Figs. 9–11, Table 6, p. 26–28).
    - At k = 10, MO and MCTS do not differ significantly (p > 0.4, p. 27).
    - At k = 20, MO beats FW at every team size, while MCTS is significantly worse than FW with 16 and 32 teams (p. 27–28).
    - MO's time cannot be capped exactly, so its time distribution is shown and a 60 s budget for MCTS is argued to be fair (p. 26–27).
  - **Horizon and asymmetric rewards** (Grid 2; Figs. 12–13, Table 7, p. 29–32). MO's edge is largest when the local reward structure is flat (small λ). A horizon of 5 beats a horizon of 2, and 10 adds nothing (p. 29); in the fitted model the horizon effect is significant only for MCTS, not for MO (Table 7, p. 32).
  - **Reported but not discussed**: the effects models have low R² (0.06–0.47; Tables 4–6). Parallelism is not explored (p. 16).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper stands between two traditions (p. 2–3):
  - AI: Coulom; Kocsis–Szepesvári; the survey by Browne et al.; progressive widening by Couëtoux et al.;
  - OR: Bertsekas's certainty-equivalent control, fluid approximations, model predictive control, robust control, chance constraints.

  MCTS is credited generously, as "one of the top performing, general purpose algorithms" for such games (p. 2), before its transfer to DRAs is questioned (p. 2–3). The paper claims the first application of MCTS to a DRA motivated by a real-world problem (p. 4). It cites his own papers on fluid models, robust optimization, the DRA framework and air traffic (refs. [5], [11]–[13], p. 33).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The comparison is framed neutrally (p. 1), and opinions are flagged as such ("In our opinion", p. 2; "we think it is fair to say", p. 2).
  - The main insights are listed before the experiments (p. 15–16), and each visual claim is checked by a fitted effects model with p-values (p. 20, p. 23, p. 27, p. 31).
  - The conclusion claims three "broad methodological contributions" and calls the MO insight "both important and not obvious" (p. 31).

  - Signs of an unpolished preprint: which side of Grid 2 is the more valuable is stated both ways (p. 18, p. 29), and there are typos ("is the one of", p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contributions are methodological and computational (p. 31):
  - MCTS's components are interdependent and must be tuned together;
  - a continuous, deterministic approximation can be an effective heuristic for large stochastic control problems;

  The limits:
  - a simplified MDP, with identical resources and no travel time (p. 5);
  - synthetic grids only;
  - no performance bounds for either method;
