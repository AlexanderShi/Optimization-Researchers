### S115 · Predicting inpatient flow at a major hospital using interpretable analytics (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management 2022, DOI 10.1287/msom.2021.0971)
Read level: **full**, medRxiv preprint (version posted 16 September 2020). The main text (pp. 1–27) and appendices A–D (pp. 31–42) were read in full; the references (pp. 27–30) were skimmed. The MSOM print was not read, so the pages below are preprint pages.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jean Pauphilet (London Business School); Jennifer Stevens and Manu Tandon (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, BIDMC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the health-care-at-scale period. The data are a partner hospital's EHR records for 2017–2018 (p. 6). The tool was deployed in May–June 2019 (p. 21) and ran in production for a year (p. 25), and a second hospital was in progress (pp. 8, 16). Pauphilet is in the skill's ML student lineage (SKILL.md, Academic Lineage). The paper uses two of Bertsimas's own methods as components: Optimal Trees (Bertsimas–Dunn 2017) and an optimization-based imputation method (Bertsimas et al. 2018) (pp. 8–9, 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + ML; DA only as decision support, since the paper calls its models predictive only, p. 21}: The entry is an application under stress plus an implementation gap.
  - The hospital needs forecasts of patient demand to match capacity with demand (p. 1).
  - Automated deep-learning representations of EHR data (Nguyen et al. 2016, Miotto et al. 2016, Rajkomar et al. 2018) predict well in retrospective studies. To the authors' knowledge, none had been integrated into a real hospital's EHR (p. 2).
  - The authors name three bottlenecks (pp. 2–3): black boxes keep clinicians from adopting the models; deep learning is costly in data, people, computing and energy; and clinical notes are rarely available in real time and raise privacy issues.
  - The problem statement, in MSOM's structured abstract: turn raw EHR data into accurate predictions of patient flows and inform daily decisions (p. 1).
  - There are six tasks (pp. 4–5, 9): remaining stay under 1 day and under 2 days; overall stay over 7 days and over 14 days (trained on the first four days of each stay only); discharge destination among four classes; and need for an ICU bed in the next 24 hours. The paper claims the ICU task had not been studied before (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: An expertise-driven patient representation on a hospital-centric clock.
  - Every inpatient becomes an observation at 11:59 pm each day. The alternative is a patient-centric clock that starts at admission (p. 7).
  - There are 600+ covariates. Most are daily counts of events (orders, labs, medications, documents written), plus the last value, average, amplitude and trend of vitals and labs. All are computed natively in SQL inside the hospital's database (pp. 8, 31–33).
  - Standard learners are run on this representation: Lasso-penalized logistic regression (LR), CART, Optimal Trees (OT/OCT), random forest (RF) and gradient-boosted trees (GBT). The authors say outright that the methods are not novel (p. 5).
  - Changes relative to Rajkomar et al. 2018 (pp. 4–5, 18): the clock; expert features in place of learned ones; no medical notes; interpretable models; deployment in production with dashboards.
  - What stays the same: off-the-shelf learners, with AUC as the accuracy yardstick (pp. 10–11).
  - Why: the three bottlenecks. The paper argues that interpretability and frugality buy adoption without losing accuracy (pp. 5, 18).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Population: BIDMC inpatients admitted January 2017 – August 2018. Psychiatry, obstetrics, newborns, and observation patients who did not stay overnight are excluded. That leaves 63,432 admissions of 41,726 patients. The IRB approved the study with a waiver of consent (p. 6).
  - Size: 323,274 patient-day observations (p. 7). Training and validation hold 50,467 admissions and the test set 12,965 (Table 2, p. 7).
  - Missing data: mostly-missing indicators get their own category; vitals and labs are filled by linear interpolation; the rest by the optimization-based imputation of Bertsimas et al. 2018 (p. 8). In production, a tree sends a missing value the way most training observations went (pp. 8–9).
  - Assumptions the authors admit:
    - Some outcomes are endogenous to physicians' decisions and to hospital inefficiencies. The models therefore learn how long a patient will stay, not how long she should stay, and must not be used as a recommendation engine (p. 10).
    - Static variables are assumed constant during a stay, which may not hold (p. 31).
    - Training observations are weighted by class prevalence (p. 10).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No optimization model is posed; the devices are statistical.
  - Temporal split: train January–December 2017 (60%), validation January–April 2018 (20%), test May–August 2018 (20%). The authors argue that random splits produce statistically similar sets and overstate out-of-sample performance (p. 10; Appendix B, p. 35).
  - AUC is reported with 95% CIs from 1,000 bootstrap samples (p. 11).
  - From individual scores to the hospital's daily discharge count, in two stages (Appendix D, pp. 41–42):
    - Option one: sum the scores and multiply by a correction factor α calibrated on the training set. Option two: threshold then sum, with the threshold set so that false positives equal false negatives.
    - Raw sums are useless because class weighting biases the scores (median absolute error for CART: 109.7 beds for the raw sum vs 6.0 with a day-of-week α).
    - A day-of-week α works best (Table 14, p. 42).
  - Tree stability: the best depth is chosen independently on each of 10 folds as the training window grows, following Zimmermann 2008. The mean settles near 5.5 with 6+ months of data, and the dispersion falls from 3.7 to 0.7 (Figure 3, pp. 18–19).
  - Difference-in-differences (DiD): April vs July 2019 against the same months of 2018, with day-of-week and hour controls and robust SEs, on 5,126 observations (Table 8, pp. 22–23).
  - Within-period check: the 2,117 emergency-department (ED) patients of May–June 2019, split into 116 day × hour strata, comparing days the tool was used with other days (Table 9, pp. 23–24).
  - Marginal value of data: training windows of m = 1–24 months ending in December 2018, tested on January–August 2019 (pp. 25–26).
  - OCT is described as Bertsimas–Dunn's large mixed-integer optimization problem (MIO), solved approximately by a local-search heuristic (p. 36). It is used as a packaged learner, and no certificate or gap is reported.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: five learners, with hyperparameters tuned on the validation set (pp. 10, 36–37). The grids are depth 5–20 and leaf size 50–300 for trees, 200–400 trees for RF, and depth 3–7 with up to 10 trees for GBT.
  - Hardware and software: Python 3.5.2 and Julia 1.0.1 on a MacBook Pro (2.5 GHz i7, 16 GB), presented as affordable for most hospitals (p. 11). No training times are reported.
  - Retrospective test-set results (pp. 11–15):
    - Same-day discharge: AUC 0.843 (RF; CI 0.839–0.847). The daily discharge count has a median absolute error of 6.2 beds and out-of-sample R² 0.841 (pp. 11–12).
    - Next-day discharge: 0.822 (GBT) (p. 12).
    - Stay over 7 days, after day 1: 0.827 (LR) and 0.830 (GBT) (p. 12). Stay over 14 days: 0.826 (LR) (p. 13).
    - Discharge destination: weighted AUC about 0.77 for trees and 0.83 for ensembles; LR only 0.561 (pp. 13–14).
    - Mortality: 0.962 with the Do-Not-Resuscitate (DNR) indicator and about 0.90 without it (RF 0.905) (p. 14).
    - ICU need: 0.968–0.977 for every method (Table 5, p. 15).
  - The optimal-vs-greedy duel, here [my reading of Tables 3–4, pp. 13–14]:
    - OT and CART are nearly tied: 0.810 vs 0.807, 0.790 vs 0.786, 0.776 vs 0.775. On destination OT is below CART (0.769 vs 0.781).
    - For the daily count, CART has the lowest median absolute error (6.0 beds) and the highest R² (0.868) of the five models; RF has the lowest median relative error (5.8%) (Table 3, p. 13).
    - The text names the ensembles the most accurate and notes that the logistic model is within 4% of the best (p. 15).
  - Literature benchmarks (Safavi 2019, AUC 0.840; McCoy 2018, mean absolute errors of 11.5 and 11.7 beds; Rajkomar 2018) are compared on different populations, with the caveat that such comparison is always difficult (pp. 12, 14).
  - Field test against the incumbent rule: on 15 of 42 working days in May–June 2019, the bed-management nurse recorded both the tool's forecast and the resource nurses' estimates.
    - Median relative error: OCT 11.5% vs nurses 16.0%. The IQRs are 8.9–13.3% vs 5.3–20.3% (p. 21; Table 7, p. 22).
    - The text calls the model significantly more accurate, but no test is reported.
    - The production error is about twice the retrospective 6%, which the authors attribute to decalibration (pp. 21–22).
  - Operational outcomes:
    - DiD: the off-service placement coefficient is −0.043 (SE 0.017, p < 0.01). The boarding-delay coefficient is −0.120 (SE 0.174), not significant (Table 8, p. 23).
    - Stratified: boarding delay has a median ATE of −0.18 h [IQR −2.02; 1.21]. Off-service placement has a median ATE of 0.00, with 38% of strata negative and 39% positive (Table 9, p. 24).
  - Maintenance (Figures 6–7, pp. 25–26):
    - The error rises about 1 percentage point every three months, holidays are poorly captured, and the error matches the test error until 8 months after training ended (p. 25).
    - About 14 months of data reach full accuracy; the curve is concave (p. 26).
    - Hence the advice to retrain twice a year with a fixed window (p. 26).
  - Failures printed in the body: decalibration; holiday errors; LR's destination AUC of 0.561; useless raw sums (pp. 14, 21–22, 25, 41–42).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The rival is deep-learning EHR pipelines, chiefly Rajkomar et al. 2018. The paper does not attack their accuracy; it matches or approaches their numbers. It attacks deployability (pp. 2–3, 18):
    - Their pipeline needs immoderate computing power and daily notes.
    - Their attribution visualizations require retraining on restricted data, so the model shown differs from the model used.
    - The authors' own experience "has been different" (p. 18).
  - A condensed literature table is restricted to recent, prediction-focused work on similar outcomes (Table 1, p. 4).
  - The temporal split is offered as a reason why the authors' numbers are conservative relative to randomly split studies (p. 10).
  - Van Walraven–Forster is credited for the two-stage count method (pp. 11, 41).
  - Self-citations cover components only (OCT; the imputation method) (pp. 8–9, 27).
  - The company behind OCT appears only as "Interpretable AI 2020" documentation and as "the commercial package" (pp. 18, 27, 41).
    - The preprint PDF contains no competing-interest or funding statement; the text was searched for conflict, competing, disclos and funding.
    - The medRxiv web page and the MSOM print were not checked.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - MSOM's structured abstract (problem definition, practical relevance, methodology, results, managerial implications) carries the first headline number, accuracy "in the 80%+ range" (p. 1). The contributions add the second: a "4% reduction (p-value < 0.01)" in off-service placements, credited to the dashboard (p. 6).
  - The paper is candid about novelty ("not novel", p. 5) and uses frequent first-person judgement ("In our opinion", pp. 2, 15, 24; "In our experience", p. 2).
  - Limitations get their own subsection (§3.5, p. 16), and the impact study names its own main limitation (p. 24).
  - Headline vs table [my reading]:
    - The "4%" is the DiD coefficient on the off-service share (−0.043, about 4 percentage points). It rests on a parallel-trends assumption that the text states (pp. 22–23).
    - The within-period check, meant to free the result from that assumption, shows an off-service median ATE of 0.00 (Table 9, p. 24). The text reads this as an improvement in the majority of cases, citing the nonpositive median (p. 24).
  - Slips: Figures 6 and 7 carry the same caption, though Figure 7 plots AUC against the length of the training window (pp. 25–26). The logistic model is called "simple linear regression" (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution level: application and deployment, with no new method. The paper delivers a representation, a production pipeline, dashboards, a field evaluation and a maintenance protocol (pp. 4–6, 27).
  - Stated limits:
    - a single centre, with a second hospital under way (p. 16);
    - the models absorb operational inefficiencies, and length of stay alone may not be the right outcome (p. 16);
    - EHR deployment needs advanced data management and excludes notes (p. 16);
    - in the impact study, nurses drove treatment assignment, which was noisy, and patients were few (p. 24);
    - decalibration (pp. 21–22).
  - Future work:
    - tools built on top of the prediction engine to turn predictions into actionable recommendations (p. 24);
    - checking that accurate predictions improve quality of care (p. 27);
    - replication at a second hospital (pp. 8, 16).

### S139 · Bootstrap robust prescriptive analytics (Mathematical Programming 2022, DOI 10.1007/s10107-021-01679-2; arXiv 1711.09974)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v2 (arXiv:1711.09974v2, 8 June 2021). The main text (pp. 1–23) and Appendices A–B (pp. 25–32) were read; the proofs were followed but not re-derived line by line. The references (pp. 23–25) were skimmed. Displayed formulas are partly garbled in the extraction (for example (22), (25) and (32)), so statements about them rest on the legible parts and the prose. The MP print was not read, so the pages are arXiv pages.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Bart Van Parys (MIT ORC, p. 1; supported by a Swiss National Science Foundation Early Postdoc.Mobility fellowship, p. 23) · **Position in trajectory**: the DA programme's next step after *From Predictive to Prescriptive Analytics* (Bertsimas–Kallus, cited as Management Science 66.3, p. 23). It joins the DRO line of Van Parys's own work (Van Parys–Esfahani–Kuhn 2017, *From data to decisions: Distributionally robust optimization is optimal*, pp. 4, 24). The arXiv identifier dates v1 to November 2017 [inferred from the identifier]; v2 is from June 2021 (p. 1). The skill's Method 5 checkpoint cites this paper as the group's fix for ML weights that turn optimistic in high covariate dimension.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + RO; CP as the engine (exponential-cone programs); ML (local learners)}: The entry is a failure mode of his own earlier method.
  - Estimate-first-optimize-later prescriptions (nearest neighbours, Nadaraya–Watson kernels; Bertsimas–Kallus 2020; Hannah–Powell–Blei 2010; Ban–Rudin 2019) are consistent. Unlike empirical risk minimization (ERM) over a restricted class, though, they have no built-in remedy when they overfit (pp. 3–4).
  - Their estimators have large variance, especially when the covariate dimension is large. Minimizing them amplifies that variance into overly optimistic prescriptions (Michaud 1989; Ban–Rudin 2019) (p. 4).
  - The paper states that data, not distributions, should be the primitive of decisions under uncertainty (p. 2).
  - It names the failure event: a decision is "disappointing" when its actual cost exceeds its budgeted cost (p. 4).
  - The problem: build robust budget formulations for contextual prescriptions whose bootstrap disappointment can be controlled explicitly (pp. 6–7).
  - The paper says the merits of DRO for problems with contextual information have not been studied in depth (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Robustify the local estimator, not the decision rule.
  - The cost estimate of a decision at context x0 becomes the worst case of the kNN or kernel estimator over all reweightings D of the training points within a relative-entropy (KL) ball of radius r (Definitions 5–6, pp. 12–13).
  - The KL ball is chosen because it is the rate function of bootstrap resampling. Bootstrap disappointment is therefore at most Σj exp(−n·max{r, r_n^j}) (Theorem 6, p. 15), or exp(−n r) for kernels (Corollary 2, p. 16).
  - Any distance larger than KL on an open set, that is, any smaller ball, loses this rate (Proposition 1, p. 16).
  - What changes relative to Bertsimas–Kallus: the estimator's distribution, from the empirical one to a KL ball around it.
  - What stays the same: the estimator; its consistency, provided r(n) shrinks fast enough (Theorems 3–4, p. 14); and the convexity of the problem (p. 8).
  - Why: out-of-sample guarantees in the style of their equation (6) need parametric classes, which the authors judge hard to justify and possibly pessimistic. So they target the bootstrap instead, a proxy that Efron (1982) already advanced (p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The decision z and the parameter y are finite-dimensional; the data are i.i.d., and time, if it matters, enters as a covariate (pp. 1–2).
  - Assumption 1: the loss is convex in z and measurable in y (p. 9). The consistency theorems also need a bounded loss (p. 14).
  - Kernel bandwidth h(n) = c·n^−δ with δ ∈ (0, 1/dim(x)); number of neighbours k(n) = ⌈min{c·n^δ, n}⌉; randomized tie-breaking for kNN (Györfi et al. 2006) (pp. 8, 14).
  - The ambiguity set contains only distributions on the observed points (D_n, p. 12). Distance functions must discriminate and be convex (Definition 4, pp. 11–12).
  - Tractability choices the authors admit: no parametric assumption on the prescription, and hence no out-of-sample theorem. They can only hope that bootstrap data are a good proxy (p. 7).
  - Data: a synthetic newsvendor only. Demand is N(100 + (t − 20) + 20·[weekend], 16), with temperature t ~ N(20, 4) and a uniform day of week. Everything is evaluated at one context, x0 = (10 °C, Friday) (pp. 18–19).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - LP characterization of the local estimator under any resampled distribution:
    - A scaling variable s with P = s·D linearizes the weighted-average ratio [my label: the Charnes–Cooper device; the paper does not name it].
    - kNN's data-dependent neighbourhood is handled through nested neighbourhoods N_n^j, so the estimator is the maximum of partial estimators (Theorem 1, p. 11; proof B.1, pp. 26–27).
  - Robust counterpart: the equality s·D = P is replaced by the perspective constraint s·R(P/s, D) ≤ s·r, which is convex for any convex distance R. The robust problem is then a minimum over z of a maximum of concave suprema, and it is convex whenever the nominal problem is (Theorem 2 and proof, pp. 12–13; Corollary 1 for kernels, p. 13).
  - The closest rival as a special case: Hanasusanto–Kuhn's (2013) robust Nadaraya–Watson is the same program with s fixed to one value (Remark 1, p. 13).
  - Consistency (Theorems 3–4, p. 14; Appendix A, pp. 25–26; B.4–B.5, pp. 28–30):
    - Pinsker's inequality turns the KL radius into a total-variation bound.
    - The numerator and denominator of the ratio estimator are bounded separately (using Walk 2010, Lemma 6).
    - The robust estimator is then uniformly consistent if √r(n)/h(n)^dim(x) → 0 (kernels) or n·√r(n)/k(n) → 0 (kNN).
    - Uniform consistency gives consistent decisions, with an optimality gap of at most 2ε.
  - Bootstrap performance:
    - Csiszár's (1984) finite-sample Sanov inequality for resampled empirical distributions on convex sets (Theorem 5, p. 14), plus a union bound over the neighbourhoods (Theorem 6, p. 15; Corollary 2 via B.6, p. 30).
    - Radius rules follow: r ≥ (log n + log(1/b))/n for kNN, or r ≥ log(1/b)/n for kernels, gives disappointment at most b. Joint scalings give consistency together with vanishing disappointment (pp. 16–17).
  - Optimality of the KL distance: Sanov's lower bound combined with a Hahn–Banach separation shows that any distance larger than KL on an open set loses the rate (Proposition 1, p. 16; B.2, p. 27).
  - Dual forms: Lagrangian duality with the Fenchel conjugate of λ log λ gives exponential-cone constraints, under Slater's condition r > r_n^j (Lemmas 1–2, pp. 17–18; B.7–B.8, pp. 30–32).
  - Boundary printed: the robust kNN formulation keeps O(n²) terms (p. 17). The radius for a target b cannot be found analytically, only numerically through the convex programs (29) (pp. 14–16).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Julia and Convex.jl, with the ECOS interior-point solver on the exponential-cone duals; versions are not stated (p. 18). A Gaussian smoother for kernels and the Mahalanobis distance for kNN; h(n) and k(n) chosen by ten-fold CV on squared prediction loss (p. 18). Disappointment is estimated with |B| = |V| = 20,000 resamples (p. 19).
  - Baselines (pp. 19–22):
    - nominal kNN (Bertsimas–Kallus) and Nadaraya–Watson (Ban–Rudin; Hannah et al.), as the case r = 0;
    - Hanasusanto–Kuhn (HK), with its radius matched to the bootstrap-robust formulation (BR) on in-sample budgeted cost;
    - Ban–Rudin ERM with linear decision rules, with and without a dummy-factor encoding of the day of week.
  - Disappointment results (Figure 6, pp. 19–20):
    - The nominal formulations disappoint, on bootstrap data and on fresh validation data alike, about half the time. The robust ones decay with n.
    - The kNN bound is loose because of the union bound.
    - The fresh-data curve tracks the bootstrap curve.
  - Against HK (Table 1, p. 20; my reading):
    - BR (b = 0.1) and HK are close to tied over 250 training sets. HK has the lower actual cost at N = 150, 200 and 250 (for example 13.6155 vs 14.3198 at N = 250), and a marginally lower bootstrap disappointment in all four rows.
    - Realized disappointments (0.001–0.094) sit well below the target of 0.1.
    - The text calls the KL distance's advantage rather small and rests the case on calibrating r in advance (p. 19).
  - Spurious covariates (n = 200, 20,000 training sets, r chosen by ten-fold CV) (Figure 7, pp. 19, 21; Remark 2, p. 21):
    - At d = 0, robust beats nominal, and both lie between the full-information and no-information costs.
    - As irrelevant covariates are added, the room for robustification shrinks.
    - The nominal methods at first improve, because CV then picks larger bandwidths or neighbourhoods.
    - The caption claims "large statistical significance" but names no test.
  - ERM: with integer-coded weekdays it is biased. With a factor encoding it reaches the minimum cost faster than the nonparametric methods (Figure 8, p. 22).
  - Solve times: all solves take under one second, and the nonparametric ones are cheaper than ERM (Figure 9, p. 23).
  - Scale: one context and a scalar decision; n up to 800 (Figure 6, p. 20) and 500 (Figures 8–9, pp. 22–23). No real data.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper builds a taxonomy of three data-driven approaches (ERM; estimate-first-optimize-later; robust budget minimization) and places itself in the third (pp. 3–4).
  - ERM is criticized for bias when the true prescription lies outside the class (p. 3). It is credited later, when a parametric assumption fits (p. 22).
  - Elmachtoub–Grigas's "predict, then optimize" is named as the same two-step idea (p. 3).
  - DRO precedents in classical stochastic optimization are listed by type of ambiguity set (pp. 4–5). They include his own robust sample average approximation (SAA) (Bertsimas–Gupta–Kallus 2017) and the second author's paper showing that DRO is optimal (Van Parys–Esfahani–Kuhn 2017).
  - The closest rival, Hanasusanto–Kuhn 2013, is restated as a restriction of the new program rather than attacked (Remark 1, p. 13). It is then matched in a fair-comparison protocol (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The abstract is modest: it illustrates the method on a small newsvendor problem (p. 1).
  - A motivating example with a picture (Figure 2) comes before the formalism (§1.2, pp. 5–7).
  - The failure event the decision-maker feels is given a name: "disappointing" (p. 4).
  - The obvious objection is pre-empted in a "careful reader may remark" paragraph. It concedes that bootstrap robustness promises nothing about actual out-of-sample data (p. 7).
  - There are three numbered contributions, the third a code release (pp. 7–8). Theorems are stated in the body and their proofs deferred to the appendix.
  - There is no conclusion or future-work section: §5 runs straight into the acknowledgments (p. 23).
  - Slips survive into v2:
    - the paper's own term appears as "estimate-first-predict-later" (p. 6) and "estimate-now-optimize-later" (p. 12);
    - Figure 6 credits the kernel bound to "Theorem 2", while the text says Corollary 2 (pp. 19–20);
    - the prose of Lemma 1 lists the dual variables as α, β and η, while the formula has α, η and ν (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Theory and algorithm: convex robust counterparts for local learners under any convex distance (pp. 11–13); bootstrap guarantees and the optimality of the KL ball (pp. 14–16); exponential-cone duals (pp. 17–18).
  - Software: a Julia package on GitLab (p. 8).
  - Experiments: an illustration only, on synthetic data (pp. 18–23).
  - Stated limits:
    - no out-of-sample guarantee (pp. 7–8);
    - the radius for a target b only numerically (p. 16);

    - only a small advantage over HK (p. 19);

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 8, 12–13, 16–18, 23).
  - The robust counterpart keeps the nominal class: it is convex whenever the nominal problem is (p. 8; Theorem 2, p. 12).
  - Its exponential-cone duals are solved by an off-the-shelf solver in under a second (Lemmas 1–2, pp. 17–18; Figure 9, p. 23).
  - The boundary is printed: O(n²) terms (p. 17), and a radius found only numerically (p. 16).
  - Structure is read from duality and from large-deviation geometry (Proposition 1, p. 16).
  - The class here is conic (CP), not LP or MIO.
- Method 3: evidence ✅ (pp. 6, 15–16, 19).
  - There is one interpretable knob, the radius r. A solution-independent bound ties it to a probability the decision-maker feels, the bootstrap disappointment b: exp(−n r) for kernels (Corollary 2, p. 16) and Σj exp(−n·max{r, r_n^j}) for kNN (Theorem 6, p. 15).
  - The knob is set in advance from the target b, and the advantage claimed over HK is exactly this interpretability (p. 19).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 7, 19–21).
  - The bound concerns bootstrap data, a proxy that the authors admit promises nothing out of sample (p. 7).
  - No price curve of cost against r is drawn: Figure 6 plots disappointment against n for three radii.
  - In the cost experiment, r is set by CV, not by the bound (p. 19).
  - The cost comparison with HK is close to a tie, and HK edges it at three of four sizes (Table 1, p. 20).
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 19–22). Only the duel step survives, outside MIO:
  - the nominal methods enter as r = 0;
  - HK is matched on in-sample budgeted cost;
  - parametric ERM is given the encoding under which it wins.
  - There is no warm start, and no certificate beyond solving convex programs.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 4, 19–22).
  - Decisions are scored, not predictions: actual expected cost (computable because the true distribution is known) and out-of-sample disappointment, set between the full-information and no-information costs (pp. 19–22).
  - But there is no owner, no current rule and no real data: the test case is a synthetic newsvendor.
  - Against the skill's checkpoint: the paper does name high covariate dimension as the source of optimism (p. 4). Yet its own spurious-covariate experiment shows the robust layer's gain at d = 0 and reports limited room for improvement as irrelevant covariates are added (Figure 7, p. 21). The fix is demonstrated for small samples, not for high dimension.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 8, 23). A programme step: a postdoc extends the line of *From Predictive to Prescriptive Analytics* (Bertsimas–Kallus 2020) into DRO, with a public Julia implementation (p. 8).
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted):
  - Heuristic 4, evidence: the near tie with HK, ERM with factors converging faster, gains shrinking as spurious covariates are added, and the loose union bound are all in the body (pp. 19–22).
  - Tension T3, evidence: the guarantee is a large-deviation theorem plus consistency asymptotics (pp. 14–16), so probability sits at the centre of an optimization paper.
  - Tension T5: about three and a half years from v1 (November 2017, by the identifier) to v2 (June 2021).
  - Tension T6, counter-evidence: the caveats sit in the abstract and the introduction (pp. 1, 7–8). The one exception is Figure 7's untested "large statistical significance" (p. 21).
- New-pattern candidate: **provable guarantee on a resampling proxy, then an empirical check that the proxy tracks fresh data** (pp. 6–7, 14–16, 19–20).
- New-pattern candidate: **rival method as a special case or relaxation of one's own formulation** (Remark 1, p. 13; compare card S010 in c16, pp. 9–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To robustify any weighted-average estimator (kernel, kNN), write it as an LP in P = s·D. Then replace the equality by a convex divergence ball in perspective form, s·R(P/s, D) ≤ s·r; convexity in the decision is kept (pp. 11–13).
- (D4) Handle a data-dependent kNN neighbourhood through nested neighbourhoods and a maximum over partial estimators (Theorem 1, p. 11).
- (D4) For a guarantee on resampled data, use the KL ball. Csiszár's finite-sample Sanov bound gives disappointment ≤ exp(−n r); for a target b, take r ≥ log(1/b)/n (kernels) or r ≥ (log n + log(1/b))/n (kNN) (pp. 14–16).
- (D4) Prove that your divergence ball is the least conservative one that delivers a rate: combine Sanov's lower bound with a Hahn–Banach separation (Proposition 1, pp. 16, 27).
- (D4) Dualize the supremum over a KL ball with the Fenchel conjugate of λ log λ. The result is an exponential-cone program that ECOS-class solvers handle (Lemmas 1–2, pp. 17–18, 30–32).
- (D4) To keep consistency while robustifying, convert the KL radius to total variation with Pinsker's inequality. The radius must vanish faster than the estimator's localization scale (h(n)^d, or k(n)/n) (Theorems 3–4, pp. 14, 28–30).
- (D5) Test a data-driven decision method on synthetic data whose conditional distribution is known, so that actual cost is exact. Draw the full-information and no-information costs as bounds (pp. 19, 21).
- (D5) Report the guarantee's target (bootstrap disappointment) and the real target (disappointment on fresh validation data) side by side, to show that the proxy holds (Figure 6, p. 20).
- (D5) Compare against a rival robust method only after matching its radius on a common in-sample quantity, here the budgeted cost (p. 19).
- (D5) Stress-test with d irrelevant N(0, 1) covariates, and say when robustification stops helping (Figure 7, Remark 2, p. 21).
- (D7) Add a "careful reader may remark" paragraph that says what is not proven, before a reviewer does (p. 7).
