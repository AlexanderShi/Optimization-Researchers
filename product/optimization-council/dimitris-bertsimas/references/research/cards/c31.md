### S347 · A stochastic benders decomposition scheme for large-scale stochastic network design (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2025, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2023.0074; arXiv 2303.07695)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v2 (arXiv:2303.07695v2, 29 Apr 2024, p. 1).
- Main text pp. 1–26 read in full.
- Appendices A–E (pp. 33–48) read; the proofs of Propositions 1, 2 and A.1 were followed but not re-derived line by line.
- References (pp. 27–32) skimmed.
- The IJOC print was not read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ryan Cory-Wright (Imperial College Business School), Jean Pauphilet (London Business School), Periklis Petridis (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a late-period IO work built on two earlier papers of the group:
- the 2021 decomposition for MIO with logical constraints (Bertsimas, Cory-Wright and Pauphilet, SIOPT): Proposition 1 follows its Theorem 2.5 (p. 9), and the regularizer is justified from it (p. 33);
- the 2022 stochastic cutting planes (Bertsimas and Li, p. 6).

It also returns to network design, which his 1993 and 1998 papers attacked with LP-relaxation approximation algorithms (cited p. 5). The SKILL.md lineage lists Cory-Wright and Pauphilet among his ML-line students; the paper itself gives only affiliations.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO; LO as the duality engine; RO in Appendix A; network design as an AP testbed}:
  - The entry is a scale verdict: "stochastic network design problems with as few as 100 nodes are, to our knowledge, currently regarded as intractable", and in practice they are handled by domain heuristics (p. 2). Perspective-based decomposition reaches about 50 nodes (p. 2). Generic branch-and-bound fails at tens of nodes: CPLEX 12.8 on ten-node instances (p. 5).
  - The second gap comes from stochastic programming. Sampled Benders cuts (Infanger 1992) are only probabilistically valid and "may cut off part of the feasible region when combined" (p. 12). Because such cuts persist across iterations, unlike SGD steps, they also suffer the optimizer's curse (pp. 12–13).
  - The problem is a stochastic multi-commodity capacitated fixed-charge network design (MCFND) problem (problem (1), pp. 3–4): demand scenarios from historical data weighted equally (SAA), binary edge decisions, a cardinality budget c0 and a quadratic flow term.
  - Generality is claimed for two-stage stochastic problems with a discrete first stage and a sample-average second-stage cost (pp. 1, 3, 26).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - The idea: sample a subset Rt (10%) of scenarios at each Benders iteration and solve only those subproblems. Every unsampled scenario receives the average of the sampled optimal duals. Any dual-feasible point yields a valid linear underestimator, so the aggregated single cut (9) is "deterministically valid (although not necessarily tight)" (pp. 12–13).
  - [My reading of (4), p. 8: the demand d enters only the dual objective, so all scenarios share one dual feasible set and an average of feasible duals stays feasible. The paper says only "cheap to compute and feasible dual average solution", p. 13.]
  - Variants:
    - sampled multi-cut needs no averaging (Algorithm 1, pp. 11–12);
    - an "accelerated multi-cut" adds one averaged cut on the unsampled ηr (Remark 3, p. 15);
    - a k-cut variant clusters scenarios by their optimal duals at z0 (Appendix C, p. 38).
  - What changes relative to the closest methods:
    - Infanger's unbiased but invalid sampled cut (8) becomes a biased but valid cut (9) (p. 13).
    - Ramírez-Pico et al. aggregate demands within fixed clusters; this paper aggregates duals and resamples for each incumbent (Remark 2, p. 15).
  - What stays: Geoffrion's generalized Benders on a perspective-type reformulation, run single-tree with lazy constraints (pp. 2, 17–18). Because the cuts are not binding, the solver's lazy-constraint logic is wrapped in an outer loop (Algorithm 3, pp. 18–19).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Class: MIO in binaries z with a convex QP second stage. The "if" logical constraint is kept instead of big-M so that the quadratic term and the capacities can give a tighter (perspective, SOC) convexification (p. 4).
  - The quadratic term 1/(2γ)(Σk x)² is an admitted tractability device. The appendix describes it as "approximating the nominal objective function (to arbitrary precision by taking γ → +∞) while improving computational tractability" (p. 33), with v(γ) − Σu²/(2γ) ≤ v(∞) ≤ v(γ) (p. 33). Section 4 uses γ = 1 (p. 21); Appendix D describes the chosen value as a balance point read off runtime and objective curves on R instances (p. 40).
  - Uncertainty: demands only, as equally weighted historical scenarios (pp. 3, 8).
  - Assumptions for the analysis:
    - a feasible second stage for Proposition 1, with Farkas feasibility cuts otherwise (pp. 8–9);
    - bounded optimal duals at fixed z for Proposition 2 (p. 36);
    - "mild assumptions" such as finite variance, with |R| large relative to |W|, for the CLT stopping rule (pp. 16–17).
  - Synthetic data, generated after Günlük and Linderoth (pp. 21, 41, 45):
    - connected k-nearest-neighbour graphs with k = 6, all-to-one commodities, demands U(5, 20), capacities scaled by the maximum cumulative demand, c0 = 2|E0|;
    - 10–700 nodes, 5–50 commodities, 10–100 scenarios, three random instances per cell;
    - "relatively complete recourse", because pre-existing edges guarantee feasibility (pp. 1, 20).
  - Benchmark data: Crainic's R instances (pp. 24–25, 41): classes 4–10, minors 1, 3 and 9, demand correlations 0.0 and 0.8, and 10–1,000 scenarios taken from Rahmaniani et al.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Dual representation of the second stage (Proposition 1, p. 8):
    - f(z; d) is a pointwise maximum of functions affine in z, hence convex. Each dual-feasible point gives a valid cut, and the optimal one gives a subgradient (p. 9).
    - The proof (p. 35) splits the flow part from the quadratic part and introduces a coupling multiplier α. It exchanges min and max with no constraint qualification, because the constraints are linear and the objective is convex quadratic (footnote, p. 35). It handles the logical constraint by the substitution y = z·w.
    - Feasibility cuts (5) come from Farkas's lemma, cited from the LO textbook (Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis 1997, Theorem 4.6, p. 9).
  - Error bound for the averaged cut (Proposition 2, pp. 13–14; proof pp. 36–37):
    - With probability 1 − 3δ, the total underestimation over unsampled scenarios is at most L√|R∖Rt|·ν plus an estimation term, where ν² is the variance of the optimal duals across scenarios.
    - Devices: Lipschitz continuity of q in the duals, a triangle inequality, Bardenet–Maillard concentration for sampling without replacement (used twice), and a reading of the second term as a bootstrap estimate of ν.
    - By the probabilistic method some sample achieves the bound, so the algorithm run without termination returns a ρ-optimal solution almost surely (p. 14).
    - [My reading: the statement calls L and M "universal constants" (p. 14), but the proof defines M as the largest dual norm at the fixed z and L as "some constant" (p. 36); both depend on the instance.]
  - Convergence: finite for exact cuts, by finiteness of {0,1}^E (p. 11); almost sure for the sampled multi-cut, because every subset is sampled infinitely often (p. 12).
  - Termination by Morton's (1998) rule (pp. 16–17):
    - An upper confidence bound on the incumbent's cost, from an independent sample W, is compared with the deterministic lower bound: stop when gap (14) ≤ ε.
    - Admitted caveat: on adversarial instances, a fixed sample size can stop at a highly suboptimal solution with high probability (Morton's Example 1, p. 17).
    - [My reading: the paper defines qα by P(N(0,1) ≤ qα) = 1 − α and builds a confidence interval "at level α" with q_{α/2} (p. 17). Its setting α = 0.90 (pp. 18, 21), read literally, gives q_{0.45} ≈ 0.13, a 10% interval. Presumably 90% confidence is meant, but the text never prints q.]
  - Single-tree safety (pp. 17–19):
    - Solvers assume that a lazy cut is binding where it was generated and never revisit that point, so a non-tight sampled cut can make the solver stop "with a highly suboptimal solution it deems optimal" (p. 18).
    - The fix is an outer loop (Algorithm 3, pp. 18–19). It moves the lazy pool into the model as ordinary constraints, purges the pool and reruns, and it tests termination only between outer iterations to limit multiple testing.
    - The authors relate it to restarting single-tree Benders (Fischetti et al. 2016, p. 18).
  - Robust reading of the regularizer (Appendix A):
    - For fixed z, the quadratic penalty and a worst case over an ellipsoid of cost perturbations give the same optimal flows (Proposition A.1; proof by duality and the monotonicity of √t, pp. 33–34).
    - With binary z the equivalence is z-specific. The Boolean-relaxation version, Proposition A.2, is stated with "proof omitted" (p. 34).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup:
    - MIT Supercloud, Intel Xeon Platinum 8260; Julia 1.7.3, JuMP 0.21.10, Gurobi 9.5.1 (p. 20; p. 18 names "Gurobi version 9.1.2").
    - 4–176 GB RAM and 1–44 cores, depending on instance size (Table E.1, p. 42).
    - Limits: 7,200 s for the whole outer loop, ε = 1%, sampling rate 10%, γ = 1, warm start from the original connected graph (p. 21).
  - Metric:
    - Gaps are computed with the incumbent's true cost over all scenarios, and "the time required to calculate this true cost is not included" in run times (p. 21). [My reading: only the sampled methods need this evaluation, so excluding it favours them.]
    - Average run times are capped at the limit; the fraction of instances solved is reported to show the censoring (Table E.3, p. 43).
  - Ablation within the method:
    - three cut schemes × three root-node warm starts (Tables 2–3, p. 22);
    - root-node cuts "more than halve" the final gap (p. 21);
    - one outer iteration suffices in 50% of runs on small instances and 70% on medium ones; otherwise a single iteration "often wrongly terminates" (Figure 1, pp. 22–23).
  - Opponents (p. 23):
    - Gurobi on the perspective formulation of (1);
    - deterministic single-cut Benders with the same root-node cuts. It is the naive variant of Table E.4, the best of four: valid inequalities made it worse, and Magnanti–Wong cuts "do more harm than good" (pp. 20, 43).
    - Every Benders-type method gets the same accelerations (p. 19). All get the same warm starts, the initial network and the all-edges network, "to better isolate the numerical benefit" (p. 19).
  - Results:
    - Gurobi solves the 10-node instances, ends with gaps above 30% at 20–70 nodes, and cannot handle 100 nodes or more within the time and memory (> 72 GB) limits (p. 23).
    - Gaps from Table 4 (p. 24):

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 4, 8–9, 23–24).
  - The stochastic MIO is written as a pure-integer master over z with linear cuts, and its structure is read off the dual: convexity in z, subgradients, Farkas certificates (p. 9).
  - The logical constraint is kept in "if" form so that a tighter-than-big-M convexification is available (p. 4), which sides with strong formulations in Tension T4.
  - The boundary is printed: Gurobi fails at 100 nodes or more (p. 23), and gaps of 22–40% remain at 500–700 nodes (p. 24).
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 19, 21–24, 43).
  - Kept: solver, version, machine and limits stated; a gap for every result, computed from the true cost; duels against the field's standard (deterministic Benders, best of four variants) and the direct solver.
  - Changed: the warm start is deliberately not an incumbent heuristic, to isolate the algorithmic gain (p. 19), and validation stays in-sample on the SAA problem.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 6). The reopened belief is a current one, not an old one: 100-node instances are "currently regarded as intractable" (p. 2). The "why now" is a transferred idea (sampling from ML) plus modern valid inequalities ("sleeping beauties", p. 6), not a rebuilt solver × hardware figure.
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 21, 33, 40). γ is one knob with a solution-independent sandwich bound and a runtime–objective curve (Figure D.1). But it prices tractability against fidelity, the authors fix it from their own plot (γ = 1), and no decision-maker owns it.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 5, 6, 9, 33). A programme continuation: it reuses the group's 2021 decomposition and regularizer and the 2022 stochastic cutting planes, with coauthors at Imperial, LBS and the MIT ORC.
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted):
  - Heuristic 1: evidence. The computational regularizer is reinterpreted as a robust counterpart over an ellipsoid (Proposition A.1, pp. 33–34).
  - Heuristic 4: evidence. Losses are printed in the body (pp. 22, 25–26), and harmful accelerations are tabulated (Table E.4, p. 43).
  - Tension T1: handled honestly, with true-cost gaps throughout (p. 21).
  - Tension T6: "certifiable optimality" in the framing (pp. 3, 7) against gaps of up to 40% (p. 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Sample scenarios inside an exact cutting-plane method without losing validity: give each unsampled scenario a dual-feasible point (here the average of the sampled optimal duals) instead of a scaled sample estimate. When the uncertainty sits only in the right-hand side, all scenarios share the dual feasible set, so the cut stays a valid lower bound (pp. 8, 12–13).
- (D4) Bound the error of such a cut by two terms: the heterogeneity of the duals across scenarios (their variance ν²) and a concentration term for sampling without replacement (Bardenet–Maillard). Then use the probabilistic method to show that some sample attains the bound (pp. 13–14, 36–37).
- (D4) With non-binding cuts, do not trust the solver's lazy-constraint bookkeeping. Run single-tree branch-and-cut inside an outer loop that converts the lazy pool to ordinary constraints, purges it and reruns, and test termination only between outer iterations (Algorithm 3, pp. 18–19).
- (D4) Stop a sampled method with Morton's rule: an upper confidence bound from a fresh independent sample against a deterministic lower bound. When scenarios are few, compute the true upper bound instead (pp. 16–17).
- (D4) Justify a computational regularizer three ways: as a cost model, as a robust counterpart (for fixed integer decisions, the penalty equals a worst case over an ellipsoid), and as an approximation with an explicit sandwich bound (pp. 33–34).
- (D5) Isolate the algorithmic gain: give every method the same naive warm starts and the same accelerations, and pick the opponent's best variant from an ablation table that you publish (pp. 19, 43).
- (D5) Report true gaps by evaluating the returned solution on all scenarios, and say that this evaluation time is excluded. Print the fraction of instances solved next to time-capped averages (pp. 21, 43).

**Quotes**:

- "However, we do not implement a more sophisticated warm-starting strategy for any of our methods, to better isolate the numerical benefit of our decomposition schemes." (p. 19)

### S381 · An artificial intelligence interpretable tool to predict risk of deep vein thrombosis after endovenous thermal ablation (Journal of Vascular Surgery: Venous and Lymphatic Disorders 2025, DOI 10.1016/j.jvsv.2025.102253)

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + ML; IO only through OCT as a packaged contender}:
  - The entry is clinical (p. 3):
    - NICE recommended EVTA in 2013 as the preferred treatment for symptomatic venous insufficiency;
    - its heat injury raises concern about venous thromboembolism;
    - guideline bodies disagree on post-procedure duplex surveillance: SVS advises it, ESVS does not;

  - The claimed gap is novelty for this procedure: "the first attempt to utilize an AI-based tool" (p. 14).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - A standard tree-model bake-off on a national registry (pp. 6–8, 10–13):
    1. impute missing values with Optimal Imputation;
    2. split 75/25;
    3. train CART, OCT, random forest and XGBoost with class weights and grid search;
    4. select by AUC;
    5. explain the winner, XGBoost, with SHAP at population and patient level.
  - The stated advantage over conventional risk models (univariate or multivariate regression) is capturing interactions and nonlinearities (p. 14). [My reading: no regression baseline is run (pp. 6–10).]
  - The four models were chosen because tree-based methods allow interpretability for clinical use (p. 7). The selected model, however, is an ensemble interpreted post hoc (pp. 8, 10).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Task: binary classification of 30-day DVT.
  - Data: ACS-NSQIP, January 2007 to December 2017, about 700 US hospitals, with CPT-code selection of EVTA and the standard NSQIP exclusions (p. 4). The vascular module is merged in, giving 21,549 patients × 34 variables (p. 5). Prevalence is 1.59% (pp. 2, 16).
  - Missing values: 17 of the 34 features have them. Because NSQIP missingness is "often not randomly distributed" [13], they are imputed by Optimal Imputation, which the paper describes as shown to beat other state-of-the-art techniques; the warrant is the group's own paper [14] (p. 7).
  - Split: 16,161 training and 5,388 test cases, with balance checked by χ² and t-tests (Table 1, pp. 5–6, 9).
  - Class imbalance is handled by sample weights (p. 7).
  - Admitted assumptions: CPT filtering assumes uniform procedures, and the registry over-represents large teaching hospitals (pp. 15–16).
  - [My reading: imputation precedes the split ("The dataset was then divided", p. 7), so test-set rows took part in imputing training rows.]
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a, a purely empirical work. OCT and Optimal Imputation are used as packaged tools; no formulation, solver, time limit or optimality gap is given (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol:
    - One 75/25 split, and a grid search over the listed hyperparameters that keeps the combination with the highest AUC (pp. 7–8). [My reading: the text does not say on which data the selection was made; no validation set or cross-validation is mentioned.]

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Arthur Delarue (Georgia Tech ISyE), Jean Pauphilet (London Business School) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a companion to the same trio's "Simple imputation rules for prediction with missing data" (TMLR 2024; cited pp. 2, 8, 13, 32). It carries the adaptive-policy hierarchy of his RO programme into machine learning (Bertsimas and Goyal 2012; Bertsimas, Iancu and Parrilo 2009 and 2011; pp. 4–5): an RO device transplanted to ML (ML + RO) in the 2019–2026 period.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + RO (adaptive policies as model classes)}:
  - The entry is an assumption mismatch. Practice treats prediction with missing data as an inference problem: impute, then regress. Imputation is valid only under missing-at-random (MAR), and MAR "cannot be tested from the data" (pp. 1–3).
  - The target is changed from inference to prediction (p. 3):
    - learn E[Y | M = m, o(X,M) = o(x,m)] directly;
    - use the missingness pattern as information;
    - stay agnostic to the missingness mechanism.
  - Gaps in the closest alternatives:
    - The fully adaptive approach, one model per pattern (Saar-Tsechansky and Provost 2007), splits the data and can need 2^d models (pp. 2, 4).
    - Adaptivity has mostly been studied for trees (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - "we view prediction with missing data as a two-stage adaptive optimization problem" (abstract, p. 1): first the pattern m is observed, then the coefficients w(m) are chosen (p. 3).
  - Missing features contribute zero (⟨w, x⟩m). The class F of decision rules w(·) indexes the models (pp. 3–5):
    - static;
    - affine, w0 + Wm;
    - order-t polynomial;
    - finite adaptability, via a learned partition of patterns;
    - fully adaptive.
  - Key equivalence (p. 7): a static model with an affinely adaptive intercept b0 + Σ bj mj equals impute-then-regress with constant imputations µj = bj/wj, learned jointly with w instead of sequentially.
  - For nonlinear learners, Algorithm 2 alternates between fitting any model f on µ-imputed data and a cyclic coordinate search on µ in steps of ±σj (pp. 8–9).
  - What changes: the imputation is chosen for downstream prediction error, not for likelihood; EM is contrasted on exactly this point (pp. 8–9).
  - What stays: off-the-shelf learners (glmnet, scikit-learn, XGBoost; pp. 7, 27).
  - The finite-adaptability partition is learned greedily (Algorithm 1, p. 5), although "the problem of finding the optimal partition … can be formulated as a mixed-integer optimization problem"; the exact version is left to future work (p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Setting: n i.i.d. samples (xi, mi) (p. 2). MAR and NMAR are defined by conditional independence (p. 3).
  - Theory (Assumption 2.1, p. 6):
    - the ground truth is a fully adaptive linear model with ‖f*‖∞ ≤ L and centred noise of variance σ²;
    - the OLS estimator is equivalent to a linear model with p(F) coefficients;
    - predictions are truncated (TL).
  - The authors concede that most of their experimental settings violate the assumption (p. 6); only linear Y with MCAR satisfies it (p. 9).
  - Practice: ElasticNet (glmnet), with α and λ cross-validated and feature-specific penalties λk = λ/nk (pp. 6–7).
  - Synthetic data, 1,568 instances in all (pp. 9, 19):
    - Gaussian X with Σ = BBᵀ + εI, d = 10, r = 5;
    - n ∈ {40, 60, …, 1000} training points and 5,000 test points;
    - SNR = 2;
    - linear Y, or a two-layer ReLU network on k = 5 features;
    - MCAR, or censoring above the (1 − p) percentile, with p ∈ {0.1, …, 0.8}.
  - Real data: 63 UCI and Rdatasets data sets (Tables B1–B3, pp. 20–21), 46 of them with a real target (p. 22).
  - Semi-synthetic signals are generated after missForest imputation under three mechanisms (p. 22):
    - MAR;
    - NMAR, where Y depends on m;
    - adversarially missing (AM), where patterns are permuted across rows to maximize Σ x_fullᵀ m.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Class preservation: each adaptive model is a linear regression over extended features.
    - Affine uses the d + d² features (1 − mj)xj and mk(1 − mj)xj (p. 4).
    - The affine intercept uses 2d + 1 variables (p. 8).
    - Order t uses O(d^{t+1}) features (p. 5).
    - Fully adaptive indicators are order-d polynomials in m, via ∏j (1 − mj − m′j + 2mj m′j) (p. 4); finite adaptability is therefore a special case of polynomial adaptivity (p. 5).
  - Generalization bound (Theorem 2.1, p. 6): E(Y − TL f̂n)² ≤ σ² + 8 b(F) + c max{σ², L}(1 + log n)/n · p(F), derived from Györfi et al., Theorem 11.3 (p. 18).
    - The bias b(F) for order t comes from a Taylor expansion of the multilinear w*(m) at E[M]. Mixed partials vanish for repeated indices and are bounded by 2^t L otherwise (Lemma A.1, p. 17). With ‖M − E[M]‖² ≤ d/4 and Stirling's formula the bound follows (Lemma A.2, p. 18).
    - Table 1 gives b(F) and p(F) for static, affine, order-t and fully adaptive models (p. 6).
    - [My reading, not checked beyond the displayed steps: the proof reaches d^{t+1}L²/((t+1)!)², which by Stirling is about (e²d)^{t+1}/(2π(t+1)^{2t+3}). That still decays super-exponentially, as the text says, but it does not give the stated O(d^{t+1}/(t+1)^{3(t+1)}) (Table 1, p. 6; Lemma A.2 and its proof, p. 18).   - Closed form for one feature (pp. 7–8): µ1 = E[Y|M1=1]·E[X1²|M1=0]/E[Y X1|M1=0]. The imputed value thus depends on Y and on the rows where X1 is missing. Under a linear Y it equals E[X1|M1=1], whereas mean imputation uses E[X1|M1=0]. The equivalence needs wj ≠ 0 (footnote 1, p. 7).
  - Algorithms:
    - Algorithm 1: greedy recursive partitioning of the patterns, stopped by depth or leaf size (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Treat what is observed as first-stage information and make the model's coefficients decision rules in it. An affine rule w0 + Wm becomes a linear regression over (1 − mj)xj and mk(1 − mj)xj, which any standard solver can fit (pp. 3–4).
- (D4) To learn an imputation jointly with a linear predictor, regress on 2d + 1 columns (zero-imputed x, the indicators m, an intercept) and read off µj = bj/wj (pp. 7–8).
- (D4) When many features are sparse indicator products, scale each feature's penalty by the number of rows where it is nonzero: λk = λ/nk (p. 7).
- (D4) Bound the bias of an order-t truncation of a multilinear function of binary indicators by a Taylor expansion at E[M]: mixed partials vanish for repeated indices and are bounded by 2^t L otherwise (Lemma A.1, p. 17).
- (D4) For any nonlinear learner, alternate between fitting f on µ-imputed data and a cyclic ±σj search on each µj, starting from the mean (Algorithm 2, pp. 8–9).
- (D5) Build not-at-random test conditions three ways: censor the top p percentile, let Y depend on the missingness indicators, and permute the missingness patterns across rows to maximize Σ x_fullᵀ m. Then report, for each data set, how often you beat the strongest naive baseline across splits (pp. 19, 22; Figure 7, p. 16).
- (D5) When you aggregate different metrics (R² and 2(AUC − 0.5)), recheck the conclusion separately by task type (p. 27).
