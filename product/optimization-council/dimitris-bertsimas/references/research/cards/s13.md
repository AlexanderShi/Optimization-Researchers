### S343 · Prescriptive analytics for reducing 30-day hospital readmissions after general surgery (PLoS ONE 2020, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0238118)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–12 (abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1–4, Fig. 1 caption, author contributions, references). The S1–S4 appendices (P-SVM and OPT formulations, subgroup analysis, thresholds) are DOCX files absent from the text: not read. First-page check: title and the authors Bertsimas, Li, Paschalidis and Wang match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC), Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis (Boston University, corresponding author; his former PhD student, 1996, per 04-mentorship.md), Taiyao Wang (Boston University) (p. 1). The contribution statement lists Bertsimas for methodology, supervision and review and editing; conceptualization, funding, resources and project administration are Paschalidis's (p. 11) · **Position in trajectory**: health-care analytics of 2019–2026 (SKILL Research Trajectory) {DA AP}, reusing his Optimal Classification Trees (ref. 26) and optimization-based kNN imputation (ref. 16) (pp. 5–6, 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA AP; ML}: The entry is a payment rule. The 2012 Affordable Care Act penalizes readmission rates above the national average, and the penalty list could extend to general surgery (p. 2). Earlier work predicted readmissions, and the interventions studied were mostly post-discharge (p. 2). The paper wants in-hospital, personalized recommendations, and picks pre-operative hematocrit (HCT) as the lever: commonly measured, tied to readmission risk and adjustable by transfusion (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two layers on NSQIP data.
  - Prediction from pre-operative variables only and from all variables, by L2-logistic regression, a sparse linear SVM, random forests, gradient boosting (GBM) and a neural network (p. 5).
  - Prescription by two methods of the authors. P-SVM fixes a sparse SVM hyperplane, then sets each patient's HCT to trade prevented readmissions against the HCT increase. Optimal Prescriptive Trees replace the OCT objective by a mix of prediction accuracy and readmission rate (p. 6). There are four doses (0–3 bags, i.e. +0/3/6/9% HCT), capped at 9% (pp. 5–6).
  - Prescriptions are scored by four predictive models used as alternative ground truths, after the transfusion is propagated to HCT-correlated covariates through fitted regressions (pp. 6, 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: NSQIP 2014 only, because variable definitions changed over 2011–2014: 722,101 surgeries, 39,641 readmitted (5.49%), 231 and 187 post- and pre-operative variables; deaths without readmission excluded; variables more than 80% missing dropped; the rest imputed by kNN (pp. 3–5). Past transfusion doses are not recorded, so the baseline treatment is assumed by HCT band (Table 1, pp. 3, 6). The prescriptions use 88 pre-operative variables (p. 5). The data cannot be shared under the data use agreement (p. 1).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a in the text, since the P-SVM and OPT formulations sit in the unread appendices (p. 6). Scoring device: each evaluator's threshold makes its predicted readmission rate equal the training rate (specificity above 96%); a readmission counts as prevented when an HCT<30 test patient predicted readmitted without treatment is predicted not readmitted after it (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Three 80/20 splits. AUC is 72.7–73.6% from pre-operative variables and 83.4–87.4% from all variables, GBM best, above the LACE index's 68.4% (Table 3, p. 7; p. 9). The prescriptions cut predicted readmissions by 12.15% (OPT) and 11.45% (P-SVM) on average with about one bag per patient, against 2.84–9.22% for giving every HCT<30 patient one bag (Table 4, p. 8). The effect depends on the evaluator: 3.78–8.66% under GBM, the most accurate predictor, and 16.37–19.51% under the neural network (Tables 3–4, pp. 7–8). Python, Matlab and Julia; no solver, run times or significance tests are reported (pp. 6, 8).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: An application with two prescriptive methods, plus a savings estimate of $20.3 million a year if the 12% held for all U.S. readmissions of HCT<30 patients (p. 9). The stated limit is specific: there is no ground truth for whether the interventions prevent readmissions, which would need a randomized trial the authors hope to motivate, and NSQIP has no long-term outcomes (p. 10). Not stated as limits: the assumed baseline doses (p. 3) and the evaluator spread of about 4–20% behind the 12% headline (pp. 1, 8–9).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 7–8, 10). Owner, decision and data are named (surgical departments, pre-operative transfusion, NSQIP). The decision is scored, not just the AUC; a naive treat-everyone rule is the baseline in the same table; and the paper says what its evaluation cannot show (step 6). Short of the full method: the hospitals' actual transfusion rule is unrecorded and assumed (Table 1, p. 3), and decisions are scored on predicted, not observed, outcomes.
- Tension T6 (headline vs caveat): the abstract's 12% average (p. 1) against the evaluator range in the body (pp. 8–9); the abstract also calls AUC accuracy (p. 1).
- New-pattern candidate: **held-out ground-truth ensemble for counterfactual scoring of prescriptions** (same name as c29's S097). Four outcome models score the same prescriptions for stability, with second-order effects of the treatment on correlated covariates (pp. 6, 8). The text does not say on which split the evaluators were trained.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the dose of a past treatment is unrecorded, print the assumed baseline as a table tied to the clinical threshold, so readers can contest it (Table 1, p. 3).
- Score a prescription with several outcome models and report each one, next to a naive uniform-treatment rule in the same table (Table 4, p. 8).
- Before re-predicting under treatment, move the covariates correlated with the actionable variable through regressions fitted on it (pp. 6, 8).
- Fix each evaluator's threshold so its predicted base rate equals the training rate before counting prevented events (pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "Predicting readmission is only one step toward preventing readmissions." (p. 7)
- "A limitation of our study is the lack of the full ground truth for the impact of the proposed interventions, namely, whether they prevent readmissions." (p. 10)

### S355 · Sparse hierarchical regression with polynomials (Machine Learning 2020, DOI 10.1007/s10994-020-05868-6; arXiv 1709.10030)
Read level: **full**: arXiv v1 of 28 Sep 2017, pp. 1–20 (all sections, Tables 1–3, Figures 1–3, references). The Machine Learning print was not read, so its revisions are unknown. First-page check: title and the authors Bertsimas and Van Parys match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Bart Van Parys (MIT ORC; his postdoc, per 04-mentorship.md) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: ML under a modern optimization lens {ML IO}. It is the companion of Bertsimas–Van Parys on exact sparse linear regression and phase transitions (cited as submitted to the Annals of Statistics, p. 18; AoS 2020, arXiv 1709.10029, per 01-publications.md), whose cutting-plane algorithm it extends to polynomial features (pp. 4, 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO}: High-dimensional nonlinear regression (p. 1). Polynomial kernel ridge regression is tractable in any feature dimension f but overfits when f ≫ n; nonparametric methods lack statistical power; the only sparse nonlinear method named is the ℓ1-based SPORE (pp. 2–3). The problem is stated as hierarchical (k, ℓ)-sparsity: the degree-r polynomial with at most k inputs and ℓ monomials that minimizes a ridge-penalized squared error (eq. 2, p. 3). Input sparsity and monomial sparsity are kept apart on purpose (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: A two-step method (Fig. 1, p. 4).
  - Input ranking: solve the unconstrained kernel ridge problem once, and rank input i by the norm of the coefficients of all monomials containing it, γ²α*ᵀK_iα*, computed in O(n²) from Hadamard powers (Prop. 1, Alg. 1, pp. 9–10).
  - Exact (k, ℓ)-sparse regression on the p₀ top-ranked inputs, written as a pure convex integer problem and solved by outer approximation (Thm. 1, Alg. 2, pp. 7, 11).
  - What changes against the Lasso and SPORE: an exact cardinality constraint with hierarchy constraints sⱼ ≤ hᵢ instead of an ℓ1 proxy (pp. 3, 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: yₜ = g(xₜ) + eₜ, with g a polynomial of degree r and γ chosen by cross-validation (pp. 1–2). The big-M MIO needs a data-dependent M that sets the formulation's strength (pp. 6–7). Synthetic data: Gaussian inputs, ±1 coefficients on a random (k, ℓ)-sparse support, √SNR = 20 (pp. 12–13). In the phase-transition runs ℓ = 20 is taken as known and no hierarchy is imposed (k = p), to match the Lasso's setting (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The ridge loss has a closed form via the matrix inversion lemma (Lemma 1, p. 7). Eliminating w yields a big-M-free binary problem in (s, h) (Thm. 1, pp. 7–8). The kernel is the Hadamard power (XXᵀ + 1)^∘r once monomials are scaled by the square root of their number of orderings (pp. 8–9). Cuts use the subgradient read off the dual maximizer (Prop. 2, p. 10). Finite convergence is credited to Fletcher–Leyffer, with exponentially many cuts possible in the worst case (Thm. 3, p. 11). The implementation is a single branch-and-bound tree with lazy constraints (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia/JuMP with Gurobi 6.5 on a Xeon E5-2690 at 2.9 GHz (p. 12). With p = 25 cubic (f = 3,276), 20 data sets per point and a 2-minute cap (the best solution is kept if not optimal), accuracy and solve time both switch at n₀ ≈ 600, and so do false alarms (pp. 13–14). Cutting planes fall from more than 300 at n = 300–400 to 25–31 at n = 900–1,000 (Table 1, p. 15). At n = 560 the exact method dominates the Lasso on accuracy against false alarms (Fig. 3, pp. 15–16). The ranking keeps all relevant inputs with p₀ = 286 at (n, p) = (2,000, 1,000) and p₀ = 78 at (10,000, 1,000) (Table 2, p. 16). On 8 UCI data sets the exact method has the lowest test error on 3 and ties on 1; ridge wins on 2 and kernel regression on 2 (Table 3, p. 17). The abstract's scale of n ≈ 10,000 and p ≈ 1,000 (p. 1) rests on the ranking step; no exact-step solve times at that scale are reported (pp. 12–17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A modelling notion first, an algorithm second (p. 4). Stated limits: possibly exponentially many cuts (p. 11); a high false-alarm rate in the ranking step (p. 16); nonlinear features help on some data sets only, and sparsity brings no benefit on three (p. 17). The conclusion names no future work (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 6–7, 11). The big-M MIO is replaced by a big-M-free convex integer formulation whose master stays a linear MIO: the device changes, not the solver.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 5–6). The old belief that integer optimization is impossible for statistics is named and declared obsolete, but the speed-up is cited from Bertsimas–King–Mazumder 2016, not rebuilt: the borrowed-figure case of Method 2's checkpoint.
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 11, 13, 15). The Lasso incumbent is dueled through the same ranking step with a ridge refit (p. 13). The heuristic is a screen, not a warm start (Algorithm 2's warm start is unspecified, p. 11). Runs stopped at 2 minutes use the best solution found and report no gaps; only Table 1's caption says that the smallest n were not solved (p. 15).
- Heuristic 4: evidence (p. 17). The UCI losses are printed in the body.
- New-pattern candidates: **computational phase transition: more data makes the exact problem easier** (pp. 4–5, 13–15; also S170, pp. 5, 17, 20). **a priori screening before solving**: the kernel-ridge ranking cuts p to p₀ before the exact solve (pp. 4, 8–10, 16). **same selection layer for the baselines**: the ℓ1 benchmark gets the same input ranking and a ridge refit, so that both methods have a comparable number of hyperparameters (p. 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a cardinality-constrained ridge problem, eliminate the continuous coefficients with the closed-form ridge loss to get a big-M-free binary convex problem (Lemma 1, Thm. 1, p. 7).
- Rank inputs from one unconstrained kernel solve: each input's coefficient-block norm is γ²α*ᵀK_iα*, with K_i obtained by Hadamard powers in O(n²) (Prop. 1, pp. 9–10).
- Run outer approximation inside one branch-and-bound tree with lazy-constraint callbacks (p. 12).
- Report support recovery as two numbers, the share of true features found and the share of selected features that are false, and trace one against the other over ℓ (pp. 5, 15).
- Tabulate the number of cuts against n to show where exactness becomes cheap (Table 1, p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "The position that exact sparse regression is an unattainable goal only to be striven for via admittedly elegant convex heuristics such as Lasso should not be held any longer." (p. 6)
- "As one could expect, considering nonlinear monomial features is not beneficial for prediction in all data sets." (p. 17)

### S157 · A pre-registered short-term forecasting study of COVID-19 in Germany and Poland during the second wave (Nature Communications 2021, DOI 10.1038/s41467-021-25207-0)
Read level: **partial**: the file is the medRxiv preprint of the same study (DOI 10.1101/2020.12.24.20248826, posted 11 Jan 2021), titled *Short-term forecasting of COVID-19 in Germany and Poland during the second wave – a preregistered study*. Read: main text pp. 1–17 in full, author contributions and team list pp. 21–22, and the availability table p. 26; the rest of the supplement (pp. 23–52) was searched only for the DELPHI rows. The Nature Communications text was not read. First-page check: the first six authors (Bracher, Wolffram, Deuschel, Görgen, Ketterer, Ullrich) match the list's author string, D. Bertsimas (MIT Sloan) is among the 38 authors, and the subject (Germany and Poland, second wave, preregistered) matches the listed title (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Voice**: the Forecast Hub coordinators. J. Bracher, T. Gneiting and M. Schienle conceived the study and wrote the manuscript; DB is among those who contributed forecasts (p. 21). His group's model is CovidAnalytics-DELPHI (M. L. Li, D. Bertsimas, H. Tazi Bouardi, O. Skali Lami, S. Soni) (p. 22) · **Position in trajectory**: COVID-19 work of 2020–2021 {AP DA}; the DELPHI line (03-process-evidence.md B4.7; 05-peer-critique.md §5.2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP DA}: Short-term forecasts, unlike scenario projections, can be checked against later data, so they test the predictive power of models (p. 2). The study compares and combines 14 models from 13 teams in real time for 12 October–19 December 2020, the onset of the second wave, under a protocol deposited at OSF on 8 October (pp. 1–2, 6). It asks about reliable horizons, calibration, agreement between models, reliable approaches and ensembles (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Standardized weekly submissions of 23 predictive quantiles for 1–4 weeks ahead, from data up to Monday (pp. 3–4). Evaluation by the weighted interval score (WIS), absolute error and 50%/95% coverage (p. 5). Three baselines, of which only the naive one was prespecified, and median, mean and inverse-WIS ensembles (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Model and data**: Truth data from ECDC (primary) and JHU, by date of reporting (pp. 3, 5). DELPHI is a country-level modified SEIR model with changing interventions and under-detection (Table 1, p. 7), one of three models that anticipate policy changes (p. 4). A missing forecast gets the worst score of any model that week (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a (no proofs). Evaluation devices: WIS approximates the CRPS from quantiles and reduces to the absolute error for point forecasts (p. 5); cumulative forecasts referring to the other truth source are shifted additively (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation** (DELPHI rows, all asterisked because at least one week was imputed with the worst score): mean WIS above the naive KIT-baseline for incident cases one and two weeks ahead (Germany 29,517 vs 13,227 and 62,173 vs 25,840; Poland 23,656 vs 18,538 and 46,427 vs 35,848) and for one-week incident deaths (362 vs 272; 337 vs 281), but below it for two-week incident deaths (310 vs 523; 444 vs 541) (Tables 2–3, pp. 15–16). DELPHI missed two German weeks and gave no cumulative case forecasts (Table 4, p. 26). Overall, almost no model beat the naive baseline for cases beyond one or two weeks, and the ensembles did not dominate (pp. 1, 12, 14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits** (the coordinators'): ten weeks only, so the conclusions are preliminary (p. 2); most models were overconfident (pp. 8, 14); inverse-WIS weighting brought no benefit (p. 17). Next steps: regional forecasts, hospital targets and the vaccine phase (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 5, 15–16, 26). His group submitted DELPHI to an external, preregistered, prospective scoreboard that scored it on the field's metric.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 5, 15–16). The checkpoint correction (score forecasts on the field's metric against the naive rule) is met here by the Hub, not by the group. Against the naive baseline DELPHI loses for cases and wins for two-week deaths, consistent with the US Hub result recorded in 05 §5.2 (relative WIS 0.99). These are forecasts, not decisions.
- New-pattern candidate: **Consortium co-authorship as a contributing modeller** (same name as c27's S110, which lists this paper): his authorship comes from contributing one model (pp. 21–22).

**Transferable techniques** (the consortium's protocol):
- Deposit the evaluation protocol (period, targets, scores, rule for missing forecasts) before the first forecast (pp. 2, 5).
- Score probabilistic forecasts with WIS from fixed quantiles; since WIS equals the absolute error for point forecasts, both kinds can be ranked together (p. 5).
- Give a missing forecast the worst score any model got that week, and flag those entries in the tables (p. 5; Tables 2–3, pp. 15–16).
- Report a naive baseline and say which baselines were added after the protocol (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "Firstly, forecasts are made in real time, as retrospective forecasting often leads to overly optimistic conclusions about performance." (p. 2)
- "Ensemble forecasts showed good relative performance, in particular in terms of coverage, but did not clearly dominate single-model predictions." (p. 1)

### S170 · Sparse classification: a scalable discrete optimization perspective (Machine Learning 2021, DOI 10.1007/s10994-021-06085-5; arXiv 1710.01352)
Read level: **full**: arXiv v4 of 30 Jun 2020, pp. 1–30 (all sections, Tables 1–5, Figures 1–9); Appendix A (pp. 31–35) skimmed for the proof skeleton; references not read. The Machine Learning print was not read. First-page check: title and the authors Bertsimas, Pauphilet and Van Parys match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jean Pauphilet (MIT ORC; his PhD student, 2020) and Bart Van Parys (MIT ORC and Sloan; postdoc and Pauphilet's informal second adviser) (p. 1; 04-mentorship.md) · **Position in trajectory**: ML under a modern optimization lens {ML IO}. It extends the cutting-plane method for sparse linear regression (Bertsimas et al. 2020), which relies on a closed form, to losses without one (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO}: Sparse classification for interpretable models. The ℓ0 problem is NP-hard, so practice uses the Lasso, which finds the true features but also returns false ones (pp. 1–2). Between the information-theoretic threshold and the Lasso's lies a regime where full recovery is possible but the Lasso fails (p. 2), and support recovery has hardly been studied for classification (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Dualize the ridge-regularized classifier through the loss's Fenchel conjugate (Thm. 1, p. 8). The best support then solves min over s of c(s), a maximum over α of functions linear in s, hence a binary convex problem (Thm. 2, pp. 10–11).
  - It is solved by outer approximation with lazy constraints in one tree, from a *rich root node*: the Lasso as warm start and cuts at the Lasso path's supports up to sparsity k + 1 (pp. 12–13).
  - Stochastic cuts estimate w on B = 10 subsamples of size max(10% n, 2k), and fall back to exact cuts when they do not tighten the approximation (Alg. 2, pp. 13–14).
  - Logistic loss, hinge loss and the 2-norm SVM are covered without a closed form (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**: Assumption 1: the loss is convex in its second argument (p. 7); the 0-1 loss is excluded because strong duality fails (Remark 1, p. 10). γ and k are cross-validated on validation AUC (pp. 14–15). Synthetic data: correlated Gaussian features, w_true ∈ {−1, 0, 1}ᵖ with k nonzeros, labels sign(wᵀx + ε) (p. 16). The support-recovery figures use p = 1,000, k_true = 30, ρ = 0.3 and SNR → ∞ (pp. 17–21); Table 3 uses SNR = 10 (p. 23). Theorem 4 assumes Gaussian x, w* ∈ {0, 1}ᵖ, a known k and p > 2k (pp. 27–28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The subgradient of c in s comes free from the dual maximizer (p. 12); finite termination is cited from Fletcher–Leyffer (Thm. 3, p. 12). Theorem 4: perfect recovery holds once n > n₀, with n₀ < C(2 + σ²) k log(p − k). The proof combines a union bound over supports with ℓ correct features, Hoeffding bounds, and orthant probabilities of bivariate and trivariate normals (pp. 28, 31–34). It concerns the empirical-misclassification minimizer over binary w with k known, not the hinge or logistic estimator that is computed (pp. 27–28). A √log p gap to Scarlett–Cevher's necessary condition remains (pp. 29–30).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Xeon 2.3 GHz, one core, 8 GB; Julia 1.0, JuMP 0.21.1, Gurobi 8.1.0 (p. 15). The baseline is glmnet's ElasticNet, with both its hyperparameters cross-validated by the same procedure (p. 15). The exact method selects the truth with somewhat fewer samples and has better out-of-sample AUC (p. 17). At low n it hits the 3-minute limit, at high n it ends in seconds, while glmnet needs less than a second (pp. 17–18). Stochastic cuts cut time by a factor of about 2 (hinge) to 10 (logistic) (p. 21). k = 5 with p = 50,000 solves in under a minute, but p = 5,000 with k = 20 leaves 40–46% gaps after 30 minutes (Table 3, p. 23). On 8 UCI data sets the exact classifiers are sparser on 7, and ElasticNet is more accurate on 3 (p. 23). On lung-cancer gene data (n = 1,145, p = 14,858) the sparse SVM uses 38 genes for an AUC of 0.977, against 378.4 genes and 0.982 for ElasticNet (Table 5, p. 25).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Formulation and algorithm, scalability, empirical recovery and a sufficient condition (pp. 4–5). Stated limits: time grows sharply with γ, and no warm start is used across the γ grid (p. 21); denser problems stay unsolved (p. 23); the UCI data sets make a weak case (p. 24); the unresolved middle regime may be an artefact of the proof (p. 30). The abstract's claim of optimal solutions for n and p in the tens of thousands within minutes (p. 1) sits beside Table 3's unsolved p = 5,000 cases (p. 23): Tension T6.

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 8–12). Duality turns the cardinality-constrained classifier into a binary convex problem, and the same dual supplies the cuts.
- Method 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 13, 15, 17–19, 21, 23). Lasso warm start and initial cuts; stated limits, machine and solver version; gaps for unsolved instances; an upper- and lower-bound trace showing that time goes into the proof (Fig. 5); and an incumbent duel at matched tuning. Missing: the low-SNR regime critics would pick, since the recovery figures assume SNR → ∞ (pp. 17–21).
- Tension T6: see D8.
- New-pattern candidates: **computational phase transition: more data makes the exact problem easier**. Cuts drop from thousands to under twenty as n grows, and here the transition is smooth, not sharp (pp. 5, 17, 20; also S355). **knob-matched baseline**: ElasticNet is chosen because, like (γ, k), it has two tuned hyperparameters, tuned the same way (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For any convex loss with a ridge term, write the support choice as a minimum over s of a maximum over α of functions linear in s; the dual maximizer at s gives the cut's subgradient at no extra cost (pp. 10–12).
- Seed outer approximation with the Lasso solution and with cuts at the Lasso path's supports up to sparsity k + 1, rather than with dense Boolean-relaxation cuts (p. 13).
- Generate cheap cuts from subsample estimates, and accept each only if it tightens the current lower approximation (pp. 13–14).
- Plot the upper and lower bounds against time to show how much of a run only certifies (Fig. 5, pp. 18–19).
- When a baseline has fewer knobs than your method, pick a variant with the same number and tune it with your own protocol (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "In contrast, while Lasso accurately detects all the true features, it persistently returns incorrect features, even as the number of observations increases." (p. 1)
- "Computational time evolves similarly: the algorithm reaches the time limit (here, 3 minutes) when n is low, but terminates in a few seconds for high values." (p. 17)

### S196 · Computation of convex hull prices in electricity markets with non-convexities using Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition (IEEE Transactions on Power Systems 2021, DOI 10.1109/tpwrs.2021.3122000; arXiv 2012.13331)
Read level: **full**: arXiv v2 of 24 Oct 2021, pp. 1–10 (all sections; the stylized examples on pp. 6–7 skimmed); references not read. The IEEE print was not read. First-page check: title and the authors Andrianesis, Bertsimas, Caramanis and Hogan match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Panagiotis Andrianesis and Michael C. Caramanis (Boston University; not his former student Constantine Caramanis) and William W. Hogan (Harvard) (p. 1); funded by NSF AitF 1733827 (p. 1). Bertsimas is second of four authors, and the text does not say who did what · **Position in trajectory**: energy-market work {IO LO AP}, beside his robust unit-commitment paper cited under SKILL Method 5.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO LO AP}: With non-convex unit commitment (start-up costs, minimum outputs), marginal-cost prices cannot support the efficient dispatch, so markets pay uplift (p. 1). Convex hull (CH) prices minimize uplift, but ISOs use approximations, because exact CH prices are computationally hard and their formation is opaque (pp. 1–2). The entry is to compute exact CH prices and explain how they form (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Write unit commitment as the choice of one feasible schedule per unit, a set-partitioning problem. Its LP relaxation is the Dantzig-Wolfe characterization and solves the Lagrangian dual whose solution gives the CH prices (pp. 4–5).
  - Column generation: the restricted master's duals are prices λ and unit profits π; each unit's sub-problem is its own MILP at current prices, read as profit-maximizing self-scheduling; the method stops when no schedule has a negative reduced cost (p. 5).
  - The units' MILP models are used as they are in market software, with no reformulation (pp. 4, 8). The idea is borrowed from crew scheduling (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**: Mixed-integer linear unit models with bounded feasible sets (p. 4); penalized slack keeps the master feasible (p. 5). Test cases: stylized examples from the literature (pp. 6–7), a ramp-constrained example (pp. 8–9), and a PJM-like FERC data set with 1,011 units, 24 hours and up to 10-block offers, with some parameters set by assumption (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The LP relaxation of the schedule formulation equals the Lagrangian dual (classic results, refs. 35–36, p. 4). Finite convergence holds because MILP feasible sets have polyhedral hulls with finitely many extreme points (p. 5). The reduced cost of a unit's optimal self-schedule is minus its lost opportunity cost, so termination means minimum total lost opportunity cost (p. 5); the duality gap is the uplift (pp. 3, 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: On the ramp example the CH duality gap is $365, against $875.45 for the integer relaxation and $1,137.75 for an extended formulation. A hand-tuned subgradient method, after 10,000 iterations, was still about 0.1 $/MWh off with its best step size, and left λ₃ near 206 instead of 276 with step 1/k (Fig. 1, p. 9). FERC data: C with CPLEX 12.7 on a Core i7-5500U with 8 GB; a few tens of iterations from warm or flat starts; about 14 s per sequential iteration, with the master under 1 s and each sub-problem about 10 ms; full parallelization is estimated, not run, at well under a minute (p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Method plus intuition, with preliminary computations (pp. 2, 10). Stated limits: none of the known column-generation accelerations were used, and testing with actual market data at a large US ISO is ongoing (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 4–5, 10). A pricing problem owned by power economics is written exactly as an LP over schedules, prices and profits are read off its duals, and tractability is shown on a 1,011-unit data set, all without changing the units' MILP models.
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 9–10). Exactness with finite convergence is set against the subgradient incumbent on a published example, and the master is warm-started from the MILP unit-commitment solution. No certificate issue arises, since the method is exact by construction.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 1–2, 9–10). The comparators are what ISOs use (integer-relaxation prices, ELMP) and the literature's extended formulations, scored by duality gap (uplift). No partner data, field test or ISO decision rule appear; the ISO test is ongoing.
- New-pattern candidate: **small dual plus optimization oracle** (same name as c02's S269). The dual has one price per system constraint and hour, and each unit's MILP is the oracle (pp. 4–5, 8). Here the oracle steps also have a market reading: sub-problem = self-scheduling, reduced cost = lost opportunity cost (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To get exact Lagrangian-dual (convex-hull) prices without reformulating component models, take each component's feasible plans as columns and solve the LP relaxation by column generation, with the component's own MILP as pricing problem (pp. 4–5).
- Read the master's duals economically: λ are prices and −π each unit's profit; a negative reduced cost means the unit would gain by self-scheduling (p. 5).
- Start the master from the MILP solution, or from trivial offline schedules with penalized slack (p. 5; Example 1, p. 6).
- Benchmark exactness on a published small example against the practice approximation, an extended formulation and a tuned subgradient method, reporting each duality gap (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "Computational performance is always an empirical question." (p. 8)
- "There was no use of any of the known methods that may enhance CG performance, although such strategies have been successful in other applications." (p. 10)

### S215 · Probabilistic guarantees in robust optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2021, DOI 10.1137/21m1390967)
Read level: **full**: the LBS Research Online copy of the authors' manuscript dated 9 Jan 2021, pp. 2–24 (all sections, Tables 1–5, Figures 1–8); appendices pp. 27–29 skimmed (statements only); references read only for refs. [6] and [8] (pp. 24–25). File p. 1 is the repository cover. First-page check: title and the authors Bertsimas, den Hertog and Pauphilet match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Dick den Hertog (University of Amsterdam; his RO book co-author) and Jean Pauphilet (London Business School; his PhD student, 2020) (p. 2; 04-mentorship.md) · **Position in trajectory**: robust optimization {RO; CP}, returning to the probabilistic guarantee of the budget set from *The Price of Robustness* (SW3; ref. [8], p. 25), which it recovers on p. 15. 05-peer-critique.md had cited it from the abstract only.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO CP}: A priori bounds are used to size uncertainty sets, but results were derived set by set, in a disparate way, and catalogues only list them (p. 3). Missing are a method for any convex set and any constraint, and a link between the set's geometry and the guarantee (p. 3). Rivals are faulted: safe approximations let distributional assumptions dictate the set; data-driven sets, including his own with Gupta and Kallus (ref. [6], p. 24), are calibrated on one constraint; DRO costs more computation; scenario sample sizes grow linearly in the dimension (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: For U = {ā + Pz : z ∈ Z}, define the robust complexity ρ(Z) as the minimum over unit vectors y of the support function δ*(y|Z), a robust analogue of Rademacher complexity with the expectation replaced by a worst case (pp. 5, 11).
  - With independent sub-Gaussian z, any robust solution violates the constraint with probability at most exp(−ρ(Z)²/2), both for linear constraints and for constraints concave in the uncertainty, via the concave conjugate (Table 1, p. 6; Cors. 1–2, pp. 12–13).
  - A solution-dependent a posteriori bound, exp(−(b − āᵀx)²/(2‖Pᵀx‖²)), is tighter (Thm. 1, p. 10).
  - The support function appears in both the robust counterpart and the bound (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**: Z convex, compact and full-dimensional with 0 in its relative interior; f concave in the uncertainty, the convex case being left out (p. 5). Assumption 1: sub-Gaussian coordinates with variance proxy 1; Assumption 2: independence (p. 9). Section 5 drops independence, for a weaker exp(−ρ(Z)²/(2L)), and gives polynomial bounds from a bounded covariance (pp. 5, 18–20).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Proof skeleton: zᵀPᵀx is sub-Gaussian with proxy ‖Pᵀx‖²; robust feasibility makes the slack at least δ*(Pᵀx|Z); a tail bound then gives the a posteriori bound, and minimizing over directions the a priori one (pp. 10–12). Closed forms or bounds for ρ(Z) cover norm balls, intersections and Minkowski sums (Table 2, p. 7; Props. 4–7, pp. 13–16). ρ(Z) equals the radius of the largest inscribed ball centred at 0 (Prop. 8, p. 17). The budget set's exp(−Γ²/(2L)) is recovered without assuming symmetric noise (p. 15). Remark 1: a single second-order cone constraint gives violation at most ε directly (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: A facility-location instance (10 facilities, 50 customers) with demand within 20% of nominal, under uniform, normal and Rademacher noise (p. 20). A posteriori bounds are much tighter than a priori ones and approach the empirical violation rate (Fig. 4, p. 21). The budget set is about 10% less conservative than the sum set a priori at every protection level, less so a posteriori and empirically (Fig. 6, p. 22). Union-bound joint guarantees exceed 1 for small Γ (p. 23). No run times (none needed).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A theory paper with one application. The authors confine the set comparison to this problem (p. 22). Future work: constraints convex in the uncertainty via safe approximations, and Bernstein-type bounds for dependent noise (pp. 23–24).

**Method links**:
- Method 3: evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 10–12, 21–23). The knob (Γ, or ρ(Z)) gets a solution-independent bound under stated assumptions and can be set from a target ε (ρ = √(−2 ln ε) for the box-ellipsoid, p. 3). The cost–violation curve is drawn for two set families, together with the case where the bound is uninformative (p. 23). The a posteriori bound adds an audit after solving.
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 6, 10). The same support function carries the tractable counterpart and the guarantee; an ε target is met by an SOC constraint, inside the conic class.
- Tension T3 (optimization, not probability): practice side; the whole contribution is a concentration-inequality analysis (pp. 9–12).
- New-pattern candidates: **Robust analogue of another field's canonical concept** (same name as c10's S029): robust complexity as Rademacher complexity with the expectation replaced by a worst case (p. 11). **tightness audit of one's own bound**: a priori, a posteriori and empirical violation rates are plotted together, and the union bound is shown to exceed 1 for small Γ (pp. 21–23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Bound the violation probability of a robust linear constraint by the sub-Gaussian tail of zᵀPᵀx, then replace the slack by the support function to get a bound that depends only on the set (pp. 10–12).
- Compute ρ(Z) as the radius of the largest ℓ2 ball centred at 0 inside Z; this works for sets without closed forms (Prop. 8, p. 17).
- After solving, report the a posteriori bound exp(−slack²/(2‖Pᵀx‖²)), which costs nothing and is tighter (pp. 10, 21).
- Compare uncertainty-set families by worst-case cost against violation probability (a priori, a posteriori, empirical) across protection levels (Fig. 6, p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "Little if no work, however, provides a disciplined methodology that enables to derive probabilistic guarantees for any uncertainty set U and any constraint function f." (p. 3)
- "This conclusion is of course valid for this particular problem only and we do not claim any generalization to other contexts." (p. 22)

### S310 · Simple Imputation Rules for Prediction with Missing Data: Contrasting Theoretical Guarantees with Empirical Performance (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv 2104.03158)
Read level: **full**: arXiv v3 of 2 Feb 2024, main text pp. 1–12 (all sections, Tables 1–2, Figures 1–3) and Appendices C–E (pp. 17–18, 22–23); the proofs in Appendices A–B (pp. 15–17) read only at their openings; the data-set tables (pp. 19–22) not read. First-page check: title and the authors Bertsimas, Delarue and Pauphilet match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Arthur Delarue (Georgia Tech; corresponding author; his former PhD student) and Jean Pauphilet (London Business School; his former PhD student) (p. 1; 04-mentorship.md) · **Position in trajectory**: the imputation line of his ML programme {ML}, written after opt.impute (2018) and S228 (2021), both cited as examples (pp. 12–13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML}: Practice handles missing data for prediction as for inference, so the missing-at-random (MAR) assumption and plausible imputations are treated as requirements (pp. 1–2). But prediction is a different task (p. 1). The questions: when is impute-then-regress consistent, and does it need plausible imputations (p. 2)?
- **D2 Core idea**: Theorem 2.1: with infinite data and a universally consistent learner, imputing a value µ(x₂:d) for a missing X₁ leads to a mixture of two conditional expectations, weighted by α(x), the posterior probability that the value was imputed (p. 4). Corollary 2.2: the pipeline is Bayes-optimal if and only if α(x) = 1, i.e. the learner can tell imputed values from observed ones, or the two conditional means agree (p. 5).
  - Hence mode imputation is inconsistent and missingness should be its own category, while mean imputation of a continuous feature is consistent (pp. 6–7): for prediction, crude is good (p. 5).
  - Theorem 4.1: data not missing at random (NMAR) can help prediction (p. 10).
- **D3 Model and data**: The theory has one missing covariate, infinite data and a universally consistent learner, and does not need MAR (pp. 3–4, 10–11). The empirics use three tiers (pp. 5–6, 17–18): synthetic designs (d = 10, n from 40 to 1,000, SNR = 2, linear or neural-network signals on 5 features, MCAR or censoring, 8 missing fractions); 63 public data sets (UCI, RDatasets) with real missingness and synthetic MAR, NMAR or adversarial signals (semi-real); and the real signals where available.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: A local-mixture argument proves Theorem 2.1 (Appendix A, p. 15). Two constructed examples show NMAR beating MAR and the reverse (Examples 4.2–4.3, p. 10). Comparisons use paired t-tests and Wilcoxon tests (Tables 1–2, pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Mode imputation lowers accuracy significantly with synthetic signals (mean differences −0.03 to −0.13) but not with real signals (+0.0122, p = 0.12) (Table 1, p. 7). mice beats mean imputation on synthetic MCAR data with few missing entries and loses under censoring (Fig. 1, p. 9). On semi-real and real data its effect is negative and significant in 5 of 7 cases and not significant in 2 (Table 2, p. 8). With a linear downstream model mice wins; with random forests the reverse holds (Fig. 2, p. 11). Xeon E5-2690 v4 with 8 GB (p. 17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Theory plus a large empirical audit, with the limits written as research directions: the role of MAR, finite samples and parametric learners, and unrealistic synthetic data (pp. 10–12). On real data the evidence on mode imputation is not conclusive (p. 2). The paper names the group's own imputation papers (Bertsimas et al. 2018, 2021) as validated on synthetic removal, where gains look large (pp. 12–13). Appendix C.2 still mentions the authors' adaptive regression models (p. 18), which the v3 main text does not present (pp. 1–12): perhaps a trace of a narrowed claim, not checked against v1.

**Method links**:
- Method 4: contradiction ✗ (pp. 4–8, 12). The duel with the incumbent is run honestly, and the crude incumbent (mean imputation) is proved asymptotically optimal; it is as good as mice with real signals and better on most semi-real tests (Table 2, p. 8). The premise that a sophisticated joint method beats the simple practice (Taste mark 3; Tension T2) does not hold for imputation-for-prediction, including in the group's own earlier line.
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 1–2, 5). Imputation is judged by the downstream prediction, not by plausibility; an imputation good for prediction encodes missingness.
- Heuristic 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 1–2, 7, 12). Inconclusive and unfavourable real-data results are in the abstract and the body.
- New-pattern candidates: **asymptotic optimality as the warrant for a simple heuristic** (same name as c03's S089): mean imputation is recommended because it is asymptotically consistent (pp. 6–7). **self-revision within a programme** (compare c40's S672): the group re-tests the value of sophisticated imputation, which its 2018 and 2021 papers promoted, against the crude rule on real data, and reports the reversal (pp. 12–13; card S228).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before engineering a pre-processing step, derive what the downstream learner needs asymptotically: here only that imputed values be recognizable, α(x) = 1 (pp. 4–5).
- Validate on three tiers (synthetic; semi-real, with real design and missingness but synthetic signals; real) and report where the tiers disagree (pp. 5–8, 12).
- Test paired differences over (data set × split) comparisons with both a t-test and a Wilcoxon test, and report the mean and pseudo-median differences (Tables 1–2, pp. 7–8).
- Impute the training set alone, then the test set with the imputed training data, to mimic production, and compare the alternatives in an appendix (pp. 7, 22–23).

**Quotes**:
- "In short, as far as prediction is concerned, crude is good." (p. 5)
- "On the contrary, we observe on real-world design matrices and missingness patterns (both for synthetic and real signals Y ) that an imputation method as sophisticated as mice leads, on average, to less accurate predictions than simple mean imputation." (p. 12)
