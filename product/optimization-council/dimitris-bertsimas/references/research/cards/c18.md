### S105 · Logistic regression: From art to science (Statistical Science 2017, DOI 10.1214/16-STS602)
**Read level**: full — all 18 pages (file pp. 1–18 = printed pp. 367–384): text, Tables 1–11 and Figs. 1–2 read; the reference list (pp. 16–18) skimmed. The online supplement (DOI 10.1214/16-STS602SUPP: the summary of purposeful selection and the discrete first-order heuristic, pp. 2, 9) is not in the file and was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Angela King, Data Scientist at End to End Analytics at publication (p. 1) · **Position in the trajectory**: the logistic sequel to best subset selection [8] (AoS 2016; p. 1), in the 2014–2019 machine-learning-under-a-modern-optimization-lens period (SW4); its robust objective comes from the group's robust classification paper with Dunn, Pawlowski and Zhuo [6] (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO RO}: a good logistic model must be predictive, interpretable, significant, robust to data error and sparse, and modelers reach it by iterative selection and refitting, which "has been more of an art than a science" (p. 1). A review of four textbooks finds that they rarely say how to weigh the goals together; Hosmer–Lemeshow's "purposeful selection" is the laborious practice; the sequential route guarantees neither the properties nor the best model (p. 2). The opening for optimization: Furnival–Wilson's leaps-and-bounds does not scale past p = 30, so statistics came to view best subset as intractable (p. 3), while MILO solvers and hardware sped up by about 450 billion in 1991–2015 (abstract, p. 1; p. 6). Problem stated: one MINLO that imposes all properties jointly; if it is infeasible, that is itself a guarantee that the properties cannot be had together (pp. 1–3).
  - Transferable: read the field's textbooks for how practitioners combine competing criteria, and use their own admission of "art" as the gap statement (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: every property becomes a constraint or objective term of Problem (7) (Table 1, p. 2; p. 5): support binaries z with big-M linking (7c), a cardinality bound (7d), equal binaries for group sparsity (7e), z_i + z_j ≤ 1 for pairs correlated beyond ρ (7f), at most one transformation per variable (7g), forced inclusion of expert-chosen variables (7h), and exclusion cuts (7i) for rejected models; robustness enters the objective through Theorem 2.1 (pp. 4–5). A three-stage loop (pp. 4–6): preprocess (50/25/25 split; k_max from a maximum-independent-set MIO (6)); solve (7) for every k ≤ k_max and every robustness value and keep the three models with the best validation AUC; bootstrap significance and condition numbers, cut off failing supports and return to stage 2. The solver: outer approximation alternating a MILO master and a fixed-support NLO (p. 8), run as a single tree through lazy-constraint callbacks (p. 9), with a first cut at the discrete first-order heuristic solution of [8] for pure best subset (p. 9); for n < p, an ℓ1 box around a 180-second solution (p. 10). Changed: properties imposed jointly instead of one at a time; kept: the likelihood objective and the classical diagnostics, now used as accept/reject tests.
- **D3 Model and data**: robustified logistic likelihood over a norm ball of data perturbations ΔX (p. 4; the radius symbol is lost in the text layer); big-M with M only required to be large enough (p. 5); defaults: five robustness values spaced logarithmically up to 0.5√p, ρ = 0.7, condition number 30, three stage-3 rounds (pp. 4–6). Synthetic data: x ~ N(0, Σ) with σ_ij = ρ^|i−j|, labels y_i = Round(1/(1 + exp(−β′x_i + ε_i))) — a rounded noisy score, not a Bernoulli draw — and k = 5 equal coefficients (p. 7), later unequal ones (p. 15). Real data: five UCI data sets (p. 12) and TCGA lung-cancer gene expression (p. 11). Admitted: bootstrap confidence intervals replace likelihood-ratio, Wald and score tests because the estimator is robustified and constrained (p. 4); selecting and testing on the same training data can inflate type 1 error (p. 6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: no new theorem; Theorem 2.1 is cited from [6] (p. 4). Devices: a maximum-independent-set MIO on the conflict graph bounds k (p. 5); post-fit failures become no-good cuts Σ_{ℓ∈S_i} z_ℓ ≤ |S_i| − 1 that cut a corner off the binary hypercube (p. 6); outer approximation with gradient cuts f(β) ≥ f(β̂) + ∇f(β̂)′(β − β̂), stopping when master and NLO values agree within ε (p. 8); lazy-constraint callbacks (CPLEX 12.3 in 2010, Gurobi 5.0 in 2012) so that only one branch-and-bound tree is built (p. 9); an initial cut at the heuristic solution (p. 9); an ℓ1 ball of radius ‖β0‖1/k that lets the solver certify optimality only "within the bounding box" (p. 10). Certificate language: MIO lower bounds give "an increasingly better guarantee of suboptimality", which heuristics lack (p. 6). No convergence proof, complexity statement or gap report.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: a solver bake-off on the NEOS server (2.2 GHz, 24 cores, 64 GB; AMPL; default options; 7,200 s cut-off; five runs averaged; true k only) of Bonmin, KNITRO, FilMINT, MINLP, SCIP and Couenne on six synthetic problems from (n, p, ρ) = (100, 10, 0.4) to (2000, 200, 0.8): SCIP and Couenne are cut off on all six and no solver solves Problem 6 (Table 2, pp. 7–8). The tailored method needs <1, 15, 16, <1, 155 and 258 s (Table 3, p. 9), on a Xeon E5440 chosen "in order to fairly compare times with the NEOS server" (p. 9); Gurobi 6.0.0, Julia 0.3.3, JuMP 0.7.0 and GLMNet 0.0.2 (p. 9). Best subset vs Lasso (ten trials): at n = 2000, p = 200 equal AUC, MINLO never above seven nonzeros for five true ones while Lasso selects far more (Fig. 1, p. 10); at n = 400, p = 1000 Lasso is "frequently, but not always" slightly better in AUC and selects 2–4 times the true count (pp. 10–11). TCGA: n = 992, p = 13,937 after dropping genes with missing values; Lasso screens 300 genes over ten splits and MINLO runs on those 300: AUC 0.973 ± 0.01 vs 0.977 ± 0.02 (p. 11). Full approach with a 60-s limit per MINLO (p. 12): high collinearity (Table 4) — similar AUC, Lasso models with maximum pairwise correlation 0.82–0.90 and condition numbers 69.7–109.1 against at most 0.30 and 4.8 for MINLO, but MINLO's true positives drop to 2.6 of 5 at σ = 5 (pp. 12–13); robustness (Table 5, p. 12); real data (Table 6, p. 13) — MINLO's AUC lower on Bank (0.956 vs 0.994, called "the price of interpretability"), Ozone 8 (0.869 vs 0.895) and Ozone 1 (0.885 vs 0.888), higher on Telescope and Mass, at up to 1,583 s per data set; Telescope rerun with ρ = 0.5 (Table 7, p. 14); a combined example (Table 8, 1,761 s, p. 14); unequal coefficients (Tables 9–10) — at n = 400, p = 1000 MINLO's AUC 0.689 vs 0.703 after 4,301 s (p. 15); adaptive Lasso (Table 11, p. 16), whose table has no MINLO column, the comparison resting on §§5.2–5.3 (p. 16). My readings of the tables: the validation-selected robustness parameter is between 0 and 0.001 wherever it is printed (Tables 4, 5, 8, 10; pp. 12, 14–15), although robustness is what made Problems 4–6 hard for the solvers (p. 8); no table reports a gap or which runs hit the limit ("Often optimality is reached before the time limit", p. 12); the figure captions number the problems differently from §4.2 (pp. 7, 10–11); the combined example is given as p = 200 (p. 13) and as 100 columns plus 100 squares "up to 1000" (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: extends [8] from linear to logistic regression (p. 1); Sato et al. [48] are the only MIO predecessors, with a piecewise-linear likelihood and the full MINLO left as their future work: "In this paper, we will directly consider the MINLO" (p. 3); its robustness is set apart from distributionally robust logistic regression [49] (p. 4); outer approximation is credited to Duran–Grossmann [21] (p. 8) and the solver landscape to Bussieck–Vigerske's 24 MINLO solvers [13] (p. 7). Priority claims recur: "the only methodology we are aware of" (p. 2), the first to bring dynamic constraint generation into statistical modeling (pp. 2–3, 9), "the first algorithm that we are aware of to make use of callbacks within optimization software to solve a statistical problem" (p. 16). Lasso is the comparator because practitioners will use it (p. 15) and is recast, after [8], as "a robust method first and foremost" (p. 11). Purposeful selection, the practice named as the problem, is summarized in the supplement and never run as a baseline (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the title states the transplant; the abstract stacks desired properties → the 450-billion speed-up → a joint MINLO → a guarantee of suboptimality → infeasibility as a guarantee (p. 1). An "Aspirations of the Work" subsection states the goal before any method (p. 2), and Table 1 maps each property to a constraint (p. 2). The modeler is "she" throughout (pp. 1, 4–6). Hedges sit on the run times (pp. 8, 12), not on the optimality wording: MINLO "trades these properties off in an optimal way given the constraints the modeler specifies" (p. 13) beside a 60-s limit and no gap columns (p. 12). The introduction promises subset selection "when the number of variables is over 30,000" (p. 1); in that experiment the MINLO saw 300 Lasso-screened genes (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: modeling (one MINLO plus a three-stage loop), algorithm (outer approximation with lazy callbacks and a heuristic first cut) and computational evidence (pp. 2–3, 16). Limits stated specifically: post-selection inference (p. 6); certification in n < p "happens slowly, if at all" (p. 10); timings are not benchmarks (pp. 8, 12); lower true-positive recovery under noise (p. 13); TCGA models "unlikely to be helpful for diagnosis" (p. 11). Directions: other robust losses, residual diagnostics and post-selection remedies can be added (p. 6); the conclusion names no open problem (p. 16).

**Method links**: Method 4 ✅ evidence — steps 1–3 and 5: one joint MINLO replaces the refit loop of purposeful selection (pp. 1–2, 5); the discrete first-order heuristic of [8] gives the first cut (p. 9); solver versions, machine and a 60-s limit are stated (pp. 9, 12); duel against Lasso and adaptive Lasso out of sample on synthetic data with known truth and on real data (pp. 10–16). **Transferable techniques**:
- Encode each modeling desideratum as a linear constraint on support binaries (equal binaries for a group, z_i + z_j ≤ 1 for a correlated pair, at most one of a variable's transforms, z = 1 for an expert-forced variable) and print a property-to-constraint table (Table 1, pp. 2, 5).
- Turn a failed post-fit check (an insignificant coefficient, a condition number above 30) into a no-good cut Σ_{ℓ∈S} z_ℓ ≤ |S| − 1, re-solve, and cap the rounds (p. 6).
- Bound the feasible model size first by a maximum-independent-set MIO on the conflict graph of the constraints (p. 5).
- Solve a convex MINLO inside a MILO solver: outer-approximation gradient cuts added by lazy-constraint callbacks in one branch-and-bound tree, with the first cut at a heuristic solution (pp. 8–9).
- When the lower bound stalls in n < p, restrict to an ℓ1 ball of radius ‖β0‖1/k around a time-limited solution and certify inside it, saying that the certificate is local (p. 10).
- Before writing a tailored solver, run the available solvers (NEOS, default options, fixed cut-off) on a graded set of instances and print the failures (Table 2, pp. 7–8).

### S013 · Data-driven robust optimization (Mathematical Programming 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10107-017-1125-8; read as arXiv 1401.0212v2)
**Read level**: full, of the arXiv version — arXiv:1401.0212v2 (23 Nov 2014), headed "Submitted to Operations Research" and first submitted in August 2013 (p. 1), 53 pages. Main text and references (pp. 1–38) read in full; e-companion: the proofs of Theorems 1–8, 10, 12 and 13 read at the level of their argument (pp. 39–47), EC.3 on multiple constraints and EC.5 on the queueing bound read (pp. 47–50), EC.6 on other goodness-of-fit tests skimmed (pp. 50–53); the text breaks off mid-sentence in Remark EC.3 (p. 53). The Mathematical Programming print (167(2), pp. 235–292) was not read: every page reference in this card is an arXiv v2 page.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vishal Gupta and Nathan Kallus, both at the Operations Research Center, MIT (p. 1) · **Position in the trajectory**: written 2013–2014 at the turn from the robust-optimization period to data-driven decisions; builds on Bertsimas–Sim 2004 and Bertsimas–Brown 2009 (pp. 1, 13) and on the three authors' own linear-convex-ordering test (p. 22); it is the paper SKILL.md's Method 3 correction cites for setting the knob from data.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO CP; AP and SD as testbeds}: robust optimization stands or falls with its uncertainty set: well-chosen sets give tractable models that perform well, poorly chosen ones over-conservative or intractable models (p. 1). Existing proposals derive sets from a priori reasoning and mild assumptions (p. 1); the growth of data motivates "a shift in thinking" towards a data-centered paradigm, and the question is how robust optimization should adapt (p. 2). Problem stated: for a constraint f(u, x) ≤ 0 ∀u ∈ U with f concave in u, construct sets with (P1) a tractable robust counterpart and (P2) a probabilistic guarantee at level ε, as before, but now from i.i.d. data drawn from P* — "this is critical" (p. 2) — so that the sets are smaller by inclusion and the solutions less conservative (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: keep the incumbent school's success criteria word for word and change only the information the construction uses (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: a three-step schema (p. 10): take the confidence region of a hypothesis test; bound the worst-case Value at Risk over that region by a convex, positively homogeneous function; take the closed convex set whose support function is that bound. Theorem 1 makes the bound the right target: a set implies the guarantee for every f concave in u if its support function dominates VaR, and fails for some bi-affine f if it does not (p. 10). Pairing a priori assumptions with tests yields the catalogue of Table 1 (p. 4): χ² and G tests for known finite support (§4), Kolmogorov–Smirnov and other goodness-of-fit tests or forward/backward deviations for independent marginals (§5), order statistics when only marginal samples exist (§6), a linear-convex-ordering test for joint samples (§7), and Shawe-Taylor–Cristianini and Delage–Ye recast as tests with bootstrapped thresholds (§8). For several constraints, the ε_j are optimized under a simultaneous guarantee (Theorem 3, pp. 11–12) by alternating between the decisions and the ε_j (Theorem 13, p. 29; EC.3, pp. 47–48). The set and the parameters (ε, α) are chosen by hold-out or 5-fold cross-validation (pp. 29–30). Kept from the a priori school: (P1)–(P2) and the Ben-Tal–den Hertog–Vial counterpart machinery (pp. 2, 7); changed: the set is calibrated from data, with the guarantee holding with probability 1 − α over the sample (Theorem 2, p. 10).
- **D3 Model and data**: a single robust constraint with f concave in u (p. 2) under a regularity condition from Ben-Tal et al. (p. 7; endnote 1, p. 36); data i.i.d. from P* (p. 2); per-set a priori assumptions: known finite support (p. 12), independent components with a known bounding box (p. 15) or bounded support (p. 18), marginal samples only, as with asynchronous data or missing values (p. 20), joint samples with possibly unbounded support (p. 22); for Delage–Ye the radius R is taken as known (endnote 3, p. 36). Admitted approximations: bootstrapped thresholds (N_B = 10^4) are "only asymptotically valid" yet recommended (p. 8); the t-test's robustness to non-normality (p. 28); union bounds (pp. 11, 18, 21); cross-validation without proof (p. 30). Both applications are synthetic: Natarajan et al.'s ten-asset return model with N = 500 and 2,000 (pp. 30–31), and a G/G/1 queue with Pareto(1.1) service times truncated at 15 and exponential interarrival times truncated at 15.25, about 90% utilization (pp. 34–35).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: tractability reduces to representing {(v, t): δ*(v | U) ≤ t} through the partial concave conjugate (pp. 7–8). Proof devices: Theorem 1 by a strict separating hyperplane between U and the set where f(·, x*) ≥ t (p. 39); Theorems 2–3 by a three-line probability chain, with a confidence region that does not depend on ε giving the simultaneous guarantee (pp. 39–40); support functions by Lagrangian duality (pp. 40, 42–43); the χ² set through the Rockafellar–Uryasev CVaR set and a hyperbolic second-order-cone constraint (pp. 40–41); the KS worst case by LP duality, which puts the worst-case distribution on the left or right edge of the KS band (Theorem EC.2, pp. 16, 41–42); forward/backward deviations by three decoupled scalar problems with a closed-form multiplier (p. 43); the linear-convex-ordering set by semi-infinite conic duality (pp. 44–45); Delage–Ye by S-lemma-type duality and Schur complements (pp. 46–47); bi-convexity in ε by second derivatives (p. 47); G and χ² regions within O(nN^−3) of each other by a Taylor expansion (Proposition 1, pp. 14, 41). Each set comes with an explicit counterpart solvable in "polynomial time using off-the-shelf software" (p. 5) and, for cutting-plane use, a separation oracle in closed form or by line search (Remarks 7, 9, 14, 17; pp. 17, 19, 23, 26), or the ellipsoid method for the linear-convex-ordering set (p. 23). Queueing: a worst-case Lindley recursion, a closed form by relaxing the integrality of j (p. 34), KKT conditions for the optimized ε_j (eq. 44, p. 34) and a busy-period truncation that splits the data between its two estimates (EC.5, pp. 48–50); the main text states that the truncated bound holds with probability at least 1 − α (p. 34), the e-companion claims 1 − 2α (p. 50).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: geometry first: Fig. 1 (an octagon; the χ² and G sets shrink with N toward the CVaR set; α = ε = 10%; p. 14), Fig. 2 (both independent-marginal sets learn symmetry and skew from 100 points; pp. 19–20), Fig. 3 (four sets at N = 100 and 1,000; pp. 27–28); Table 2 — bootstrapped thresholds are an order of magnitude or more below the concentration-inequality ones, which do not even apply for N ≤ 50 (Shawe-Taylor–Cristianini) and N ≤ 50,000 (Delage–Ye) (p. 25); Fig. EC.1 (a bootstrapped set from 100 points is smaller than the unbootstrapped set from 50 times as many; p. 48). Portfolio (pp. 30–33): sets M, LCX, CS, DY and CS ∩ supp(P*) against Calafiore–Monastero (CM), calibrated "to also provide a bound on the 10% worst-case return that holds with at least 90%" (p. 31); 5-fold cross-validation picks LCX or CS, and the true model confirms the choice; M, CS ∩ supp and DY put all wealth in the first asset (true 10% worst-case return −1.095 against −0.373, −0.416 and −0.425 for LCX, CS and CM at N = 500); over 100 repetitions CS is best and most stable (average −0.397; LCX −0.411; CM −0.539) (Table 3, pp. 31–32); with N = 2,000 LCX beats CM more clearly (p. 33). Queueing (pp. 33–36): the bounds on customer 10's median wait tighten with N and with optimized ε_j (Fig. 5, p. 35); at N = 10,000 they average 34.6, 25.8 and 14.4 against 55.1 for a sample Kingman bound, with standard deviations 0.3–1.2 against 8.7 (Table 4, p. 36). Not reported: any solve time, solver or machine (none appears in the text); any real data; and, as I read §11, any non-data-driven set as a baseline in either application, although the abstract (p. 1) and §11's summary (p. 30) say the new sets outperform traditional ones; the simulations behind preferring KS to the other goodness-of-fit tests are summarized, not shown (p. 17). Failures printed: CS ∩ supp(P*) is smaller than CS but worse out of sample (p. 32); in-sample objective values are loose bounds and should not select the set (p. 32).
- **D6 Positioning**: against Ben-Tal et al.'s φ-divergence sets for uncertain probabilities with finite support, it claims sets for general uncertain parameters with continuous support, which makes retrofitting existing applications more direct (pp. 3–4); Klabjan et al. and Goldfarb–Iyengar used tests in special settings that do not obviously generalize (p. 4); Calafiore–El Ghaoui and Delage–Ye are reinterpreted "through the lens of hypothesis testing" and improved by the bootstrap (pp. 4–5, 23–27); Campi–Garatti's scenario approach is conceded to be "more generally applicable" with a similar guarantee, but is more conservative when f is concave in u, needs N ≥ N(ε) and yields N dense constraints (p. 5; Remark 5, pp. 14–15); Chen–Sim–Sun's deviation set is generalized to unknown mean and support (pp. 17–18). The a priori proposals, Bertsimas–Sim 2004 among them, are called "theoretically motivated and experimentally validated" (p. 1), and the paper says "We are not the first" (p. 3). Self-citations supply the robust queueing testbed and a reformulation-versus-cutting-plane study (pp. 3, 33).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: abstract: data availability → schema → tractable "both theoretically and practically" → a finite-sample guarantee → selection procedures → computational evidence (p. 1). Seven numbered contributions (pp. 5–6); Table 1 maps assumptions × tests × geometry × separation for the whole paper on one page (p. 4); theorems carry the claims, twenty Remarks the computational advice, and all proofs go to the e-companion (p. 6). Hedges are explicit and frequent: "Our list is non-exhaustive" (p. 3), "we believe practitioners can safely use bootstrapped thresholds" (p. 8), "we cannot prove" for cross-validation (p. 30), "we do not claim that our new bounds are the best possible" (p. 33). The conclusion is one paragraph calling the work "a first step" (p. 36).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory (Theorems 1–3), a catalogue of sets with explicit counterparts, algorithms (separation oracles; allocation of ε across constraints), a selection protocol and two illustrations (pp. 5–6). Limits are specific: exponential-cone sets are numerically challenging and LMI solvers less developed (pp. 13, 17, 27); intersecting sets loses the guarantee and choosing the set with the best objective is biased (p. 29); a hold-out split halves the calibration data (p. 29); the marginal-sample set lacks the simultaneous guarantee (p. 21); the queueing bounds are not the best possible (p. 33). Directions: retrofit robust models in information theory, supply chains and revenue management, and extend the queueing analysis to networks (p. 33).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence — steps 2–4: sets are designed so that the robust counterpart stays in named convex classes (second-order cone; polyhedral plus one entropy constraint; exponential cone; LMI), each with an explicit description (Table 1, pp. 3–4), and the paper prefers the more mature class when two sets are close (χ² over G, p. 14; CS over the LMI-based DY, p. 27); duality supplies worst-case distributions (the KS band's edges, pp. 16, 41–42) and the VaR characterization (p. 10). The stop rule's measured solve time is missing: "practically" tractable (p. 1) is never timed. Method 3 ⚠ variant — confirms the correction recorded in SKILL.md: the set and its parameters ε and α are chosen from data, by hold-out (which keeps the guarantee) or 5-fold cross-validation (pp. 29–30); but the Bertsimas–Sim budget set is not among the sets (Table 1, p. 4), and no price curve is printed — the trade-off appears only as in-sample against true out-of-sample values (Table 3, p. 31). Method 5 ⚠ variant — sets are judged by the decision's performance under the true model, not by their size: "size calculations alone cannot predict performance" (p. 32); but there is no owner, current rule or real data (pp. 30–36). New-pattern candidate: hypothesis test → uncertainty set (pp. 3–4, 10). New-pattern candidate: reinterpret rivals inside your lens, then upgrade them with its tools (pp. 4–5, 23–28). New-pattern candidate: retrofit existing robust models with data-driven sets (pp. 4, 33). New-pattern candidate: knobs chosen by hold-out validation (pp. 29–30; also S105, S030).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Calibrate an uncertainty set from data with a guarantee: confidence region of a test → convex upper bound on the worst-case VaR → the set with that support function; check the guarantee with the support-function-dominates-VaR condition (pp. 10, 39).
- Replace concentration-inequality thresholds by bootstrapped ones (Algorithm 1) and print a threshold table against N to show the shrinkage (pp. 8–9, 25).
- Allocate risk across m uncertain constraints by optimizing the ε_j under a simultaneous guarantee, alternating between decisions and ε_j with a trust-region LP on shadow prices (κ = 0.05) so that the objective never increases (pp. 11–12, 29, 47–48).
- Select among candidate sets by evaluating the resulting decisions on a hold-out half, which preserves the guarantee because the halves are independent; fall back to k-fold cross-validation for small N and say that it is unproven (pp. 29–30).
- Calibrate a non-set competitor to the same guarantee before comparing, and audit the cross-validated choice against a known generative model (p. 31).
- Give each set a separation oracle so that the counterpart can run inside a cutting-plane method instead of a heavy conic reformulation (Remarks 7, 9, 17; pp. 17, 19, 26).

### S030 · From predictive methods to missing data imputation: an optimization approach (Journal of Machine Learning Research 2018, JMLR 18(196); http://jmlr.org/papers/v18/17-073.html)
**Read level**: full — all 39 pages (file p. N = printed p. N): text, Algorithm 1, Tables 1–10, Figs. 1–5 (values only where the text layer prints them) and the reference list (pp. 37–39).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Colin Pawlowski and Ying Daisy Zhuo, Sloan School of Management and Operations Research Center, MIT (p. 1) · **Position in the trajectory**: submitted February 2017, published April 2018 (p. 1), in the machine-learning-under-a-modern-optimization-lens period; it presents itself as the next step after the group's MIO and robust work on regression and trees (p. 4) and uses the OptimalTrees package (p. 20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO HS}: missing data is "arguably the most common issue" practitioners face (p. 1); Table 1 lists 19 imputation methods (p. 2), but joint models are not effective on non-normal data and crash on singular covariance matrices, and conditional methods such as pmm "lack theoretical foundation and have no explicit formulation as an optimization problem", in contrast to other areas of machine learning (p. 4). Entry: optimization advances (integer and convex optimization for median and sparse regression; optimal trees) have driven ML progress, so reconsider imputation "from this perspective" (p. 4). Problem stated: choose the imputed values (and auxiliary model variables) to minimize a predictive-model cost with the observed entries fixed (Problem (1), p. 6).
  - Transferable: when a field's standard procedure has no objective, write down the objective its iterations seem to improve, then make the procedure descend it (pp. 4, 7).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: one problem over all missing entries at once, with three costs (Table 2, p. 7): K-NN (distances to K nearest neighbours with binary assignments, (7), p. 9), mixed SVM regression and classification with dummy-encoded categorical values as decision variables ((11), p. 12), and optimal-tree leaf co-membership ((15), p. 15). opt.impute (Algorithm 1, p. 8) alternates: fit the model given the imputations (sorting for K-NN, SVM duals, tree training), then update the imputations by block coordinate descent (a linear system or an integer program) or coordinate descent (closed-form weighted averages and modes) (pp. 10–17). Warm starts: mean, knn, bpca and five random draws, keeping the lowest objective (p. 19). The model and its hyperparameters are chosen by hiding a further 10% of the entries (opt.cv), and the benchmarks get the same treatment (benchmark.cv) (pp. 17, 20). Multiple imputation runs opt.impute from m warm starts drawn by mice (opt.mi) (pp. 18, 21–22). Kept: the K-NN, SVM and tree predictors and iterative re-imputation; changed: an explicit joint objective, monotone descent and, for K-NN, a reverse-neighbour term in the update (eq. 10, p. 11).
- **D3 Model and data**: mixed data with continuous columns scaled to unit standard deviation and categorical columns left as classes (p. 6); scaling replaces a weight between the continuous and categorical costs "for simplicity of notation" (p. 7); RBF kernel in the experiments (pp. 18, 20); for continuous variables opt.tree uses "a single-leaf regularized regression tree" fitted with glmnet — no splits — and optimal classification trees only for categorical variables (p. 20). Missingness is imposed on complete UCI data: MCAR and NMAR at 10–50%; MAR is left out "Because this assumption is less general than NMAR" (p. 20); validation masks are always MCAR (p. 21). 84 UCI data sets with n = 23–5,875 and p = 2–124 (p. 22). Admitted: the integer formulations take "a long time" to certify, so only local solutions are sought (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: binary neighbour assignments with Σ_j z_ij = K and z_ii = 0 ((7), p. 9); category mismatch linearized with a big-M of k_d (p. 11); SVM subproblems as dual QPs (p. 13) and per-observation nonlinear integer problems ((14), pp. 13–14); tree constraints imported from Bertsimas–Dunn ((15), p. 15). Analysis: strict descent by at least δ0 bounds the iterations by ⌈c(U⁰, W⁰, V⁰; X)/δ0⌉, with no guarantee of a global minimum (p. 7); greedy trees void the monotone descent (p. 15); closed-form updates Qw̃ = Rx̃ (pp. 10–11), weighted averages over neighbours and reverse neighbours (eq. 10, p. 11), leaf means and modes (pp. 16–17); the coordinate-descent bottleneck is the O(n log n) neighbour assignment (p. 31). In practice the categorical block step is replaced by the faster mode update (p. 11). A small slip: Q is called "positive semidefinite and therefore invertible" for large K, with a diagonal perturbation when singular (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: benchmarks mean, pmm (MICE), bpca (pcaMethods), knn (impute) and iknn (own R and Julia implementation) (p. 19); a 12-hour limit per method and data set (p. 20); MAE and RMSE on hidden entries (p. 17). At 30% MCAR some opt.impute method has the lowest MAE on 76.2% of the data sets, trees most often (p. 24; Table 10, pp. 35–36); opt.cv wins the most data sets in every scenario (Fig. 2, p. 25) and has the lowest geometric-mean MAE and RMSE (Fig. 3, p. 27): 0.100 vs 0.118 for knn at 10% missing and 0.142 vs 0.172 at 50% (p. 24), and 0.118 vs 0.131 against benchmark.cv at 30% (p. 24); Wilcoxon signed-rank and paired t-tests with adjusted p-values (Table 5, p. 26). Downstream (10 UCI tasks, 50% train/test split, m = 5, 100 repetitions; pp. 21–22): opt.cv beats benchmark.cv at every missing level, by 0.0171 in accuracy and 0.0237 in R² at 50%; opt.mi beats mice in 3 of 5 classification and 5 of 5 regression levels (Tables 7–8, p. 30). Sensitivity to K and C on iris (Fig. 5, p. 31). Timing on one thread of a Xeon E5-2650 with 8 GB, a 4-hour limit and mean warm starts only (pp. 31–32): svm.CD does not finish on mushroom (30–50%), skin-segmentation or cnae-9, tree.CD not on mushroom or cnae-9, and knn.CD needs 1,144–2,582 s on skin-segmentation (n = 245,057) (Table 9, p. 32). My checks against the printed numbers: (0.172 − 0.142)/0.172 ≈ 17%, not the stated 12.1% (p. 24); opt.mi beats opt.cv significantly in 7 of the 10 scenarios of Tables 7–8 (2 of 5 classification, 5 of 5 regression; p. 30), not "8/10" (p. 28); Table 5's MAE differences are not significant for opt.knn and opt.svm against bpca but are for opt.tree against iknn, so the prose summary is looser than the table (pp. 24, 26); Table 9's libras-movement rows repeat the iris rows in every column but bpca (p. 32), so the claim about scaling in p drawn from that data set (p. 31) cannot be checked; missForest, listed in Table 1 and the related work (pp. 2–3), is not a benchmark (p. 19); the MIO certification runs are mentioned without numbers (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: a 19-method taxonomy partly adapted from Liew et al.'s review (p. 2); criticism of mean imputation, joint modelling and pmm (pp. 2–4); the lineage claimed is optimization-driven machine learning, cited entirely from the group's own papers (Bertsimas–Van Parys, Bertsimas–Mazumder, Bertsimas–Dunn, robust classification; p. 4); the benchmarks are described as "the most commonly used state-of-the-art methods" (p. 18). Unlike S105, no solver speed-up figure or intractability belief is invoked (pp. 1–5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the abstract runs problem → framework → "high quality solutions in seconds" → five benchmarks on 84 data sets → headline numbers (8.3% lower MAE than the best cross-validated benchmark; downstream R² 0.339 vs 0.315 and accuracy 86.1% vs 84.4%; 8 of 10 scenarios against mice) (p. 1). Four numbered contributions repeat the numbers with a slightly different win rate ("more than 75.8%", p. 5, against 76.2% in the body, p. 24). The method is named Optimal Impute (p. 19) although it is a local method (p. 7). Scaling claims grow from body to discussion: n "in the 100,000s" and p "in the 1,000s" (p. 5), and coordinate descent for SVM solving such problems "in seconds" (p. 33), whereas §3.2.5 says SVM imputation "scales relatively poorly" and Table 9 shows svm.CD unfinished after 4 hours at n = 245,057 and at p = 856 (pp. 31–32). The title has the From-X-to-Y form.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a family of formulations, first-order algorithms, a large benchmark and a multiple-imputation extension (pp. 5, 33–34). Limits stated: no global optimum (p. 7); MAE can rise late in the descent, which suggests overfitting to the known entries, to be met by regularization or early stopping (p. 22); SVM scaling (p. 31); multiple imputation is slower, needs pooling and gives a less interpretable ensemble (p. 33); validation masks are MCAR only (p. 33). Directions: other costs (a regularizer, L1 or Mahalanobis distances), faster optimal trees, and solving Problem (1) globally, which "remains an open question" (pp. 33–34).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write a fill-in task as minimization over the missing entries of a model-fit cost with the observed entries fixed; alternate model fitting with closed-form re-imputation and use the objective as the convergence monitor (pp. 6–8).
- Create validation data by hiding a further 10% of the observed entries, choose model and hyperparameters by validation MAE, then re-impute under the original pattern (pp. 17, 20).
- Give the baselines the same selection layer (benchmark.cv) before comparing composite methods (pp. 17, 20, 24).
- Report win counts, geometric means with geometric-SD bands, and multiplicity-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank and paired t-tests (Figs. 2–3, Table 5; pp. 24–27).
- Score an intermediate product (imputations) by the out-of-sample performance of downstream models across missing levels, for single and multiple imputation (pp. 21–22, 28–30).
- Start the local method from each incumbent's output plus random draws and keep the best objective; for multiple imputation, use draws from a probabilistic incumbent (mice) as the m warm starts (pp. 18–19).
