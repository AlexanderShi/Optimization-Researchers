His co-authors are his students Doan, Iancu, Goyal, Sun, Frankovich and Rikun and his MIT colleagues Parrilo, Gamarnik and Odoni. - How is a tractable policy class or robust model justified: a bound, its tightness, or an exact benchmark?
- Does the robust model keep its nominal class?
- How is the knob set (Γ, the quantile α, the polynomial degree d, the geometry of the set)?
- In applied work, what is the baseline: the owner's current rule, or a constructed proxy?
- How are limits printed?

### S233 · Robust and data-driven approaches to call centers (European Journal of Operational Research 2010, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2010.05.040)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–27 of the November 2008 author preprint (all text; figures survive only as captions). The preprint has no separate conclusion section: §4.3.5 closes the paper (pp. 23–26).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Xuan Vinh Doan (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: applied robust versus data-driven optimization, citing the parallel Bertsimas–Thiele data-driven newsvendor preprint (pp. 5, 27); NSF grant 0556106 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + DA; SD (fluid queueing model); AP}: The problem is staffing and dynamic routing for call centers with several customer classes and agent pools (pp. 1–2). The entry is an assumption the literature keeps. Arrival rates are random and time-varying, "as opposed to the usual assumption of constant or known arrival rates", and customers abandon (p. 2). Prior staffing methods assume a known probabilistic structure of arrival rates (Harrison–Zeevi 2005) or estimate the distribution from historical data (Bassamboo–Zeevi 2007) (p. 2). Given historical data, the paper offers a data-driven model and a robust model (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A discrete fluid model puts staffing (pool capacities b_j(s) per shift) and routing (reserved allocations d_ij(t)) into one LP (eqs. (1)–(3), pp. 3–4). Two models are built on it:
  - a risk-averse data-driven model that minimizes expected shortfall over K historical arrival paths, justified by second-order stochastic dominance (pp. 5–7);
  - a robust model with the per-period set λ_i(t) ≤ λ̄_i(t) + Γσ_i(t) (eq. (14), p. 12). It reduces to the nominal LP with inflated arrivals (Theorem 3, p. 12).

  Online, a routing rule keeps service shares close to the offline allocation d_ij(t) (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model assumptions: fluid dynamics with abandonment rate θ_i < 1 and service rate μ_ij < 1 per period (p. 3). Service rates are held constant, because time-varying ones "will significantly affect the tractability of our model" (p. 5). Abandonment follows Harrison–Zeevi's exponential-patience model, and the time step is assumed fine enough (p. 5). The uncertainty set is separable by class (p. 9).
  - Data: SEESTAT records from a US bank, about 300 weekdays. The day runs 6:00–18:00 in T = 720 one-minute periods with S = 24 half-hour shifts, covering the six busiest classes and five pools. A cross-pool agent is assumed at most 10% slower (p. 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Two LP devices. First, the empirical expected shortfall is the mean of the K_α largest costs; that sum is an LP over x ∈ [0,1]^K with Σx = K_α, whose dual gives v + w_k ≥ C_k, at the price of one copy of the dynamics per historical path (Theorem 2, pp. 7–8). Second, the penalty is nondecreasing in each λ_i(t): relaxing the queue equation to ≥ leaves the optimum unchanged, and the value is a maximum over dual extreme points with π_i(t) ≥ 0 (Proposition 1, pp. 9–11). So the worst case over an upper-bound set is its corner (eq. (13), p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: About 300 days are split 150/75/75 into training, validation and test sets; α runs over 0–1 and Γ over 0–3 in steps of 0.1; the solver is CPLEX 9.1's barrier method (p. 14). Decisions are scored by event-driven simulation with thinning-generated arrivals (p. 13) on designs I, V, N, W (Gans et al.) and C1–C3 (pp. 15, 23). The robust staffing has the lower mean test cost in I and V (pp. 17, 19), the data-driven one in N and W (pp. 20–23). Failures are printed: in V, two test paths incur penalties of about 45,000 and 240,000 under the robust staffing, against under 30,000 for every path under the data-driven one (p. 19); in N, one path incurs more than 600,000 (p. 21). Solve times are 0.08–3.45 s (robust) against 99.89–44,469.47 s (data-driven) (Table 2, p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on the call-center modelling line (Gans–Koole–Mandelbaum, Harrison–Zeevi, Bassamboo–Zeevi), cited rather than criticized (pp. 1–2). The single-pool square-root safety-staffing rule is mentioned (p. 1) but not used as a baseline.
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract carries the verdict: "significantly more tractable" and better "in most experiments" (p. 1). Each network gets the same paragraph: validation frontier, chosen parameter, test result, explanation (pp. 15–24). Explanations are hedged with "probably" (pp. 17, 19, 20).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are two LP models and a computational comparison on real data (pp. 2, 26). Two limits are stated. Neither model copes when arrivals move away from history (p. 21). The data-driven model fits better once routing grows complex and depends on the arrival pattern (pp. 19, 23). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅: both models stay LPs. The data-driven one stays an LP through LP duality on the sum of the largest costs (Theorem 2, pp. 7–8). The robust one stays an LP through monotonicity, which turns it into the nominal LP with λ̄ + Γσ (Proposition 1 and Theorem 3, pp. 9–12). Solve times are measured at the target scale (Table 2, p. 18).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: each model has one knob (Γ; the quantile α). The price is printed as mean–standard-deviation frontiers over the knob (Figures 4, 6, 7, 10, 13–15; pp. 15–26), with the bad cases (pp. 19–23).
  - The variant: no knob–property bound is proved.
  - Set from data: the knob is chosen on a validation split and judged on a test split (pp. 13–14). Method 3's correction credits this to his group's later work; it is already here in the 2008 preprint.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: decisions are scored by simulated cost on held-out days of the bank's own data (pp. 13–14). The baseline is the rival data-driven model, though, not the bank's current staffing rule.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (not a Method): the negative cases are in the body (pp. 19, 21, 23).
- New-pattern candidate: *monotone recourse makes the corner the worst case* (pp. 9–12).
- New-pattern candidate: *robust versus scenario-based model on the same history*, with train/validation/test splits, cost frontiers and solve times (pp. 13–26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Prove that the recourse cost of an LP is monotone in the uncertain data from the sign of the dual multipliers at all extreme points. Then the robust counterpart over an upper-bound set {λ ≤ λ̄ + Γσ} is the nominal LP with inflated data (pp. 9–12).
- (D4) To minimize CVaR (expected shortfall) over K historical scenarios, write the sum of the K_α largest costs as an LP and dualize it into v + (1/K_α)Σw_k with v + w_k ≥ C_k. An inner minimization on the small side of a ≥ constraint can be replaced by its feasible set (pp. 7–8).
- (D5) Evaluate optimized fluid decisions by event-driven simulation of the stochastic system, under a routing rule that tracks the optimized allocation. Choose the knob on a validation split before scoring on the test split (pp. 12–14).

**Quotes**:
- "The result also shows that both models do not perform well if arrival data change significantly from the past data." (p. 21)
- "For the network design C3, the robust approach needs less than 4 seconds while the data-driven approach takes more than 12 hours on average." (p. 24)

### S075 · A hierarchy of near-optimal policies for multistage adaptive optimization (IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control 2011, DOI 10.1109/tac.2011.2162878)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–27 of the author preprint dated June 15, 2009, including Appendices 8.1–8.3 and the references. The plots survive only as axis labels.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Dan A. Iancu (MIT Operations Research Center), Pablo A. Parrilo (MIT LIDS) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: follows the authors' MOR 2010 paper, which showed affine policies optimal for one-dimensional systems (pp. 3, 17; reference p. 25). It belongs to the adaptive-policy cluster with S060 and S165.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + CP; SD (multistage control)}: The systems are finite-horizon linear dynamical systems with additive disturbances in compact basic semialgebraic sets, linear state–control constraints, piecewise-affine convex costs and a min–max objective (pp. 1–2). The entry has three parts:
  - DP suffers the curse of dimensionality (p. 2).
  - Affine disturbance-feedback rules, adopted "for purposes of tractability", lose performance, sometimes by a large gap (pp. 3, 6).
  - Other nonlinear rules leave the functional form to guesswork, with no systematic way to trade performance for computation (pp. 3–4).

  Example 1 makes the gap concrete: the best affine policy costs N where the optimum is 1 (p. 6; Lemma 1, p. 22).
- **D2 Core idea**: Make the recourse a polynomial of degree d in past disturbances (eq. (11), p. 7). The states are then polynomials whose coefficients are linear in the decision matrices, so each robust constraint becomes polynomial nonnegativity on a semialgebraic set (pp. 7–8). A Putinar-type SOS certificate with capped multiplier degrees enforces it, and the whole problem becomes one SDP (pp. 8–9). Piecewise costs are replaced by a polynomial upper bound per stage (pp. 9–10). The six steps are listed on p. 10, and the degree d is the user's only choice (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**: The disturbances are additive and independent across time (p. 2). Time-dependent sets, multiplicative uncertainty, known distributions (via moments) and distributional ambiguity are sketched, but "we do not pursue them here" (pp. 11–12). The approximation layers are admitted: the SOS degree cap could be conservative (p. 9), and the cost over-bound and fixed-degree SOS are sufficient, not necessary (p. 10). The tests use interval demand sets |w_k − d̄_k| ≤ ρd̄_k (pp. 16, 18).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Theorem 1: for intersections of convex quadratic sets, J*_AFF ≥ J*_{d=1} ≥ J*_{d=2} ≥ …, because every feasible affine solution stays feasible at d = 1 (nonlinear Farkas lemma; a nonnegative quadratic is SOS); the S-lemma quadratic rule is dominated at d = 2 (p. 14; proof pp. 23–24). Exact benchmark: for polytopic sets, an LP over all extreme disturbance sequences, of size O(∏|ext W_k|), gives the optimum at small horizons (pp. 14–15). Theorem 2: under box uncertainty, multi-affine policies of degree T − 1 are optimal, because their 2^{T−1} coefficients can interpolate that LP's vertex decisions (p. 16; proof pp. 22–23). The size of the SDP is counted in T, n_w and d (pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Two inventory problems: a single echelon with cumulative order bounds (T = 4–10), which keep it out of the one-dimensional case where affine policies are already optimal (p. 17), and a serial supply chain (T = 7, J = 2–5) (pp. 16–18). For each size, 100 random instances are generated "in which the performance of polynomial policies of degree 1 (affine) is suboptimal", and gaps are measured against the exact LP (p. 18). The software is MATLAB, YALMIP and SDPT3 on a 3 GHz Xeon 5050 (p. 19). Affine gaps average 2.8–3.7%, quadratic policies cut them about threefold, and all cubic gaps are below 1% (Table 1, p. 19). At T = 10, cubic solves take a mean of 1,846.94 s (maximum 4,547.09 s), while quadratic solves have a mean and median under 15 s (p. 19; Table 2, p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself between DP and ADP (Bertsekas, Powell), stochastic-programming decision rules (Garstka–Wets), and robust control and RO's affine rules (Ben-Tal–Nemirovski, Kerrigan–Maciejowski, Skaf–Boyd) (pp. 1–3). It thanks a referee for pointing out Ben-Tal–El Ghaoui–Nemirovski (2009) (p. 12) and shows that its hierarchy contains those methods (pp. 13–14).
- **D7 Writing**: The contributions are bulleted with headline numbers: "typically by a factor of 2 or 3", and a median gap below 0.01% (p. 4). Each approximation layer is listed along with why it is judged second-order (pp. 9–10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contribution is a tractable hierarchy with one knob and strong numerical results (pp. 21–22). The limits:
  - The tests cover only the min–max setting with interval demand.
  - The exact benchmark exists only for small horizons (p. 15).
  - Cubic SDPs are expensive (p. 19).

  Future work: structure-exploiting SOS (Nie), first-order SDP methods (p. 19) and commercial interior-point solvers (pp. 21–22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: formulation first. A problem owned by DP and robust control is written as one SDP over policy coefficients (pp. 7–10). The class is not kept: the size grows with d. The boundary is printed in solve times (Tables 2 and 4, pp. 20–22) and in an exact benchmark limited to small horizons (p. 15).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: there is one interpretable knob, the degree d, which "directly controls the trade-off" (p. 9). The curve is printed as gaps and times by degree (Tables 1–4, pp. 19–22). But the knob prices computation, not a property the decision-maker wants. The only bound is the monotone ordering J*_AFF ≥ J*_{d=1} ≥ … (Theorem 1, p. 14).
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (certificate and incumbent steps): every gap is certified against an exact optimum (pp. 14–15, 18). The incumbent class (affine) is dueled on the same instances, with solver and machine stated (p. 19). The instances were generated so that affine policies are suboptimal (pp. 17–18), which conditions the comparison.
- New-pattern candidate: *power-and-limits theorem for a tractable policy class*. A small example shows the incumbent class arbitrarily bad (Lemma 1, pp. 6, 22), and a degree is found at which the class is exact (Theorem 2, p. 16). The pattern recurs in S060 and S165.
- New-pattern candidate: *build the tractable policy from the fully adaptive optimum*: multi-affine interpolation of the exact vertex decisions (pp. 22–23).
- New-pattern candidate: *exact exponential formulation as a small-scale benchmark* (pp. 14–15, 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Write constraints that must hold for all w in a semialgebraic set, on polynomial recourse, as SOS certificates with capped multiplier degree. The result is one SDP, and the policy degree becomes the single quality-versus-size knob (pp. 7–10).
- (D4) To claim that a new hierarchy dominates an established method, show that every feasible point of the method is feasible at the hierarchy's lowest level (proof of Theorem 1, pp. 23–24).
- (D5) Benchmark approximate policies at small horizons against an LP over all extreme disturbance sequences. Report the mean, standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum of gaps and solve times over 100 instances per size (pp. 14–15, 18–22).

**Quotes**:
- "it avoids ad-hoc modeling decisions and has the advantage of keeping a single parameter that is adjustable to the user (the degree d), which directly controls the trade-off between the size of the resulting SDP formulation and the quality of the overall solution." (p. 9)
- "While computations for cubic policies are rather expensive, the quadratic case, shown in Figure 2(b), shows promise for scalability" (p. 19)

### S120 · Optimal Selection of Airport Runway Configurations (Operations Research 2011, DOI 10.1287/opre.1110.0956)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13 of the journal print (Oper. Res. 59(6) pp. 1407–1419). The font layer garbles table digits; the numbers below are decoded (see the batch header) and checked by arithmetic.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Frankovich and Amedeo Odoni (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: air-transportation work with Odoni. The paper was received in November 2009 (p. 1) and partly funded by MIT Lincoln Laboratory, with which a field-implementation process had started (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO; AP}: Two linked choices set an airport's delay costs: the sequence of runway configurations (RCM) and the balance of arrivals and departures (ADRB) (pp. 1–2).
  - The entry is a practice gap. The FAA solves these problems "largely on the basis of experience". The hard cases are variable or poor weather, where changeovers cost capacity and backlogs build (p. 2).
  - Changeover times make a greedy choice of configuration suboptimal (p. 3).
  - The paper presents itself, "as far as we are aware", as the first optimization approach to the combined RCM and ADRB problems (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: One MIP, P1 (p. 4). A binary z_kt selects the configuration. The arrivals and departures served must lie inside that configuration's capacity envelope (RCCE), a concave piecewise-linear envelope that can be fitted to observed operations (pp. 1–3). Queues carry over through the backlog equations (1)–(2).
  - The changeover device: discretize time so that one interval equals the changeover time C (about 10 minutes), and forbid service in the interval in which the configuration changes (constraint (5), pp. 3–4).
  - Extensions: several changeover times through a finer grid and constraints (31)–(32) (p. 9); a metroplex with coupling constraints (40)–(41) (p. 11); environmental rules (47)–(51) (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Model and data**: Data are treated as known with certainty. This is justified by a horizon of a few hours and by re-solving on a rolling horizon, said to be every 30 minutes (pp. 3, 13) and every 15 minutes (p. 6).
  - Envelopes: RCCE breakpoints are integral, and the IMC envelope lies inside the VMC one (p. 3).
  - Queues clear without cancellations (pp. 3–4).
  - Test problems 2–4 have 4, 8 and 12 configurations, 11, 22 and 33 RCCE, and 30 ten-minute intervals (pp. 7–8). Problems 5–7 are described only by their availability tables (pp. 8–9).
  - The instances are called realistic (pp. 3, 7, 13), but the text does not say where their data come from.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: There are no theorems. Integrality of the served flows is dropped because it holds automatically once z is fixed (p. 4). The valid inequalities add each switching constraint in the reverse direction (11) and, per period, the convex hull of the union of the available RCCE (12), "the tightest possible bound" (p. 4). The paper calls the formulation strong (p. 3) and the IP–LP difference small (p. 8), yet Table 2 prints differences of 7.6%, 28.9% and 16.2% (p. 9, decoded). The growth of the model with several changeover times is counted (Table 6, p. 10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: The setup is AMPL with CPLEX 11.2.10, one thread, on a Core 2 Duo E7400 with 2 GB of RAM (p. 5); problems 2–4 solve in under 3 s (p. 8; Table 2, p. 9). The baseline is a "smart" greedy heuristic that solves its own MIPs (P2(t), P3) to mimic "a highly skilled controller" (pp. 6–7). P1 beats it by 2.0–51.1% on problems 2–7, and the 2% case is printed (Table 4, p. 9). With two and three changeover times, solves take up to 24.2 s and 122.1 s (Tables 7–8, p. 10). The metroplex (5 airports, 30 configurations, 79 RCCE) solves in 1.3–79.6 s without a system-wide envelope. With one, nothing is optimal within five minutes, and gaps of 0.6–2.7% are printed (Tables 9–10, p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Gilbo's integer programs for ADRB and on the RCCE literature (Gilbo, Hall, de Neufville–Odoni) (pp. 1–3). The output is tied to the airport acceptance rate used in the FAA's collaborative decision making (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract leads with solve times and an expected improvement "of at least 10%" (p. 1). A small worked example (Figures 3–6) shows how early decisions propagate (pp. 4–5). Claims about the baseline are hedged with "confident" and "expect" (pp. 6, 9).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions: a strong MIP solved to optimality quickly enough to be implemented, a baseline-based lower bound on the benefits, and extensions (p. 13). The limits: the metroplex model is less effective (p. 12), and the data are treated as deterministic (p. 3). The next step is an offline comparison with the FAA's actual sequences and observed delays, run with MIT Lincoln Laboratory, followed by rolling-horizon use (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · evidence ✅: optimal solutions are certified, and the LP values are printed (Table 2, p. 9). Where the metroplex model is not solved within five minutes, bounds and gaps are printed (Table 10, p. 12). The incumbent, a greedy proxy for a controller, is dueled on the same instances, and the weak 2% case is reported (Table 4, p. 9).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the owner (the FAA) and its current rule (experience) are named (p. 2). The baseline, though, is a constructed proxy for a skilled controller, said to make the gains a lower bound on practical benefit (p. 6). The field test against actual FAA sequences is planned, not done (p. 13).
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (IO as engine), with a caveat: a decision made by experience is written as an MIP that CPLEX solves in seconds (pp. 4, 8–9), and the boundary is printed (the metroplex, p. 12). But the "strong formulation" claim sits beside IP–LP differences of up to 28.9% (pp. 3, 8–9). This bears on Tension T4 (formulation strength).
- Taste mark 3 · evidence ✅ (not a Method): exact joint optimization replaces a greedy practice, which is suboptimal because of changeovers (pp. 3, 6).
- New-pattern candidate: *practitioner-mimicking optimization baseline as a lower bound on benefit* (pp. 6–7, 9, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To model a changeover that costs capacity, set the time step equal to the changeover time and forbid service in the switching interval. Pairwise constraints rule out configuration k at t − 1 together with any k′ ≠ k at t. Refine the grid when changeovers differ in length (pp. 3–4, 9).
- (D4) To strengthen a "pick one envelope" disjunction, add per period the convex hull of the union of the envelopes as an aggregate valid inequality, and add each switching constraint in the reverse direction (p. 4).
- (D5) When no data on current practice exist, build the baseline as an optimization-based imitation of a skilled practitioner: commit to the best configuration available for at least an hour, and re-plan when it lapses. Present the gains as a lower bound (pp. 6–7, 9).

**Quotes**:
- "In practice, these problems are solved today largely on the basis of experience." (p. 2)
- "Overall, we expect to see an average improvement of the order of at least 10% in practice, given the results presented here, and the advanced nature of our baseline heuristic, which involves solving a relevant optimization problem." (p. 9)

### S165 · A geometric characterization of the power of finite adaptability in multistage stochastic and adaptive optimization (Mathematics of Operations Research 2011, DOI 10.1287/moor.1110.0482)
Read level: **partial**: the 48-page author manuscript.
- Read:
  - abstract and §1–§3 (pp. 1–20);
  - §4, with the proofs of Theorems 4.1–4.2, the scaling bound, Table 1 and the examples of sets (pp. 20–26);
  - Theorem 5.1, its algorithm and the start of its proof (pp. 26–29);
  - the statements of §6, and §6.3 (pp. 31–35);
  - the statements of §7–§8 (pp. 35–37);
  - §9, the conclusions (p. 38).
- Not read: the geometric proof of Lemma 2.1 (pp. 13–14), the rest of the proof of Theorem 5.1 (pp. 29–31), the proof of Theorem 6.2 (pp. 32–33), and Appendices A–B (pp. 41–48).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Vineet Goyal (Columbia IEOR) and Xu Andy Sun (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: generalizes Bertsimas–Goyal (MOR 2010) from perfectly symmetric sets to general convex sets, and from two stages to many (pp. 4, 17). It builds on finite adaptability (Bertsimas–Caramanis) (p. 3). Funded by NSF EFRI-0735905 (p. 38).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + SD; IO in §8}: The paper starts from two stated drawbacks of stochastic optimization (p. 2):
  - Only historical data exist, and a distribution is a modelling choice.
  - Multistage problems are intractable even approximately (Shapiro–Nemirovski; Dyer–Stougie's PSPACE-hardness).

  Static robust solutions are tractable but may be conservative, and fully adaptable robust policies are intractable (p. 3). The question: how good are static solutions (two stages) and finitely adaptable ones (many stages) against the fully adaptable stochastic and adaptive optima, and what decides it (pp. 3–4)?
- **D2 Core idea**: The answer is the geometry of the uncertainty set: Minkowski's symmetry s (p. 12) and a translation factor ρ that measures how far the set sits from the origin (p. 16).
  - Key fact: (1 + ρ/s)·u₀ dominates every point of the set coordinatewise (Lemma 2.4, pp. 16–17).
  - Hence, scaling the expectation of the fully adaptable solution by τ = 1 + ρ/s gives a feasible static solution (Theorem 4.1, pp. 20–21).
  - A finitely adaptable policy fixes one solution per path of a directed network of uncertainty sets. It is computed by one LP, (5.2) (pp. 12, 28).
- **D3 Model and data**: Only right-hand sides are uncertain, the constraints are of covering type Ax + By ≥ b, and the uncertainty sets lie in the nonnegative orthant (pp. 5–6, 9). Further assumptions:
  - z_Stoch is finite and the expectations exist (p. 5).
  - The measure condition E_μ[b] ≥ b₀ is called mild (pp. 20–21).
  - Multistage uncertainty is a DAG of sets with known transition probabilities and independent measures, which generalizes scenario trees (pp. 6–8).
  - Admitted cost of the model: capacity constraints on order quantities cannot be modelled, since constraints must be "≥" with nonnegative right-hand sides (p. 11).
  - In the stochastic problem, integer variables are allowed in the first stage only (p. 36).

  There are no data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Two stages: Theorem 4.1 gives z_Rob ≤ (1 + ρ/s)·z_Stoch, independent of A, B, c and d (pp. 20–21). Translated L_p balls make it tight for every (s, ρ) (Theorem 4.2, p. 22). Without its assumptions it fails: gaps become unbounded outside the orthant or with uncertain A, B (p. 21). Since s ≥ 1/m, the factor is at most m + 1 (pp. 15, 18). Table 1 covers 14 sets; the budgeted set Δ_k has gap 1 + m/k (pp. 24, 26).
  - Multistage: the factor is (1 + ρ/s) with RHS uncertainty (Theorems 5.1 and 6.1, pp. 27, 31), and (1 + ρ/s)² for the adaptive problem when costs are uncertain too (Theorem 6.2, p. 31). ### S227 · Performance analysis of queueing networks via robust optimization (Operations Research 2011, DOI 10.1287/opre.1100.0879; arXiv 1009.3948)
Read level: **partial**: arXiv:1009.3948v1, 35 pages.
- Read: abstract and §1–§3 (pp. 1–17), §4.1 with the proof of Theorem 1 (pp. 17–19), the start of §5.1 (pp. 21–22), and §6, the conclusion (pp. 26–27).
- Not read: the proof of Theorem 3 (pp. 19–21), the rest of §5 (pp. 22–26), and the Appendix (pp. 30–35).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: David Gamarnik (MIT Operations Research Center and Sloan), Alexander Anatoliy Rikun (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: returns to performance analysis of queueing networks, the subject of his 1990s papers [BPT94], [BGT96], [BGT01] and [BNM99] (cited p. 2). This time he uses the RO toolkit and the Bertsimas–Sim budget terminology (p. 11). Funded by NSF DMI-0556106 and CMMI-0726733 (p. 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + RO}: Non-asymptotic performance bounds for queueing networks are scarce (pp. 2–3).
  - Outside product-form networks, the known bounds need Markovian assumptions and weaken as traffic intensity nears one. His own bound with Gamarnik and Tsitsiklis is cited as O((1 − ρ*)^−2) ([BGT01], p. 2).
  - Most other results are asymptotic.

  The entry: replace the probability laws by "the implications of such probability laws", namely linear constraints taken from the Law of the Iterated Logarithm (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: The interarrival and service sequences are deterministic but must satisfy |Σ U_i − k/λ| ≤ Γ√(k ln ln k) (constraints (9)–(12), pp. 11–12). Γ is called one of the "budgets of uncertainty", after [BS04] (p. 11). The steps:
  - Bound the worst case explicitly.
  - The LIL makes these constraints hold almost surely with a random Γ (Proposition 1, p. 13), so every bound carries over to the stochastic network.
  - Taking expectations gives Corollaries 1–2 (pp. 13–15).

  Cruz's burstiness constraints A(t) ≤ λt + B are violated almost surely by every renewal process with positive variance. LIL-type constraints are consistent with stochastic models (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**: Two networks, with i.i.d. renewal primitives and infinite buffers (pp. 6–9):
  - TSC: a tandem single-class network under FIFO.
  - MCSS: a multiclass single server with routing matrix P (P^n = 0) under any work-conserving policy.

  In the robust model, TSC processes n jobs from an empty start, and MCSS starts with one job per class (pp. 11–12). A technical condition λΓ ≥ e^{2e} is imposed (p. 12). Convergence to steady state (1) and the ergodic properties (3)–(6) are assumed, not derived (pp. 7, 10). There are no data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: A Lindley-type recursion bounds the sojourn time by a maximum over chains of indices (Theorem 3, p. 18). Maximizing the concave function x(μ_min^−1 − λ^−1) + 2JΓφ(x) explicitly gives W_n ≤ (7J²Γ²λ/(1 − ρ*)) ln ln(JλΓ/(1 − ρ*)) + Jλ^−1 (Theorem 1, pp. 13, 18–19). For MCSS, the workload is at most −at + b√(t ln ln t) + c, which bounds both the busy period and the workload (Theorem 2, pp. 15–17). The stochastic corollary goes through E[W∞] ≤ E[B∞²]/E[B∞] and is conservative, of order (1 − ρ)^−4 (ln ln)² (eq. (7), p. 10; p. 17). Admitted weaknesses: crude constants (p. 4); a quadratic dependence on J where linear is known, and a lost ln ln factor (p. 14); an MCSS scaling that "deviates significantly" from the correct one (pp. 15–16).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none (no numerical section).
- **D6 Positioning**: Related to Cruz's network calculus and to adversarial queueing theory, which are credited and then distinguished on probabilistic consistency (p. 4). His own Markovian-bound papers are listed among the results that weaken in heavy traffic (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract states the premise in one sentence (p. 1). The derivation is called involved and the concept "very simple" (p. 4). An informal proof outline comes before the formal proofs (pp. 16–17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contribution is explicit, non-asymptotic bounds for non-Markovian networks, derived from constraints implied by probability laws (pp. 26–27). The limits are those in D4, and the correct heavy-traffic scaling is left open (p. 16). Future work (pp. 26–27):
  - general multiclass networks via Lyapunov functions;
  - laws tighter than the LIL;
  - tail bounds from large-deviations constraints;
  - links to adversarial queueing;
  - the same "philosophy" outside queueing.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: a central problem owned by applied probability is re-posed as a worst case over a budgeted set (pp. 11–13), and the boundary is printed: the heavy-traffic scaling is wrong (pp. 14–16). But the output is a closed-form bound, not a formulation solved within a solver class. The paper stresses that its results are "explicit, as opposed to numeric" (p. 4).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: Γ is borrowed from the price-of-robustness line (p. 11). Here, though, it is a random constant set by the LIL (Proposition 1, p. 13), not a knob for a decision-maker.
- New-pattern candidate: *probability laws as uncertainty sets*: replace distributional assumptions by constraints that a limit theorem implies, bound the deterministic worst case, and carry the bound back almost surely (pp. 1, 4, 13, 27). This 2010 paper is an early instance of the Domain Map's SD row ("robust queueing").

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To bound a stochastic system without distributional assumptions, pick a limit law that the primitives satisfy almost surely (here the LIL). Write what it implies as linear constraints with a random constant, bound the deterministic worst case, then take expectations (pp. 11–14).
- (D6) Before adopting a class of deterministic constraints for random inputs, check that the stochastic model satisfies it almost surely. A(t) ≤ λt + B fails for every renewal process with positive variance (p. 4).
- (D8) Print each derived scaling next to the known correct heavy-traffic order, and name the gap as an open problem (pp. 14–16).

**Quotes**:
- "rather than assuming that the stochastic primitives of a queueing model satisfy certain probability laws, such as, for example, i.i.d. interarrival and service times distributions, we assume that the underlying primitives are deterministic and satisfy the implications of such probability laws." (p. 1)
- "These explicit bounds however, come at a price of not caring much for the constants corresponding to the leading coefficient." (p. 4)

### S060 · On the power and limitations of affine policies in two-stage adaptive optimization (Mathematical Programming 2012, DOI 10.1007/s10107-011-0444-4)
Read level: **partial**: the 36-page author manuscript "Submitted to Math Programming", dated October 20, 2009.
- Read:
  - abstract and §1 (pp. 1–4);
  - §2, Lemma 1 and the proof of Theorem 1 (pp. 4–8);
  - §3: the instance, Theorem 2 and Lemmas 2–4 (pp. 8–12);
  - §4: the instance, Theorem 3 and Lemma 5 (pp. 14–16);
  - §5: the construction for Theorem 4, Algorithm A and Lemmas 10–11 (pp. 23–26);
  - §5.1: Theorem 5 and Lemma 12 (pp. 29–30);
  - §6: Theorem 6, Lemma 13 and the final step (pp. 31–32, 35);
  - §7, the conclusions (p. 35).
- Not read: the remaining proof steps on pp. 12–14, 16–23, 26–29, 30–31 and 32–35.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Vineet Goyal (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: follows Bertsimas–Goyal on static robust solutions, which are a 2-approximation for symmetric sets but lose Ω(m) on other sets (p. 3). It sits beside Bertsimas–Iancu–Parrilo on the optimality of affine policies (p. 3). Funded by NSF DMI-0556106 and EFRI-0735905 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + IO (approximation and hardness)}: Affine decision rules are tractable and "perform extreme well in practice as observed empirically", yet no theoretical performance bounds exist for them in general (p. 2).
  - The problem: a two-stage adaptive LP with right-hand-side uncertainty and a min–max objective (eq. (1), p. 2).
  - Hardness: it cannot be approximated within O(log m) unless NP ⊆ TIME(2^{O(√n)}), by reduction from max–min fractional set cover (Feige et al.) (p. 2).
  - Static robust solutions lose a factor Ω(m) on non-symmetric sets, which arise, for instance, when modelling at most k resource failures (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: A full map of where the affine class works (pp. 3–4):
  - It is exactly optimal when the set is a simplex: interpolate the optimal adaptive decisions at the m + 1 vertices (Theorem 1, pp. 6–8).
  - With only m + 3 extreme points, it can be worse by a factor 2 − δ (Theorem 2, p. 9).
  - On sets whose exponentially many extreme points lie near the unit sphere, it can be worse by Ω(m^{1/2−δ}) (Theorem 3, pp. 14–15).
  - When A ≥ 0, it is within 3√m (Theorem 4, p. 23), and within O(√k) when only k parameters are uncertain (Theorem 5, p. 29).
  - For general A, a non-affine O(√m)-approximation solves the problem on a dominating simplex, where the affine class is exact (Theorem 6, p. 31).
- **D3 Model and data**: The uncertainty set is convex and lies in R^m_+, with c, d ≥ 0 and x, y ≥ 0 (p. 2). Theorem 4 needs the set compact and full-dimensional (p. 23), and the affine upper bound needs A ≥ 0 (pp. 4, 23). There are no data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: The worst case sits at an extreme point (Lemma 1, p. 5). Lower bounds use symmetrization: averaging an optimal affine solution over the permutations that leave the instance unchanged gives an optimal one with equal constant terms (Lemmas 3–4, pp. 10–12). The upper bound builds a linear policy from the adaptive optimum at the coordinate maximizers β^j, y(b) = Σ(b_j/μ_j)·y*(β^j), with the first stage scaled by √m (eqs. (42)–(44), pp. 23–24). Algorithm A partitions the coordinates in at most 2√m iterations (Lemmas 10–11, pp. 25–26). The general case reduces to the dominating simplex conv{2√m β^j, 2β} (Lemma 13, p. 31), which gives 4√m (p. 35).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none.
- **D6 Positioning**: Approximation algorithms for demand-robust covering (Dhamdhere et al., Feige et al., Khandekar et al.) are described as specific to their problems (pp. 2–3). Affine policies are traced from Rockafellar–Wets through Ben-Tal et al. and the control literature (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract promises "a sharp characterization of the power and limitations" of affine policies (p. 1). The results are labelled by type: Optimality, Suboptimality, Lower Bound, Upper Bound (pp. 3–4). The lower bound is set against practice: "in contrast to strong empirical evidence" (p. 4). The general-case guarantee is announced as O(√k) in the abstract and the contributions (pp. 1, 4), but §6 and the conclusion prove and state O(√m) (pp. 31, 35).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contribution is the first bound on the performance of affine policies "in such generality" (p. 35). The worst case for affine policies is essentially a hypersphere intersected with the orthant (p. 35). Open problems: the gap between O(√m) and the O(log m) hardness, and the study of polynomial and piecewise-affine policies (p. 35).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (step 5, print where tractability ends): the boundary of the affine class is mapped from exact (simplices), through 2 − δ and Ω(m^{1/2−δ}), to the O(log m) hardness of the problem (pp. 2–4, 9, 15).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: a price of affinity with a bound independent of the solution (3√m; O(√k)) and a printed bad case (pp. 15, 23, 29, 35). There is no decision-maker's knob and no computed curve.
- Heuristic 1 · variant ⚠ (not a Method): affine rules work well in practice (p. 2). The paper does not find the robust problem they solve exactly. It finds the sets on which they are exact (simplices) and bounds their loss elsewhere (pp. 4–8, 23).
- New-pattern candidate: *power-and-limits theorem for a tractable policy class* (pp. 1–4, 35). The pattern recurs in S165 and S075.
- New-pattern candidate: *build the tractable policy from the fully adaptive optimum*: affine interpolation at the vertices (pp. 6–8), or a linear combination of the solutions at the coordinate maximizers with √m scaling (pp. 23–24). The pattern recurs in S165 (scaling) and S075 (multi-affine interpolation).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To bound a restricted policy class, build one of its members from the unrestricted optimum. Interpolate at the vertices of a simplex, or combine the optimal recourse at the coordinate maximizers with weights b_j/μ_j and scale the first stage. The cost of the construction is the bound (pp. 6–8, 23–24).
- (D4) In lower-bound instances with permutation symmetry, average an optimal policy over the symmetry group. The result is an optimal policy with few free parameters, over which the analysis then optimizes (pp. 10–12).
- (D4) For general sets, replace the set by a dominating simplex on which the restricted class is exact, and pay the domination factor (pp. 31–32, 35).

**Quotes**:
- "This lower bound is in contrast to strong empirical evidence of the performance of affine policies." (p. 4)
- "Therefore, the intersection of a hypersphere with the non-negative orthant is essentially the worst-case example for affine policies." (p. 35)
