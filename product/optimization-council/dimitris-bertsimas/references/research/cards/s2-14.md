### S183 · Solving large-scale sparse PCA to certifiable (near) optimality (Journal of Machine Learning Research 2022, arXiv 2005.05195)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v4: pp. 1–30 (abstract; §1 with contributions; §2 with Theorems 1, 3, 5, 7, 11, Propositions 8, 12, their proofs and Algorithm 1; §3 with Algorithm 2 and Theorem 15; §4 experiments and guidelines; §5 extensions). References and Appendix A (pp. 30–35) not read. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Cory-Wright and Pauphilet match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ryan Cory-Wright (MIT), Jean Pauphilet (London Business School) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the sparse-ML programme carried by his ML lineage (Pauphilet, Cory-Wright). It extends the authors' own framework for MIO with logical constraints (p. 5) and the Bertsimas–Cory-Wright SOC relaxation (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+IO+CP}: Sparse PCA (maximize x'Σx with ||x||₂ = 1 and at most k non-zeros) yields interpretable principal components (pp. 1–2). Each of the three method families stops short (pp. 3–5):
  - heuristics carry no guarantee and can miss the optimum without the user knowing; Berk and Bertsimas found no heuristic right on all 14 test instances (p. 3);
  - SDP relaxations need O(p⁶) interior-point memory, cannot be solved for p ≥ 300, and come without rounding schemes (pp. 3–4);
  - exact branch-and-bound stops at p = 40 (Moghaddam et al.) or p = 250 (Berk–Bertsimas) (p. 5).

  Entry: no method certifies optimality beyond p in the 100s (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Exact MISDO: add support indicators z and drop the rank-one constraint without loss. Tight constants M_ii = 1, M_ij = 1/2 and row sums Σ_j |X_ij| ≤ √k z_i keep the relaxation non-trivial (Theorem 1, Remark 2, pp. 7–8).
  - Saddle-point form: the inner problem's dual makes f(z) concave, so outer approximation applies. It runs inside one branch-and-bound tree with lazy-constraint callbacks (Theorem 3, Algorithm 1, pp. 9–10).
  - SDP-free cuts: optimal duals come in closed form from the leading eigenvalue of Σ on the support and one linear system (Theorem 5, pp. 11–12).
  - A stronger master: Gershgorin bounds become big-M inequalities and a node-pruning test (Theorem 7 and (13), pp. 12–13). Brauer's ovals give a stronger MISOCP bound, not used because of its cost (pp. 15–16).
  - At scale: relax z to [0, 1], strengthen with Σ_j X_ij² ≤ X_ii z_i and ||X||₁ ≤ k, round z greedily and re-optimize X (Algorithm 2, Theorem 15, pp. 17–18). The PSD cone is replaced by SOC constraints on 2×2 minors plus a few cuts (Remark 13, p. 17; p. 23).
- **D3 Model and data**: Correlation matrices of normalized data (p. 19). The sample covariance is used for simplicity; a shrinkage estimator is recommended in practice (footnote 1, p. 2). Data:
  - UCI pitprops, wine, miniboone, communities, arrhythmia and micromass, p = 13 to 1300 (p. 19);
  - Wilshire 5000, Gisette and Arcene, up to p = 8000 (p. 24);
  - a spiked-covariance design after d'Aspremont et al.: p = 150, 20 instances, signal-to-noise ratio 2 (pp. 24–26).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 1 by decomposing a feasible X and keeping its best rank-one component (p. 8).
  - Theorem 5 by Slater's condition, complementary slackness and the generalized Schur complement. β is set to the ⌈√k⌉-th largest |α| (max-k optimization), which gives stronger cuts (pp. 11–12).
  - Finite convergence: OA never revisits a point (p. 10).
  - Bound quality: the Gershgorin bound is within a factor 1 + ρ for scaled diagonally dominant Σ, tight for diagonal Σ and within 2 for diagonally dominant Σ (Proposition 8, Remark 10, pp. 13–14). The ovals bound is within 2 for doubly diagonally dominant Σ (Proposition 12, p. 16).
  - The relaxation (20) is exact when its optimum has rank one (Theorem 15, p. 18).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Set-up: Julia 1.3, Gurobi 9.1 and JuMP 0.21.6 on a MacBook Pro (i9, 16 GB). Every method is warm-started with the truncated power method; limit 600 s; tolerance 10⁻³; gaps of unsolved runs in brackets (pp. 19–20). Results:
  - With the circle bound, Algorithm 1 certifies micromass (p = 1300) at k = 5 and 10 in 164 s and 242 s. No method closes p > 300 with k > 10 in 600 s (Table 1, p. 20).
  - Relax+round with (20) finds the known optimum on every instance, with relaxation gaps under 2% (p. 21; Table 3, p. 22).
  - With SOC minors: 8.87% on arrhythmia (k = 20) in 22 s, against 18.45% for Berk–Bertsimas in 600 s (p. 23). On 600 GB of RAM it reaches p = 8000, with gaps from 0.01% to 54.4% (Table 5, pp. 24–25).
  - Synthetic support recovery (AUC): sorting 0.7028, truncated power 0.7482, exact at 600 s 0.7483, greedy 0.7561, relax+round 0.7593. Only exact and relax+round print gaps: 30% or more against 1% or less (p. 26).
  - Code is on GitHub (p. 19).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Two contributions: the exact MISDO reformulation, and algorithms that certify optimality, or come within 1–2% in practice, at larger scale than before (p. 6). Printed limits:
  - the exact method fails beyond p = 300 with k > 10 (p. 20);
  - relax+round is near-optimal in practice, not in theory (p. 26);
  - the Brauer inequalities are too costly to use (p. 16), and Goemans–Williamson rounding was no better than greedy (pp. 17–18);
  - non-negative sparse PCA cannot be reformulated the same way, because the completely positive cone is NP-hard to separate over (p. 28).

  Future: first-order SOC solvers with feature screening for p in the 10,000s (p. 24); non-negative, rectangular and multi-component sparse PCA (pp. 28–30).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 7–12, 20, 28): a problem held by heuristics and SDP relaxations is written exactly as a convex MISDO. The dual (Theorems 3 and 5) yields a cut that needs no SDP solver. The boundary is printed twice: no certificate beyond p = 300 with k > 10, and the non-negative variant falls outside the method.
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 19–27): steps 2–6 complete. Every method gets the same warm start; solver, version, machine, limit and tolerance are stated; every unsolved run has a gap; the incumbent B&B and three heuristics are dueled; guidelines say which method to use in which regime (pp. 26–28). Variant ⚠ on the stop rule: when the exact bound stalls, the paper moves to relaxation plus rounding, which still prints a bound gap, rather than to unlabelled local search (pp. 22–26). The certificate survives at scale (cf. Heuristic 3, Tension T1).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 4–5, 19): the ML cohort extends its own framework and relaxation. It also finds and corrects a bug in the group's earlier Berk–Bertsimas code before dueling it (footnote 4, p. 19).
- New-pattern candidate: *bound-gap certificate at scale*. Where exact certification is out of reach, relax, round, and print the gap between the rounded solution and the relaxation bound (pp. 22–26).
- New-pattern candidate: *repair the comparator before the duel*. A bug in the rival (own earlier) implementation is fixed and disclosed (p. 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When dropping a rank-one constraint from a lifted model, argue exactness from the convexity of the objective (a rank-one extreme optimum exists). Then check that the relaxation is not trivial: 2×2-minor constants (M_ij = 1/2) and row-sum inequalities stop it from collapsing to λmax (Theorem 1, Remark 2, pp. 7–8).
- Solve each outer-approximation subproblem in closed form via complementary slackness, and run the whole OA in one branch-and-bound tree with lazy-constraint callbacks (pp. 10–12).
- Use a cheap eigenvalue-inclusion bound (Gershgorin) both as master inequalities and as a node-pruning test, and report runs with and without it (pp. 13–14, Table 1).
- Warm-start every compared method with the same heuristic for fairness, and close the experiments with regime-by-regime guidelines (pp. 19, 26–28).

**Quotes**:
- "Unfortunately, while these methods are often very effective in practice, they sometimes badly fail to recover an optimal sparse principal component, and a practitioner using a heuristic method typically has no way of knowing when this has occurred." (p. 3)
- "Once both k and p are sufficiently large (e.g. p > 300 and k > 10), no approach is able to solve the problem to provable optimality within 600s." (p. 20)

### S235 · Using artificial intelligence to find the optimal margin width in hepatectomy for colorectal cancer liver metastases (JAMA Surgery 2022, DOI 10.1001/jamasurg.2022.1819)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–9 (abstract and Key Points, Methods, Results, Tables 1–3, Figures 1–2 as extracted, Discussion, Limitations, Conclusions, article information). The eMethods and eResults supplement is not in the file and was not read. First-page check: the title matches; Bertsimas is first of 35 authors, Margonis corresponding (p. 1); the two contributed equally (p. 8).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: G. A. Margonis (MSKCC, corresponding), Y. Ma and S. Tang (MIT ORC), S. Sujichantararat (MIT EECS), and 30 coauthors from hospitals and institutes in the US, Germany, France, Norway, Austria, Greece, Japan and Argentina, including T. M. Pawlik and M. D'Angelica (pp. 1, 8) · **Position in trajectory**: the Margonis colorectal-liver line of his health programme, with Optimal Policy Trees (ref. 10, Amram–Dunn–Zhuo) as the prescriptive engine (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA+ML+AP}: Margin width and surgical technique are the only variables under the surgeon's control, and there is no consensus on the optimal width (pp. 1–2). Prior studies recommend one width for all patients and so assume the margin effect does not depend on patient characteristics (p. 2). Causal forests and similar methods handle only binary treatments and are hard to interpret; OPT allows several treatment levels in an interpretable tree (p. 2). Question: which margin width minimizes 5-year death probability, and should it be individualized (pp. 1–2)?
- **D2 Core idea**: two steps (pp. 2–3):
  - a random-forest counterfactual model estimates each patient's 5-year death probability under every margin width from 0 to 20 mm;
  - an OPT trained on these counterfactual probabilities groups patients and prescribes a width for each group in an interpretable tree.

  Validation has two parts: SHAP plots of the RF model as a second method (p. 3), and a new RF trained on an external cohort that re-estimates the benefit of the frozen OPT margins (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**: Cohorts:
  - internal: 965 patients from Johns Hopkins, MSKCC and Charité (2000–2017); the 386 with KRAS-variant tumours were analysed with an 80/20 split (pp. 1–3);
  - external: 878 patients (375 KRAS-variant) from France, Norway, the US, Austria, Argentina and Japan (pp. 1, 5).

  Only pre- or intraoperative variables; the 5-year endpoint was fixed a priori (p. 2). Wild-type tumours were dropped after a test AUC of 0.57 (p. 3). Patients without follow-up to the time point were censored and excluded (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a. The article prints no formulation, solver or tree settings beyond 5-fold cross-validation for rewards and for tree fitting (p. 3); OPT is cited, not described (p. 2).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - RF AUC is 0.76 in training and test (KRAS-variant), and 0.78 for the external RF (pp. 3, 5).
  - OPT widths are 6, 7, 12 and 7 mm for subgroups A–D. SHAP agrees except for subgroup C (7–8 mm against 12 mm) (p. 3).
  - Predicted relative reduction of 5-year death risk against R1 (0 mm) margins: 10.57–13.06% in training and 5.76–7.06% in the external cohort (Table 2, p. 6).
  - Most of the benefit comes from a submillimetre or 1-mm margin (pp. 6–7).
  - No confidence intervals for the AUC (p. 8).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A possible resolution of the margin debate: the OPT found no heterogeneous effect and suggests a single 7 mm margin for KRAS-variant tumours (p. 8). Printed limits:
  - causal language avoided, because the causal structure is uncertain and confounders may be unmeasured (p. 7);
  - censored patients excluded, few predictive variables, no AUC confidence intervals (p. 8);
  - the survival benefit is a statistical projection that only a prospective study can confirm (p. 8);
  - the liver-recurrence analysis lacks an external cohort (p. 3).

  The authors advise against changing operative indications (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 8): the decision (margin width) is the object, learned inside a prescriptive tree because surgeons must see why (step 4), and the paper says what its evaluation cannot show (step 6). Variant ⚠ on step 2: the benefit is scored against R1 (0 mm) margins, not against current practice, whose median margin is 3.0 mm (Table 1, p. 4). Table 2 lets a reader compare 7 mm with 3 mm (node 3: 63.66% against 66.68%, p. 6), but the headline reduction is taken against 0 mm.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2–3): the OPT is presented as a globally optimal decision tree (p. 2), with no solver, limit, gap or certificate. Its opponent is a second explainer (SHAP) on the same RF, not an incumbent policy (Tension T1).
- New-pattern candidate: *external re-estimation of a prescription's benefit*: train a fresh outcome model on an external cohort and score the frozen prescriptions with it (pp. 3, 5).
- New-pattern candidate: *the null-heterogeneity result as headline*: a method built to personalize reports that one width fits all KRAS-variant patients (p. 8; cf. Heuristic 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a dose-like treatment (a continuous margin width), estimate counterfactual outcomes on a grid of treatment levels with one outcome model, then fit a prescriptive tree to that counterfactual table (pp. 2–3).
- Print the outcome at several non-prescribed levels (0, 0.5, 1, 3, 5, 7 and 10 mm) next to the prescribed one, so readers see the flat region and the marginal value of a wider margin (Table 2, p. 6).
- Validate a prescription by retraining the outcome model on an external cohort and re-scoring the frozen prescriptions (pp. 3, 5).

**Quotes**:
- "Although the OPT framework can infer causality, we have elected not to use causal language throughout the article, for 2 reasons." (p. 7)
- "It should be kept in mind that the presented survival benefits from applying optimal margins is a statistical projection and only prospective validation can determine whether these recommendations can translate into a real survival benefit." (p. 8)

### S243 · Course scheduling under sudden scarcity: Applications to pandemic planning (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management 2022, DOI 10.1287/msom.2021.0996)
Read level: **full**, author manuscript: pp. 1–29 (abstract, §§1–4, formulations (1)–(4x), Tables 1–4, Figure 1–4 captions) and the appendix of specialized constraints (p. 32); references (pp. 29–31) skimmed. First-page check: the title and the authors Barnhart, Bertsimas, Delarue and Yan match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Cynthia Barnhart (MIT Civil and Environmental Engineering), Arthur Delarue and Julia Yan (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: COVID-19 operations at MIT itself; Delarue's applied line moves from school buses (S137) to university timetabling.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+AP}: Physical distancing cut classroom capacity about fourfold (pp. 1, 26). Universities weighed more terms, hybrid teaching and rotation (p. 2). The literature: timetabling is well studied but split into curriculum-based and post-enrollment views, exact post-enrollment methods have been considered unscalable (hence metaheuristics), and term planning has drawn less attention (pp. 3–5). Problem: term planning and timetabling under sudden scarcity, as MIT and Sloan actually faced them (pp. 2, 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: One two-stage binary model: a first-stage course schedule x and second-stage student schedules z, linked by z ≤ x (eq. (1), p. 6).
  - Term planning (2a)–(2n): each course is offered as often as in a normal year, so faculty load is shifted, not increased. It adds prerequisites, density caps per term, and minimum and maximum terms on campus per student (pp. 8–10).
  - Timetabling (4a)–(4x): lessons with length and weekly multiplicity, sections, room blocks of one or two rooms, and a fictitious zero-capacity online block. Rotation enters through a lesson-dependent capacity; daily density caps and section balance complete the model (pp. 11–15).
  - Objectives are ranked and solved lexicographically, not weighted (p. 16).
- **D3 Model and data**: Last year's enrollments stand in for demand (p. 17).
  - Term planning: 4,287 undergraduates, 620 courses and 4,054 distinct course sets. Required courses are those taken by at least 10% of a major's students, plus unfulfilled prerequisites (p. 18).
  - Timetabling: 1,455 Sloan students, 114 courses, 115 faculty and 26 rooms with a mean distanced capacity of 19.46 (p. 18).

  The main admitted assumption: a student's demand does not depend on when a course is offered (pp. 7, 28). On-campus course-takings weigh 1 + w against w off campus, with w = 0.1, so 11 off-campus course-takings equal one on campus (p. 10); the paper's Greek letter is lost in extraction, w is my label.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: No theorems. Modelling devices:
  - start-period variables (after Da Fonseca et al.) remove contiguity constraints (p. 13);
  - the online block guarantees feasibility, so conflicts become objective loss (pp. 12, 16);
  - designated start periods (up to three times fewer variables), a time-then-room decomposition and super-students for identical course sets control size (pp. 16–17);
  - the appendix adds lesson spacing, time-zone windows, room changes and schedule gaps (p. 32).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi via JuMP; term planning on eight cores of a server, timetabling on the authors' laptops (p. 18). Results:
  - Term-planning scale test, up to 241,917 variables and 514,212 constraints: at the 720-minute limit the gap is 2.4%, with 10% reached at 120 minutes and 5% at 252 (Table 2, p. 21).
  - Policies: under two semesters students take 51–59% of their required courses on campus, under three 90–96%; the two largest runs stop at gaps of 2.4% and 5.1% (Table 3, p. 22).
  - MBA core: 60,582 variables, each step under a minute, the last step optimal in seven minutes (p. 24). Non-core courses: 1.5 million variables, 30-minute steps, gaps of at most 2% (p. 26).
  - Against the status-quo schedule, which would have put most courses online, the optimized schedule gives about half of all courses an in-person component (Table 4, pp. 26–27).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A unifying framework, a flexible model, and an approach that scales without metaheuristics (p. 5). Outcomes:
  - MIT kept its two-semester calendar despite the model's numbers (p. 22), and the conclusion says so again (p. 28);
  - Sloan raised its first-year cohorts from six to nine and implemented the schedule in Fall 2020 (p. 28).

  Limits: historical enrollments, rotation left to instructors, no fairness component, demand assumed independent of timing (pp. 27–28). Future: test that assumption with course-selection mechanisms (p. 28). Joint term planning and timetabling is out of scope (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 17–28): owner (MIT Registrar, Sloan leadership), decision, last year's data, and the status-quo schedule as baseline (Table 4). The model's run times are reported, and its metrics were shown at town halls and focus groups. The non-adoption of the three-semester calendar is reported as such (p. 22), so the stakeholder-contest step that the SKILL lists as a correction was done here (cf. Heuristic 4).
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 5, 12, 16–17): the problem stays a binary IO at university scale. Feasibility is designed into the model (the online block) and size is controlled by the formulation, not by a metaheuristic.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 4–5, 21): the *why now* is a citation of solver progress (Gonzalez et al. 2018) plus measured solve times on growing subsets of the real data, with no solver × hardware figure.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 23–25): the number of MBA cohorts acts as a knob. Figure 4a shows its effect on days on campus, time slots and the need for shared rooms. There is no bound, but the curve went to Sloan, which chose nine.
- New-pattern candidate: *infeasibility-proof fallback option*: a fictitious zero-capacity alternative that every activity can use, so hard constraints never make the model infeasible (pp. 12, 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give a hard assignment a fictitious zero-capacity fallback (here online teaching). Conflicts then show up as objective loss, and the solver always returns a schedule (pp. 12, 16).
- Rank objectives and solve them lexicographically, holding earlier objectives within a tolerance, instead of tuning weights (p. 16).
- Test tractability on nested subsets of the real data, built by adding the most closely related departments. Report variables, constraints, time and gap for each size, and the time to reach 10% and 5% gaps (pp. 19–21).
- Encode rotation through a lesson-dependent room capacity (section enrollment divided by weekly multiplicity) rather than new variables (pp. 13–14).

**Quotes**:
- "In our application, we are guaranteed to obtain a feasible schedule because (i) any course can be scheduled online, and (ii) scheduling conflicts for individual students manifest in objective deterioration rather than infeasibility." (p. 16)
- "As such, MIT decided to keep the two-semester calendar." (p. 22)

### S272 · POTTER-ICU: An artificial intelligence smartphone-accessible tool to predict the need for intensive care after emergency surgery (Surgery 2022, DOI 10.1016/j.surg.2022.03.023)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–6 (abstract, Introduction, Methods, Results, Tables I–II, Discussion, limitations, disclosures, references). First-page check: the title matches; Bertsimas is second to last, Kaafarani corresponding (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: A. Gebran (first), A. Vapsi, L. R. Maurer, M. El Moheb, L. Naar, S. S. Thakur, R. Sinyard, D. Daye, G. C. Velmahos and H. M. A.
