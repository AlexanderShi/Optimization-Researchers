### S072 · Optimization of multiclass queueing networks: Polyhedral and nonlinear characterizations of achievable performance (The Annals of Applied Probability 1994, DOI 10.1214/aoap/1177005200)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–33 (printed pp. 43–75): every section, every proof given in the text, the four numerical tables, and the references (pp. 32–33, skimmed). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Paschalidis and Tsitsiklis match (p. 1). The OCR garbles the displayed formulas, among them inequalities (2)–(8), (13)–(14), (18), (23)–(24) and (43); statements about them come from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis and John N. Tsitsiklis (MIT, p. 1). Proofs left out of the paper are in Paschalidis's 1992 MIT M.S. thesis (pp. 8, 10; reference list p. 33) · **Position in trajectory**: received January 1993, revised June 1993. A preliminary version was given at a workshop on hierarchical control of manufacturing systems (October 1992), the full paper at ORSA/TIMS on 2 November 1992, and a technical report appeared as LIDS-P-2155 (pp. 1, 32). Funding: NSF, his Presidential Young Investigator award with Draper Laboratory matching funds, MIT's Leaders for Manufacturing Program, and ARO (p. 1). It is the network half of the achievable-region programme (SKILL SW1). Bertsimas–Niño-Mora (1992) supplies the conservation-law theory it extends (pp. 4, 24), and the 1995 sole-author survey S185 retells its method (card S185, pp. 33–46).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + LO + CP; IO in the polynomial-formulation result}: The problem is to choose sequencing (and, in §5, routing) policies that minimize a weighted sum of expected response times in an open multiclass network, or that maximize throughput in a closed one (pp. 1–2). Applications are named only generically: packet-switching networks, job shops, multiprocessor scheduling (p. 2). The gap: even FCFS with Poisson arrivals and exponential services has no product-form or analytical solution. Simulation is therefore the common way to evaluate heuristic policies, and simulation does not say how far from optimal a policy is (p. 2). The entry is a transplant: lower bounds "in the tradition of discrete optimization" (p. 2). The paper adds a methodological thesis: instead of optimizing the stochastic system directly, characterize all achievable performance vectors. The problem then becomes a mathematical program, open to mature algorithms and to objectives that involve variances (pp. 2–3). Formally, X is the set of response-time vectors of policies that satisfy Assumption A, and its convex hull X′ (reached by time sharing) is the "achievable region". Exact characterization "appears to be very difficult", so X′ is approximated from outside, and minimizing Σ c_r x_r over the larger set gives a lower bound (pp. 6–7).
- **D2 Core idea**: Every policy's steady state must satisfy identities that potential functions generate. For R(t) = Σ f(r) n_r(t), write the one-step recursion of E[R²(τ_{k+1}) | n(τ_k)] in the uniformized chain, and use the fact that E[R²] does not change from step to step in steady state (pp. 8–9, 12–13). Two variants follow.
  - *Parametric*: f-parameters that satisfy condition (17) aggregate the busy indicators of each station, which are then bounded by 1. The result is one linear inequality per class subset S, 2^R − 1 in all (Theorem 4.1, pp. 12–14).
  - *Nonparametric*: the products I_rj = E[1{B_r} n_j] become new variables, and the identity is required for every f. This yields O(R²) equalities (Theorem 4.2, pp. 15–16), whose projection lies inside the parametric polyhedron (Theorem 4.3, pp. 17–18).

  Higher-degree potentials capture interactions among i classes (p. 20). Jensen's inequality, imposed for every positive semidefinite Q, adds convex constraints (pp. 21–22). For Klimov's problem, the multiclass M/M/1 queue and homogeneous networks, the first-order polyhedron is exactly the achievable region (pp. 22–26), hence the claim that the method is "the natural extension of conservation laws" (pp. 1, 3, 32), or their "generalization" to queueing networks (p. 23). The closest prior device is Kumar's (1992) single potential-function inequality with fixed weights, a "fairly crude" bound that Kumar himself called weak (pp. 3, 14). Against it, what changes is free weights, a whole family of inequalities and a nonlinear layer (pp. 3, 14); what stays is the Markovian model and the achievable-region object of the conservation-law literature (pp. 4, 6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: N single-server stations and R classes; independent Poisson arrivals per class; exponential, class-dependent services; Markovian routing (pp. 5–6). Policies are Markovian and may idle and preempt (p. 6).
  - Assumptions: Assumption A (a unique invariant distribution, finite second moments of the queue lengths) is adopted "For technical reasons" (p. 6); the third-order version needs finite third moments (p. 20). Stability requires Σ_{r∈C_i} λ_r/μ_r < 1 at every node (p. 11). The exactness results hold only for work-conserving policies (pp. 10, 23–26).
  - Extensions: routing control (p. 18), closed networks with K customers (p. 19), phase-type services and bounds for a given policy (p. 19) are outlined, not worked out; the details are referred to the technical report (p. 18).
  - Data: none real. The examples are small synthetic networks with chosen rates (Tables 2, 4, 6; pp. 28–31). No owner, partner or current rule appears [my reading of the whole text].
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Uniformization with fictitious service completions, so that the embedded discrete chain has the invariant distribution of the continuous one (pp. 8–9, 12).
  - Proof skeleton, given for inequality (2) only ("The other inequalities can be derived similarly"): expand the squared terms; bound the busy indicator by 1; take steady-state expectations; cancel the R² terms; use E[1{B_r}] = λ_r/μ_r and Little's law n_r = λ_r x_r (pp. 8–10). Restricted to work-conserving policies, the same argument yields the M/M/1 conservation law with equality (p. 10).
  - Choice of weights: setting f_S(r) to the expected remaining processing time until the job leaves S (eq. (19)) makes the denominator the heavy-traffic term 1 − Σ_{r∈S} λ_r/μ_r, "a key reason why we believe that it leads to tight bounds" (p. 14).
  - Coefficient matching: both sides of the steady-state recursion are quadratic forms in f. Equality for all f forces Q = Q₀, which gives the diagonal equalities (23) and the off-diagonal ones (24) (p. 16). Test functions g = n_r² and n_r n_r′ give the same system (p. 16).
  - Problem-specific cuts in the two-station example:
    - a stability argument gives Theorem 3.2 (p. 10);
    - a cost vector whose optimal policy is known gives the valid bound (14): the costs under which classes 1 and 3 form a tandem queue (pp. 10–11);
    - the cµ-rule bound is subsumed by Theorem 3.1 (p. 11).
  - Nonlinear layer: E[YᵀQY] ≥ E[Y]ᵀQE[Y] for every PSD Q (problem (34)). Solve first with Q = I, then separate over PSD matrices ("a semidefinite programming problem") and add the violated cuts. With the ellipsoid method or Vaidya's algorithm plus interior-point SDP, the procedure runs in polynomial time (pp. 21–22). [My reading: the PSD cone is self-dual, so for each class r the family (34) is equivalent to one linear matrix inequality, H_r − I_r I_rᵀ ⪰ 0; the cutting-plane loop thus imposes a moment-matrix condition. S007 later writes such constraints directly as PSD moment matrices (card S007, pp. 5–6, 15).]
  - Exactness: under preemptive priority to S, the bounded inequality (42) holds with equality, so a priority rule attains each constraint. Strong conservation laws then give the region through Theorem 7.1 of Bertsimas–Niño-Mora: an extended polymatroid over which one linear-time pass optimizes, despite 2^R − 1 constraints (pp. 24–26).
  - Polynomial-size reformulation: the nonparametric system is an exact O(R²) description of that extended polymatroid. It verifies, "for this special case", the conjecture that polynomially solvable problems of this type have LP formulations of polynomial size (pp. 23, 26).
  - Routing: a closed-form bound (43), garbled in the file. Join-the-shortest-queue attains it asymptotically as ρ → 1 (Foschini–Salz), so the bound is asymptotically exact (pp. 29–30).
  - Complexity: the first-order LP has O(R²) variables and constraints (pp. 1–2). The order-i system grows as O(R^i), "prohibitively large" for moderate i (p. 20). Tractable here means polynomial: "efficiently (in polynomial time)" (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Instances, all small and synthetic:
    - (i) the Harrison–Wein two-station, three-class network at seven load pairs (Tables 1–2, pp. 27–28);
    - (ii) a six-class, two-station network at four load pairs, with fixed costs (Tables 3–4, pp. 28–29);
    - (iii) routing to two exponential servers at three loads (Tables 5–6, pp. 29–31);
    - (iv) a closed version of network (ii), with a 50–50 product mix and populations 7, 10 and 20 (Table 7, pp. 30–31).
  - Metric: "efficiency" = best lower bound / best upper bound × 100% (p. 27). The upper bound is the best policy the authors could find, "of course, an overestimate" of the optimum, so the efficiency understates the bound's tightness (p. 26).
  - Upper-bound policies:
    - FCFS;
    - Harrison–Wein's threshold policy, with the threshold set "by doing several simulation runs" (p. 27);
    - the best strict priority rule found, non-preemptive only (p. 28);
    - join-the-shortest-queue, known to be optimal (p. 30);
    - Harrison–Wein's heavy-traffic priority policy, for the closed network (p. 30).
  - Results:
    - Efficiency: 83–96% in (i) (Table 1, p. 27); 62–95% in (ii) (Table 3, p. 29); 72–92% in (iii) (Table 5, p. 30); 97–99% in (iv) (Table 7, p. 31).
    - The two bounds coincide in all seven cases of (i) (Table 1, p. 27). The nonparametric bound is strictly higher in (ii), e.g. 16.67 against 15.72 under heavy–heavy load (Table 3, p. 29).

The first point holds in Table 1 (83 → 84 → 87% in balanced load, p. 27), and the text admits that it may come from the threshold policy improving in heavy traffic, i.e. from the upper bound (p. 27). In Table 3 the balanced rows fall from 67% (medium) to 62% (heavy) (p. 29). Item 4 of the summary concedes this ("especially when the traffic intensity is not very close to 1") and presents 62% as a floor (p. 31).
  - Failures and limits printed: 62% under heavy balanced traffic (p. 31). The higher-order and nonlinear bounds are not computed (p. 32).

- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions:
    - a method: potential functions generate LP and convex relaxations of a performance region;

    - a small numerical study (pp. 2–4, 26, 31–32).
  - Limits stated, concretely:

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D1) When a field judges policies by simulation alone, enter by supplying the missing yardstick: a bound computed in closed form from the data, offered as a tool (pp. 2–3, 5).
- (D2) To turn one potential-function inequality into a polyhedron, make the weights free, keep the cross moments as variables, and require the identity for all weights; quadratic forms are then equated coefficient by coefficient (pp. 15–16).
- (D4) Choose the potential weights so that indicator terms aggregate per station before they are bounded. Weights equal to expected remaining processing times recover the heavy-traffic denominator (pp. 13–14).
- (D4) Collect known-optimum cuts: every cost vector whose optimal policy and cost are known gives a valid inequality on the achievable region (pp. 10–11).
- (D4) To prove a relaxation exact, show that a specific policy (here, priority to S) attains each inequality, then invoke a polyhedral characterization theorem (pp. 24–26).
- (D4) Add Jensen-type PSD constraints on expectations of products to any relaxation whose variables are moments, and separate the violated ones by an SDP or an eigenvalue test (pp. 21–22).
- (D5) Report efficiency as lower bound over best policy found, across a grid of balanced and imbalanced loads. Say that the denominator overestimates the optimum. Explain where the bound is tight by the exactly solved special case: one bottleneck behaves like one station (pp. 26–31).
- (D2, D5) Automate the derivation: generate the constraints by symbolic manipulation of polynomial identities, feed them to an LP solver, and present the pipeline as a tool (pp. 17, 21).

### S098 · Fluid models of sequencing problems in open queueing networks; an optimal control approach (Stochastic Networks, IMA Volumes in Mathematics and its Applications 71, 1995, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4757-2418-9_11)
Read level: **full**, of the June 1994 preprint: pp. 1–34 (preprint pages = file pages): every section, every proof given, and the references. The policy-map figures (Figs. 7 and 11–16; pp. 20, 30–32) survive only as point markers and captions, so the comparisons are taken from the prose.

Version: the file is a 34-page preprint dated June 1994, titled "An Optimal Control Approach to Optimization of Multiclass Queueing Networks". Its authors (Avram, Bertsimas, Ricard) and its subject (an optimal control approach to fluid models of open multiclass networks; abstract, p. 1) are those of the listed chapter, so it is the same work under its preprint title, not the IMA print. The preprint refers to an appendix with the maximum-principle derivations (p. 8) that is not in the file, and several references print as "[?]" (pp. 3, 6). Cite as [S098 preprint p. N].

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Florin Avram (Department of Mathematics, Northeastern University) and Michael Ricard (Operations Research Center, MIT) (p. 1). Avram is a repeat co-author (S158, 1992; card S158) · **Position in trajectory**:
- The policy half of the 1992–1995 queueing-network programme. It cites S072 as published (AAP 1994; ref. [4], p. 33) and leaves lower bounds to that line (p. 3).
- The 1995 survey S185 lists it among the cohort works (card S185, p. 4).
- It was funded by the PYI award with Draper Laboratory matching funds (p. 1).
- Its open problem, an optimal algorithm for the fluid problem (p. 33), is the subject of Luo–Bertsimas (S156, received October 1995), which cites Avram–Bertsimas–Ricard (card S156, p. 2).
- S007 later pairs fluid-control policies with SDP lower bounds (card S007, p. 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + LO; HS for the learning heuristic}:
  - The problem and the opening paragraphs are those of S072: sequencing in open multiclass networks to minimize a weighted sum of expected sojourn times; no product form even under FCFS; simulation as the common practice (p. 2).
  - The entry is a decomposition of the difficulty into three characteristics: the problem is dynamic, combinatorial and stochastic (p. 2). The paper takes "the philosophical view" that the first two matter most, and that a deterministic dynamic model reveals the structure of optimal policies (p. 2).
  - It starts from Chen and Yao's fluid model, which they solved suboptimally by a sequence of myopic LPs (p. 2).
  - Problem statement (CONTROL): minimize ∫₀ᵀ c′x(t) dt subject to ẋ = Au + b, Du ≤ e, x(0) = x̃, x ≥ 0, u ≥ 0. A encodes service and routing, D is the station–class incidence matrix, and u_i is the fraction of effort spent on class i. The question asked is the optimal u(0) as a function of the initial state x̃ (pp. 5–6).
- **D2 Core idea**: One model, three tools (pp. 2–3).
  - The maximum principle gives explicit optimal policies for small networks. Each station serves the class with the largest dynamic index P_i(t) = μ_i(y_i − y_n(i)), built from the adjoint multipliers. It is a time-varying version of the myopic index f_i = μ_i(c_i − c_n), which is optimal only for one-server networks (pp. 6–7). The optimal policy "foresees" downstream depletions and switches before they occur (p. 8).
  - In a tandem with c1 < c2 and μ1 < μ2, it is optimal to hold station 1 while x̃1/x̃2 < (c2/c1)(μ1 − λ)/(μ2 − μ1). This is a threshold policy with an explicit linear switching curve. The myopic rule would never serve here, "with disastruous long term consequences" (pp. 8, 11–14).
  - (CONTROL) is also an infinite-dimensional LP. Solved by time discretization, it is the numerical yardstick (pp. 3, 6, 18–20).
  - The heuristic: with A nonsingular, u can be eliminated, and the feasible velocities form a polyhedron Āẋ ≤ b̄ whose extreme points include the optimal control. Every extreme point is the unique minimizer of some linear objective w′ẋ, so a policy amounts to a choice of w (pp. 20–21).
    - w is fitted to building blocks solved exactly: two competing classes (w = c, Theorem 6, p. 23) and two classes in tandem (Theorem 7, p. 24).
    - The blocks are combined through the ratios w_i/w_j in a least-squares system Mw = 0, w′e = 1 (pp. 21–22).
    - "Learning" means that each newly solved special network can add equations (p. 22).
  - Against Chen–Yao, explicit thresholds replace a myopic sequence of LPs (pp. 2, 6–8). The fluid model itself is kept (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The model is a deterministic fluid model: continuous queue contents; controls u_i(t) in [0, 1] that sum to at most 1 per station; given routing; an external arrival vector b; a horizon T long enough to empty the system (pp. 5–6). Stochasticity is removed by assumption, the "philosophical view" (p. 2).
  - Assumptions:
    - The examples assume x̃ > 0 for all classes (p. 9), each with its own stability condition (pp. 11, 15, 17).
    - The maximum-principle simplification assumes b = 0, so that a depleted class stays empty (p. 7).
    - The heuristic assumes A nonsingular (p. 20).
    - Theorem 5 is stated "without proof" (p. 17).
  - Data: none. The parameter values are illustrations (pp. 20, 29–32). No owner or current rule appears [my reading of the whole text].
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Hamiltonian H = c′x + y′(Au + b), with adjoint ẏ_i = −c_i and transversality conditions; on interior arcs, u optimizes H pointwise (p. 7).
  - Because H is continuous, a switch from class i to its competitor c requires P_i(t) = P_c(t). Switches are classified as DEPLETIONS (forced) and INTERRUPTS (interior), and the solution follows "provided that we can guess the time order" of these events (pp. 7–8).
  - Proposition: the cofactors are decreasing linear functions, and the indices are piecewise linear with at most N_i + 1 changes of slope (p. 8). "Tooth"-shaped indices explain why holding can be optimal; a fork with K routes can have up to 2K − 2 interrupts (p. 8).
  - The μc rule is re-derived from the maximum principle (Theorem 2, p. 10). It is offered as support for the philosophical view, since the same policy is optimal in the stochastic model (p. 10).
  - Tandem, Case 3:
    - The maximum principle allows at most one switch.
    - The cost of never holding, F_N(x̃1, x̃2), is computed in closed form and compared with holding for a small Δ.
    - The difference is Δ²A + ΔB with A > 0, so holding pays exactly when B < 0, which gives the threshold (pp. 12–14).
    - Case 4 is "very similar" (p. 14).
  - Reduction: competition at the first node becomes a "modified tandem" with effective costs c1μ1 − c3μ3 and c2μ1 (Theorem 4, p. 16).
  - Discretization:
    - Steps of length h give an LP with 2nT/h variables. Its solution yields a feasible solution of the original problem (Pullan), hence an upper bound (p. 19).
    - h is halved repeatedly, and the coarse solution stays feasible on the finer grid, so simplex starts from it.
    - The loop stops when the objective falls by less than 5% and u(0) is binary (p. 19).
  - Heuristic correctness: Theorems 6–7 show that the LP with the chosen w returns the exact optimal u(0) for the two building blocks (pp. 23–25). The three-class network of Theorem 4 is then reproduced (pp. 26–27).
  - No complexity bound is given for any of the three tools [my reading].
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Check of the LP: on a tandem with c = (3, 5), λ = 1, μ = (2, 3), the discretized LP reproduces the analytical threshold over a grid of initial states (Fig. 7, p. 20).
  - Heuristic against the discretized LP:
    - A four-class, two-station network, with x0 = 4, x3 = 2 and x1, x2 from 1 to 10: the heuristic finds the threshold, and its slope is "not exact but is very close". With x3 = 7 the two policies are identical; for a third parameter set they are "quite close" (p. 29; Figs. 11–13, pp. 30–31).
    - A four-class, three-station fork: the threshold is found and its slope is missed slightly (p. 29; Fig. 14, p. 31).
    - A six-class reentrant line: the same U(0) = (0,0,0,1,1,1) over the whole grid (p. 29; Fig. 15, p. 32).
    - The nine-class Kumar–Perkins network: only two instances, "Because of the difficulty to solve the discrete LP to optimality", both with identical U(0) (pp. 29–30; Fig. 16, p. 32).
  - Metric: agreement of the time-zero control over a grid of initial states, not cost or run time. The numerical mode simulates the heuristic's cost (p. 28), but no cost is reported (pp. 28–30). "many problems were processed", and some are shown (p. 28). No solver, machine or timing is given.
  - Scale: the exact method reaches "up to ten stations" (p. 33). The heuristic is claimed to handle "hundreds of classes" (p. 33) and to be accurate "even for large scale problems" (p. 3). The largest network reported has nine classes (p. 29).
  - Baseline: the myopic Chen–Yao rule is analysed (pp. 6–8) but not run in §6.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Chen–Yao (and Atkins–Chen) are credited with the fluid model and a myopic solution. "In contrast with the work of Chen and Yao [6], our approach offers explicit threshold policies" (p. 2), and some of the examples are said to be solved for the first time (pp. 2–3).
  - Optimal control of fluid models in communications (Filipiak and others) is cited for its local methods and special-case global methods (p. 3).
  - The Brownian heavy-traffic school is credited, and limited, in S072's words. Harrison–Wein's threshold for the two-station, three-class network is characterized explicitly here (p. 3).
  - Lower bounds are left to Ou–Wein and to Bertsimas–Paschalidis–Tsitsiklis, with Kumar–Kumar (p. 3).
  - For the infinite LP, the paper cites the separated continuous LP literature (Anderson, Anderson–Nash, Pullan) and notes that no efficient general algorithm exists (p. 18).
  - Self-reuse: the first three paragraphs of the introduction and the paragraph on Brownian models repeat S072's text almost word for word (pp. 2–3; S072 pp. 2, 4–5).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - A stance stated up front, the "philosophical view" (p. 2), and returned to after the μc example (p. 10). Contributions are numbered 1–3 (pp. 2–3).
  - Speed claims carry no timings: "very fast and completely automatic" (p. 1), "extremely fast" (p. 3). The roadmap promises "extensive computational results" (p. 4); §6 shows a handful of networks (pp. 28–30).
  - The language of exactness is used for a discretization stopped at a 5% improvement: "an optimal numerical approach" (p. 1) and "We solve then the problem exactly" (p. 3), against the stopping rule (p. 19).
  - Draft marks:
    - "[?]" citations (pp. 3, 6);
    - a roadmap that does not match the section order (pp. 3–4, against §§3–6);
    - "obtained in the appendix", with no appendix in the file (p. 8);
    - typos ("dicretizes", "hte", "Offcourse"; pp. 1, 3, 22).
  - Candid admissions sit beside the claims: the learning capability is "not, however, implemented" (p. 3); the three-way heuristic was not built (p. 22); the symbolic output needs "manual" simplification (p. 28).
  - The close hedges with "We conjecture" and "we suspect" (p. 33).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions (pp. 2–3, 27–28):
    - insight: explicit threshold policies and dynamic indices for small networks;
    - an algorithmic yardstick: the refined discretized LP;
    - a heuristic;
    - software: a Maple pipeline with a symbolic mode, which writes LaTeX tables of conditions, objective values and controls, and a numerical mode, which outputs a policy and its simulated cost.
  - Limits stated:
    - the exact method's dimensionality limits it to about ten stations, and an optimal algorithm is "a challenging open problem" (p. 33);
    - the heuristic misses slopes (p. 29);
    - learning and three-way interactions are not implemented (pp. 3, 22);
    - the symbolic output needs manual simplification (p. 28);
    - it is unknown whether stochastic arrivals and services change the insights (p. 33).
  - Future work: higher-interaction heuristics. The paper also conjectures that under randomness the threshold curves become higher-order curves while the policies stay qualitatively the same (p. 33).
  - Not stated [my reading]: the policies are never evaluated in a stochastic network, so the paper's own premise (that the fluid model reveals the structure) is untested here, and the cost of the heuristic policies is not reported.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (SD → LO): a stochastic control problem is rewritten as a deterministic linear control problem, i.e. an infinite LP (a separated continuous LP), and solved as a finite LP after discretization (pp. 5–6, 18–19). Structure is read off the multipliers: the dynamic indices come from the adjoint variables (p. 7), the analogue of Method 1 step 4. The boundary is printed: about ten stations, and no optimal algorithm (p. 33). The departure from Method 1 is in how the class is reached: by a relaxation (the fluid model), not an exact reformulation, with the link back to the stochastic problem conjectured, not shown (pp. 2, 33).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: an exact solve is the yardstick for a fast heuristic (pp. 3, 20, 28–30), and the heuristic is the challenger. There is no duel with the incumbent: the myopic rule is analysed but not run (pp. 6–8). The "exact" solution is a discretization stopped at a 5% improvement (p. 19) yet is called optimal (pp. 1, 3), an early form of Tension T1. The comparison covers the time-zero control, not cost (pp. 28–30).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (step 3):
  - It is a cohort work with an MIT Operations Research Center co-author, listed in the 1995 survey (card S185, p. 4), and funded by the PYI award with industry matching funds (p. 1).
  - Its open problem becomes the next student paper (S156; card S156, p. 2).
- Method 2 · evidence ✅ (step 6 only, across cards): the special-case policies of 1994, whose threshold curves "are hyperplanes" under the fluid model (p. 33), are taken up again under the ML lens in S402. S402 proves hyperplane structure in general and learns the policy with OCT-H (card S402, pp. 3, 9). The paper itself makes no quantified "why now".
- Tension T3 (optimization, not probability) · evidence for the optimization side in 1994, [stated-joint]: the structure of optimal policies "can be revealed by a deterministic and dynamic model" (p. 2). The μc example is offered as support (p. 10), and the stochastic layer is left as a conjecture (p. 33). This also qualifies the Domain Map's "no stated methodology found" for SD.
- Tension T6 (headline vs caveat): "very fast", "large scale" and "hundreds of classes" (pp. 1, 3, 33) against a largest network of nine classes, only two instances at that size, and no timings (pp. 29–30).
- New-pattern candidates:
  - *deterministic fluid core first, stochastic layer conjectured* (pp. 2, 10, 33);
  - *learn a policy from exactly solved small motifs*: building blocks solved by the maximum principle fix the weights of an LP-vertex policy for larger networks (pp. 3, 21–27). It is a 1994 precursor of SKILL Heuristic 5 (learn the optimizer's strategy offline), learning from small exact cases rather than from data;
  - *exact optimum on a small solvable instance as the certificate for a heuristic* (pp. 3, 20, 28–30);
  - *re-derive classical theorems with the new device*: the μc rule from the maximum principle (p. 10);
  - *symbolic-algebra pipeline from model description to bound or policy* (pp. 22, 27–28; also S072);
  - *one problem split into a bounds paper and a policies paper*: the bounds of S072 and the policies of S098 share an introduction (p. 2; S072 p. 2), and the two are joined later (card S007, p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D1) Name the components of a problem's difficulty, say which one you drop and why, and test the stance on a case where the answer is known (the μc rule) (pp. 2, 10).
- (D2) Parametrize a policy as the LP vertex that objective w picks over the polytope of feasible velocities. Fit w so that the policy reproduces exact solutions of small subsystems, and combine those by least squares (pp. 20–24).
- (D4) To locate a switching curve, compare the closed-form cost of acting now with the cost of waiting Δ and then acting; the sign of the term linear in Δ gives the threshold (pp. 12–14).
- (D4) Reduce a new network to a solved one through effective costs: competition at node 1 becomes a "modified tandem" with costs c1μ1 − c3μ3 and c2μ1 (p. 16).
- (D4) Refine a time discretization by halving, warm-start each LP from the coarse solution (which stays feasible), stop on a relative-improvement rule, and report the rule (p. 19).
- (D5) Compare a heuristic with the exact solution as a policy map over a grid of initial states. The map shows whether the switching curve exists and how far its slope is off (pp. 20, 28–29).
