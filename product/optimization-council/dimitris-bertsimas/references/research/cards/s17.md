### S482 · THEMIS: a framework for cost-benefit analysis of COVID-19 non-pharmaceutical interventions (medRxiv 2022, DOI 10.1101/2022.04.09.22273656)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–18 (abstract, §§1–5, Appendix A on DELPHI and Appendix B's parameter table; references skimmed). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Li and Soni match (p. 1). The file is the medRxiv preprint in a "Nature Human Behavior manuscript" template, and its pages carry medRxiv's notice that it is not peer reviewed (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Lingzhi Li (MIT ORC, p. 1; PhD 2022, postdoc 2022–2023 per 04-mentorship.md), Saksham Soni (MIT Sloan, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a sequel to DELPHI, the group's 2020 COVID-19 forecaster. The deployed predictive model becomes the simulator of a retrospective policy evaluation (pp. 2–4) {AP DA}.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP (health policy) + DA; SD as the simulator}: NPIs cut transmission but cost output, jobs and mental health (abstract; pp. 1–2). Earlier cost–benefit studies priced only the implemented policy or compared two NPIs, mostly by GDP (p. 2). Entry: use the realized pandemic to cost any region's actual NPI sequence and compare it with a wide range of alternatives (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A policy is a sequence of NPIs with switching dates (p. 3).
  - DELPHI simulates the epidemic with a region-specific reduction of the infection rate for each NPI, constant while that NPI is in force (eqs. 3–4, pp. 4–5).
  - Five cost modules price the simulated path: QALYs lost; hospital, ICU and ventilator days; depression and PTSD; GDP through consumption and investment; and unemployment (eqs. 5–14, pp. 6–7).
  - An NPI never used in a region gets an imputed effect from a linear regression of regional on global effects (Fig. 3, p. 5). GDP and unemployment effects are imputed the same way (p. 7).
  - The search is enumeration. Six mobility NPIs, each held for one month over three months, give 6³ = 216 policies (p. 8, where the extraction prints "63").
- **D3 Model and data**: DELPHI is an 11-state SEIR extension with under-detection, an arctan-plus-resurgence response function and declining mortality (pp. 3–4, 15–16).
  - The parameter counts disagree: 16 parameters, with 7 calibrated and 9 fitted (p. 4), against 13 fitted per area (p. 16).
  - The fit minimizes a recency-weighted MSE, by TNC daily and dual annealing weekly (p. 16).
  - Data: NPIs of 167 countries and regions in March–April 2020. The first wave runs 15 March–15 June 2020. The regions are Germany, Spain, Brazil, Singapore, New York State and Florida, with sourced cost parameters (pp. 7–9, 17–18).
  - Admitted simplifications: costs are linear and additive (p. 11); depression counts only under the two severe NPIs (pp. 6, 8); GDP leaves out government spending and net exports, which are confounded (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a for proofs. Closed-form cost formulas sit on a simulated trajectory (eqs. 5–14, pp. 6–7), and the best policy is found by enumerating all 216 policies, not by an optimization model (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - All policies are plotted per region in a humanitarian–economic cost plane, with the actual policy marked (Fig. 4, pp. 8–9). The 20 cheapest are decomposed by cost type (Fig. 5, p. 10).
  - The frontier is flat: humanitarian costs span orders of magnitude while economic costs stay at 10–30% of GDP (p. 8).
  - Three months of lockdown (6-6-6) is never the total-cost optimum (p. 9).
  - New York's actual policy lies far from the frontier: the optimum would have saved about $120 billion in humanitarian and $4 billion in economic cost. Germany's lies close to the frontier (pp. 8–9).
  - Nothing validates the counterfactuals, and DELPHI's accuracy is asserted (pp. 3–4), not measured.
  - The introduction claims that acting one week earlier could cut total cost by 90% (p. 2). I found no experiment behind it in §3 (pp. 7–11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A modular, open-source framework (code on GitHub, p. 3) plus first-wave policy insights (pp. 2–3). §4 states four specific limits (p. 11):
  - only NPIs used somewhere before can be evaluated;
  - residual confounding is likely;
  - SEIR models need large populations;
  - costs are linear and additive.

  The conclusion's claim about developing countries (p. 11) rests on the two lower-GDP regions, Spain and Brazil (pp. 10–11). Future work is a generic call for further research (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 5: evidence ✅ (pp. 8–11). The owner's actual policy is the baseline in every region, and policies are scored by their costs, not by forecast accuracy. The limits of the evaluation are printed. Gaps: nothing is field-tested, and DELPHI is called "highly accurate" (p. 4) without the field's own metric. Method 5's correction in SKILL.md applies here: DELPHI's relative WIS was 0.99 against the naive 1.00 (05 §5.2).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 8–9). A trade-off curve between the two costs is published per region, but it comes from enumeration, with no single knob and no bound.
- Heuristic 4: evidence ✅ (p. 11). A separate limitations section names four concrete limits.
- New-pattern candidate: **counterfactual replay against the historical decision-maker** (existing candidate). The first wave is replayed under all 216 policies and set against the policy actually implemented (pp. 8–9).
- New-pattern candidate: **hindsight-free counterfactual menu**. The alternatives are only NPIs used somewhere in March–April 2020, so none needs information that was unavailable at the time (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: **deployed forecaster reused as the policy simulator**. DELPHI, built for forecasting and used in the CDC's ensemble (p. 2), drives the counterfactuals (pp. 2–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Limit counterfactual alternatives to actions actually taken somewhere at the time, and say so, so the comparison does not reward hindsight (p. 8).
- To estimate the effect of an action a region never took, regress the region's observed effects on the global effects of the actions it did take. Reuse the same map for secondary outcomes (Fig. 3, p. 5; p. 7).
- Drop outcome components that concurrent policies confound, such as government spending and net exports, and state why (p. 7).
- Plot the whole policy set in a two-cost plane with the actual decision highlighted. The frontier and the decision-maker's implied trade-off then become visible (Fig. 4, pp. 8–9).
- For a non-convex ODE fit:
  - weight recent errors by t²/T²;
  - balance cases against deaths;
  - run a local solver daily within ±20% bounds and a global one weekly within ±50% (p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "In fact, across all countries tested, the most severe policy, 6-6-6, is never the optimal policy in terms of achieving minimum costs in the sum of the two dimensions." (p. 9)
- "However, despite these measures, it is likely that residual confounding remains." (p. 11)

### S149 · Learning Sparse Nonlinear Dynamics via Mixed-Integer Optimization (Nonlinear Dynamics 2023, DOI 10.1007/s11071-022-08178-9; arXiv 2206.00176)
Read level: **full, arXiv version**: arXiv pp. 1–19 (abstract, §§1–4, Appendices A–B; references skimmed). The file is arXiv 2206.00176v1 of 1 June 2022 (p. 1), not the journal print. Cite it as [S149 arXiv p. N]. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Gurnee match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Wes Gurnee (MIT ORC, p. 1; thesis record 2025 per 04-mentorship.md) · **Position in trajectory**: the best-subset MIO line (SW4) carried into scientific machine learning, where SINDy's sparse regression is solved exactly (pp. 1–2) {ML IO}.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO as engine}: SINDy, "powered by heuristic sparse regression", is a dominant tool for learning governing equations from data (abstract). Its sequential-threshold least squares misses small coefficients and resists structural constraints. Those weaknesses led to SR3, conditional-gradient, greedy and convex alternatives (p. 1). Entry: the claim that such heuristics are "unnecessary, and ultimately, inadequate" (p. 1). The claim is backed by an MIO speed-up of over a trillion times since 1990, cited to three sources (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: MIOSR fits each state dimension by ridge regression with at most k_j nonzero coefficients.
  - SOS-1 constraints tie each coefficient to a binary. The objective is in Gram form, so the model has D continuous and D binary variables whatever the sample size n (eqs. 9–15, p. 4).
  - SOS-1 replaces big-M because coefficient bounds are costly to compute and do not carry over to extra constraints (p. 4).
  - Sparsity is a hard constraint, not a penalty. The paper cites better statistical behaviour when library terms are strongly collinear (p. 4).
  - Constraints that couple coordinates use one joint block-diagonal model, optionally weighted by the energies of POD modes (eq. 16, p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**: Polynomial libraries, noise added as a percentage of the data's norm, and 50 random initial conditions per setting. The first 2/3 of each trajectory trains and the last 1/3 selects the model by AICc (pp. 5–6). The weak (integral) form handles high noise and PDEs (pp. 3, 10–13). Admitted scale limit: general-purpose solvers handle "up to a few thousand SOS-1 constraints". That suffices here, because a six-dimensional system with a fifth-order library has only 462 terms (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Formulation devices only, with no theorems: SOS-1 support modelling, the Gram-form objective, and the joint model with linear equalities derived from a potential function (pp. 4–5, 10). Optimality is certified by the solver's dual bound (p. 2).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Nine canonical systems (pp. 7–13):
  - Lorenz, Hopf and MHD for data length and runtime;
  - the 2D Duffing system for physical constraints;
  - Van der Pol, Lotka–Volterra and Rössler under noise;
  - the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky and reaction–diffusion PDEs.

  Setup and results:
  - Baselines are only ℓ0-class methods: STLSQ, SR3, SSR and ensembled STLSQ. LASSO, probabilistic and deep-learning methods are excluded (p. 6).
  - Runs use Gurobi 9.5.0 and one cluster core per trial, with 30 s per dimension (pp. 5–6).
  - Metrics are support intersection-over-union, coefficient error and derivative RMSE, averaged in log space over 50 trials (pp. 6–7).
  - MIOSR wins with little data, small coefficients or large libraries (pp. 7–8). It is often faster as n grows, and up to 100× faster on a laptop than on one core (p. 9).
  - Failures are printed: breakdown at very high noise (p. 12), no gain with small libraries and coefficients of similar size (pp. 13–14), and a smaller edge with tight libraries (p. 8).
  - The PDE runs set hyperparameters from the known truth (p. 13).
  - The share of runs certified within 30 s is not reported. Code and data are public (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An algorithm plus an implementation that follows the PySINDy interface (p. 2). The conclusion sees "no reason why MIOSR should not be the default choice of optimizer for real applications" (p. 14), although every test system is synthetic (pp. 7–13). Future work: SINDy variants for control and for implicit equations, and better data preparation for high noise (pp. 12, 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 5–14).
  - One MIO replaces greedy thresholding.
  - The time limit, solver version and machine are stated, and the dual bound certifies.
  - The community's incumbents are dueled on systems with known truth.
  - The paper says where MIOSR wins and where it does not.

  Partly a variant: warm starts are only suggested (p. 9), the certification rate is missing, and there is no real data.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 8–9). The "one trillion times" figure is borrowed from three surveys, not rebuilt. But the paper times its own model class on two machines (p. 9; Fig. 7, p. 19), which meets half of the correction in SKILL.md.
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 3–5). A problem owned by system identification is written exactly as an MIQO that stays in the solver class, and the scale boundary is printed.
- Heuristic 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 8, 12–14).
- New-pattern candidate: **adoption-compatible design** (existing candidate). The implementation follows the PySINDy interface, so it slots into existing workflows (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: **oracle-tuned achievability test**. Every method gets hyperparameters set from the known truth, so the test asks whether recovery is possible at all. This separates optimizer failure from model-selection failure (p. 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write an ℓ0-constrained least-squares objective in Gram form so the MIO does not grow with n. More data then tightens the bounds and can shorten solves (pp. 4, 9).
- Prefer SOS-1 to big-M when coefficient bounds are unknown or constraints will be added, and state what it costs in scale (p. 4).
- Fit coordinates separately unless a constraint couples them; then switch to one block-diagonal model (pp. 4–5).
- Randomize initial conditions instead of using the field's usual fixed one. Average errors in log space so one outlier cannot dominate (pp. 5, 7).
- Time the exact method on two hardware configurations to show its parallel headroom (p. 9; Fig. 7, p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "For system identification problems, we claim that such relaxations and heuristics are unnecessary, and ultimately, inadequate." (p. 1)
- "At very high levels of noise, MIOSR starts to break down, converging to the approximate performance of heuristic methods." (p. 12)

### S218 · Sparse plus low rank matrix decomposition: A discrete optimization approach (Journal of Machine Learning Research 2023, arXiv 2109.12701)
Read level: **partial**:
- Read:
  - pp. 1–5 and 7–8;
  - p. 10 (§3.2);
  - pp. 11–13 (Algorithm 1, Proposition 10, §3.3 through Theorem 12, Remark 13 and the opening of the proof);
  - pp. 15–19 (§4);
  - pp. 22–36 (§5.2, §6, §7);
  - pp. 47–49 (Tables 2–4).
- Not read:
  - p. 6 (§2.2);
  - the closed-form subproblem results (pp. 9–10);
  - p. 14;
  - pp. 20–21 (§5.1);
  - pp. 37–46 (references, Appendices A–D);
  - pp. 50–51.

The file is arXiv v3 of 2 October 2023 in JMLR's typesetting (p. 1). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Cory-Wright and Johnson match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ryan Cory-Wright (Imperial College Business School, p. 1; his PhD 2022 per 04-mentorship.md), Nicholas A. G. Johnson (MIT, p. 1; listed as Johnson 2024 in the ML thesis lineage of 01-publications.md) · **Position in trajectory**: submitted 9/21 and published 10/23 (p. 1). It applies S274's device, projection matrices with perspectives, to Robust PCA (p. 15) {ML IO CP}.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO and CP as engines}: Sparse-plus-low-rank decomposition, also called Robust PCA, is solved by convex surrogates (S-PCP) or by non-convex heuristics (GoDec, Fast RPCA, ScaledGD, AccAltProj).
  - Their guarantees rest on incoherence or on starting inside a basin of attraction, which is hard to verify (pp. 3–5).
  - The one exact method, Lee–Zou's branch-and-bound, reported bound gaps but no optimal solutions at n = 10 (pp. 2, 5).

  Entry: keep the rank and ℓ0 constraints explicit and add Frobenius ridges (eq. 1, p. 2). For this NP-hard problem, seek both good solutions and certified bounds (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three tools (pp. 2–3):
  - Algorithm 1 alternates two closed-form subproblems: a truncated SVD and a hard threshold (pp. 8–11).
  - An SDP relaxation lifts the support to binaries Z and the column space to a projection matrix P, then adds perspective constraints and convex hulls (eqs. 18–20, p. 15).
  - A custom branch-and-bound over sparsity patterns uses Algorithm 1 for upper bounds and the SDP for lower bounds (Algorithm 2, pp. 22–23).

  The ridge terms come from robustness: the regularized problem equals a robust problem against adversarial perturbations of the data (Propositions 3–4, p. 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: A square data matrix, a target rank k0, a target sparsity k1, and λ, µ > 0. If either λ or µ is 0, the relaxation's bound is 0 (pp. 2, 15). The data are synthetic only: Gaussian low-rank factors, a symmetric sparse matrix with entries from U(−5, 5), and Gaussian noise (p. 24). The paper calls Theorem 12's conditions on λ, µ and a ratio of singular values easy to verify and often met (p. 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Proposition 10 bounds Algorithm 1's iterations using the starting objective and the unconstrained minimum (p. 11).
  - On a fixed support, Algorithm 1 is projected gradient descent (Proposition 11). Theorem 12 then imports a linear-convergence result from Ha et al. 2020 (pp. 12–13).
  - Remark 13: with enough regularization, enumerating supports and running Algorithm 1 on each is certifiably optimal (p. 13).
  - The low-rank subproblem has hidden convexity (Theorem 16, p. 16).
  - The relaxation is at least as strong as Lee–Zou's, and strictly stronger on a 2×2 instance (Propositions 17–18, pp. 17–18).
  - The relaxation acts as a reverse-Huber penalty, while the unregularized big-M relaxation reduces to Lasso plus the nuclear norm (Proposition 19 and eqs. 27–28, pp. 18–19).
  - Branch-and-bound terminates finitely, and it is ε-optimal under Theorem 12's conditions (Theorems 21–22, pp. 22–23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Julia 1.5.2, JuMP 0.21.7 and Mosek 9.2 on MIT Supercloud, using at most 192 GB of RAM (pp. 23–24). λ and µ are set by 30-fold block cross-validation (pp. 24–25). Five baselines are named, AccAltProj through its authors' MATLAB code (p. 24).
  - Algorithm 1 has lower errors than the baselines in most trials at comparable time, up to n = 10,000 (pp. 25–28).
  - ScaledGD wins on sparse-support discovery in high dimension, and on low-rank error in 3 trials (pp. 28, 34).
  - The relaxation's bound gaps are reported up to n = 140, at up to 14,731 s (Table 3, p. 48).
  - Branch-and-bound with ε = 5% solves n ≤ 25. The hardest case explores 14,709 nodes in 28,977 s (Table 1, p. 33).
  - Table 3's gaps (0.54–0.96) carry no unit. The text's "very small bound gaps" (p. 2) suggests percent; that is my reading.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A formulation, algorithms and benchmarks (pp. 2–3). Stated limits:
  - an SDP must be solved at every node (p. 33);
  - most branch-and-bound time goes to certifying optimality, and its final solution is only marginally better than Algorithm 1's (p. 36);
  - there is no real data, although compression, collaborative filtering and imaging open the abstract (p. 1).

  Future work: second-order-cone bounds at each node and other branching rules (p. 36).

**Method links**:
- Method 4: evidence ✅ with a variant ⚠ (pp. 22–36). The work runs a heuristic, then a certificate, then exact branch-and-bound, and duels named incumbents. Software versions are given, and the losses to ScaledGD are printed. The variant: synthetic data only, and a custom branch-and-bound replaces a commercial MIO solver.
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 15, 33). The discrete problem is stated exactly and relaxed within the semidefinite class. Each algorithm's size boundary is printed: n = 10,000, 200 and 25.
- Heuristic 1: evidence ✅ (p. 7). The ridge regularizer is shown to be robustness against adversarial perturbations of the data. This extends Bertsimas–Copenhaver 2018 to matrices, offered as "of independent interest to the RO community".
- New-pattern candidate: **rival method as a relaxation of the exact formulation** (existing candidate). Lee–Zou's bound is dominated, and the Lasso-plus-nuclear-norm problem is the unregularized big-M relaxation (pp. 17–19).
- New-pattern candidate: **regularization as a tractability knob**. Enough ridge makes Algorithm 1 globally optimal on a fixed support, which gives a "phase transition" in the problem's difficulty (Theorem 12, Remark 13, pp. 12–13).
- New-pattern candidate: **exact optimum on a small solvable instance as the certificate for a heuristic** (existing candidate). Branch-and-bound at n ≤ 25 shows that Algorithm 1 is near-optimal (pp. 33, 36).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Lift rank to a projection matrix and support to binaries. Then convexify with perspective constraints and the convex hull of the projection matrices (p. 15).
- Prove that one relaxation dominates another in two steps: map feasible points to show it is at least as strong, then give one small instance where it is strictly stronger (pp. 17–18).
- Bound the iterations of alternating minimization by the ratio of the starting objective to the unconstrained minimum (Proposition 10, p. 11).
- On a fixed discrete pattern, recast block-coordinate updates as projected gradient descent and inherit its convergence theorem (pp. 12–13).
- Cross-validate a matrix decomposition by holding out a random l×l block and scoring its reconstruction from the other blocks (pp. 24–25).
- Report branch-and-bound per instance as root gap, nodes and time, and say where the time goes (pp. 32–33, 36).

**Quotes**:
- "Our heuristic scales to n = 10000 in minutes and our convex relaxation scales to n = 200 in hours." (p. 2)
- "This implies that the final solution returned by Algorithm 2 is, in general, only marginally better than the solution returned by Algorithm 1." (p. 36)

### S223 · The power and limits of predictive approaches to observational data-driven optimization: The case of pricing (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2023, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2022.0077; arXiv 1605.02347)
Read level: **partial, arXiv version**: pp. 1–33 (abstract, §§1–7 and endnotes; the references on pp. 33–35 skimmed) and the opening of the proof of Theorem 2 (p. 36). The other e-companion proofs (pp. 36–45) were not read. The file is arXiv 1605.02347v2 of 20 May 2017 (p. 1), and its title lacks the journal's subtitle, *The case of pricing*. Cite it as [S223 arXiv p. N]. First-page check: the authors Bertsimas and Kallus match, and the abstract announces "a particular focus on pricing" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nathan Kallus (Cornell Tech, p. 1; PhD 2015, postdoc 2015–2016 per 04-mentorship.md) · **Position in trajectory**: a companion to From Predictive to Prescriptive Analytics (SW5) for confounded observational data {DA AP}. It went from arXiv in 2016 (id 1605) to a journal version in 2023.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA; AP (revenue management)}: In transactional data, decisions and outcomes may be confounded. Practice nevertheless optimizes the best prediction of reward given the decision (Problem 2), not the causal objective (Problem 1) (pp. 1–5). Entry: only the objective value of the final decision matters (p. 1). The paper therefore asks when the predictive decision is still near-optimal and how to test it (pp. 3, 6–7). A parable makes the gap concrete: two consultants fit models that both reproduce the MIT Coop's data exactly but recommend different prices for a hoodie (Example 1, pp. 7–9). The optimal price is therefore not identifiable, even under the Gauss–Markov assumptions (Theorem 1, pp. 9–10).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two sides.
  - The power side bounds the true-profit ratio of the predictive price, using the confounding error and the structure of pricing (Theorems 2–4, pp. 10–14).
  - The limits side builds a test of H0: R(z*) = R(ẑ) for any data-driven decision rule (pp. 14–24). The test uses a non-parametric partial-means kernel estimate of the causal objective under ignorability, an asymptotic χ²₁ null, and a bootstrap for the unknown scale.

  A parametric method based on the generalized propensity score (Hirano–Imbens) is added for comparison (pp. 25–26).
- **D3 Model and data**: Pricing with profit z − c, a single price variable, and linear, non-increasing mean demand (p. 12). Each bound adds one assumption on the confounding:
  - a bound on its size relative to the market (Theorem 2);
  - joint normality with non-positive correlation, justified because promotions coincide with discounts (Theorem 3, p. 13);
  - confounding that does not increase with price (Theorem 4, p. 14).

  The test assumes decision rules that converge at the √n rate, ignorability given the covariates X, and regularity of kernel, optimum and distributions (Assumptions 1–5; pp. 15, 18, 21–22). Data: a simulated confounded instance (p. 27), and the Columbia CPRM online auto-loan data in 16 segments by FICO range, loan term and season (pp. 16, 29–31).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Non-identifiability is proved by a constructed counterexample (pp. 7–10).
  - The proof of Theorem 2 sandwiches the predictive maximizer between the maximizers under extreme confounding (p. 36). The bound stays non-negative up to δ ≈ 0.17 (p. 12).
  - Theorem 3's bound depends only on average demand relative to the market size, which can be estimated (p. 13).
  - Theorem 4's bound stays non-negative up to δ = 1 (p. 14).
  - Each bound is checked against a worked curve: 0.71 ≤ 0.75, 0.94 ≤ 0.96 and 0.79 ≤ 0.84 (pp. 11–14).
  - Theorems 6–8 give the estimator's asymptotics, the statistic's null law and the bootstrap scale (pp. 22–24). Theorem 9 shows that the generalized propensity score suffices (p. 25).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - In the simulated example, the predictive price earns zero true profit against an optimum of 31.25 (p. 28).
  - Over 256 replicates, the test reaches its nominal 5% size only after a few hundred observations (p. 29).
  - On the auto loans (B = 100 bootstrap draws; n = 690–4,495 per segment), the test rejects the non-parametric predictive rule at p < 0.05 in 13 of 16 segments and the parametric one in 9 (Table 4, pp. 30–31).
  - The prescriptive parametric rule passes in all but 2 segments (p. 31). It recoups 70% or 36% of the profit lost by the two predictive rules (p. 32).
  - Software and run times are not reported in the pages read.

- **D2 Core idea**: The Matrix Perspective Reformulation Technique (MPRT) has three steps (pp. 12–14):
  - an orthogonal projection matrix Y models rank (X = YX, tr Y ≤ k), "the matrix analog of binary variables" (p. 1; pp. 12–13);
  - the spectral regularizer tr f(X) is replaced by the trace of its matrix perspective (Definition 1, p. 10);
  - Y is relaxed to {0 ⪯ Y ⪯ I, tr Y ≤ k}, and the result can be written with semidefinite constraints.

  What changes from the MIO technique is the type of variable: projection matrices replace binaries, and an operator perspective replaces the scalar one. What stays is the order: take perspectives first, then relax the indicator to its convex hull (pp. 6–9, 12–14).

- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 1 shows that the projection form and the perspective form have the same optimal value. The proof reduces commuting X and Y to eigenvalues and the scalar perspective (Proposition 4; pp. 13–14).
  - Theorem 2 shows that the MPRT gives an extended formulation of the convex hull of {tr f(X) + µ rank X ≤ t, rank X ≤ k} (pp. 14–15). The proof projects Y onto the matrices that commute with X and invokes Günlük–Linderoth's convex hull for vectors (p. 15).
  - Corollaries give the convex hulls of the matrix-completion and D-optimal-design substructures (pp. 21, 24).
  - Left open: whether a condition weaker than matrix convexity keeps tr g_f jointly convex (p. 14).
