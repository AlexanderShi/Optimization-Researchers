# Batch s2-09 · Dimitris Bertsimas · supplement (round 2)

**Batch intent**: Eight supplement works, all from 2019. They sit at the hinge between his "modern optimization lens" years (2014–2019) and health care at scale (2019 on). The co-authors are ORC students (Berk, Weinstein, Yan, Ng, Korolko, Dunn, Wang, Li, Zhuo), a past collaborator (Nohadani), Silberholz (Michigan Ross) and clinical partners (Trikalinos and Steele at Brown; Boston Children's urologists; Vahdat). The works form four strands:
- ML method papers built on exact or robust MIO: sparse PCA (S184) and online allocation in clinical trials (S303).
- Robust and adaptive optimization run on a partner's data: staffing at Sapient (S152) and ambulances in Washington DC (S224).
- Robust statistics: robust maximum likelihood with a radiation-therapy case (S222).
- Clinical-journal papers: OCT against the PECARN head-trauma rules (S231), OCT for targeting VCUG after a first UTI (S281), and a meta-analytic data asset for metastatic breast cancer (S292).

Questions asked of each work:
1. Method 1: is the nominal class kept? If not, what exact device replaces the solver?
2. Methods 4–5: who is the incumbent (the field's deployed rule or a literature model), at what matched operating point, scored by decisions or by predictions? Are losing regimes printed (Heuristic 4)?
3. Method 3: how is the knob (Γ, α, ρ, set width) set: by judgment, cross-validation, an oracle or randomization?
4. Method 2: is the "why now" quantified, and on the tool actually used?
5. In the clinical papers: what does the paper say it cannot show?

### S152 · Prescriptive analytics for human resource planning in the professional services industry (European Journal of Operational Research 2019, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2018.06.035)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–6 (abstract, §§1–5, acknowledgments, references). First-page check: title and authors L. Berk, D. Bertsimas (corresponding), A. M. Weinstein, J. Yan match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Lauren Berk, Alexander M. Weinstein and Julia Yan, ORC (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: an RO/adaptive-optimization application with an industry partner (Publicis.Sapient grant, steering committee, p. 6), branded "prescriptive analytics"; Berk is also first author of S184 and Weinstein co-author of S303.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO AP}: Consulting firms must hire about one quarter ahead, before contracts close, on uncertain deal forecasts (pp. 1, 3). Prior work predicts sales probabilities or staffs from point estimates and buffers; Kawas et al. noted their model was highly sensitive to prediction quality (pp. 1–2). Stochastic optimization is set aside for the curse of dimensionality and for distributions hard to estimate without much data; hiring is conservative by nature, which suits worst-case models (p. 2). The gap claimed: no affinely adaptive RO model applied to HR planning (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three models of one decision.
  - A deterministic LP over requests × channels (bench, new hire, contractor) is shown equivalent to a greedy rule (Algorithm 1) that the authors expect mimics current ad hoc practice (pp. 2–3).
  - A robust version uses an ℓ1-norm set (p = 1) around the forecasts d with spread σ, after Bertsimas–Pachamanova–Sim 2004 (p. 3).
  - A two-stage affinely adaptive version hires now; contractors y(u), transfers z(u) and fulfilled demand w(u) are affine in the realized requests. Static second-stage variables recover the pure robust model (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Model and data**: Continuous staffing variables (part-time allowed) (p. 3). Contractors are assumed dearer than new hires; bench staff cost nothing extra (p. 2). The set is one standard deviation wide; two were judged over-conservative (p. 4). Data: one quarter of bill rates, labor costs and requests from a Sapient financial-services division. Transfer costs are modelled as travel plus training. Three business units are solved separately (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Greedy optimality by a feasible-direction argument (p. 3). Affine rules are adopted as the tractable restriction of fully adaptive policies (p. 3). The robust reformulation is delegated to JuMPeR (p. 4); the class of the counterpart is not stated. No theorems.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol: first-stage plans are fixed, then 500 simulated realizations per method with normal noise σ = 0.5d (a stand-in for forecast error), the second stage re-solved each time. Julia/JuMP/JuMPeR on a laptop (i7-6500U, 16 GB) (p. 4).
  - Run times: deterministic and robust ≤ 3.4 s; adaptive 1,327.1 s (North America) and 1,832.6 s (India) (Table 2, p. 5).
  - Profits (Table 3, p. 5): in North America the adaptive plan dominates (mean $14.05M against $12.66M deterministic and $12.76M robust). In Europe and India the deterministic plan keeps the highest mean (3.28 against 3.09 adaptive; 31.57 against 31.04); the adaptive plan buys a higher minimum and CVaR and a lower sd.
  - The robust plan hires only 4.3% of forecast demand in North America (p. 5). India's small differences are explained by low labor and transfer costs (pp. 5–6).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: an RO framework for the uncertain, multi-stage problem and a demonstration "through simulations on real data" (p. 2). Stated limits: a simplified two-stage timeline; multi-period and hiring-calendar extensions are described, not built (p. 4). Not stated by the authors (my reading):
  - The realizations are synthetic draws from the model's own noise family (p. 4).
  - The conclusion credits the adaptive plan with "performing well in expectation" (p. 6), but its mean beats the deterministic plan only in North America (Table 3, p. 5).
  - No deployment is reported.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ {AP RO}: partner data, a partner committee (pp. 4, 6) and a decision metric (profit distribution, CVaR) (p. 5) are present. But the baseline is the deterministic plan the authors expect mimics practice (p. 3), not an observed current rule, and there is no field test.
- Method 3 · evidence ✅: the knob (set width in standard deviations) is chosen against over-conservatism (p. 4). The bad case is printed: the robust plan loses mean and median profit in Europe and India (p. 5), and the null result in India is explained (pp. 5–6) (Heuristic 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Before building the robust model, show the nominal LP is solved by a simple greedy rule that mirrors practice. That rule becomes an interpretable baseline the robust and adaptive plans must beat (p. 3).
- (D5) Score a here-and-now plan by re-solving the recourse on many realizations. Report min, CVaR at 0.1, median, mean and sd per segment, next to run times (pp. 4–5).
- (D5) When a segment shows no gain, explain it by the cost structure that makes recourse cheap there (pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "This conservatism is well-suited to robust optimization, which considers worst-case cost rather than expected cost." (p. 2)
- "However, the robust method could be seen as over-conservative: although the median and mean robust profits are slightly better than the deterministic profits in North America, these metrics are significantly worse in Europe and India." (p. 5)

### S184 · Certifiably optimal sparse principal component analysis (Mathematical Programming Computation 2019, DOI 10.1007/s12532-018-0153-6)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–37 (abstract, §§1–6, Appendix A). The §4 tuning tables (pp. 19–24) were read in part; references (pp. 37–40) not read. First-page check: title and authors L. Berk, D. Bertsimas (corresponding) match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Lauren Berk, ORC (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the ML-under-MIO-lens programme (SW4), submitted March 2017 (p. 1); it places itself in the literature "reconsidering difficult problems in statistics and machine learning" through modern optimization (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML IO}: PCA loadings are dense, which hurts interpretability and variable selection, for instance in genetics with 10,000+ variables (p. 2). The authors judge that no method solves the ℓ0-constrained problem to provable optimality in practical time (p. 3). The prior art they map (pp. 4–6):
  - ℓ1 and convex relaxations; DSPCA stalls near n = 100.
  - Fast first-order methods with no certificate.
  - Exact attempts that are too slow (Moghaddam et al.: over an hour at n = 40, k = 20) or only local (Carrizosa–Guerrero).

  The why-now is the MIO speed-up: solvers about 1,250,000 times faster over 1990–2016, 2 trillion with hardware (p. 5). The ℓ0-constrained form is chosen over the penalized and ℓ1 forms: k is more interpretable than γ; solving every k solves the penalized problem, but not the reverse; ℓ1 shrinks loadings and loses variance (pp. 6–7).
- **D2 Core idea**: Optimal-SPCA, a custom branch-and-bound over support indicators. The model is SPCA-MIO (p. 8); nodes are pairs (l, u) of fixed supports (pp. 9–11).
  - Upper bounds: λmax of the masked matrix Q_u, the truncated trace, and Gershgorin column sums maximized over feasible supports through a truncation routine.
  - Lower bounds: eigenvector truncation, and Yuan–Zhang's truncated power iteration adapted to fixed supports (pp. 13–17).
  - Termination at ε-optimality (p. 11).
  - The general MINLP solver Couenne could not solve n = 13, k = 10 within an hour (p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Any PSD covariance on centered data, plus correlation-matrix versions (p. 26). Data: Pitprops (13 variables), Wine (13), MiniBooNE (50), Communities (101), breast-cancer gene expression (22,283 variables; random subsets up to 10,000) and Micromass (pp. 25–26, 33). Parameters were tuned on 500 draws of 80 of Communities' 101 variables (p. 18).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Correctness: the nodes are finite in {0,1}^{2n}, and only dominated or partitioned nodes are removed (Theorem 1, p. 12).
  - Bounds come from relaxing the support, the trace inequality and Gershgorin's theorem (pp. 14–16). Two 3×3 matrices show that the trace and Gershgorin bounds each dominate somewhere (p. 18).
  - Further components come only by deflation, with no joint optimality (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Quality: Optimal-SPCA reaches the certified optimum on every data set at k = 5 and 10, and every competitor misses at least one (Tables 6–7, pp. 26–28).
  - Speed: the eventual optimum is found within the competitors' times; proofs take up to 167 s (Communities, k = 10) (Table 10, p. 32).
  - Couenne 0.5 on the same SPCA-MIO often runs over an hour, with gaps up to 211,018%; Optimal-SPCA takes at most 339 s (Table 11, p. 34).
  - Scaling at k = 5: proofs for Cancer subsets up to 10,000 dimensions (1,595 s); 91–100% of runs proved within 1 h (Table 12, p. 34). Under early termination it ties Yuan–Zhang in 94.5–99% of runs (Table 13, p. 36).
  - Setup: a four-core 2.4 GHz machine with 16 GB, or a single cluster core; Julia 0.6 and no solver; competitors run from their authors' Matlab and R packages (p. 25). The authors note that language confounds the run times (p. 31).
  - Anomaly: Table 6 lists Jolliffe et al. at 0.41344 on Communities, k = 5, above the asterisked optimum 0.27689, and the text does not comment (p. 27).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: an exact algorithm class that controls sparsity and stays tractable at scale (p. 36), with released code (Zenodo DOI, p. 1; GitHub, pp. 36–37). Limits:
  - Stated: proof time grows with k (p. 31); multi-component optimality is not guaranteed (pp. 12, 29).
  - Seen in the setup: scaling is tested only at k = 5 (p. 33).
  - My reading: Communities serves both for tuning and for testing (pp. 18, 26). The abstract promises proofs "in seconds" for n in the hundreds and k in the tens (p. 1), while the body reports minutes at k = 10 (pp. 31–34).

  Future work: better node bookkeeping, reuse of eigenvalues as warm starts for lower nodes (p. 36), parallel branch-and-bound (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · evidence ✅ {ML IO}: the fastest incumbent heuristic (Yuan–Zhang) is both the node lower-bound routine and the strongest opponent (pp. 17, 25, 35–36). Certificates come from Theorem 1 (p. 12). Time-to-best is reported apart from time-to-proof, with the share of runs proved in 1 h and in 5 h (Table 12, p. 34); early-terminated runs are labelled as such (p. 35). Wins (each competitor fails somewhere, p. 26) and ties (Table 13, p. 36) are both stated. Code is released (p. 1).
- Method 2 · variant ⚠: the solver × hardware figure (1,250,000; 2 trillion, 1990–2016) is the why-now (p. 5). Optimal-SPCA itself uses no MIO solver (p. 25), and the general solver tested fails (pp. 33–34). The figure is borrowed, not measured on the tool used (Method 2's checkpoint).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: the exact formulation (SPCA-MIO, p. 8) is nonconvex and leaves the solver classes. In place of a class-keeping device, the paper builds a special-purpose exact algorithm from linear-algebra bounds (pp. 8–18).
- New-pattern candidate: *exact special-purpose algorithm when the formulation leaves solver classes* (pp. 8, 25, 33–34). S303 in this batch does the same with closed forms and enumeration.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) For a cardinality-constrained eigenvalue problem, branch on support indicators. Bound each node with cheap quantities: λmax of the masked matrix, the truncated trace, Gershgorin sums via one truncation routine. Take the minimum (pp. 13–18).
- (D4) Show that two bounds are non-redundant with two tiny matrices, each dominated by the other bound (p. 18).
- (D2) Run the fastest heuristic as the node lower-bound routine. The exact method then never does worse than the heuristic and adds a certificate (pp. 17, 25).
- (D5) Report time-to-best and time-to-proof separately, with the share of runs proved in 1 h and in 5 h. Run a general solver on the same formulation to justify a custom algorithm (Tables 11–12, p. 34).

**Quotes**:
- "These speedups encourage us to rethink whether NP-hardness should in fact be synonymous with intractability." (p. 5)
- "None of these methods provide a certificate of optimality, but are very useful as warm starts for exact methods." (p. 5)

### S222 · Robust maximum likelihood estimation (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2019, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2018.0834)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–15 (INFORMS cover, abstract, §§1–5, references). The online supplement (trust-region and robust-SA steps) is not in the file. First-page check: title and authors D. Bertsimas, O. Nohadani match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Omid Nohadani, Northwestern IEMS (p. 2), his co-author on robust local search and robust simulated annealing (2010; refs, p. 14) · **Position in trajectory**: an RO-meets-statistics paper, received January 2016 (p. 2); it reuses his 2010 nonconvex robust machinery on estimation.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO ML; HS as global search; AP}: MLE assumes complete, error-free data, yet measurement, input, implementation, numerical and model errors can erase its advantages (p. 2). Entry: maximize the worst-case likelihood over errors in an uncertainty set (eq. (1), p. 2). A table separates this from Huber's robust statistics: the worst case sits in the likelihood rather than in the estimator, and true data lie near observed data rather than the reverse (Table 1, p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: The adversarial robust estimator (ARE) maximizes Σ log f(θ; x_i − Δx_i) under the worst errors in per-sample ℓ2 balls of radius Γ (eqs. (4)–(5), p. 4).
  - Local computation: a Danskin gradient at the inner worst case (eq. (10), p. 5), inside the robust local search of Bertsimas–Nohadani–Teo 2010 (Theorem 1 restated, p. 5).
  - For multivariate normal data the inner problem separates into n trust-region problems (eq. (13), pp. 5–6); a projection keeps Σ positive semidefinite (p. 6).
  - Global computation: robust simulated annealing (p. 6).
  - The distributionally robust estimator (DRE), over distributions with a bounded support, collapses to one shared error vector. It is a special case of the ARE and therefore less conservative (eq. (16), pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Model and data**: Errors are assumed much smaller than the data so that estimators are identifiable; identifiability in general is out of scope; the model is semiparametric (p. 4). Simulation: n = 400 samples in R⁴ and 40 error matrices, normal errors and later errors uniform on a ball (pp. 7, 11). Clinical data: 491 delivered IMRT plans across 18 tumor sites, five DVH dose points, one protocol and one planning software (pp. 12–13). Normality is justified by the independence of patients (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): No new theorem. The descent-direction theorem and the convergence result are cited from the 2010 paper (p. 5). Ye's O(log log(1/ε)) trust-region method solves the inner problem (p. 6). Linear convergence is asserted for the first-order scheme (p. 6). The DRE-to-ARE reduction uses the shared support (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Simulation: robust log-likelihood beats nominal by up to 10% (ARE) and 15% (DRE) for ρ ≥ 1 (p. 8). A δ × ρ grid shows a window ("dip") where robust estimates are closer to the truth (pp. 9–10).
  - Robust SA beats a 100-start local search for δ > 0.3, at about 215 s against 100 s on a 3.4 GHz Xeon (p. 10). Uniform errors give similar trends (p. 11).
  - Factor analysis beats robust MLE on covariances by 0.6b in Frobenius norm (p. 11).
  - Clinical (3,000 subsamples of 50 plans, plus an 80-sample check): robust means are as stable as nominal up to ρ ≤ 2 Gy and insensitive to sample size. Σ_rob(D95, D2) has a narrower spread but is worse for ρ ≤ 0.8 (pp. 13–14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: two robust estimators, local and global algorithms, simulated and clinical demonstrations (p. 14). Stated limits:
  - identifiability (p. 4);
  - benefits only inside an error-size window (p. 12);
  - inferior to a correct parametric model (p. 12);
  - organs other than tumors deferred (p. 12).

  Not tested in the paper (my reading): the claimed savings in clinical-trial sample size (p. 14) and robustness to overfitting (p. 7). In the simulation, ρ* is picked by distance to the true estimators (p. 10), an oracle that real data do not provide.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅: one knob (Γ, scanned as ρ) with its effectiveness window printed at both failing ends (pp. 10, 12), plus a clinical region where robust is worse (ρ ≤ 0.8) (p. 14). Variant: in simulation the knob is set against the true data (p. 10), not from data alone.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: the robust MLE leaves convex classes. Tractability comes from separable per-sample trust-region problems and first-order outer steps, with SA for global search (pp. 5–6); no LP, conic or MIO counterpart.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: delivered clinical plans, with the institution's nominal estimators as baseline (p. 13). But the score is estimator spread, not decisions or outcomes; clinical value is argued (p. 14).
- Domain Map HS and Tension T2: robust SA serves as the global component and is printed as the costlier choice for large errors (p. 10). Heuristic 1: §2.4 calls RO a unifying justification for regularization (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Differentiate a max–min objective by Danskin's theorem: the gradient at the inner worst case only (eq. (10), p. 5).
- (D4) Exploit separability over samples: with a Gaussian likelihood and ℓ2-ball errors, each sample's worst case is a trust-region problem (pp. 5–6).
- (D5) Map a robust method's range of effectiveness on an error-size × set-size grid against ground truth. It coincides with nominal for small errors and fails for large ones; print both ends (pp. 9–10, 12).
- (D5) Pit the method against a correctly specified parametric competitor and print where the competitor wins (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "In data-driven applications, the analysis of the dip can be used reversely to motivate the appropriate size of the uncertainty set" (p. 10)
- "This demonstrates that although robust maximum likelihood estimation offers advantages to estimates of uncertain input data, it underperforms when the data-generating process is known and can be leveraged." (p. 12)

### S224 · Robust and stochastic formulations for ambulance deployment and dispatch (European Journal of Operational Research 2019, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2019.05.011)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–9 (abstract, §§1–5, Appendices A–B). For pp. 10–14 only the captions are in the file (the tables are images); references (p. 15) skimmed. First-page check: title and authors D. Bertsimas (corresponding), Y. Ng match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yeesian Ng, ORC (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: adaptive robust MIO in health operations; funded by ONR (p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO IO AP}: Tactical ambulance deployment in EMS (p. 1). Covering models ignore busy ambulances or assume a constant busy fraction (MEXCLP, MALP); site-specific versions are nonconvex and solved heuristically (pp. 1–2). Chance-constrained or robust static models restrict dispatch and turn over-conservative; fully adaptive models meet the curse of dimensionality (p. 2). Entry: column-and-constraint generation (C&CG, Zeng–Zhao 2013) now solves fully adaptive robust problems within minutes, and EMS data have improved. Can a static deployment under closest-idle dispatch beat the probabilistic models without repositioning (p. 2)?
- **D2 Core idea**: The recourse Q(x, d) is the minimum shortfall when dispatch within a period is optimized (eq. (1), p. 3); gradual coverage enters through a dummy location and edge costs φ (p. 3). Stochastic (sampled periods) and robust (worst case over D(α)) versions of min c'x + Q(x) follow (pp. 3–4).
  - D(α) bounds demand at four levels: single region, neighbourhood, station catchment and city. Each bound is a Poisson quantile at level α (eq. (9), p. 4).
  - Recourse duality turns the robust problem into an MIQP. Binary expansion of d and big-M linearization make it an MILP, solved by a C&CG master/oracle loop (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Model and data**: Integer deployment x and dispatch y. Demand is modelled as inhomogeneous Poisson counts; the Poisson choice is called not fundamental (negative binomial for overdispersion) (p. 4). No probabilistic guarantee is claimed, and α is set by cross-validation (pp. 4, 7). Data: 179,160 Washington DC EMS records (Jan 2012–Mar 2013, from a FOIA release); the first 3 months build the models, the other 12 evaluate them. Weekdays only; 217 regions; 10-minute drivetime coverage from OpenStreetMap (p. 6). Simulation: closest available ambulance, no chute time, lognormal turnaround in stable, normal and volatile regimes with a mean of 40.25 min (pp. 6–7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Proposition 1: any scenario lies in D(α) for α small enough (p. 4; proof p. 9). Proposition 2: recourse duality (p. 4; proof p. 9). Finite convergence of C&CG follows Zeng–Zhao (Propositions 3–4, pp. 6, 9).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Solve times (Gurobi 6.0, Julia/JuMP, MacBook Pro i5 2.6 GHz, 16 GB; p. 8): the stochastic deterministic equivalent with 500 scenarios takes about a minute and 1–2 GB. C&CG converges in fewer than 20 iterations (17 at 35 ambulances, α = 0.01), and all robust runs finish within 10 minutes.
  - Baselines: MEXCLP and MALP, with q cross-validated to 0.654 (p. 8). Fleets range over 10–50 ambulances, in peak and off-peak periods and three turnaround regimes (pp. 6–7).
  - At 50 ambulances, shortfalls are 3.7 and 3.53 against 4.36 and 4.89, about 22% fewer. Response times improve about 34.6% (peak) and 23% (off-peak) (p. 8).
  - Gains appear only when the busy fraction is below 1 (n ≥ 25 peak, n ≥ 15 off-peak), and stochastic ≈ robust (p. 8). The data-driven models need 35 ambulances to match what the probabilistic models reach with 50, about $7 million a year (pp. 8–9).
  - The per-fleet tables (E.1–E.8) are images whose shaded cells "should be ignored" (p. 13); they could not be checked from the file.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: tractable stochastic and robust formulations, a data-driven structured set, partial coverage and realistic experiments (p. 2). Stated limits:
  - the best α is city-specific, so no general prescription is given (p. 7);
  - the robust model saturates for α above 0.01 (p. 11);
  - closest-idle dispatch is assumed (p. 2; footnote p. 6).

  Future work: real-time redeployment on top (p. 9). Not stated: there is no field implementation, and the baselines are literature models, not the agency's current plan.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ {RO IO}: a two-stage robust problem with integer recourse is kept in MILP by duality, binary expansion and big-M, and solved exactly by C&CG with measured times (pp. 4–5, 8). The intermediate MIQP is called hard in general (p. 4).
- Method 3 · evidence ✅: one knob α scales all the quantile bounds (p. 4) and is set by cross-validation on held-out data (p. 7), the data-set knob that Method 3's correction asks for. Saturation is printed (p. 11).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: decisions are scored by simulated shortfall and response time on 12 held-out months and translated into money (pp. 6–9). The baselines are tuned literature models, not the agency's deployment, and there is no field test.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠: the why-now is an algorithmic advance (C&CG) plus better data (p. 2), not a measured solver × hardware figure.
- New-pattern candidate: *resource saved at matched performance*: 35 ambulances match the probabilistic models' 50 (p. 8). S303 and S231 in this batch do the same.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3) Build a data-driven polyhedral set by bounding demand at several aggregation levels with Poisson quantiles, one α scaling all of them. Bounds at only one level either concentrate adversarial demand or overstate the total (p. 4).
- (D4) For integer recourse: dualize the recourse, write integer scenarios as sums of binary vectors, linearize the bilinear terms with big-M, then run C&CG (pp. 4–5).
- (D5) Tune the baseline fairly: cross-validate the competing models' parameter on the same data. Train on the first months and test on the following year (pp. 6, 8).
- (D5) Use a load statistic (here the busy fraction) to say when the method helps (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "The approach is data-driven, and does not rely on model-driven assumptions to provide any probabilistic guarantees on the resulting coverage provided by the model." (p. 4)
- "This is expected as the stochastic and robust formulations consider the dispatch decisions, whereas the MEXCLP and MALP do not." (p. 8)

### S231 · Comparison of machine learning optimal classification trees with the pediatric emergency care applied research network head trauma decision rules (JAMA Pediatrics 2019, DOI 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2019.1068)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–8 (structured abstract, Methods, Results, Discussion, Limitations, Conclusions, article information). ### S303 · Covariate-adaptive optimization in online clinical trials (Operations Research 2019, DOI 10.1287/opre.2018.1818)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–13 (INFORMS cover, abstract, §§1–6, references). The e-companion (closed forms EC.2, proof EC.4, extra experiments) is not in the file. First-page check: title and authors D. Bertsimas, N. Korolko, A. M. Weinstein match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Nikita Korolko and Alexander M. Weinstein, ORC (p. 2); ONR funding (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the online sequel to the offline paper on optimization over randomization in small samples (Bertsimas, Johnson & Kallus, Oper. Res. 2015; pp. 2–3, reference list p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO IO; AP clinical trials}: Randomization risks accidental covariate imbalance (Efron 1971). The online state of the art is biased-coin designs and on-the-fly matching, while the offline MIO (OPT) needs all covariates up front (pp. 2–3). Entry: subjects arrive one by one, so the covariates of future subjects are treated as uncertain (p. 3). Stakes: $32.5 billion spent on clinical trials in 2010 (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: At each arrival, solve a robust MIO that minimizes the largest pairwise group discrepancy in means and approximate variances (eqs. (1)–(3), pp. 4–5).
  - Future covariates lie in an ellipsoid around the current sample mean and covariance, of radius Γ√((N−t)S).
  - The worst cases have closed forms (KKT conditions plus eigenvalues), so assignment is an enumeration over at most m scenarios. The complexity is 2^(mr), independent of N (pp. 3, 5).
  - CA-RO(r) aggregates r arrivals; CA-RO(N) equals OPT (pp. 5–6).
  - Defaults: Γ drawn uniformly from [0.5, 4] at each step, Γ = 0 near the end, ρ = 6 on a rescaled objective (pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Model and data**: N and k are known in advance, arrivals are exchangeable, covariates normalized (pp. 3–4). Data:
  - the Mayo Clinic PBC trial, 312 patients and 20 covariates, three of them chosen by logistic regression (p. 7);
  - synthetic response models NL, LIN and NR, with two N(0,1) covariates and noise sd 0.75 (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Closed-form robust counterparts (p. 5; details in the e-companion, not read). Power is tested by randomization under Fisher's sharp null (p. 8). Proposition 1 bounds the imbalance in unseen Lipschitz factors through matching problems (pp. 11–12; proof not read), extended by the Weierstrass theorem (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Balance: CA-RO has the lowest first- and second-moment imbalance among the online methods and is comparable on higher moments (Table 1, p. 7).
  - Power (N ∈ {40, 80, 120}, δ0 = 0.5, Q = 800, B = 500): no gain under NR, equal after regression adjustment under LIN, large gains under NL (p. 9).
  - Sample size for 80% power: 58 against 122 subjects per group at δ0 = 0.75, and 22 against 30 at 1.75, the best randomized method being Pocock–Simon (pp. 9–10).
  - Type I error of CA-RO(1): 6.5–7.1% at N = 40 and 4.5–5.6% at N = 120; Pocock–Simon's mean rate is uniformly higher (p. 10; Table 4, p. 11).
  - Assignments come in under one second (p. 6); no assignment sequence is more likely than 6% (p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a tractable online robust MIO, better balance, power at least as high in all tested scenarios, and protection against bias (p. 13). Stated limits:
  - certifiability trades against selection bias (pp. 10–11);
  - the distribution of allocations is not uniform (p. 12);
  - excluding outliers is left to future work (p. 6).

  My reading: gains concentrate in the nonlinear-response setting (p. 9); no trial was run, only simulations on historical and synthetic data; Type I error above 5% at N = 40 goes undiscussed (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ {AP RO}: the decision metric is power and sample size, measured against the randomization rules the field uses (p. 3) and turned into trial cost and duration (pp. 2, 9–10). No field test.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: duels the incumbents (pure randomization, Pocock–Simon, Atkinson, Antognini–Zagoraiou, Kapelner–Krieger) and prints where the method does not help (NR; LIN after adjustment) (p. 9). There is no warm start or certificate: enumeration is exact (p. 5).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: the robust model leaves commercial solver classes (p. 5). Exactness is kept by closed-form worst cases and an enumeration whose cost does not grow with N (pp. 3, 5).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: Γ is not priced but randomized, to keep randomization's protection against selection bias; a fixed Γ trades that for certifiability (pp. 5, 10–11).
- Taste mark 3 and the anti-pattern "randomization in small samples": evidence, in its online form (pp. 2–3). Heuristic 2: the paper is framed by the rival school's own criteria, Efron's three advantages of randomization (pp. 3, 10–12).
- New-pattern candidates: *randomized robustness parameter as a randomization device* (pp. 5, 10); *resource saved at matched performance* (Table 3, p. 10); *exact special-purpose algorithm when the formulation leaves solver classes* (p. 5), with S184.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Model future arrivals as uncertain parameters in an ellipsoid around the running sample mean and covariance. Solve the worst cases in closed form, then enumerate the current assignment (pp. 4–5).
- (D2) Randomize the robustness parameter at each step to keep randomization's bias protection; fix it when certifiability matters more (pp. 5, 10–11).
- (D5) Report power under several hidden response models, with both unadjusted and regression-adjusted estimators, so the regimes where the method does not help are visible (p. 9).
- (D7) Evaluate against the rival school's own criteria (Efron's freedom from selection bias, freedom from accidental bias, reasoned basis for inference) (pp. 3, 10–12).

- "However, post hoc regression adjustment using ordinary least squares, which exactly replicates the covariate-response model with additive treatment effect, reduces the need for the a priori covariate balance provided by CA-RO." (p. 9)
