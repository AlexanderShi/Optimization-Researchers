### S103 · Forecasting COVID-19 and analyzing the effect of government interventions (Operations Research 2023, DOI 10.1287/opre.2022.2306)
Read level: **full**: the medRxiv preprint version (posted 27 June 2021), pp. 1–34. Body pp. 1–27 read in full. References pp. 28–31 skimmed. Appendices A–C (pp. 32–34: per-country MAPE figure, policy-mapping Tables A1–A2, reopening figures) read; their figures carry no extractable values beyond the captions. The OR print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michael Lingzhi Li, Hamza Tazi Bouardi, Omar Skali Lami (MIT ORC), Thomas A. Trikalinos (Brown, Center for Evidence Synthesis in Health), Nikolaos K. Trichakis (MIT Sloan) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the DELPHI model paper of the 2020 COVID-19 programme. The model was created in late March 2020 (p. 2). The text was updated to mid-December 2020 (p. 2), posted in June 2021 and printed in 2023. SKILL.md (Honest Boundary) separates it from the 21-author paper (S099). Note 03 B4.7 records that the DELPHI repository's commits are all by students.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + DA; HS as the calibration engine}: The paper asks what government interventions did and "when society can return to normal" (abstract, p. 1). Two obstacles make this hard. Regions applied several policies at once, and the same policy works differently across demographics, density and culture (p. 2). The entry is to extend the classical SEIR model (Kermack–McKendrick 1927) with deaths, under-detection and changing interventions (p. 2). The innovation the paper names is an "explicit and parametric characterization of government interventions" (p. 2). It is an embedded curve fitted to the reported cases and deaths (pp. 4, 8), chosen because official policy and behaviour "on the ground" diverge (p. 4). Users are named up front: the CDC core ensemble, the Federal Reserve, Hartford HealthCare's ICU planning (p. 2) and Janssen's choice of Phase III trial sites (pp. 3, 21–23).
- **D2 Core idea**: Policy covariates are replaced by a fitted multiplier γ(t) on the infection rate. An arctan term captures the onset, strength and saturation of the response, and a Gaussian bump captures resurgence; the bump was added in late June 2020 (pp. 7, 11; four phases, Figure 2). Recovery and death are split into separate flows, so the mortality share becomes its own parameter instead of the artefact ν/(ν+τ) (p. 10; Figure 1c, p. 6). Biological constants are fixed from a meta-analysis of over 190 papers, and the rest is fitted per area (pp. 6, 8). The fitted γ(t) then serves as the policy lever in three ways. Averaged by policy category, it prices each intervention (pp. 17–20). Shifted, or held at γ(0), it gives two counterfactuals: interventions one week earlier, and no intervention at all (pp. 20–21). Rescaled by category ratios, it simulates reopening (p. 23). Relative to SEIR, the infection rate becomes time-varying, the model has 11 states, and under-detection and separate outcome flows are added. The compartment-ODE structure is kept (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Model and data**: An ODE system with 11 states in 8 groups, plus auxiliary counters (pp. 4–5).
  - Fixed values: Td = 2 days, Tβ = 5, Tσ = 10, Tκ = 15; detected share pd = 20%; hospitalized share ph = 15% (pp. 7–8).
  - Twelve parameters are fitted per area. The text's count does not add up: "11 parameters" plus 2 initial-state parameters gives "12", and only 10 symbols are listed (p. 8).
  - pd is fixed because it is unidentifiable early on and serology was sparse. The paper calls this a major simplification (the extraction drops the ligature) and points to the sensitivity analysis (pp. 9–10).
  - Testing data were left out because regressing γ(t) or pd on them significantly decreased empirical performance (p. 9).
  - Data: reported cumulative cases and deaths, with a rolling window of up to 4 months, excluding days before an area passed 100 cases (p. 8). Policy data come from the Oxford tracker (outside the US) and IHME (US) for 1 January–19 May 2020, mapped to five observed combinations (p. 19; Tables A1–A2, p. 33).
  - Assumptions admitted (pp. 26–27): constants are fixed and do not vary in time; there is no asymptomatic-transmission state; a nominal policy is taken as comparable across countries; a policy's effect when removed is taken to equal its effect when imposed.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Calibration is a nonconvex weighted least-squares problem. Residuals are weighted by t²/T², favouring recent data, and the death term carries λ = min{DT(T)/(3·DD(T)), 10}, "obtained experimentally" (p. 8).
  - The optimizer runs on two tracks: truncated Newton daily (Nocedal–Wright) and dual annealing weekly (Xiang et al. 1997, a generalized simulated annealing). The weekly run is there to re-adjust "if the underlying mechanics have changed" (pp. 8–9).
  - Policy-effect estimator: p_i = γ̄_i / γ̄_1, the mean fitted γ over the area-days under policy i, divided by the mean under no measure. The standard deviation treats each area as an independent sample (pp. 19–20). No identification argument is given. [inferred] γ(t) decreases in t apart from the bump (p. 7). If stricter categories tend to be in force later in the window, the category means mix policy with elapsed time. The text does not discuss timing or confounding (§4.1, pp. 17–20; §6, pp. 26–27).
  - Counterfactuals: t₀ is shifted by m days, or γ is held at γ(0) (pp. 20–21). For reopening, a correction proportional to p_j − p_i is scaled so that γ stays within [0, 2] (p. 23).
  - There are no proofs and no uncertainty intervals on the counterfactual totals (pp. 21–23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Implementation: Python 3.7 with SciPy and NumPy, multiprocessing, code on GitHub (p. 12). The model covered 167 areas in April and 216 in September (pp. 3, 15).
  - Retrospective out-of-sample tests: 15-day windows after 27 April and after 21 September. Table 2 gives median country-level MAPE and RMSE by region (p. 15). World figures: cases 5.8% and deaths 10.6% in the first window; 6.5% and 4.8% in the second. Death MAPE fell after the mortality-decay term was added (pp. 13–14).
  - Failures are printed. Table 1 lists eight areas with few deaths and death MAPEs of 44–193% (p. 14), explained by the SEIR class and by MAPE's penalty on small counts (p. 13).
  - Metric naming: the abstract reports a "two-week out-of-sample Median Absolute Percentage Error" (p. 1). The body reports the median over areas of each area's Mean Absolute Percentage Error (p. 13; Table 2 caption, p. 15).
  - Comparison with the CDC models: DELPHI is set against seven models consistently included in the CDC ensemble (UT-Mobility, IHME-CurveFit, YYG, MOBS-GLEAM, PSI-DRAFT, LANL, NotreDame). The target is US deaths 4 weeks ahead, scored on the actual weekly submissions from July to September by MAPE and weekly rank. DELPHI's MAPE stayed at or below 3.5%; it ranked first in 6 of 13 weeks and never below 4th of 8 (pp. 14–16). The authors chose the metric (MAPE of point forecasts) and the window ("the majority of the period of resurgence", p. 15). Intervals are not scored, and the paper does not say whether the target is cumulative or incident (p. 14).
  - Sensitivity: each of the six fixed parameters is perturbed by N(0, 0.2·|θ|) and the model refitted. The 30-day MAPE is compared with that of the prediction actually published on that date, over all US states plus six countries at three dates (57 points per box). The interquartile range stays mostly within ±5% (pp. 16–18).
  - Scenario outputs: one week earlier would have avoided more than 280,000 deaths (68%) by 17 May; no intervention would have meant more than 14.8 million deaths (p. 21). Further outputs: reopening in France and Brazil, with up to 6.8 million extra Brazilian cases by 15 July (p. 23), and three country clusters (p. 24).
  - Not reported: run times, hardware, annealing settings, parameter uncertainty.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places DELPHI in the SEIR family and its refinements: age and severity strata, mobility and policy covariates (Woody et al., Chinazzi et al.), and non-mechanistic deep-learning and time-series forecasters (pp. 3–4). A full review is deferred to Dean et al. (p. 4). It sets itself apart by features rather than criticism: an embedded curve instead of policy data, a joint fit of cases and deaths "in contrast to" IHME's separate models, and "consistent projections on all 6 populated continents" (p. 4). Rivals are met on the CDC's leaderboard (p. 14). The limits are framed through Holmdahl and Buckee's note on SEIR models, "Wrong but useful" (pp. 27, 29). The parameter source is the group's own clinical data set (Bertsimas et al. 2020, pp. 6, 28).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract stacks headline numbers: 6% and 11% error, 14 million deaths, 280,000 deaths, and effects of 29.9%, 17.3% and 74.4% (p. 1). The introduction pairs each contribution with an adoption claim (CDC "top 4", Federal Reserve, Hartford, Janssen) (pp. 2–3).
  - Model changes are logged "for full transparency" (p. 6). A separate Limitations section lists concrete items (pp. 26–27). Hedges appear: "seems to predict ... relatively well" (p. 13); the sensitivity result "comforts us in some of our core hypotheses" (p. 17).
  - Effects are stated causally in the abstract ("reduced infection rates", p. 1) but computed as ratios of averaged fitted multipliers (pp. 19–20).
  - Several headlines go beyond the body:
    - DELPHI "successfully predicted the large-scale epidemics in many areas months before" (p. 1). The worked example spans about one month (17 April to 12 May, p. 12). §6 concedes that DELPHI cannot detect new waves before they occur (p. 27).
    - DELPHI "consistently outperforms all other models" (p. 15), in the same sentence as a first rank in 6 of 13 weeks (p. 15).
    - Most early deaths, "up to 90%", avoidable (p. 3), against 68% worldwide (p. 21).
    - The school-closure effect is ±6.7 in the abstract (p. 1) and ±6.6 in the body (p. 20).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a model, a forecasting record, a ranking of interventions, counterfactual and reopening tools, and use by agencies and a company (pp. 2–3, 27). The stated limits are concrete (pp. 26–27):
  - constants are fixed;
  - the arctan mortality curve cannot follow the December 2020 rise;
  - asymptomatic transmission is left out for lack of reliable data;
  - small populations;
  - long horizons and new waves;
  - cross-country comparability of policies;
  - permanent behaviour change after reopening.
  Future work: smoothed testing data (p. 10), an exponential bump for mortality (p. 26), and an asymptomatic state once data allow (p. 27). Unstated: causal identification of the policy effects, uncertainty on the counterfactual totals, and any outcome measure for the partners' decisions.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2–3, 13–16, 21–23): owners and decisions are named (Hartford's ICU capacity, Janssen's trial sites, the CDC ensemble), but predictions are scored (MAPE, RMSE), not decisions. No partner outcome is reported; the Janssen use is called a "successful deployment" without a measure (p. 23). The comparison with the CDC models uses the authors' metric and window, not the hub's. This is the self-chosen scoring that the skill's Method 5 correction targets (05 §5.2: relative WIS 0.99 in the hub's own evaluation, S021, not read here).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 4–11): the problem belongs to epidemiology, and the group enters by re-specifying that tradition's own model (SEIR → DELPHI) with a parametric lever. It is not an LP, conic or MIO formulation. The optimization is only the nonconvex calibration, with no certificate (pp. 8–9).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 19–20): Table 3 is in effect a price list for stringency. Residual infection rates fall from 100% (no measure) through 88.9% and 59.0% to 41.7% and 25.6% (stay-at-home), and the social cost is named as the other side of the "dilemma" (p. 20). But there is no single knob, no bound and no priced cost.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 12, 21–23): the impact gate's adoption proxies are met: CDC ensemble, Federal Reserve, Hartford HealthCare, Janssen, a public website, code on GitHub. The Janssen deployment has its own applied paper (INDEX S428, not read), which fits the pattern of "one applied paper per deployment".
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 13–14, 26–27): the failure table and a concrete limitations section are in the body, and the weekly forecasts were submitted prospectively to the CDC (p. 14). The scoring in this paper, however, is the authors' own.
- Tension T2 · evidence ✅ (pp. 8–9): dual annealing, a generalized simulated annealing, is the weekly global optimizer in daily production use.
- Tension T6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 3, 12, 15, 20–21, 27): headlines on foresight ("months before"), dominance and avoidable deaths go beyond what the body shows.
- New-pattern candidate: *a parametric lever inside a mechanistic model, with counterfactuals made by editing the lever*. Fit one interpretable curve (γ(t)) and answer policy questions by averaging, shifting or rescaling it (pp. 7, 19–23).
- New-pattern candidate: *two-track recalibration*: a cheap local refit every day and a global search every week, to catch regime changes (pp. 8–9; consistent with the annealing-versus-TNC comparison recorded in note 03 B4.7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) When a compartment model fixes a quantity as an artefact of two rates (here the mortality share ν/(ν+τ)), split the compartment so that the quantity becomes a parameter of its own (p. 10; Figure 1c, p. 6).
- (D4) For a nonconvex model refitted every day, run a fast local method daily and a global method weekly, and say which runs when (pp. 8–9).
- (D4) Weight recent residuals by t²/T² and balance two endpoints with a capped ratio, and state that the constants were set by experiment (p. 8).
- (D5) Sensitivity protocol: perturb each fixed parameter by N(0, 0.2·|θ|), refit, and compare the out-of-sample error with the archived prediction actually published that day (pp. 16–17).
- (D5) Print a failure table that shows the worst areas next to the counts that explain the failure (Table 1, p. 14).

### S110 · Predictive performance of multi-model ensemble forecasts of COVID-19 across European nations (eLife 2023, DOI 10.7554/eLife.81916)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–20. p. 1 is the DiVA cover sheet; the body (pp. 2–15), funding and author contributions (pp. 15–17) and the supplement and data statements (p. 18) were read; references (pp. 18–20) skimmed. Plotted values in Figures 1–4 are not in the text. Supplementary file 2 (team metadata) is not part of the file, so the MIT team's model and its scores cannot be read here.
**Role**: core, in the organizers' voice · **Coauthors**: 129 authors (my count, p. 2). Katharine Sherratt (LSHTM) did the conceptualization, analysis and first draft, and Sebastian Funk supervised (pp. 16–17). Bertsimas is the 58th author, listed as "Bertsimas Dimitris" next to Michael Lingzhi Li and Soni Saksham (MIT, affiliation 27; pp. 2–3). His role is "Methodology, Writing – review and editing", the same line as nearly all contributing modellers (p. 17). · **Position in trajectory**: one of several forecast-hub consortium papers on which his group appears as a contributing team. The references list two German/Polish hub papers with "Bertsimas D" among the authors (Bracher et al. 2021b, 2021c, p. 18 = INDEX S294, S157); the US hub evaluation is S021. None of these is his method.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP; DA only in that forecasts serve policy users; no optimization}: Varied methods and formats make forecasts hard to compare. This "confounds the selection of a single representative forecast" and weakens the evidence behind decisions (p. 5). A hub standardises targets and quantiles so that forecasts can be compared like for like and combined (pp. 5–6). The question is how ensembles of COVID-19 case and death forecasts performed in 32 European countries from 8 March 2021 to 7 March 2022 (pp. 3, 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: A public, prospective, standardized hub. Teams submit weekly forecasts for 1–4 weeks ahead in up to 23 quantiles, and the submissions pass automated validation (p. 6). Each week the hub forms an ensemble by averaging the models' forecasts quantile by quantile with equal weights. It used the mean at first and the median from 26 July 2021, after anomalous forecasts produced "extremely wide uncertainty" in the mean (p. 6). Mean and median ensembles, unweighted or weighted by inverse relative WIS, are then compared retrospectively (pp. 7–9).
- **D3 Model and data**: Targets are weekly incident cases and deaths per country, taken from Johns Hopkins University and aggregated over MMWR weeks (p. 6). Participation was open (p. 6). Of 48 models, 29 were evaluated. Five of these were left out of the ensemble for incomplete quantiles, and no weekly ensemble had fewer than 3 models (p. 9). Forecasts made around data points later revised by more than 5% are removed (p. 7). The baseline holds the last observation constant, with widening uncertainty (p. 7). Only relative performance is reported; absolute scores are on the hub's website (pp. 6–7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (no proofs; the scoring and ensemble devices):
  - The weighted interval score (WIS) is computed over all quantiles; the paper notes it is a strictly proper scoring rule (p. 7).
  - Relative WIS comes from a pairwise tournament. Mean score ratios are computed on the targets two models share, then combined by geometric mean, and the result is scaled by the baseline's score (p. 7).
  - Calibration is checked by coverage of the 50% and 95% intervals (p. 7).
  - The ensembles average quantiles (Genest 1992), with weights 1/S_i normalized and a Harrell–Davis weighted median (pp. 7–9).
  - No formal test of score differences is run; the Diebold–Mariano test is named and set aside (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - There are 2139 forecast scores (p. 9).
  - Overall: the ensemble's median scaled relative WIS is 0.71 (IQR 0.61–0.88), against 1.04 (0.82–1.36) for all models (p. 10). It beats 83% of case scores (N = 886, 23 models) and 91% of death scores (N = 763, 20 models) (p. 10).
  - By horizon: case scores worsen from 0.62 at one week to 0.9 at four; death scores move only from 0.69 to 0.76. Case forecasts grow overconfident beyond one week, while death forecasts are well calibrated at the 95% level (p. 10).
  - By country: for cases, the ensemble beats 75% of models in 22 countries and all models in 3. For deaths, the counts are 30 and 8 (p. 10).
  - Ensemble methods (p. 10; Table 1, p. 12): the text reports that the median beat the mean for every horizon and target except 1-week cases; in the table, the unweighted pair ties at 0.69 for 2-week cases. The unweighted median is equal to or better than the weighted median in every row. Weighting improves the mean for deaths, where the unweighted mean scores 1.81 at one week.
  - Limits stated (pp. 13–14): no statistical test; dependence on the metric and the baseline; data revisions in an aggregated source; method descriptions collected without a rigorous framework, and changes to a model over time not documented.
- **D6 Positioning**: Hubs come from climate modelling and were then used for dengue, US influenza and US COVID-19, with German/Polish hubs alongside (p. 5). The infrastructure is built to be compatible with those hubs (p. 6). On weighting the evidence is mixed: Taylor and Taylor against Bracher et al. (pp. 8, 13). The median result agrees with Brooks and with Ray et al. (pp. 8–9, 13). The paper presents itself as confirming earlier findings ("Our results support previous findings", p. 13).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract is structured (Background / Methods / Results / Conclusions), and every percentage carries its N (pp. 3–4). The editor's evaluation is printed (pp. 4–5). Claims are calibrated: the ensemble was "not strictly dominant" (p. 13), and the limitations get their own paragraph (pp. 13–14). Policy use is reported through the ECDC's weekly threat reports (p. 14). An EPIFORGE reporting checklist, an MDAR checklist, and open data and code are supplied (p. 18).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: infrastructure, an open data set and an evaluation. Practical advice: use median ensembles, and put more weight on death forecasts than on case forecasts beyond 2 weeks (p. 4). Limits are as in D5. Future work: link performance to trends and turning points; add vaccination, variant and policy data; try trimming and quantile-regression averaging; forecast hospitalisations; model longer-term scenarios (pp. 14–15).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 6–7, 10, 13–14): here the judge is external and the metric is the field's. Time-stamped prospective forecasts are scored by relative WIS against a persistence baseline, and coverage is checked. The owner's rule and the decision metric that Method 5 asks for are absent, because these are forecasts, not decisions. This is the regime the skill's Method 5 correction asks for ("score forecasts on the field's metric"). Set against S103's self-chosen MAPE comparison, this paper is evidence for the correction, not for his own practice.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 6, 14, 17): his team's forecasts sit on an external, prospective scoreboard built to "avoid retrospective claims of performance" (p. 14). The text does not name the team's model or report its score.
- New-pattern candidate: *consortium co-authorship as a contributing modeller* (pp. 2, 17, 18). In the COVID-19 period his group joined hubs run by others, and the resulting co-authorships do not carry his voice; see also Bracher et al. 2021b/c (p. 18) and INDEX S021, S063, S157 and S294 (not read). For the evidence ledger, treat such papers as external evaluation of his group's work.
- Cross-card note: S103 says DELPHI was "continuously updated", with the changes documented (S103 p. 6). This hub says it "did not document whether modellers changed the methods" of a submitted model over time (S110 p. 14). [inferred] A model scored by a hub is therefore not one fixed method: the hub's scores mix the versions a team ran over the period.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Score probabilistic forecasts with a proper score (WIS). When participation is partial, make the scores comparable with the pairwise-tournament relative WIS, scaled by a persistence baseline (p. 7).
- (D5) Before scoring, drop forecasts made around data points that later releases revised by more than 5% (p. 7).
- (D2) Combine component forecasts by the median, not the mean, when some components can be anomalous. Weighting by past scores did not reliably help (pp. 6, 10, 12–13).
- (D5) Archive time-stamped forecasts before the outcome is known, so that out-of-sample claims can be checked (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "Collating time-stamped predictions ensures that we can test true out-of-sample performance of models and avoid retrospective claims of performance." (p. 14)
- "we did not document whether modellers changed the methods for a particular submitted model over time." (p. 14)

### S150 · Dynamic optimization with side information (European Journal of Operational Research 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2022.03.030; arXiv 1907.07307)
Read level: **full**: arXiv v2 (arXiv:1907.07307v2, 21 Jul 2020; first submitted May 2019 and revised May 2020, p. 1). The main text (pp. 1–29) and endnotes (p. 30) were read in full; references (pp. 30–32) skimmed. The electronic companion (pp. 33–42 = ec1–ec10) was **skimmed**: the theorem statements and proof structure of EC.1–EC.4 were read, but the displayed inequalities were not checked line by line. The EJOR print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Christopher McCord and Bradley Sturt (MIT ORC, p. 1), both in the DA lineage that SKILL.md lists among his students · **Position in trajectory**: the paper merges two student lines of the DA programme. One is McCord's multistage predictions-to-prescriptions (Bertsimas and McCord 2019 = INDEX S193). The other is Sturt's sample robust optimization (Bertsimas, Shtern and Sturt 2018a = S113; 2019 = S163). The paper builds on Bertsimas and Kallus 2020 (S008) and cites Bertsimas and Van Parys 2017 (S139) for robust weights (pp. 2–4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + RO; ML supplies the weights; SD as the multistage setting}: Decisions are made over several periods after side information is observed once (pp. 1–2). The examples are a fashion retailer that knows a new item's brand, style and colour, and a fund manager who sees the monthly CPI and BLS releases. The goal is a policy that minimizes the conditional expected cost (1), learned only from i.i.d. historical tuples (γ, ξ₁, …, ξ_T), with no parametric structure (p. 2). Two gaps. First, single-period prescriptive methods do not carry over: for T ≥ 2 the weighted SAA (2) becomes "anticipative", which the paper calls "a form of overfitting", even with big data (Example 1, p. 3; endnote 1, p. 30). Second, scenario-tree methods (Ban et al. 2019; his own Bertsimas and McCord 2019) are asymptotically optimal but "can require hours or days" for ten or fewer periods (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Robustify the ML-weighted SAA. Put an ℓp ball of radius ε_N around each historical sample path, and minimize the ML-weighted average of the worst-case costs (eq. 4, p. 9). The weights can come from kNN, kernel regression, CART or random forests (Definitions 1–4, pp. 9–10). This combines the weights of prescriptive analytics with sample robust optimization (pp. 3–4). Table 1 arranges four methods in a 2×2 (p. 22):
  - SAA: ε = 0, equal weights.
  - Bertsimas and Kallus: ε = 0, ML weights.
  - Bertsimas et al. 2018a: ε > 0, equal weights.
  - This paper: ε > 0, ML weights.
  For tractability, a multi-policy approximation gives each uncertainty set and stage its own auxiliary linear decision rule. Each rule need only be "locally accurate" (pp. 20–21). Kept from earlier work: the predictions-to-prescriptions (PtP) weights, the SRO uncertainty sets and the linear decision rules of adaptive RO.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Setting: a finite horizon, with side information observed before the first decision and fixed, or slowly varying, over the horizon (p. 2). The data are i.i.d. "for the purpose of proving theorems" (p. 8).
  - Assumption 1 (p. 11) covers kNN with k_N = min(⌈k₃N^δ⌉, N − 1) and δ ∈ (1/2, 1), or Gaussian, triangular or Epanechnikov kernels with h_N = k₄N^(−δ). Both use ε_N = k₁/N^p, with p bounded through d_γ and d_ξ. The guarantee therefore covers kNN and kernel regression only. The abstract also lists random forests (p. 1), and SRO-CART is used in the experiments (p. 26).
  - Assumptions 2–4 (p. 13): conditional sub-Gaussianity; conditional distributions Lipschitz in γ under d₁; a compact Γ with a small-ball lower bound. Each comes with a gloss in words.
  - Assumptions 5–6 are growth bounds and upper semicontinuity. The authors call them a "minor modification" of Bertsimas et al. 2018a and "nearly identical" to Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn (pp. 17–18).
  - The approximation is restricted to costs of form (15): linear stage costs plus an LP recourse, which is convex in ξ, so even the worst case is hard to evaluate (p. 20). EC.4's reformulation further assumes no constraints on the decisions and nonnegative uncertainty (pp. 41–42).
  - The experiments use synthetic data from published generators (pp. 23, 25, 28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 2 is the new result: the ML-weighted empirical conditional distribution comes within ε_N of P_γ̄ in type-1 Wasserstein distance, almost surely for all large N (p. 13). Three terms are split off by the triangle inequality (p. 14):
    - Term 1 is bounded through the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual, Jensen, Assumption 3 and the locality (8) of the weights (p. 14).
    - Term 2 is bounded by truncation to a growing box B_N, with a Gaussian tail inequality (p. 15).
    - Term 3: the 1-Lipschitz functions are approximated by piecewise-constant functions on a dyadic partition (|H_l| = (2^(l+1)+1)^(2^(l·d_ξ))). Then a union bound, Hoeffding's inequality with the moment-generating-function bound (9) on the weights, and summability by comparison with 1/N² (Borel–Cantelli) (pp. 16–17).
    The authors call this a "novel technique" (p. 13).
  - Theorem EC.1: kNN and kernel weights satisfy (5)–(9), by binomial tails, Hoeffding and Borel–Cantelli (pp. 33–35).
  - Theorem 1: the robust problem (4) is asymptotically optimal for (1) over general decision rules (p. 11). The lower half adapts a lemma of Bertsimas et al. 2018a to weighted measures (Lemma EC.1). The upper half follows Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn, using Lipschitz approximations and monotone convergence. The link is SRO = type-∞ Wasserstein DRO (pp. 18, 35–39).
  - Theorem 3: single-period Wasserstein DRO around the conditional empirical measure is asymptotically optimal. The proof is Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn's with Fournier–Guillin's concentration result swapped for Theorem 2 (p. 19). Tractability is unchanged, with 1/N replaced by w_N^i (p. 20).
  - Theorem 4: the multi-policy problem (16) equals (4) for costs of form (15), proved by two inequalities (p. 21; EC.3, pp. 39–40). Restricting to linear decision rules gives an upper bound and feasible policies (p. 21).
  - EC.4 turns the result into a deterministic conic program. Strong duality turns each maximum over a norm ball into a dual norm; zero blocks keep the rules non-anticipative; the rules need O(d_ξ·max{d_x, N·d_y}) variables (pp. 40–42).
  - Not provided: rates of convergence. There is no bound on the multistage approximation gap; the two-stage counterpart, where the gap goes to zero, is credited to Bertsimas et al. 2019 (p. 6). No lower bound or gap certifies the approximation.

- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Two literatures frame the paper. One is data-driven DRO: Delage and Ye; Xu, Caramanis and Mannor; Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn; Van Parys et al. The other is prescriptive analytics: Hannah et al.; Ban and Rudin; Bertsimas and Kallus; Ho and Hanasusanto; Elmachtoub and Grigas (pp. 2–3, 5).
  - The rivals for (1) are Ban et al. 2019, which needs a correctly specified parametric model, and his own Bertsimas and McCord 2019. Both are faulted for scenario-tree enumeration (p. 3).
  - Credit is stated openly: the final steps "draw heavily from" Mohajerin Esfahani and Kuhn and from Bertsimas et al. (p. 5); assumptions are borrowed (p. 18); Theorem 3 swaps one lemma (p. 19).
  - The closest approximation schemes are Chen, Sim and Xiong 2020 and Bertsimas, Shtern and Sturt 2019 (p. 6).
  - The group's own methods frame the paper. Table 1 casts three of them as special cases (p. 22), and the paper says it "unifies our understanding of these disparate models" (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract runs: approach → ML weights → new concentration result → asymptotic optimality → approximation → "up to 15%" and "less than one minute ... twelve stages" (p. 1).
  - Concrete examples come before notation (pp. 1–2), and Example 1 shows the obvious extension failing (p. 3). The contributions are summed up "in a nutshell" (p. 3) and listed as (a)–(f) (pp. 4–5), with "first" claims (pp. 4, 5, 6).
  - The paper puts the practitioner first: it says what has to be chosen before it states the technical assumptions (p. 11), and it explains each assumption in words (p. 13).
  - Hedges appear only in the results ("We believe", "typically", p. 26). The conclusion restates the claims, with no limitations and no future work (p. 29).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: theory (Theorems 1–4), a method (SRO with side information plus the multi-policy approximation with linear decision rules) and experiments on three problems (pp. 4–5, 29). No limitation is stated as such; the restrictions sit inside the assumptions (pp. 11, 13, 17–18, 20, 41). Unstated:
  - the guarantee covers only kNN and kernel weights;
  - side information is observed only at time 0, whereas the competitors also handle side information that arrives over time (p. 3);
  - there are no convergence rates;
  - the multistage approximation has no gap bound;
  - the data are synthetic only;
  - solver, hardware and code are not reported.
  No future work is stated (p. 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To prove Wasserstein concentration of a weighted empirical measure, split the distance by the triangle inequality into a bias term (Lipschitz in the covariates), a truncation term (sub-Gaussian tails) and a fluctuation term. Handle the fluctuation term by approximating 1-Lipschitz functions on a dyadic partition, then apply a union bound, Hoeffding and Borel–Cantelli (pp. 14–17).
- (D4) Reuse an existing asymptotic-optimality proof by swapping its concentration inequality for your new one, and state which step was swapped (Theorem 3, p. 19).
- (D2) When a data-driven multistage estimator becomes anticipative, put a small uncertainty ball around each sample path and optimize the weighted worst case. Show the failure at ε = 0 in your own table (pp. 3, 9; Table 2, p. 24).
- (D4) Approximate a recourse cost that is convex in the uncertainty with one auxiliary linear decision rule per uncertainty set ("multi-policy"); each rule need only be accurate locally. Prove that the unrestricted version is exact, then reformulate by conic duality (pp. 20–21, 40–42).
