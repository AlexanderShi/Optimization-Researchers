### S403 · Multistage stochastic optimization via kernels (arXiv preprint 2023, arXiv 2303.06515)
Read level: **partial**. The main text, pp. 1–22, was read in full: abstract, §§1–8, Algorithms 1–2, Assumptions 1–3, Theorem 1, Corollary 1, Tables 1–3 and the figure captions. From the appendices I read the statements of Lemmas 6–7 and Theorem 2 with the opening of its proof (pp. 29–31) and Appendix D on lower bounds (pp. 31–32). Not read: Appendix A (the RKHS overview), the lemma proofs of Appendix B and the references. First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Kimberly Villalobos Carballo match; the file is arXiv v1 of 11 March 2023, set in a Mathematical Programming manuscript template (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Kimberly Villalobos Carballo (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the prescriptive-analytics line (SW5) carried into multistage problems. It follows the group's sample robust optimization (Bertsimas et al. 2022b), its covariate version (Bertsimas and McCord 2019) and the single-period kernel paper of Bertsimas and Koduri (2022) (pp. 2–4). The authors declare no funding (p. 22).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA SD; ML as engine}: The problem is multistage stochastic optimization with side information, in which decisions do not affect the uncertainty (abstract, p. 1). The entry is the limit of the group's own earlier methods. Those methods need scenario-tree enumeration and can take hours for a few stages (p. 2). Local kernel methods need more data and do worse in high dimension (p. 3). The Representer-Theorem formulation of Bertsimas and Koduri carries one parameter per data point, with time and memory cubic in the data size (pp. 3–4). The goal: decision rules that are learned globally, stay tractable as the data grow, and are asymptotically optimal (pp. 2, 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Each stage's decision rule is an element of a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) generated by a universal kernel. Its domain is augmented with the covariates, so that one unconditional problem replaces the conditional one (p. 5). The regularized empirical loss (3) is minimized by functional stochastic gradient descent (FSGD) (pp. 6–8). After each step, kernel orthogonal matching pursuit (KOMP) drops the dictionary point whose removal changes the function least, until the Hilbert-norm error would exceed ε (Algorithm 1, pp. 9–10). SMOK, the resulting algorithm, returns the best iterate by empirical loss (Algorithm 2, pp. 10–11). Relative to the group's earlier work, the decision rule itself is learned (a "global" method), instead of reweighting samples or fitting linear rules over uncertainty sets (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Model and data**: Convex losses; independent and identically distributed sample paths with no assumption on the correlation across stages (pp. 2, 6). The theorem assumes a compact data space with bounded universal kernels (A1), a Lipschitz loss (A2), and convexity and differentiability, relaxable to subgradients (A3) (p. 11). Constraints enter only through a quadratic penalty ψ, and "feasibility is not guaranteed"; decisions were projected onto the feasible set before evaluation (p. 14). Data: synthetic inventory paths whose demands are linear in truncated-Gaussian covariates plus noise (p. 15), and a reproduced synthetic shipment-planning problem (p. 20).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Theorem 1: with constant step η and pruning tolerance ε = P2η², the expected excess regularized loss is O(η/λ) (p. 12). Corollary 1: with η = √(P1/N), λ → 0 and λ√N → ∞, the expected loss converges in L1, hence in probability, to the optimum over continuous rules, at rate O(1/(λ√N)) (p. 12). The device is the standard SGD descent inequality, with the projection error entering as a bias of order ε (Lemma 6, p. 29); a penalty lemma gives feasibility as ψ → ∞ (Lemma 7, p. 30). Complexity: a covering number that depends on η and P2 but not on N bounds the dictionary, which gives worst-case O(NM³) time and O(NM²) space, against Ω(N²) without pruning; that covering number has no known closed form (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Python 3 and NumPy on a 4-core i7-8557U laptop with 16 GB (p. 15). In a 10-period inventory problem with 10-dimensional data and controls and N = 2,000, SMOK reaches loss 491.30 in 0.3 h with 1.5 × 10³ parameters, against 493.74 in 0.1 h for unpruned FSGD (MOK) and 496.04 in 14.36 h for SAA-knn; SRO and SRO-knn were intractable at this size (Table 1, p. 16). Sweeps over periods, data size, data dimension and control dimension against affine decision rules (ADR): SMOK and MOK sit near the lower bounds, which exist for T ≤ 5 only, ADR "results in very poor performance", and SMOK keeps fewer than 15 parameters (pp. 16–19). In shipment planning, MOK has the highest profit and SMOK the second; SMOK's time stays at 150–260 s while SRO-knn reaches 54,000 s at N = 5,000, though SAA-knn is the fastest throughout (Tables 2–3, p. 21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Claimed as the first tractable RKHS approach to multistage problems with large data (p. 4). Printed limits: decisions must not affect the uncertainty (pp. 1, 21); feasibility comes only from the penalty (p. 14); the covering number is unknown (p. 13); a constant step trades accuracy against memory (p. 12). All data are synthetic (pp. 15, 20). The conclusion lists no future work (pp. 21–22).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13–21): decisions, not predictions, are scored out of sample (loss, profit), as in step 5. But the baselines are the group's own SRO, SRO-knn and SAA-knn and the textbook ADR, not an owner's current rule, and the data are synthetic (pp. 15, 20). Step 4 is varied: the decision rule itself is learned globally, instead of ML weights inside a sample average (pp. 2–3).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 16–17, 31–32): the "honest certificate" moves out of MIO. An optimality gap is reported against an analytic lower bound, obtained by dropping two constraints found "quite lose" and solving the rest with the newsvendor fractile under normal noise (Appendix D). The bound exists only because the data-generating model is known, and only for T ≤ 5 (p. 16).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 6–13): a problem owned by the scenario-tree tradition is rewritten as kernel learning, not as an LP or MIO. Tractability comes from pruning rather than from a class-keeping device, and the boundary is printed (penalized constraints, unknown covering number).
- New-pattern candidate: *own earlier result's limit as the entry* (pp. 2–4): the group's SRO and SRO-knn (hours, scenario trees) and the Bertsimas–Koduri kernel formulation (cubic growth) are the named limits.
- New-pattern candidate: *bound-referenced quality yardstick for heuristics and policies* (pp. 16, 31–32): a relaxation plus a closed-form solution on synthetic data turns "near optimal" into a measured gap.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a stochastic program has covariates, add them to the decision rule's domain, so that one unconditional learning problem replaces the conditional one; then evaluate the rule at the observed covariates (p. 5).
- Pair functional SGD with greedy dictionary pruning at tolerance ε = P2η², so that model size is bounded through η rather than N; state time and memory with and without pruning (pp. 9–13).
- On synthetic instances, build a lower bound from the known generator (drop non-binding constraints, solve the rest in closed form) and report gaps, not only a ranking (pp. 31–32).
- When sweeping dimensions, rescale the instance (constant total demand; order bounds divided by the control dimension) so that the optimal value stays fixed and the curves are comparable (pp. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "However, these dynamic methods are affected by the curse of dimensionality; they require scenario tree enumeration and can require many hours for solving problems with only a few stages." (p. 2)
- "A constant step size is then what allows us to control the trade-off between accuracy and memory required" (p. 12)

### S530 · Robust Regression over Averaged Uncertainty (arXiv preprint 2023, arXiv 2311.06960)
Read level: **partial**. Read: pp. 1–13 (abstract, §§1–3, §4.1.1–4.1.2) and pp. 16–26 (§5 with Lemma 12, Theorems 6–8 and their proofs; §6 with Tables 1–2; §7). Seen as statements only: pp. 13–16 (Lemmas 6–9, whose result matrices print as dots, with proofs deferred to the Appendix; the Schatten isotropic-constant results; the simplex-integration results, Theorem 5 and Corollaries 5–8). Not read: the references (pp. 27–29) and the Appendix (pp. 30–34). Figures 1–2 are captions only. First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Yu Ma (MIT) match; the file is arXiv v2 of 9 October 2024, dated October 10, 2024 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yu Ma (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a return, with a student, to the robustness–regularization programme (Heuristic 1). It starts from the group's own worst-case equivalence (Bertsimas and Copenhaver 2014; Bertsimas, Brown et al. 2011) (p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML RO}: In the group's worst-case equivalence, robust regression gives root-mean-square regression, which "is not practically used", while practice solves least squares (pp. 2, 9). Worst-case protection can also be "too conservative" (p. 8), and no exact regularization strengths were available for the least-squares case (p. 2). The question: which robust formulation is solved exactly by ridge regression, and what λ does each uncertainty set imply? (pp. 2–4)
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace the maximum over the set U by the integral over U (Definition 1, p. 9), which is a stochastic program with the uniform distribution on U (p. 9). The squared loss equals its exact second-order expansion in the perturbation ∆ (Lemma 12, p. 17). Over a symmetric U the linear term integrates to zero (Corollaries 1–3, pp. 12–13), which leaves least squares plus βᵀ(∫∆ᵀ∆ d∆ / vol U)β: ridge regression whose λ is the second moment of the set (proof of Theorem 6, p. 18). What changes is average instead of maximum, and squared instead of root loss; the norm-ball sets are those of the worst-case literature (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Linear regression with additive perturbations of the feature matrix, uniform on ellipsoidal, box, diamond, budget, Schatten or polytopal sets (pp. 7–8). There is no data model beyond the set. Real data: ten UCI regression sets (Table 1, p. 22), min–max scaled, with perturbations drawn by hit-and-run from the four ℓp sets at six levels ρ from 0.001 to 0.3, 10 seeds per level and an 80/20 split (pp. 21–22). Synthetic data: 300–900 samples with 3–10 informative features, 20 seeds, and Γ = 0.8ρ for the budget set (p. 24).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Closed-form λ for each symmetric ℓp set: box nρ²/3, diamond 2nρ²/((nk+2)(nk+1)), and a two-term expression for the budget set, valid for √2/2·ρ ≤ Γ ≤ ρ (Theorem 6, pp. 18–19). For Schatten balls the result holds only asymptotically and for square matrices, through isotropic constants (Theorem 7, p. 19; Remark, p. 12). For non-symmetric polytopes a linear term remains, so ridge is not recovered (Theorem 8, p. 20). Volumes come from Coxeter's formula and a corner-truncation argument (Lemmas 2–3, pp. 11–12), and polytope integrals from simplex formulas (pp. 15–16). My reading of the extraction: the ellipsoid constant is printed as 1/k in the statement and in terms of V(nk, ρ) and k in the proof (p. 18). Neither scales as ρ² like the box and diamond constants; the paper's own general step gives nρ²/(nk+2) for a uniform Frobenius ball (my computation). Also, the ℓ1 and ℓ∞ radii of the budget set are swapped between definition (4) (p. 7) and Lemma 3's proof and validity range (pp. 12, 19).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi (printed "0.11.5"), Julia 1.9.3 and Python 3.10.6 with the barrier algorithm; the code is public (p. 21). AUR (averaged-uncertainty ridge) is compared with WUR (worst-case, root-loss regression) by out-of-sample MSE in 2,400 real-data runs. AUR is better by 0.4–0.9% on average, rising with the perturbation, except for the box set (p. 22). The theorem's λ beats the cross-validated λ by 0.6–0.8% MSE (p. 22), and cross-validation picks up to six different λ across the ten noise seeds of one data set (Table 2, pp. 22–23). On synthetic data the gains shrink as samples and informative features grow (p. 24). No dispersion, significance tests or run-time numbers appear in the text read.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Claimed: closed-form λ, a theoretical justification for least squares under a robust lens, symmetry as the condition for the equivalence, and empirical gains (pp. 4–5). Printed limits: non-symmetric sets break the equivalence (p. 20); the Schatten results are asymptotic and square only (p. 12); AUR needs a larger λ (p. 25). Future work: matrix regression, robust optimization with solution constraints, and discrete robust optimization (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 9, 12–13, 18–20): ridge regression, a tool owned by statistics, is written exactly as an averaged robust problem. The device that keeps it in its class is symmetry (the odd term integrates to zero) plus the set's second moment, and the boundary is printed (non-symmetric polytopes; asymptotic Schatten balls).
- Heuristic 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 9, 18): the procedure practice already runs (least squares with a ridge penalty) is shown to solve a robust problem exactly. The digest records this under Method 1.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 8, 18, 21–23): the answer to over-conservatism is averaging, not a budget Γ. The knob ρ maps to λ in closed form, independent of the solution, and replaces cross-validation (pp. 21–23); but there is no probability bound and no price curve.
- New-pattern candidate: *exact dictionary between a statistical procedure and a robust model, with its boundary* (pp. 9, 18–20): ridge ⟺ averaged robust regression for symmetric sets; it fails for non-symmetric polytopes.
- New-pattern candidate: *own earlier result's limit as the entry* (p. 9): the group's worst-case equivalence ends in root-mean-square regression, "not practically used".
- New-pattern candidate: *average over the uncertainty set instead of its worst case* (pp. 8–9): the same sets, integrated uniformly, as the remedy for conservatism.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Expand the loss exactly to second order in the perturbation and integrate over a symmetric set: the odd terms vanish and the regularizer equals the set's second-moment matrix, a one-page route from a set to a regularizer (pp. 17–18).
- Test hyperparameter stability: rerun cross-validation over several noise seeds of the same data set and count how many distinct λ it selects, next to a closed-form choice (Table 2, pp. 22–23).
- When noise levels are generated from nested sets, require each sample at a higher level to lie outside the smaller set, so that the levels strictly increase (p. 24).
- Check each closed-form constant against the general formula λ = ∫∆ᵀ∆ d∆ / vol U, for example by Monte Carlo at small n and k, before reusing it (p. 18; the ellipsoid case above).

**Quotes**:
- "This implies that ridge regression is a general regularization method that protects against global perturbations of different noise structures" (p. 20)
- "This implies that CV is not the most reliable methodology for computing regularization strengths, as it provides unstable selections as we are exposed to randomness." (p. 22)

### S531 · Adaptive pricing in unit commitment under load and capacity uncertainty (arXiv preprint 2023, arXiv 2309.08162)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–10 (abstract, §§I–VII, Theorems 1–3, all tables, the Fig. 1 caption, references). The extraction fragments the displayed formulas and §II's dual derivation, so claims about formulas rest on the prose and the tables. First-page check: the title and the authors Dimitris Bertsimas and Angelos Georgios Koulouras (MIT ORC) match; the file is arXiv v1 of 15 September 2023 (p. 1). Its running header names IEEE Transactions on Power Systems, vol. 38, no. 3, May 2023; whether that records a publication or is a template header was not verified (no searching in this batch).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Angelos Georgios Koulouras (MIT ORC, IEEE student member, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the robust-optimization-in-energy line. It builds on the group's adaptive robust unit commitment ([10], IEEE TPWRS 28(1), S006) and its convex-hull pricing paper with Andrianesis, Caramanis and Hogan ([23]) (pp. 1, 10). Partly funded by ARPA-E award DE-AR0001282 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO AP; LO duality; IO with commitments fixed}: Adaptive robust optimization (ARO) and stochastic unit commitment (UC) already make commitment and dispatch decisions that adapt to uncertainty, and "have been successfully applied in practice" (abstract, p. 1). Prices are still deterministic. ISO marginal pricing and convex-hull pricing do not adapt to load and capacity uncertainty and need uplifts set by ad hoc procedures that motivate self-scheduling (p. 1). Only two earlier papers price robust UC, neither with ARO (p. 1). The problem: pay-as-bid and uniform prices for ARO unit commitment that are functions of the uncertainty (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Solve ARO unit commitment with linear decision rules (LDR) p(d, r) = u + Vd + Zr over norm-ball sets for load (size Γq) and capacity (size ∆p) (pp. 2–3). Fix the binaries at their optimal values, as in the integer-programming pricing of O'Neill et al. [18], and price from the dual of the robust counterpart (p. 3). Day-ahead payments cover commitment and the non-adaptive dispatch u. The adaptive part is an upper bound on intra-day payments, tight when the worst case is realized (p. 4). The uniform scheme adds payments proportional to Γq‖V_i‖ and ∆p‖Z_i‖, so it prices the uncertainty itself (p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**: Scarf-type UC with start-up and unit costs; ℓ1, ℓ2 and ℓ∞ sets (budget, ellipsoidal, box) whose sizes "control the conservativeness" (p. 2); a constraint ties the total non-adaptive dispatch to the expected load (p. 3). Assumptions adopted for tractability: LDR, which "may not be optimal" (p. 2), and binaries fixed for pricing (p. 3). Data: the Scarf example (two Type-1 and six Type-2 generators, five loads totalling 40) and the two-generator, three-hour, ramp-constrained example of Chen et al. [35] (pp. 7, 9). No ISO data are used.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Theorem 1: the day-ahead pay-as-bid payment equals the uniform-price payment with uplift, by complementary slackness between the robust problem and its dual (p. 4). Theorem 2: total payments coincide if the worst case is realized (p. 5). Theorem 3: under the centralized prices, no generator finds a more profitable schedule in its own robust problem, so there is no incentive to self-schedule; this too holds at the worst-case realization (pp. 5–6). The duals come from dual-norm robust counterparts (pp. 3–4). There is no complexity or solve-time analysis.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Worked examples only; no solver or timings are reported. In the Scarf example, the deterministic objective is $260 at price 2. ARO with Γq = 20 has objective $378 at price 3, and day-ahead payments of $328.5, identical under both schemes; each Type-2 uplift falls below the deterministic 30 (p. 7). Adding ∆p = 0.5 raises the payments to $352 (p. 8). The intra-day LP cost stays below the adaptive part and meets it at the worst case (Fig. 1, p. 8). In the example of Chen et al., the deterministic objective is $7340 and convex-hull pricing pays a $365 duality-gap uplift; ARO gives $7860 with $7640 of payments and no duality gap (p. 9). Two inconsistencies (my arithmetic): the text compares a deterministic cost of $280 with $328.5, while the same page prints an objective of $260 and payments summing to 260 (p. 7). And the adaptive uplifts (53, 53, 23, 0, 0, 26.5, 26.5, 26.5; 208.5 in total) exceed the deterministic total of 180 (p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Claimed: the first pay-as-bid and marginal pricing methods for non-convex markets under uncertainty, which eliminate uplifts and corrections (pp. 1, 10). As read, uplifts remain in the uniform scheme but are fixed day-ahead from duals (pp. 4, 7); the guarantees hold only at the worst-case realization (pp. 5–6); LDR and fixed binaries are assumed (pp. 2–3); the examples are small. There is no future-work statement (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–6): the class is kept by LDR plus norm-ball sets, and the prices and uplifts are read off the dual (step 4). The boundary is printed only in part: LDR may be suboptimal, and the theorems hold at the worst case.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 4, 7–9): Γq and ∆p are the knobs, and the uncertainty is itself priced (p. 4). But the cost of protection is shown at single points ($260 → $328.5 → $352; $7340 → $7860), with no curve and no probabilistic bound.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 7, 9): the owners' current rules (ISO marginal pricing, convex-hull pricing) are the comparators, but only on textbook examples, with no ISO data or field test. Compare the ISO New England evaluation of the 2012 ARO paper [10] cited in SKILL Method 5.
- New-pattern candidate: *own earlier result's limit as the entry* (pp. 1–2): the group's ARO unit commitment [10] made commitments robust, while pricing stayed deterministic.
- New-pattern candidate: *robust analogue of another field's canonical concept* (pp. 3–4): O'Neill et al.'s IP pricing (binaries fixed, prices from duals) carried into ARO with LDR.
- New-pattern candidate: *price the uncertainty set itself* (p. 4): payments proportional to Γq‖V_i‖ and ∆p‖Z_i‖, through the duals of the robust constraints.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To price a mixed-integer market model under uncertainty: fix the binaries at the optimum, write the LDR robust counterpart, read the payments off its dual, and prove that pay-as-bid equals uniform pricing by complementary slackness (pp. 3–4).
- Split payments into a firm day-ahead part (non-adaptive dispatch) and an upper bound on intra-day payments (adaptive part), and show that the bound is tight at the worst case (pp. 4, 8).
- Run the new scheme on the field's canonical toy examples (Scarf; Chen et al.) beside the incumbent pricing, and report objective, prices, payments and uplifts per generator (pp. 7–9).

**Quotes**:
- "In contrast to deterministic pricing, we also price the uncertainty by introducing payments based on sizes Γq and ∆p of the uncertainty sets." (p. 4)
- "The adaptive dispatch is an upper bound to the optimized intra-day dispatch." (p. 8)

### S707 · Renalase Identified by Machine Learning Methods As A Novel Independent Predictor Of Mortality In Hospitalized Patients With COVID-19 (Research Square preprint 2023, DOI 10.21203/rs.3.rs-2492699/v1)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–21 (abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, limitations, conclusion, declarations with the author contributions, references, Tables 1–3, figure captions). The supplementary files (Tables S1–S5, Figures S1–S3) are not in the file. First-page check: the title matches, and Dimitris Bertsimas (MIT) is the third of 13 authors (p. 1). The preprint was posted on 27 February 2023; its version of record appeared in the Journal of Biotechnology and Biomedicine on 1 January 2024 (not read) (p. 2). The list's note (a Crossref DOI for posted content) matches the file.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Basmah Safdar (first author), Matthew Sobiesk (MIT), Armin Nowroozpoor (Duke), Yanhong Deng, Gail D'Onofrio, James Dziura, Joe El-Khoury, Xiaojia Guo, Michael Simokonov, R. Andrew Taylor, Melinda Wang and Gary Desir (Yale unless noted) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: COVID-19-era health care with a partner hospital team (SKILL Research Trajectory, 2019–2026). The group's OptImpute imputes the missing data (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP ML}: The clinical question: find traditional and novel predictors of 180-day mortality in hospitalized COVID-19 patients, and test whether low renalase, a flavoprotein released by the kidneys and linked to cytokine release, predicts mortality independently (abstract, p. 3; pp. 3–4). The entry: traditional predictor models have "predictable biases", depend on the known literature and validate poorly externally, while ML may generalize better and capture interactions (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Compare three models on EHR data plus novel serum markers: an a priori logistic regression with clinically chosen confounders as the baseline, a backward-stepwise logistic regression (by AIC), and XGBoost with SHAP values for feature importance (p. 6). Renalase is called "independent" because it appears as a predictor in all three (p. 8). There is no optimization model, and interpretability is post hoc (SHAP, p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**: A single-centre retrospective cohort, March–June 2020. Of 3,450 admitted patients, 473 opted in with samples, and 437 remained after exclusions, of whom 71 (16%) died; they gave 897 blood samples (pp. 3, 7). Features with more than 30% missing were dropped and OptImpute filled the rest; biomarker cut-offs were set by "introspection of quantiles and clinical significance" (p. 5). Split 50/20/30 into training, validation and test; 200 random splits for the confidence intervals; XGBoost depth 3–10 and eta 0.1–0.9 (p. 6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a. There is no formulation and no proof; the statistics are univariate tests (Fisher's exact, χ²) and quartile-trajectory comparisons (pp. 5, 8–9).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Test AUC: a priori logistic regression 0.72 (0.62, 0.82), backward logistic regression 0.70 (0.55, 0.77), XGBoost 0.85 (0.77, 0.93) (Table 3, p. 18). The abstract and one discussion sentence give 0.87 for XGBoost (pp. 3, 9); the results and a later discussion sentence give 0.85 (pp. 7, 10). The training and validation AUCs of 1 for XGBoost and the backward regression (Table 3, p. 18) are not discussed. Table 2 prints identical rows for IFN-α and IFN-λ, and for IL-6 and TNF-α (p. 18), probably a table error (my reading).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Renalase is reported as a consistent predictor, and ML is said to augment traditional statistics in finding predictors (pp. 8, 11). Printed limits: a single site, the first wave, the pre-vaccination era, and few serial samples (p. 11). - **D2 Core idea**: HIFO is a three-horizon model whose granularity matches the horizon (Table 2, p. 9). Costs are piecewise linear and convex, with off-service and off-level penalties calibrated "through discussion with our partner hospital" (pp. 11, 13). Scheduled admissions are treated as certain (p. 16). Proposition 1 assumes independent Poisson flows (Assumption 2), relaxed to independent patients (Assumption 3) in Appendix A.2 (pp. 19–20). - **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Proposition 1: for a fixed flow f, the probability that the realized cost exceeds the robust cost decays as exp(−(c̄/c∞)·Γc²/(1+Γc)), from a new large-deviation bound for sums of independent Poisson variables (Lemma 1). The bound is a posteriori, specific to the solution, and reads the worst-case objective as a value-at-risk bound (p. 19). The guarantee covers open-loop day-level flows only; closed-loop guarantees are left open (p. 20). The robust counterpart is built by an epigraph and constraint-by-constraint robustification (p. 21). **Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Cheol Woo Kim (MIT ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the learning-to-optimize line (Heuristic 5). It moves from mixed-integer convex problems (Bertsimas and Stellato 2021, 2022; Bertsimas and Kim 2023) to ARO (p. 2), using Optimal Policy Trees from the group's company Interpretable AI (p. 20). Partly funded by a grant to MIT from Lincoln Laboratory (p. 29).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO DA; IO for binary first-stage decisions, ML}: Two-stage linear ARO with binary here-and-now decisions and polyhedral sets is computationally heavy. Affine rules carry no guarantee of near-optimality or even feasibility, and exact methods (Benders, column-and-constraint generation (CCG), scenario reduction, branch-and-bound, Fourier–Motzkin) "may not scale well" (p. 2). The authors measure the blow-up themselves: CCG's solve time for unit commitment jumps at n = 5 generators, while the deterministic version stays flat (Fig. 1, pp. 7–8). Real-time uses need answers in seconds or milliseconds (p. 2). The entry is the group's own line: strategies learned for mixed-integer convex problems do not carry over directly, because ARO is infinite-dimensional and needs decisions, worst cases and recourse together (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Encode an ARO solution as three finite "strategies" (pp. 9–10): the binary here-and-now decision; that decision plus its worst-case extreme point; and that decision plus the tight constraints of the second-stage LP at a sampled scenario. Generate instances by sampling the key parameter θ in a ball, solve them by CCG, and train XGBoost classifiers or Optimal Policy Trees (OPT, trained on reward matrices of suboptimalities) that map θ to strategies (pp. 10–12). Online, a prediction gives the decision and the worst case directly, and the recourse by solving the LP with only the predicted tight constraints (p. 10). Extensions: contingencies as categorical features for varying dimensions (pp. 14–15); learned warm starts and variable fixing inside CCG to cut data-generation time (Algorithms 5–6, pp. 15–17); and merging rare tight-constraint sets into one union to cut the number of classes (Algorithm 7, pp. 17–19).
- **D3 Model and data**: Fixed recourse, with A(d) and c(d) affine in d. Because the sets are polyhedral, worst cases are extreme points (pp. 4–5). The labels are only near-optimal: the inner max–min problem is solved by an alternating-direction heuristic with three starts, so "CCG can output suboptimal solutions" (p. 5; Remark 1, p. 10). θ is sampled uniformly from small balls around fixed centres (Remark 2, p. 10; pp. 20–21). Problems: synthetic facility location and multi-item inventory control, and unit commitment on the Carrion–Arroyo data with budget sets (pp. 20–21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Algorithmic; no theorems. CCG works on the extreme-point reformulation (pp. 4–6). The quality of each predicted strategy is its infeasibility and its relative suboptimality against Q̃, the heuristic evaluation of the second stage (pp. 9–10). The threshold p for warm-starting or fixing binaries is the smallest value whose validation misclassification rate stays below a bound (p. 16).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia 1.4.1 and Gurobi on a 2.6 GHz MacBook Pro with 16 GB; OPT from Interpretable AI (pp. 7, 20). Instances are split 70/30, and a prediction counts as accurate if it is feasible with suboptimality below 10⁻⁴ (p. 22). With near-optimal labels, accuracy never falls below 0.93, no prediction is infeasible, and the maximum suboptimality is at most 0.001. Speed-ups reach 3.37 million times, but only tens to hundreds for the wait-and-see decisions, which still need an LP (pp. 22–25). OPT and XGBoost perform alike (p. 25). For the large unit commitment problem (n = 100, m = 24, loose labels, 40 of 3,341 strategies kept), accuracy is 0.18 and the maximum suboptimality 0.0205, at a speed-up of 8.49 × 10⁷ for the first-stage decision (Table 12, pp. 27–28). Algorithm 6, with 4,000 training samples, speeds up data generation more than tenfold at negligible loss (p. 28). The speed-up ratios exclude offline training, which is reported only in the Supplementary Material (p. 20).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Claimed: the first ML approach to ARO, and drastic speed-ups with high accuracy (pp. 2–3, 29). Admitted limits: a heavy offline phase (p. 2), suboptimal labels from CCG (pp. 5, 10), and many classes when the uncertainty sets are large (p. 17). The headline of a speed-up "more than 10 million times" (p. 3) comes from the setting with 0.18 accuracy (p. 28), an instance of Tension T6. §7 lists no future work (p. 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode the solution of a two-stage or infinite-dimensional problem as a finite label (binary first-stage decision, worst-case extreme point, tight-constraint set), so that classifiers can learn it and an LP restores the continuous recourse (pp. 9–10).
- Score each predicted strategy by feasibility and relative suboptimality, not only by label accuracy, and report the maximum suboptimality among feasible predictions (pp. 9–10, 22).
- Warm-start or fix only the binaries whose predicted probability lies beyond p or 1 − p, with p chosen on a validation set so that the misclassification rate stays below a bound (p. 16).
- Merge rare classes into the union of their tight-constraint sets: the reduced LP grows a little but still contains each instance's tight set, and the number of targets drops (pp. 17–19, 25).
