### S037 · An integer optimization approach to large-scale air traffic flow management (Operations Research 2011, DOI 10.1287/opre.1100.0899)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17, including the Appendix (the facet proof of Theorem 1, pp. 15–17) and the references. Displayed formulas are partly garbled and Tables 1–3 lose their decimal points, so claims about formulas and numbers rest on the prose and the legible parts.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Guglielmo Lulli (University of Milano "Bicocca"), Amedeo Odoni (MIT, Aeronautics and Astronautics and ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received April 2008, accepted June 2009 (p. 1). It continues the air-traffic line he began with Odoni, his PhD adviser (per SKILL Academic Lineage). That line includes Vranas, Bertsimas and Odoni (1994) and Bertsimas and Stock Patterson (1998, 2000), all cited (pp. 2, 13, 17). This paper merges the line's two earlier models, with NASA and NSF funding (p. 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + AP; LO as the relaxation engine}: Delays are costly. In 2007 about 26% of U.S. flights arrived more than 15 minutes late and 3% were cancelled, and delays added about $12 billion to U.S. airlines' operating costs (p. 1). Airport-only models (ground holding, since Odoni 1987) no longer suffice, because en route sectors have become bottlenecks too (pp. 1–2). The gap is stated plainly. None of the cited models with airport and sector capacities considers rerouting or speed control. The one model with rerouting (Bertsimas and Stock Patterson 2000) was computationally inadequate for realistic large instances (p. 2). The problem: choose, flight by flight, the departure time, route, time in each sector and arrival time, subject to airport departure and arrival capacities and sector capacities (pp. 2–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep the variables of the 1998 model and add rerouting through constraints only. The variable w^f_jt is 1 if flight f has arrived at sector j *by* time t; the paper calls the "by" "critical" (p. 4). The sectors a flight may use form an acyclic digraph, a poset whose maximal chains are the routes (p. 3). Local precedence conditions, "analogous to the mass balance constraints in network flow problems" (p. 3), make each flight follow exactly one route (constraints (4)–(6), p. 5). What changes from the 1998 model: the route is no longer fixed. What stays: the variables and their strong relaxation (p. 2). Three classes of valid inequalities (fork, joint, antichain; Propositions 1–3, pp. 5–6) and two facet theorems (pp. 6, 15–17) strengthen the relaxation. Two add-ons also need no new variables:
  - polygonal capacity envelopes for the arrival–departure trade-off at airports (pp. 6–8);
  - fairness through slightly superlinear delay costs (pp. 4, 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: a deterministic 0–1 program in discrete time with known capacities (pp. 4–5). Cancellations are excluded (p. 4). The route digraph is assumed acyclic "without loss of generality" (p. 3). The superlinear costs are approximated linearly, as total delay minus a ground-delay credit, valid when the exponents are close to zero (p. 4).
  - A max term in the sector-capacity constraint (3) fixes an over-count pointed out by Castelli and Corolli (2010, personal communication; p. 5). How the max is linearized is not stated in the text read.
  - Data: random instances. Sectors form a grid of equal size, and every flight takes the same time to cross every sector. The maximum flight time is the minimum o–d time plus six units, and demand is hub-and-spoke, drawn uniformly (15 per period at peak, 8 off peak). A moving "weather front" cuts the capacity of three sectors at a time for five periods (p. 9).
  - Each flight's sector set is its shortest route, plus contiguous sectors when a route sector's demand exceeds 80% of its capacity (p. 9). The regional sector capacity of 71 per period is "an unrealistically high number" chosen to absorb the artificially high demand (p. 10).
  - There is no real schedule; a national database is future work (p. 15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Route choice without path variables: posets and maximal chains, with fork and joint sectors (p. 3). Constraints (4)–(6) give connectivity between sectors and (9) connectivity in time (p. 5).
  - Valid inequalities: fork (Proposition 1, p. 5), joint (Proposition 2, p. 6), and the antichain inequality, which lets each flight cross at most one sector of each rank (Proposition 3, p. 6).
  - Facets: under exclusivity conditions, constraint (4) at a joint sector (Theorem 1) and Proposition 1 at a fork (Theorem 2) are facet-defining (p. 6).
  - The facet proof (Appendix, pp. 15–17):
    1. An acyclic o–d digraph has at most |V| − 2 linearly independent paths, from the path–node incidence matrix (Proposition 4).
    2. Each flight has a bounded number of independent "4D paths" (Proposition 5).
    3. A block-diagonal matrix of affinely independent integer solutions, one block per flight, gives the dimension of the polyhedron.
    4. N − 1 affinely independent solutions on the face make it a facet.

    Remark 2 shows the face is not a facet when the condition fails (p. 16). The proof of Theorem 2 is called "analogous" and omitted (p. 15).
  - Size: the number of variables is bounded by |F|·D·N (p. 9).
  - Capacity envelope: each airport's (arrivals, departures) pair in each period must lie in a convex polygon (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: CPLEX 11.0 through AMPL, on a PC with four AMD-Xeon processors at 3 GHz and 8 GB RAM under Linux (p. 10). Clique cuts and probing are set to moderate. Solutions are accepted at a 1% gap, and the gap is reported (p. 10).
  - Regional instances: 20 airports (10 hubs) and 113 sectors, "about one-third of the high altitude sectors" in U.S. airspace. The horizon is 20 periods of 15 minutes, with about 3,000 flights and five shares of connecting flights (p. 10). Sector capacity is cut from nominal down to the infeasibility border (pp. 10, 12). After preprocessing there are about 270,000 constraints and 150,000 variables (p. 10).
    - The average time is 305 seconds (p. 11).
    - One instance (50% connecting, 13% capacity) misses the 1% gap within the 3,600-second limit. It is the only one that branches (440 nodes). At a 3% tolerance it finishes in 595 seconds with a 1.92% gap (p. 11).
    - Symmetry among identical flights is offered as the reason that instances with 50% connections run slower. With 70% connections the LP relaxation "closely approximates" the convex hull, judged by the few cuts added (pp. 11, 13).
    - Unlike Vranas et al. (1994), solving does not slow down near the infeasibility border (p. 13).
    - In "a few tests" without the valid inequalities, no acceptable solution was found within 3,600 seconds (p. 13).
  - National instances: 30 airports, 145 sectors, 22 periods and 6,475 flights (80% connecting), giving about 570,000 constraints and 305,000 variables (p. 13). The average time to a 1% gap is 743 seconds and the median 640 (p. 13). With capacity envelopes at the hubs the average falls to 522 seconds, almost 30% less, and no instance needs branching (p. 14).
  - Value of rerouting: up to about 25% lower cost than holding alone (p. 14).
    - At 40% capacity, ground delay falls from 699 to 600 time units and airborne delay from 132 to 114 (pp. 14–15).
    - At 10% capacity, ground delay falls from 3,096 to 2,640, at the cost of more airborne delay. 399 flights (6.1%) are rerouted, only 58 of them onto longer routes (p. 15).
    - Under extreme congestion the benefit shrinks again (p. 14).
  - Fairness: one instance compares the cumulative delay curves of the superlinear and the linear objectives with an equal-distribution line (Figure 7, pp. 8–9).
  - Reporting: only "one typical subset" of results is shown, "for the sake of conciseness" (p. 9). The baselines are the same model without rerouting or with a linear objective, not current FAA practice.
  - Scale, my inference from the paper's own numbers: if 113 sectors are about a third of U.S. high-altitude sectors (p. 10), the 145-sector national instances (p. 13) cover under half of them. The yardstick the instances do meet is the paper's description of the two largest systems, "more than 100 en route sectors" with several thousand flights at a time (p. 2).
- **D6 Positioning**: The lineage is credited. Odoni (1987) was the first to formulate the problem; surveys are Bertsimas and Odoni (1997), Ball et al. (2007) and Hoffman et al. (2011) (p. 1). Prior network models are judged one by one (p. 2):
  - Helme (1992): easy to understand but computationally weak.
  - Lindsay et al. (1993): the TAM.
  - Bertsimas and Stock Patterson (1998): facets and efficient computation.
  - Lulli and Odoni (2007): macroscopic.

  The paper presents itself as combining the flexibility of the 2000 model with the polyhedral strength of the 1998 model (p. 2). Sherali et al.'s APCDM is called complementary, more local and more detailed, not a rival (p. 2). For fairness, rationing by schedule is the agreed rule for single-airport programs, and the paper notes that networks have no equivalent (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract leads with the model's scope and the compact rerouting device. It then states the practical claim: computation times "short and reasonable" at the size of the U.S. system, and "the potential of serving as the main engine" (p. 1). Each constraint and inequality is explained in words and with small figures (pp. 5–7). Claims are hedged ("a good, if not necessarily optimal, solution", p. 11; "near-optimality", p. 15). The conclusion ranks the two contributions: computational viability is "from the practical viewpoint, most important" (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a formulation (rerouting by constraints, valid inequalities, facets), and evidence that it solves at national scale. Its times are consistent with the 10–15-minute decision cycles of the FAA's Air Traffic Control System Command Center (p. 15). Envisioned use: a "Tier 1" national model, solved several times a day, that tells detailed "Tier 2" models where to run, such as stochastic ground-delay models or APCDM (p. 15). Limits: tactical conflict resolution and altitudes are not modelled (p. 2); fairness among airlines is not examined (p. 9); the data are random. Future work (p. 15):
  - faster times with CPLEX 12;
  - a national database, with Metron Aviation and NASA Ames;
  - equity;
  - a two-stage stochastic version.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅: formulation first, kept in its class.
  - Rerouting, speed control and capacity envelopes enter as constraints on the 1998 variables, with no new decision variables (pp. 2, 4–8, 15).
  - Tractability is measured as solve times and gaps at the target scale (pp. 10–15), and its source is named: the valid inequalities, without which the model failed in 3,600 seconds (p. 13).
  - The problem is IO's own, so step 1 of the method (a problem owned by another tradition) does not apply.
  - For Tension T4, this is 2011 practice on the strong-formulation side (pp. 2, 13).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: it keeps only the honest-gap half of the method: solver version, machine, 1% gap, 3,600-second limit, and the one instance that misses the gap, with its 3% fallback (pp. 10–11). There is no warm start and no incumbent heuristic; the comparison is the model with and without rerouting (pp. 14–15).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the owner is named (the FAA command center), and its decision cycle is the run-time test (p. 15). But the instances are synthetic, and the baseline is the model without rerouting rather than current practice. Field testing on real schedules is left to future work (p. 15).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: fairness is handled only qualitatively. The knob is the curvature of the cost (slightly superlinear exponents), shown on one instance (Figure 7, pp. 8–9), and no price of fairness is computed. The value of rerouting is shown as a curve over capacity, and its weak end is printed: the value diminishes at extreme congestion (p. 14).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅, for the lineage step: the paper fuses two models from his own ATFM line with Odoni (p. 2) and plans deployment with NASA and Metron Aviation (p. 15).
- Heuristic 4 · evidence (negative results in the body): the instance that misses the gap (p. 11), the runs without inequalities (p. 13), and the shrinking value of rerouting (p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *add decisions by constraints, not variables*. Extend a model whose relaxation is known to be strong by local conditions on its existing variables, so the new options inherit its polyhedral strength (pp. 2, 4–6, 8, 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *the owner's decision cycle as the run-time test* (p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *tiered models*: a macroscopic national model whose output says where detailed models should run (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) In time-indexed scheduling, define cumulative binaries ("arrived by t", not "at t"). Precedence and capacity then become differences of consecutive variables, and the relaxation stays strong (pp. 4–5).
- (D2) Model route choice over an acyclic digraph with local flow-balance conditions at forks and joints, not path variables. Add the antichain inequality, at most one sector per rank (pp. 3, 5–6).
- (D4) Prove a facet by building, flight by flight, a block-diagonal matrix of affinely independent integer solutions: paths first, then unit delays in each sector. Add a remark showing the inequality is not a facet when the condition fails (pp. 15–17).
- (D3) Rewrite a two-part superlinear cost (airborne, ground) as a cost in total delay minus a ground credit. It stays linear in the cumulative variables and favours spreading delay (p. 4).
- (D5) Report the solver version, machine, cut settings, gap tolerance, time limit and preprocessed size, plus the instance that misses the target and what it takes to finish (pp. 10–11).
- (D5) Run with-and-without ablations for the inequalities (p. 13) and for the new option, rerouting, and say where the option stops helping (pp. 14–15).

### S194 · Revealing rival marginal offer prices via inverse optimization (IEEE Transactions on Power Systems 2013, DOI 10.1109/TPWRS.2012.2234144)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–9, including the illustrative example, the IEEE RTS case study and the author biographies. The displayed equations (1)–(7) and the contents of Tables I–X are missing from the text layer, so statements about them come from the surrounding prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Carlos Ruiz (Universidad Carlos III de Madrid), Antonio J. Conejo (Universidad de Castilla-La Mancha) (p. 1). Bertsimas is third author, and the work of Ruiz and Conejo was funded by a Spanish grant (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: received July 2012 (p. 1), in the years of his ISO New England unit-commitment paper (DOI 10.1109/TPWRS.2012.2205021, cited under SKILL Method 5). His biography here lists recent work in robust optimization, health care and finance (p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {LO + AP; DA-adjacent: inverse optimization}: A strategic producer sells in a network-constrained pool that is cleared by an LP, as in ISO New England or PJM. It wants its rivals' offer prices, which are proprietary (p. 1). After clearing it can see the dispatched quantities and the locational marginal prices (LMPs) (p. 1). Inverse optimization recovers the "data" of an optimization problem from its solution (p. 1). Prior methods for a related question, identifying inverse demand curves, use time series or clustering and are "approximate in nature" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Write the inverse problem with the optimality conditions of the LP market clearing in primal–dual form (p. 4):
  - primal feasibility, given by the observed outcomes;
  - dual feasibility;
  - strong duality.

  With quantities and LMPs observed, this form avoids the nonconvex complementarity conditions of a KKT-based formulation, used by Ahuja and Orlin [7], and the problem becomes an LP (pp. 3–5). The objective keeps the rival offers close, in a weighted norm, to initial guesses (pp. 3–4). The problem decomposes by day (p. 4). The outcomes pin down a marginal block uniquely. A non-marginal block can take any value without changing them, so the LP returns its initial guess (p. 5). A second step estimates each rival block's marginal cost as the minimum revealed offer over days and periods, assuming offers are never below cost (rule (6), p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Market model: an LP that maximizes social welfare, with a DC network (both Kirchhoff laws), ramping limits, line capacities and bounded phase angles (pp. 2–3). It has no discrete decisions, so all units are committed. The method "is not effective" for non-convex unit-commitment markets with side payments (p. 3).
  - Assumptions (p. 4):
    - the producer knows its own parameters;
    - rival capacities and ramp limits come from public data;
    - quantities and LMPs are published, so only markets such as EEX, Nord Pool and OMIE qualify;
    - demand bids are set to a large constant or to the price cap ($180/MWh in the Iberian market);
    - line data are known;
    - when block sizes are unknown, a small block size serves as a "screening step".
  - Strategic behaviour is assumed to act only through offer curves, a limitation the authors acknowledge (p. 4). Each node holds at most one strategic unit, one rival unit and one demand (p. 2).
  - The data are simulated: market clearing is run on known offers, and the recovered offers are compared with them (four-step protocol, p. 1). Rival offers are drawn at random, never below cost (rule (7), p. 5).
  - Instances: a three-node system with 100 MW blocks over three representative days of 24 hours (pp. 5–6). The 24-node IEEE RTS over 30 days of 24 periods, with eight blocks per unit and ten demand blocks; four lines are limited to 20 MW to weaken the north–south link (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No theorems are stated. The devices are:
  - Dual feasibility plus strong duality as the optimality conditions of an LP. They are equivalent to KKT, and necessary and sufficient by convexity, but avoid complementarity (p. 4). Under non-degeneracy the multipliers are unique (p. 4).
  - An identifiability argument: a non-marginal block offered below its LMP could have been offered lower without changing any outcome, so it cannot be recovered (p. 5).
  - Decomposition by day (p. 4).
  - From offers to costs: the minimum revealed offer (rule (6)). If producers may offer below cost, another rule, such as the average, is suggested (p. 5).
  - The [minimum, maximum] interval of offered prices per block is proposed as input to robust offering models (p. 7).
  - Not checkable from the text layer: how rule (6) treats periods in which a block is not marginal. There its estimate equals the initial guess, which is zero in the case study (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Three-node example: most offers and costs are revealed. The fifth block at node 3 is never marginal and cannot be estimated; a "simple heuristic" is suggested to fill it, drawing random prices until the curve increases (p. 7). All estimates lie within the interval of offered prices (Table III, p. 7).
  - IEEE RTS: good estimates for most rival units, with deviations at nodes 15, 22 and 23, whose blocks were never marginal (p. 8). The conclusions drawn: every price-setting rival offer is identified, and congestion helps identification (p. 8).
  - Not reported: solver, run times, a numerical error metric, a competing method (the time-series or clustering approaches of p. 1 are not run), and noisy or inconsistent data. The efficiency claim rests on the LP class (pp. 1, 9).
  - My inference: the offers are generated never below cost (p. 5), which is the same assumption rule (6) relies on. So the case study tests the method under its own assumption.
- **D6 Positioning**: It finds no prior use of inverse optimization in electricity markets to reveal rival offers (p. 1). The identification of inverse demand curves ([3]–[6]) is called related but conceptually different (p. 1). Inverse optimization is credited to Ahuja and Orlin [7] and to an MIT S.M. thesis [8] (p. 2). The paper departs from [7] by using the primal–dual form instead of KKT conditions (p. 4). For the robust-offering use of its intervals it cites Bertsimas, Brown and Caramanis (SIAM Review 2011) and Baringo and Conejo (p. 7).
- **D7 Writing**: Three contributions in a numbered list: an efficient procedure, its LP form, and accuracy on a realistic case (pp. 1–2). The approach is a four-step protocol (p. 1). The assumptions are enumerated, one with an explicit admission of its limitation (p. 4). Claims are confident ("precise manner", "accurately revealed", "highly efficient", p. 1); "Needless to say" appears twice (pp. 4, 5). The conclusion is short (pp. 8–9).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a modelling idea (an inverse LP via strong duality) applied to a new domain, with simulated evidence. Stated limits (pp. 3–5):
  - only markets that publish outcomes;
  - only convex market clearing;
  - strategic behaviour only through offer curves;
  - non-marginal blocks cannot be identified;
  - small producers need longer horizons.

  Future use: intervals of revealed prices as inputs to robust offering strategies (p. 7). There is no future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅: a question usually handled by time series or clustering (p. 1) is written exactly as an LP.
  - The device that keeps the class is strong duality in place of complementarity (p. 4). LMPs are read as dual variables (p. 3).
  - The boundary is printed: non-convex unit commitment is excluded (p. 3), and so are markets that do not publish outcomes (p. 4) and non-marginal blocks (p. 5).
  - Caveat: tractability is asserted from the class, with no solve times (pp. 1, 9). This is SKILL Taste warning sign 2.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the owner is a strategic producer (p. 1). But the output is an estimate scored against known simulated truth (pp. 1, 7–8). No offering decision is computed or scored, and no competing method is run.
- New-pattern candidate: *inverse optimization through strong duality*. Recover the data of an LP from its observed primal and dual solutions, keeping the inverse problem an LP (pp. 3–5).
- New-pattern candidate: *say what the observations can identify*. Only marginal (active) elements can be recovered; the rest default to the prior guess (pp. 5, 7, 8).
- New-pattern candidate: *simulate, then recover, with known truth* (p. 1). S359 does the same by re-aggregating ground-truth trips (S359 p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To invert an LP whose primal solution and duals are observed, impose dual feasibility and the equality of the primal and dual objectives. With the observed quantities fixed, these conditions stay linear in the unknown cost data (p. 4). This is my reading, since the equations are not in the text layer.
- (D4) Before reporting estimates, argue identifiability. Show that non-binding parameters can move without changing the observations, and say what the estimator returns for them (p. 5).
- (D5) Evaluate an estimator by simulating the forward problem on known inputs and comparing the recovered inputs with the truth (p. 1).
- (D8) Turn the range of revealed values into an uncertainty interval for a downstream robust model (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "On the other hand, the technique is highly efficient as it relies on solving a linear programming problem." (p. 1)
- "Network congestion facilitates the revealing procedure." (p. 8)

### S328 · A new local search algorithm for binary optimization (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2013, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1110.0496)
Read level: **full**: pp. 2–15 (p. 1 is the INFORMS download cover), including all proofs and Tables 1–6. The online supplement, which holds the remaining steps of the worked example (p. 4), is not in the file and was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Dan A. Iancu (Stanford Graduate School of Business), Dmitriy Katz (IBM T. J. Watson Research Center) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: received September 2008, revised four times in 2010–2011, accepted November 2011 (p. 2). It is the full text behind the citation of DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1110.0496 in SKILL Heuristic 3, and HS evidence from his RO period. Jingting Zhou is thanked for help with the computational study (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + HS}: The problem is the general binary program max c'x, Ax ≤ b, x ∈ {0,1}^n (p. 2). The entry is a gap that solver progress has left.
  - Major codes now solve problems that were out of reach a decade earlier. Progress came from cutting planes and heuristics, and the authors "believe" cutting planes mattered more (p. 2).
  - Yet especially large and dense binary problems still cannot be solved, and practice wants feasible solutions that improve current practice, without a proof of optimality (p. 2).
  - General-purpose binary heuristics are few: lift-and-project, pivot-and-complement, the feasibility pump, pivot-cut-and-dive (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A local search over one-bit flips that may pass through infeasible solutions, with a memory that prunes. A solution is "interesting" if (p. 3):
  - (A1) it violates no constraint by more than one unit;
  - (A2) its trace (violations plus loose constraints) is within distance Q of the incumbent's trace;
  - (A3) it beats every examined solution with the same trace; these values are kept in "trace boxes".

  When a better feasible solution appears, the list and the boxes are cleared (pp. 3–4). The single parameter Q sets the depth of the neighbourhood, and so the trade-off between run time and quality (p. 3). The novelty claimed against the general heuristics cited is generality: the algorithm uses no combinatorial structure (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Data are integer, and the run-time bound depends on ||c||₁ (pp. 3, 5).
  - The approximation theorem assumes the ideal implementation: a FIFO list, an injective trace map, and unweighted w-set packing with exactly w ones per column (pp. 5–6). The implementation hashes traces into a fixed memory, and the paper admits that the guarantee is then lost (p. 9).
  - Random instances come from a generator script (pp. 12–13):
    - set covering and set packing with m = 1,000 and n = 2,500, with w = 3, w = 5, or w uniform on 3–7 (Tables 4–5 also have w = 7 rows for packing);
    - weights uniform on [400, 500] for the weighted versions;
    - a larger covering set with m = 4,000 and n = 10,000.
  - Three MIPLIB 2003 instances: manna81, fast0507 and seymour. manna81 has general-integer knapsack constraints and needed an adjusted algorithm (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Running time O(||c||₁² · n · C(2m, Q) · max(m, n)) (Theorem 1, p. 4). Lemma 1 counts the trace boxes as binary vectors of length 2m within distance Q, each updated at most ||c||₁ + 1 times. Lemma 2 prices one examination at O(n · max(m, n)), using the incremental update Ay − b = Ax − b ± A_i (pp. 4–5). For c = e the algorithm is strongly polynomial for fixed Q (p. 5).
  - Approximation: with Q = w², Z*/Z_H ≤ w²/(2w − 1) for w-set packing (Theorem 2, p. 6).

### S359 · From physical properties of transportation flows to demand estimation: an optimization approach (Transportation Science 2018, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2017.0802)
Read level: **full**: pp. 2–11 (p. 1 is the INFORMS download cover), including Tables 1–3, Figures 1–3 and the references.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Julia Yan (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: received July 2016, published online June 2018, funded by a Mitsubishi Electric grant to MIT (p. 2). A transport-analytics paper from his ML/DA period, built on public data from four transport systems in three regions: Boston, New York City and the San Francisco Bay Area (pp. 7–9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP; CP as the engine, a convex QO}: Planning needs time-dependent origin–destination (OD) demand. Flat-fare subways record only turnstile entries and exits. Surveys resolve time only as peak and off-peak, are costly, and are run once or twice a decade (p. 2). Recovering an OD matrix from its row and column sums is "generally underspecified" (p. 2). Much of the literature uses link counts plus survey target matrices, or richer tracking data from smart cards, licence plates and phones (pp. 2–3). This paper uses only aggregate counts, which are public and protect privacy (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Make the matrix unique by asking it to be physically plausible. One convex quadratic program (QO) holds soft constraints with squared slack penalties for four properties (pp. 3–5, formulation (9)):
  - data fit (row and column sums);
  - temporal smoothness of proportions;
  - spatial smoothness toward the nearest station;
  - symmetry of round trips.

  Against maximum entropy (ME), the paper derives ME's closed form from its Lagrangian. The closed form gives every origin the same destination preferences, "a strong condition that is not entirely credible", and it ignores time because it separates by period (pp. 5–6). The QO keeps proportionality only between neighbours and couples the periods (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Periods are assumed long relative to trips, so the counts aggregate the OD tensor (p. 3).
  - Noise model: slack variables absorb exit undercounting and trips that cross periods (p. 4).
  - Smoothness is imposed on proportions, not volumes, because peaks would violate it (p. 4). The nearest neighbour can be generalized to a feature space, as in k-NN (p. 4). Symmetry holds only "in aggregate" (p. 5).
  - Weights: N on the data terms, squared neighbour distances on the spatial terms, a volume normalization on the symmetry terms (pp. 4–5).
  - Data with ground truth, aggregated into entries and exits from pickup and drop-off times (p. 6):
    - Hubway bike share (768,948 weekday trips): 4 municipalities (384 cells) and 131 stations (411,864 cells) (p. 7);
    - New York City taxis on May 14, 2013 (474,708 trips; 266 districts; 1,698,144 cells) (p. 8);
    - BART on February 5, 2016 (523,802 trips; 46 stations; 50,784 cells) (pp. 8–9).
  - Data without ground truth: Boston MBTA turnstile counts for February 2014 (61 of 113 stations; 89,304 cells) (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: The model is a convex QO with linear absolute-value constraints, d ≥ 0 and a weighted sum of squared slacks (pp. 4–5). The analysis is one formal comparison: the Lagrange conditions of the ME problem give d = a·b/Σb, which exposes ME's proportionality assumption and its separability in time (pp. 5–6). No uniqueness or consistency result is proved, although uniqueness is claimed (pp. 4, 10).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Metrics: R², plus CVaR at α = 0.1 of the percentage error, for the extreme errors that R² hides (p. 6). Measures of physical irregularity and flow imbalance are reported alongside (p. 6).
  - Baseline: the ME closed form, given perfect aggregated data (p. 6).
  - Results (Table 1, p. 6):

    | Data set | R² (ME → QO) | CVaR (ME → QO) |
    |---|---|---|
    | Hubway municipalities | 0.916 → 0.991 | 5.29 → 3.14 |
    | Hubway stations | 0.360 → 0.413 | 0.97 → 1.12 (worse) |
    | NYC taxis | 0.805 → 0.870 | 1.24 → 1.03 |
    | BART | 0.676 → 0.764 | 1.02 → 0.87 |

    By my arithmetic, the abstract's "~5%–10%" matches absolute R² gains of 0.053–0.088.
  - Times: 6 seconds, 2.5 hours, 8 hours and 80 seconds for the four ground-truth sets, and just under an hour for MBTA (pp. 7–9). Everything runs in Julia with JuMP on a laptop (i7-6500U, 16 GB), or on a server (E5-2660 v4, 256 GB) for the two largest (p. 6). The QO solver is not named.
  - Assumption checks: each assumption is tested by its own predictor (last hour, nearest neighbour, return flow), scored by R² (Table 3 and p. 8):
    - municipalities: 0.977, 0.173, 1.000;
    - stations: 0.385, 0.119, 0.918;
    - NYC: 0.884, 0.803, 0.992.
  - Ablation: dropping spatial smoothing raises CVaR from 3.14 to 6.19 for the municipalities and lowers R² from 0.413 to 0.382 for the stations (p. 8).
  - Sparsity: 2.2% of the true NYC cells are nonzero, against 2.0% of the estimate (p. 8).
  - MBTA: only qualitative checks. The physical properties follow the pattern of the other data sets, and the line-transfer matrices are compared with the 2008–09 survey and 2010 ridership data (pp. 9–10). ME sends most demand to the Red Line. The QO concentrates demand on the diagonal, like the survey, but misses Green–Green trips, whose surface stations have no turnstiles (pp. 9–10).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper surveys traffic OD estimation: Bayesian, generalized least squares, maximum likelihood, multiobjective, state-space updating of target matrices, ME and network equilibrium (pp. 2–3). It also surveys transit tracking: trip chaining with smart cards, automatic vehicle identification and phones (p. 3). It claims kinship in philosophy with ME and equilibrium, but offers "an interpretable list of desired physical properties", lighter than a model of passenger behaviour (p. 3). The weighting idea is credited to Brenninger-Göthe et al. (p. 4). Liu and Zhou's route-choice constraints are named as an extension (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing**: Three numbered contributions: public data, privacy, light assumptions (p. 3). The abstract carries one headline number (p. 2). Some claims are strong: "a truly large-scale application", and "a computational validation of the formal result in Section 3" (p. 8). The negative results sit in the body with explanations (station-level CVaR, noisy granular data; p. 8). The extrapolation to MBTA is hedged ("we expect", p. 9).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: a convex estimation model, a formal critique of the standard baseline, and evidence from four real systems (pp. 3, 6–10). Limits shown:
  - a worse tail error on the noisy station-level set (p. 8);
  - a weak nearest-neighbour regularity (p. 8);
  - no ground truth for the target system, and missing Green Line data (pp. 9–10).

  Future use: replacing outdated surveys in planning models and in real-time estimation (p. 10); route-choice constraints for complex networks (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅: a problem owned by transportation statistics (ME, generalized least squares, Bayesian and equilibrium methods; pp. 2–3) is written exactly as a convex QO. The rival's implicit assumption is read off its Lagrangian (pp. 5–6), and solve times are printed per data set (pp. 7–9). Caveats: uniqueness is claimed, not proved (pp. 4, 10), and the solver is not named (p. 6).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the owner (transit planners) and its current rule (outdated surveys) are named (pp. 2, 10). But estimates are scored, by R² and CVaR, against ground truth and against the standard method (ME), not decisions. The target system (MBTA) gets only a qualitative comparison with the survey (pp. 9–10).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: the duel step (against the incumbent, on data with known truth) is here, with ME as the incumbent and three ground-truth data sets (p. 6). Nothing needs a certificate, since the QO is convex, and there is no warm start.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence: the station-level CVaR loss and the weak nearest-neighbour regularity are printed and explained (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: *physical regularities as soft constraints for an underdetermined estimation* (pp. 4–5).
- New-pattern candidate: *test each modelling assumption by its own predictive R², then ablate it* (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: *expose the rival method's hidden assumption through its Lagrangian* (pp. 5–6).
- New-pattern candidate: *known-truth evaluation by re-aggregation* (p. 6; cf. S194 p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To recover a matrix from its marginals, add soft constraints for the regularities you believe (smoothness in time and space, symmetry) with squared slack penalties. Weight them so that no term dominates as the problem grows (pp. 4–5).
- (D3) Impose smoothness on proportions (a cell divided by its marginal) rather than on volumes, so that peaks do not violate it (p. 4).
- (D4) Before running any experiment, derive the baseline's closed form from its Lagrangian and state the assumption it implies (pp. 5–6).
- (D5) Build test inputs by aggregating data sets whose disaggregated truth is known. Report a tail metric (CVaR of the percentage error) beside R² (p. 6).
- (D5) Validate each structural assumption on ground truth with its own simple predictor (Table 3), and ablate the corresponding constraints (p. 8).

### S028 · Adaptive distributionally robust optimization (Management Science 2019, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2017.2952)
Read level: **full**: pp. 2–16 (p. 1 is the INFORMS download cover), including Tables 1–6 and the references. The electronic companion is not in the file and was not read. It holds the proofs of Theorems 1, 3 and 4 (EC.1–EC.3), the comparison with deflected decision rules (EC.4), and the generalization to the ambiguity sets of Wiesemann et al. (EC.5).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Melvyn Sim (NUS Business School), Meilin Zhang (SUSS Business School) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: received March 2016, accepted August 2017, funded by the Singapore Ministry of Education (p. 2). A late paper of his RO programme with Sim, his former student (per SKILL Academic Lineage) and co-author of "The price of robustness", cited on p. 2. It extends the adaptive line of Bertsimas, Iancu and Parrilo (2010; cited p. 3) to ambiguity sets of distributions.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + CP; SD for the multistage version; AP (appointment scheduling, inventory) as testbeds}: Uncertainty is often left out of models because it makes them intractable (p. 2).
  - Dynamic models suffer the curse of dimensionality. Linear decision rules (LDRs) were abandoned in stochastic programming for their suboptimality, then revived by Ben-Tal et al. (2004) (p. 3).
  - The regular LDR can be infeasible even under complete recourse (p. 7).
  - Ambiguity sets based on moments "may not necessarily yield tractable distributionally robust counterparts" (pp. 4–5).
  - The applied motive is software: a general framework should run in packages "with reasonable computational effort" (p. 3).

  The problem: min c'x + sup over P in F of E_P[Q(x, z)], with a linear second stage with fixed recourse and an SOC-representable ambiguity set F (pp. 4–5). A multistage version follows (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea**: Lift the ambiguity set. Each nonlinear moment function g_i(z) gets an auxiliary random variable u_i with g_i(z) ≤ u_i. The lifted set then has only linear expectation constraints, over an SOC-representable support (pp. 5–6, after Wiesemann et al. 2014). Then:
  - the adaptive DRO equals a classical robust problem over the lifted support (Theorem 1, p. 6);
  - a decision rule affine in both z and u, the "new LDR", gives a bound between the true value and the regular LDR's (Theorem 2, p. 7);
  - under complete recourse with finite first absolute moments, some lifted set makes the new LDR feasible (Theorem 3, p. 7);
  - the new LDR turns the DRO into a classical affinely adaptive robust problem (Proposition 2, p. 7);
  - it is exact under complete recourse with one second-stage variable (Theorem 4, p. 8).

  What changes from the regular LDR is the information the rule may use. What stays is the LP or SOCP class and the existing robust-optimization software (pp. 3, 7). A partial cross-moment set, made of quadratic bounds along chosen directions, approximates the intractable full covariance constraint conservatively (p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The second stage is a linear program with fixed recourse and data affine in z (p. 4). Theorem 1 assumes relatively complete recourse and a Slater condition (Assumption 1, p. 6). Theorem 3 needs E|z_i| < ∞ (p. 7).
  - The SOC restriction is adopted "for tractability considerations" (p. 2). Full cross-moment sets are excluded (p. 5). The partial cross-moment directions are chosen by hand (p. 5) and later called "ad hoc" (p. 15).
  - All instances are synthetic:
    - appointment scheduling, set up as in Mak et al. (2014): N = 8 patients, overtime cost γ = 2, means uniform on [30, 60], coefficients of variation up to 0.3, five correlation levels α and seven instances each (p. 12); then N = 12–30 with α = 0 (p. 13);
    - inventory with IMA(0,1,1) demand and the parameters of See and Sim (2009) (p. 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1 by conic duality, which "avoids analysis of functional spaces" (p. 6; the proof, in EC.1, was not read). The reformulation enumerates the extreme points of the dual recourse polyhedron, so it is "generally an intractable problem unless the number of extreme points of P is small" (p. 6).
  - Theorem 2 by composition: the lifted rule evaluated at u = g(z) is feasible for the original problem (p. 7).
  - A one-line counterexample (y ≥ |z|) shows the regular LDR infeasible under complete recourse (p. 7).
  - A negative result: on a relatively complete recourse problem with N₂ = 1, every lifted LDR is infeasible. Because u is unbounded above, its coefficient is forced to zero (Proposition 3, p. 8).
  - Policy caveat: take y ≥ |z| with mean 0 and variance at most 1. The LDR policy (1 + z²)/2 has the optimal worst-case value, but it is pointwise worse than |z| except at ±1. The worst case is a two-point distribution, which explains the tie (pp. 9–10).
  - Enhancement: partial Fourier–Motzkin elimination (Zhen et al. 2016) before the LDR (p. 9).
  - Model sizes as functions of N for each approach (Table 2, p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Appointment scheduling compares five approaches (p. 12):
    - The regular LDR is ultraconservative, at about 2,000 against about 20–220 for the others, because it sees only the mean and the support (p. 12).
    - Exact MM is marginally better than Approx MM. Approx PCM beats both at low correlation and coincides with Approx MM at α = 1. The new LDR "does not replicate" the optimal solution of Mak et al. (pp. 12–13).
    - The SDP approach (Approx CM) is lowest where verified, but unstable. Its results are kept only when at least one of SDPT3, SeDuMi and MOSEK confirms optimality (p. 12), and it is unverified at high correlation and at N = 30. Its time grows from 7 (N = 12) to 378 (N = 25), while Approx PCM needs under 1 up to N = 25 and 2 at N = 30 (Tables 1, 3–4, pp. 12–13; the time unit is not stated in the text layer).
  - Out of sample: Monte Carlo with 10,000 trials under truncated normal and log-normal distributions, with standard errors. Exact MM and Approx MM come out close, and so do Approx PCM and Approx CM (Table 5, pp. 13–14).
  - Inventory: Approx PCM is "reasonably close" to a dynamic-programming lower bound, computed under i.i.d. uniform factors (a member of the ambiguity set), and well below Approx MM. At T = 30 and α = 0 the values are 740.8 against a bound of 725.0 and 838.6 for Approx MM. The gap grows with correlation: at α = 0.5, 882.9 against 732.0 (Table 6, pp. 14–15). The model is formulated in ROC and solved with CPLEX (p. 14).
  - Not reported: solver versions and the machine.
  - My cross-check: the Approx CM objectives of instances 6 and 7 differ between Table 1 (93.23, 158.43; p. 12) and Table 5 (88.42, 156.15; p. 14). The text does not explain the difference; it is consistent with the SDP instability the authors flag.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper frames itself by risk versus ambiguity (Knight, Ellsberg) and by RO's tractability advantage, citing his own RO papers alongside those of RO's founders (p. 2). It credits Wiesemann, Kuhn and Sim (2014) for lifted sets, and traces the history of LDRs: Garstka–Wets, Ben-Tal et al., Bertsimas–Iancu–Parrilo, and the deflected and segregated variants (p. 3). Similar results by Isii, Bertsimas–Popescu and Shapiro–Kleywegt are acknowledged; the difference is the proof by conic duality (p. 6). A rival's insight is praised: Mak et al.'s hidden tractable reformulation was "cleverly exploited" (p. 11). Delage and Iancu are cited on degenerate worst-case policies and on time consistency (p. 10). An endnote prefers "adaptive" to Ben-Tal et al.'s "adjustable" (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing**: Four numbered contributions (p. 3). The abstract moves from a "modular and tractable framework", through an equivalence, to a software package (p. 2). Surprise markers flag the key results ("More interestingly", "Quite surprisingly", pp. 2, 8). There is an explicit "negative result" (p. 8) and a normative caution about policies (p. 10). The software is presented "as a proof of concept" (p. 10). Proofs go to the electronic companion; the main text keeps short proofs and counterexamples (pp. 6–10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: theory (the equivalence, the lifted LDR, an exactness class, a negative result); software (ROC in C++, while XProg emerged independently in MATLAB, p. 10); and two applications (pp. 10–15). Limits:
  - Theorem 1 is tractable only with few extreme points (p. 6);
  - no exactness under relatively complete recourse (p. 8);
  - the decision rule is not a policy, and time consistency can fail (pp. 9–10);
  - the partial cross-moment set is ad hoc (p. 15).

  Practical advice: implement only the here-and-now decision and re-solve on a folding horizon (p. 10). Future work: adapt infinitely constrained ambiguity sets (Chen, Sim and Xu 2016) to adaptive problems, by adding violated expectation constraints (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To make a moment-based ambiguity set tractable, introduce one auxiliary random variable per nonlinear moment function so that only linear expectations remain. Let the decision rule depend on the auxiliary variables too (pp. 5, 7).
- (D4) Order approximations by composition. A rule optimal for the lifted problem, evaluated at u = g(z), is feasible for the original. So the lifted bound is at most the regular one, and the true value at most the lifted bound (Theorem 2, p. 7).
- (D4) Approximate a semidefinite covariance constraint by finitely many directional variance bounds, E[(f_k'(z − μ))²] ≤ f_k'Σf_k. The approximation never deteriorates as directions are added (p. 5).
- (D4) Mark the edge of a result with a small explicit counterexample: y ≥ |z| for the regular LDR (p. 7); a problem on the box ‖z‖∞ ≤ 1 for the lifted LDR (p. 8).
- (D5) Keep results from numerically fragile baselines (SDPs) only when at least one of several solvers certifies optimality, and say so (p. 12).
- (D5) Bound a robust policy's cost from below by solving the DP under one distribution that belongs to the ambiguity set (pp. 14–15).
- (D8) In multistage use, implement the first-stage decision and re-solve on a folding horizon instead of committing to the decision rule (p. 10).
