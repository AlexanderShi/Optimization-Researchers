### S112 · The voice of optimization (Machine Learning 2021, DOI 10.1007/s10994-020-05893-5; arXiv 1812.09991)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v3: body pp. 1–32 in full, Tables 1–4 read row by row; Appendices A–B (pp. 32–36) read; references (pp. 36–41) skimmed. The Machine Learning print and arXiv v1 were not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Bartolomeo Stellato (affiliation not stated in the file) · **Position in trajectory**: the optimal-trees programme (Bertsimas & Dunn 2017, 2019) turned on optimization itself. v1 dates from December 2018 (arXiv id; 03 B5.4). A speed-focused sibling, "Online mixed-integer optimization in milliseconds" (arXiv 1907.02206), applies the same framework (pp. 6, 32, 37).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML + IO; LO and convex QP as testbed classes}: Entry is a practitioner's complaint plus a usage pattern. Practitioners treat solvers as black boxes and want to see "how problem parameters affect the optimal decisions", and real applications solve "multiple similar instances" with varying data (p. 2). Goal: predict the optimal solution as parameters vary, in an interpretable way (p. 2). The naive alternative, regressing the solution on the parameters, is dismissed as intractable (an output with "hundreds of thousands of components") and imprecise (regression error yields suboptimal or infeasible points) (p. 2). Problem classes: parametric convex problems (1) and mixed-integer convex problems (5) with parameter θ ∈ R^p (pp. 9, 12).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Encode the optimum by a *strategy*: the set of tight constraints for continuous problems, s(θ) = T(θ) (p. 10); for MIO, the pair (tight constraints of the integer-fixed problem, optimal integer values) (p. 14). Number the strategies and learn θ ↦ s(θ) as multiclass classification with OCT, OCT-H or a feedforward NN (pp. 2, 17–18).
  - Online: predict the three most likely strategies, solve each reduced problem (3) or (7), keep the best by infeasibility and suboptimality (pp. 10, 14, 23). For LO, QO, MILO and MIQO the reduced problem is a KKT linear system, so the problems can be solved "without the need to apply any optimization solver" (pp. 10, 14, 21).
  - Sampling: draw θ i.i.d. and stop when a Good-Turing bound on the chance of an unseen strategy falls below ε (Theorem 3.1, Algorithm 1, pp. 19–20).
  - Changed relative to the nearest prior method (Misra et al. 2019): one classifier instead of evaluating all candidate active sets in parallel; MIO covered; one estimator instead of a sampling scheme that depends on at least four parameters tuned per problem (p. 5). Kept: the solver (Gurobi) as the offline oracle that labels the samples (pp. 20–21).
- **D3 Model and data**: f and g convex in x, with no assumption on how they depend on θ (pp. 7, 9). Every encountered θ is assumed feasible (p. 8). Degeneracy is handled by labelling with all tight constraints rather than support constraints, and one optimum is kept when there are several (pp. 9–10). Data: i.i.d. samples of θ only (p. 20). In the experiments θ is drawn uniformly from intervals or balls around set points, described as "unimodal and therefore closer to the ones encountered in practice", a choice the authors say the theory does not require (p. 22). All four benchmarks are generated textbook problems with random data: transportation LP, Markowitz QP with a factor covariance, capacitated facility location MILO, and the hybrid-vehicle MIQO of Takapoui et al. (pp. 23–30). No real data set is used. The approach rests on an empirical regularity: few strategies occur ("we very rarely hit all the possible strategy combinations", p. 11), and |S| ≤ 43 in every table (pp. 25–31).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Size of the label space: support constraints number at most n (Hoffman 1979; Calafiore 2010), the stated reason the method pays off when n ≪ m (p. 9).
  - Fixing the integer part turns (5) into the continuous convex problem (6) with the same optimal cost (p. 14).
  - Theorem 3.1 (missing strategies bound): with confidence 1 − β, P(unseen strategy) ≤ N1/N + c·sqrt((1/N) ln(3/β)), c = 2√2 + √3. The proof is a citation of McAllester and Schapire (2000, Theorem 9) (p. 19).
  - Theory and implementation diverge. Algorithm 1 stops on the bound (p. 20); MLOPT stops when the point estimate falls below 0.005 (p. 21). My arithmetic (not in the paper; β is not stated): at N = 10,000, the sample size in every table (pp. 25–31), the confidence term alone is about 0.09 for β = 0.05. The theorem therefore certifies roughly 9%, not 0.5%; about 3.4 million samples would be needed for ε = 0.005.
  - No analysis links classification error to suboptimality; that wrong predictions stay nearly feasible and nearly optimal is shown only empirically (pp. 22–31).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Setup*: Dell R730 cluster with 28 Intel E5-2680 CPUs, 256 GB RAM and a Tesla K80 (p. 22). Gurobi is cited only as the 2020 reference manual, with no version number (pp. 20, 38). CVXPY, OptimalTrees.jl and PyTorch (pp. 20–21). Tuning on a 90/10 split: tree depth 5, 10 or 15 and minimum bucket 1, 5 or 10; NN learning rate, batch size and epochs on small grids (p. 21). N = 10,000 training samples per instance and 100 unseen test samples (pp. 22, 25–31). Training takes 2–12 hours (p. 23).
  - *Metrics*: a prediction is accurate only if the recovered solution violates the constraints by at most 1e-3 (normalized) and is within 1e-3 of optimal. The maxima of infeasibility (p̄) and suboptimality (d̄) over the test set are reported, together with the time ratio against Gurobi (pp. 22–23).
  - *Results*: transportation, up to 6,400 variables: accuracy 95–100%, speed-ups of at most about 5×, and Gurobi faster on small instances (time ratio 0.04 for OCT at 20 × 20) (pp. 24–25). Portfolio, up to 400 assets: 100% accuracy, speed-up "not significant" (p. 26). Facility location, up to 6,480 variables: 100% accuracy, speed-ups up to 2,201× (pp. 27–28). Hybrid control, T = 10–40: accuracy 89–100%, speed-ups up to 6,846×; at T = 40 OCT scores 93% and OCT-H 89% against 100% for the NN, with d̄ about 3% (pp. 30–31).
  - Losing rows and weak speed-ups are printed in the body (pp. 24, 26, 31–32).
- **D6 Positioning**: Five literatures, each set apart (pp. 3–6):
  - learning to tune algorithms (ParamILS, SMAC, irace): they predict parameters and are not interpretable; "SMAC could benefit from interpretable predictors such as OCTs" (p. 3);
  - learning heuristics (learned branching of Khalil et al. and Alvarez et al.; Bonami et al. on linearizing MIQPs; Kruber et al. on decompositions) (p. 4);
  - reinforcement learning for combinatorial problems (Dai et al.) (p. 4);
  - constraint programming and SAT (SATzilla; Selsam et al.) (pp. 4–5);
  - learning parametric programs: Misra et al. 2019 are criticised directly ("we found several limitations of their approach", p. 5), and Klaučo et al. 2019 for lacking a sampling scheme with probability bounds (p. 6).

  Sensitivity analysis (his LO textbook, ch. 5; Boyd and Vandenberghe §5.6) is distinguished in philosophy. It studies perturbations of uncertain data around a nominal point and relies on duals, hence its limited reach for MIO; here the problems are deterministic and the data need not lie near a nominal point (p. 6). Self-citations: OCT (2017), the 2019 book, a manuscript "submitted for publication" claiming OCT-Hs are as powerful as NNs, and the online-MIO sibling (pp. 6, 33, 37).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - A brand metaphor carries the paper: OCTs "give optimization a voice" (p. 1; also pp. 7, 32).
  - Headline vs table: the abstract's accuracy "in the 90%-100% range" (p. 1) is repeated in the contributions and conclusions (pp. 7, 32), although Table 4 has an 89% row (p. 31). The conclusion calls the method "significantly faster" (p. 32), while the body reports insignificant speed-ups and solver wins (pp. 24, 26).
  - Generated benchmarks are called "realistic examples" (pp. 1, 7).
  - A historical digression on Turing, Good and the Enigma (p. 19).
  - Small instances with depth-2 or depth-3 trees show the mechanism before the benchmarks (pp. 10–18), and appendices on OCTs and NNs keep the paper self-contained (pp. 32–36).
  - Claim narrowing, checked within v3 only: the abstract says "a broad class" and "a novel insightful understanding" (p. 1), but the body keeps "novel, reliable and insightful framework" (p. 7) and "any continuous and mixed-integer convex optimization problems" (p. 32).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions: the framework, the Good-Turing exploration scheme, the open-source MLOPT tool (github.com/bstellato/mlopt), and the benchmarks (pp. 7, 20).
  - Limits stated: training time (p. 23); the feasibility assumption (p. 8); OCT accuracy degrades as the number of strategies grows, which the authors tie to "how the parameters affect the optimal solution" rather than to problem size (p. 32); no speed gain on small instances or portfolios (pp. 24, 26).
  - Future work: online settings with limited time and computing, via the sibling paper (pp. 6, 32).
  - Unstated (my reading): parameters drawn from narrow balls keep |S| small; there is no test on real parameter histories; the claim to beat (s, S) policies "because it takes into account future predictions" (p. 12) is not tested; and the theorem–implementation gap above.

**Method links**:
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 9–10, 14, 21). The direction is reversed: "we redefine optimization as a multiclass classification problem" (p. 1), instead of recasting another tradition's problem as LP, conic or MIO. The class-keeping move survives online: a known strategy turns an MIO into a smaller convex problem, and LO, QO, MILO and MIQO into a KKT linear system.
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 21–23, 31, 33–34). For: a tuned duel against the strongest available learner (NN), a stated machine, decision-quality metrics, and the losing rows printed. Against: no tree is exact or certified, and the solver version is unstated. Tension T1 evidence: Appendix A says the MIO training runs "in a reasonable amount of time" (p. 33), but the package trains OCTs by local search from random restarts (p. 34).
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 22–23). Decisions, not labels, are scored: a prediction counts only if the recovered solution is feasible and within 1e-3 of optimal, and the top three strategies are tried before one is chosen. But there is no owner, no partner data and no current rule; the time baseline is the solver.
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 21, 31–33). Interpretability is priced in accuracy (OCT below OCT-H, OCT-H close to NN), with the bad case printed (T = 40), and depth and split sparsity serve as knobs. But no curve or bound is published, and the abstract folds the 89% case into "90%-100%" (Tension T6).
- Heuristic 5 (learn the optimizer's strategy offline): evidence ✅ for the first half (pp. 2, 6, 17–21). The second half, "narrow the claims as the paper matures", holds for the v3 abstract only (pp. 1, 7, 32).
- New-pattern candidate: **solution-as-label** (strategy = tight constraints plus integer values) (pp. 2, 10, 14, 17).
- New-pattern candidate: **Good-Turing coverage stopping rule** for sampling the strategy set of a parametric problem (pp. 5–6, 18–21).
- New-pattern candidate: **explain the optimizer with an interpretable model** (pp. 1, 7, 10–18, 32).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Encode each parametric optimum as (tight-constraint set, integer values) and learn it as a class label. Online, solve only the reduced problem; for LO, QO, MILO and MIQO that is a KKT linear system, with no solver at run time (pp. 10, 14, 21).
- Before training, sample parameters in batches (5,000 per round here) until the Good-Turing estimate N1/N of unseen strategies is small. If you claim the high-confidence bound of Theorem 3.1, compute the N it requires: the point estimate is all that the implementation controls (pp. 19–21).
- Score a learned optimizer by the decisions it returns: normalized constraint violation and relative suboptimality with explicit tolerances, taking the maximum over test points. Let it propose its top-k strategies and keep the best after solving each reduced problem (pp. 22–23).
- Report the speed ratio against the solver for every size, keeping the rows where the solver wins (pp. 24–26).
- Show the mechanism on small instances with shallow trees whose leaves read as policies (inventory: wait t0 periods, then order the demand; compare with (s, S)), then move to large benchmarks (pp. 10–12).

**Quotes**:
- "Instead, our approach encodes the optimal solution with a small amount of information that we denote as strategy." (p. 2)
- "OCTs show competitive accuracy in most comparisons apart from the cases with higher number of strategies where the prediction performance degrades." (p. 32)

### S175 · A unified approach to mixed-integer optimization problems with logical constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2021, DOI 10.1137/20M1346778; arXiv 1907.02109)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v3: body pp. 1–24 in full, Tables 1–9 read; Appendices A–C (pp. 26–29) read (proofs of Theorems 3.1 and 3.2, Lemma B.1, Corollary B.2, Table 10); references (pp. 24–26) skimmed. The SIOPT print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ryan Cory-Wright (Operations Research Center, MIT) and Jean Pauphilet (London Business School) (p. 1), both in SKILL.md's ML lineage · **Position in trajectory**: the umbrella paper of the group's 2016–2020 sparse line: sparse portfolio [8], sparse PCA [9], best subset [10], sparse inverse covariance [11], sparse regression [12], [13] (pp. 24–25). It reports that ridge regularization scales sparse regression three orders of magnitude beyond his own 2016 big-M best-subset paper (p. 19).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + ML; CP as devices (Fenchel and conic duality, SOCP, SDP); AP testbeds}: Entry is a modelling convention. Since Glover (1975), the logical constraint "x = 0 if z = 0" has been written with big-M, to the point that "textbooks in the field introduce facility location, network design or sparse portfolio problems with big-M constraints by default, although they are actually reformulations of logical constraints" (p. 1). The abstract frames the paper as a challenge to this "longstanding modeling practice" (p. 1). Problem (1.1): minimize c'z + g(x) + Ω(x) over z ∈ Z and x, subject to x_i = 0 if z_i = 0, read in two stages: activate binaries, then optimize the continuous variables (pp. 1–2, 4). Instances: network design, sparse empirical risk minimization, sparse portfolio, unit commitment, facility location, sparse PCA (via an exact mixed-integer SDP), binary quadratic optimization, and unions of ellipsoids and polytopes (pp. 4–7). The claim is that these are "different facets of a single unified story" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Treat big-M as a regularizer rather than a modelling trick, and offer the ridge term ‖x‖²/(2γ) as an alternative (pp. 1, 7–8).
  - Substitute v = Diag(z)x and dualize the inner problem: f(z) = max over α of h(α) − Σ z_i Ω*(α_i), with Ω*(β) = M|β| for big-M and γβ²/2 for ridge (Theorem 2.5, p. 9). Then f is convex in z, and one continuous solve on the support of z gives the subgradient −Ω*(α*(z)_i) (p. 10).
  - Solve by outer approximation (Algorithm 3.1), run as a single branch-and-bound tree with lazy callbacks (pp. 11–12).
  - Add-ons: the Boolean relaxation, solved by Kelley's method or projected subgradient ascent on the dual, for lower bounds and root cuts (p. 14); randomized rounding, sequential rounding by subgradient signs and one-flip local search for upper bounds (pp. 15–16); feasibility cuts and conic infeasibility certificates (pp. 12–13).
  - What changed: outer approximation itself (Duran–Grossmann; Quesada–Grossmann) is unchanged; the choice of regularizer now drives tractability. Ridge makes the inner dual strongly concave, so α* is unique and cut degeneracy disappears (pp. 10, 13). Under ridge the perspective formulation and its cuts are recovered as the same outer-approximation cuts (Theorem 3.2, p. 16).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - *Assumptions*: Assumption 2.2, Ω is the big-M indicator or the ridge penalty (p. 7); Assumption 2.4, each inner problem is either infeasible or strongly dual (p. 8); Z ⊆ {0,1}^n and g convex, possibly encoding constraints through +∞ (p. 2). The proof uses only decomposability, convexity and 0 ∈ argmin Ω (Remark 2.7, p. 9). The conic feasibility cuts assume g has conic form, and "This assumption gives rise to some loss of generality" (p. 12).
  - *Admitted trade-offs* (Table 2, p. 11): big-M makes the problem infeasible below some M0 and is exact above some M1; ridge keeps the feasible set but "Systematically leads to a different objective for any γ > 0". The rounding theorem needs a uniform bound L on |α*(z)| (p. 14), made explicit under strong concavity in Appendix B (pp. 27–28).
  - *Data*: synthetic network design from Günlük and Linderoth's generator, adapted, with the capacity constraint penalized (λ = 1,000) "to allow for a fair comparison" (p. 17); Biq-Mac binary quadratic instances (pp. 18–19); synthetic sparse ERM (p. 20); six sparse PCA data sets (p. 21); Wilshire 5000 and Russell 1000 portfolios (pp. 20, 23); 112 OR-Library capacitated facility-location instances (p. 21); Frangioni–Gentile unit commitment with the quadratic coefficient scaled by α ∈ {0.1, 1, 2, 5, 10} (p. 22).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Nonlinear reformulation v = Diag(z)x and Fenchel duality on the coupling constraint; z_i² = z_i makes the ridge term linear in z (Theorem 2.5, p. 9).
  - Remark 2.6: without regularization the saddle point carries hard constraints α_i = 0 if z_i = 1; regularization replaces them by a soft penalty, so it acts as a relaxation (p. 9).
  - f is convex as a pointwise maximum of functions linear in z, which gives the outer-approximation cut (2.16). Under ridge, strong concavity makes α* unique: "ridge regularization naturally inhibits degeneracy" (p. 10).
  - Proposition 2.9: Lipschitz-type bounds on f(z′) − f(z), with constants proportional to M or γ (pp. 10–11).
  - Theorem 3.1: Bernoulli rounding of a Boolean-relaxation solution is ε-optimal with probability at least 1 − |R| exp(−ε²/κ), where κ = 2M²L²|R|² (big-M) or γ²L⁴|R|²/2 (ridge). The integrality gap is therefore at most sqrt(κ ln|R|), and the theorem "quantifies the price of fixing variables" (pp. 14–15). Proof: a sub-Gaussian maximum over the |R| positive extreme points of the ℓ1 ball (Rigollet–Hütter, Theorem 1.16) (p. 26). My reading: the bound grows like M·L·|R|·sqrt(ln|R|), so "provably near-optimal" (p. 24) depends on few fractional coordinates.
  - Theorem 3.2: dualizing the inner maximization under ridge gives the perspective formulation, and perspective cuts are the outer-approximation cuts, "an elementary derivation of perspective cuts" (pp. 3, 16; proof p. 27).
  - The regularizer is coercive, which rules out asymptotic feasibility, so conic infeasibility certificates exist (footnote 5, p. 13).
  - Lemma B.1 and Corollary B.2 give an explicit L under strong concavity, e.g. for least squares with cardinality k (pp. 27–28).
  - Printed boundaries: outer approximation terminates finitely but takes exponentially many iterations in the worst case (p. 12); SDP relaxations that control two moments are "usually intractable when n > 300, with current technology" (p. 15); footnote 9 says what "polynomial time" means (relaxation dimension, fixed precision, symmetric cones) (p. 24).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - *Setup*: Julia 1.0, JuMP 0.18.4, CPLEX 12.8.0; one Xeon E5-2690 v4 core and 32 GB RAM (p. 17). The opponent is CPLEX on the natural big-M or MISOCP formulation (p. 17).
  - *Network design*, m = 40–200 nodes, one-hour limit, five instances per (m, p). No method closes the gap, so incumbents are compared by their unregularized cost. With big-M, the cuts beat CPLEX in 94% of 100 instances, by 9.9% on average and up to 40%. With ridge, the cuts beat CPLEX's MISOCP by 11% on average, and that MISOCP often cannot finish the root node (Table 3, p. 18). Gaps are mostly "uninformative (> 100%)": with big-M CPLEX gives tighter gaps; with ridge the cuts do, 86% of the time (pp. 18, 29).
  - *Binary quadratic (Biq-Mac)*: warm start from 10,000 Goemans–Williamson roundings; triangle inequalities added by lazy callbacks. Only bqp-50 and bqp-100 are solved within the hour. My reading of Table 4 (p. 19): the text's "faster than CPLEX" holds for the triangle variant (0.4 vs 0.6 s; 38.6 vs 51.7 s), whereas plain cuts are slower on bqp-50 (30.6 vs 29.4 s) and leave a 25.3% gap on bqp-100. CPLEX gets tighter gaps on denser matrices; the cuts find better incumbents everywhere, and only the cuts fit in 32 GB on bqp-1000 (Tables 4–5, pp. 18–20).
  - *Sparse ERM, PCA and portfolio*, summarized from companion papers [12], [9] and [8]: p in the 100,000s (regression) and 10,000s (classification); PCA certified for p in the 100s and near-optimal for p in the 1000s (Table 6, 600 s limit, one thread); 3,200 securities against 400 in prior work (Table 7) (pp. 19–21).
  - *Facility location* (112 OR-Library instances): both methods find the optima; the cuts are faster than CPLEX with big-M in only 12.7% of instances (big-M) and 23.85% (ridge) (p. 21). Ablation: outer approximation plus Kelley, plus local search, plus both (Table 8, p. 22). Table 8's ridge row "OA + Both" (92.5% wins, 91.7% improvement) does not match the text's 23.85% and 54.5%, and the caption does not say which CPLEX formulation is the reference (my reading: different baselines).
  - *Unit commitment*: CPLEX on both formulations. Big-M is faster for α ≤ 1; for α ≥ 2 the MISOCP (ridge) converges while big-M stalls at gaps of 0.24–2.82% after an hour (Table 9, p. 22).
  - *Portfolio regularization paths*: M < 1/k makes the problem infeasible; the ridge paths are smoother (Figs. 2–3, pp. 22–23).
  - Losing cases are printed in the body (pp. 18–22).
- **D6 Positioning**: Two literatures "often considered in isolation" (p. 2) are joined (pp. 2–3):
  - forcing-constraint formulations: big-M from Glover [34], first used in MINLO by Bienstock [14]; perspective cuts [28]; MISOCP [1]; ridge in sparse regression [47], [13];
  - decomposition: generalized Benders [33], Benders [6], outer approximation [22], [25], branch-and-cut [45], [48], modern Benders [23], [24] and Pareto-optimal cuts [43].

  The paper claims to synthesize "the aforementioned and seemingly unrelated three lines of research" (p. 3), and claims that its general link to perspective cuts is new, the only prior link being Xie and Deng [54] for sparse-regression relaxations (p. 16). Critiques in passing: high M brings numerical instability and weak bounds (p. 3); Pareto-cut selection rules "can do more harm than good" (p. 13); perspective-cut codes needed tailored branch-and-bound, while this framework runs in CPLEX or Gurobi callbacks (p. 16). Self-citation is heavy: six of the 55 references are the group's own [8]–[13], and three experiment subsections report results from them (pp. 19–21, 24–25).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract runs class, convention, "we challenge this longstanding modeling practice", reformulation, then headline numbers: 100s of nodes, solutions up to 40% better, 3,200 against 400 securities, 100,000 covariates (p. 1). That is Heuristic 9's skeleton. One section title is playful, "Duality to the Rescue" (p. 8). Each comparison closes with a "merits" summary from three angles: theoretical (Table 2, p. 11), algorithmic (§3.5, pp. 16–17) and experimental (§4.4, pp. 23–24). The summaries give explicit guidance: ridge is "the method of choice" (p. 23) when the objective has a "naturally occurring strongly convex term, which is sufficiently large" (p. 24). Untested points are hedged: "we intuit" (p. 14), "This suggests" (p. 9), "We believe" (p. 24). Practical guidelines are numbered (the three ingredients of fast outer approximation, p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution levels: modelling (the regularization view), theory (the saddle-point reformulation, the rounding guarantee, the perspective link), algorithm (the outer-approximation recipe) and computation (pp. 2, 4, 24).
  - Limits stated specifically: the choice of regularizer depends on the instance (benefits "largely even", p. 17); efficiency hinges on fast separation and on cardinality-type structure (pp. 13, 24); ridge changes the objective (p. 11); big-M can make the problem infeasible (pp. 8, 22–23); restarts and multi-threading are not implemented (pp. 13, 21); gaps are uninformative on network design (p. 18).
  - Future work: other regularizers (p. 9), higher-level relaxations (p. 15), mixed-integer semidefinite optimization (p. 24).
  - Absent: no code link appears in the arXiv text.

**Method links**:
- Method 1: evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 3, 9–10, 12, 15–16, 22–24). The model, here the regularizer, is redesigned so that a standard solver's lazy callbacks apply (p. 16). The dual is read for cuts and for uniqueness (p. 10), and the boundary is printed: exponential outer approximation, SDP relaxations beyond n > 300, fast separation required. The paper also bears on Method 1's limitation and Tension T4. By 2019–2021 the group named big-M's instability and weak bounds (p. 3) and preferred ridge or perspective formulations when the objective is strongly convex (pp. 22–24). It is not a reply to the big-M critique of OCT and does not treat trees, so the Roundtable Card's blind spot "formulation strength" should be narrowed, not dropped.
- Method 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 14–19, 21–22, 29). Warm starts from the Boolean relaxation, rounding and local search (Goemans–Williamson for BQP); an exact branch-and-cut under a stated limit, solver version and machine; gaps printed even above 100%; a duel against the solver's own formulation; an ablation; and the losing cases (CPLEX's tighter gaps, and CPLEX faster on most facility-location instances).
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 14–15, 22–23). One knob (M or γ), a solution-independent rounding bound that "quantifies the price of fixing variables", and published curves with the bad case (M < 1/k infeasible). But the priced property is tractability, not a property a decision-maker wants.
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 3, 12, 19, 21). The reopening comes from remodelling, not from a rebuilt solver-times-hardware figure. The "why now" is qualitative (decade-long solver improvements, p. 3; lazy callbacks "now standard tools", p. 12), and the only numbers are scale ratios against previous attempts (pp. 1, 19, 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For on/off logical constraints, write x_i = 0 if z_i = 0 as v = Diag(z)x, add a decomposable convex regularizer minimized at 0 (a big-M box or ridge), and dualize the inner continuous problem. Then f(z) = max over α of h(α) − Σ z_i Ω*(α_i) is convex in z, and one continuous solve on supp(z) gives the cut (pp. 8–10, 13).
- Run outer approximation as one branch-and-bound tree with lazy callbacks, generating cuts at binary (hence sparse) z. For infeasible subproblems add the no-good cut Σ z^t_i (1 − z_i) + Σ (1 − z^t_i) z_i ≥ 1, or a cut from a conic infeasibility certificate (pp. 12–13).
- Feed the root: solve the Boolean relaxation (Kelley's method, or subgradient ascent on the dual q(α)) for a lower bound and initial cuts. Round its solution at random (z_i ~ Bernoulli(z*_i)) and sequentially by the subgradient's sign, then polish with one-flip local search that stops when it cycles (pp. 14–16).
- Derive natural regularizers from the data: M from flow conservation (the sum of positive demands), 1/γ = λ_min(Q), or a diagonal q from maximizing e'q subject to Q − Diag(q) ⪰ 0 (p. 8).
- To compare formulations that optimize different objectives, fix each returned z and re-evaluate the unregularized cost. Do not compare gaps across formulations (pp. 17–18).
- Choose between big-M and ridge by scaling the objective's quadratic coefficient (α from 0.1 to 10) and timing both formulations (p. 22).
- Ablate the recipe on a public library (outer approximation plus the lower-bound step, plus the upper-bound step, plus both) and report the share of wins and the mean improvement (Table 8, pp. 21–22).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + ML; no optimization model}: Entry is an application gap plus an untested belief. Multimodal clinical AI is promising, and gains of 1.2–27.7% in accuracy are reported in a 2020 review, but "developing unified and scalable pipelines" that consistently beat single-modality systems "has remained challenging" (p. 1). The untested belief: previous works "assume advantageous properties to multimodality without clear validation of the dynamics" as modalities are added (p. 4). The problem is stated as a generalizable pipeline (HAIM) plus a systematic measurement of each modality's and each source's value across 12 tasks (pp. 1, 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A modular pipeline. Patient-centric files are built from MIMIC (pp. 3, 6). Each source gets one embedding from a frozen, exchangeable extractor: normalized tabular values; summary statistics of time series; Clinical BERT for notes, chunked at 512 tokens and averaged; DenseNet121-res224-chex probabilities and dense features for X-rays, averaged over images (pp. 6–7). The embeddings are concatenated into one 4,845-dimensional fusion vector and fed to XGBoost (pp. 3, 7). A model is trained for every non-empty subset of sources (2,047 per task with 11 sources; 1,023 per chest task with 10), and AUROC is attributed to sources and modalities by Shapley values (pp. 3, 8). Changed relative to earlier multimodal systems: no end-to-end fused architecture; any extractor can be swapped "without requiring to re-train other feature extractors" (pp. 4–5, 7).
- **D3 Model and data**: MIMIC-IV v1.0 (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston, 2008–2019) joined with MIMIC-CXR-JPG v2.0.0 into HAIM-MIMIC-MM, restricted to patients with at least one chest X-ray: 34,537 samples, 7,279 stays, 6,485 patients (pp. 1, 3, 6).
  - Labels: ten chest pathologies taken from MIMIC-CXR-JPG's labels derived from radiology notes, keeping only confirmed presence or absence (p. 7); discharge alive within 48 h, and death within 48 h, with samples at X-ray time points (N = 45,050) (p. 8). Radiology notes are excluded as inputs for the chest tasks to avoid label leakage (pp. 7–8).
  - A single learner, XGBoost, chosen after preliminary runs of seven architectures "to heuristically decide" on the best (p. 8).
  - Shapley assumptions: AUROC 0.5 with no source; a modality's AUROC is the average over the models of its sources (p. 3). Missing-data handling is only in Supplementary Table 3, not read (p. 7).
  - Admitted scope: MIMIC "was only used to exemplify our pipeline" (p. 6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a: no optimization formulation, algorithm analysis or proof. The analytic devices are the exhaustive enumeration of source subsets (p. 8) and Shapley attribution over 11 sources and 4 modalities (p. 3); small or negative Shapley values are also read as a sign of information loss in feature extraction (p. 3).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:

Each is trained on five random 80/20 splits stratified by patient (and by pathology for the chest tasks), with hyperparameters chosen by fivefold cross-validated grid search inside the training split: depth 5–8, 200 or 300 trees, learning rate 0.05, 0.1 or 0.3 (pp. 7–8). **Transferable techniques**:
