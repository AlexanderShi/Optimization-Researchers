### S048 · Probabilistic combinatorial optimization problems (PhD thesis, Massachusetts Institute of Technology 1988, no DOI or arXiv)

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + IO; HS in §3.7; AP as motivation only}: The entry is a practitioner's chore stated in general form. In many applications the same combinatorial problem is re-solved regularly on a subset of the original instance; re-optimizing means exponentially many NP-hard solves, often without the time or the computing resources (p. 12). The thesis optimizes instead an a priori solution plus a real-time update rule: the a priori solution minimizes expected cost over all 2^n instances (1.1), and the rule should bring that expectation close to the re-optimization expectation (1.2) while being easy to update (pp. 12–13). Two motives are stated: models in which randomness is a major concern, and the robustness, with respect to optimality, of deterministic optima when instances change, simulated by the presence or absence of nodes (p. 11). Non-computational reasons for an a priori rule are named per problem: the PMST rule never changes the network structure (p. 27); delivery operators value regularity and personalization of service, with the same vehicle and driver (pp. 71, 127); information may arrive gradually (p. 68). The applications are motivations, not data: VLSI processors, communication centres and an organization of spies (pp. 27–28); parcels, meals, forklifts, mail, strategic planning and job-shop changeovers (pp. 71–72); cash collection from bank branches and ATM restocking (p. 126). The thesis concedes that its examples idealize reality and answers that the PMST is a generic problem (p. 28). The entry into the literature is the predecessor: sharpen Jaillet's 1985 results and prove some of his conjectures, then move into "entirely new territory" (p. 69); the PMST had never been examined before (p. 19).
  - Transferable: when one problem is re-solved for many perturbed instances, make the deployment rule (solve once, update trivially) the object of optimization, and write down both criteria at the start: its expected cost and its distance to the re-solve ideal (pp. 12–13).
- **D2 Core idea**: One framing carries four problem families. A fixed object (tree, tour, route, or location plus tour) is updated in O(n) by retracing the tree or skipping absent nodes (pp. 14, 26, 125). The objective changes from a length to an expectation over instances, the combinatorial object stays, and the deterministic problem is the special case with every node present (p. 24). The update rules may seem arbitrary, the thesis grants, but they are easy to apply, useful in practice and asymptotically equivalent to re-optimization (p. 13). The conclusion ranks the existence of this alternative to re-optimization first: "Probably the most important issue raised" (p. 160). The second idea concerns algorithms: since the a priori problems are hard, use heuristics whose deterministic guarantees survive the probabilities. Sorting along a spacefilling curve preserves order, so the a priori tour restricted to any instance is the curve's own tour on that instance; its O(log n) guarantee then holds instance by instance, even with dependent presence, and the heuristic "ignores the probabilistic nature of the problem" (pp. 99–100). Other constructions follow the same line: a tour induced by the MST, within 2d of optimal where d is the MST's diameter (pp. 89–90); the cyclic heuristic for the PVRP, which tries every rotation of a TSP tour (pp. 135–136); and a spacefilling-curve location heuristic for the PTSFLP (p. 155).
  - Transferable: if a heuristic's output is unchanged by deleting points, its deterministic guarantee holds on every instance under any presence distribution, and it needs no probabilities, so it still works when they are unequal or not accurately known (pp. 100, 110).
- **D3 Model and data**: A graph whose nodes are present at random. A general p(S) can encode dependence (pp. 29, 128), but most explicit results assume independent presence with probabilities p_i, often one common p (pp. 31–32). The PMST graph need not be complete (p. 29); the routing problems use complete graphs. The triangle inequality is needed for the upper bounds and the routing heuristics (pp. 82, 96, 131), but not for the LP-duality lower bounds (p. 82). Symmetric distances are assumed "for convenience" (pp. 132, 135), and distinct costs in one proposition, called not truly restrictive because a small perturbation produces them (p. 88). There are two probabilistic models: the random Euclidean model, with i.i.d. uniform points in the unit square and a fixed p, and the random-length model, with uniform costs on [0, 1] (pp. 53–54). The PVRP has unit demands with probability p_i and a capacity Q_n allowed to grow with n (pp. 128, 141). One choice is admitted as made for exposition: the plane, because its geometry is clearer, and the uniform law, because it is more intuitive; d dimensions and general densities are claimed, not proved (p. 146). The thesis says which results survive dependent presence: the tree-to-tour bound and the spacefilling-curve bound (pp. 86, 100). There are no real data; the experiments use random uniform instances (p. 120).
  - Transferable: for every theorem, say whether it needs independent presence or holds for any p(S); the dependence-free results are the ones a practitioner can use when presence is correlated (pp. 86, 100).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Closed-form expectations. An edge contributes its cost times the probability that both sides of it hold a present node, written by inclusion–exclusion with h(S) = Pr{no node of S present}, valid for any p(S) (Theorem 2.1, p. 30). Under independence this becomes a product form (Theorem 2.2, p. 31), evaluated in O(n) by peeling leaves (p. 32). For the PVRP, a recursion for the probability that exactly r of the first m customers have demand gives the expected route length under both strategies (Theorem 4.1, pp. 128–130).
  - Hardness. The PMST is NP-complete even with unit costs and one common p, by reduction from EXACT COVER BY 3-SETS; the proof uses properties of the weight function φ(k) and an exchange step showing that adding a missing arc lowers expected cost (Proposition 2.3, Theorem 2.4, pp. 33–38). The same holds on complete graphs with costs in {1, M} (Theorem 2.5, pp. 39–40), while complete graphs with equal costs are easy (p. 39). How hardness was first suspected: as p → 0 the objective tends to that of the network design problem on a tree, which is NP-complete, and "it is this observation that originally led us to suspect that the PMST problem is hard" (pp. 38–39). Whether some tour restricts optimally to every subset is placed in Σ2^P, and in co-NP when no two edge sets have equal cost (Proposition 3.12, p. 93).
  - The coverage probability as a knob. For the PMST the optimal value f(p) is continuous, increasing, piecewise differentiable and concave for np > 2 (Proposition 2.6, pp. 40–41); for the PTSP, E/p² is decreasing and piecewise convex (Proposition 3.5, p. 84). Both limits are worked out (p → 1 gives the MST and the TSP; p → 0 gives network design and the tour minimizing Σ r²L^(r)) and sketched as curves (Figures 2.5, 3.2; pp. 42, 84–85). Concavity yields the bounds of Proposition 2.7 (pp. 41–42).
  - Bounds and their tightness. The MST used as a PMST solution can be off by a factor O(n) as p → 0, and an explicit example shows that this bound "is the best possible" (Proposition 2.8, pp. 43–44). A lower bound on MST re-optimization comes from backward induction over subset sizes (Proposition 2.10, pp. 45–47). A star example shows the a priori tree beating MST re-optimization when np = c < 3 and losing when c > 3 (pp. 47–48).
  - Exchange arguments for polynomial cases. Whenever the MST is a star it is also the PMST (Theorem 2.11, p. 49). With unequal p and unit costs, the star at the most likely node is optimal (Theorem 2.13, pp. 50–51). A triangle condition a + tb ≥ c with t < 1, stronger than the triangle inequality, makes some star optimal; the proof removes inner nodes one at a time without raising expected cost (Theorem 2.14, pp. 51–53). With d(i, j) = u_i + v_j every PTSP tour has the same expected length (Proposition 3.11, p. 91).
  - Closing a predecessor's conjecture. Jaillet's bound needed n prime, an "unnatural distinction" (p. 78). For odd n the thesis compares the optimal tour with the tour that skips one point, whose cycle structure is a permutation of the original's. The resulting bound is stronger, stated in the optimal tour's own length and free of the triangle inequality; even n stays open (Proposition 3.1, pp. 78–80).
  - LP duality as a bounding device. Subtract node potentials u_i + v_j from the distances, keep the reduced costs nonnegative and maximize the bound that remains; its dual is a transportation problem (Proposition 3.2, pp. 80–82), obtained "by using a mathematical programming rather than a combinatorial approach", from an idea of Berman and Simchi-Levi (p. 80). A corollary gives an assignment-problem bound (p. 82). The same device bounds both PVRP strategies (Proposition 4.5, pp. 134–135).
  - Probabilistic analysis with Steele's subadditive Euclidean functionals. Subadditivity of the PMST is shown by gluing subsquare solutions through always-present ("black") representative leaves, with an induction bounding the cost of making a leaf black (Theorem 2.16, pp. 58–61). The constant is then identified in two steps. The expected cost of an instance depends only on its size, and the number of present nodes concentrates around np (a Chernoff bound, cited to Raghavan 1988), so the constant has the form A√p; bounds with p → 1 then fix A (pp. 61–63). The same skeleton serves the re-optimization strategies (Theorem 2.15, pp. 55–57), the PTSP, where it identifies Jaillet's constant as β_TSP√p (Theorem 3.19, pp. 102–104), and random lengths with Frieze's ζ(3) (Theorems 2.17–2.18, pp. 64–66).
  - Worst-case ratios. A TSP heuristic with guarantee c gives a ratio of order c/p on the PTSP (Proposition 3.14, p. 97). Any tour is within a factor of order np of the optimal a priori tour (Proposition 3.15, pp. 97–98), and balancing the two bounds gives O(√n) (Theorem 3.16, p. 98). The spacefilling curve is O(log n) against re-optimization (Theorem 3.17, pp. 99–100). For the PVRP, the cyclic heuristic is within a constant under strategy a (2 minus a term in p and Q, garbled in the OCR; Theorem 4.10, p. 139); under strategy b, the optimal PTSP tour is within Q + 1 and the spacefilling curve within Q + O(log n) (Theorem 4.11, p. 140). Berman and Simchi-Levi's TSFLP heuristic is within ½(1 − p_i*) (Theorem 5.4, p. 153).
  - A lower bound on re-optimization from a heuristic's labels. The nearest-neighbour edge lengths satisfy the hypotheses of a lemma of Rosenkrantz et al., and Jensen's inequality over the binomial number of present points finishes the bound (Proposition 3.13, pp. 94–96).
  - Regimes. The PVRP asymptotics split by how the capacity grows against √n. The radial collection term dominates for constant capacity or slow growth, the local TSP term for fast growth, and the boundary case "is not covered" (Theorems 4.12–4.14, pp. 141–146; p. 143, where the OCR prints the uncovered case as "Q_n = O(n)", presumably Q_n of order √n).
  - Average-case failure of a classical heuristic. Under random lengths the nearest-neighbour tour has expected PTSP length of about p(1 − p)n/2, while re-optimization stays bounded (Theorem 3.22, pp. 107–109).
  - An approximation scheme that fails on time. Karp's partitioning converges completely to the optimal PTSP tour (Theorem 3.21, p. 106). But dynamic programming fails for the PTSP (p. 75), so the subsquares must be solved by enumeration and the scheme is superpolynomial; an O(c^n) exact algorithm would repair it (pp. 106–107).
  - Transferable: to suspect hardness, push the parameter to its limits and look for a known hard problem (pp. 38–39); to lower-bound an expected cost, dualize a node-potential LP (pp. 80–82); to get a √(np) law, use size-only instance cost plus Chernoff concentration plus a p → 1 sandwich (pp. 61–63).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation** (§3.7, pp. 110–123; the acknowledgments credit Louis Howell with a considerable part of this section, p. 4):
  - Scope: the Euclidean PTSP only (pp. 22, 110). The PMST, the PVRP and the facility problems get no computation.
  - Methods: the spacefilling curve with heapsort, O(n log n), faster than evaluating its own expected length and independent of the p_i, shown on 5,000 nodes (pp. 110–111). Local search to a local optimum (3.39) from varied starts (p. 110). 2-opt and 3-opt TSP routines, with 3-opt pruned by a bound built from the longest tour link and the shortest distance (p. 112). 2-p-opt made affordable by scanning moves in a fixed order with two auxiliary matrices, at O(n) per move in phase 1 and O(1) per move in phase 2 (pp. 113–114). The 1-shift neighbourhood (move one node) comes from a structural observation: in the PTSP, global coupling grows as p falls, so reversing a long section is a major perturbation, not a minor one. 1-shift then uses best-improvement because its gains can appear anywhere (pp. 114–116). Angular sorting is suggested by the near-starlike shape of low-p tours (p. 116).
  - Protocol: 10 random 100-node uniform instances are averaged, and E[L]/√(np) is read against the asymptotic line β_TSP ≈ .765, with a random tour as the floor baseline (pp. 119–120). Further runs: n = 25, 50, 100, 200 for convergence (Figure 3.8, p. 122); 2-opt TSP tours at n = 100 and 500 (Figure 3.9, p. 123); tours of one 150-node instance at four values of p (Figure 3.5, p. 117).
  - Findings: 3-opt is excellent for large p whatever the start; for small p the 1-shift solutions are "usually optimal", a verdict based on reaching the same tour from every start; the recommended pipeline is the curve, then 3-opt if p is large, then 1-shift; 2-p-opt rarely helps and "does not seem to be worth the trouble". A start-position confound is named: near p = 1, 2-opt beats 2-p-opt because the starts differ (p. 120).
  - Negative results and limits printed: angular sorting wins only in a narrow range near p = 0, and "we doubt that this algorithm will be very useful in practice" (p. 116). The 3-opt threshold is uncertain and problem-dependent. Beyond a few hundred nodes, run time and the memory for the distance and auxiliary matrices become excessive, forcing heuristics without O(n²) memory (p. 120). Convergence to the asymptote is nonuniform, explained by the low-p regime in which even two present nodes are unlikely (pp. 120–121).
  - Stress test of the incumbent: at n = 100 the TSP tours look "surprisingly good", which would make Jaillet's error bound a false alarm and the PTSP pointless. At n = 500 the TSP tours degrade with n, so the small-n result had masked the gap (pp. 121–123).
  - Not reported: timings, machine, real data, certificates. The conclusion's claim that the PTSP heuristics are "within 5% of the optimium" (p. 161) has no corresponding number in §3.7 (pp. 110–123).
  - Transferable: run the deterministic incumbent on the stochastic objective at two sizes before claiming the stochastic model matters (pp. 121–123); choose the neighbourhood by asking which moves are minor under the new objective (pp. 114–116).
- **D6 Positioning**: Three literatures are joined: probabilistic analysis in the Euclidean plane (Beardwood–Halton–Hammersley, Karp 1977, Steele 1981), analysis with random lengths (Karp 1979, Frieze 1985, Steele 1987, Walkup 1979), and the PCOP literature, called "very sparse" (Jaillet 1985; Jaillet and Odoni 1988) (pp. 17–18). Stochastic-programming VRPs (Stewart–Golden, Dror–Trudeau, Laporte–Louveaux) are set aside as differently defined, not attacked (pp. 18–19, 124). Network reliability is distinguished by its questions and its motivation, and random graphs, simulated annealing and random NP-completeness are named as not relevant (p. 19). The predecessor is treated by sharpening: Jaillet's results are reviewed to keep the thesis self-contained (p. 72), his n-prime condition is called an "unnatural distinction" and removed for odd n (pp. 78–80), and his asymptotic constant is identified (pp. 102–104). Berman and Simchi-Levi's TSFLP heuristic is called good in a section title, analysed more sharply (pp. 150–153) and criticized for giving a location without its cost (pp. 153–154). Their 1988a algorithm for the PTSFLP with a given a priori tour, which shows that this problem predates the thesis, is beaten by a factor n (pp. 148, 156). Platzman and Bartholdi's O(1) conjecture is adopted as the hinge of three results, always labelled a conjecture (pp. 100–101, 164–165). The TSP is presented as the field's testing ground, from asymptotics and dynamic programming to cutting planes, Lagrangian relaxation and polyhedral combinatorics (p. 68). The bibliography has 66 entries, with one self-reference, in preparation (p. 166), and one private communication from Odoni (p. 171).
- **D7 Writing**: Chapters 2–4 share one order: informal definition (with a figure in Chapters 2 and 4), roadmap, applications, closed form, complexity, combinatorial properties and special cases, probabilistic analysis, heuristics (pp. 23–25, 68–72, 124–127). The five research areas announced in the overview (pp. 19–22) form this skeleton. Surprise is the recurrent framing: "surprising" or "surprisingly" appears ten times (pp. 21, 24, 87, 90, 100, 121, 146, 160, and twice on 161). Conjectures sit beside theorems and are numbered (Conjectures 2.20, 3.18, 5.6; the Steiner ratio of 1.15, pp. 54, 63). Hedges sit on the empirical claims ("it is hoped", p. 110; "seems" and "probably", p. 120; "we doubt", p. 116). Claims grow stronger from body to conclusion. The random-length result is "asymptotically better" (p. 54) and then "better" (p. 161), though what is proved is pζ(3) ≤ E[L_Tp] ≤ ζ(3), the re-optimization limit, that is, at least as good (Proposition 2.19, p. 66); strictness needs Conjecture 2.20 (p. 66). The "within 25%" of the spacefilling-curve heuristic rests on a simulation constant of 0.956 from Platzman and Bartholdi set against β_TSP (pp. 105, 159, 161). "Within 5% of the optimium" has no number behind it in §3.7 (p. 161). The closing register is aspirational: "excellent potential", and the hope to have broadened understanding of "this very fascinating new class" (p. 165). Two cross-reference slips: "Theorem 3.18" for Theorem 3.19 (p. 104), and "section 3.6.4" for the partitioning scheme of §3.6.3 (p. 165).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are theory and algorithms: one new problem, the PMST (p. 19), and a formal statement of the PTSFLP, presented as "fundamentally different" from the TSFLP (p. 154) although Berman and Simchi-Levi (1988a) had already solved it for a given a priori tour (p. 148); complexity results, bounds, asymptotic theorems, worst-case and asymptotically optimal heuristics, and a PTSP heuristic study. No software is released and no application is carried out (pp. 19–22, 160–161). The general conclusions: the a priori strategies update in linear time and are asymptotically equivalent to re-optimization in every problem considered (p. 160); heuristics for the deterministic problems do well when presence probabilities are large and can be bad when they are small (p. 161). The limits are concrete: O(log n), not O(1), for the spacefilling curve (pp. 100–101); the even-n case of the cycle argument (p. 80); the uncovered capacity regime (p. 143); the superpolynomial partitioning scheme (pp. 106–107); memory and run time beyond a few hundred nodes (p. 120); the Steiner strategy left unanalysed in the random-length model for lack of sharp theorems (pp. 66–67). A finding that also limits the analysis [inferred]: the location component of the TSFLP and PTSFLP vanishes asymptotically, so the asymptotics cannot rank locations (pp. 157–159). Future work: the probabilistic shortest path, assignment and m-PTSP problems, announced as a report in preparation (pp. 162–164, 166); and open problems: prove the spacefilling curve O(1), on which three results hinge; find an O(c^n) exact PTSP algorithm, which would give an approximation scheme; find constant-factor heuristics for the PTSP and the PMST on general networks (pp. 164–165). The hinge broke within a year: Bertsimas and Grigni (1989) showed the O(log n) bound tight (S038 p. 10; S161 in s01).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: formulation first, a dual device and a printed boundary are all present in 1988, but class preservation is not. The re-solve chore becomes one a priori problem with an expected-cost objective (pp. 12–13). LP duality gives lower bounds through a transportation problem, "by using a mathematical programming rather than a combinatorial approach" (pp. 80–82, 134–135): a dual device earlier than any Method 1 evidence the SKILL cites (1993–1995). The boundary is printed: NP-completeness even with unit costs (pp. 33–38), Σ2^P (p. 93), O(log n) against a conjectured O(1) (pp. 100–101), open problems (pp. 164–165). But the probabilistic version leaves the polynomial class (the MST is solved greedily, the PMST is NP-complete), and the thesis prices this loss instead of choosing a class-keeping device (p. 29); no solve times are measured for the hard problems.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: the property is the robustness and globality of an a priori solution, and its price is stated in tractability (p. 29). The knob is the coverage probability p: using the deterministic solution costs a ratio in p that is O(n) as p → 0 and tight by example (pp. 43–44); TSP heuristics' guarantees degrade like c/p (p. 97); the optimal value is drawn as a curve in p with both limits (pp. 40–42, 84–85). Differences: p is a model parameter, not a protection level chosen by a decision-maker, and there is no violation-probability bound. [inferred] The 1988 phrase pricing robustness predates *The price of robustness* (2004) by 16 years; no text links the two.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: spacefilling-curve tours warm-start 2-p-opt and 1-shift (pp. 110, 116), and the deterministic incumbent (TSP tours) is dueled on the stochastic objective at a second, larger size that reverses the small-n verdict (pp. 121–123). There is no exact solve and no certificate: "usually optimal" rests on convergence from every start (p. 120), and the conclusion's "within 5%" (p. 161) has no number in §3.7, an early instance of Tension T1. When memory runs out beyond a few hundred nodes, the study falls back on the sorting heuristic (p. 120), a loose analogue of Heuristic 3.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: strategies are judged by expected cost against the re-optimization ideal (p. 160), not against an owner's current rule, and the applications are motivations without data (pp. 27–28, 71–72, 126). Two elements point toward Method 5. Operator priorities define the strategies (network structure unchanged, p. 27; the same vehicle and driver, pp. 71, 127). And Chapter 5 rejects a heuristic that leaves the planner knowing a good location "without knowing the cost of this decision" (p. 154), replacing it with one that returns an operating tour for every instance and its expected cost (p. 156).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅, for the lineage step only: the problem class came from his adviser's group (pp. 18, 71, 125), which documents the origin that the SKILL's Research Trajectory marks [inferred] for 1985–1995. The thesis's extensions and open problems (pp. 162–166) seed S038, S148 and S161 (cards c01, s01). The gate, course, book and company steps are not visible.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅: negative results are printed in the body: angular sorting doubted (p. 116), memory limits (p. 120), the nearest neighbour O(n) on average (pp. 107–109), the superpolynomial partitioning scheme (pp. 106–107), the unproved even case (p. 80).
- Tension T3 (optimization, not probability): the thesis is the practice side; probability theory is its main instrument throughout (pp. 2–3, 53–67, 101–109).
- New-pattern candidate: *a priori solution with a cheap update rule, priced against re-solving* (pp. 12–13, 26, 160). The thesis is the origin; S038, S017 and S148 recur it.
- New-pattern candidate: *asymptotic regimes for sharp constants* (pp. 55–63, 102–104, 141–146).
- New-pattern candidate: *carry deterministic devices into the stochastic version and measure what survives*: tree doubling survives (p. 86), the constant TSP survives (p. 91), TSP-heuristic guarantees weaken with p (p. 97), non-crossing of optimal tours and dynamic programming fail (p. 75), so Karp's partitioning loses polynomial time (pp. 106–107).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D1) For a problem re-solved on many perturbed instances, optimize the deployment rule (an a priori solution and a trivial update) for expected cost, and compare it with the re-solve ideal both in the worst case and asymptotically (pp. 12–13, 160).
- (D4) Write an expectation over 2^n instances edge by edge: each edge's cost times the probability that it is used, by inclusion–exclusion on "no node present" events, which also covers dependent presence; order the sum by peeling leaves to make it linear-time (Theorems 2.1–2.2, pp. 30–32).
- (D4) To guess whether a parametrized problem is hard, push the parameter to its limits and see which known problem appears (p → 0 gives network design on a tree); then prove hardness by a reduction with an exchange step (pp. 33–39).
- (D4) Lower-bound a combinatorial expected cost by LP duality: subtract node potentials from the distances, keep the reduced costs nonnegative, and maximize; the dual is a transportation problem, and no triangle inequality is needed (pp. 80–82, 134–135).
- (D4) For a √(np) law: show that an instance's expected cost depends only on how many nodes are present, concentrate that number with a Chernoff bound, then pin the constant with sandwich bounds as p → 1 (pp. 55–63, 102–104).
- (D4) When a capacity can grow with n, split the asymptotics by its growth against √n (radial against local term) and state the regime left uncovered (pp. 141–146).
- (D5) Choose the local-search neighbourhood by asking which moves are minor under the new objective (global coupling at low p makes section reversal major, so move a single node), and switch from first- to best-improvement when gains can appear anywhere (pp. 114–116).
- (D5) Before claiming that the stochastic model matters, run the deterministic incumbent on it at two sizes; a small instance can hide the gap (pp. 121–123).
