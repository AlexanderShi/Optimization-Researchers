### S057 · Conservation laws, extended polymatroids and multiarmed bandit problems; a polyhedral approach to indexable systems (Mathematics of Operations Research 1996, DOI 10.1287/moor.21.2.257)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–50, every section and proof as printed, with the references (pp. 48–50). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Niño-Mora match (p. 1). The OCR garbles most displayed formulas, among them Figure 3's algorithm, the conservation-law right-hand sides and the transform computations. Formula content therefore comes from theorem statements and prose, and the proofs were followed through their stated steps.
**Role**: core · **Coauthor**: José Niño-Mora (Operations Research Center, MIT, p. 50). His 1995 Sloan PhD dissertation is cited for one proof (pp. 14, 49), and the SKILL lists him in the SD lineage · **Position in trajectory**: the paper behind SKILL SW1. It was received in March 1993 and revised three times, last in August 1995 (p. 1); a preliminary version appeared at IPCO 1993 in Erice (p. 48). Funding: his Presidential Young Investigator award, with matching funds from Draper Laboratory (p. 48). Three companion papers from the same group are cited: Bertsimas–Paschalidis–Tsitsiklis on networks, which gives bounds, and on branching-bandit stability (pp. 4, 32), and Bertsimas–Teo's primal-dual algorithm, which coincides with adaptive greedy on extended polymatroids (p. 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + LO; IO's polyhedral toolkit}: The entry is a gap between two traditions. Mathematical programming defines variables and constraints, describes the feasible space and applies algorithms. Stochastic scheduling relies primarily on dynamic programming, using "ingenious but often ad hoc methods" (p. 1). The opening borrows a complaint from a survey of deterministic scheduling (Lawler, Lenstra, Rinnooy Kan and Shmoys 1989, quoting Coffman, Hofri and Weiss): results in stochastic scheduling are scattered and hard-won (pp. 1–2).
  - The multiarmed bandit is the field's success story; the original proof and the seven later proofs or simplifications are listed by type (p. 2).
  - Table 1 lists 14 indexable problem–criterion rows (p. 3).
  - Three questions follow: which problems are indexable, when they are decomposable, and, above all, whether a general approach gives structural understanding (p. 3).
  - The stated goal is to derive the problems' structural properties from properties of the polyhedra of achievable performance (p. 4).
  - The paper states a method for SD jointly with Niño-Mora, attributing it to the previous decade's work: describe the feasible space as a polyhedron, translate the problem into optimization over it, then attack it with mathematical programming (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Choose performance vectors that satisfy *generalized conservation laws*: expected discounted completions, completion times or time in system. Under every policy a weighted total is invariant. For each subset S of classes, a weighted subtotal is extremal under any policy that gives priority to the classes outside S (Definition 5, pp. 19–20).
  - The achievable region is then an extended (contra-)polymatroid whose vertices are the static-priority policies (Theorem 4, p. 20).
  - A linear objective over it is solved by a one-pass adaptive greedy algorithm (Figure 3, p. 10; Proposition 1, pp. 11–12).
  - Summing the algorithm's dual variables gives the allocation indices, which are the Gittins indices (Definition 4, p. 13; Proposition 5, p. 30; p. 41).
  - Against the nearest classics: Shanthikumar–Yao's strong conservation laws are the special case with all weights 1, which gives a polymatroid (p. 20). Tsoucas introduced extended polymatroids for Klimov's problem and proved adaptive greedy optimal directly (pp. 9–10, 12).
  - What changes: the weights A_i^S, a duality proof, a decomposition condition (Assumption 2, p. 17), and one general model, the branching bandit, which covers bandits, Klimov's queue and batch scheduling (pp. 22–23). What stays: the route from conservation laws to a polyhedron to a priority rule (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**: The model is the branching bandit (pp. 23–39):
  - A single server serves finitely many job classes. Each class has a random service time and a random set of offspring jobs, with an arbitrary joint distribution, independent across jobs (p. 23).
  - Policies must be nonanticipative, nonidling and nonpreemptive (p. 23).
  - Two criteria: a discounted reward–tax criterion with rate α > 0 (p. 24), and an undiscounted holding tax. The undiscounted case needs Assumption 3: the spectral radius of E[N] is below 1, which gives stability by Bertsimas–Paschalidis–Tsitsiklis 1994b (p. 32). It also needs all expectations to be finite (p. 33). The necessary and sufficient conditions promised there for finiteness are not given later in the paper.
  - The data enter through joint transforms Φ_i and the initial population L(0) (pp. 30–31). For the multiarmed bandit, processing times are unit and offspring multinomial (p. 39).
  - Assumptions taken for the polyhedral theory: Assumption 1 (every priority vector v(π) is feasible) defines the class (p. 9), and Assumption 2 is needed for decomposition (p. 17). The fixed point (42) has a closed form only for exponential service times (p. 32).
  - No data sets or instances are used.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Adaptive greedy is optimal by LP duality. The algorithm builds a dual solution supported on a nested family S_1 ⊃ … ⊃ S_n. Dual feasibility is shown by induction, primal feasibility follows from Assumption 1, and complementary slackness closes the proof (Proposition 1, pp. 11–12). The operation counts are polynomial, and the method is oracle-polynomial; the exponents are garbled in the extraction (p. 12).
  - The vertices are the priority vectors: each vertex is the unique maximizer of some linear objective, and the algorithm always returns some v(π) (Theorem 1, p. 13). With all A_i^S = 1 the algorithm reduces to the classical greedy algorithm, which shows that b is submodular (remark after Theorem 1, after Edmonds, p. 13).
  - The indices are well defined under ties, via an unambiguous version of the algorithm that returns a partition (Figure 4; Proposition 2, pp. 14–15). Equivalent optimality conditions (Proposition 3, p. 15) give three consequences on p. 16: an O(n log n) optimality test; indices that are piecewise linear in the rewards; a closed form for the optimal value.
  - Index decomposition: under Assumption 2 the global indices equal the per-project indices (Theorem 3, pp. 17–18; Corollary 1). The condition fails in general for Klimov's problem (p. 18).
  - Conservation laws for branching bandits. Equality under priority policies follows from a partition of the busy period (Proposition 4, pp. 23–24). Nonanticipativity makes the selection times independent of the service times and offspring (pp. 27, 35). The inequality for every other policy is proved by a pairwise *sample-path interchange* (Figure 5, pp. 28–29; again pp. 35–36). The interchange argument of the older proofs (p. 2) is not eliminated, then: it moves into the verification of the conservation laws.
  - Two reductions. Bell's (1971) accounting turns holding taxes into rewards: an entrance charge plus a departure refund (p. 25). A retirement option is a class 0 with infinite service and a pension r_0; by decomposition, the index equals the break-even pension (Proposition 5, p. 30).
  - A and b are computed from Laplace transforms through the fixed point (37) (Proposition 6, pp. 31–32). In the undiscounted case they come from linear systems and the conditional-variance formula (pp. 37–38).
  - For the multiarmed bandit, A and b come from the linear system (65) (Proposition 7, p. 40). The Gittins–Jones decomposition follows from Theorem 3 (Theorem 11, p. 41), and the Varaiya–Walrand–Buyukkoc algorithm is shown equivalent by induction (Proposition 9, p. 42). The optimal reward Z(H) is the expectation of a maximum of independent random indices (Theorem 12, p. 43), via a product form of b (Proposition 10, p. 43). Submodularity follows at once (Corollary 5, p. 44).
  - For Klimov's queue, the time-average criterion reduces to one busy period, after Nain, Tsoucas and Walrand. This gives a top-down algorithm with explicit parameters. Rescaling by K_{S^c} removes the arrival rates, which explains why the optimal priorities do not depend on them (pp. 46–47).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a: pure theory. Algorithms are judged by operation counts (p. 12) and by matching the complexity of the fastest known off-line algorithm for Gittins indices (p. 42). No instance is solved.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues the polyhedral line (pp. 3–4): Coffman–Mitrani, Gelenbe–Mitrani, Federgruen–Groenevelt, Ross–Yao, Shanthikumar–Yao, Tsoucas, and Bertsimas–Paschalidis–Tsitsiklis. It generalizes Shanthikumar–Yao (p. 4).
  - Credit is explicit:
    - Tsoucas introduced extended polymatroids, and Bhattacharya–Georgiadis–Tsoucas named them (pp. 9–10).
    - Tsoucas gave the first optimality proof of adaptive greedy (p. 12).
    - Weber first proved submodularity (p. 44).
    - Whittle and Weber gave the retirement interpretation (p. 30).
    - Lai–Ying and Nain–Tsoucas–Walrand gave the top-down Klimov algorithm (p. 47).
  - The earlier proofs of the Gittins result are listed without judgement (p. 2).
  - The one critique is specific: Tsoucas gives no closed forms for the right-hand sides, so the performance of the optimal policy cannot be evaluated, and his algorithm runs bottom-up (p. 46).
  - Uptake before print: Glazebrook (1994) had already applied "these polyhedral results" to suboptimality bounds (pp. 16, 49).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract lists ten consequences and the introduction eleven (pp. 1, 4).
  - A fully worked two-armed bandit comes before any theory (§2, pp. 5–8).
  - Three tables map the field (Table 1, p. 3), the LP theory (Table 2, p. 19) and the branching-bandit formulations (Table 3, p. 38). Theorems carry descriptive names.
  - Hedging is low. The conclusion is two sentences, with "we believe" and "we hope" (p. 48); they are all that §1's promise of "some thoughts on the field" (p. 5) delivers.
  - One claim outruns its proof. Abstract item (1) and contribution 1 promise "a characterization of indexable systems as systems that satisfy generalized conservation laws" (pp. 1, 4). The body proves only the sufficient direction: conservation laws give an extended polymatroid, which gives optimal priority-index policies (Theorems 4–5, pp. 20–21). No converse is stated. For decomposition the paper does say "sufficient" (p. 21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - **Theory**: a polytope class "which has a genuinely applied origin" (p. 48), with its LP theory.
  - **Algorithms**: adaptive greedy (Figure 3, p. 10); a top-down Klimov algorithm (pp. 46–47).
  - **Unification**: Klimov's and Gittins' algorithms as one algorithm (p. 4; Table 3, p. 38).
  - **New proofs**: the Gittins decomposition by LP duality (p. 41); submodularity (p. 44).
  - **Closed formulas**: pp. 16, 43.

  Limits printed in the body: decomposition fails for Klimov's problem in general (p. 18); the fixed point has no closed form in general (p. 32); finite expectations are assumed (p. 33); nonlinear objectives are left to Bhattacharya et al. (p. 22). The scope is a single server: networks appear only through the 1994a bounds paper (p. 4). No future work is stated (p. 48).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 3, 11–13, 20–21, 30, 41): the paper is the template for steps 1–4.
  - Step 1 names the owning tradition: dynamic programming with ad hoc methods (p. 1).
  - Step 2 takes variables over a describable set, the performance vectors (pp. 6, 19).
  - Step 3, the class-keeping device: conservation laws make the region an LP feasible set whose vertices are priority rules (p. 20).
  - Step 4 reads structure off the dual. The indices are sums of dual variables (p. 13), "a purely algebraic characterization" (p. 41), and they are also break-even pensions (p. 30).
  - Step 5, the boundary, is only partial: Klimov's indices do not decompose (p. 18), and the fixed point has no closed form (p. 32).
  - Correction for SKILL SW1: "Indexable systems characterized exactly" should be replaced by *a sufficient condition for indexability* (pp. 20–21).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅, for the lineage step. The IPCO 1993 version became a journal paper with a student whose 1995 thesis it fed (pp. 1, 14, 48–49). The companion papers with Paschalidis, Tsitsiklis and C.-P. Teo (pp. 4, 12, 32) form the cohort that the SKILL lists for SD. The gate, course and book steps are not visible here.
- Domain Map SD row ("no stated methodology found"): this paper states one, jointly with Niño-Mora (pp. 1, 3; see D1).
- Tension T3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 23–38, 48): the polyhedral reformulation rests on applied probability (busy-period decompositions, transforms, sample-path interchanges), and the conclusion addresses applied probabilists first (p. 48).
- Tension T6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 4, 20–21): "characterization" in the abstract, sufficiency in the body.
- New-pattern candidate: *unify classical algorithms as one algorithm on one polytope, with a table of special cases* (pp. 3–4, 22, 38, 42, 46–47). The cμ rule, Smith's rule, Klimov's algorithm and the Gittins-index algorithms, including Varaiya–Walrand–Buyukkoc's, all appear as adaptive greedy.
- New-pattern candidate: *permutation-indexed polytopes*. The vertices are orders, n! of them, and one greedy pass optimizes over them (pp. 8, 13). Compare S024's permutohull, handled by the dual of an assignment LP (S024 p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate: *points of a convex region read as randomizations*. By Carathéodory, any achievable performance vector is a randomization of at most n static-priority rules (p. 20). Compare S033, where an SDP solution is read as a covariance (S033 pp. 5–7), and S024, where a risk measure is a worst-case expectation over reweightings (S024 pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) To prove that a control problem is solved by a priority rule, find weights A_i^S and a set function b such that each subset S satisfies a conservation law, with equality when the classes outside S have priority. Theorems 4–5 then give the region and the index policy, with no further dynamic programming (pp. 19–21).
- (D4) To prove a greedy algorithm optimal, construct its dual solution on a nested family of sets, check dual feasibility by induction, and close with complementary slackness (pp. 11–12).
- (D4) For subset inequalities over all policies, prove equality under the priority policy by decomposing the busy period. Then prove the inequality for every other policy by one pairwise sample-path interchange (pp. 23–24, 28–29).
- (D4) To interpret an index, add a retirement option that pays r_0 and show that the break-even pension equals the index (p. 30). To handle holding costs, charge each job's lifetime holding cost on entry and refund it on departure, which turns taxes into rewards (p. 25).
- (D7) Run the whole method on the smallest nontrivial example before the general theory: here the two-armed bandit, posed as a three-class closed queue (pp. 5–8). Put prior results in a table of problem, criterion and who proved what, and mark the cells the paper fills (Table 1, p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "For the most part, the tradition for stochastic and dynamic scheduling problems has been, however, quite different, as it relies primarily on dynamic programming formulations." (p. 1)
- "Faced with these results, one asks what is the underlying reason that these nontrivial problems have very efficient solutions, both in theory and in practice." (p. 3)

### S033 · Semidefinite relaxations, multivariate normal distributions, and order statistics (Handbook of Combinatorial Optimization 1998, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4613-0303-9_24)
Read level: **full**: file pp. 1–17: the ResearchGate cover (p. 1), the whole text (pp. 2–16) and the references (pp. 16–17). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Ye match (p. 2).
**Version**: the file is the authors' preprint, a manuscript dated November 1997 (p. 2), which Bertsimas uploaded to ResearchGate on 12 August 2014 (p. 1). It is not the Handbook print, whose pp. 1473–1491 are not in the file. Cite as [S033 preprint p. N]. In the extraction, ligatures, relation signs and π survive only as invisible control characters, and minus signs are lost, so constants are checked against the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthor**: Yinyu Ye (Department of Management Sciences, University of Iowa, p. 2), now seated in the council's NLP delegation (product/nonlinear-team) · **Position in trajectory**: the SKILL's only joint work with a guest member (Academic Lineage), from the 1995–2001 CP period.
- The paper continues his papers on dependent randomized rounding with C.-P. Teo and R. Vohra (IPCO 1995 and 1996; p. 4, references p. 16).
- Funding: NSF DMI-9610486 for Bertsimas; DMI-9522507 and DMS-9703490 for Ye (p. 2).
- The SDPs were solved with Benson, Ye and Zhang's code, and Benson and Zhang are thanked for the computational test (pp. 15–16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + CP}: The paper lists recent approximation results (p. 4):
  - Goemans–Williamson (GW): 0.878 for max-cut and s–t max-cut.
  - Frieze–Jerrum: 0.65 for maximum bisection.
  - Nesterov: 2/π for boolean QP with positive semidefinite Q.
  - Ye: an extension to quadratic constraints.

  All of them follow GW's recipe: an SDP relaxation, then randomized rounding by the relaxation's geometry (p. 4). Two gaps are named. There is no SDP-based guarantee for s–u–v max-cut (p. 4), and, implicitly, no rounding that respects linear side constraints (pp. 3, 10). The problem class: maximize x'Qx subject to linear equalities and x_j² = 1 (pp. 3–4). The instances are graph bisection (GB), the two s–t max-cut variants (ST−, ST+) and s–u–v max-cut (SUV).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three roundings are proposed:
  - **H1** (pp. 5–7): read the SDP optimum X* as the covariance matrix of a zero-mean normal vector, sample x ~ N(0, X*) as x = V'u with iid standard normal u, and round by sign. Sheppard's 1900 formula gives E[x̂_i x̂_j] = (2/π) arcsin x*_ij (Proposition 2, p. 7). The authors stress that the method "does not use the geometry" of the relaxation (p. 5).
  - **H2**: a median split of iid uniform numbers, which needs no SDP (pp. 13–14).
  - **H3**: a median split of x ~ N(0, X*), putting the nodes above the median on one side (pp. 13–14).

  [My reading] H1 is distributed exactly like the GW rounding that the paper reviews on p. 6. sign(V'u) depends only on the direction of u, which is uniform both for a standard normal u and for u uniform on the ball. What is new in H1 is therefore the analysis. H3 is a new algorithm, because the median uses the magnitudes of x.
- **D3 Model and data**: Q is symmetric, and the linear equality constraints are a_i'x = b_i (p. 3). In §3.3 the constraints are homogeneous and Q is positive semidefinite (p. 10). The SDP relaxation is assumed feasible, which Nesterov, Todd and Ye's method can detect. Its dual has an interior, so there is no duality gap (p. 5). Data: the G-set max-bisection graphs of Helmberg and Rendl, generated by Rinaldi's rudy, with 800–3,000 nodes (p. 15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Z_QP ≤ Z_SD, since X = xx' is feasible (Proposition 1, p. 6).
  - Feasibility with probability 1, from moments. For ST−, E[(x̂_s + x̂_t)²] = 0 (Theorem 1, p. 8). For ST+, E[(x̂_s + x̂_t)²] = 4 and E[(x̂_s + x̂_t)⁴] = 16, so the square has zero variance and |x̂_s + x̂_t| = 2 almost surely (Theorem 2, p. 8). The objective bound uses the inequality behind GW's 0.878 (p. 8). For max-cut, the expected number of nodes on each side is n/2 (p. 8).
  - SUV: a lemma bounds the sum of the three arcsines by 3 arcsin(−1/3), by cases on their signs. As extracted, the display repeats arcsin(x12) three times (Lemma 1, p. 9). The trivariate orthant probability (Tong) then gives feasibility with probability > 0.912 and E[Z_H] ≥ 0.878 Z_SD (Theorem 3, Corollary 1, p. 9).
  - Constrained QP: a matrix sandwich X + ((π − 2)/2)(Σ x_jj) I ⪰ arcsin[X] ⪰ X, from the Taylor series of arcsin and positive semidefinite Hadamard powers. The right-hand inequality is Nesterov's (Lemma 2, p. 10). It yields three results (Theorem 4, pp. 10–11):
    - every constraint is satisfied in expectation;
    - the normalized violation is at most √(1 − 2/π) (my reconstruction from the proof; the display is garbled);
    - E[Z_H] ≥ (2/π) Z_SD.
  - Bisection: (2/π) a' arcsin[X] a ≤ 0.122 (e'a)², via GW's bound (Lemma 3, p. 12). The root-mean-square imbalance is then at most 0.3493 n (Theorem 5, p. 12). Chebyshev's inequality plus repeated sampling gives, in polynomial time, a cut within 0.878 of the bound with at least 32% of the nodes on each side (pp. 12–13).
  - H2 is analysed exactly. The event U_j < U_MED < U_i is an order-statistics configuration whose probability is a beta integral, so E[Z_H] = ((k − 1)/(2k − 1)) Σ c_ij (Theorem 6, pp. 13–14).
  - H3 is not analysed: the analysis "appears to be" hard, since it involves the median of correlated variables. A heuristic argument takes the proof's place (p. 14): e'X*e = 0 makes the sample mean exactly 0, so the median should be near 0 and the 0.878 analysis should nearly apply.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: "We have solved 54 problems" (p. 15). Table 1 shows 25 "representative" G-set graphs without saying how they were chosen (pp. 15–16).
  - Columns: Z_SD; the average |e'x̂| of H1 over 2,000 samples (3.38–21.15); the best H3 value over 2,000 samples, always feasible; and Z_H/Z_SD (pp. 15–16).
  - The ratios run from 0.7819 (G19, 800 nodes) to 0.9819 (G50, 3,000 nodes). G18–G20 lie at 0.78–0.80, and G11–G13 and G32–G34 at 0.83–0.86, with no comment (p. 15).
  - Because Z_SD bounds the optimum (Proposition 1, p. 6), each ratio certifies a fraction of optimal.
  - Not reported: run times or machine; the GW or Frieze–Jerrum rounding on the same graphs; the 29 graphs not shown.
  - The abstract's "near optimal solutions" (p. 2) rests on this table.
- **D6 Positioning**: Credit for the central idea goes to GW (p. 4). Lovász–Schrijver are credited for the relaxation, and Nesterov–Nemirovskii and Alizadeh for its polynomial solvability (p. 5).
  - Bellare–Rogaway's negative results are cited (p. 4), and Frieze–Jerrum's similar bisection results are acknowledged (p. 12).
  - The advantage over GW is claimed softly: it is "not obvious" that GW's technique is feasible in expectation (p. 11). [My reading] By the equivalence in D2 it is, so the claim covers GW as well.
  - The probability sources are classical: Sheppard 1900, Johnson–Kotz and Tong (pp. 5, 7, 9).
- **D7 Writing**: The contributions are enumerated with items (a)–(d) and mirrored in the abstract (pp. 2, 4–5).
  - The age of the probability formula is highlighted: it dates from 1900 (p. 5).
  - The conjecture sits in the contribution list (p. 5), backed by a labelled "heuristic argument" and a table (pp. 14–16).
  - There is no conclusion section: the text ends with Table 1's description and an acknowledgment (pp. 15–16). The proofs are terse (pp. 6–14).
  - Two contribution-list results are rounded up. H2's 0.5 (p. 5) is (1/2)(1 − 1/(n − 1)) in Theorem 6 (p. 14). The 0.878 for bisection allows a 32% balance, not an exact bisection (pp. 12–13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - **Contributions** (pp. 4–5): an analysis framework for normal rounding; guarantees for s–t and s–u–v max-cut, balanced max-cut and linearly constrained boolean QP; a simple bisection heuristic without SDP; a conjectured near-0.878 bisection heuristic with computational support.
  - **Stated limits**: H3 is unanalysed (p. 14); the constrained-QP result holds only in expectation, with a bounded violation (pp. 10–11).
  - **Unstated limits**: those listed in D5.
  - **Future work**: implicit in the conjecture (pp. 5, 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3–6): the exact boolean QP does not stay in its class. It is relaxed to a polynomially solvable SDP (p. 5), and the boundary is printed as approximation ratios, not solve times. The dual only rules out a gap (p. 5). Reading the SDP solution as second moments (p. 5) is akin to his moment line.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 15–16): the certificate step is present, since each instance's value is reported as a ratio to the SDP bound. But the incumbent GW and Frieze–Jerrum roundings are not run, no time limits or run times are reported, and H3 is scored by its best of 2,000 samples.
- Tension T3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 5, 7, 9, 12–14): the decisive devices come from probability (Sheppard's formula, orthant probabilities, order statistics, Chebyshev).
- Tension T6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 5, 14–15): "near optimal" for ratios down to 0.78 of the bound; "0.5" for (1/2)(1 − 1/(n − 1)); [my reading] "new" for a rounding distributed as GW's.
- Roundtable Card, likely disagreement "Ye (1998 co-author), Nesterov: self-certifying, complexity-first methods". Here, jointly with Ye, Bertsimas wrote in that very style. The paper proves approximation ratios, certifies each instance by the SDP bound and builds on Nesterov's 2/π result (pp. 4, 10–11, 15).
- New-pattern candidate: *a relaxation's solution read as a probability distribution and rounded by sampling* (pp. 5–7). Compare S057's randomized priority rules (S057 p. 20) and S024's reweighted samples behind a risk constraint (S024 pp. 4–5).
- New-pattern candidate: *when the proof fails: a conjecture, a labelled heuristic argument and computations* (pp. 5, 14–16).

**Transferable techniques**:

- (D5) Report heuristic values as ratios to the relaxation bound, so that each instance carries its own certificate (p. 15).

### S024 · Constructing uncertainty sets for robust linear optimization (Operations Research 2009, DOI 10.1287/opre.1080.0646)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–14, with the endnotes (p. 13) and the references (p. 14). P. 1 is a JSTOR cover page. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Brown match (pp. 1–2). **Role**: core · **Coauthor**: David B. Brown (Fuqua School of Business, Duke University, p. 2), listed in the SKILL's RO lineage · **Position in trajectory**: the RO period. The paper was received in July 2007, revised in March 2008 and accepted in April 2008 (p. 2). It comes after his data-free norm and budget sets of 2004, which it cites among the ad hoc constructions (p. 2). It comes before the group's data-driven sets of 2018, named in SKILL Method 3. A paper by Natarajan, Pachamanova and Sim, done independently at the same time, takes the reverse direction (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO; risk theory from finance (AP) supplies the primitive}: The entry is a gap in his own field. RO computes solutions that stay feasible for every A in a set, yet the literature says almost nothing about how to construct that set (p. 2).
  - Ellipsoids and other norms are the common choice, with Bertsimas et al. 2004 cited as an example. They have statistical motivations, but the paper calls them "fundamentally ad hoc" and chosen to preserve tractability (p. 2).
  - Two practical issues frame the problem. The distribution is unknown and only N observations exist. Even with the distribution in hand, it is unclear how to build the set (p. 4).
  - The problem: from a decision maker's coherent risk measure and the samples, construct the equivalent uncertainty set for one uncertain linear constraint (pp. 2–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: The representation theorem says a coherent risk measure is a worst-case expectation over a family Q of measures (Theorem 2.1, p. 4). The paper reads it as a duality statement. On a finite sample space, a constraint μ(a'x − b) ≤ 0 is the same as a robust constraint over U = conv{Aq : q ∈ Q}. Conversely, any convex U inside the convex hull of the data induces a coherent risk measure (Theorem 3.1, p. 5).
  - The paper then restricts to distortion risk measures: coherent, comonotonic and law invariant (Definition 4.5, p. 7). These induce polyhedral sets of a special form, the q-permutohulls (Definition 4.7, p. 9).
  - The robust counterparts over these sets are polynomial-size LPs (Theorem 4.3, p. 9). All of them are generated by N CVaR measures (Theorem 4.2, p. 8; Corollary 4.3, p. 9).
  - Against his 2004 sets, the primitive changes: a risk preference plus the data replaces a norm or budget picked for tractability (pp. 2–3). What stays is the robust LP, which duality keeps an LP (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**: The model is one uncertain constraint a'x ≥ b. Several constraints are handled one at a time, because vector-valued risk measures are "very much open" (p. 4).
  - Assumption 3.1 makes the probability space finite, with N atoms that are the data (p. 5). Shortcomings are admitted: N may be far smaller than the true sample space, and events outside the samples cannot be captured (p. 5). Remark 3.1: the Section 3 results do not need finiteness (p. 5).
  - Assumption 4.1 gives every sample the probability 1/N. Rational probabilities can be handled by replicating samples, at the price of a larger robust problem (p. 7).
  - Remark 4.2: Assumption 4.1 is critical for one direction of Lemma 4.1, as a counterexample with probabilities 1/3 and 2/3 shows (p. 8).
  - The Fatou property holds automatically on a finite Ω (endnote 3, p. 13). No real data set is used.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 3.1 follows from the representation theorem plus one fact: the infimum of a linear function over a bounded set equals its infimum over the convex hull (p. 5).
  - Three examples show the induced sets (pp. 5–6):
    - scenario-based measures give the convex hull of the scenario points;
    - CVaR with equal weights and α = j/N gives the convex hull of all j-point averages of the data;
    - one-sided-moment measures (Fischer) give norm-bounded sets.
  - Law invariance is argued from estimability. If X and Y have the same distribution but μ(X) ≠ μ(Y), any estimator built from samples is asymptotically wrong for one of them (p. 7).
  - Lemma 4.1 says that in the equal-weights discrete case the distortion measures are exactly the mixtures of CVaRs. The proof starts from Schmeidler's Choquet representation (Theorem 4.1, p. 7). Law invariance makes the capacity a function θ of probability. Submodularity, read as nonincreasing second differences, makes θ concave, and Föllmer–Schied's Theorem 4.64 then applies (pp. 7–8). The authors write that, as far as they know, this case "has not been shown", and that the proof follows the atomless case (p. 7).
  - Theorem 4.2: the N vectors of the CVaR_{j/N} measures generate the restricted simplex, with explicit nonnegative weights (p. 8).
  - Theorem 4.3: the robust constraint over Π_q(A) is an LP with 2N extra variables and N² constraints, obtained through the strong dual of an assignment-type LP over doubly stochastic weights. Π_q(A) may have one extreme point per permutation, up to N!; the count printed in the text is garbled. For CVaR the counterpart needs O(N) extra variables and constraints (p. 9).
  - Sets that are centrally symmetric through the sample mean are those with q = 2e_N − q_σ (Proposition 4.1, pp. 9–10). This subclass is generated by ⌊N/2⌋ + 1 measures (Theorem 4.4, pp. 10–11). Its sets induce a norm, through the Minkowski functional (Proposition 4.2, pp. 11–12).
  - Inner approximation: shrinking a symmetric set toward the mean scales its norm (Lemma 4.2, p. 12). One LP finds the largest such scaled copy inside any polytope. The copy corresponds to a distortion measure iff the optimal λ* satisfies (16), which keeps q nonnegative; (16) is garbled here (Theorem 4.5, pp. 12–13). In risk-measure terms this is a problem like the Sharpe ratio, with a deviation measure in place of the standard deviation (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a: no computational study. Figures 1–4 illustrate the sets on two-dimensional examples with N = 5 and N = 6 (pp. 9–13). Tractability is argued by counting variables and constraints (p. 9), not by timing.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + HS; AP (engineering design) as testbed}: The abstract opens with the engineer's experience: an optimized design turns out suboptimal once errors occur (p. 1). Two failures follow.
  - Stochastic optimization needs distributions that are "seldom available", and chance constraints are intractable (p. 1).

- **D2 Core idea**: Carry descent theory over to the worst-case cost g.
  - A direction d is a descent direction for g iff it has a negative inner product with every worst implementation error (Theorem 1, pp. 3–4).
  - A robust local minimum is a point with no such direction (Definition 2, Proposition 1, p. 4).
  - The best direction solves an SOCP that maximizes the angle to the worst errors (problem (9), p. 5).
  - In practice the worst errors are unknown. Any set of high-cost neighbours whose conic hull contains them suffices (Proposition 2, pp. 5–6).
  - The result is the practical robust local search (Algorithm 2, §3, pp. 6–8). It explores the neighbourhood by n + 1 gradient ascents, then makes an SOCP move that pushes the bad neighbours out of the new neighbourhood.
  - Parameter uncertainty is folded in by treating the parameters as decision variables pinned at their estimates (problem (28), p. 12).

  What changed from 2007: a directional-derivative framework replaces a purely geometric one, so the method can serve as a building block for other gradient-based routines (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**: f is unconstrained and continuously differentiable, with value and gradient from a subroutine. In the electromagnetic (EM) design problem the adjoint method gives the gradient at no extra cost (pp. 2, 11).
  - The errors lie in an ℓ₂ ball; other p-norms are possible (p. 3).
  - The convergence theorem assumes convex f with a bounded set of minima, an oracle for all worst errors, and diminishing, non-summable steps (Theorem 2, p. 5).
  - The authors say that the practical algorithm embodies "a heuristic strategy" and that Proposition 2's condition can only be checked numerically (p. 6).
  - The set size comes from data. The laboratory errors have a standard deviation of 40 μm. Assuming independent normal errors, Γ = 550 μm covers 99% of perturbations (p. 12).
  - In Example III each of the 16 coefficients is multiplied by (1 + 0.05p) (p. 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Danskin's min–max theorem gives the directional derivative of g, and its convexity when f is convex (Appendix A, p. 16). The KKT conditions make the gradient at a worst error parallel to that error (Proposition 3, p. 16). Together they prove Theorem 1 (Appendix B, p. 17).
  - Robust local minima come in three types (Figure 2, p. 4). Type (c), with a worst error in the interior of the neighbourhood, can be a bad robust solution, for example near the nominal global maximum. The algorithm therefore targets condition (i) of Proposition 1 (p. 4).
  - Convergence: −d* lies in the convex hull of the worst errors, by a separating-hyperplane argument (Proposition 4, p. 17). That hull is the subdifferential of g (Corollary 1(b), p. 16). Algorithm 1 is thus a subgradient method and converges under Shor's step rules (Theorem 2, pp. 5, 17).
  - Inner maximization: the barrier parameter r is fixed instead of driven to zero. With the standard method the time to a local maximum is unpredictable and can be very long, and by Proposition 2 exact maxima are not needed (p. 6). The ascents start from 0 and from ±(Γ/3)e_i (p. 7). Every evaluation is kept in a history set and reused to estimate worst cases (p. 7).
  - The move:
    - High-cost neighbours form a bad-neighbour set controlled by a cost factor σ_k, initially 0.2 times the gap between worst-case and nominal cost (p. 7).
    - σ_k is divided by 1.05 when the SOCP is infeasible. The search stops when the SOCP is infeasible and σ_k is below a threshold (p. 7).
    - The step length comes from the cosine rule, at the cost of k function evaluations (p. 8).
    - The direction is rechecked against neighbours just beyond the new neighbourhood (p. 8).
  - Generalization: the constraint d_p = 0 is added to the direction SOCP (pp. 13–14).
