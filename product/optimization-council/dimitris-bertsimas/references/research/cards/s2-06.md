### S558 · Weapon coordination in a fleet: Centralized and distributed optimization (MIT Lincoln Laboratory working paper 2013, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–21 (abstract, §1–5, notation, references). Figures 1–5 (geography and firing patterns) have no text layer. First-page check: the file is titled "Weapons Coordination in a Fleet: Centralized and Distributed Optimization", and Bertsimas is the second of six authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Adrian Becker (Dynamic Ideas; the reference list gives Dynamic Ideas as the publisher of *Optimization over Integers*, p. 20); Joel Cofield, Dan Finkel, Sung-Hyun Son and Jessica Stigile (MIT Lincoln Laboratory) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a defence application of the IO and conic toolkit, written with a laboratory sponsor.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+CP+AP}:
  - The task: coordinate hard-kill interceptors and soft-kill weapons (decoys, jamming) across a fleet against incoming missiles (pp. 1–2).
  - Entry: a soft-kill weapon can act on several threats and can retarget a threat onto another ship (p. 2). Published weapon–target assignment (WTA) methods either rest on "threat ownership" (auction and greedy algorithms) or, for the asset-based problem, assume identical weapons or use evolutionary search (pp. 2–3). The soft-kill model follows an earlier Lincoln Laboratory report (ref. [20], p. 4).
  - Ahuja et al. had found exact integer models with logarithmic objectives too hard even for small instances. The paper accepts that judgement and keeps its exact model offline (p. 3).
  - Problem: minimize firing cost subject to a survival threshold γ_s for each ship (eqs. 2–6, p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: One model in three tiers (pp. 3, 16):
  - exact: the product-of-powers survival constraint becomes a binary SOCP. It is solved offline and serves as the benchmark (pp. 5–7).
  - real time: each threat's factor must reach γ_s^{1/|T_s|}; taking logs gives a linear binary IP (pp. 7–8). A slack weighted by ship priority handles thresholds that cannot be met (p. 9).
  - distributed: each ship solves its local IP and broadcasts two messages (pp. 11–14):
    - a "leakage" matrix of log kill and retargeting effects;
    - a sparse "linkage" matrix of threats hit by the same soft-kill shot.

    The ships then update in a fixed sequence.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Stated assumptions (p. 4): any hit destroys a ship; all ships know the threats and their destinations; weapon effectiveness is predicted exactly.
  - At most one soft-kill weapon may engage a threat, because the combined effect of several is unknown to the authors (pp. 4–5).
  - Data: one synthetic case (p. 15):
    - 3 ships, nine threats [my count], and per ship 5 interceptors and 1 soft-kill weapon;
    - kill probabilities drawn from 50–100%, thresholds from 0.7–0.95;
    - unit firing costs.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  1. For binary x, (1 − P)^x = (1 + (P/(1 − P))x)^{-1}. With constraint (1), the leak probability D_{j,s} enters only through products of linear factors (eqs. 7–8, p. 6).
  2. Theorem 1: a constraint 1 ≤ ∏ x_i over nonnegative variables is the projection of a system of rotated second-order cones. The proof is an induction with geometric-mean auxiliaries (p. 7).
  3. The per-factor split is sufficient but not equivalent, which the paper flags as a source of an optimality gap (p. 8).
  4. The exact model cannot take a slack, because γ_s appears as a reciprocal. Infeasible cases are therefore solved by a lexicographic sequence of SOCPs over the ships in priority order (pp. 9–10).

  No complexity or approximation bound is given for the heuristics.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - One instance, four methods (Table 1, p. 15). Firing costs:
    - no coordination: 18 (ship 1 misses its threshold, and soft-kill shots conflict);
    - distributed and centralized heuristics: 13 each;
    - exact: 12.
  - Missing [my reading]: no solver, version, machine or run time for the case. The "less than one second" claim and the promised scalability discussion (both p. 3) do not appear in §4.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: the WTA extension with soft-kill weapons and resource conservation, its binary SOCP form, the real-time heuristic and its message-passing version (p. 3).
  - Stated limits: the exact model works offline only (p. 16).
  - Future work: multiperiod engagements and onboard weapon-control constraints (p. 16).
  - Not stated [my reading]: the evidence is a single synthetic instance, with no timing and no test of the update order.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 5–7): a nonlinear problem the literature had left to heuristics is written exactly in a class that commercial solvers handle. Footnote 1 names Gurobi 5.0 and later, CPLEX and Xpress (p. 5).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 15–16): the roles are reversed. The exact model certifies the heuristics after the fact; no heuristic warm-starts the exact solve. No time limit, gap or run time is printed.

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a binary x, rewrite a^x as the linear factor 1 + (a − 1)x. A product of such factors bounded below by 1 is then SOC-representable by the induction of Theorem 1 (pp. 6–7).
- To get a real-time version of a product constraint ∏_{j≤k} f_j ≥ γ, require each factor to reach γ^{1/k} and take logs. Say that this is sufficient, not equivalent (p. 8).
- Build an exact-offline / heuristic-real-time / distributed ladder and run every tier on the same instance, so each tier's loss is visible (Table 1, p. 15; p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "A novel technique is also used to reformulate the problem as a binary second-order optimization problem that can be solved offline and serve as a benchmark for online solutions." (p. 1)
- "While inequality (26) implies inequality (25), it should be noted that these two expressions are not equivalent, introducing the potential for an optimality gap." (p. 8)

### S172 · Optimal design for multi-item auctions: A robust optimization approach (Mathematics of Operations Research 2014, DOI 10.1287/moor.2014.0645)
Read level: **partial**. Read:
- pp. 1–9: cover, abstract, §1, and §2.1–2.3 with ROM.a and ROM.b;
- pp. 14–18: Theorem 1 and its proof, §2.4–2.5, §3.1 and Proposition 8;
- pp. 20–27: Propositions 9–10, §3.3, the opening of §4, Theorem 3, §5 and §6.

Not read: the proofs of Propositions 2–6 (pp. 10–14) and the ROM-UB algorithm (pp. 23–25). First-page check: the title and the authors Bandi and Bertsimas match (pp. 1–2).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chaithanya Bandi (Kellogg, Northwestern) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: part of a "broader research program" with Bandi that replaces probabilistic primitives with uncertainty sets. The program's other applications are queueing, option pricing and network information theory (p. 4). Received September 2011 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+LO+AP}:
  - Myerson solved the single-item auction without budgets, assuming known independent distributions (p. 2).
  - Three extensions remain "by and large open" in both the Bayesian and the prior-free school, with impossibility results: public budgets (P1), private budgets (P2) and correlated valuations (P3) (pp. 2–4).
  - Entry (p. 4):
    - incentive compatibility and individual rationality are robustness properties, since they must hold for every valuation;
    - a fitted distribution is a modelling choice;
    - prior-free methods discard the bidding history.
  - Problem: maximize worst-case revenue over valuations in an uncertainty set built from historical moments (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**: Mechanism design becomes one robust LP over allocation and payment rules indexed by each valuation v ∈ U, with IC, IR and budget constraints (eqs. 5–10, p. 7). It is solved constructively (pp. 8–9):
  - ROM.a, before bidding: a bilinear problem (12) gives the worst-case valuation z, nominal allocations and reservation prices r*_ij that depend on both bidder and item.
  - ROM.b, after bidding: a VCG-like allocation that maximizes Σ y_ij(v_ij − r*_ij), with opportunity-cost payments.

  Without budgets this reduces to a second-price auction whose reserve is computed by an LP (Proposition 8, eq. 63, pp. 17–18). Extensions:
  - indivisible items, by randomization, with IC, IR and budget feasibility holding in expectation (Theorem 2, p. 16);
  - private budgets, through a budget uncertainty set (Theorem 3, p. 25).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - n buyers, m divisible items and a worst-case revenue objective (pp. 5, 7).
  - Valuation sets (pp. 5–7):
    - a CLT set with conservativeness Γ (Γ = 2 or 3 covers about 95% or 99.7% asymptotically);
    - a common-factor set for correlation;
    - typical sets built from a known pdf (Proposition 1).
  - Budgets are public in §2–3 and uncertain in §4.
  - Theorem 1 covers finite and bounded sets (p. 14). The tractability claims are for polyhedral sets (p. 15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Optimality proof (pp. 14–15): for a finite U, LP duality applies (dual (11), p. 7), and a primal–dual pair with equal values proves optimality. For a bounded U, a discrete subset containing the worst case z sandwiches the value.
  - Computation (pp. 15–16): an extreme point of U solves (12) (Proposition 7), so sets with polynomially many extreme points give a polynomial algorithm. The general bilinear problem is NP-hard. It is solved by a generalized Benders decomposition that alternates between the inner and the outer LP.
  - Closed forms: for example, the reserve under the CLT set is μ − Γσ/√n (Propositions 9–10, pp. 20–21).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Simulated single-item duels against Myerson's auction (pp. 21–23):
    - realized distribution gamma, beta, triangle or uniform with the same mean and standard deviation: relative revenue +0.271 to +1.038 (Table 1, p. 22);
    - ROM-Si beats Myerson only once the total-variation distance from the assumed normal exceeds 0.22 (Fig. 8, p. 22);
    - it loses when the realized mean exceeds the assumed one (Table 3, pp. 22–23).
  - Run times for n ≤ 100 and m ≤ 50: roughly 4–73 minutes [my reading; decimals garbled] (Tables 5–7, p. 26).
    - No solver or machine is named.
    - §5.1 says the times are reported "in terms of the number of iterations", while the captions say "(in minutes)" and name ROM-Si, the single-item mechanism, on a grid of up to 50 items (pp. 25–26).
  - The revenue gain from modelling correlation is shown only as a plot (Fig. 11, p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a worst-case optimal mechanism with reservation prices that depend on bidder and item. It covers multiple items, budgets, correlated valuations and uncertain budgets, and it avoids the Dobzinski et al. impossibility (pp. 4, 25, 27).
  - Stated limits:
    - the bilinear step is "theoretically difficult in general" (p. 27);
    - the indivisible case is "a partial solution", feasible only in expectation (p. 16).
  - No future work is listed (pp. 26–27).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 7, 15, 17–18): a problem owned by economics becomes a robust LP. It stays an LP without budgets; with budgets it is bilinear and solved by alternating LPs. The paper also says where polynomial time stops (p. 15).
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 4–5, 26): tractability is redefined as practical efficiency, with the simplex method as the precedent. No solver × hardware figure is given, and the run-time tables name neither solver nor machine.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 5, 21–23):
  - Present: Γ is an interpretable knob tied to a CLT coverage figure, and the losing cases are printed (distance below 0.22; a larger realized mean).
  - Absent: a published curve of revenue against Γ.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (p. 4): an explicit programme statement listing sibling applications carried by the same student.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write IC and IR as constraints indexed by every valuation in a set, then read the mechanism's structure off the dual: reservation prices and VCG-like payments (pp. 7–9).
- Build an uncertainty set from a limit theorem: bound the normalized sum by ±Γ and report its asymptotic coverage 2Φ(Γ) − 1 (p. 5).
- Prove a min–max optimum over a bounded set by restricting to a finite subset that contains the worst case, then use LP strong duality on that subset (pp. 14–15).
- Duel the robust design against the classical optimum under graded misspecification (total-variation distance, a shifted mean, a scaled standard deviation), and print where it loses (pp. 21–23).

**Quotes**:
- "We use exactly this notion of practical efficiency: the ability to solve problems of realistic size relative to the application we address." (p. 5)
- "The probabilistic approach chooses to fit a specific probability distribution to these data, which is, in our view, a model of reality, not reality itself." (p. 4)

### S210 · Dynamic resource allocation: A flexible and tractable modeling framework (European Journal of Operational Research 2014, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2013.10.063)
Read level: **partial**:
- Read:
  - pp. 1–4: abstract, §1 and §2.1–2.3;
  - from pp. 5–6: the valid inequalities of §2.4.2, the fairness variables of §2.5, and §2.6;
  - pp. 6–7: §2.7 and §3.1;
  - pp. 9–13: §4–6.
- Skimmed: the constraint lists of §2.4.1 and §2.5 (pp. 4–6).
- Not read: the application formulations of §3.2–3.4 (pp. 7–9). The proofs of Theorems 1–4 are in the online supplement, not in this file (p. 9).

First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Gupta and Lulli match (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Shubham Gupta (MIT ORC), Guglielmo Lulli (Milano-Bicocca) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: generalizes the Bertsimas–Stock ATFM model (S015, c04). It names two companion generalizations: fairness among airlines with Gupta (S076, c16) and rerouting with Lulli and Odoni (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+AP}:
  - Allocating resources over time recurs across industries, but no unified, tractable approach exists. It is "widely believed" that optimization does not suit scheduling, and commercial schedulers are rule-based (p. 1).
  - Entry: the success of the 1998 ATFM model. The paper asks whether a general model can be flexible, tractable and widely applicable, and why it works (p. 1).
  - Dantzig's "final test of any theory" is the stated yardstick (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: One binary, time-indexed model, DRAP (p. 3).
  - Its variables say whether request i has begun using resource r "by" time t, not "at" t (p. 4).
  - The same variables express all constraints:
    - capacity, precedence, request continuation and acceptance (1a–1f, p. 4);
    - alternative resource sets, strengthened by antichain and "island" inequalities (pp. 4–5);
    - three fairness rules: reversals and overtaking under first-scheduled-first-served, proportional fairness and min–max fairness (pp. 5–6).
  - An iterative wrapper rescales durations by L_k and narrows each time window to ±Δ around the last solution (pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Discrete time. Requests come with feasible windows, durations, resource amounts, priorities and owners; resources have a capacity per period (pp. 3–4).
  - Data:
    - ATFM: real flights of the five largest US carriers, 55 airports, a 10 × 10 sector grid; about 300,000 variables and 800,000 constraints (p. 10);
    - aircraft maintenance: random instances with 2–4 aircraft and 30–90 activities (p. 10);
    - job shop: the FT10 instance (p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Every precedence row has two nonzeros (Remark 2, p. 4).
  - Polyhedral results (p. 9):
    - the single-request polyhedron P(i) and the fairness polyhedron are integral (Theorems 1 and 4);
    - antichain inequalities are facets (Theorem 2);
    - island inequalities are (dim − f)-faces, and facets when f = 1 (Theorem 3, Remarks 5–7).

    The paper offers these results as the reason the solver rarely branches.
  - The resource-constrained project scheduling problem (RCPSP) is the single-request case (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi 4.5.2 via AMPL, 4 AMD-Xeon processors at 3 GHz, 8 GB RAM; stop at a 1% gap or 3600 s (p. 10).
  - ATFM (Table 3, p. 10):
    - with fairness imposed at k = 0 to 25 airports, reversals fall from 753–924 to 9–25, at 13–25% more delay cost;
    - every instance ends within 1% except one, at 4.1% when the time limit hits;
    - only one instance branches, with 3 nodes.
  - Maintenance:
    - the standalone model averages 2,190–2,647 s, and a few instances end above a 10% gap (Table 4, pp. 10–11);
    - the iterative procedure (Table 5, p. 11): with L1 = 2 it cuts run time by 45% for under 1% extra cost; with L1 = 4 by 90% for about 10%. One L1 = 4 run is infeasible.
  - FT10 (Table 6, p. 12): best makespan 953 against the known 930; best minsum 755 against a lower bound of 655.
  - [my reading] Two summary claims conflict with the tables (p. 13 against Tables 4–5, p. 11):
    - the claim that the framework takes "less than an hour" for all three applications does not cover Table 5's 4,095 s standalone run, which also exceeds the 3,600 s limit;
    - the maintenance claim of "less than 30 minutes in all cases" holds only for the iterative procedure.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Claimed: flexibility, wide applicability, tractability, and an optimization-based iterative procedure (p. 13).
  - Stated limits:
    - the time-indexed formulation can grow large (p. 6);
    - for job shop, "the standalone framework remains far off from optimality" (p. 13).
  - No future work is listed (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 4, 9): scheduling, which rule-based systems own, is written as one binary model. Its polyhedral strength is proved and offered as the reason solvers rarely branch.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 10, 13): the paper opens against the belief that optimization does not suit scheduling, but answers it with solve times on Gurobi 4.5.2, not with a quantified speed-up.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 6, 10):
  - Present: fairness gets two knobs, the trade-off weight λ and the number k of airports with fairness, and a printed cost curve of reversals against delay cost.
  - Absent: a solution-independent bound.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define time-indexed binaries cumulatively ("started by t"). Starts, activity and precedence then become differences and two-nonzero rows, which can give integral sub-polyhedra (pp. 4, 9).
- Pair a modelling framework with polyhedral theorems (integrality, facets) that explain why the solver rarely branches, and report node counts as the evidence (pp. 9–10).
- Wrap an exact time-indexed model in a coarsen-then-refine loop: scale durations by L_k, re-solve within ±Δ of the last solution, and print the time–quality trade-off for each scale (pp. 6–7, 11).

**Quotes**:
- "On the contrary, it is widely believed that optimization might not be the right approach for certain classes of resource allocation problems such as scheduling for example." (p. 1)
- "Finally, one of the most striking observations is that the solver did not branch at all for all but one instance, which highlights the strength of the framework." (p. 10)

### S245 · Scheduling, revenue management, and fairness in an academic-hospital radiology division (Academic Radiology 2014, DOI 10.1016/j.acra.2014.05.009)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–15 (all text of the author manuscript). Figure 1, a screenshot of one scheduled block, has a text layer; Figure 2, the revenue spreads, has none. First-page check: the manuscript is titled "Scheduling, Revenue Management, and Fairness in an Academic-Hospital Division: An Optimization Approach"; the authors are Baum, Bertsimas and Kallus (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**:
- Richard Baum, director of the Brigham and Women's Hospital division of angiography and interventional radiology, who is responsible for its daily schedule (pp. 1–2);
- Nathan Kallus (MIT ORC, on an NSF Graduate Research Fellowship, p. 14).

[my reading] The header of the Figure 1 screenshot shows a local file in Kallus's spring-2012 research folder, dated 6/21/12 (p. 12). This suggests the student built the interface.

**Position in trajectory**: an applied paper in a clinical journal, with the owner as coauthor and the student who is also his coauthor on the prescriptive-analytics line (S008, c21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+AP}:
  - With extended campuses, the quarterly schedule of eight physicians across the hospital and three suburban clinics had become complex. It was still made "informally by hand", without quantified objectives (pp. 1–2).
  - Entry: revenue, fairness and fatigue are all at stake, and no operations-research work had treated scheduling, revenue management and fairness together in this setting (pp. 2–3).
  - Problem: one MIO that maximizes the least physician revenue and minimizes the worst preference mismatch in each block (p. 9).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Objective: a Rawlsian maximin over revenue and a minimax over preference mismatch, joined by an exchange rate λ (p. 9).
  - Small multipliers on total revenue and total mismatch stand in for the two-step Pareto-robustly-optimal solve; the paper puts "greater weight on simplicity" (p. 9).
  - Constraints (8–10, p. 10):
    - spread limits for desirable and undesirable assignments (on call, float, academic days);
    - effort limits over any two consecutive days and over a block.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Binary x_bdas: block b, day d, assignment a, staff member s. B = 7 two-week blocks, D = 14 days, 15 assignments, 8 physicians (p. 11).
  - Inputs:
    - expected revenue per physician and assignment: a recency-weighted average of historical daily revenues (p. 7);
    - each physician's ideal block schedule, filled in by the physician (p. 7);
    - effort ratings, effort limits and λ, tuned with the manager until the schedules looked acceptable (pp. 7, 11).
  - Site rules are added as linear constraints. Examples: weekend on-call runs Friday to Sunday, and nobody is on call two weekends in a row (p. 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - A plain MIO. The max, min and absolute-value terms are linearized with auxiliary variables, whose counts are given (p. 11). No theory.
  - The group's 2011 price of fairness (S012) is discussed and set aside (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - About 160,000 variables and 140,000 constraints, written in IBM OPL and solved with CPLEX 12.3 on a PC "in well under one ten minutes" (sic) (p. 12).
  - Against the hand-made schedule (p. 13; the division head is excluded from the spread):
    - projected revenue: +8.2%;
    - coefficient of variation of individual revenues: −11.9%;
    - least individual revenue: +5.04%;
    - individual changes range from −1.57% (one physician) to +19.1%.
  - No out-of-sample or realized revenue is reported.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Claimed: flexibility, tractability and impact. The system runs with a relational database and a web interface, and re-optimizes as realized revenues come in (pp. 3, 12–13).
  - Stated limit: the quarterly horizon also limits the memory of fairness (p. 11).
  - Not stated [my reading]: the +8.2% is projected from the same revenue estimates the model optimizes; it is not measured after deployment (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 6–7, 12–13):
  - the owner is a coauthor, and his decision, data and current (hand-made) schedule define the problem and the baseline;
  - the decision is scored by revenue and fairness;
  - the run time is reported, and the system is deployed.

  ⚠ The score is a projection from the model's own revenue estimates.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (p. 5): the price of fairness is named, and the paper says managers can use it. Here it is declared a "moot point", because fairness is a hard business rule and the fair schedule already earns more than the existing one. No curve is computed.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Let the people being scheduled submit their ideal schedule, then score each person's mismatch per block with a minimax term. The paper reports that seeing their inputs reflected built trust (pp. 7, 13).
- Tune hard-to-quantify limits such as fatigue with the manager in the loop: guess effort ratings, generate schedules, ask whether they are acceptable, retune (pp. 7, 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+LO}:
  - Optimal adjustable solutions are intractable: Feige et al. show the problem is hard to approximate within Ω(log n). Static solutions are cheap but "perceived to be highly conservative" (p. 2).
  - Earlier static-solution bounds cover only covering constraints with right-hand-side uncertainty. They miss packing problems with uncertain coefficients, such as revenue management and resource allocation (pp. 2–3).
  - Problem: in a two-stage packing LP with an uncertain constraint matrix B ∈ U, when is a static solution optimal, and how much can it lose? (pp. 3–4)
- **D2 Core idea**: Dualize the inner LP and rescale (λ = hᵀα, α = λμ). Then:
  - the one-stage adjustable value is min{λ : λb ≥ d, b ∈ T(U, h)}, with T(U, h) = {Bᵀμ : hᵀμ = 1, B ∈ U, μ ≥ 0};
  - the static value is the same problem over conv T(U, h) (Lemmas 3–4, pp. 7–10).

  Consequences:
  - a static solution is optimal iff T(U, h) is convex for all h > 0 (Theorem 1, p. 9);
  - otherwise its loss is bounded by κ(S) = min{α : conv(S) ⊆ αS}, a measure of non-convexity (Definition 1, p. 12; Theorem 3, p. 14).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Two-stage LP: max cᵀx + min_B max_y dᵀy subject to Ax + By ≤ h (p. 3).
  - Assumptions: nonnegative data (B ≥ 0; c, d ≥ 0; h > 0); U compact, convex and full-dimensional, and down-monotone without loss of generality (p. 3).
  - Extensions (pp. 5–6):
    - objective uncertainty in a product set U_B × U_d;
    - right-hand-side uncertainty.
  - No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Convex cases: constraint-wise sets and sets with symmetric projections, including permutation-invariant sets (Lemmas 1–2, pp. 8–9).
  - Optimality is read as a min–max equality that the saddle-point theorem does not give (p. 12).
  - Tightness: the family U^θ attains κ = n^{1/θ} (Theorem 2, pp. 13–14).
  - Against the symmetry bound: κ is at most 1 + 1/sym(U), and Table 1 shows cases such as 1 against 1 + mn (Theorem 4, Table 1, pp. 17–18).
  - Extensions (pp. 5–6, 25):
    - with objective uncertainty, the result does not depend on U_d;
    - with right-hand-side uncertainty, only a sufficient condition survives, and there is no bound.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none: there are no computations.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions (pp. 4–6, 25):
    - an iff characterization of static optimality;
    - a tight non-convexity bound;
    - an improvement on the symmetry bound;
    - extensions to objective uncertainty.
  - Stated limits: the bounds do not extend to right-hand-side uncertainty, where κ is "not even well defined" (p. 25).
  - Stated use: guidance in choosing uncertainty sets (p. 25). No future work is listed.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 7–10, 25): the dual of the inner problem is the device that exposes the structure, and the paper prints where its result stops (no bound under right-hand-side uncertainty).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 12–14, 17–18): the loss of the simple policy gets a solution-independent, tight bound computed from the uncertainty set alone. There is no knob to tune and no computed curve.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To compare adaptive and static robust values, dualize the inner problem and normalize the multipliers. The two values become one optimization over a set and over its convex hull, so the gap is a property of that set (pp. 7–10).
- Grade a yes/no property with a scaling measure: κ(S) is the smallest factor at which S covers its own hull. Then show tightness with a family that attains it (pp. 12–14).
- Compare a new bound with the previous one on a table of standard uncertainty sets (Table 1, p. 18).

**Quotes**:
- "This is highly surprising in view of the usual perception about the conservativeness of static solutions." (p. 1)
- "Therefore, our results provide useful guidance in selecting uncertainty sets such that the adjustable robust problem can be well approximated by a static solutions." (p. 25)
