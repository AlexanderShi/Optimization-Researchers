### S392 · The benefit of uncertainty coupling in robust and adaptive robust optimization (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2025, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2023.0007; arXiv 2302.10369)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v2 (arXiv:2302.10369v2, 29 Oct 2024): main text pp. 1–41 in full; appendices A–D (pp. 45–58) read, proofs followed but not re-derived line by line; references (pp. 41–44) skimmed. The IJOO print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Liangyuan Na, Bartolomeo Stellato, Irina Wang (p. 1; this version prints no affiliations, acknowledgements or funding). Na is also a coauthor of the group's multimodal-health papers cited in S426 (S426 pp. 9, 11) · **Position in trajectory**: a late item of his adaptive-RO line. The 2022 Bertsimas–den Hertog book is the first citation (p. 1); the solution methods reuse the two-level Benders and outer-approximation algorithm of Bertsimas et al. 2012 (card S006 in c13; p. 58) and lot-sizing settings from Bertsimas–de Ruiter 2016 (p. 39); the adaptability bound is measured against the group's own symmetry bounds (Bertsimas–Goyal 2010, 2013; Bertsimas–Goyal–Sun 2011) and non-convexity bound (Bertsimas–Goyal–Lu 2015) (pp. 7, 23). The Price of Robustness (card S001 in c07) is now cited as evidence that RO "can be overly conservative" (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO; LO and CP as engines for counterparts and factor computations}: The entry is a diagnosed cause of conservatism. RO and ARO "can exhibit too conservative solutions", and one of the main reasons is that uncertain constraints are designed constraint-wise "without taking into account couplings over multiple constraints" (abstract, p. 1); in practice parameters of different constraints share sources (p. 2). The gap: coupled uncertainty appears only in RO toy examples (Gorissen et al. 2015; Atamtürk–Zhang 2007) and application-specific ARO (Ben-Tal et al. 2004; Bertsimas–de Ruiter 2016; Zhen et al. 2018), with "a limited focus in the literature on the generic framework" (p. 2). A second entry is the power of adaptability: under constraint-wise compact sets a static solution is fully adaptive for linear problems (Ben-Tal et al. 2004), so coupling is what lets adaptivity help (pp. 2, 7), and the group's earlier bounds on that power are "quite loose in some instances" or "not necessarily tractable to compute" (p. 7). Statement: coupled set = constraint-wise set ∩ coupling set (pp. 2, 9); bound z_cp/z_ro and z_acp/z_aro (p. 11). A running example (two sources, two centres, two stores) shows the static cost falling from 600 to 450 (25%) in one coupling scenario and none in the other, and gains in both scenarios once the second stage adapts (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Coupling is measured by two scalings between sets: the largest ρ with ρS1 ⊆ S2 and the smallest γ with S2 ⊆ γS1 (Definition 3.3, p. 13). For static RO the constraint-wise set is compared with the projections of the coupled set (Definition 3.4, p. 13), for ARO with the coupled set itself (Definition 3.5, p. 17), and the objective ratio is sandwiched between the two factors (Theorems 3.1–3.2, pp. 14, 18). The power of adaptability under coupling is bounded by ρ_adapt, the scaling between the projection box and the coupled set (Definition 3.6, Theorem 3.3, pp. 21–22), with ρ_adapt ≥ 1/m for every convex set (Lemma 3.5, p. 22). What changes relative to the closest results: the reference set is the constraint-wise model a modeller would otherwise write, instead of a Minkowski symmetry point or a transformed set (pp. 7, 23), and the factors are "obtained independently from the objective of the robust problem", unlike the calibrated radius of robust satisficing (p. 8). What stays: standard robust counterparts and Ben-Tal et al.'s static-equals-adaptive theorem, used as a lemma (p. 22). Why this design: "The power of our approach lies in its simplicity, as the analysis is based on the structure of the uncertainty alone" (p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Linear RO and ARO with right-hand-side uncertainty u ∈ R^m_+ (pp. 9–12), with constraint-coefficient uncertainty after translating the sets to the point of symmetry so that {0} ⊆ U (pp. 24–25), and nonlinear extensions under convexity or concavity with f(0) = 0 and g(0, 0) = 0 (Assumptions 5.1–5.6, pp. 30–32). Assumptions adopted for the analysis: convex (for adaptability, compact) sets and positive finite optimal values (Theorems 3.1–3.3, pp. 14, 18, 21); Assumption 3.1, bounded recourse from Ben-Tal et al. 2004 (p. 21); fixed recourse for the nonlinear static-equals-adaptive step (Marandi–den Hertog 2018; p. 48). Down-hulls replace right-hand-side sets without loss of generality (Definition 3.2, p. 12; Lemma A.1, p. 45). Data are all synthetic: a supply chain with J = K = M, 50 instances per size, costs U(0, 5) or U(0, 5001) for unavailable paths, store groups coupled by u¹ ≥ u² + α and a radius ‖u‖₂ ≤ Γ (pp. 34–35); a portfolio with eight uncertain sources per asset (four common, four asset-specific), 100 instances per size, polyhedral sets with q = 30, common sources forced equal and a polyhedral coupling Dz ≤ d (pp. 36–38); lot sizing with a box and a budget Σu ≤ 20√n, 50 instances in Bertsimas–de Ruiter's settings (p. 39). [my reading] Γ in §6.1 is a 2-norm radius (p. 34), not the Bertsimas–Sim budget.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Scaling device: because the constraints are linear in (x, y) with the uncertainty on one side, an optimal solution of one problem divided or multiplied by the shrinkage factor is feasible for the other, and the objective scales with it (proofs of Theorems 3.1–3.2, pp. 15, 18–19), a three-step procedure the authors restate (p. 29). Coefficient uncertainty inverts the factors and flips the inequalities (p. 29); nonlinear problems keep one side only, from convexity or concavity (p. 32; proofs pp. 48–53); uncertainty in both objective and constraints squares the factors (Theorems 5.7–5.9, p. 32).
  - Computable factors: ρ_ro = min_i d̄_i/d_i and γ_ro = max_i d̄_i/d_i from the projection bounds (8) (p. 14), each bound a convex program (10) (p. 23); ρ_aro and ρ_adapt from convex robust programs (pp. 17, 22, 26–28); γ_aro = γ_ro (p. 18); ρ_ro ≥ ρ_aro, so coupling can help the adaptive problem more (Corollary 3.2.3, p. 21).
  - Tightness: every bound is attained by a two-dimensional instance with chosen costs (Corollaries 3.1.1, 3.2.1, 3.3.1, pp. 15, 19, 22; Example 3.3, p. 22; appendix proofs, pp. 46–47, 53), with sufficient conditions: all variables with nonzero cost must be scaled alike (Corollaries 3.1.2–3.1.3, 3.2.2, pp. 16–17, 20).
  - Adaptability: z_acp/z_cp ≥ ρ_adapt ≥ ρ_aro/γ_ro (Lemma 3.4, Theorem 3.3, pp. 21–22), through Ben-Tal et al.'s Theorem 2.1 applied on the projection box; for q-norm couplings ρ_adapt = β/(α m^{1/q}), "tighter than the symmetry bound" 1/(1 + m^{1/q}) and as tight as the non-convexity bound but computed by the convex program (9) (Example 3.4, p. 23); the coefficient analogue is 1/ρ_adapt ≤ mp (Lemma 4.1, p. 28).
  - Counterparts (Appendix D): projections by Fourier–Motzkin elimination with cddlib, citing his LO textbook §2.8 (p. 54); the coupled counterpart through the support function of an intersection, written as an infimal convolution (Theorem D.1, p. 55; Ben-Tal et al. 2015; Beck 2017), costing mℓ extra variables and constraints for ℓ polyhedral coupling constraints (p. 55); a most-violated-scenario cutting-plane algorithm (Algorithm 1, p. 56); linear-decision-rule counterparts (Theorems D.2–D.3, p. 57); Benders with an outer-approximation inner loop, multistarted from sampled uncertainties (p. 58); a finite-scenario approach with hit-and-run samples pushed to the boundary (p. 58).
  - [my reading] Some labels slip: the program for ρ_adapt is headed "ρaro =" (p. 28); Example 4.3 bounds z_acp/z_aro where Theorem 4.3 concerns z_acp/z_cp (pp. 28–29); the tightness proofs of Theorems 4.1–4.2 cite Theorems 3.1–3.2 (pp. 46–47); the §6.3 budget uses an undefined n (p. 39); §6.1 attributes an RHS bound to Example 4.2 (p. 35).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Intel Xeon E5-2650, Python 3.6, Gurobi (a 2020 reference manual is cited, no version) (p. 33); no time limits; Benders and cutting planes capped at 1,000 iterations with tolerance 10⁻³, Benders started from the static robust solution and 100 random uncertainty points (pp. 35, 38, 39).
  - Metrics: z_cp/z_ro, z_acp/z_aro and running times, means with one-standard-deviation bands (p. 33).
  - Supply chain: the ratio against α lies in the theoretical band [1 − α, 1] for M = 5, 50 and 500, its mean independent of M and its spread shrinking with M (Figure 9a, p. 35); against Γ for M = 25 the static problem gains only for Γ < 1, while the adaptive ratio and its theoretical lower bound rise linearly with Γ (the gain shrinks) and gains remain possible up to Γ = √M (Figure 9b, pp. 35–36). With random α and Γ, the coupled counterpart and cutting planes agree "For 100% of the static coupled problems", cutting planes slower; Benders gains more at more effort (Figure 10, p. 36; objectives plotted to M = 80, times to M = 200 by the axis ticks).
  - Portfolio: equal common sources raise the worst-case return and asset-specific coupling adds more; cutting planes are faster than the constraint-wise counterpart and the coupled counterpart slightly slower; "neither one dominates the other in solving times" (Figure 11, p. 38; m to 140).
  - Lot sizing: no static gain because the projections do not shrink (p. 40); the adaptive gain grows with m and scales like 1/√m, as the bound does; vertex enumeration is exact only for small m; Benders gives "a close lower bound" in under 100 s; decision rules and sampling give upper and lower bounds at longer times (Figure 12, p. 40).
  - Not reported [my reading]: any measure of protection (violation rates, or cost when the assumed coupling is wrong); real data; code (text searched for `github`, `code`, `available`); the solver version; numeric gaps between the decision-rule upper bounds and the Benders or sampling lower bounds (plots only, p. 40).
- **D6 Positioning**: Coupling is set apart from remedies that relax constraints (light robustness, globalized RO, robustness optimization), which "do not address another main cause of conservatism in RO" (pp. 5–6); from Roos–den Hertog's aggregation into one distributionally robust constraint, which needs rescaling and "might lose some individuality for each constraint" (p. 6); from Nohadani–Sharma's multi-period connected uncertainty (p. 7); and from robust satisficing (p. 8). Data-driven sets (Bertsimas–Gupta–Kallus 2018; Esfahani–Kuhn 2018) are read as implicit uses of coupling (p. 7). The adaptability precedents are mostly his group's own, criticised as loose or hard to compute and then beaten or matched (pp. 7, 23); Ben-Tal et al. 2004 is the hinge (pp. 2, 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: One running toy example is introduced in §1.1 and recalled after each definition and theorem (Examples 2.1–3.3 and the tightness proofs, pp. 3–4, 10–11, 14–15, 18–19, 22), as the authors promise, to show "conditions under which coupling may bring benefits, and where it does not" (p. 3). Each theorem is followed by an "Obtaining shrinkage factors" recipe (pp. 14, 17, 22, 26, 27, 28) and each block by a cross-section comparison (pp. 29, 32). The contribution phrase is "tight and easily computable" bounds (pp. 8, 40). Hedges: "might result in less conservative solutions", "possible relationships" (p. 2), "sometimes significantly" (p. 41). Advice to the decision-maker appears twice (pp. 17, 20). There is no limitations or future-work paragraph (pp. 40–41).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Three stated contributions: tight, computable bounds for RO and ARO, linear and nonlinear, under right-hand-side, coefficient and objective uncertainty; adaptive versus static under coupling; experiments in three applications (p. 8). Limits stated: projections are in general intractable in high dimension (pp. 6, 54); nonlinear problems get one-sided bounds (p. 32); coupling may not help at all (Examples 2.1–2.2, pp. 10–11; static lot sizing, p. 40). No future work (pp. 40–41). Limits unstated [my reading]: the benefit is counted only in objective value, while a coupling set is a modelling assumption whose misspecification costs protection that is never measured (metrics, p. 33); the ratios need positive optimal values; for coefficient uncertainty the translation to the point of symmetry is said to maximise the factors only "for most cases" (p. 24).

### S426 · Efficient domain adaptation of multimodal embeddings using constrastive learning (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2502.02048)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:2502.02048v1, 4 Feb 2025): main text pp. 1–8 and appendix A (pp. 12–13, tables) in full; references (pp. 9–11) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Georgios Margaritis and Periklis Petridis (both MIT Operations Research Center, the two corresponding authors), with Bertsimas (MIT Sloan) as last author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a 2025 item of his multimodal health-AI line (the skill's trajectory row 2019–2026). The data come from "our ongoing collaboration with Hartford Healthcare Hospital System" (p. 5); the pipeline being adapted is the group's multimodal framework of Soenksen et al. 2022 (npj Digital Medicine, Bertsimas last author; pp. 3, 11), and the text serialisation follows the group's TabText (Carballo et al. 2023; pp. 5–6, 9). No optimization formulation appears.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + AP}: The entry is an adoption barrier in compute-constrained clinical settings. Out-of-the-box foundation-model embeddings "may be too general for specific tasks and frequently high-dimensional, potentially introducing noise in the classifier", while fine-tuning needs GPU servers and personnel that hospitals lack, and hospitals cannot send sensitive data to cloud providers (p. 1); adoption hinges on either high performance without adaptation or costly infrastructure (p. 2). Statement: binary classification from m modalities with frozen feature extractors (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A small nonlinear projection on frozen embeddings (one hidden ReLU layer, 128 outputs; Table 1, p. 6), trained with the task labels by a pairwise contrastive loss: pairs within a minibatch carry label 1 if their labels agree, and a binary cross-entropy acts on the similarity of their projections over a temperature τ, "similar to the SimCLR loss" (eqs. 1–2, p. 4). Two variants: one projection of the concatenated embeddings, or one per modality (p. 4; Figure 2, p. 5). The foundation models run once and the stored embeddings serve new tasks and modalities (p. 2). Changed relative to the contrastive literature: supervision by task labels instead of augmentations (SimCLR, SwAV, MoCo) or implicit pairings (CLIP, ConVIRT, ALIGN), and a frozen backbone (pp. 2–3). Kept: the SimCLR-type loss (p. 4) and the concatenate-then-classify pipeline downstream (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Model and data**: Proprietary EHRs of 4,000 HHC patients from the EPIC database under an IRB-approved protocol: the History & Physical note, the Progress Note, and ICD-9/ICD-10 codes turned into text through HCUP mapping tables (p. 5); labels diabetes and hypertension (p. 5); extractors ClinicalBERT and GTE-pretrained Qwen2 (7B), the latter chosen for its MTEB rank (p. 5). The public mm-imdb data: 3,606 movies labelled comedy or drama, metadata serialised as a text card plus the poster image, with BERT and ViT-16 extractors (pp. 5–6; Figure 3, p. 6). Fixed hyperparameters: learning rate 10⁻³, minibatch 128, 10 or 15 epochs, τ = 0.1, one hidden layer, projection size 128 (Table 1, p. 6). Assumption: binary labels only (pp. 3–4). [my reading] Class imbalance is not discussed, though hypertension averages F1 of 17.1–27.6 across the five arms against accuracy of about 83–85% (Tables 2 and 8, pp. 6, 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a: no formulation, analysis or guarantee. The method content is loss (2) and the single versus per-modality design (p. 4). [my reading] As extracted, both terms of (2) read log σ(g_j(u)ᵀg_j(v)/τ), so the negative-pair term does not push different labels apart as written, and the projection is f_j in the text but g_j in (2) (p. 4); the extraction may have lost a minus sign.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol: three arms (unprojected concatenation; PCA to 128 dimensions; the contrastive projection), each as a single or per-modality projection (p. 6); downstream random forest, XGBoost, CART, logistic regression, RBF support-vector classifier and MLP, all at default parameters and untuned (p. 6); 5-fold cross-validated out-of-sample F1, with AUC and accuracy in Appendix A (pp. 6, 12–13).
  - Averages over models and extractors (Table 2, p. 6): per-modality contrastive F1 76.3 against 58.3 unprojected (diabetes), 27.6 against 19.8 (hypertension), 76.4 against 56.2 (movies). Per-cell deltas (Tables 3–5, p. 7) are mostly positive for the per-modality arm, with losses for logistic regression and MLP in several cells and −26.6 for the single projection with GTE-Qwen2 and the support-vector classifier (diabetes, Table 3). A t-SNE plot is offered as the reason simpler models gain (Figure 4, p. 7).
  - Time and quality (Table 6, p. 8): one run per method, on a Xeon Silver 4316 restricted to 8 cores "to emulate" a constrained site and on an 80 GB A100. Unprojected F1 0.818 (14 s on CPU); contrastive 0.845 (51 s on CPU, 34 s on GPU, 3 MB of GPU memory); full fine-tuning 0.909 (39,562 s, 33,770 MB). The 6-point loss to fine-tuning is printed (p. 8).
  - [my reading] Where the gains come from: the largest deltas sit on degenerate defaults. The support-vector classifier's unprojected F1 is 0.1 on both clinical tasks with ClinicalBERT and 0.3 on movies (Table 12, p. 12), giving +81.2 and +75.1 (Tables 3 and 5, p. 7). The best model per task barely moves: best F1 81.2 → 82.5 (diabetes), 35.5 → 32.5 for the per-modality contrastive arm while per-modality PCA reaches 38.4 (hypertension), 77.9 → 77.4 (movies) (Table 9, p. 12); best AUC likewise (Table 10, p. 12). The medical tables omit random forest although their legends define it (p. 7), and the movie text speaks of "3 out of the 5 cases" for a six-model table (p. 7). No code, seeds or lightweight fine-tuning baselines (text searched for `code`, `github`, `adapter`, `LoRA`, `supervised contrastive`).
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits noise-contrastive estimation and word2vec as origins; positions against unsupervised vision methods (SimCLR, SwAV, MoCo), multimodal methods that need implicit pairings (CPC, CLIP, ConVIRT, ALIGN) and clinical methods bound to a data structure (Xia et al.) - **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + DA + ML}: The entry is a limit of prescriptive analytics, his own included. Methods that predict optimization inputs from covariates "often attempt to optimize the mean value of an uncertain objective", but for constraints "satisfying a crucial constraint in expectation may result in a high probability of violation" (abstract, p. 1). Smart predict-then-optimize (Elmachtoub–Grigas 2022) and Bertsimas–Kallus 2020 both handle objectives, and "neither approach extends" to uncertainty in a constraint (pp. 1–2). RO rivals that use covariates (Sun et al. 2023; Tulabandhula–Rudin 2014) get guarantees from Rademacher complexity and need vast validation data, at least 240,000 points for a 90% guarantee in Sun et al. (p. 2). Statement: protect g(y, x) ≤ 0 for all y in a set built from a trained predictor of y (pp. 1–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The uncertainty set is the sublevel set of the trained model's loss around its prediction, U(X) = {y | ℓ(y, f*(X)) ≤ ρ} (eq. 3, p. 3), on the intuition that "a high loss indicates greater uncertainty" (p. 3); it can be used "off the shelf", with validation data needed only for guarantees (p. 4). Several models' losses are combined by Kendall et al.'s uncertainty weighting, whose optimal weights have the closed form σ_j² = E[ℓ_j] (twice that for classifiers) (Theorem 2.1, p. 5), giving a sum of losses each divided by its expected loss (eq. 5, p. 6). For heteroscedastic regression a mean–variance network (Nix–Weigend loss) yields an ellipsoid with predicted centre and scales (pp. 8–9). The radius is the empirical quantile of validation losses (Theorem 3.1, p. 10). What changes relative to the classical ellipsoid: centre and scale come from a covariate-driven predictor instead of a marginal mean and variance (pp. 2, 9). What stays: the classical set geometries and their counterparts (pp. 7–9).
- **D3 Model and data**: A constraint g(y, x) ≤ 0 with g concave in y for tractability (contribution 3, p. 2; p. 6); covariates X ⊂ R^p and a joint law on X × Y; decisions are functions of X (pp. 3–4). Guarantees assume i.i.d. validation losses independent of the new one (Theorem 3.1, p. 9); conditionally independent sub-Gaussian components, ŷ in the relative interior of dom g and ρ > 0 (Theorem 3.2, p. 11), plus a law-of-large-numbers approximation in m for the sharper form (13) (p. 12). Sub-Gaussianity is justified by boundedness and independence by a modeller "deciding to train separate models" (p. 14). Data: synthetic only, generated as in Elmachtoub–Grigas (p. 15); every predictor has one hidden layer of five ReLU units with early stopping on a 30% split (p. 15).
