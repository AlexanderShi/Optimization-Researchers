### S316 · Solving Large‐Scale Weapon Target Assignment Problems in Seconds Using Branch‐Price‐And‐Cut (Naval Research Logistics 2025, DOI 10.1002/nav.22249)

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Alex Paskov (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**:
- A 2025 return to integer optimization proper (branch-price-and-cut), on a defense problem.
  - MIT Lincoln Laboratory staff are thanked for support and comments: Sung-Hyun Son, Zachary Serlin, Rajmonda Caceres, Paula Donovan (p. 12).
- The Lincoln Laboratory line goes back at least to S558, a 2013 working paper on weapons coordination in a fleet.
  - Its authors are Becker, Bertsimas, Cofield, Finkel, Sung-Hyun Son and Stigile (S558 p. 1).
  - S316 does not cite it (references, p. 13).
- His two textbooks serve as the toolkit:
  - reduced costs come from Bertsimas–Tsitsiklis (1997) (p. 5);
  - the robust WTA comes from Bertsimas–den Hertog (2022) (p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + AP (defense); RO only as an extension}:
  - The first entry is a scale gap in exact methods for a classic nonlinear assignment problem.
    - The WTA comes from Manne (1958), who credits Dantzig. It is NP-hard (Lloyd–Witsenhausen 1986) (pp. 1–2).
    - Decades of heuristics followed, such as genetic algorithms and local search (p. 2).
    - Exact algorithms reached about 100 targets and weapons (Ahuja et al. 2007).
    - They then reached about 400 in under two hours (Andersen et al. 2022, "branch‐and‐adjust") (p. 2).
    - Ahuja et al. called the WTA the "holy grail" of operations research for defense (p. 1).
  - The second entry is a competitor's belief.
    - Lu and Chen (2021) used the same exponential reformulation but enumerated the columns, because they judged the pricing problem difficult (p. 5).
    - Their method does not beat Andersen et al. (p. 2).
  - Statement (eqs. (2)–(3), p. 2):
    - minimize Σⱼ vⱼ Πᵢ(1 − Pᵢⱼ)^xᵢⱼ;
    - each weapon is assigned to exactly one target;
    - rewritten as Σⱼ vⱼ exp(sⱼᵀxⱼ), with sᵢⱼ = ln(1 − Pᵢⱼ).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Enumerate target "states" (eq. (4), Fig. 1, p. 3).
    - A state is the subset t of weapons assigned to target j.
    - Each state costs qⱼₜ = vⱼ exp(Σ_{i∈t} sᵢⱼ).
    - The master problem is then a linear set-partitioning problem with exponentially many columns and a much tighter LP relaxation.
  - Make column generation work, instead of enumeration, through five components:
    - initial columns from a loosened convex relaxation, in which each weapon is spread over α = 2 targets, solved by block coordinate descent (pp. 3–5);
    - exact pricing of the nonlinear binary problem (9): an O(n log n) solver for its relaxation plus a tiny branch-and-bound (pp. 5–6);
    - clique cuts separated among fractional states (pp. 6–7);
    - a freeze-and-solve primal heuristic (p. 7);
    - branching on aggregated weapon–target values, which absorb duplicate weapons (p. 7).
  - Relative to Lu–Chen:
    - kept: the reformulation, credited to Lu–Chen and Barnhart et al. (p. 3);
    - changed: columns are priced, not enumerated, plus the five components above (p. 2).
  - Why this change: the relaxations of the compact model (2) give loose bounds, so branch-and-bound must branch extensively (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Problem class: a 0-1 assignment problem whose objective is separable by target and convex in each target's column (p. 2; §3.4, p. 9).
  - The kill probabilities Pᵢⱼ are deterministic.
  - Weapons with identical probabilities are "duplicates", and the branching rule exploits them (p. 7).
  - The robust and average-case extensions assume independent Pᵢⱼ with interval support (p. 8).
  - Data: synthetic only, from two literature generators (p. 9).
    - Andersen et al.: Pᵢⱼ ~ U(0,1) and vⱼ ~ integer U[1,100]. It is used "more heavily" because it gives looser relaxations and longer solve times.
    - Lu–Chen, Ahuja et al. and Sonuç et al.: Pᵢⱼ ~ U(0.6,0.9) and vⱼ ~ integer U[25,100].
  - There is no partner data, although Lincoln Laboratory supported the work (p. 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - A log transform turns the objective into an exponential of a linear function (p. 2).
  - A state reformulation of the Dantzig–Wolfe type, via Weyl–Minkowski (p. 3).
  - Initialization (Algorithm 1, pp. 4–5):
    - Problem (5) relaxes the assignment equality to Σⱼ xᵢⱼ = α.
    - Its row subproblems (7) are solved by KKT case analysis (Slater's condition holds) and bisection on the multiplier.
    - No convergence statement is given for the block coordinate descent; Wright (2015) is cited instead (p. 4).
  - Pricing:
    - the reduced cost comes from LP duality (eq. (8), p. 5);
    - the relaxation (10) is solved by KKT cases and a sort of λᵢ/(v sᵢ) (Algorithm 2, p. 6);
    - at most one variable is fractional, so the branching rule is fixed, and each branch reuses the sort, "often O(1)" (p. 6).
  - Clique cuts: two screening properties, each with a short proof (pp. 6–7):
    - if a violating clique exists, one exists among fractional states only;
    - a violating clique spans at least two targets.
    - From Balas–Padberg: all clique cuts together define the convex hull, and finding a violating clique is NP-complete (p. 6).
  - Near-integrality (p. 7):
    - at most m + n variables are basic;
    - so the heuristic fixes the variables at one and solves the small remaining binary problem with Gurobi, in milliseconds.
  - Branching uses x̂ᵢⱼ, the value of weapon i and its duplicates at target j, and chooses the pair with the largest rounding effect (p. 7).
  - Extensions, formulated but not computed (pp. 7–9):
    - cost-minimizing WTA (12): its pricing problem (13) keeps the form of (9);
    - robust WTA: the objective is monotone in P, so the worst case over intervals reduces to the WTA at the interval ends (p. 8);
    - average-case WTA: by independence, it reduces to the WTA with E[P] (p. 8);
    - dynamic WTA over revealed-target trajectories (16)–(17) (pp. 8–9);
    - general separable nonlinear assignment (18): pricing stays efficient when f_b is integer-convex and Ψ adds no constraints (p. 9).
  - There is no complexity bound for the whole algorithm.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: a MacBook Pro (M2 Max, 16 GB); C++11; Gurobi 10 for the LPs and the integer subproblems (p. 9).
  - Duel 1: against the prior exact method, on its own 21 instances (text p. 9; Table 1, p. 10).
    - My reading of the merged cells:
      - 400–1,000 targets;
      - 200–500 distinct weapon types;
      - 1–3 copies of each type, so 200–1,500 weapons.
    - BPC: 0.058–3.584 s, with the lower bound equal to the best objective on every instance (p. 9).
    - Branch-and-adjust:
      - 419.9 s up to the 7,200 s cap;
      - unfinished on 15 of 21 instances;
      - its objective is never better than BPC's.
    - Speed-up factors are 2,008–42,857. For the 15 capped runs they are lower bounds, computed from the cap.
    - Not stated: whether branch-and-adjust was rerun on the same laptop, or its times were taken from the 2022 paper (p. 9).
  - Duel 2: against three heuristics (pp. 9–10).
    - The heuristics are artificial bee colony, simulated annealing and very-large-scale neighborhood search.
    - All three were re-implemented in C++11 with maximum compiler optimization.
    - All start from the same warm start, Ahuja et al.'s min-cost-flow construction.
    - Instances: one each of 100 × 150, 500 × 750 and 1,000 × 1,500 (targets × weapons) (p. 10; Fig. 4).
    - Results (p. 10):
      - BPC's first solution comes about as fast as the heuristics' on the smallest instance, and several times faster on the two larger ones;
      - it is closer to optimal;
      - on the two larger instances, BPC proves optimality before the heuristics' warm start has finished.
  - Duel 3: column generation against Lu–Chen's column enumeration (pp. 10–11).
    - Instances: 10 sizes × 10 instances, from the second generator, with as many weapons as targets.
    - BPC is about two orders of magnitude faster and solves every instance in under a second.
    - It needs fewer LP solves and fewer pricing calls. Each pricing call is about 100× faster: an O(n log n) sort against an O(n²) topological sort.
    - Admitted:
      - Lu–Chen's method became unstable with more weapons than targets, and slow on the other generator;
      - so these instances are "easy": their relaxation gap under the reformulation is zero (p. 10).
  - Scale (text p. 11; Table 2, p. 12):
    - My reading of Table 2: 2,500–10,000 targets, half as many weapon types, 1–3 copies of each.
    - Solve times are 2.65–390.51 s.
    - Three of the eight runs end with the lower bound below the best objective in the third decimal: 119.688 vs 119.690; 10.074 vs 10.075; 122.378 vs 122.380. No optimality tolerance is stated.
    - Limits printed (p. 11):
      - larger instances exceed the laptop's memory, because the probabilities take quadratic storage;
      - the Gurobi LP solves take "a strong majority" of the runtime (Fig. 6);
      - so further scaling needs a custom LP solver for the sparse set-partitioning relaxation.
  - Not reported: ablations of the α-initialization, the clique cuts or the branching rule.
    - Yet the contributions promise experiments on "the importance of the methodology’s various components" (p. 2).
    - Only qualitative remarks are given: random initial columns give unstable duals (pp. 3–4), and naive branching rules cause "intractable" tree blow-ups (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) When the master problem's columns are subsets:
  - write the pricing problem's continuous relaxation;
  - solve it by KKT case analysis plus a sort;
  - check how many variables can be fractional.
  - With one fractional variable, branch-and-bound is a fixed rule that reuses the sort (pp. 5–6).
- (D4) Seed column generation from a loosened convex relaxation, not from random columns (pp. 3–5).
  - Here each weapon may cover α = 2 targets.
  - Solve it by block coordinate descent with bisection on the row multiplier.
- (D4) Before separating clique cuts, prove screening lemmas that shrink the search: fractional states only, at least two targets. Then grow cliques greedily from the surviving pairs (pp. 6–7).
- (D4) With duplicate items, branch on aggregated original variables (an item plus its duplicates), not on master columns, to break symmetry (p. 7).
- (D5) Race an exact method against heuristics on time–quality curves (pp. 9–10).
  - Start every heuristic from the same construction warm start.
  - Plot the exact method's lower bound as well.
- (D5) When a rival runs only on easy instances, say why they are easy (a zero relaxation gap), and report the harder generator elsewhere (p. 10).

### S400 · Constructing uncertainty sets from covariates in power systems (IEEE Transactions on Power Systems 2025, DOI 10.1109/TPWRS.2025.3530410)
Read level: **full**, from the author's accepted version (p. 1).
- Read in full: pp. 1–11: §§I–VIII, Theorems 1–2 with their proofs, and Tables I–VIII.
- Skimmed: the references (pp. 11–12).
- Figures 1–3 are captions only. Figure 3, the outer-approximation timing, has no numbers in the text (p. 11).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Thodoris Koukouvinos and Angelos Georgios Koulouras (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1). Partly funded by Advanced Research Projects Agency award DE-AR0001282 (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**:
- The energy line of his robust optimization programme:
  - S006 (2012), adaptive robust unit commitment at ISO New England, cited as [12];
  - S337 (2024), marginal pricing, with Koulouras, cited as [16];
  - this paper.
- Koukouvinos and Koulouras finished robust optimization PhDs in 2025 (note 01 §1.5).
- A conference version exists: S427 (IEEE PESGM 2024; Andrianesis, Bertsimas, Koukouvinos, Koulouras).
  - S427 ensembles wind forecasts to size data-driven uncertainty sets.
  - The first three sentences of its abstract are those of S400's abstract (S427 p. 1).
  - S400 does not cite it (refs [1]–[33], pp. 11–12).
- A sibling paper from his group is S440 (Bertsimas–Boucher 2025; card c32).
  - It also builds sets around covariate-based predictions, but calibrates the radius on validation data.
  - S400 does not cite it.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + DA + ML; IO as the engine; AP (energy) as the testbed}:
  - Entry: the size of an uncertainty set controls cost and conservativeness, yet "there has been little work in determining their size" (abstract, p. 1).
    - Robust unit-commitment papers report results over a grid of budget values. That approach "does not offer a principled framework" (p. 1).
    - Box bounds are chosen "somewhat arbitrarily or with strong probabilistic assumptions" (p. 1).
    - The newer data-driven structures (spatio-temporal, convex hull, partition-and-combine) still have size parameters that are not chosen from data and are not tied to metrics stakeholders use (pp. 1–2).
  - A market motive: robust locational marginal prices and robust bidding depend on the size of the set (p. 1).
  - Statement (p. 3): learn the regression coefficients β and the tightest norm-bounded set around the predictions that holds p × 100% of the training points.
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - One MIO learns the centre and the size together (pp. 3–4).
    - It minimizes the mean absolute error (MAE) of a linear regression plus the mean radius.
    - Binaries zᵢ, with big-M constraints, mark the covered points. Σzᵢ ≥ pn enforces empirical coverage.
    - Variants: box (1), budget (3), box-budget (5), and an ellipsoid (a mixed-integer conic quadratic problem).
  - The size follows the model's error: a less accurate model gets larger bounds at the same p (p. 2).
  - A covariate-dependent radius, δⱼ(x) = δ⁰ⱼ + qⱼᵀx (pp. 4–5).
    - It is likened to the second learner in gradient boosting.
    - At the optimum it equals the largest covered in-sample residual.
  - For larger data sets: outer approximation on z, with cuts from the dual of the inner LP (pp. 5–7).
  - Relative to a normal confidence interval [19] and to fixed-size data-driven sets (p. 2):
    - kept: the norm-bounded structures;
    - changed: who sets the size (an optimization over data), and its link to a metric stakeholders use, p.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Sets: U(ŷ, δ) = {y : ‖y − ŷ‖ ≤ δ}, for the ℓ1, ℓ2 and ℓ∞ norms (p. 3).
  - A robust counterpart needs f linear or concave in y and U convex (p. 2).
  - The guarantees assume (pp. 3–4):
    - ‖x‖₂ ≤ R and yⱼ ≤ Q almost surely; the bound on y is justified because wind is bounded by installed capacity (p. 3);
    - ‖β*ⱼ‖₂ ≤ B;
    - an i.i.d. sample;
    - n → ∞.
  - Application: deterministic unit commitment (UC), problem (17), with a dc power flow, minimum up and down times and ramping (p. 7).
    - Only the wind capacity is uncertain.
    - It is handled by linear decision rules (LDR), following [32]: "LDR often achieve near-optimal performance" (p. 7).
    - LDR are optimal for the convex-hull set (p. 8).
  - Data:
    - 24-hour forecasts from 5 vendor models, for 5 wind resources of "a large US ISO", with realizations (p. 8);
    - the covariates are the 5 forecasts, so the regression is a forecast ensemble (pp. 8–9);
    - training: the two prior days (2 × 24 × 5 points);
    - test: one operating day (p. 9).
  - Grids: IEEE 14-bus and 118-bus systems (pp. 9–10).
    - The 5 wind generators are placed on the grid "at random". Line limits are 120 MW (p. 9).
    - Wind is 130% of load on the 14-bus system and 11% penetration on the 118-bus (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Big-M coverage MIOs (1), (3) and (5), plus the ellipsoid variant (pp. 3–4).
  - Theorem 1 (box): the true probability that the robust constraint holds is at least p (p. 3).
    - Proof: a Rademacher-complexity bound for the ρ-Lipschitz MAE (Bartlett–Mendelson), then a chain of probability inequalities.
    - As stated, the guarantee holds for n → ∞ (p. 3).
  - Theorem 2 (budget): the probability is at least 1 − m(1 − p), by summing per-coordinate events (p. 4).
    - The authors call it "meaningful only in problems where m is not very large" (p. 4).
  - Box-budget sets inherit both theorems (p. 4).
  - The ellipsoid inherits Theorem 2 through ‖R‖₂ ≤ ‖R‖₁ ≤ √m‖R‖₂ (p. 4).
  - No guarantee is stated for covariate-dependent bounds (pp. 4–5).
  - Outer approximation:
    - Problem (7) splits the MIO into a minimum over z of an inner LP f(z). The inner LP's dual is Proposition 1 (pp. 5–6).
    - f is called convex "as it is the point-wise maximum over affine functions of z". Its subgradient is Σⱼ µ*ᵢⱼ yᵢⱼ (p. 6).
    - Cutting planes of the Kelley and Duran–Grossmann type. They converge finitely but exponentially in the worst case (p. 6).
    - They run as a single tree with lazy callbacks (Quesada–Grossmann) (p. 6).
    - The warm start sets zᵢ = 1 for the OLS residuals below the p-quantile (p. 6).
    - Duals (14) and (16) cover the covariate-dependent and budget cases (pp. 6–7).
  - Robust counterparts by LP duality: box (21), box-budget (22), convex hull (23). The full model is (24) (p. 8).
  - My reading, three gaps:

### S685 · Can artificial intelligence deliver on the promise of precision medicine? (HepatoBiliary Surgery and Nutrition 2025, DOI 10.21037/hbsn-2025-455)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–4, including the references.

**Role**: core. An editorial commentary, commissioned and externally peer reviewed (p. 3). Its voice is co-authored ([stated-joint]). · **Coauthors**: Chen Lin (Peking Union Medical College Hospital), Samuel Singer (MSKCC), Georgios Antonios Margonis (MIT and MSKCC; the corresponding author) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**:
- A position statement from his clinical-AI programme with Margonis. It cites four of the group's works (refs. 4, 8, 12, 13, p. 4):
  - S235 (JAMA Surgery 2022, margin width);
  - S252 (Lancet Oncology 2024, imatinib);
  - S393 (npj Digital Medicine 2024, R.O.A.D.; card s20);
  - a 2025 Research Square preprint on R.O.A.D. trial emulation.
- "our 2018 study at Johns Hopkins" (p. 1) is ref. 7, an Ann Surg 2017 paper with Margonis as first author. The first-person plural is the team's voice. It is not evidence of Bertsimas's own practice.
- Submitted 28 June 2025, published online 26 September 2025 (p. 1). That is six months after S526 was published (24 March 2025).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML; AP (health care)}:
  - Entry: the gap between an aim stated 60 years ago and its methods.
    - Bradford Hill remarked that RCTs "do not tell the doctor what he wants to know" (p. 1).
    - That remark frames heterogeneity of treatment effects as the root of precision medicine (p. 1).
  - Two barriers (p. 1):
    - unobserved confounding in observational data, illustrated by the association between margin width and survival in colorectal liver metastases;
    - no objective way to stratify patients for subgroup analysis, even in RCTs. Subgroups examined one variable at a time invite multiple testing, confirmation bias and oversimplification.
  - The question: can AI deliver? To do so, it must "(I) generate clinically trustworthy patient stratifications, and (II) adjust for—or at least be robust to—unobserved confounding" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - AI can do the first task, but not, by itself, the second (p. 2).
    - Trees (OPTs) and "interpretable meta-learners like the X-learner" find subgroups objectively, with interactions (p. 2).
    - But TMLE, IPW and double machine learning "still rely on the assumption that all confounders are measured" (p. 2).
  - How population-level training can serve individual care: subgroups are "intermediate strata between the global and the individual" (p. 2).
  - The team's two frameworks for confounding (pp. 2–3):
    - withholding an effective therapy from patients who may not need it. This needs observational cohorts that record the natural history of the disease;
    - finding treated patients who are unlikely to benefit. This needs RCTs.
    - Both depend on disease-specific data.
  - A contrast between domains (p. 3):
    - diagnosis is where AI succeeds and black boxes are accepted: images are rich and structured, labels are objective, and interpretability is less needed;
    - prognosis needs calibration, because the absolute benefit grows with baseline risk.
- **D3 Model and data**: n/a (no model and no data).
  - The data a method needs are named: real-world data, natural-history cohorts, RCTs (pp. 1–3).
  - Calibration is said to depend on two conditions (p. 3):
    - covariate shift;
    - concept shift, which is often driven by unmeasured prognostic variables.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a (no formulation and no proof). The argument's devices:
