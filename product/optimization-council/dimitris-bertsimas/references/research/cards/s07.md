### S263 · An analysis of the guaranteed withdrawal benefits for life option (SSRN working paper 2008, SSRN abstract 1312727; no DOI)
Read level: **full** for the main text, pp. 1–26 (abstract, introduction, model, §3 analysis, §§4–6 numerical results, §7 conclusion, Tables 1–4). Appendix C (computational methods, pp. 30–34) was skimmed. Appendices A–B (Whittaker functions and reflection-principle derivations, pp. 26–30) were not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Premal Shah, first and corresponding author; Bertsimas is second (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a finance side work (AP) of the RO decade; no optimization model.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP: finance and insurance; SD only as the stochastic-calculus toolkit}: A product forced the problem. Guaranteed withdrawal benefits for life (GWB) are a fast-growing option embedded in variable annuities (VAs). Over 95% of VA products carry some guarantee, with 2007 VA sales above $180 billion, and the election rate of the GMWB family rose from 24% (2004) to 43% (first half of 2007) (pp. 1–2). GWB for life "is yet to be analyzed in detail in the literature" (p. 3). GMWB studies ignore the ratchet step-up and rest on Black–Scholes (pp. 3–4). The problem: value a typical GWB and size its risk factors for insurers, investors, clients and regulators (p. 1; p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: The paper climbs a ladder of models.
  - First, a continuous-time Black–Scholes version with exponential retirement lifespans gives an analytical value and the sensitivities (§3, pp. 8–14).
  - Then the realistic product (annual step-ups, any mortality table) is repriced under three models tuned to the same yield curve and option-implied volatility (pp. 4–5, 18–22): BSM; SILN, with two-factor Vasicek rates; and SISV, which adds Heston volatility.
  - The spread across the calibrated models is reported as "model risk" (p. 5).

  Investor behaviour is fixed: she withdraws exactly the guaranteed amount, because taxes and risk aversion deter the "optimal" dynamic withdrawals assumed by earlier papers (pp. 6–7). Nothing is optimized.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Contract: a benefit base B_n with ratchet, account C_n, waiting period W, retirement age A_R, withdrawal rate q and a fee h on the benefit base (pp. 5–6). The fee is charged separately, to avoid a fixed-point problem (p. 6, fn. 6).
  - Mortality is independent of markets, and the insurer is risk-neutral to it; its risk is sized, not priced (p. 7).
  - §3 admits two tractability assumptions: continuous step-ups and cash flows, and a constant death intensity after retirement (p. 8).
  - Inputs:
    - the 2008 mortality table of the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation;
    - W = 3, A_R = 65, q = 6%, h = 0.65%; ages 50–70; equity exposure 20–80%; and a client "meta-portfolio" (p. 15);
    - σ_e = 20%, σ_b = 2%, r = 3.5% (p. 16), plus published Vasicek and Heston parameters (pp. 21–22).
  - No jumps (p. 19). A backtest uses Shiller's S&P data for 1871–2007 (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Proposition 1 shows homogeneity of degree one in (B, C), which reduces the state to x = C/B (pp. 9–10). Proposition 2, memorylessness after retirement, removes time (p. 10).
  - Lemma 1 gives an ODE with a smooth-pasting condition at x = 1, by a "rough proof" (pp. 10–12), solved with Whittaker functions (p. 12).
  - The inception value uses the joint law of the running maximum and the account (reflection principle and change of measure), integrated numerically (pp. 13–14).
  - The discrete models use backward recursion on a 301-point grid (pp. 20, 30) and Monte Carlo with 10,000 paths (pp. 32, 34).
- **D5 Evaluation** (one line): Net value and break-even fee are computed by age and asset mix (Fig. 1, p. 16; Tables 2–3, pp. 23, 25). For the meta-portfolio, the break-even fee is 0.47% (BSM), 0.55% (SILN) and 0.58% (SISV), and the net value is 3.75%, 2.11% and 1.40% of sales (p. 24). Longevity is shifted by one year either way (Tables 1 and 4, pp. 18, 26). No Monte Carlo errors are reported in the parts read.
- **D6–D7** (brief):
  - Positioning: the paper extends GMWB studies that ignore the ratchet and use Black–Scholes (pp. 3–4). It brings interest-rate and systemic-mortality arguments from the annuity literature to GWB (pp. 4–5), and it questions a jump result for mixing jumps with higher effective volatility (p. 19, fn. 10).
  - Writing: findings are listed up front (pp. 4–5) and again as five numbered conclusions (pp. 25–26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Five conclusions (pp. 25–26):
  - A fee of 65 basis points seems enough overall, though this "rests on the assumptions made about the distribution of investor profiles".
  - One price for every cohort invites adverse selection.
  - Interest-rate volatility and fat tails raise the cost.
  - The choice of model moves the value by more than 2% of sales, about $200 million a year for a company selling $10 billion of VAs, so regulators should standardize a pricing model.
  - Longevity risk is large and hard to hedge.

  Stated limits: §3 "cannot be used as an absolute valuation tool" (p. 4); no jumps (p. 19); Gaussian rates can turn negative (p. 21). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the object judged is the owner's current rule, a single fee of roughly 50–90 basis points for every cohort and asset mix (p. 2). It is scored by the insurer's own metrics: net value, break-even fee and liabilities (pp. 16–17, 23–25). The verdict is to price by age and investment style (p. 25). But no decision is optimized, there is no partner or field data, and the recommendation stays qualitative.
- New-pattern candidate: *model ladder with matched calibration*: a closed-form toy for intuition, then realistic models tuned to the same market observables, with the spread across models reported as model risk (pp. 4–5, 21–26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Before solving a path-dependent valuation, look for degree-one homogeneity in the state (here benefit base and account) to remove a dimension, and for memorylessness to remove time (pp. 9–10).
- (D5) Calibrate competing models to the same observables (the yield curve and the one-year at-the-money implied volatility), so that their differences measure model structure, not inputs (pp. 5, 21–22).
- (D5) Backtest the contract on long public data over several windows, and show where the conclusion flips: 1950–1972 against 1871–1972 (pp. 7–8).
- (D3) Charge fees outside the account. Break-even fees then need no fixed-point solve; argue that the approximation is small (p. 6, fn. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Our analysis reveals that 1) GWB has insufficient price discrimination and is susceptible to adverse selection and 2) valuations can vary substantially depending on which class of models is used." (p. 1)
- "We start with a simple model to build intuition and then incrementally refine it to make it more realistic." (p. 4)

### S278 · Robust chirped mirrors (Applied Optics 2008, DOI 10.1364/ao.47.002630)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–7, the whole paper with its references.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Omid Nohadani (corresponding author, MIT Operations Research Center and Sloan); Jonathan R. Birge and Franz X. Kärtner (MIT Research Laboratory of Electronics and EECS). Bertsimas is last author (p. 1). **Position in trajectory**: in this batch, the first application of the robust local search of S090 to engineering design. S090 is cited as [17], "to be published" (p. 7). The line continues in S116 and S141.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + HS; AP: optics}: An application forced the problem. Mirror pairs for ultrafast lasers need more than 200 layers at nanometre precision, which "challenges even the best manufacturers"; small thickness errors can destroy mode-locking (p. 1). Earlier fault-tolerant designs optimize first-order tolerances in commercial codes, or a Monte Carlo average over a neighbourhood (Yakovlev and Tempea) (pp. 1–2). Robust optimization results are limited to convex problems, but these designs are nonconvex and defined by simulation (p. 2). The problem: minimize the worst-case merit over a Euclidean ball of layer errors (eqs. (2)–(4), p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: A robust local search tailored to thin films, in three steps.
  1. Worst cases lie on the shell of the ball, so the inner search runs there (p. 3).
  2. The error set is cut to minimum-coherence errors: uniform Fourier amplitudes with free phases and no zero-frequency term. This drops the correlated, systematic shifts that design cannot fix, reduces the search to ⌊N/2⌋ dimensions and leaves an unconstrained inner search (pp. 3–4).
  3. A second-order cone program (SOCP) finds the direction at maximal angle to all the worst errors found. Its infeasibility marks a robust local minimum (eq. (9) and Algorithm 1, p. 4).

  The merit function and its analytic gradients are unchanged (p. 2).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The design: a 208-layer SiO2/Ta2O5 mirror pair for a Ti:sapphire laser, optimized over 156 wavelengths from 650 to 1200 nm (pp. 2–3).
  - The merit: the fourth power of the reflectivity error plus a weighted squared group-delay error (eq. (1), pp. 2–3).
  - Errors are independent, additive and normal with σ = 0.5 nm; systematic errors are left to production (p. 3).
  - The radius Γ = 0.0075 μm covers 95% of errors (p. 3).
  - The reduced set is "justified empirically": by Monte Carlo, its cumulative distribution never exceeds the full set's by more than 4% (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): There is no theorem.
  - Parseval keeps the Fourier-parametrized errors on the shell (eq. (7), p. 4).
  - The SOCP requires β ≤ −ε, with ε chosen heuristically (p. 4).
  - The step is the shortest that clears all bad neighbours (p. 4).
  - Robustness is said to be "guaranteed to be equal or better" than the nominal design's (p. 2), with no proof given.
- **D5 Evaluation** (one line): One instance, started from the nominal optimum. Plots show worst-case and nominal cost by iteration (Fig. 2, p. 5), the worst case as the error ball grows beyond Γ (Fig. 5, p. 6), and the mean and 95th percentile of cost over 10^6 sampled designs per error size (Fig. 6, p. 6). The nominal design is the only baseline. No run times are given beyond "on the order of a million" merit evaluations (p. 2).
- **D6–D7** (brief):
  - Positioning: it credits Yakovlev and Tempea with showing that robustness can be greatly improved at the design level, and differs by probing the exact merit function over a bounded set, not a Monte Carlo average (pp. 1–2).
  - Writing: claims are hedged ("arguably more robust", p. 2), and "price of robustness" is borrowed as a named term (p. 5).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A robust optimization technique for thin-film filters whose advantage grows when errors exceed the assumed size (pp. 6–7). Limits: it reaches a robust local minimum only (p. 5); the layer count is fixed (p. 7); Γ may misstate the real errors (pp. 5–6). Future work: use the robust cost inside needle optimization, which changes layer counts (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · contradiction ✗ (with its stop rule, not its scope): the paper leaves the solver class on purpose, since no internal structure can be exploited (p. 2), and reports no solve time at the target scale. Tractability is argued by dimension reduction instead (⌊N/2⌋, p. 4).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: the "price of robustness" is named and shown, as the rise in nominal cost and ripple (p. 5, with the Fig. 2 caption), and performance is plotted against error size (Fig. 5, p. 6). But Γ is fixed from the error statistics (p. 3); there is no curve over a knob and no solution-independent bound.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: the nominal optimum is both the warm start and the incumbent beaten (pp. 5–6). There is no certificate, only SOCP infeasibility with a heuristic ε (p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate: *robust local search family*: when no class can be kept, robustify the search, not the model. Neighbourhood ascents find the worst errors, and an SOCP step moves away from them (pp. 3–4; also S116, S141).
- New-pattern candidate: *stress-test the robust design beyond the set it was built for* (Figs. 5–6, p. 6; also S053, S064, S116).
- New-pattern candidate: *cut the uncertainty set to the errors design can fix, and check the cut by Monte Carlo* (pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3) Remove from the uncertainty set what design cannot fix (systematic shifts) and what is statistically negligible (highly correlated errors). Then check by Monte Carlo that the reduced set gives almost the same cost distribution (pp. 3–4).
- (D4) Parametrize an error shell by Fourier phases with fixed, equal amplitudes. Parseval preserves the norm, and the worst-case search becomes unconstrained and half the dimension (eqs. (6)–(7), p. 4).
- (D4) Find a robust descent direction with an SOCP that maximizes the angle to all known worst errors, and use its infeasibility as the stopping test (eq. (9), p. 4).
- (D5) Report a robust design beyond its design tolerance: the worst case as the error ball grows, and the Monte Carlo mean and 95th percentile against error size (Figs. 5–6, p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Optimized chirped mirrors may perform suboptimally, or completely fail to satisfy specifications, when manufacturing errors are encountered." (p. 1)
- "The unavoidable “price of robustness” through a decrease of the nominal performance of the robust solution is apparent, with increased ripple in the group delay and reflectivity." (p. 5)

### S413 · Approximating integrals of multivariate exponentials: A moment approach (Operations Research Letters 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2007.07.002)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–6, the whole paper.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Xuan Vinh Doan (MIT Operations Research Center, corresponding author) and Jean Lasserre (LAAS, Toulouse) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: CP; the moment–SDP line of SW2 applied to numerical integration. The multivariate case is in an Operations Research Center technical report of January 2006 (ref. [1]; pp. 2, 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP; motivated by statistics and finance}: Multivariate integrals, such as normal probabilities over compact sets, are computed by Monte Carlo or cubature. Cubature is mostly limited to boxes and simplices, and Genz's algorithms cover rectangular bivariate and trivariate normals (p. 1). The problem: lower and upper bounds for ∫ g e^h over a compact set given by nested polynomial bounds (eqs. (1)–(2), pp. 1–2). Such sets include those of order statistics (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: The integral is a linear functional ⟨g, z⟩ of the moments z of the measure e^h dx on the set (p. 2). Integration by parts gives linear equations between these moments and the moments of boundary measures (eqs. (7)–(9), p. 2). Positive semidefinite moment and localizing matrices are added, and the functional is minimized and then maximized. The result is a hierarchy of SDPs that brackets the integral (eq. (14), p. 3). Lasserre's moment hierarchy is kept; what is new is the integration-by-parts equations, which pin down the measure in the limit.
- **D3 Model and data**: g and h are polynomials. The exposition covers only two variables on a box, "for clarity"; the general case is in the report (p. 2). There are no data: the tests use Gumbel's bivariate exponential (θ = 0.5) and normals whose mean and factor entries are random in [−1, 1] (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Lemmas 1–2: any measures that satisfy the equations are the true measures. The proof uses density of polynomials in C¹ and a signed-measure argument (pp. 3–4).
  - Theorem 1: the SDPs are solvable for large r, with lower bounds rising and upper bounds falling to the integral. The proof uses compactness, a diagonal subsequence and Putinar's theorem (pp. 4–5).
  - No convergence rate is given.
- **D5 Evaluation** (one line): Matlab and SeDuMi on a 2.4 GHz Pentium IV (p. 5).
  - Gumbel: the half-gap falls from 6.5e−3 (r = 2) to 2.9e−9 (r = 5), in 0.38–6.23 s (Table 1, p. 5).
  - 100 random bivariate normals reach four digits at average r = 3.64, in about 1 s (p. 5).
  - Trivariate normals take about 250 s (r = 4) and 4000 s (r = 5) and are "not competitive" with Genz (p. 5).
- **D6–D7** (brief):
  - Positioning: against Monte Carlo, cubature and Genz's specialized algorithms (p. 1), building on Lasserre's and Laurent's moment-matrix machinery (p. 3).
  - Writing: a numbered list of contributions mapped to sections (p. 1); the general case deferred to a report (p. 2); a plain admission of where the method loses (p. 5).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The framework, a convergence proof and computations (p. 1). Limit: the trivariate run times (p. 5). Future work: rational integrands, and using the linear equations to eliminate moment variables and shrink the SDPs (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ {CP}: a quantity owned by numerical analysis and statistics is written as two SDPs whose optimal values bracket it. The boundary is printed: the trivariate cost, and the loss to the specialized code (pp. 2–5).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: there is no MIO and no warm start, but every answer carries a certificate: the upper–lower half-gap (Table 1, p. 5). The paper duels the incumbent (Genz), concedes the loss, and names the cases only it covers (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: *two-sided certified bounds from one relaxation*: minimize and maximize the same linear functional over a feasible set that shrinks to the true measure (pp. 3–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To compute an integral of g e^h, treat it as a linear function of moments and generate linear moment equations by integration by parts. Add moment and localizing PSD constraints, and solve min and max SDPs for a certified interval (pp. 2–3).
- (D4) To prove that such a hierarchy converges, show that the equations determine the measure uniquely. Then use bounded moments, a diagonal subsequence and Putinar's theorem (pp. 3–5).
- (D5) Report the half-gap and the run time at each relaxation order, and stop at a stated tolerance, here four digits (p. 5).
- (D7) Name the specialized competitor, concede where you lose, and say what you cover that it does not (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "We propose a method to calculate lower and upper bounds of some exponential multivariate integrals using moment relaxations and show that they asymptotically converge to the value of the integrals when the moment degree increases." (p. 1)
- "Clearly, in comparing with results obtained with algorithms specially designed for rectangular normal probabilities by Genz [4], our method for trivariate normal distributions is not competitive." (p. 5)

### S053 · Models for minimax stochastic linear optimization problems with risk aversion (Mathematics of Operations Research 2010, DOI 10.1287/moor.1100.0445)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–23. This covers the abstract, the introduction and contributions, §2 (Theorem 2.1 with its proof, Lemma 2.1, Theorem 2.2 and the Jensen reduction), §3 (the reduction in Theorem 3.1's proof, Theorem 3.2 and the constraint-generation algorithm) and §4 (both experiments). The distribution constructions in the proofs of Theorems 2.2 and 3.3 (pp. 7, 11–12) were skimmed. The paper has no conclusion section; it ends with §4.2 (p. 22).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Xuan Vinh Doan (MIT Operations Research Center), Karthik Natarajan (City University of Hong Kong) and Chung-Piaw Teo (National University of Singapore) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: CP and RO; the moment–SDP line of SW2 carried into two-stage stochastic programming under ambiguity. Received April 2008 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO, distributionally robust; CP; LO in the recourse}: The distribution of the random parameters is rarely known precisely (p. 1). Minimax stochastic programs go back to Žáčková and Dupačová and are solved by sampling, subgradient or cutting-plane methods. The classical first-moment bounds are Jensen's and Edmundson–Madansky's (p. 1), and Delage and Ye proved polynomial solvability with an ellipsoid method (p. 2). Risk aversion enters through a convex, nondecreasing, piecewise-linear disutility (p. 2). Problem (2): the first-stage cost plus the worst expected disutility of the recourse cost, over all distributions with a given mean and second-moment matrix. The randomness sits either in the recourse objective or in its right-hand side (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Objective uncertainty becomes one polynomial-sized SDP (Theorem 2.1, pp. 4–5). The steps: dualize the inner moment problem (Isii's strong duality), show that the matrix multiplier is PSD, swap inf and max (Sion's theorem), and write one linear matrix inequality (LMI) per disutility piece.
  - The SDP dual reads as scaled conditional moments, from which distributions attaining the bound are built (Theorem 2.2, pp. 6–8). In the risk-neutral case the bound is Jensen's; with risk aversion it is a mix of Jensen bounds (p. 8).
  - With randomness in the right-hand side, the problem is NP-hard (Theorem 3.1, pp. 9–10). It becomes an SDP when the dual extreme points are known (Theorem 3.2, p. 10); otherwise it is solved by constraint generation with a biconvex separation step (p. 12).
  - Against Delage–Ye, whose class is broader, this result "reduces the minimax stochastic linear program to solving a single semidefinite optimization problem" (p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The ambiguity set: all distributions on ℝ^p with a known mean and second-moment matrix (eq. (4), p. 3).
  - Assumptions 1–4: a bounded, nonempty first-stage polytope; complete fixed recourse; dual feasibility; a positive definite covariance (p. 3). Assumption 5: the dual extreme points are known (p. 10).
  - The data are synthetic:
    - 5 facilities and 20 customers in the unit square, with moments from 1,000 uniform cost vectors (p. 14);
    - 20 clustered customers, with moments from 1,000 perturbations (p. 19).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - Moment duality, a scaling argument along the most negative eigenvector to show PSD, Sion's theorem and one LMI per piece (pp. 4–5).
  - A feasible dual point built from any distribution (Lemma 2.1, p. 6), and a limiting construction from Bernoulli mixtures (pp. 7–8).
  - NP-hardness by reduction from 2-norm maximization over a polytope, through the equivalence of separation and optimization (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Evaluation** (one line): Two illustrative instances. The minimax SDP (Matlab 7.4, SeDuMi, YALMIP) is set against a data-driven LP on 10,000 normal samples (CPLEX 9.1) (pp. 13–14, 19).
  - Measures: costs; each solution's cost under its extremal distribution; and the contamination (1 − λ)P_d + λP_worst(x_d).
  - Results: minimax wins on mean cost beyond λ = 0.75 in the first instance and 0.5 in the second, and on dispersion for nearly all λ (pp. 15–22).
  - No run times are reported.
- **D6–D7** (brief):
  - Positioning: Delage–Ye is compared head-on and credited with the broader class (pp. 2, 5). Dupačová's contamination test is adopted and given the missing choice of contaminating distribution (pp. 15–16).
  - Writing: a numbered list of contributions keyed to sections (p. 3), and no conclusion section (p. 22).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions (pp. 3, 16):
  - a tight SDP for objective uncertainty;
  - NP-hardness and a solvable special case for right-hand-side uncertainty;
  - explicit worst-case distributions, which double as stress tests.

  Limits: the general right-hand-side case is NP-hard, the extreme points can be very many, and separation is biconvex (p. 12). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ {CP RO}: a problem owned by stochastic programming is written as one polynomial-sized SDP, and its dual is read as the worst-case distribution. The boundary is printed: NP-hardness for right-hand-side uncertainty (pp. 4–10).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: there is no knob. The price of protection is read along a contamination axis: the minimax plan costs more under the data distribution (p. 14; Table 1, p. 15) and wins past a crossover in λ (pp. 16, 20).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the standard practice, the sample-based solution, is the baseline, and decisions are scored by their realized cost under contamination (pp. 14–22). But the data are synthetic, and there is no owner.
- New-pattern candidate: *the worst-case distribution from the dual as a stress test for any candidate solution* (p. 1; pp. 15–16, 20).
- New-pattern candidate: *stress-test the robust solution beyond the set it was built for* (pp. 16–22; also S278, S064, S116).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) For a sup of an expectation over a class with given mean and covariance:
  1. Dualize to a semi-infinite constraint.
  2. Prove the matrix multiplier PSD by scaling along its most negative eigenvector.
  3. Swap min and max by Sion's theorem.
  4. Write one LMI per piece (pp. 4–5).
- (D4) Read the variables of the dual SDP as scaled conditional moments, and build the extremal distribution from them (pp. 5–8).
- (D5) Stress test (pp. 16–17):
  1. Solve the data-driven model.
  2. Compute that solution's own worst-case distribution.
  3. Mix it into the data distribution with weight λ from 0 to 1.
  4. Compare solutions by mean, standard deviation and upper semideviation.
- (D4) Prove NP-hardness of a robust or minimax model through its separation problem and the equivalence of separation and optimization (pp. 9–10).

**Quotes**:
- "The minimax solutions thus guarantee to hedge against these worst possible distributions and provide a natural distribution to stress test stochastic optimization problems under distributional ambiguity." (p. 1)
- "This suggests that if there is significant uncertainty in the knowledge of the exact distribution, the minimax solution would be a better choice." (p. 21)

### S064 · A soft robust model for optimization under ambiguity (Operations Research 2010, DOI 10.1287/opre.1100.0821)
Read level: **full** for the main text, pp. 1–15: the theory, both applications and the endnotes. The appendix and the electronic companion are not in the file; they hold the proofs of Propositions 2.1–2.2 and Lemma 3.1, Tables 2–5 and Figure 2. The extraction drops many spaces between words and encodes decimal points as a control character, so the numbers below come from the prose.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Aharon Ben-Tal (Technion) and David B. Brown (Duke) (p. 1); Brown is a former student (SKILL.md Academic Lineage, RO) · **Position in trajectory**: RO; published in a Special Issue on Computational Economics, received March 2008 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO under ambiguity; AP: finance}: The paper opens with two critiques of ambiguity sets, posed as questions: how to obtain the set of distributions, and why treat all distributions inside it equally while ignoring those outside (p. 1). Standard robustness can be relaxed only by shrinking the set, which drops every guarantee outside it (p. 3). Problem (4): maximize the expected payoff under a reference distribution, subject to E_Q f(x) ≥ −ε for every Q in Q(ε) and every ε ≥ 0. The guarantee weakens as distributions move away (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Soft robustness is a nested convex family of sets whose guarantees relax linearly.
  - Theorem 2.1: soft robustness is equivalent to a constraint on a normalized convex risk measure, and conversely, so feasibility is a jointly convex constraint (pp. 4–5).
  - Proposition 2.1: optimizing over it needs only a bisection over ε, with one standard robust problem per step (p. 7).
  - Proposition 2.2: a standard robust optimum is soft robust up to a computable level (p. 8).
  - Theorem 3.1: downside probability bounds under ambiguity, each a one-dimensional convex problem (pp. 10–11).

  What is kept is the convexity of the nominal problem; what changes is that one set becomes a graded family.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The payoff is concave in x and bounded, and X is closed and convex (p. 3). The families come from φ-divergences, with relative entropy, the Hellinger distance, optimized certainty equivalents (OCEs) and CVaR as cases (pp. 6–7).
  - Bonds: 49 bonds with independent defaults and default probabilities in [0.001, 0.05], with a 20% leverage cap; robustness levels are set by an error factor ρ on the default probabilities (pp. 11–12).
  - Asset allocation: 11 asset classes, monthly returns from April 1981 to February 2006, a 36-month window and yearly rebalancing (p. 14), with no transaction costs (endnote 7, p. 15). The entropic parameter is set to 50 because it was "convenient to illustrate the approach" (endnote 6, p. 15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief):
  - The Föllmer–Schied dual representation of convex risk measures, with joint convexity in (μ, x) (pp. 4–5).
  - Concavity of the value function in ε, which justifies bisection (p. 7; the proof is in the appendix, not in the file).
  - Lemma 3.1: tail bounds from OCEs, tightened with the support and inflated over a divergence ball (p. 9).
  - The entropic bounds are exponential and beat a sharp Chebyshev bound for large deviations (p. 11).
- **D5 Evaluation** (one line):
  - Bonds, with ROME in MATLAB at about 30 s per problem (p. 12): at a 10% perturbation (ρ = 1.1), standard robustness puts 80%+ in the riskless asset for an extra 0.05% expected return. At the same ρ, soft robustness earns about 1.2% more expected return and over 14% more maximum return (pp. 12–13).
  - Assets, over 25 years out of sample, against CVaR, standard robust and comprehensive robust portfolios (p. 14): soft robust beat standard robust in every case, by +0.38% expected return on average, at some extra downside risk. It also beat CVaR on realized risk for α = 0.3, …, 0.8.
- **D6–D7** (brief):
  - Positioning: the paper draws on the economics of ambiguity (Ellsberg, Gilboa–Schmeidler, Hansen–Sargent, Maccheroni et al.) and says preferences are not its focus (pp. 1–2). It contrasts Fischetti and Monaci's light robustness, where optimality is enforced and feasibility is an output, with soft robustness, where feasibility levels are enforced and optimality is the output (p. 2).
  - Writing: it opens with two critiques posed as questions (p. 1) and hedges its empirical claims ("seemingly favorable", p. 1).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the framework, its duality with convex risk measures, complexity, conservatism, probability guarantees and two applications (pp. 1–3). Limits:
  - Proposition 2.2 gives no explicit bound on optimality (p. 8).
  - The OCE bound is "often loose" (p. 10).
  - In-sample CVaR constraints are generally not met out of sample (p. 14).

  Future work: explain the out-of-sample benefit, "perhaps using tools from statistical learning theory" (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅: the property is protection against ambiguity. The paper gives it one interpretable knob (ρ, an error factor on default probabilities) and solution-independent downside bounds (Theorem 3.1). It prints a priced table with its bad case: standard robustness turns highly conservative even at small ρ (pp. 11–13). The entropic parameter, fixed because it was convenient (endnote 6, p. 15), also supports the skill's correction that knobs get fixed arbitrarily.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅: soft robustness keeps convexity and costs a few standard robust solves (pp. 5, 7).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: a 25-year rolling out-of-sample test against the practitioners' benchmark (CVaR), with decision metrics such as realized CVaR and loss probabilities (pp. 13–14). There is no owner or partner.
- New-pattern candidate: *graded guarantees over nested uncertainty sets* (soft robustness, and comprehensive robustness in Example 2.3) as the remedy for over-conservatism (pp. 1–3, 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *stress-test beyond the set*, here out of sample over 25 years (p. 14; also S278, S053, S116).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Replace a single uncertainty set by a nested family whose guarantee relaxes linearly with the set's size. Check the equivalence with a convex risk measure, so that the constraint stays convex (pp. 3–5).
- (D4) When a constraint must hold at a continuum of levels, prove the value function concave in the level and bisect, solving one standard robust problem per step (p. 7).
- (D4) Tighten tail bounds from a risk measure with the support, here an upper bound on the payoff. Then inflate them for ambiguity through a one-dimensional convex problem over a divergence ball (pp. 9–11).
- (D5) Calibrate the robust parameter to the benchmark: take the smallest level whose robust constraint is no weaker than the CVaR constraint, so that the comparison is like for like (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "relaxing standard robustness with soft robustness yields a seemingly favorable risk-return trade-off: each case results in a higher out-of-sample expected return for a relatively minor degradation of out-of-sample downside performance." (p. 1)
- "We note that Proposition 2.2 provides a generic result relating the standard robust and soft robust approaches, but it does not provide any explicit bounds on the difference in optimality." (p. 8)

### S116 · Nonconvex robust optimization for problems with constraints (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2010, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1090.0319)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–15, the whole paper, in its Articles in Advance version (online May 19, 2009). The displayed formulas of §6 (eqs. (33)–(41)) are garbled in the extraction, so claims about them rest on the prose.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Omid Nohadani and Kwong Meng Teo (MIT Operations Research Center and Sloan) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO + HS. The paper extends the unconstrained robust local search ("Bertsimas et al. (2009)", that is, S090) to constraints and to parameter uncertainty (p. 1). The IMRT model comes from a joint project with the radiation oncology group of Massachusetts General Hospital (p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + HS; AP: health care (IMRT, intensity-modulated radiation therapy)}: Robust optimization had focused on convex problems with explicit structure (p. 1). The authors' earlier method handled unconstrained, simulation-based, nonconvex problems; here, constraints must stay feasible under perturbation (p. 1). Problem (10): minimize the worst-case cost over a Euclidean error ball, subject to the worst case of every constraint being ≤ 0 (p. 3). Parameter uncertainty is folded in by stacking the parameters into the decision vector and fixing them (eq. (22), p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: A constrained robust local search.
  - Gradient ascents in the neighbourhood find high-cost and infeasible neighbours, with n + 1 ascents per constraint.
  - If the design is not yet robust, an SOCP moves it away from the infeasible neighbours, ignoring cost.
  - If it is robust, an SOCP moves it away from the high-cost neighbours and from infeasible designs just beyond the neighbourhood.
  - The search stops at a "robust local minimum" (Definition 1) (pp. 3–5; Algorithm 2, p. 5).

  Structure is used where it exists (Table 1, p. 5). For linear, quadratic or concave constraints, the inner maximum is an LP, one trust-region SDP or one gradient ascent, and a linear constraint gets its exact robust counterpart. Parameters are given zero steps (pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Implementation errors lie in a 2-norm ball (p. 2). Cost, constraints and their gradients come from a simulator (p. 1).
  - Application I: a two-dimensional polynomial with two nonconvex constraints, Γ = 0.5 (pp. 7–8).
  - Application II (pp. 10–11):
    - 1,192 voxels;
    - 85 variables: 5 beams × 16 beamlets, plus 5 angles;
    - doses from the radiotherapy code CERR at 2° steps, with linear interpolation.
  - The clinical case was "downsized due to numerical restrictions" (p. 10), and the robust problem is approximated by dropping second-order terms (p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): There is no convergence theorem. The algorithm rests on:
  - the SOCP angle device (eqs. (7), (14), (15), pp. 2–4);
  - termination by Definition 1 (p. 4);
  - closed-form inner maxima for convex constraints (p. 5);
  - step sizes from "a simple geometric problem" (p. 5).
- **D5 Evaluation** (one line):
  - Application I: two starts reach two robust local minima, with the worst-case cost cut by more than 70% (Fig. 7, p. 9; p. 15), in under 10 min on a 3.4 GHz Xeon (p. 8). With explicit linear counterparts, a run takes 96 s instead of 3,600 s (p. 9).
  - Application II: Γ is swept in steps of 0.001 from two starts, and every design is scored on 10,000 normal scenarios. The results are Pareto frontiers of mean cost against probability of violation (pp. 12–13), at about 20 h per search (p. 13).
  - Comparator: convex robust optimization with the angles fixed. Its SOCPs took CPLEX 9.1 hours, so they were replaced by LPs that solve in under 5 s (p. 14). The convex approach wins at high violation probabilities, robust local search below 2% (Fig. 11, p. 14).
- **D6–D7** (brief):
  - Positioning: the paper judges polynomial SDP relaxations (Henrion–Lasserre, Kojima) not yet practical (p. 8). It claims robustness over beam angles and intensities jointly, "for the first time"; Chan, Bortfeld and Tsitsiklis had fixed the angles (p. 10).
  - Writing: an intuition-building two-dimensional example comes before the real application (p. 1), and figures carry the algorithm's geometry (Figs. 1–5, pp. 2–7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: constrained robust local search, with shortcuts for convex constraints and with parameter uncertainty. The original treatment plan "proves to always violate the constraints" once uncertainty is introduced (p. 15). Limits:
  - only local minima are found, and there can be several (p. 8);
  - the worst-case cost is estimated by random sampling, which is "not a good gauge" (p. 13);
  - runs take 20 h (p. 13);
  - the clinical case was downsized (p. 10).

  No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: the class is left on purpose, since no practical method exists even for the two-dimensional robust polynomial problem (p. 8). But it is kept wherever a constraint is convex, through exact counterparts (Table 1, p. 5), and the gain is measured: 96 s against 3,600 s (p. 9).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: Γ is the knob, swept to map "the Pareto frontier in a single sweep". The frontier is handed to clinicians, who pick a violation probability (p. 13). But violation probabilities are measured by sampling; there is no solution-independent bound.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠: a duel with the incumbent, convex robust optimization with fixed angles, reports where each method wins (the 2% threshold, p. 14). There is no certificate.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠: the model comes from a hospital partner, and the nominal plan is the baseline, which fails (pp. 10, 15). There is no field test.
- New-pattern candidate: *Γ-continuation*: sweep the uncertainty size in small steps, warm-starting each robust search from the last design, to trace the frontier between robustness and cost (pp. 12–13).
- New-pattern candidate: *robust local search family* (also S278, S141), and *stress-test beyond the set*, here on 10,000 scenarios (p. 12; also S278, S053, S064).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) In a constrained robust local search, first restore feasibility under perturbation and ignore cost. Then cut the worst-case cost, while also avoiding infeasible designs just outside the neighbourhood, within a radius enlarged to (1 + δ)Γ (pp. 3–4).
- (D4) Replace sampled inner maximizations by closed forms wherever a constraint is convex (an LP, a trust-region SDP or one gradient ascent), and time the gain (pp. 5, 9).
- (D3) Handle parameter uncertainty in an algorithm built for implementation errors: stack the parameters into the decision vector and give them zero steps (pp. 6–7).
- (D5) Trace the frontier from two different starts, the nominal optimum and a strict interior point, and score every design on the same sampled scenarios (pp. 12–13).
- (D5) Build the convex comparator by fixing the nonconvex variables (here the beam angles), and report the regime where each method wins (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "The proposed method iteratively moves along descent directions for the robust problem with nonconvex constraints and terminates at a robust local minimum." (p. 1)
- "The original treatment plan, found using optimization without consideration for uncertainties, proves to always violate the constraints when uncertainties are introduced." (p. 15)

### S134 · On the power of robust solutions in two-stage stochastic and adaptive optimization problems (Mathematics of Operations Research 2010, DOI 10.1287/moor.1090.0440)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–22. This covers:
- the abstract, the introduction, the models, and the contributions with Tables 1–2;
- §2: Theorem 2.1 with Lemmas 2.1–2.3 and its proof, and Theorems 2.5–2.7;
- §3: Theorem 3.1 with its proof;
- §4.1: Theorem 4.1 with its proof, then Theorem 4.4;
- §5.1: Theorem 5.1 with its proof; §5.2: Theorem 5.3 with its proof;
- the conclusions.

The proofs of Theorems 2.2–2.4, 4.2–4.3 and 5.2 were skimmed.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Vineet Goyal (MIT Operations Research Center) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO; the adaptive-optimization line with Goyal, next to the paper on the limits of affine policies (S060, cited as [4]). Received June 2009 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO + IO; stochastic programming as the comparator}: Stochastic optimization is "by and large computationally intractable" (p. 1). Robust optimization is tractable but may be conservative (pp. 1–2). Fully adaptive two-stage optimization is hard to approximate within O(log m) (Feige et al., p. 3). The question: how much does one static robust solution lose against the optimal two-stage stochastic solution (the stochasticity gap) and against the fully adaptive one (the adaptability gap)? The setting is covering-type two-stage mixed-integer problems with uncertain right-hand sides, later also uncertain costs (pp. 2–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: One scaling argument.
  - A symmetric set in the nonnegative orthant lies below twice its centre (Lemma 2.3, p. 8). So doubling the solution for the centre scenario is feasible for the robust problem.
  - By linearity, and with no integers in the second stage, the expected recourse is feasible at the centre. Hence the robust cost is at most twice the stochastic cost (Theorem 2.1, pp. 7–9).
  - The same move gives:
    - a bound for positive sets (Theorem 2.7, p. 14);
    - an adaptability gap of at most 2 for right-hand sides (Theorem 4.1, p. 16), and at most 4 when costs are uncertain too (Theorem 5.1, p. 19);
    - a gap of exactly 1 for hypercubes, even with uncertain constraint coefficients (Theorem 5.3, pp. 20–21).

  Each bound is bracketed by a tight or an unbounded example (pp. 12–18; Tables 1–2, p. 6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Constraints have the form Ax + By ≥ b, with c, d ≥ 0, nonnegative mixed-integer variables and b ≥ 0. This excludes packing constraints and maximization, as the paper admits (pp. 2–4).
  - Complete recourse is not needed (p. 3).
  - The measure is symmetric, or satisfies the weaker condition E[b] ≥ b₀ (pp. 7–8).
  - The bounds fail with binary variables, because the proofs scale solutions by 2 (pp. 10, 16).
  - There are no data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): A bounding-hypercube lemma and the point of symmetry (Lemma 2.3, p. 8), then scaling and linearity of expectation (p. 9). The examples:
  - a hypercube with uniform b is tight at 2 (p. 12);
  - the simplex with a uniform measure gives a gap of n + 1 (p. 13);
  - since E[min of n uniforms] = 1/(n + 1), cost uncertainty makes the gap unbounded (p. 15);
  - the hypercube's worst corner gives a gap of 1 (pp. 20–21).
- **D5 Evaluation**: n/a (theory only; no computations).
- **D6–D7** (brief):
  - Positioning: robust optimization is set between the intractability of stochastic programming (Shapiro–Nemirovski) and the inapproximability of adaptive optimization (Feige et al.), with affine policies as the usual compromise. These include his own work with Iancu and Parrilo and with Goyal (pp. 1–3). The nearest models, approximation algorithms for stochastic set cover, are more restrictive and need complete recourse (pp. 3–4).
  - Writing: results are summarized in two tables with tightness marks (p. 6), and the positive findings are called "surprising" (pp. 5, 18, 21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A map of gaps by type of uncertainty set (Tables 1–2, p. 6). The paper reads it as "a surprising approximate equivalence" between two-stage robust and stochastic optimization (p. 5), and concludes that robust optimization is "more powerful than believed previously" (p. 21). Limits: the covering structure, nonnegativity and no binaries; and the stochasticity gap is unbounded under cost uncertainty (pp. 4, 10, 15). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ {RO IO}: the tractable static robust model stands in for intractable stochastic and adaptive models. It is "a single mixed integer optimization problem" whose size does not depend on the scenarios and which needs no knowledge of the distribution (pp. 5, 12). Counterexamples map the boundary of the claim (pp. 12–18).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: the price of static robustness is given as solution-independent worst-case ratios, with the bad cases printed: nonsymmetric sets and cost uncertainty (Tables 1–2, p. 6). There is no knob or curve.
- New-pattern candidate: *bracket every approximation bound with a tight example and an unbounded example on the next wider class, summarized in one table* (pp. 6, 12–18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To bound the gap between robust and stochastic optima, scale the solution of the centre scenario. If every scenario is at most twice the centre (symmetric sets in the nonnegative orthant), doubling that solution is robust-feasible (pp. 8–9).
- (D4) State the weakest distributional condition the proof uses (E[b] ≥ b₀ instead of symmetry), and say which result needs which (pp. 7–8).
- (D4) For each bound, give a tight instance and an unbounded instance one assumption away. Collect them in a table with tightness marks (pp. 6, 12–18).
- (D3) Extend a result on symmetric sets to "positive" sets: sets inside a symmetric set whose centre lies in the set. These cover budget sets such as k-fault tolerance (pp. 4, 14).

**Quotes**:
- "We refer to the ratio of the optimal cost of the robust problem and the optimal cost of the two-stage stochastic problem as the stochasticity gap." (p. 1)
- "Therefore, the results in this paper show that the robust optimization approach is more powerful than believed previously and provides a tractable and good approximation for both the two-stage stochastic and the two-stage adaptive optimization problem under fairly general assumptions." (p. 21)

### S141 · Robust optimization with simulated annealing (Journal of Global Optimization 2010, DOI 10.1007/s10898-009-9496-x)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–12, the whole paper, in an online-first print (received 17 March 2009, accepted 11 November 2009).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Omid Nohadani (corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: HS + RO; the global version of the robust local search (refs. [6, 8], p. 2). This is the 2010 side of Tension T2, set against the 1993 review S005 (card c01).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {HS + RO; AP: nanophotonics}: Optimized designs can fail outright under implementation errors: the Tacoma Narrows bridge, and an antenna array whose radiation characteristics 5% errors can destroy (p. 2). Robust optimization results are "limited to convex problems", and their reformulations can grow. Simulation-based design has no structure to exploit, and earlier robust local searches gave only local robust optima (p. 2). The problem: find the global minimum of the worst case g(x), the maximum of f(x + Δx) over ‖Δx‖ ≤ Γ, using only a black box that returns f and its derivative (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Robust simulated annealing (RSA).
  - Gradient ascents in the uncertainty set find "bad neighbors" (the paper's term, p. 3).
  - The worst case is smoothed as (1/β) log Σ e^{βf} over these neighbours, with β the inverse temperature (eq. (3), p. 4).
  - Gaussian trial moves are accepted by the Metropolis rule on Boltzmann weights of the smoothed worst case.
  - β grows by Eq. (7), extracted as "βn = β0ecn" with c = 1 + (a symbol lost) (pp. 4–5).

  For the photonic problem, the paper adds adaptive sampling (eq. (19), after Ingber), re-annealing (β0 up 5% every 100 acceptances) and re-heating (β0 down 5% every 50 rejections) (pp. 9–10). Kept from robust local search: the neighbourhood ascents. Changed: the SOCP descent step becomes a stochastic global move.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - f is unrestricted, and the uncertainty set is a 2-norm ball (pp. 2–3).
  - Theorem 1 assumes a bounded set of minima, a global robust optimum, and that all designs become equally accessible as n → ∞ (p. 5).
  - Application I: the two-dimensional polynomial of S116, with Γ = 0.5 (p. 6).
  - Application II: 50 dielectric cylinders (100 coordinates) placed to match a top-hat power profile. The finite-difference Helmholtz model was verified against laboratory experiments (pp. 8–9). Errors are normal with σ = 40 μm, so Γ = 550 μm gives 99% coverage (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Theorem 1 (global convergence) is proved by citing Geman and Geman's condition that β grow "not faster than βn = ln(n)/β0", plus Ingber's bound for the Gaussian generator and detailed balance (pp. 5–6). As extracted, Eq. (7) makes β grow exponentially in n. Yet the proof states: "The first condition is satisfied by the choice of the annealing schedule in Eq. 7" (p. 5). [inferred reading; the extraction loses symbols, so check the PDF.] The convergence rate is declared out of scope (p. 6).
- **D5 Evaluation** (one line):
  - Application I: the global robust minimum is checked by enumerating the worst-case function on a fine mesh (p. 7). One run is shown, and other starts are said to reach the same optimum (pp. 7–8).
  - Application II: re-annealing is compared with re-annealing plus re-heating (Fig. 4, p. 10). RSA is set against the earlier robust local search result from the same start (p. 10), which the prose puts at about 8% (p. 9); Fig. 5 is labelled 8% and 40% (p. 11).
  - There are no wall-clock times for RSA and no statistics over repeated runs. Runs were stopped when trials exceeded "an empirical threshold" (p. 11).
- **D6–D7** (brief):
  - Positioning: RSA follows convex RO, which is limited to convex problems, and the authors' own local robust searches, which give local optima (p. 2). It stands in the line of Kirkpatrick et al., Metropolis, and Geman and Geman (pp. 3, 5). Polynomial SDPs are dismissed as "not widely used in practice" (p. 7).
  - Writing: the paper opens with famous failures (p. 2). Its claims are strong and unhedged ("warrants", p. 9; "any previously available methods", p. 12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The paper offers a generic black-box global robust optimizer with a convergence theorem (pp. 2, 5, 11–12), said to outperform "any previously available methods" (p. 12). Limits admitted: the parameters are problem-specific and "cannot be generalized" (p. 10), the rate is out of scope (p. 6), and runs are non-deterministic (p. 11). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · contradiction ✗: the paper says RSA "warrants a global robust optimum" (p. 9), for runs stopped by an empirical threshold (p. 11). The claim rests on an asymptotic theorem (pp. 5–6), with no certificate, no gap and no comparison at equal computing time. This goes against Method 4's rule never to call uncertified results optimal, and against Integrity Rule 3. It also goes against the 1993 review's own yardsticks: S005 called time-asymptotic results "largely irrelevant" and judged heuristics at equal computing time (card c01, S005 pp. 3–4). Evidence for Tensions T1 and T2.
- Method 1 · variant ⚠: the class is left. The case made against convex relaxations is that polynomial SDPs grow with degree and dimension and are "not widely used in practice" (p. 7).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠: "price of robustness" names the slight rise in nominal cost (pp. 7, 11). There is no knob or curve.
- New-pattern candidate: *robust local search family* (also S278, S116). Here the stochastic search is robustified too, by annealing a smoothed worst case (eq. (3), p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Smooth a worst case over sampled bad neighbours into a log-sum-exp at the annealing temperature, so that the Metropolis rule compares worst cases at equal β (pp. 4, 6).
- (D5) Check a claimed global robust optimum on a low-dimensional instance by enumerating the worst-case function on a fine mesh, before moving to high dimension (p. 7).
- (D3) Size the uncertainty ball from measured error statistics for a stated coverage: σ = 40 μm gives Γ = 550 μm for 99% (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, applying the proposed RSA to this model warrants a global robust optimum." (p. 9)
- "We terminated the simulations in Fig. 5 after the number of trials for the same acceptance step exceeded an empirical threshold." (p. 11)
