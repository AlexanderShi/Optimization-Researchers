### S039 · Robust sample average approximation (Mathematical Programming 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10107-017-1174-z; arXiv 1408.4445)
**Read level**: full — the whole arXiv v3 text: main text pp. 1–30 (§§1–8) and references pp. 30–32 read closely; the appendix of proofs pp. 33–45 (§10.1–10.18) read through, with each proof followed to its device but not re-derived line by line. The journal print was not read; all pages are preprint pages.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vishal Gupta (Marshall School of Business, USC) and Nathan Kallus (ORIE and Cornell Tech, Cornell) at v3; funding line: an NSF Graduate Research Fellowship, Grant No. 1122374 (p. 1). SKILL.md's lineage lists Gupta (RO) and Kallus (DA) as his students. · **Position in the trajectory**: the distributionally robust sibling of the same trio's "Data-driven robust optimization" (cited as [7], arXiv 1401.0212; pp. 4, 31), written while the RO programme turned data-driven (2014) and in print four years later (2018, batch metadata).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO DA; AP as testbeds}: the incumbent is SAA, credited first with two virtues — almost-sure convergence of optimal values and solutions as N → ∞, and tractability — which the authors say underlie its popularity (p. 2). Two faults follow: no useful a priori finite-sample guarantee except in special cases, and instability of the SAA solution and its true out-of-sample cost for moderate N, documented for portfolios and ill-conditioned regression (p. 2). Two-sample SAA (hold-out) buys a bound by "sacrificing half of the data", and its Student-t bound can fail (pp. 2, 5); data-driven DRO (Delage–Ye [15], Calafiore–El Ghaoui [13]) gives a finite-sample guarantee but no asymptotic convergence (pp. 2–3). Problem as stated: a method for the historical-data setting (one fixed sample of size N, p. 4) that inherits SAA's convergence and tractability and adds a finite-sample guarantee and stability (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open by granting the incumbent's virtues, name exactly two faults, and define success as keeping the virtues while removing the faults (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Robust SAA is a three-step recipe: choose a goodness-of-fit (GoF) test and a level α independently of the data; take the test's confidence region as the distributional uncertainty set (DUS); solve the min–sup problem (§2.1, p. 7). The insight is the duality between tests and confidence regions: any data-driven DUS that contains the true F with probability at least 1 − α is the confidence region of some test, including the sets of [13, 15], which test only the first two moments (pp. 7–8). Significance of the test then gives the finite-sample guarantee (Proposition 1, p. 8), and a new property, uniform consistency, characterizes convergence (Theorem 2, p. 13). Test menu: Pearson χ² and the G-test for known finite support (p. 8); Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Kuiper, Cramér–von Mises, Watson and Anderson–Darling for univariate data (p. 9); a test of marginals (p. 11); and a new test of the full joint distribution based on the linear-convex ordering (LCX), which the authors call the first of its kind (p. 11). For unbounded support, Proposition 2 shows that the worst case is infinite for any unbounded continuous cost, so the GoF region is intersected with a test on a generalized moment E[φ(ξ)] that dominates the cost (eqs. 9–11, p. 10). Kept from SAA: the empirical distribution stays at the centre (it serves as the Slater point of the dual, p. 18 fn. 4) and the problem stays a single-level convex program; changed: the plug-in distribution becomes a worst case over a ball whose radius shrinks as O(N^−1/2) (pp. 8–9). A by-product is the "price of data": the expected reduction of the bound from one more observation (§6, p. 23).
- **D3 Model and data**: min over x ∈ X ⊆ R^dx (closed) of E_F[c(x; ξ)] with ξ drawn IID from an unknown F (pp. 1, 4). Existence needs a finite point, equicontinuity of c in x, and either compact X or coercivity (Theorem 1, p. 5); convergence adds Assumptions 1–3: equicontinuity; X bounded, or uniformly coercive on a set D with F(D) > 0; bounded support, or a φ with c = O(φ) whose worst-case deviation from the sample mean vanishes (p. 13). Only simple hypotheses are tested, not composite ones (fn. 2, p. 6). Assumptions adopted for tractability: the cost is a maximum of K pieces convex in x (Theorems 9, 13; pp. 16, 19), and for the LCX test the pieces are also concave in ξ (eq. 37, p. 21), at a reformulation size of 2^K − 1 (p. 22). Thresholds come from one of three sources: distribution-free tables (KS for continuous F), a proven bound (Theorem 16), or the bootstrap, which is only asymptotically exact (p. 6). In the portfolio study the moment test's level is effectively set to zero, which the sub-quadratic structure allows (p. 22). Data: none real. Newsvendor demands are synthetic (normal, right-skewed Gumbel and a mixture, truncated to [0, 250], plus unbounded variants) (pp. 24, 27); portfolio returns come from a 10-asset factor model with a normal market factor and negative-Pareto idiosyncratic terms; the authors offer, as their opinion, that a low-dimensional factor model and long tails are common in financial data (p. 28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Guarantee: if F lies in the region, the robust value bounds the true cost of the robust solution, so P(bound holds) ≥ 1 − α (Proposition 1, two lines, p. 8).
  - Characterization: a test is uniformly consistent if and only if Assumptions 1–3 imply convergence of objectives (uniformly on compacts), optimal values and solutions (Theorem 2, p. 13). The "only if" side uses Proposition 8 (portmanteau lemma and uniform integrability) and Proposition 9 (pp. 36–37). The "if" side builds four costs ‖x‖(2 ± cos x′ξ) and ‖x‖(2 ± sin x′ξ), whose convergence pins down characteristic functions (Lévy continuity theorem, Cramér–Wold device) (pp. 37–38).
  - Strictness: uniform consistency is strictly stronger than consistency (Proposition 4). The witness is a constructed test that accepts F0 whenever F0(ξ1) falls in a dyadic interval and otherwise runs a KS test at a re-adjusted level on ξ2, …, ξN; it is valid and consistent but not uniformly consistent (pp. 35–36).
  - Classical tests: χ² and G are uniformly consistent via Cauchy–Schwarz and Pinsker bounds on total variation (p. 38); KS and Kuiper via the Lévy metric; CvM, AD and Watson via counting arguments (pp. 38–39). The marginals test is not consistent, but it is c-consistent for separable costs (Proposition 5, p. 14). The Delage–Ye and Calafiore–El Ghaoui sets are c-consistent only for costs quadratic in ξ (Proposition 6, p. 15). LCX is consistent (Theorem 6) and uniformly consistent for bounded support (Theorem 7: integrate hinge functions to recover second moments of projections, then Cramér–Wold; p. 40). Table 1 marks the cases shown to be tight (p. 13).
  - Reformulation: finite support goes through Fenchel duality of φ-divergences (after Ben-Tal et al. [3]) to an SOCP for χ² and an exponential-cone program for G (Theorem 8, pp. 16, 40–41). Univariate tests become conic constraints in ζ_i = F0(ξ_(i)) (Theorem 10, pp. 17–18); the inner sup is dualized by semi-infinite conic duality (Shapiro) with the empirical distribution as generalized Slater point (Theorems 11–12, p. 18, fn. 4). Dual reading: the worst-case distribution sits on at most N + 1 points (p. 19). For LCX, an LP built from concave conjugates gives upper and lower bounds and is exact under a sub-quadratic condition (Theorem 14, pp. 21–22, 43–45).
  - Complexity: polynomial-time ε-optimality via weak separation ≡ optimization (Grötschel–Lovász–Schrijver) (Theorems 9, 13, 15; pp. 16, 19, 22, 42, 45).
  - Closed form: the KS newsvendor is solved in closed form, certified by a primal–dual feasible pair with equal objectives (Proposition 7, pp. 19, 42–43).
  - Thresholds: an exact LCX threshold comes from a VC bound (halfspaces, VC dimension ≤ d + 1) and Vapnik's relative-deviation inequality (Theorem 16, pp. 34–35). The bootstrap threshold needs a nonconvex sup, solved "with a standard MILP formulation or by discretizing the space and enumerating" (p. 34).
  - Price of data: estimated by resampling one extra point (eq. 44), with a closed-form approximation for the KS newsvendor (eq. 45) (p. 23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Set-up: all LP and SOCP problems are solved with Gurobi 5.5 (pp. 24, 28), the AD test with IPOPT 3.11 (p. 27). No machine and no solve time are reported anywhere (I searched the text for time units). α = 20% for every method (p. 24); α1 = 15% and α2 = 5% for unbounded demand (p. 27); 6.67% per marginal (p. 27). Replications per N are chosen so that the standard error of the mean is at most 0.1 standard deviations, and the plots show means with 20th–80th percentile bars (p. 24).
  - Newsvendor (b = 19, h = 1, a 95% service level; N from 10 to 10^8; Fig. 4, pp. 24–25): the SAA estimate lies below the full-information optimum 65% of the time at N = 100 (p. 24); two-sample SAA shows about twice SAA's variance (p. 25); the Delage–Ye bound converges to Scarf's 1958 bound, not to the optimum, and bootstrapping its thresholds tightens it without changing the limit (p. 25); Robust SAA is valid, convergent and less variable in the order quantity at small N (the inequality sign is lost in the extraction) (p. 26). The price-of-data estimate is "virtually indistinguishable" from the true value on a log scale (Fig. 5, p. 26).
  - Five tests compared (p. 27): comparable; AD slightly better for N ≥ 100; Kuiper and Watson best at small N; recommendation: KS, because it gives the simplest optimization problem.
  - Multi-item newsvendor: the guarantees converge, as c-consistency predicts (Fig. 7, p. 27).
  - Portfolio (10 assets, 10% CVaR, LCX test with bootstrap; the Delage–Ye thresholds are also bootstrapped, for a fair comparison, since its constants exist only for bounded support; p. 28): SAA is biased low and appears to beat the optimum at small N (sign lost) (p. 28); the two-sample SAA guarantee holds only about 50–60% of the time against the 80% target, and N > 10^7 would be needed for its t-approximation (p. 28); Delage–Ye is valid but neither convergent nor stable (p. 29); Robust SAA's bound holds in all samples, it has the best average out-of-sample suboptimality for every N, and the lowest variance of decisions (p. 29). Transaction costs are admitted as an omission (pp. 29–30).
  - What is missing: failures are reported for the competitors only, none for Robust SAA; the data are synthetic only; there are no timings; and there is no comparison with Wasserstein-ball DRO (D6).
- **D6 Positioning**: the paper presents itself as a unification of three literatures, SAA, DRO and GoF testing (pp. 3–4).
  - SAA: historical-data versus Monte Carlo use (Shapiro et al.; Kleywegt–Shapiro–Homem-de-Mello), with Mak–Morton–Wood behind two-sample SAA (pp. 4–5).
  - DRO: Scarf 1958 as the first DRO, then moment sets (including Bertsimas–Popescu [8]) and the Wiesemann–Kuhn–Sim tractability theory (p. 4).
  - Data-driven DRO: [13, 15] lack asymptotics (p. 3) and are re-derived as tests of two moments (pp. 7–8, 15); Wang–Glynn–Ye's KS formulation over unbounded support gives an infinite bound (p. 10); the trio's own data-driven RO [7] is a finite-sample cousin without asymptotics (p. 4); φ-divergence work (Ben-Tal et al., Bayraksan–Love, Klabjan et al.) is credited and extended to continuous distributions (p. 4).
  - Tone: rivals are restated, not attacked ("We consider this viewpoint to both unify and extend these previous results", p. 4), and one rival is improved with the new lens before the duel (bootstrapped Delage–Ye thresholds, pp. 15, 25, 28). The 56-item reference list (pp. 30–32) contains no Wasserstein-ball DRO paper.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: six numbered contributions (p. 3), the sixth being the price of data; a boxed three-step recipe (p. 7); the main connection stated in words before any formalism (pp. 2–3); "this is key" marks the one property rivals lack (p. 3); "In our opinion" introduces judgements (pp. 2, 12, 28); the empirical findings are given as bullets before the experiments (pp. 23–24); one table summarizes every test's convergence status with tightness marks and a "?" for the open case (Table 1, p. 13); an explicit open question carries a conjecture (p. 15); all proofs except Proposition 1's are in the appendix (p. 3); claims about the tests are hedged ("should not, however, be taken as general conclusions", p. 27). Small slips include "that that" (p. 28).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: contribution at four levels: theory (the significance and consistency characterization, a new LCX test), reformulation (LP and SOCP counterparts, closed forms), modelling guidance (choose the test by the structure of the cost; bootstrap rivals' thresholds), and the price of data (pp. 3, 30). Stated limits: uniform consistency of LCX for unbounded support is open, conjectured true (p. 15; "?" in Table 1, p. 13); the marginals test weakens in high dimension (p. 11); the reformulation grows as 2^K (p. 22); the exponential cone is "numerically challenging", and its SOC recast grows with n and N (p. 16); bootstrap thresholds are approximate (p. 6); composite hypotheses are excluded (p. 6). Unstated: the data are synthetic and there are no run times. The conclusion restates the contributions and gives no further agenda (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a data-driven DRO that reduces to an LP or SOCP for off-the-shelf solvers in most cases of practical interest, including two-stage problems with linear recourse (pp. 3, 16–23), with a dual reading (worst case on ≤ N + 1 points, p. 19) and a printed boundary (exponential cone, 2^K growth, the open LCX case; pp. 15–16, 22). Steps 3–5 all appear. The test itself is chosen by the simplicity of the resulting optimization problem (p. 27). Note for the limitation on "tractable": this 2014–2016 text defines practical tractability as solvability by off-the-shelf LP and SOCP solvers, with no timings (p. 16), between the 2004 complexity sense and the 2019 solve-time sense.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: one knob (the significance α), set from data through a statistical test, with a solution-independent guarantee P(bound ≥ true cost) ≥ 1 − α (Proposition 1, p. 8), as step 3 asks, plus a new "price of data" (pp. 3, 23). But α is fixed at 20% in every experiment, and no price curve over α is published (pp. 24, 27–28).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: a duel with the incumbent SAA, its hold-out variant and the leading data-driven DRO, the last upgraded to bootstrapped thresholds for fairness (p. 28), on synthetic data with a known full-information optimum (pp. 24, 28). There is no MIO, warm start, certificate or time limit (convex problems), and no run times.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: decisions are scored by their true out-of-sample cost, the empirical coverage of each bound, and the variance of the recommended decisions (pp. 25–26, 28–29), not by estimation accuracy. But there is no owner, current rule or real data.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: two students on one paper, and a sibling paper by the same trio on data-driven uncertainty sets, cited as [7] (pp. 1, 4, 31).
- new-pattern candidate: **exact dictionary between a statistical procedure and a robust model, with its boundary** — significance ↔ finite-sample guarantee, uniform consistency ↔ convergence (an iff), and rivals' sets ↔ moment tests (pp. 7–8, 13–15). The same move recurs in S085 and S104 in this batch; it is related to Heuristic 1.
- new-pattern candidate: **upgrade the rival with the new lens before the duel** — Delage–Ye thresholds bootstrapped once their set is read as a hypothesis test (pp. 15, 25, 28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the uncertainty set as the confidence region of a GoF test at level α, chosen before seeing the data; the finite-sample guarantee then follows in two lines (Proposition 1, p. 8).
- To compare competing data-driven sets, convert each into the test it implies and ask whether that test is (c-)consistent for your cost: separable costs need only marginal tests; costs quadratic in ξ need only moment tests (pp. 7, 13–15).
- When unbounded support makes the robust bound infinite (Proposition 2), intersect the region with a test on E[φ(ξ)] for a φ that dominates the cost, and split the level by the union bound (α1 + α2) (p. 10).
- Dualize the sup over distributions by conic duality with the empirical distribution as the generalized Slater point; the worst case then lives on at most N + 1 points (pp. 18–19).
- Estimate the value of one more observation by resampling: PoD ≈ z̄(data) − average over i of z̄(data plus a copy of ξ_i) (eq. 44, p. 23).
- Experimental protocol: choose replications per N so that the standard error is at most 0.1 standard deviations; plot means with 20th–80th percentile bars; report the empirical coverage of every bound and the variance of the decisions, not only of the objective (pp. 24–29).

**Quotes**:
- "Unlike other data-driven DRO proposals, however – and this is key – we prove that Robust SAA also satisfies an asymptotic convergence property similar to SAA." (p. 3)
- "For practical purposes, if the convergence rates are comparable as they are here, we recommend to choose the test that yields the simplest optimization problem, which in this case is the KS test." (p. 27)

### S085 · Characterization of the equivalence of robustification and regularization in linear and matrix regression (European Journal of Operational Research 2018, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2017.03.051; arXiv 1411.6160)
**Read level**: full — the whole arXiv v2 text, pp. 1–31: main text, references, Appendix A (the proof of Theorem 4, pp. 27–30) and Appendix B (the example behind Theorem 5, p. 31), which I recomputed numerically. The paper itself omits the proofs of most matrix results (pp. 18, 20–22). The EJOR print was not read; all pages are preprint pages.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Martin S. Copenhaver (Operations Research Center, MIT; supported by an NDSEG fellowship) (p. 1) · **Position in the trajectory**: the ML-under-a-modern-optimization-lens years; it sits between Xu–Caramanis–Mannor's robust-Lasso line and the group's robust classification paper (S104), which cites it to justify dropping the SVM regularizer (S104, pp. 3, 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO ML}: the entry is a known but uncharacterized fact. Robust optimization had shown that some regularized regressions are robustifications against adversarial perturbations of the data (El Ghaoui–Lebret, Xu et al., Caramanis et al., Ben-Tal et al.; pp. 2, 7), and the adversarial view was spreading in machine learning (generative adversarial networks, adversarial examples; p. 2). The gap: exactly when are robustification and regularization equivalent, and does the link reach matrix problems (matrix completion, PCA) (pp. 1–2)? Stated form: min_β max_{∆∈U} g(y − (X + ∆)β) against min_β g(y − Xβ) + h(β) (eq. 1, pp. 4–5). Motivations offered: robustification names the perturbations a regularizer protects against, and data-driven uncertainty sets could then design regularizers "in a principled fashion" (pp. 2, 5).
  - Transferable: when an equivalence is folklore, make the paper the characterization of where it fails, not one more instance where it holds (p. 10).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: four steps.
  - (i) A general equivalence for induced seminorm uncertainty sets: max_{∆∈U(h,g)} g(z + ∆β) = g(z) + λh(β) (Theorem 1, pp. 7–8), from which the known ridge and Lasso results follow as corollaries (Corollary 1, p. 8).
  - (ii) For any compact U, robustification is bounded above by a seminorm regularizer h(β) = max_{∆∈U} g(∆β), with equality at z = 0 (Proposition 2, pp. 10–11). "Equivalent" means this bound is tight for every z.
  - (iii) Non-equivalence is measured by a discrepancy function δm(a, b) = m^{1/a − 1/b} for a ≤ b (Definition 1, pp. 11–12). For ℓp loss under Frobenius-q sets, regularization gives two-sided bounds with weights λδm(p, q) and λ/δm(q, p), and the two problems are equivalent iff p ∈ {1, q, ∞} (Theorem 4, p. 12; Table 2, p. 15). Outside equivalence, the robust and regularized solution paths may share no point (Theorem 5, p. 13).
  - (iv) Matrix case: a linear perturbation map ∆(X) (p. 17); induced sets give σp penalties (Theorem 6, Corollary 2, p. 18). Nuclear-norm matrix completion is a robustification (Corollary 3, Proposition 6, p. 19). Robust PCA (F1 loss plus nuclear norm) arises from additive linear uncertainty only when the loss is F1, so not as a robust version of ordinary PCA (Proposition 7, p. 20).
  - Surrogates from robustness: the ℓ1 penalty from an ℓ0-induced set (Proposition 1, p. 9) and the nuclear norm from a rank-induced set (eq. 10, p. 19; Proposition 8, p. 20). Kept from prior work: regularization in penalized form (p. 4). Changed: the question, from tractability or the Lasso's implications to characterization (p. 10).
- **D3 Model and data**: linear regression with data (y, X), a convex loss g (typically a norm or seminorm) and a penalty h (p. 4). Uncertainty is additive in X only; uncertainty in y is folded into a new convex loss ḡ and set aside (pp. 6, 17). Sets are norm balls of fixed radius λ > 0: induced (h, g), p-Frobenius and p-spectral (pp. 3–5). The strictness results need m ≥ 2 (p. 12). Regularization is restricted to the penalized form, a loss plus a penalty (p. 4). Ridge and Lasso are recovered with the ℓ2 rather than the squared ℓ2 loss, a technicality the paper flags (fn. 2, p. 8). Matrix models: [∆(X)]ij = ⟨∆(ij), X⟩, and a column-wise model (eq. 15) (pp. 17, 21–22). No data and no instances.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1: the triangle inequality gives the upper bound; a rank-one worst case ∆̂ = (λ/g(z)) z v′, with v maximizing v′β over the dual-norm ball, attains it, and the case g(z) = 0 is treated separately (pp. 7–8).
  - Propositions 1 and 2: two set inclusions (pp. 9–10), and a check of the seminorm axioms for h (p. 11).
  - Theorem 4 (Appendix A, pp. 27–30): a Hölder chain gives the upper bound, attained by ∆̂ = λuv′ with u ∈ argmax δm (p. 27); sign alignment gives equality for p ∈ {1, ∞} (p. 27); strictness for p ∈ (1, ∞), p ≠ q follows from Minkowski's equality condition and the finiteness of argmax δm, "a geometric property of ℓp balls" (p. 28); ∆̂ = λzv′/‖z‖q gives the lower bound (p. 28); its tightness comes from scaling z → αz, a directional-derivative limit and an ε-net of the q-sphere (pp. 28–30).
  - Propositions 3–4 reduce to Theorem 4 ("Theorem 4 in disguise") via max_{∆∈U} ‖z + ∆β‖ = max_{‖u‖ ≤ λ‖β‖} ‖z + u‖ (pp. 14–15). The matrix results are said to have proofs "essentially identical" to the vector case and are not proved (pp. 18, 21–22); Proposition 8 is stated "without proof" (p. 20).
  - Theorem 5 rests on the example in Appendix B (p. 31). My recomputation of that example as printed (y = (1, 1, 1)′, X = (2, 1, 0)′, ℓ2 loss, ‖u‖1 ≤ λ|β|) gives the robust minimizer β*(λ) = (3 − λ)/(5 + λ²), checked on a fine grid for λ ∈ {0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 2.9}. So β* moves with λ instead of staying at 3/5. It lies on the regularization path, which is [0, 3/5) (β = 0 for ρ ≥ √3, not the open interval stated). The example as printed therefore does not show disjoint paths. The stated β* = 3/5 for every λ does hold if the perturbation radius is λ instead of λ|β|, i.e. uncertainty in y (also checked). The appendix also has typos: "m = m", U(1,2) beside U(1,1), and "Corollary 4" where a Lasso-type corollary is meant (p. 31). Theorem 5 itself is not refuted by this. The EJOR print was not read.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: n/a — no computations, data or solvers; the only numbers are the three-point example (p. 31).
- **D6 Positioning**: credits the equivalence line — El Ghaoui–Lebret [18], Xu–Caramanis–Mannor [45], Caramanis–Mannor–Xu [11], Ben-Tal–El Ghaoui–Nemirovski [2] — and labels Corollary 1 and Theorems 2–3 as their results (pp. 7–10). It differs from them by its aim: they address the tractability of the uncertain problem [2] or the Lasso's implications [45]; this paper, characterization (p. 10). It keeps robust statistics (Huber, Rousseeuw, Hampel) at arm's length, claiming no connection (p. 6). It casts error-in-variables and total least squares as the optimistic counterpart of the adversarial min–max (p. 7). Matrix references: Candès–Recht, Recht–Fazel–Parrilo, Candès–Li–Ma–Wright (pp. 16–17, 19). Self-citations: Bertsimas–Brown–Caramanis 2011 and the Bertsimas–Gupta–Kallus data-driven RO preprint (pp. 2, 5). Adversarial deep learning (Goodfellow et al., Shaham et al.) appears only as motivation (pp. 2, 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: abstract and contributions as two lettered or numbered items (pp. 1–2), with one strong phrase, "We break new ground" (p. 2). Definition–theorem–proof throughout, each result followed by a one-paragraph reading: the elastic net as two perturbation types, "feature-wise" and "global" (p. 10); Proposition 3 as "Theorem 4 in disguise" (p. 14). The message is carried by two summary tables (loss × set × induced penalty × iff condition; Tables 2–3, pp. 15, 22). Borrowed results are credited explicitly (pp. 7–9). The conclusion is restrained, with no future directions (pp. 22–23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory only (a characterization), with no algorithm, software or application (pp. 2, 22–23). Scope limits stated: penalized form only (p. 4); additive uncertainty in X only (pp. 6, 17); uncertainty sets beyond the induced ones are not examined in depth (p. 18); most matrix proofs omitted (pp. 18, 20–22). The motivating use — data-driven uncertainty sets that design regularizers — is not attempted (pp. 2, 5). The example behind Theorem 5 fails as printed (my recomputation, D4). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: a procedure owned by statistics (penalized regression) is rewritten as a min–max problem, its structure read off dual norms (the rank-one worst case, pp. 7–8, 27–28), and the boundary printed as iff tables (Tables 2–3, pp. 15, 22). But the boundary is one of equivalence, not of solver class or solve time; no formulation is solved.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the radius λ is read as an interpretable knob — equal to the regularization weight when the problems are equivalent (Corollary 1, p. 8), and for the elastic net λ and µ "directly control the tradeoff between two different types of perturbations" (p. 10). Outside equivalence the link is lost (p. 12) and the two paths may be disjoint (Theorem 5, p. 13). No curve, no data.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: written with a doctoral student (p. 1); its result is consumed by the group's next robust-ML paper (S104, pp. 3, 10). The step from one paper to the next is visible.
- new-pattern candidate: **exact dictionary between a statistical procedure and a robust model, with its boundary** — regularization ↔ robustification, with iff conditions and two-sided bounds where it fails (pp. 7–15, 18–22); also in S039 and S104. Full-text evidence for Heuristic 1, with a nuance: the Lasso-as-robustification result is credited to Xu–Caramanis–Mannor (Corollary 1, p. 8); this paper's own contribution is where the reading breaks.
- new-pattern candidate: **derive the convex surrogate from an uncertainty set induced by the nonconvex penalty** — ℓ1 from ℓ0 and the nuclear norm from rank, "without directly invoking convexity" (pp. 9, 19–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove that a robust problem equals a regularized one: bound above with the triangle inequality, then exhibit a rank-one worst case built from the dual-norm maximizer of β (pp. 7–8).
- Use h(β) = max_{∆∈U} g(∆β) as the canonical induced regularizer of any compact uncertainty set; equivalence holds iff this upper bound is tight for every residual z (Proposition 2, pp. 10–11).
- Quantify a failed equivalence by norm-discrepancy constants (δm), and show the constants are tight by a scaling limit (z → αz) plus an ε-net (pp. 11–12, 28–30).
- Close a characterization paper with one table: loss × uncertainty set × induced penalty × iff condition (Tables 2–3, pp. 15, 22).
- Check a small counterexample numerically before printing it; the one in Appendix B fails as printed (p. 31; my recomputation).

### S104 · Robust classification (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2019, DOI 10.1287/ijoo.2018.0001)
**Read level**: full — the IJOO print pp. 2–34: main text pp. 2–31, Appendix A pp. 31–32, Appendix B pp. 32–33 and references pp. 33–34. The per-data-set accuracy Tables 3 and 7 (pp. 22–23, 28–29) were scanned and their rows counted (75 each), not checked cell by cell; the aggregates in Tables 4–6, 8 and 10 were recomputed. Figures 1–5 are images not in the text layer (unread). File p. 1 is the INFORMS cover sheet.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jack Dunn, Colin Pawlowski and Ying Daisy Zhuo (all Sloan School and Operations Research Center, MIT) (p. 2); funding: an NSF Graduate Research Fellowship, Grant 1122374 (p. 2); Dick den Hertog editor-in-chief; received October 17, 2017, accepted May 8, 2018, online October 19, 2018; Vol. 1, No. 1, pp. 2–34 (p. 2). · **Position in the trajectory**: robust optimization joins optimal classification trees (Bertsimas–Dunn 2017) and the MIO-statistics line (least quantile regression, best subset, algorithmic regression; p. 3). Dunn is in SKILL.md's ML lineage.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML RO IO}: the three most-used classifiers (SVM, logistic regression, CART) deal with noisy training data mainly through regularization (p. 2). The entry is data error: features carry measurement and imputation error (the Wisconsin breast cancer images); labels carry survey dishonesty (contraceptive method choice), subjective clinical labels (remission) and labelling-personnel errors (p. 3). Gap claim: "To date, there has not been a principled way of modeling data uncertainty directly for classification problems in the literature" (p. 3). Prior robust SVM and logistic regression mostly cover feature noise, with theory and "limited testing on synthetic data" (p. 4), and robust decision trees did not exist (p. 4). Problem: derive feature-robust, label-robust and robust-in-both counterparts of SVM, logistic regression and optimal trees, and test whether they beat nominal and regularized versions out of sample (pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable: anchor each kind of uncertainty in a named public data set whose error mechanism a reader can picture (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: one recipe for all three classifiers: write training as an optimization problem (trees through the OCT MIO, pp. 6–8), add an uncertainty set, dualize the inner worst case (pp. 9–19).
  - Features: per-point ℓq balls of radius ρ (eq. 8, p. 9) turn into a dual-norm term inside the loss. SVM: the margin is shifted by ρ‖w‖q* (Theorem 1, p. 10; equivalent to Xu et al.'s theorem 3). Logistic regression: +ρ‖β‖q* in the exponent (Theorem 2, p. 11). Trees: a margin ρ around each split threshold (Theorem 3, p. 12).
  - Labels (new): at most Γ flipped labels (p. 13). Each loss term is affine in the binary flip, the budget polytope is integral, and LP duality gives the counterpart: SVM as an MIO (Theorem 4, p. 13), logistic regression as a constrained concave program (Theorem 5, pp. 14–15), trees still an MIO, with a strict-majority margin of 2Γ in every leaf (Theorem 6, pp. 16–17).
  - Both: composition of the two steps (Theorems 7–9, pp. 17–19).
  - Two design choices differ from the classical models. The regularizer is dropped from the nominal SVM "to avoid double counting the effect of robustness" (p. 10). The knobs ρ and Γ are chosen by validation, with ρ = Γ = 0 (the nominal model) inside the grid, so a robust model is chosen only when validation prefers it (pp. 9, 13, 17).
- **D3 Model and data**: binary data {x_i, y_i}, y ∈ {−1, 1} (p. 5). Features are perturbed additively inside per-point ℓq balls (a Cartesian product, eq. 8, p. 9), a set chosen because it "admits a simple geometric interpretation and only requires tuning a single parameter ρ" (p. 10); polyhedral and ellipsoidal sets are mentioned, not used (p. 10). Labels: at most Γ flips, a set that is not a Cartesian product (p. 13). q = ∞ (dual norm ℓ1) in all experiments (pp. 20, 21). Trees: univariate splits, the big-M OCT formulation with a minbucket constraint (pp. 6–8). Data:
  - Synthetic: two Gaussian clusters N(±1.5e, I) of 25 points each in R², plus 10 outliers N(0, 3I) with random labels; a 75/25 train/validation split; a test set of 10,000 points drawn from the two clusters only (p. 19).
  - Real: 75 UCI data sets, each turned into a one-versus-rest problem for its first class, with n up to 245,057 and p up to 857 (p. 21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Feature robustness: the dual-norm lemma (eq. 7, p. 9). For logistic regression, monotonicity of the log-likelihood in the margin reduces the inner minimum to that lemma (p. 11), and a first-order Taylor expansion links the result to regularized logistic regression (pp. 11–12). For univariate tree splits ‖a_j‖q* = 1, so the counterpart is a margin ρ and stays a linear MIO "regardless of the original choice of q" (p. 12).
  - Label robustness: f_i(∆y_i) = [f_i(1) − f_i(0)]∆y_i + f_i(0) for binary flips, the integrality of the convex hull of the budget set, then strong LP duality (pp. 13–17). Robust-in-both: feature step, then label step (Appendix B, pp. 32–33).
  - Class table: SVM goes from LP to MIO under label robustness, logistic regression from unconstrained to constrained convex, trees stay MIO (Table 9, p. 30). Appendix A recalls (Fertis 2009) that the ℓ2- or ℓ1-regularized SVM is exactly a feature-robust hinge loss under an aggregate set (pp. 31–32).
  - My recomputation (a): the label-robust SVM needs no binaries. Substituting s_i = r_i + ξ_i turns Theorem 4's counterpart into the LP min Γq + Σ s_i subject to s_i ≥ max(1 − m_i, 0), q + s_i ≥ max(1 + m_i, 0), q ≥ 0, where m_i = y_i(w′x_i − b). On six small random instances (scipy, HiGHS) its optimal value equalled both the scenario-enumeration LP and the paper's MIO (19). So the change from LP to MIO in Table 9 belongs to the chosen formulation, not to the problem.
  - My recomputation (b): in Theorem 5 the right-hand side log((1 + e^−m)/(1 + e^m)) equals −m, so label-robust logistic regression has linear constraints (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Software: Julia and JuMP; Gurobi (2015 reference manual, version not given) for LP and MIO; IPOPT for logistic regression; RPART (4.1-9 per the references) for CART (pp. 19–20, 24, 34).
  - Synthetic study (2,000 repetitions; ℓ1 norms in both the regularized and the robust models, for a fair comparison; p. 20). Table 1 (p. 20), out-of-sample error for SVM: nominal 2.571%, regularized 2.643%, features 2.516%, labels 2.396%, both 2.363%; for logistic regression: 2.717 / 2.694 / 2.701 / 2.450 / 2.436%. At the 90th percentile the robust-in-both models err 3.325% (SVM) and 3.515% (logistic regression), against 3.941% and 4.041% for the regularized ones (Table 2, p. 20). The text's "robust methods match or outperform nominal and regularized methods across the board" (p. 20) does not hold for feature-robust logistic regression, which is above regularized logistic regression at the 90th, 70th and 50th percentiles (4.050 vs 4.041, 2.917 vs 2.910, 2.393 vs 2.385; Table 2) — my reading. Label noise is built into the design (outliers carry random labels; the test set has no outliers), and the label-robust models win; the conclusion limits this to "the simple synthetic problems considered here" (pp. 19–20, 31).
  - Real data protocol: 60/20/20 split; validation of ρ and Γ (and of cp, minbucket and the exploration depth around the warm start for trees); retraining on training plus validation data; five random splits (p. 21). Robust trees are compared with CART, and a nominal-OCT row is added later (pp. 21, 25–26).
  - Pairwise results (Table 4, p. 24), wins/losses/ties of the robust model against the nominal one: SVM 37/19/19 (features), 35/18/22 (labels), 39/20/16 (both); logistic regression 34/20/21, 35/21/19, 40/20/15; CART 36/23/16, 33/24/18, 33/24/18. So the abstract's "improves the accuracy in the majority of the data sets" (p. 2) holds for decided data sets, not for all 75.
  - Accuracy bins (Table 5, pp. 24–25): where nominal SVM's in-sample accuracy is below 60%, robust-in-both SVM wins all six data sets, by 10.7 ± 5.6%.
  - Decision rule: log10(p) ≥ 0.05a − 2.5, where "the coefficients 0.05 and 2.5 were selected manually" (p. 25), and its success is reported on the same 75 data sets (Table 6, p. 27). Above the line: SVM 14/4/3 (+5.3 ± 1.9%), logistic regression 17/2/1 (+4.0 ± 1.0%), CART 9/3/2 (+1.3 ± 0.9%), nominal OCT 7/3/4 (+1.4 ± 0.8%). Below the line: −0.2, +0.4, −0.3 and −0.7%. The abstract's headline gains are the above-the-line figures (p. 2). My tally against nominal OCT over all 75 data sets: 24 wins, 28 losses.
  - Against regularized models (Tables 7–8, pp. 27–29): feature-robust SVM matches ℓ1-regularized SVM (0.2 ± 0.4%, p. 27); above the line robust-in-both gains +0.5 ± 1.1% (SVM) and +1.9 ± 1.6% (logistic regression); below it regularized SVM is better by 0.7% (pp. 27, 29). **Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: feature robustness keeps every class, and label robustness keeps trees as MIO and logistic regression convex. But the label-robust SVM leaves its class (LP to MIO, Table 9, p. 30), at measured costs of up to about three orders of magnitude in time (Table 10, p. 30), while the abstract says robustness "does not materially change the complexity" (p. 2). My verified LP reformulation (D4) shows a class-keeping device existed, so Method 1's stop rule (change the device, not the solver) applies (Tension T4).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the budget Γ of Bertsimas–Sim is reused as a cardinality bound on flipped labels (p. 13), and §8.7 is titled "The Price of Robustness" (p. 30). The knobs are set from data by validation, with the nominal model at zero (pp. 9, 13, 17), as the method's correction asks. But no price curve over ρ or Γ is published; the "price" is read as out-of-sample accuracy and solve time (p. 30).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the incumbents are the opponents — nominal, ℓ1-regularized, RPART CART and nominal OCT — on synthetic data with a known truth (2,000 repetitions) and on 75 UCI data sets with five splits (pp. 19–29). **Transferable techniques**:
- Model label noise as a budget Γ of flips: each loss term is affine in the binary flip, the budget polytope is integral, so the LP relaxation is exact; dualize (pp. 13–17). For a hinge loss, substitute s_i = r_i + ξ_i to keep the counterpart an LP (my derivation, checked numerically).
- Put the nominal model inside the validation grid (ρ = Γ = 0) so the robust variant is chosen only when it validates better (pp. 9, 13, 17).
- Strip the nominal model's built-in robustness (its regularizer) before adding explicit robustness, then compare against both the nominal and the regularized model (pp. 3, 10, 27).
- Synthetic recovery test: plant a known separating hyperplane, contaminate the training data, and report out-of-sample error, distance to the true hyperplane and error percentiles over 2,000 repetitions (pp. 19–20).
- Timing protocol: one thread, a stated machine, a one-hour limit, and a flag on every time that is not a proven optimum (pp. 29–30).
- Report wins/losses/ties per data set beside average gains (Tables 4–6, 8; pp. 24–29). *Critic-informed, not his practice*: hold out data sets when fitting a when-to-use rule.
