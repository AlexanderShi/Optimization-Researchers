# Batch s2-05 · Dimitris Bertsimas · supplement (round 2)

**Batch intent**: Eight supplement works from 2009–2013: the late robust-optimization years, just before the 2014 "modern optimization lens". They fall into three groups:
- **Adaptivity in robust optimization (RO).** S049 (finite adaptability, with Caramanis, IEEE TAC 2010); S153 (robust and adaptive network flows under arc failures, with Nasrabadi and Stiller, OR 2013); S217 (static robust solutions approximate adjustable robust convex problems, with Goyal, MMOR 2013).
- **Mixed-integer optimization (MIO) for machine learning, before the speed-up figure existed (ML+IO).** S412 (2010 workshop paper); S561 (2011 ORC working paper typeset for JMLR); S448 (2011 ORC working paper submitted to the Annals of Statistics; the list names a NIPS 2012 poster). All three are with Allison Chang and Cynthia Rudin.
- **Partner data without an optimization model (AP, DA).** S125 (depression and health-care costs from D2 Hawkeye claims, Psychosomatics 2009); D012 (citation networks to predict academic impact, ICIS 2013 research-in-progress).

Questions asked of each work:
- Does the model keep a solver class, and where is the boundary printed (Method 1)?
- Is a property priced by one knob, with a curve and a bad case (Method 3)?
- Is a heuristic bounded or an exact model certified, and is the incumbent dueled on fair terms (Method 4)?
- How is the *why now* argued before the 2014 figure existed (Method 2)?
- Is a decision scored, or only a prediction (Method 5)?
- Which lineage carries the work (Method 6)?

### S125 · Depression and costs of health care (Psychosomatics 2009, DOI 10.1176/appi.psy.50.4.392)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–10. Read: the abstract, introduction, Method, Statistics, Results and Discussion, the disclosure and the references. Figures 1–4 keep only axis labels and captions. First-page check: the title and the authors Welch, Czerwinski, Ghimire and Bertsimas match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Charles A. Welch, M.D. (Massachusetts General Hospital; correspondence), David Czerwinski, Bijay Ghimire. The affiliations MGH, MIT ORC and D2 Hawkeye, Inc. are listed jointly, not per author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the 2001–2013 health-care-data period. The data are claims from D2 Hawkeye, a medical analytics company in which Bertsimas discloses a stockholding (pp. 2, 8). This is a clinical-journal paper, not an OR one.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP (health care); no optimization model}: The interaction between depression and chronic comorbid disease "remains incompletely understood" (abstract, p. 1). Earlier studies measured the cost difference overall or in one disease at a time (diabetes ratios 1.6–4.5; CHF 1.29). No study had compared the cost difference across the most prevalent chronic diseases of one primary-care population, and the cause of the higher cost was unknown (p. 1). The study has two objectives (p. 2):
  - the association between depression and non-mental-health cost in 11 chronic diseases;
  - utilization, comorbidity and prevalence patterns as evidence on causation.
- **D2 Core idea**: A cross-sectional case–control design on one large administrative claims dataset. Such data are argued to measure costs accurately across settings and to "sometimes" reflect real-world practice better than trials (p. 2).
  - "Depressed" requires a depression ICD-9 code AND an antidepressant fill. Patients with only one of the two are removed as diagnostically heterogeneous; the rest form the non-depressed cohort (p. 3).
  - Per disease, median annual non-mental-health cost is compared in dollars and as ratios: overall, by service type, across adapted Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) levels, and alongside cross-prevalences (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Data: de-identified claims of 618,780 patients in self-insured plans, mainly in the eastern US, Texas and California, with no Medicare or Medicaid. Only patients enrolled from 1 Sep 2004 to 31 Aug 2005 are included (p. 2).
  - Diseases: 41 ICD-9 codes grouped into 11 chronic diseases. Each needs two outpatient visits or one admission (p. 2, Table 1).
  - Study cohort: 548,062 patients, 14.3% with one of the 11 diseases (p. 3).
  - Admitted data limits: administrative coding has low sensitivity, so prevalences are useless as absolute measures (p. 5). The cohort is probably biased toward more symptomatic and more severe patients (p. 7). Only 14.1% of antidepressant users carried a coded depression diagnosis (p. 8).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a as a formulation. Analysis devices: medians, because costs are right-skewed, with a sensitivity check on means and on first and third quartiles; R 2.3.1 (p. 3). Differences are reported as "significant" (pp. 3–4), but the Statistics section names no test (p. 3).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Depressed patients had higher median non-mental-health costs in all 11 diseases; the obesity difference was not significant. Differences ran from $1,570 (obesity) to $15,240 (CHF), ratios from 1.5 to 2.9 (p. 3).
  - Pharmacy was the largest component in most diseases. Inpatient differences mattered only for CAD, epilepsy and CHF (p. 4).
  - Mean CCI was 0.22 vs 0.15, and higher cost "independent of severity" rests on Figure 3, which has no text layer (p. 4).
  - Cross-prevalence ratios were 1.4–6.8 (p. 4).
  - Inconsistencies (my reading): the depressed share is 3.0% on p. 3 but 2.3% on p. 7. The abstract speaks of "controlling for number of chronic comorbid diseases" (p. 1), while the method uses an adapted CCI (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - The new finding is that the cost difference is consistent across 11 diseases (p. 4).
  - The paper is explicit about what it cannot show: causation (p. 6); which factors are causal; and whether treating depression would reduce non-mental-health costs (p. 8).
  - Biases are argued one by one, each with its direction. The sensitivity bias cancels in ratios (pp. 5, 7). The severity bias in the depressed cohort probably inflates the difference (p. 7). Undiagnosed depression in the control cohort probably shrinks it (p. 8).
  - The excluded single-criterion groups showed cost increases not significantly different from those of the depressed cohort (p. 8).
  - Disclosure: Bertsimas is a stockholder in D2 Hawkeye (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2–3, 8): partner data, and an honest statement of what the analysis cannot show (step 6). But there is no owner's decision, no current rule as a baseline and no decision metric. A descriptive association study, so mark 4 (prescription, not prediction) does not apply.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–2, 8): a company-held claims database is the data channel. The clinical work went to a clinical journal (Workflow E step 2), and the company tie is disclosed, as Research Integrity Rule 5 asks.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define the exposed cohort by two independent data channels (diagnosis code AND prescription). Set aside the single-channel groups, then rerun the analysis on them as a check (pp. 3, 8).
- With a source of known low sensitivity, report ratios between cohorts. For each bias, say whether it cancels in the ratio and, if not, which way it pushes the result (pp. 5, 7–8).
- For right-skewed costs, use medians as the headline and rerun with means and quartiles (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "Although these prevalences are not useful as absolute measures, they are useful in comparing the prevalence of chronic comorbid conditions in depressed and non-depressed patients, because the bias applies equally to both the depressed and non-depressed cohorts." (p. 5)
- "This study does not establish causation in the association between depression and increased non-mental health costs, but several possibilities should be considered." (p. 6)

### S049 · Finite adaptability in multistage linear optimization (IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control 2010, DOI 10.1109/TAC.2010.2049764)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–16. Read: the abstract, §§I–IX, the references and the biographies. Extraction caveat: displayed formulas and most inline symbols are missing (for example, "for small the problem is tractable", p. 10), and Tables I–IV keep only their captions. Claims about formulas and table values therefore rest on the surrounding prose. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Caramanis match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Constantine Caramanis (UT Austin ECE, p. 1). Details are deferred to his 2006 MIT PhD thesis [11] and a 2006 MIT technical report [7] (pp. 2, 15–16) · **Position in trajectory**: the RO period, adaptive branch. Received May 2007, accepted April 2010 (p. 1). The partition idea returns in the 2016 adaptive-partitions paper S087 (card s10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO; IO (discrete recourse); LO; AP (air traffic) as testbed}: Robust optimization handles single-stage problems tractably, but multistage problems "present significant tractability challenges" (abstract, p. 1). Both extremes fail:
  - the static robust solution is conservative;
  - complete adaptability is "overly optimistic": exact observation is rare, computing it is intractable, and full second-stage flexibility may be too costly to implement (p. 1).

  No existing proposal varies the degree of adaptability, and none handles integer second-stage variables under set-based uncertainty (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Choose k contingency plans for the second stage; equivalently, partition the uncertainty set into k regions ((6) and (7), p. 3). Raising k gives a hierarchy from static robust (k = 1) toward complete adaptability (pp. 2–3). Because the plans are discrete, the scheme accommodates discrete variables (p. 2). It is "not comparable" to affine adaptability: each beats the other on some example (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: a two-stage LP with polytopic uncertainty in the matrices, affine in the parameter. Right-hand-side and objective uncertainty are folded into the matrix (pp. 2–3). The static robust problem is assumed feasible (p. 3).
  - Proposition 1 needs a "relatively mild" Continuity Assumption, and §VI shows that it cannot be dropped (pp. 4–5, 12).
  - Instances: random scheduling problems, with the uncertainty set the convex hull of random rate matrices or of matrices with a single product degraded (pp. 13–14). A stylized air-traffic example: 100 flights into one hub, four storm scenarios (pp. 14–15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Lemma 1: the static robust problem equals complete adaptability over the smallest "hypercube" containing the set, so it cannot model correlation across constraints or nonconvexity. The proof sandwiches it with a non-implementable "utopic" formulation and uses duality (p. 4).
  - Proposition 1: convergence as partitions get finer, by compactness and a finite subcover (p. 5).
  - Propositions 2–3: points of a polytope tied to the dual of the static problem map to necessary conditions for an ε-improvement; the conditions are also sufficient in the single-stage case. A short list of them can certify that k plans cannot help (pp. 5–7).
  - Proposition 4: optimal 2-adaptability as a finite bilinear program, proved by strong duality (pp. 7–8).
  - Proposition 5: NP-hard, by a reduction from Partition, even with right-hand-side uncertainty (pp. 8–9).
  - Proposition 6: polynomial when the smallest of three quantities is fixed: the uncertainty dimension, the problem dimension or the number of uncertain constraints (pp. 9–10).
  - Heuristics: hyperplane splits whose normals come from pairs of extreme points (or from facets), each a quasi-convex search, applied iteratively (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - One 6×6 instance: 2- and 4-adaptability close 25.8% and just over 40% of the static–adaptable gap. With binary stations, a single split reaches the fully adaptable cost of 4 (p. 13).
  - Ensembles of 50 instances per category: for 6×6 matrices, 4-adaptability closes over 70% of the gap on average. Doubling the uncertainty set drops 2-adaptability from over 63% to just over 42%, and 4-adaptability from over 70% to about 52% (p. 14).
  - The gap is upper-bounded by sampling 500 points, so the reported benefits are lower bounds (p. 14).
  - The air-traffic results sit in Tables III–IV, whose values are not in the text layer (p. 15); not verified.
  - The computations report no solver, no run times and no comparison with affine policies.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The "value of adaptability", and a hierarchy suited to discrete variables and coarse information (pp. 15–16). Admitted limits:
  - optimal partitions are hard to find, and the heuristic gives no guarantee that another partition would not do better (pp. 7, 16);
  - Proposition 6 gives only theoretical tractability (p. 10);
  - the full air-traffic model is deferred to [7] (p. 13).

  Future work: nonconvex uncertainty sets, treated "elsewhere", and nonconvex constraints (pp. 15–16).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 4–10, 13):
  - the dual is read for structure (Lemma 2, Proposition 2);
  - there is an exact finite reformulation (Proposition 4);
  - the boundary is printed (Propositions 5–6), with the caveat that large applications need "more than theoretical tractability" (p. 10);
  - the heuristics only call a static robust solver, so they inherit its class, discrete variables included (p. 13).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 11, 13–14, 16): k is a one-integer knob, and the gap closed by k = 2 and 4 is reported. The bad cases are printed: 2-adaptability gives no gain in §VI, and the benefit shrinks as the set grows (pp. 11, 14). But the cost of extra plans is named without a price, and the guarantees are convergence and necessary conditions, not a solution-independent probability bound.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 7, 11–12, 14): certificate logic without MIO. Necessary conditions certify that k regions cannot reach an ε-improvement. The intractable benchmark is estimated on the side that understates the method's benefit. But the incumbent class (affine policies) is dueled only on constructed examples (pp. 11–12).
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–2, 16): a thesis-to-journal paper of the RO lineage (Caramanis), with the full models kept in the thesis and the technical report.
- New-pattern candidate: *policy hierarchy bridging static and fully adaptive* (pp. 2, 5, 12): one integer indexes the hierarchy, convergence holds under a stated assumption, and a counterexample shows that the assumption is needed.
- New-pattern candidate: *geometry of the uncertainty set explains the value of adaptivity* (p. 4): the static–adaptive gap lies exactly in the hypercube hull of the set (compare S217, pp. 9–11).
- New-pattern candidate: *conservative-side benchmark bound for an intractable optimum* (p. 14): complete adaptability is estimated from 500 sampled points, so the reported share of the gap closed is a lower bound on the true benefit (compare S153, p. 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Bracket the adaptive problem between the static robust problem and a non-implementable "utopic" relaxation in which every variable sees the uncertainty. Then use duality to locate the gap (p. 4).
- Map extreme points of the dual's optimal face to necessary conditions. A few of them can certify that k plans cannot improve by ε (pp. 5–7, 11).
- When the ideal benchmark is intractable, estimate it from the side that makes your method look worse, and say so with the results (p. 14).
- To claim complementarity with an incumbent policy class, construct one example where each class beats the other (pp. 3, 11–12).

**Quotes**:
- "in large scale applications we require more than theoretical tractability" (p. 10)
- "Indeed, all that is required for the algorithms is a method to solve the static robust problem." (p. 13)

### S412 · A discrete optimization approach to supervised ranking (Proceedings of the 5th INFORMS Workshop on Data Mining and Health Informatics 2010, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–6. Read: the abstract, §§1–4, Tables 1–2 and the references. Subscripts in the formulas are partly lost. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Chang and Rudin match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Allison Chang (MIT ORC; contact author), Cynthia Rudin (MIT ORC per p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the first of three Chang–Rudin MIO-for-ML texts (S412 → S561 → S448). S561 (p. 4) calls it "a shorter nonarchival version", and its Tables 1–2 equal S561's Tables 6–7, so count its method links together with S561's. Its *why now* cites CRIO (S073, card c11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO as engine}: Supervised ranking optimizes a rank statistic (AUC, DCG, P-Norm Push), but every other method replaces it by a convex surrogate (abstract, p. 1). Small ranking gains matter in applications: manhole events, drug screening, Netflix (p. 1). Entry point: optimize the true objective exactly, and use the exact optimum as a "benchmark" (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Linear scores f(x) = wᵀx.
  - P_AUC(ε): a binary z_ik for each positive–negative pair, set to 1 only if the positive scores higher by a margin ε (p. 4).
  - A "rank risk functional" (RRF) unifies WRS, partial WMW, WTA, MRR, DCG and the P-Norm Push (p. 3). P_RRF(ε) adds rank-threshold binaries (pp. 4–5).
  - Ties count as misranks (strict inequalities), which rules out the trivial w = 0 (p. 4).
  - The LP relaxation serves as a fast approximate method (p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**: Binary labels; w_j ∈ [−1, 1]. ε trades speed against generalization: smaller values run faster but may generalize worse (p. 4). Six data sets with n_train ≤ 1,000 and d ≤ 10: five from UCI and LIBSVM, plus the artificial "ROC Flexibility" (pp. 5–6).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Two MIPs and their LP relaxations (pp. 4–5); no theorem.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: ten random train/test splits. Competitors: RankBoost, logistic regression, an SVM-style ranker and the LP relaxation (p. 5).
  - The text reports the MIP as "the statistically highest" in every row, i.e. not significantly below the row maximum by a matched-pairs t-test at 0.05 (p. 5).
  - By raw means, the MIP is below the LP on the FourClass and SVMGuide1 test sets. The large gain is on the artificial set: test AUC 81.8 vs 65.9–67.8 (Table 1, p. 5).
  - The time limit was up to ten hours, and not all MIPs reached provable optimality. No solver is named; Gurobi tests are announced as future work, and the RRF formulation has no results (p. 6).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A new MIP technique for ranking, with "preliminary evidence" on AUC (p. 6). The limits are visible in the paper: uncertified MIPs and an untested RRF (p. 6). The "benchmark" claim (p. 2) needs optimality that was not always reached (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–5): a statistic owned by information retrieval and statistics is written exactly as a MIP, and one formulation covers a whole class of statistics (RRF).
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (p. 2): the *why now* in its pre-figure form. It cites the belief, dating from the early 1970s, that MIP is intractable (via Bertsimas–Shioda) and "major advances within the last decade", with no number.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 5–6): the surrogate step is replaced by an exact model, and the incumbents are dueled out of sample. But there is no warm start and no reported gap, and the "benchmark" is uncertified: Tension T1 in 2010.
- New-pattern candidate: *promote the evaluation metric to the training objective* (p. 2): the objective is also the measure used to evaluate ranking quality (same candidate as S470 in s01).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Count ties against yourself (a tie is a misrank), so that degenerate solutions such as w = 0 cannot score well (p. 4).
- Report the LP relaxation of your MIP as a separate competitor; it shows how much integrality buys (pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "This means that the cost function that we optimize with MIP is also the measure that we use to evaluate ranking quality, while other methods use heuristic loss functions that differ from the evaluation measure." (p. 2)
- "Not all of the MIPs solved to provable optimality within the specified time limit – up to ten hours for the larger datasets – so given more time, it is possible that the MIP solutions could be even better." (p. 6)

### S561 · Integer optimization methods for supervised ranking (MIT ORC Working Paper OR 388-11 2011, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–35. Read: the cover, abstract, §§1–7 and Tables 1–12; Figures 1–9 by caption; references skimmed. First-page check: the title and Bertsimas, Chang and Rudin match (pp. 1–2). The list's "D Bersimas" is a misspelling in the list, not in the file (p. 2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Allison Chang (MIT ORC), Cynthia Rudin (MIT Sloan) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the archival form of S412, typeset for JMLR ("Submitted 2/11", p. 2); publication not verified. A condensed version was a finalist in the INFORMS 2011 Data Mining Best Student Paper Competition (p. 2). Supported by Draper Laboratory, the MIT-Ford Alliance and NSF (p. 30).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO as engine}: Ranking methods approximate rank statistics by convex surrogates "in order to accommodate extremely large problems, at the expense of exact solutions" (abstract, p. 2). The entry point: "not all ranking problems are large". Re-ranking, ranking manholes or chemicals, and symptom prediction are domains where "a better solution is worth the extra computation time" (pp. 3–4). The authors know of no earlier use of integer optimization for ranking (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two stated contributions (p. 4):
  1. One formulation unifies a class of rank statistics (the RRF), and new statistics can be derived from it, such as a "staircase" statistic for tiered manhole inspection (pp. 6–7).
  2. A "guarantee of optimality", or at least a solver bound where heuristics give none (p. 4).

  Ties are broken pessimistically (p. 7). The formulations:
  - P_AUC (p. 13);
  - P_RRF,rank and the leaner P_RRF,minrank (pp. 14–15);
  - big-M and SOS variants, judged weaker or too memory-hungry (pp. 15–17);
  - a pairwise-preference generalization (p. 17).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Linear scores with w ∈ [−1, 1]^d, to bound the search. Nonlinearity enters through added features (pp. 5, 13).
  - Exactness needs the smallest positive score gap to be at least ε. This is checked after solving, by recomputing the AUC independently (p. 13).
  - Data: ROC Flexibility (500 examples, only 20 of them unique, p. 27), five UCI and LIBSVM sets for AUC, and Haberman and Pima for DCG@N (pp. 25–26).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Formulation strength (a linear relaxation close to the convex hull) is argued with reference to the IO book (p. 10). Constraints (22)–(25) are added as valid inequalities (p. 15).
  - Section 4 (pp. 18–22): a pathological case with duplicated examples shows that minimum ranks can mislead (p. 18). Lemma 4 and Theorem 5 relate the two objectives (p. 19). Lemma 6 breaks ties by a random perturbation that preserves orderings with probability one (pp. 20–21). Theorem 7: with distinct examples, a maximizer of the leaner minrank model also maximizes the rank model (p. 21).
  - *Why now* (pp. 10–12): three borrowed tables, shown separately and not multiplied, of which the two solver tables are LP benchmarks: supercomputer speeds; PILOTS LP times across machines (a factor above 6,000); PDS-30 times across CPLEX versions (a factor of 350 in 11 years).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: RankBoost, logistic regression, an SVM-style ranker and the P-Norm Push (p = 2, 4, 8) in MATLAB 7.8.0, with no tuning and no regularization for any method. The MIO runs on Gurobi 3.0.0 (CPLEX 11.2.1 for ROC Flexibility) on two Xeon E5440 processors with 32 GB; ten random splits (pp. 23–24).
  - On ROC Flexibility, the MIO's AUC is 11.8% (train) and 20.6% (test) above the best competitor, in relative terms (p. 24). On the five real sets, the MIO's test AUC is within 0.1 points of the best competitor (training gains up to 1.1 points, on Liver), and the LP edges the MIO on two test sets (Table 6, p. 26).
  - DCG@N: the MIO wins on ROC Flexibility and Haberman, but has the lowest Pima test score and is best in 0 of 10 trials (pp. 27–28).
  - Cutoffs were 1–10 h, and "Most of the MIOs did not solve to optimality". Only the time to the final solution is reported, with no gaps (p. 29).
  - Theorem 7 is checked on data: the rank and minrank objectives coincide on Pima, whose examples are all unique (p. 27).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The claims are unification and an optimality guarantee (p. 4). The conclusion is narrower: optimal MIO solutions have higher training objectives, and the methods "compete well" out of sample (p. 30). Printed limits:
  - P_RRF,rank is too hard except for small or sparse problems (p. 22);
  - run times vary hugely (p. 29);
  - the Pima results did not generalize to the test data (p. 27).

  Future work: data conditions under which the LP relaxation is tight, and ε and bounds on w as regularization natural to MIO (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 4–22): the objective owned by information retrieval and statistics is written exactly as MIO and unified, and a new statistic is derived from the class. A theorem shows when the cheaper formulation is exact (Theorem 7), and the boundary is printed (p. 22).
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 10–12): the 2011 *why now* concedes the 1970s belief and shows hardware and software progress as separate, borrowed tables. The solver tables are LP benchmarks (PILOTS, PDS-30), not MIO, and are neither rebuilt nor multiplied. The per-paper rebuilt figure of 2014–2018 does not exist yet. Using LP timings to argue for MIO anticipates the [05 §1.6] correction.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 4, 23–29):
  - Present: one exact joint model duels standard incumbents out of sample. The paper says where it wins (on data with "this level of flexibility", p. 24) and prints a loss (Pima).
  - Missing: the promised "guarantee of optimality" (p. 4) meets "Most of the MIOs did not solve to optimality" (p. 29). No gaps are reported, despite the solver-bound argument of p. 4, and there are no warm starts. This is Tension T1, six years before OCT.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3): a student-carried line (workshop paper, then working paper, then student-paper competition). It continues the CRIO programme (Bertsimas–Shioda 2007, p. 3) and comes before the 2014 lens.
- New-pattern candidate: *promote the evaluation metric to the training objective* (pp. 3, 7–9).
- New-pattern candidate: *cheap formulation certified equivalent under a genericity condition* (pp. 18–22, 27): prove that the lean model is exact when the examples are distinct, then confirm it on data.

**Transferable techniques**:
- After solving a margin-based MIO, recompute the true metric from the returned weights to verify that the formulation was exact for that ε (p. 13).
- When a lean formulation can fail on degenerate data, prove a genericity condition under which it is exact, and show both a counterexample (p. 18) and an empirical check (p. 27).
- Plot solution paths (objective against time, per trial) to show where an MIO stalls and how much run times vary (pp. 29–30).

**Quotes**:
- "Even if an MIO problem is too large to solve to provable optimality, solvers provide a bound on the optimal objective value, which may be a useful measure of closeness to optimality." (p. 4)
- "Most of the MIOs did not solve to optimality before the cutoff time indicated in Table 12, in which case the time recorded was the time until the last (possibly suboptimal) solution was found." (p. 29)

Read level: **full** (version WP): pp. 1–31. Read: the cover, abstract, §§1–8, Appendix A and Tables 1–17; Figures 1–6 by caption; the references. Version: the ORC working paper of October 2011, "Submitted to the Annals of Statistics" (pp. 1–2), not the NIPS 2012 poster named in the list. Pages below are pages of this version ("S448 WP"). First-page check: the title and Bertsimas, Chang and Rudin match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Allison Chang, Cynthia Rudin (MIT ORC address, p. 31). Supported by NSF IIS-1053407 (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the third Chang–Rudin MIO-for-ML text: interpretable rule lists, five years before Optimal Classification Trees.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; IO as engine}: The starting point is a dichotomy (p. 2):
  - accurate black boxes such as SVMs;
  - interpretable models such as trees, which are not "specifically optimized to achieve the highest accuracy".

  Associative classification orders its rules by an arbitrarily chosen interestingness measure (p. 5). Rule mining meets "combinatorial explosion": L rules can be ordered in L! ways (p. 3). The authors know of no other mathematical programming approach to decision lists (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two MIOs.
  - RuleGen (formulation (13), pp. 10–11; Figure 1, p. 12) traces the support–coverage frontier of rules. It maximizes support under a coverage bound and enumerates ties with no-good cuts. This frontier contains the optimizer of every interestingness measure that is monotone in coverage, prevalence and support (pp. 7–9; Table 4, p. 9). It extends Bayardo and Agrawal's border from fixed heads to general rules (p. 4).
  - Formulation (15) then orders the rules to maximize regularized training accuracy. A reward on the rank of the higher default rule shortens the list (pp. 13–16).

  Interpretability is made operational as "convincing" (reasons given for each prediction) and "concise" (short rules and short lists, citing Miller 1956) (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Binary items: continuous features are binned, and rows with missing values are dropped (pp. 26–27).
  - Data: eleven sets with n = 267–2,201 and d = 8–41, plus Wine with n = 4,898 and d = 44 (Table 7, p. 18; p. 24).
  - One parameter setting for every data set: R_gen_x = 0.1/n, R_gen_b = 0.1/(nd), R_rank = 1/L, minimum coverage 0.05, at most five iterations per bound. The values were chosen in "preliminary experiments" to yield a reasonable number of rules (p. 17).
  - Haberman's year-of-operation feature was removed as not predictive before modelling (p. 26).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The rule polytope uses 2d binaries; the 3n auxiliary variables are continuous but provably integral (pp. 6–7). Valid inequalities (19)–(20) improve the linear relaxation (p. 15), (31) is added to speed up computation (p. 16), and an assignment matrix gives one-to-one ranks (p. 15). No generalization theorem.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: three-fold cross-validation against LR, SVM (RBF kernel, C = 1), CART, C4.5, RF and AdaBoost in R 2.15.0. ORC runs in AMPL with Gurobi 3.0.0 or 4.5.2, on two machines (p. 16).
  - Accuracy is "on par" (p. 17): ORC beats CART on 9 of 11 data sets and C4.5 on 5 of 11 (Table 8, p. 19).
  - Model size: ORC lists average 11.8 rules, against 8.7 leaves for CART and 14.5 for C4.5 (Table 9, p. 19).
  - Certification: ranking time limits ran from 10 minutes to 7 hours. The solver was "generally terminated" before proving optimality, and gaps are reported only for TicTacToe (pp. 17, 27; Table 16, p. 29).
  - Wine: RuleGen was too slow and Apriori replaced it. The ranking MIO ran for about 20 hours, and RF stayed best (pp. 24–25).
  - Against an SVM tuned "in hindsight" on test accuracy, the untuned ORC is still "on par" (p. 27; Table 17, p. 30).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Claims: accuracy at the level of the top algorithms, plus interpretability advantages (pp. 3, 25–26); and "among the first to use MIO methods for machine learning" (p. 26), although the companion S561 cites earlier MIO-for-ML work (S561 p. 3).
  - Printed limits: MIO is "computationally intensive" (p. 22); ORC lists are larger than CART's (p. 19); Wine needed Apriori (p. 24).
  - The Crime2 example list contains race and sex rules, including "Black ⇒ 1" for a violent offense. It is presented as an interpretability example, with no fairness discussion (Table 12, p. 21; my reading).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 5, 16–19, 27, 29):
  - Present: one accuracy-maximizing MIO replaces ordering by a heuristic measure, and six incumbents are dueled with honest "on par" wording. The hindsight-tuned SVM handicaps ORC, not the baseline.
  - Missing: there is no warm start, and certification is dropped ("generally terminated"; gaps only for TicTacToe). Tension T1.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13–17, 19): conciseness has a knob (R_rank), but it is fixed at 1/L so as "to not sacrifice classification accuracy" (p. 17). Size and accuracy are reported in separate tables, with no curve between them.
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 6–9, 24): data-mining heuristics (Apriori plus a ranking by interestingness) are recast as MIOs over a polytope with provably integral auxiliary variables. The boundary is printed when Apriori must replace RuleGen on Wine.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (p. 24): the *why now* is qualitative ("the speed of computers continues to advance exponentially", cloud computing), with no figure.
- New-pattern candidate: *frontier instead of a criterion* (pp. 7–9): mine the non-dominated frontier that contains every monotone criterion's optimum, so that no interestingness measure has to be chosen.
- New-pattern candidate: *give the baseline the advantage* (p. 27; Table 17, p. 30): tune the incumbent in hindsight on test data, and run your own method untuned (compare S153, pp. 23–24).
- New-pattern candidate: *promote the evaluation metric to the training objective* (pp. 13–14): the ranking MIO maximizes training accuracy itself rather than an interestingness proxy.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Show that auxiliary indicator variables are forced integral by the constraints, and declare them continuous; the binary count stays at 2d (p. 7).
- Use no-good cuts (the paper's "infeasibility constraint", (12) and (14)) to enumerate every optimum at a frontier point before moving the bound (pp. 10–11).
- Add a hindsight-tuned version of the strongest baseline in an appendix. If the untuned method still competes, the comparison is robust to tuning (p. 27).

**Quotes**:
- "trying to explain a support vector kernel to a medical doctor is not likely to persuade him to use an SVM-based diagnostic system" (p. 2)
- "We generally terminated the solver before (15) solved to provable optimality." (p. 17)

### S153 · Robust and adaptive network flows (Operations Research 2013, DOI 10.1287/opre.2013.1200)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–26. Read: the publisher cover, abstract, §§1–7, Tables 1–2 and Figures 1–12 by caption; references skimmed. Extraction caveat: the font layer maps parentheses, commas and periods to digits in formulas and in some table cells (for example "G = 4V 1 E5" for G = (V, E)), and Γ appears as "â". Table 2's values are unreadable. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Nasrabadi and Stiller match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ebrahim Nasrabadi (MIT Sloan and ORC), Sebastian Stiller (TU Berlin) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the RO period. The budget idea of S004 (card c07) moves here from uncertain coefficients to structural failures. The interdiction line continues in S191 (2016, with Nasrabadi and Orlin; card s10). Received May 2012, accepted March 2013 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO; LO (network flows); IO (cuts, interdiction); AP (pipelines, logistics) as motivation}:
  - The gap: prior work treated uncertain parameters or demand. Here the network structure itself is uncertain: arcs and nodes fail (pp. 2–3).
  - The motivation: crude-oil pipelines, "managed manually", that must still deliver after the worst attack (p. 3).
  - A modelling choice: normal operation must be a full flow. This avoids fixing an arbitrary ratio between nominal cost and disruption cost when disruption probabilities cannot be quantified (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Four models.
  1. Robust maximum flow: weak flow conservation must hold in every failure scenario, with a per-node budget Γ_v on failing in-arcs. The inner problem (the sum of the Γ_v largest inflows into a node) is dualized, which turns exponentially many constraints into an LP with 2m + n − 1 variables and constraints (Theorem 1, pp. 5–6).
  2. Adaptive maximum flow: after at most Γ failures in total, the flow is rerouted within the initial arc flows (pp. 8–9).
  3. A path-based robust flow (pp. 16–18).
  4. Adaptive flows over time (pp. 20–21).

  Here Γ limits how many rows fail, not how many entries deviate within a row, so the 2003–2004 budget results do not transfer (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: a single commodity from s to t; deterministic capacities; binary arc failures. Local budgets in §2, a global Γ afterwards (pp. 5, 8). Node failures reduce to arc failures by node splitting (p. 5). The super-source arcs are infallible (pp. 8, 23).
  - Instances: 10 NETGEN benchmark instances of two sizes (Table 1, p. 23; the digits of the larger size may be garbled by the font layer), and random tree-like networks, one of them shown (Table 2, p. 24).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 1, by LP duality (p. 6).
  - The robust minimum cut is strongly NP-hard: it is equivalent to nodewise network interdiction, which is hard by a reduction from matching interdiction (Lemmas 1–3, Theorem 2, pp. 7–8).
  - Computing the adaptive value of a flow is a network-interdiction problem: NP-hard, with approximation hardness carried over (Lemma 5, Corollary 1, pp. 12–13). It is polynomial for constant Γ (p. 11) and on s-t-planar graphs (p. 13). The problem is placed in Σ2p, which explains how an NP-hard problem can have a strong dual (p. 13).
  - The adaptive value as a concave QP whose optimum lies at binary extreme points, then linearized through KKT conditions with binaries or SOS constraints for MIO solvers (Lemma 6, pp. 13–14).
  - An adaptive minimum cut, and a flow-versus-cut zero-sum game whose equilibrium follows from Wald's minimax theorem (Theorems 3–4, pp. 15–16).
  - LP (39) approximates the path-based robust and the adaptive problems. It is at least a 0.58197-approximation when the robust value is at least half the nominal (Theorem 5, Corollaries 2–3, pp. 18–20; budgeted-maximum-coverage bound).
  - Flows over time: temporally repeated flows can fail (Figure 7). The problem is weakly NP-hard even with one failing arc on series-parallel graphs, by a reduction from Partition (Theorem 7, pp. 21–22).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: GLPK, a free solver, on a 2.13 GHz Core 2 Duo with 2 GB RAM and a one-hour limit (p. 23).
  - Quality: the LP (39) flows are scored by their gap to the network-interdiction value, an upper bound, and reach 94.8% of it on average (Table 1, p. 23).
  - Against the nominal maximum flow: its adaptive value averages 74.0% of the heuristic's. The text says "up to 40% worse", but Table 1 lists 36.6% for instance 4 (p. 23; my reading).
  - Against the robust flow: robust flows vanish at Γ = 1–4, adaptive flows only at Γ = 17–69. The instances favour the robust model (pp. 23–24).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The notions of robust and adaptive network flows, with insight into their "structure, complexity, approximability, and computability" (p. 4). Printed limits:
  - NP-hardness for every model except the robust maximum flow;
  - no known approximation bounds for interdiction, any of which "would immediately carry over" (p. 13);
  - robust flows are too conservative (p. 23).

  Future work: the game in which the adversary moves first, which links to interdiction (p. 25). The pipeline application is not computed on real data (pp. 3, 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 5–14): the class-keeping device (a per-node budget plus a dualized inner problem) keeps the robust maximum flow an LP. The boundary is printed precisely: the minimum cut, adaptive and over-time versions are NP-hard, with polynomial special cases. Page 9 records where the 2003–2004 device stops transferring.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 9, 23–24): Γ is the knob, and values are plotted against Γ with upper bounds (Figures 9–12). The bad case of the nonadjustable model is printed: it vanishes at Γ ≤ 4. But there is no probabilistic bound, and Γ is not set from data.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 13–14, 23): a heuristic with a measured gap to a relaxation bound, dueled against two simpler competitors on terms that favour one of them. There is no exact MIO solve, although one is formulated (p. 14), and the solver is free (GLPK), not commercial.
- New-pattern candidate: *conservative-side benchmark bound for an intractable optimum* (p. 23): use a classical problem's optimal value (network interdiction) as an upper bound, and report the heuristic's ratio to it (compare S049, p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *give the baseline the advantage* (pp. 23–24): instances designed in favour of the competitor (compare S448 WP, p. 27).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When failures make a flow infeasible, relax strict flow conservation to weak conservation and dualize the inner worst-case problem over the Γ failing arcs; the robust counterpart then stays an LP (pp. 5–6).
- Map the evaluation problem of an adaptive model onto a known hard problem (here network interdiction), and inherit its hardness and any future approximation result (pp. 12–13).
- Use small hand-built networks to show non-integrality and the conflict between nominal and adaptive optima before stating the theory (Figures 2–4, pp. 9–11).

**Quotes**:
- "whereas here the restriction is to the number of rows that are subject to change" (p. 9)
- "We assess the quality of our heuristically found flows by their gap to the interdiction value." (p. 23)

### S217 · On the approximability of adjustable robust convex optimization under uncertainty (Mathematical Methods of Operations Research 2013, DOI 10.1007/s00186-012-0405-6)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–21. Read: the abstract, §§1–6 and the references. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Goyal match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Vineet Goyal (Columbia IEOR; corresponding author; NSF CMMI-1201116) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the third paper of a joint line on static and finitely adaptable policies, after S134 (2010, card s07) and S165 (2011, card s08). It extends them from linear covering problems with right-hand-side uncertainty to convex problems with uncertain constraints and objectives (pp. 1, 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO; CP (convex, SOCP and SDP instances); AP (revenue management) as motivation}:
  - Stochastic optimization is intractable, and its distributions are hard to estimate (p. 2).
  - Adjustable robust policies are intractable too: two-stage set covering is hard to approximate within Ω(log n) (p. 3).
  - The line's earlier bounds covered only covering constraints with right-hand-side uncertainty. They miss "packing constraints and uncertainty in constraint coefficients", as in revenue management (p. 3; the multi-period example (1.1), pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: A single static robust solution is within a factor of the fully adjustable optimum, and the factor is set by the geometry of the uncertainty set: 1 + ρ/s, where s is Minkowski symmetry and ρ the translation factor. The factor is 2 for symmetric sets such as hypercubes, ellipsoids and norm balls (pp. 9–11). The proof scales the adjustable optimum's first stage, and its recourse at the symmetry point, by α = 1/(1 + ρ/s). The result is feasible for every u, and concavity loses at most the factor α (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Model and data**: A two-stage maximization (1.2) under four assumptions (p. 4):
  - (A1)–(A2): concave objectives with f(0) = 0;
  - (A3): constraint functions convex in the decisions and concave and increasing in u ∈ U ⊂ R^p_+, with g(0, 0, u) = 0 and a polynomially solvable maximum over u;
  - (A4): convex X and Y containing 0.

  Concavity in u is adopted "for the tractability of the static-robust problem" (p. 5). The framework cannot model covering constraints (p. 6). Applications fitted to the framework: two-stage LP under constraint uncertainty, revenue management, production planning, network design, portfolios with an uncertain covariance, SDP (pp. 5–9). With objective uncertainty, (A5) adds f2(y, βu) ≥ f2(y, u)/β (p. 15). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Lemmas 2–3: homogeneity inequalities from concavity and convexity with value zero at the origin (pp. 11–12). Lemma 1, from S165: every u ≤ (1 + ρ/s)u0 (p. 11).
  - Theorem 1: the 1 + ρ/s bound (p. 11).
  - Theorems 2 and 4: the static problem is polynomial, by the equivalence of separation and optimization (pp. 13, 16).
  - Theorem 3: the bound degrades to (1 + ρ/s)² under objective uncertainty (p. 16).
  - Theorem 5: the same bound for multistage problems whose uncertainty network is a path, with the proof sketched "in the interest of space" (pp. 18–20).
  - §3.1: a one-line linear example in which the static solution is arbitrarily worse in the stochastic setting (p. 14).
  - No tightness example is given for the robust bounds (my reading).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none (theory only).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Claims "the first approximation bounds for adjustable robust convex optimization problems in such generality" (pp. 1, 11). Printed limits:
  - no stochastic analogue (p. 14);
  - covering constraints are excluded (p. 6);
  - multistage results only for path networks, with the general case asserted to extend "in a straightforward manner" (p. 18).

  The conclusion turns the bound into modelling guidance (choose a set on which static performs well) and into a justification of static robust optimization in practice (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 5–6, 13–16, 18): the static counterpart stays polynomially solvable, and the assumption that buys this (concavity in u) is named. The boundary is printed: covering constraints, the stochastic gap, multistage problems beyond paths.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 9–11, 16, 20): a solution-independent bound on the price of not adapting, indexed by set geometry and handed to the modeller for choosing the set (step 6). But no curve is computed and no bad case is shown for the robust bound; the stochastic gap example plays that role only for the stochastic objective.
- New-pattern candidate: *build the tractable policy from the fully adaptive optimum* (pp. 12–13): a scaled copy of the adjustable solution at the symmetry point is the feasible static solution (as in S165, card s08).
- New-pattern candidate: *geometry of the uncertainty set explains the value of adaptivity* (pp. 9–11, 20): symmetry and the translation factor set the bound (compare S049, p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate: *power-and-limits theorem for a tractable policy class* (pp. 11, 14): a power bound for the robust case and an unbounded gap for the stochastic case (as in S165).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To bound a static policy against the fully adaptive optimum, scale the adaptive solution toward the origin by a factor fixed by the geometry of the uncertainty set, and use f(αx) ≥ αf(x) for concave f with f(0) = 0 (pp. 11–13).
- Prove that the robust counterpart is tractable through a separation oracle (maximizing a concave function over a convex set) rather than through an explicit reformulation (pp. 13, 16).
- Open with a gallery of applications that satisfy the assumptions (LP, revenue management, network design, QCQP, SDP), to make the generality concrete (pp. 5–9).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, the static solution can be arbitrarily worse as compared to a fully-adjustable solution for the stochastic optimization problem under constraint coefficient uncertainty." (p. 14)
- "Since our results relate the performance bounds to the geometric properties of the uncertainty set, they provide useful guidance towards choosing a specific uncertainty set for a particular application such that the performance of static-robust is good." (p. 20)
