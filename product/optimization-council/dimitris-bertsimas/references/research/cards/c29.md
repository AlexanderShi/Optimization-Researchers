# Batch c29 · Dimitris Bertsimas · core (round 1)

**Batch intent**: See how Bertsimas's group, 2021–2025, writes trained machine-learning models into mixed-integer programs, and what that does to the skill's methods. S097 (DA+IO; with Maragno, Wiberg, Birbil, den Hertog and Fajemisin) learns *unknown* constraints and objectives from decision–outcome data and embeds them to prescribe decisions (food aid, chemotherapy). S266 (IO+HS+DA; with Margaritis) learns the feasible regions of *known* but awkward or black-box nonlinear constraints to approximate global optimization problems, and duels BARON on MINLPLib. Questions asked of each: what forces the problem; how the learned pieces keep the solver class; how learning error is guarded (trust region, ensembles, robust counterparts, relaxations); what the baseline is and how certificates and gaps are reported; whether the headline matches the tables. Read against Methods 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6, Heuristics 4, 5 and 6, Tensions T1, T4 and T6, and the Roundtable Card's predicted disagreements with the MINLP team (Leyffer, Sahinidis, Grossmann, Misener).

### S097 · Mixed-integer optimization with constraint learning (Operations Research 2025, DOI 10.1287/opre.2021.0707; arXiv 2111.04469)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v3 (arXiv:2111.04469v3): main text pp. 1–35 and appendices A–E (pp. 42–62) read in full; references (pp. 36–41) skimmed. Figures survive only as extracted labels (Figure EC.1 on p. 44 and Figure EC.3 on p. 52 as axis text), so claims about them rest on the prose. The OR print and arXiv v1–v2 were not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Donato Maragno and Holly Wiberg (equal contribution; Maragno at the Amsterdam Business School, Wiberg at the MIT Operations Research Center), Ş. İlker Birbil, Dick den Hertog and Adejuyigbe O. Fajemisin (Amsterdam Business School) (p. 1); Bertsimas is third author · **Position in trajectory**: the DA programme's step from predictions inside a decision (Bertsimas–Kallus 2020, S008, cited p. 5) to trained models inside the MIO itself. It extends the 2016 chemotherapy paper (S078), reuses its trial database (pp. 27–28, 56) and embeds Optimal Trees (p. 10). Funding: the Dutch NWO grant OPTIMAL and an NSF fellowship for Wiberg (pp. 35–36). works.json lists Wiberg's 2022 MIT thesis "Data-driven healthcare via constraint learning and analytics" (S536; metadata only). S266 builds on this paper as its reference [16] (S266 pp. 4, 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + IO; ML and RO as devices; AP (humanitarian food aid, oncology) as testbeds}: The entry is a modelling gap: MIO needs explicit functions, but for many decisions the outcome cannot be written as a function of the decision, although data relate the two (p. 2). Two examples open the paper. In chemotherapy with a toxicity limit, only observational data link treatment to toxicity; a diet must be "palatable", which cannot be written as a function of the food choices (p. 2). The question is how to use data to learn these functions (p. 2). The literature gap has three parts. Earlier applications learn one function with one ML method fixed in advance (p. 4). JANOS and EML are restricted to limited problem structures and model classes (p. 4). Prescriptive analytics (Bertsimas–Kallus 2020; Elmachtoub–Grigas 2021) uses predictions as inputs instead of embedding the model (p. 5). The problem is stated as model M(w): min f(x, w, y) s.t. g(x, w, y) ≤ 0, y = ĥ_D(x, w), x ∈ X(w). Here x are the decisions, w the fixed context, y the learned outcomes and X(w) the trust region (eq. (1), pp. 6–7). "Constraint learning" covers learned objectives too (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Many ML models are MIO-representable with linear constraints, so a trained model can be written into the MIO, and the decision is optimized over the model instead of over a prediction (pp. 5, 9). Two guards against learning error come with it. A trust region, the convex hull of the observed data, keeps the optimizer away from points where the model extrapolates (pp. 13–15). A "model wrapper" requires at least a 1 − α share of P bootstrapped or multi-class estimators to satisfy each learned constraint (p. 17). Compared with the nearest work, the learner class is chosen per outcome by cross-validation (pp. 5, 31). The 2016 chemotherapy model's single linear regression and single toxicity constraint become several model classes and several toxicity constraints (p. 28). Two things stay: an off-the-shelf MIO solver (p. 3) and, for chemotherapy, the 2016 encoding of a regimen by drug indicator, average dose and maximum instantaneous dose (pp. 29, 57). The reason for the robustness layers: a misspecified learned constraint can make the solution infeasible, while a misspecified objective only makes it suboptimal (pp. 5–6, 34).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: decisions x ∈ Rⁿ; context w ∈ Rᵖ, fixed in the optimization. f and g are known explicitly but may depend on y (p. 7), and the discussion "mainly revolves around linear functions" (p. 7).
  - Learners: linear and logistic regression, linear SVMs only, CART and Optimal Trees, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, ReLU MLPs (pp. 4, 9–13, 42). Data may be continuous, two-class or one-class (pp. 4–5).
  - A bounded outcome is either learned as a regression and bounded in the MIO (function learning), or binarized at τ and learned as a classifier (indicator function learning) (p. 9). The hull formulations assume continuous x̂ (p. 14).
  - WFP: a simplified Peters et al. (2021) supply-chain-and-diet LP for Syria with one learned constraint (pp. 18–21). Palatability data come from a simulator built on WFP experts' suggestions: 121,589 samples of 25 commodity amounts, with threshold t = 0.5 (pp. 19, 22; the score is eq. (19), p. 53).
  - Chemotherapy: 495 trial arms from 1979–2012, reduced to 461 by exclusions, then 320 training arms (through 2008) and 96 test arms (2009–2012). There are 9 context variables; 28 drugs give 84 decision variables; regimens have at most three drugs (pp. 30–31, 57–58).
  - Assumptions admitted: no unobserved confounding (p. 29); toxicity groups independent when aggregated to "any DLT" (p. 58); missing values multiply imputed (pp. 57–58). For chemotherapy the hull is taken over x only, because the study year puts every test cohort outside the hull of (x, w) (p. 31).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Trees: each leaf is a polyhedron (p. 11). If the tree is the only learned constraint, the problem splits into one subproblem per feasible leaf, solved in parallel; these are LOs when x is continuous (pp. 11–12). Otherwise the tree gets one binary per leaf with big-M split constraints (eqs. (8a)–(8c), p. 43). The paper calls this "similar to" Verwer et al. (2017) and gives each formulation its merits (p. 12). Each split's M is computed by maximizing the split expression over the known constraints and the trust region (eq. (9), p. 44). In the WFP test, the single MIO takes about the same time at any number of leaves, while sequential LOs grow with the leaves; one LO takes 1.8 s (pp. 44–45).
  - Ensembles: random forests average their trees; boosting takes a weighted sum (pp. 12–13).
  - MLPs: a ReLU big-M (eqs. (10a)–(10e)), which careful bounds or Anderson et al.'s cuts could tighten. Both are mentioned as possible improvements; the text does not say they were used (p. 45). A sigmoid output becomes a linear threshold on the logit, ln(τ/(1 − τ)). A softmax becomes an argmax big-M (eqs. (11a)–(11b), p. 46).
  - Trust region: the hull is written with convex-combination weights λ (eq. (2)), so its facets, exponential to compute, are never needed (p. 14). For low-density regions: cluster the data, then use a union of cluster hulls with one binary per cluster (eq. (3)), or solve one MIO per cluster in parallel (p. 14). The ϵ-CH enlargement adds a slack s with ||s||_p ≤ ϵ (eq. (5)), with p limited to 1, 2 or ∞ to keep the problem class (p. 18).
  - Violation limit: (1/P) Σ I(y_i ≤ τ) ≥ 1 − α, written with big-M indicators (eq. (4), p. 17). α = 0 and the "mean" constraint are special cases that need no big-M (p. 47). The embedded estimators, not the wrapper, drive the complexity (p. 47).
  - Column selection for huge data sets (App. B.2, pp. 48–51). A restricted master problem runs over a subset of points. The reduced cost of each unused point comes from the KKT stationarity condition of the Wolfe dual (eqs. (13)–(15), pp. 49–50). Wolfe duality is chosen over Fenchel duality. In the linear case the method reduces to Dantzig–Wolfe without solving a dual problem, because the points can be enumerated (p. 48).
  - Lemma 1: the point with the most negative reduced cost is a vertex of CH(Z), because pricing is an LP over the hull (pp. 50–51). It is stated for convex, continuously differentiable problems; for an MIO it would have to sit inside branch and bound (p. 15), which the case study does not do (p. 24). There is no approximation, complexity or statistical guarantee beyond Lemma 1.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - WFP setup: Gurobi v9.1 on an Intel i7-8665U 1.9 GHz with 16 GB RAM, Windows 10 (pp. 22–23).
  - Table 5 gives, for six learners, the validation MSE and the MSE of the learned palatability at 100 optimal solutions (100 cost vectors), without and with the trust region; true palatability comes from the simulator (p. 23). Without the trust region, the errors at the optimizer's chosen points are far above the validation errors (LR 0.046 → 0.256; CART 0.014 → 0.273). With it they come back close to validation (0.042; 0.059). The MLP is best (0.055 → 0.001).
  - The trust region costs time (LR 0.003 s → 1.813 s; GBM 0.513 s → 60.032 s, p. 23), a cost called "acceptable" for the accuracy gained (p. 24).
  - Clustering (K-means, GBM, 100 runs): 89.2% faster at K = 50, with an average objective gap under 0.25%; the effect is not monotone in K (p. 24).
  - Robustness: a hypothesis is stated before the experiments (p. 25). On a 1,000-sample subset with P ∈ {2, 5, 10, 25} bootstrapped trees, α = 0 rather than 0.5 gives 11.3% higher true palatability (20.8% predicted) for 2.5% more cost. Violation rate and margin fall as α falls (p. 26; Figure 7, p. 27).
  - ϵ-CH (200 runs, one tree): the objective improves as ϵ grows, and true palatability stays near the 0.5 bound for ϵ < 0.25 (pp. 27–28).
  - Appendix robustness results: P = 10 and α = 0.25 raise cost by 0.0–1.6% and true palatability by 1.2–9.4% across learners (Table EC.5, p. 55). An ensemble of six methods is more conservative (p. 54).
  - Limits reported: solve times for RF and GBM grow steeply with P, to averages of 44.87 and 133.13 min at P = 25 (Table EC.6, p. 56), with maxima of 2,109.72 and 1,603.51 min. Under a 4-hour limit, 95% of RF runs and 82% of GBM runs are solved to optimality, and four GBM runs end without a feasible solution. The remaining MIP gaps average 1.02% and 5.21% (Table EC.7, pp. 55–56). Gains from more estimators level off at P = 25–50 (pp. 55–56).
  - Column selection: a log-sum-exp test problem with linear learned constraints, solved by MOSEK through a conic reformulation, with 500 to 5 × 10⁵ points and 10 random instances. With column selection, run time stays nearly flat as the points grow (pp. 15, 51–52).
  - Chemotherapy: a temporal split. Learners are chosen per outcome by 5-fold validation, so the choice need not match the best test score (pp. 31, 58). Out-of-sample R² for the toxicities is at most 0.268 (Table EC.9, p. 58). Counterfactuals are missing, so both the given and the prescribed regimens are scored by a "ground truth" ensemble trained on all data (pp. 32–33).
  - Settings: toxicity limit at the training data's 0.6 quantile; 25% violation limit for RF (p. 32). Results are reported only for test cohorts with a feasible solution (p. 32). The count is not stated; [my inference] Table 6's proportions fit only 85 of the 96.
  - Results: prescribed against given, any-DLT satisfaction rises from 0.847 to 0.941 (+11.10%) and predicted survival from 10.855 to 12.092 months (+11.40%). Constraining only the aggregate DLT lowers blood (−13.00%) and infection (−9.30%) satisfaction below the given regimens (Table 6, p. 33); the full model costs 0.38 months of survival against it (p. 34). Average solve times: 0.511 s with all constraints, 0.203 s with the DLT constraint only (Table EC.13, p. 60).
  - Head-to-head (App. E): against JANOS on its own student-enrolment problem, with logistic regression only, OptiCL reaches better objectives at all three sizes (50, 500 and 5,000 students) and is faster on the larger ones (pp. 60–61). A direct comparison with EML "is not possible" (p. 61). OptiCL solves EML's workload-dispatch "ANN1" model with a 10-node ReLU network in 19.4 s; EML's authors had found a 2-node tanh network intractable (pp. 61–62).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper follows the guidelines of Fajemisin et al. (2021), a survey by three of its own authors (pp. 6, 38). It builds on Biggs et al.'s convex-hull idea (2021) (pp. 13–14). Rivals are named and then tested, not dismissed: JANOS and EML are called restricted (p. 4) and compared on their own test problems (App. E). Verwer et al.'s tree formulation is called similar, with merits on each side (p. 12). Against prescriptive analytics, including Bertsimas's own work with Kallus, the line is drawn by embedding: those papers use predictions as inputs, while this one embeds the model (p. 5). The predict-then-optimize weakness flagged by Elmachtoub–Grigas (2021) is conceded to apply here too (p. 35). Optimal Trees are described as an optimization framework that "approximates globally optimal trees" (p. 10), a narrower phrase than *optimal*. Balestriero et al. (2021) justify enlarging the hull (p. 18). Goldfarb–Iyengar (2003) is cited for the robust linear case (p. 35).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract opens with a foundation claim, "We establish a broad methodological foundation" (p. 1). Two concrete examples (toxicity and palatability) come before any symbol (p. 2). An umbrella term, "constraint learning", is coined and defined (p. 3). Contributions are numbered and end with a GitHub link (pp. 5–6). A hypothesis precedes the robustness experiments (p. 25). Hedges are specific: no parameter setting is best, because it depends on the stakes and the turnaround time (p. 26); cohorts without a feasible solution are left to a sensitivity analysis under clinical guidance (p. 32). The close looks to adoption: the authors "envision" OptiCL inside modelling software (p. 35). The abstract's "high-quality prescriptions" (p. 1) rests on scoring by a simulator and by a model ensemble (pp. 19, 32). There is one slip in v3: the chemotherapy MIO is printed as a minimization of y_OS (pp. 29–30), although survival is the objective to raise (pp. 29, 33).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are a framework, the open-source OptiCL package (Python, on Pyomo), two case studies and one lemma (pp. 5–6, 35, 50). The limits are concrete:
  - Point predictions are constrained, so misspecification remains a risk; model selection and ensembles only mitigate it (p. 35).
  - Unobserved confounding is assumed away (pp. 29, 35), and counterfactual evaluation is hard (p. 32).
  - The trust region costs time (pp. 23–24), and column selection is not combined with branch and bound (p. 24).
  - Run time grows steeply when ensemble learners are themselves embedded P times (pp. 55–56).
  - Future work: causal inference to relax the confounding assumption, and robust optimization for prediction uncertainty in general ML models, open beyond the linear case (pp. 34–35). S266 takes up the second for SVMs and trees (S266 pp. 16–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Embedding a trained tree: one binary per leaf and big-M split constraints, with each M computed by maximizing the split expression over the known constraints and the trust region (eqs. (8), (9), pp. 43–44). If the tree is the only learned constraint, solve one LP or MIO per feasible leaf in parallel (pp. 11–12, 44–45).
- (D4) Keeping solutions near the data: write the convex hull with convex-combination weights λ (eq. (2), p. 14). Cluster first and take a union of hulls with one binary per cluster (eq. (3), p. 14). Enlarge it with a 1-, 2- or ∞-norm ball to stay in class (eq. (5), p. 18).
- (D4) Huge point sets: start from about 100 points, price the rest by the KKT/Wolfe reduced cost and add the most negative one, which is always a hull vertex (Lemma 1; pp. 49–52).
- (D4) Robustness to the learner: allow at most an α share of P bootstrapped or different-class estimators to violate a learned constraint (eq. (4), p. 17). α = 0 and the mean constraint need no big-M (p. 47).
- (D4) Embedding classifiers: threshold the logit at ln(τ/(1 − τ)) instead of modelling the sigmoid, and use an argmax big-M instead of a softmax (p. 46).
- (D5) Report three errors per learner: on validation data, at the optimal solutions without a trust region, and with one; add the time with and without (Table 5, p. 23).
- (D5) For MIOs with heavy embedded ensembles, report the mean, median and maximum time, and under a time limit the share solved to optimality, the share feasible and the remaining gap (Table EC.7, p. 56).

### S266 · Global optimization: a machine learning approach (Journal of Global Optimization 2025, DOI 10.1007/s10898-024-01434-9; arXiv 2311.01742)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v1 (arXiv:2311.01742v1): main text pp. 1–33 read in full, including Table 1 (pp. 26–27), which I tallied row by row; references (pp. 33–35) skimmed. Figures 1, 2 and 5–7 are absent from the extracted text apart from their captions; Figures 3–4 survive as formulas and weights. The JOGO print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthor**: Georgios Margaritis (MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1); author order is alphabetical · **Position in trajectory**: the step after OCTHaGOn (Bertsimas & Öztürk; listed as "To appear" in JOGO, 2023, p. 34; S298 in works.json, arXiv 2202.06017). OCTHaGOn already learned constraints with hyperplane Optimal Trees (OCT-H) (pp. 3–4). This paper adds S097's framework, here called Constraint Learning (pp. 4, 12), and the robust-counterpart tools of Bertsimas and den Hertog's 2022 book (p. 16). With S298 and the 2026 IJOC cone-product paper (S415, metadata only), it marks a global-optimization line, 2022–2026. The skill's trajectory table does not list that line; the cone-product paper appears only under "Latest" and in the CP row.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + HS + DA; ML as the engine; RO as a device}: The entry is the global solvers' restriction to the primitives they can relax. BARON handles only exp, ln, x^a and b^x (p. 3), and such optimizers are "generally ineffective" when constraints are black-box, for instance simulation-based (p. 3). Each rival family has a weakness. Gradient methods need good starting points and cannot easily handle integers. Relaxations and outer approximations fit only specific nonlinearities. Branching suffers from the curse of dimensionality (pp. 2–3). The second entry is the group's own earlier method: OCTHaGOn "sometimes yields infeasible or suboptimal solutions due to being approximate in nature" (p. 3). The problem is a general MINLP, eq. (1), with one requirement: the variables of the nonlinear constraints must be bounded (pp. 2, 5). The full text bears out the title-based domain tags (DA, ML, HS; references/domains/work-domains.json): black-box constraints are named in the abstract (p. 1) and simulation-based ones on p. 3, and the method ends in local search (p. 8). It adds IO, since the core object is an MIO approximation of an MINLP.
- **D2 Core idea**: Each nonlinear constraint is replaced by a classifier of its feasibility, and a nonlinear objective by a regressor. Each learner is drawn from a menu of MIO-representable models (OCT-H, linear SVM, GBM, ReLU MLP) and chosen per function by test accuracy or R² (pp. 7, 12). The resulting MIO is solved with a commercial solver, and the answer is repaired by projected gradient descent (pp. 6, 8). Against OCTHaGOn, four things change: more learner classes; adaptive sampling where learners disagree; robust counterparts for the learned parameters; and penalized relaxation when the MIO is infeasible. The repair step also gains momentum (pp. 4, 7–8). Each step of the eight-step pipeline is labelled same, enhanced or new (pp. 7–8). What stays: the first three sampling steps (boundary, Latin hypercube, kNN quasi-Newton), the MIO solve and the PGD step (pp. 6–8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Problem (1) allows any objective and constraints, with integer or continuous variables (p. 2).
  - Assumptions, admitted in the limits section (pp. 32–33): the variables of nonlinear constraints have bounds, which the method otherwise tries to infer from the linear constraints (p. 5); constraints are cheap to evaluate; automatic differentiation is available for the PGD step.
  - Only explicit problems were tested, because benchmarks with black-box constraints are scarce (p. 32).
  - Data: samples from boundary, Latin hypercube, kNN quasi-Newton and OCT sampling (pp. 6, 13–15).
  - SVMs are linear, with no kernel, to keep the MIO linear (p. 9).
  - Uncertainty sets: p-norm balls of radius ρ on multiplicative perturbations of the learned coefficients (p. 16). GBM ensembles are kept small, so that robustness in every learner does not make the MIO infeasible (p. 17).
  - Benchmarks: bounded continuous nonconvex problems (p. 23); the speed reducer adds one integer variable (p. 31).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Embeddings: a linear SVM becomes one linear constraint (eq. (5), p. 9). A tree becomes leaf polyhedra (eq. (6)) and a big-M disjunction, with ϵ for the strict inequalities (eq. (7), p. 10); feasibility is y_DT ≥ 0.5 (eq. (8), p. 10). A GBM is a weighted sum of tree embeddings (eq. (10), p. 11). A ReLU MLP becomes a big-M network, and the output sigmoid is dropped by requiring a positive logit (eq. (12), p. 12).
  - OCT sampling: K OCT-H trees are trained on random subsets of size C. The method keeps the points where the trees disagree, |P(x) − N(x)| ≤ Kτ (eqs. (16)–(17), p. 14). For each such point it intersects the K leaf polyhedra that contain it; the trees are guaranteed to disagree throughout that polyhedron (eqs. (18)–(19), p. 14). The interior is sampled by hit-and-run (pp. 14–15).
  - Relaxation: slacks u ≥ 0 with penalty λ (eq. (20)), used only when the unrelaxed MIO is infeasible (pp. 15–16). The relaxed problem is said to be feasible whenever the original problem is (p. 15); no argument is given.
  - Robust counterparts, via the tools of Bertsimas and den Hertog [24]. The SVM constraint becomes β̄₀ + β̄ᵀx − ρ||β̄ ⊙ x||_q ≥ 0, with q the dual norm (eqs. (21)–(22), p. 16); the tree splits become eqs. (23)–(24) (p. 17). Multiplicative uncertainty keeps zero coefficients zero, so parallel splits stay parallel (p. 16). L1 or L∞ sets keep the MIO linear; L2 makes it conic quadratic (p. 22).
  - There are no convergence, approximation or complexity results. The limits section says the method does not necessarily find global optima (p. 32).

- **D6 Positioning**: The rival approaches are reviewed even-handedly: all are "very effective" on certain problem types, and each has named weaknesses (pp. 2–3). Work on ML for MIO (learning to branch, learned tree search) is cited as context (pp. 3–4). The self-cited core is [7], [8], [9], [16] and [24] (pp. 34–35).
- **D7 Writing**: The contributions are listed (i)–(iv) (p. 4). The eight-step pipeline is labelled same, enhanced or new against the predecessor (pp. 7–8). A two-variable example walks through every step with numbers (pp. 17–23). Results are given as counts out of 77 (p. 25). Hedges bracket the paper ("despite its approximate nature", pp. 5, 32), and a separate Limitations subsection is concrete (pp. 32–33). Against these stand the title and the closing claim of a method "new in the global optimization literature" (p. 33): in the body BARON solves 73 of 77, and global optimality is not guaranteed (pp. 25, 32). This is Tension T6.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic: four enhancements to an existing framework, built on the public OCTHaGOn code base in Julia (p. 24). v1 gives no code link for GoML. The limits are specific (pp. 32–33):
  - no global-optimality guarantee, so solutions may be suboptimal or infeasible;
  - never tested on true black-box constraints;
  - bounds required;
  - cheap evaluations and automatic differentiation assumed (finite differences offered as a fallback);
  - OCT-H training is the bottleneck, so OCT-H is limited to small problems, and training time is linear in the number of nonlinear constraints;
  - MIO time grows with deeper learners.
  Future work is stated only broadly: "still a work in progress", and applications that lack explicit formulations (pp. 32–33).

- Method 1 · variant ⚠: The class is kept on purpose: linear SVMs keep the MIO linear (p. 9); L1 and L∞ robust sets stay linear and L2 becomes conic quadratic (p. 22); constraints that Gurobi can take exactly are passed through (p. 23). But the known problem is approximated by learned surrogates, not written exactly (pp. 7, 12–13); compare S097, where the trained model itself is embedded exactly. - New-pattern candidate: *disagreement-driven adaptive sampling*. A committee of trees fitted on subsamples, a disagreement threshold, intersected leaf polyhedra, and hit-and-run sampling inside them (pp. 13–15). It improved the gap in 14 instances (p. 28).
- New-pattern candidate: *robustness layer against learning error* (see S097). Here, robust optimization applied to the trained parameters rather than to the data (p. 16), with multiplicative sets that keep sparsity (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *knobs placed after training*. Robustness and relaxation act on the embedding, so a hyperparameter grid only re-solves and never retrains (pp. 24, 28).
- New-pattern candidate: *learn only what the solver cannot take exactly*. Linear constraints pass straight through (pp. 5–6), and with GoML NonCVX so do convex and nonconvex quadratic ones (p. 23). Compare S097, where explicit f and g sit beside the learned ĥ (S097 p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) For each awkward constraint, learn a feasibility classifier from a menu of MIO-representable learners and pick it by held-out accuracy. Pass every constraint the solver handles exactly straight through (pp. 7, 12, 23).
- (D4) Adaptive sampling: train K trees on random subsets, select the points with |P(x) − N(x)| ≤ Kτ, intersect their leaf polyhedra and sample inside by hit-and-run (pp. 13–15).
- (D4) Robustify a learned linear rule with multiplicative uncertainty: β̄ᵀx − ρ||β̄ ⊙ x||_q. It keeps zero coefficients zero and stays linear for L1 or L∞ (pp. 16, 22).
- (D4) Infeasibility fallback: solve first without slacks; if the MIO is infeasible, re-solve with penalized slacks u ≥ 0 (pp. 15–16).
- (D5) Put the knobs after training, so a hyperparameter grid only re-embeds and re-solves (p. 24).
- (D5) Bound unknown optima with the rival solver's lower bound and the best feasible point of either method (p. 30).
- (D5) Run an attribution table: switch each enhancement on and off and count the instances improved and unchanged (Table 2, p. 28). Add a row for the instances made worse, which Table 2 lacks.
