### S173 · Transient laws of non-stationary queueing systems and their applications (Queueing Systems 1997, DOI 10.1023/a:1019100301115)
Read level: **partial**. Parts read:
- pp. 1–9: abstract, introduction and contributions, notation, Theorems 1–2 with proofs, Assumptions A.1–A.4;
- pp. 11–14: Theorems 6–7 and the proof of Theorem 6;
- pp. 19–23: the results table, §5.1, Proposition 11 and the statement of Theorem 12;
- p. 25, p. 26 and pp. 28–30: the §5.4 set-up, and Proposition 15 with its numerical check;
- pp. 33–36: Corollary 18 and the numerical results;
- p. 38 (conclusions) and pp. 39–41 (appendix and references).

Not read: the §4.1 review (pp. 10–11), the §4.3 multiclass law (pp. 15–19), the derivations of Theorems 13–17 (pp. 24–32) and §5.5 (pp. 36–38). Figures are captions only.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Georgia Mourtzinou, MIT Operations Research Center (p. 1). Her 1995 MIT thesis, "An axiomatic approach to queueing systems", is ref. [24] (p. 41). · **Position in trajectory**: the SD line, still active in 1997. The paper was received August 1995 and revised August 1996 (p. 1). It was funded by his PYI award with Draper Laboratory (p. 1). It carries the steady-state distributional-law approach of the authors' earlier papers [4, 5] into the transient domain. Bertsimas–Nakazato [6] had earlier given transient results for phase-type systems (pp. 2, 40).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD}:
  - Transient analysis "has long been considered a very difficult problem", and time-varying rates make it harder (p. 1). Yet non-stationary models are needed for production, service, communication and air transportation systems, and convergence to steady state is often too slow to describe the system (pp. 1–2).
  - The question: are there "transient laws", generic relations between the number in system L(t) and the delay S(t), and how can they be used (p. 2)?
  - The entry is methodological: find structural relations instead of simulating or solving numerically, building on Little's law and the Haji–Newell distributional laws (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - A transient Little's law. Theorem 1 covers a system that starts empty, Theorem 2 one with k initial customers under Assumption B (pp. 6–7): E[L(t)] = ∫₀ᵗ h(u) P{S(u) > t − u} du, plus Σᵢ P{Vᵢ > t} for the initial customers. Unlike the stationary law, it needs the whole distribution of S and the initial state (p. 8).
  - Transient distributional laws for overtake-free systems (Theorems 6–7, pp. 12–14). They link the generating function of L(t) to P{S(u) > t − u} through the generating function of later arrivals. Multiclass versions are Theorem 8 (p. 15, not read).
  - For every application the authors "use the same approach": the laws supply as many integral equations as unknowns, and each system is then solved by whatever technique fits it (p. 3).
  - Results by system (table, p. 19):
    - exact formulae for M(t)/G(t)/∞;
    - integral equations for GI(t)/GI(t)/1 and ΣGI(t)/GI(t)/1;
    - integral equations plus asymptotics for transient GI/GI/1, GI/D/s and ΣGI/GI/1;
    - an exact numerical algorithm for M(t)/GI(t)/1.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions (p. 9):
    - A.1: each interarrival time is independent of earlier ones given the arrival epoch, which covers renewal and nonhomogeneous Poisson arrivals (p. 5);
    - A.2: FIFO exits, called "the crucial assumption" because it restricts the laws to overtake-free systems;
    - A.3: no blocking, balking or reneging;
    - A.4: independent class streams.
  - Service requirements are stationary and identically distributed, while the service rate may vary in time (p. 5).
  - Initial customers are served first and the server does not idle (Assumption B, p. 7; the stronger Assumption C, p. 21).
  - The heavy-traffic regime is ρ → 1 with t → ∞ (p. 28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 1: the sample-path count is written as an integral of indicators, then Fubini (p. 7).
  - Overtake-free laws: {L(t) ≥ n} is the event that the nth most recently arrived customer is still present. It splits into an arrival at u, n − 1 later arrivals, and a sojourn longer than t − u, which are independent by A.1 (pp. 12–13).
  - M(t)/GI(t)/∞ is not overtake-free. It is split into M(t)/D/∞ classes with deterministic service times, then general service is obtained as a limit of mixtures (Theorem 10, pp. 20–21).
  - The single server is reduced to its emptiness function idle(t) (Proposition 11, Theorem 12, pp. 22–23).
  - Heavy traffic: kernel expansions as z → 1 and s, w → 0 (Proposition 15, p. 28), and an analyticity argument for the double transform (appendix, pp. 39–40).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation (one line)**:
  - The kernel expansion is checked against Laplace inversion by two algorithms (Hosono; Abate–Whitt), programmed in Matlab, which "got exactly the same results". The expansion is exact for t > 20 and most accurate near Poisson arrivals (p. 29).
  - idle(t) and E[Q] − E[Q(t)] are compared with Brownian approximations and with Odoni–Roth's exact values. The asymptotics agree with these comparators only after a time t₀ of about 12 to 80, depending on the variability of arrivals and service, and values for t < 15 are withheld as inaccurate (p. 34).
  - The M(t)/GI(t)/1 numbers are omitted because they coincide with those of Choudhury et al. (p. 25).
  - The introduction promises a comparison with simulation results (p. 3), but "simulation" occurs in the text only on pp. 2–3, and no simulation runs are reported. No run times are given.
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**:
  - The paper takes a "different standpoint" from simulation and numerical work (p. 2).
  - Theorem 10 is credited to Palm, Bartlett, Doob, Khintchine and Prékopa, all before 1958, and to Massey–Whitt (p. 21).
  - Choudhury et al. reached the M(t)/G(t)/1 recursion independently, through Takács's equation (p. 25).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: numbered contributions and a results table by system (pp. 2–3, 19); the applications are framed as demonstrating "the power" of the laws (pp. 1, 3, 38); the reviewer is thanked for substantial improvements (p. 38).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions are both theoretical (the laws) and applied (six system types) (pp. 2–3, 38).
  - Stated limits:
    - overtake-free systems only (p. 9);
    - the multiclass law with arbitrary initial conditions is omitted because "The analysis is complicated" (p. 19);
    - the GI/GI/1 integral equation is "still difficult to solve analytically" (p. 26);
    - Corollary 18 holds only asymptotically in heavy traffic (p. 34).
  - Future work: multiserver systems and networks, the paper's "major open problem" (p. 38).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the work is formulation first, but the formulation is a complete set of laws giving "a sufficient number of integral equations and unknowns" for each system (p. 3), not a class-kept LP, conic or MIO model. The boundary is printed: overtake-free systems only, with multiserver systems and networks open (pp. 9, 38). No optimization appears anywhere in the paper, so it is evidence for the probability side of Tension T3.
- New-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device*:
  - Theorem 10 recovers the pre-1958 Poisson result for M(t)/G/∞ (pp. 20–21);
  - the heavy-traffic analysis recovers the authors' steady-state E[Q] and E[W] (pp. 28, 33);
  - the M(t)/GI(t)/1 recursion matches Choudhury et al. (p. 25).
- New-pattern candidate: *own earlier result's limit as the entry*: the steady-state distributional laws of [4, 5] and the stationary Little's law are made transient, with the initial conditions explicit (pp. 2, 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To prove a time-t version of a steady-state law, write the sample-path count as an integral of indicators and exchange expectation and integral (Fubini). Initial customers then appear as a separate additive term (pp. 6–8).
- In a FIFO system, derive distributional relations from the event that the nth most recent arrival is still present. Split it into an arrival time, the count of later arrivals and a sojourn tail (pp. 12–13).
- Extend a result from deterministic to general service times by treating each service value as a thinned Poisson class, then passing to mixtures (pp. 20–21).
- Validate an asymptotic expansion against numerical inversion computed by two independent algorithms that must agree, and report the time t₀ after which the expansion holds (pp. 29, 34).

**Quotes**:
- "This paper takes a different standpoint, trying to identify structural relationships between the fundamental quantities of queueing systems that evolve over time." (p. 2)
- "The major open problem is to identify queueing laws for these systems." (p. 38)

### S323 · Estimation of time-varying parameters in statistical models: an optimization approach (COLT '97 Proceedings 1997, DOI 10.1145/267460.267519)
Read level: **full**, journal version. The file is Machine Learning 35, 225–245 (1999), received 30 July 1997 (pp. 1, 21), not the COLT 1997 proceedings paper that the index row names; that journal version is index row D006 (no open text). All 21 pages were read, including the appendix (pp. 19–21). Figures 1–3 are captions only.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: David Gamarnik (MIT ORC, at IBM T. J. Watson by publication, p. 1; in the skill's SD lineage [04 A.4]) and John N. Tsitsiklis (LIDS and ORC, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**:
- A statistics problem written as a convex program in 1997–1999, five years before the earliest ML-programme item the skill cites (CRIO, submitted 2002).
- Its slowly-varying extension (pp. 17–18) is cited in 2025 by S317 (card c30) as early work on slowly varying regression (S317 p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML; CP as the engine}:
  - The problem is nonlinear regression Y = f(X) + ψ; the motivating special case is the time-varying mean E[Y(t)] (p. 1).
  - The entry: parametric fits assume a functional form, while kernel regression and smoothing splines are the nonparametric standard (p. 2).
  - The paper asks what convex optimization can do when f is known only to be Lipschitz (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Step 1 minimizes Σ(Yᵢ − f̂ᵢ)² subject to |f̂ᵢ − f̂ⱼ| ≤ K‖Xᵢ − Xⱼ‖∞; this is QP (2) (p. 3).
  - Step 2 extends the fitted values by f̂(x) = maxᵢ{f̂ᵢ − K‖x − Xᵢ‖∞}. The extension keeps the Lipschitz constant K and interpolates the fitted values (Proposition 1, pp. 3–4).
  - In time, consecutive constraints suffice, with linear interpolation between observations (p. 4).
  - The same constraints turn maximum likelihood with an input-dependent parameter into DMLE (p. 6). By family:
    - Gaussian with known σ reduces to QP (2);
    - Bernoulli and exponential give concave objectives;
    - Gaussian with unknown σ is "not convex" (pp. 7–9).
  - Two differences from smoothing splines:
    - no differentiability is needed, and the smoothness bound is a hard constraint, not a penalty (p. 2);
    - with zero noise the fit reproduces f at the data points, which kernel and spline estimators do not. The authors call it "more robust with respect to small noise levels" (p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The noise is conditionally zero-mean and independent (p. 3).
  - f is Lipschitz in the max norm with unknown K₀; K is guessed, and K ≥ K₀ suffices (pp. 3, 5).
  - Theorem 2 adds:
    - X bounded in [0,1]^d and Y in [0,1];
    - a density of X bounded below by β > 0 (pp. 12–13).
  - The authors note that boundedness excludes Gaussian noise and Example 4.1 (p. 12).
  - Data: synthetic only, Y = sin X + N(0, σ²) on an equispaced grid over [0, 2π] (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Average unbiasedness (Theorem 1): adding a constant c to every fitted value stays feasible, so optimality in c gives Σf̂ᵢ = ΣYᵢ (pp. 5–6).
  - Uniform almost-sure consistency with exponential tails (Theorem 2). The proof chain:
    1. the estimator is empirical risk minimization over the Lipschitz class (p. 14);
    2. Lee–Bartlett–Williamson's uniform-deviation bound (Proposition 2, p. 15);
    3. a Kolmogorov–Tihomirov covering-number bound that does not depend on n, with log H = O((K/ε)^d) (Proposition 3, pp. 15, 19–20);
    4. L2 tail bounds (Propositions 4–5, p. 16);
    5. a lemma passing from L2 to sup-norm convergence for Lipschitz functions under the density floor (Lemma 1, p. 16);
    6. Borel–Cantelli (p. 17).
  - Tractability is asserted, not measured: a convex QP with linear constraints, polynomial by interior-point methods (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation (one line)**:
  - Set-up (Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11):
    - Y = sin X with n = 100 and σ from 0.5 down to 0.001;
    - K ∈ {1, 2} against Gaussian-kernel regression with δ ∈ {0.3, 0.1};
    - d∞ and d2 errors.
  - Findings:
    - the method is insensitive to K, and the best K lies slightly below the true K₀ = 1 (Fig. 2, n = 50, pp. 11–12);
    - kernel regression ties or wins at some (σ, δ) pairs, and the QP wins as the noise shrinks (p. 12);
    - a sample-size curve averages 40 repetitions (p. 12).
  - Not reported: solver, run time, or the n(n − 1)/2 constraint count (pp. 5, 9–12).
  - Two slips:
    - the Fig. 1 caption says K = 1 while the text says K = 2 (p. 10);
    - the text calls Table 2's errors larger, but Table 1 (d∞) holds the larger values (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**:
  - Uniform almost-sure consistency with exponential tails exists for kernel regression (Devroye 1978) but, to the authors' knowledge, not for smoothing splines (p. 2).
  - The paper concedes that kernel regression attains the optimal O(n^−2/3) rate, which it cannot prove for its own method (p. 18).
  - Statistical learning theory (Vapnik, Haussler, Lee–Bartlett–Williamson) supplies the proof machinery (pp. 14–15).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: a three-item contribution list with section pointers (p. 2); the impracticality of the constants is admitted in the introduction (p. 2) and repeated (pp. 13, 17); lists of remarks follow each algorithm (pp. 5, 7, 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: the convex-optimization estimator, DMLE, average unbiasedness and strong consistency (pp. 2, 19).
  - Limits:
    - the constants are "too large for practical purposes, even for dimension d = 1" (p. 17);
    - the number of samples grows exponentially with d, which is "unavoidable" (p. 13);
    - the rate is unproven: the bounds give O(n^−2/5), while simulation suggests O(n^−2/3) (p. 18);
    - the choice of K is "crucial" and left open (p. 19).
  - Future work: consistency of DMLE, bounds on the expected error, ways to set K, and a hybrid of kernel regression with Lipschitz constraints (pp. 18–19).
  - A varying-coefficient model b(t)′z with Lipschitz b(t) is proposed. The logarithm of its VC entropy is O(d), better than the known lower bound for general Lipschitz functions of d variables (pp. 17–18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (ML, 1997–1999): a problem owned by nonparametric statistics is written exactly as a convex QP. The class is named: convex objective, linear constraints, polynomial-time interior-point methods (abstract; p. 5). The boundary is printed: Gaussian with unknown variance is nonconvex (p. 8), and sample needs grow exponentially in d (p. 13).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: one interpretable knob K indexes the estimators. It comes with a guarantee condition, K ≥ K₀ (p. 13), and an error-against-K curve (Fig. 2, pp. 11–12). But the knob is a smoothness bound, not a property priced for a decision-maker, and how to set it is left open (p. 19).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the method duels the incumbent, kernel regression at two bandwidths, on synthetic data with known truth, and says where the incumbent ties or wins (p. 12). There is no warm start or certificate, since the QP is solved exactly, and no solve time is reported (pp. 5, 9–12).
- New-pattern candidate: *print the looseness of your own constants and the conjecture that would close it*: the constants are admitted to be unusable (pp. 2, 13, 17), and the proven O(n^−2/5) rate is set against the simulated O(n^−2/3) (p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a qualitative shape assumption into pairwise linear constraints on the fitted values. Extend off-sample with f̂(x) = maxᵢ{f̂ᵢ − K‖x − Xᵢ‖}, which keeps the constant (pp. 3–4).
- Prove average unbiasedness by shift invariance: if adding a constant keeps the solution feasible, optimality in that constant forces the residuals to sum to zero (pp. 5–6).
- Prove consistency of a constrained least-squares estimator in three steps (pp. 14–17):
  1. write it as ERM over the function class;
  2. bound the class's covering number independently of n;
  3. convert L2 convergence to sup-norm convergence through the Lipschitz constant and a density floor.
- For maximum likelihood with smoothly varying parameters, check concavity family by family before claiming tractability (pp. 7–9).

**Quotes**:
- "The resulting optimization problem has a convex objective function and linear constraints, and as a result, is efficiently solvable." (p. 1)
- "the constants in our estimates of the tail probabilities are too large to be practically useful." (p. 2)

### S345 · The parsimonious property of cut covering problems and its applications (Operations Research Letters 1997, DOI 10.1016/s0167-6377(97)00032-1)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–10, all sections and the references. OCR-garbled formulas were followed through the prose.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chung-Piaw Teo (National University of Singapore, p. 1; in the skill's IO lineage [04 A.4]) · **Position in trajectory**: the IO line. The paper was received January 1996 and revised May 1997 (p. 1), and was funded by his PYI award (p. 1). It continues "the program" of Goemans–Bertsimas 1993, S052 (card c02) (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO; LO as the engine}:
  - The class is the cutset IP: minimize Σcₑxₑ subject to x(δ(S)) ≥ f(S) for every S, plus degree constraints at the nodes in D. It models Steiner tree, TSP, VRP, matching, T-join and survivable network design (p. 1; Table 1, p. 4).
  - The entry: Goemans and Bertsimas proved, for survivable network design, that the degree constraints do not change the LP value; this is the parsimonious property (Theorem 1, p. 2).
  - The goal is to find for which f the property holds, and what it implies (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Conditions B are symmetry, node subadditivity and quasi-supermodularity (QS). They are more general than Goemans–Williamson's proper functions (Proposition 1, p. 3). They cover b-matching with b(i) ∈ {a, a+1} and the capacitated-tree relaxation of the VRP, neither of which is proper (p. 3).
  - Theorem 2: under the triangle inequality, Z_f(D) = Z_f(∅) for every D (p. 4). Theorem 3 is the dual-integral version (p. 5).
  - Applications:
    - non-algorithmic integrality proofs: T-join integrality from the perfect-matching polytope, the Steiner 2-connected polytope with at most 5 terminals, and single-source uncapacitated multicommodity flow (Theorems 5–7, p. 6);
    - a new proof of Goemans–Williamson's 2(1 − 1/|T|) bound for 0–1 proper f (Theorem 8, p. 7). It yields an algorithm that avoids their reverse-deletion step at the cost of all-pairs shortest paths (pp. 7–8);
    - weaker Conditions C (weak subadditivity and 2-QS), which unify classical edge-disjoint-path results (Theorem 9, Proposition 2, pp. 8–10).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Undirected graph with nonnegative costs (p. 1).
  - Theorem 2 needs the triangle inequality. When D = ∅ it holds without loss after replacing costs by shortest-path distances (Theorem 4, p. 5).
  - Integral costs are assumed for Theorem 8, without loss since rational costs can be rescaled (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - The LP-duality proof (pp. 4–5):
    1. uncross the dual support into a laminar family, using QS;
    2. replace every set containing v by its complement V∖A;
    3. move Δ = min(−y(v), y(A)) among y(v), y(A ∪ {v}) and y(A).

    Feasibility follows from the triangle inequality, and optimality from symmetry and node subadditivity.
  - Two small counterexamples, each with an empty P_f(V), show that both conditions are needed (p. 5).
  - Integrality proofs sandwich the IP value between a known integral polytope and the LP (p. 6).
  - Theorem 8 is proved by a minimal counterexample (fewest nodes, then smallest total cost) that contradicts dual feasibility (p. 7).
  - Conditions C are handled by Lovász's splitting-off operation (pp. 8–9).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation (one line)**: none; there is no computation (pp. 1–10).
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**:
  - The paper generalizes Goemans–Bertsimas and Goemans–Williamson (pp. 2–3), and its proof extends Frank's observation for matching (pp. 2, 4).
  - It acknowledges twice that Goemans reached the same generalization independently, in unpublished work (pp. 2, 10).
  - It re-derives the edge-disjoint-path theorems of Rothschild–Whinston, Seymour and Lomonosov (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: four numbered contributions (p. 2); the property is called "interesting and indeed surprising" (p. 4) and the integrality proofs "genuinely simple" (p. 6); a "tedious" case analysis is omitted as off-theme (p. 10); there is no conclusion section (p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a characterization of the property, integrality proofs, a new approximation proof with an algorithm, and a unified view of edge-disjoint paths (pp. 1–2, 6–10). For arbitrary proper f the approximation factor becomes 2·W(p₁, …, pₙ) (p. 8).
  - Limits:
    - the triangle inequality is required;
    - shortest paths must be recomputed because the reduced costs need not satisfy it (footnote, p. 8);
    - the edge-disjoint-path proof is incomplete in print (p. 10).
  - Future work: none stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (IO): LP duality is the proof device, and structure is read off a chosen optimal dual: a laminar support with nonnegative duals on the degree constraints (pp. 4–5). The boundary is printed through counterexamples (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: *relaxation-centred IO*: the LP relaxation is the object of study (its value does not depend on the degree constraints), the tool (integrality proofs) and the yardstick (the IP/LP ratio) (pp. 1–8). It recurs with S052 (card c02) and S166 (this batch).
- New-pattern candidate: *re-derive classical theorems with the new device*:
  - T-join integrality from Edmonds's matching polytope (p. 6);
  - the MST heuristic's bound for Steiner trees as a special case of Theorem 8 (p. 8);
  - edge-disjoint-path theorems unified by the 2-QS property (p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate: *own earlier result's limit as the entry*: the 1993 survivable-network theorem is generalized to a class of set functions (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To show that extra constraints leave an LP value unchanged, choose a structured optimal dual (uncross it into a laminar family). Then move dual weight off the extra constraints while keeping feasibility and the objective value (pp. 4–5).
- Get the triangle inequality for free: replace costs by shortest-path distances and reroute flow along shortest paths (Theorem 4, p. 5).
- Prove integrality by sandwiching the IP value between a known integral polytope and the LP value, using the structural property (Theorems 5–7, p. 6).
- Prove an approximation ratio by a minimal counterexample (p. 7): drop zero-demand vertices, contract zero-cost edges, then lower all costs by a uniform dual step.
- Show that each hypothesis is necessary with a 3- or 4-node counterexample (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "It is thus interesting and indeed surprising that the parsimonious property holds for the LP relaxations of these problems." (p. 4)
- "At first sight these results appear to be unrelated without a unifying characteristic." (p. 9)

### S802 · Pricing and hedging derivative securities in incomplete markets: an ε-arbitrage approach (NBER Working Papers 1997, DOI 10.3386/w6250)
Read level: **partial**, NBER working-paper version (OCR scan). Parts read:
- pp. 1–12: front matter, abstract, contents, introduction with Table 1, and the §2.1 set-up and assumptions;
- pp. 15–26: Theorems 1–2, §2.4 and all of Section 3, including Theorem 3;
- pp. 36–38: §5.1–5.2;

### S140 · Asymptotic buffer overflow probabilities in multiclass multiplexers: An optimal control approach (IEEE Transactions on Automatic Control 1998, DOI 10.1109/9.661587)
Read level: **partial**, technical-report version: Boston University report MNS-97-107 (June 1996, revised June 1997), whose cover gives the TAC citation (p. 1). Parts read:

- pp. 16–21: the optimal control formulation, Lemma 5.1, homogeneity and the start of §5.1;
- pp. 24–26: most likely paths, the admission-control remark and the GPS upper bound;
- pp. 34–35: Theorem 9.1 and Corollary 9.2;
- pp. 40–46: §10 comparison, Theorem 10.5 and the conclusions.

Not read: the lower-bound proofs (pp. 13–16), the GPS and GLQF control solutions (pp. 21–24), the GLQF upper-bound proof (pp. 26–34), Theorems 9.3–9.6 (pp. 36–40) and the proofs of Lemmas 10.2–10.4 (pp. 42–44). Figures are captions and axis labels only.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ioannis Ch. Paschalidis (Boston University, corresponding author) and John N. Tsitsiklis (MIT EECS) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the SD line moving into communication networks.
- The results are part of Paschalidis's 1996 MIT thesis; a preliminary version was presented in 1995 (p. 1). Paschalidis is in the skill's SD lineage [04 A.4].
- Companion reports cover acyclic networks of G/G/1 queues [BPT97a] and the GPS upper bound [BPT97b] (pp. 6, 47).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + AP (communication networks)}:
  - Quality of service in ATM-type networks needs loss probabilities of order 10^−9 (the exponent's sign is lost in the text layer, p. 3).
  - Exact queue-length distributions in multiclass networks of G/G/1 queues with correlated arrivals are out of reach. The paper therefore studies a simplified version that keeps the salient features: two classes with dedicated buffers sharing one server whose capacity is stochastic (pp. 3–4, 9).
  - The question: the large-buffer tail of each buffer's overflow probability, under GPS and under a new policy, GLQF (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Each overflow scenario ω has probability of about e^−Uθ(ω). The tightest lower bound takes the most likely scenario, and finding it is a deterministic optimal control problem (pp. 12, 16–18):
    - states: the scaled queue levels;
    - controls: the empirical rates of the arrival and service processes;
    - cost: the integral of their rate functions;
    - free end time.
  - A sample-path upper bound matches the lower bound to first degree in the exponent (pp. 2, 12).
  - The policy enters only through region-wise linear dynamics, so the same method applies to other policies (pp. 5, 46).
  - The optimal trajectories are the most likely overflow modes, two per policy (pp. 12, 24–25).
  - Differences from earlier work: unlike Courcoubetis–Weber's formulation, which needs Lipschitz controls, the exponent here is the value of the control problem (pp. 4–5); and GPS results are extended to stochastic service capacity (p. 5).
  - §10 compares policies by their vectors of class-wise exponents. The two strict-priority policies are the extreme points (pp. 40–41), an exponent-space analogue of SW1's performance vectors.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - A slotted-time fluid model. The arrival and service processes are stationary and mutually independent, with dependence over time within each process (pp. 9–10).
  - Stability: E[B] > E[A1] + E[A2] (p. 9).
  - Assumptions A (Gärtner–Ellis), B (a sample-path lower bound on polygonal paths) and C (its "convex dual analog"). The authors state, citing [DZ95, Cha95], that they hold for renewal, Markov-modulated and mixing stationary processes (p. 8).
  - Two streams only: N streams bring an exponential explosion in the number of overflow modes (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Lemma 5.1: by convexity of the rate functions, time-averaged controls within a region cost no more, so controls can be taken constant in each region (pp. 18–19).
  - Homogeneity: scaled versions of optimal paths remain optimal (pp. 19–20).
  - Together these leave a few piecewise-linear candidates and the finite-dimensional formulas of Theorem 9.1 (pp. 21–24, 34–35). Strict priority is the special case φ₁ ∈ {0, 1} (p. 35).
  - The GPS upper-bound proof is "quite involved" and deferred to [BPT97b] (p. 26).
  - The GLQF upper bound does not use the control formulation. It computes the exponent as the largest root of a convex function (p. 26).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation (one line)**:
  - Figure 6 plots the exponent pairs of GPS(φ₁) and GLQF(θ) for Bernoulli inputs A1 ~ Ber(0.3), A2 ~ Ber(0.2) and B ~ Ber(0.9), using the alternative formulas of Theorems 9.3 and 9.6 (pp. 41–42).
  - The dominance seen in the plot is then proved for all admissible processes (Theorem 10.5, p. 44).
  - There is no simulation of overflow, and no tool or run time is named.
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**:
  - Extends de Veciana–Kesidis and Zhang, whose GPS results assume deterministic capacity and use the departure process of a G/D/1 queue (pp. 4–5).
  - Generalizes Gail et al.'s deterministic LQF analysis to GLQF with traffic statistics (p. 4).
  - Argues that a worst-case design as in Parekh–Gallager forgoes the multiplexing gain that the most likely modes reveal (p. 25).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: four contribution bullets (p. 5); the control problem is motivated "heuristically", with rigour supplied by the sample-path LDP in a footnote (p. 17); the policy recommendation is hedged, holding "at least in the absence of fairness considerations" and for delay-insensitive traffic (pp. 5, 41).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: tight log-scale asymptotics for GPS and GLQF, the control approach, stochastic capacity, and a new policy that dominates GPS (pp. 5, 44).
  - Limits:
    - two streams only (pp. 4, 46);
    - asymptotics to first degree in the exponent only (p. 2);
    - one upper-bound proof given elsewhere (p. 26);
    - fairness and delay are not modelled (p. 41).
  - Future work: N streams (p. 46); admission control, pursued in [Pas97] (p. 4); other scheduling policies through the same formulation (p. 46).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (SD): a probability question, the overflow exponent, is written as an optimization problem. Its optimal value is the answer and its optimal solution gives the structure, the most likely modes (pp. 5, 12, 24–25). Convexity reduces it to finite-dimensional programs (pp. 18–20, 34–35). The class is convex rate-function minimization, not LP, conic or MIO. The approach is close to SW2 (probability questions as optimization problems).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: each policy family is indexed by one knob, the GPS weight φ₁ or the GLQF slope θ. The curve of class-wise exponents is published and compared across families, and dominance is proved for every admissible input, not only the plotted one (pp. 40–44). The bad case is named but not modelled: fairness and delay constraints (pp. 5, 41).
- New-pattern candidate: *asymptotic regimes for sharp constants*: the large-buffer exponent ranks the policies (pp. 12, 40–44).
- New-pattern candidate: *rare-event exponent as an optimal-control value*: a lower bound from the best scenario, a matching upper bound, and the optimal trajectory read as the dominant failure mode (pp. 12, 16–18, 24–25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Compute a large-deviations exponent as the value of a deterministic control problem: the scaled state as state, the empirical rates as controls, the integrated rate functions as cost, and a free horizon. Put the policy into the dynamics only (pp. 16–18).

### S166 · Rounding algorithms for covering problems (Mathematical Programming 1998, DOI 10.1007/bf01582131)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–27. Proof algebra garbled by OCR (pp. 5–7, 9–11, 16–19) was followed through the prose. The references were skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Rakesh Vohra (Ohio State University, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the IO line on LP-relaxation approximation (1985–1995 in the skill's table).
- The paper was received February 1994 and revised January 1996 (p. 1), and was funded by his PYI award (p. 1).
- It extends the cut-covering primal–dual work of Goemans–Williamson and Goemans et al. to general covering problems (p. 3).
- S345 (this batch) is its companion on the parsimonious property.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO; LO as the engine}:
  - Hardness results give a "unifying picture" of what cannot be approximated. Approximation algorithms, by contrast, are problem-specific, and insights rarely transfer from one problem to another (abstract, pp. 1–2).
  - Two questions frame the paper (p. 2): why can some problems be approximated better than others, and is there a systematic way to generate good, ideally best-possible, algorithms?
  - The class: covering IPs, minimize cx subject to Ax ≥ b, with A, b, c nonnegative integers and x in {0,1}ⁿ or in the nonnegative integers (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Every heuristic rounds the LP relaxation; they come in three kinds.
  - Randomized rounding with a nonlinear function (pp. 3–4): set P{xⱼ = 1} = 1 − (1 − x*ⱼ)^k, that is, flip a coin with bias x*ⱼ k times; then derandomize. Results:
    - set cover: an O(log D) bound exists, and the derandomized algorithm reaches O(log m), weaker since D ≤ m (Theorem 1, pp. 4–7);
    - general covering: O(a_max log m) (Theorem 3, pp. 10–11);
    - facility location: O(log n) directly from the standard strong formulation, with correlated rounding so that x_ij ≤ y_j always holds (Theorem 4, pp. 12–14).
  - Deterministic threshold rounding at 1/f, where f is the maximum row sum, gives the row-sum bound f for all covering problems, not only those with 0–1 coefficients (Theorem 2, pp. 8–9).
  - Dual-based rounding: set xⱼ = ⌈x*ⱼ⌉ where the jth dual constraint is tight. Results:
    - f + 1 for integer variables (Theorem 5, p. 15);
    - f for 0–1 variables, using a specially structured optimal dual (Proposition 2, Theorem 6, pp. 16–19).

    Run in reverse, the proof becomes an LP-free O(n²) algorithm that works in phases (pp. 20–22).
  - Cut covering: Conjecture 1 links random-graph thresholds to rounding guarantees (p. 24). Alon's proof of the f ≡ 1 case gives an O(log n) result for a network-design problem (p. 25).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Nonnegative integer data, with a_ij ≤ bᵢ without loss (p. 7).
  - Bounds are stated in terms of m, n, D (the maximum column sum), f (the maximum row sum) and a_max (pp. 4, 8–10).
  - Cut covering with a set function f (p. 22). For arbitrary f the LP gap is Ω(n), even with 0–1 values (pp. 22–23), so structure on f is needed.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Covering events are positively correlated, so P(all rows covered) ≥ Π P(each row covered) (p. 5).
  - The expected cost conditional on feasibility is bounded by k·Z_LP/(1 − e^−k)^D, with D the maximum column sum, and k is chosen to balance the two factors; k = log D gives Theorem 1 (pp. 5–6).
  - Derandomization uses a potential, cost + M·(number of violated rows), with M chosen by a small optimization, then the method of conditional expectations (pp. 6–7).
  - A Chernoff-type lower-tail bound for independent but not identically distributed variables (Proposition 1, pp. 9–10).
  - A "reduction" operation turns any optimal dual into one with the needed structure in at most min(m, n) steps (pp. 16–19).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation (one line)**: none; there is no computation (pp. 1–27).
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**:
  - Builds on Raghavan–Thompson's linear rounding and Goemans–Williamson's nonlinear rounding for MAXSAT (pp. 2–4), and on the dual heuristics of Hochbaum and Hall–Hochbaum (pp. 8, 14, 20).
  - Concedes that Dobson's column-sum bound, O(log m + log a_max), is stronger (p. 11).
  - Reads the primal–dual results for cut covering as instances of its own dual heuristic (p. 23).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: "in our opinion" framing (p. 2) and four design questions for the generic heuristic (p. 4). The headline outruns the body: the contribution list claims "the best possible approximation bounds for all these problems" (p. 2), the conclusion says "best known bounds for several covering problems" (p. 25), and one bound is admitted to be weaker (p. 11). The closing belief is labelled "not a formal mathematical statement" (p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: two generic rounding schemes, with bounds for set cover, general covering, facility location and unconstrained covering; an LP-free O(n²) algorithm; and the random-graph conjecture (pp. 2–3, 25).
  - Limits:
    - the dual heuristic gives no better guarantee and its analysis is "considerably more complicated" (p. 20);
    - there is no systematic way to choose the rounding function, which would require solving a calculus-of-variations problem (p. 26);
    - the randomized bound for general covering is weaker than Dobson's (p. 11);
    - Conjecture 1 is proved only for f ≡ 1 (p. 24).
  - Future work: which min-sum problems admit O(log n) guarantees, the best rounding function, and Conjecture 1 (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (IO, LO as the engine): every algorithm starts from the LP relaxation. For the dual heuristic it starts from a chosen optimal dual, whose structure drives both the proof and the algorithm (pp. 3, 14–22). The boundary is printed: an Ω(n) gap for arbitrary f (p. 22), and a weaker bound conceded (p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Nonlinear randomized rounding for covering (pp. 4–7):
  1. round xⱼ up with probability 1 − (1 − x*ⱼ)^k;
  2. bound the expected cost conditional on feasibility, using the positive correlation of covering events;
  3. derandomize with a penalty potential and conditional expectations.
- For linking constraints such as x_ij ≤ y_j, round the parent variable first and the child conditionally, so the constraint always holds (p. 12).
- Threshold rounding at 1/f, with f the maximum row sum, gives a feasible cover within a factor f of the LP (pp. 8–9).
- In a primal–dual analysis, first turn an arbitrary optimal dual into one with the structure the proof needs. Running that transformation in reverse can yield a combinatorial algorithm that needs no LP solve (pp. 16–22).
