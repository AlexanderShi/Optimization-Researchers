### S681 · Optimal Control of Fluid Restless Multi-armed Bandits: A Machine Learning Approach (Machine Learning 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10994-026-07022-0; arXiv 2502.03725)
Read level: **full**, from arXiv v2 (arXiv:2502.03725v2, 7 May 2026, p. 1).
- Read in full: pp. 1–30, including Appendix A (proofs, pp. 27–28) and Appendix B (additional experiments and the shooting method's runtimes, pp. 28–30).
- Skimmed: the references (pp. 31–35).
- Figures 1–3 (pp. 14, 20, 26) are captions only in the text file, so the robustness curve of Figure 3 is known only from the prose.
- The journal print was not read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Cheol Woo Kim (MIT Operations Research Center) and José Niño-Mora (Universidad Carlos III de Madrid); all three are marked as having contributed equally (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**:
- A 2026 return to the restless bandit with Niño-Mora, his former student and the coauthor of S051 (2000). Remark 1(b) contrasts S051's LP-based index with the new index (p. 8).
- Bertsimas supported Niño-Mora's two summer research visits to MIT's Operations Research Center, during which part of the work was done (p. 30).
- The tool is his own OCT-H (Bertsimas–Dunn 2017; their 2019 book, p. 9), run with Interpretable AI's software (p. 24). It follows Bertsimas–Kim's 2023 preprint, which applies OCT-H to fluid multiclass queueing networks (arXiv 2307.12405; pp. 3, 31).
- The arXiv identifier dates the first version to February 2025 [inferred from the identifier].
- SKILL.md cites this paper in Method 2 (step 6) and under Latest.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + ML + DA (learning to optimize); AP models as testbeds}:
  - The entry is hardness plus an incumbent whose guarantees cannot be checked.
    - Restless bandits are PSPACE-hard (Papadimitriou–Tsitsiklis), so practice relies on heuristics, chiefly Whittle's index (p. 2).
    - Whittle's index exists only for indexable models (p. 2).
    - Its asymptotic-optimality conditions are "often notoriously hard to verify" (p. 2).
  - The gap:
    - Fluid relaxations are classical for multiclass queueing networks (MCQNs). There they serve both stability analysis and policy design (p. 2).
    - Fluid restless bandits, by contrast, have been studied as mean-field limits over occupancy measures (p. 2).
    - The ODE view, models "of the MCQN type", has had limited attention. Its one exception, Larrañaga et al. (2016), treats a single model and relaxes the coupling constraint (pp. 2–3).
  - The chore: in practice the control problem is re-solved from many initial states and remaining horizons, which is prohibitive (pp. 3, 5). So the object of study is a time-dependent state-feedback policy π(x, t) (p. 6).
  - The statement, Problem (2) (p. 4):
    - maximize the reward of n projects, integrated over [0, T];
    - each project's state follows ẋᵢ = uᵢφ¹ᵢ(xᵢ) + (1 − uᵢ)φ⁰ᵢ(xᵢ);
    - controls satisfy uᵢ ∈ [0, 1] and Σuᵢ ≤ m.
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Pontryagin's principle (PMP) reduces each instantaneous control choice to an LP (p. 7). The LP's dual gives a priority-index rule: full effort goes to at most m projects, those with the highest nonnegative indices γᵢ(t). So an extremal control is binary and piecewise constant (Proposition 1, pp. 7–8).
  - On each constant-control piece, state and costate have closed forms (pp. 10–11). A shooting method therefore searches only over the initial costate (pp. 11–13).
  - Many solves from sampled initial states produce (x, t) → u data. OCT-H imitates this map, "imitation learning" in reinforcement-learning terms (p. 3; Algorithm 3, p. 18).
  - Features come from the analysis.
    - Control switches occur where two indices tie or one reaches zero: the switching curves (p. 14).
    - Within one piece, eliminating the costate writes γᵢ as an affine combination of a few state terms (Propositions 2–3, pp. 15–16).
    - The authors call it a heuristic to treat these terms as global features (pp. 14–15).
    - Terms that the observed controls never trigger are pruned (Algorithm 2, pp. 16–17).
  - Relative to Whittle's index and S051's LP index:
    - kept: the priority-index form;
    - changed: the indices come from the joint state–costate trajectories of the full system, so they are coupled and time-dependent, with no time-averaged relaxation and no indexability required (Remark 1(b), p. 8).
  - Relative to Bertsimas–Kim (2023):
    - kept: OCT-H imitation;
    - changed: the problem class, plus the structure-derived features (pp. 3, 13).
  - Why trees:
    - optimal continuous-time policies are often piecewise constant, which hyperplane splits can represent (p. 3);
    - deep networks are set aside as black boxes that do not exploit the problem's structure (p. 13).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Two classes are treated (p. 5):
    - affine dynamics with affine rewards;
    - quadratic dynamics without intercept, with affine rewards.
  - Scope choices:
    - The intercept is dropped to keep the closed forms (p. 10).
    - Quadratic rewards are called straightforward but not treated (p. 11).
    - The approach needs two ingredients: closed-form state and costate, and costates affine in state-and-time primitives (p. 5).
  - Assumptions admitted for tractability:
    - State constraints are dropped because the chosen dynamics keep states in range (forward invariance, p. 5). This is proved for each model (Proposition 4, p. 19; Propositions 6–8, pp. 27–28).
    - Chattering is assumed away: finitely many switches (Remark 1(a), p. 8; p. 12).
    - Parameters are fixed (p. 27).
  - The data are synthetic. Three textbook models are used, each with parameters drawn uniformly from stated ranges (pp. 21–23):
    - machine maintenance (Kamien–Schwartz 1971; affine; p. 21);
    - SIS epidemic control (Pollett 2024; quadratic; the ranges follow Pollett's examples; p. 22);
    - logistic fisheries (Schaefer 1957; Gordon 1991; quadratic; pp. 22–23).
  - Sizes: n ∈ {5, 10} projects, T ∈ {1, 5} and m = ⌊0.3n⌋ (p. 23; Tables 1–3, p. 25). There is no real data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - The index rule: the PMP conditions (Lemma 1, p. 7) give a per-instant LP (6), whose dual yields the rule (Proposition 1 and proof, pp. 7–8). Remark 1 notes that PMP extremals can chatter even under affine dynamics (p. 8).
  - A negative result, proved and printed (pp. 8–9):
    - the derived Hamiltonian is a pointwise maximum of affine functions (hence convex), or of concave quadratics (not concave in general);
    - so Sethi's concavity condition fails, and PMP extremals cannot be called optimal;
    - local optimality would need second-order conditions, which are not pursued.
  - Closed-form state and costate on constant-control intervals (eq. (8), p. 10; eq. (10), p. 11).
  - Shooting: the condition g(y₀) = y(T) = 0 is solved by Broyden's derivative-free quasi-Newton update (eq. (11), p. 12; Algorithm 1, p. 13).
    - A new interval starts when the index ranking changes (p. 11).
    - The closed forms remove ODE-discretization error, the usual source of instability in shooting (Remark 2, p. 12).
  - Feature derivation:
    - Within an interval, the costate is written case by case in xᵢ. The binary controls unify the cases (eq. (13)), and the result is substituted into γᵢ.
    - Affine case: the terms t, xᵢ, xᵢ² and 1/(xᵢ + αᵘᵢ/βᵘᵢ) for u ∈ {0, 1} (Proposition 2, p. 15).
    - Quadratic case: xᵢ, 1/xᵢ and the same two reciprocal terms (Proposition 3, p. 16).
  - A worked case with a closed form: admission and routing to parallel infinite-server queues.
    - The index is γᵢ(t) = R − Cᵢ(λ/μᵢ)(1 − e^(−μᵢ(T−t))) (Proposition 5, pp. 19–20).
    - For the stated parameters, the policy switches at t = 10 − log 9 ≈ 7.802 (eq. (18), p. 20).
  - Not found in pp. 1–30: a convergence result for the shooting method, a learning or generalization guarantee, or a bound on the policy's suboptimality.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Pipeline:
    - 3,000 sampled initial states are solved by Algorithm 1. Ten state–time–control points are taken from each constant-control interval, with ε = 10⁻⁵ and δ = 10⁻⁴ (p. 23).
    - OCT-H comes from Interpretable AI, with depth tuned over {5, 10, 15}, on a MacBook Pro (M2 Pro, 16 GB) (p. 24).
  - Metrics (pp. 23–24):
    - accuracy on 1,000 held-out state–time points;
    - PMP-Gap: (extremal objective − policy objective) / |policy objective|, computed on discretized dynamics, maximum over 100 test instances;
    - speed-up: shooting time from scratch over tree inference time (inference takes milliseconds), averaged over 100 instances and over the initializations that converged;
    - OCT-H training time, excluding feature augmentation and data generation.
  - Results (Tables 1–4, pp. 25–26; observations pp. 24–25):
    - accuracy ≥ 0.98;
    - maximum PMP-Gap ≤ 0.0181, the worst case being machine maintenance with n = 10, T = 5;
    - speed-ups from 47,625 to 2.65 × 10⁷ (epidemic, n = 10, T = 5), growing with n and T;
    - training from 2:23 to 143:20 (min:s);
    - 13–37 features and 2–22 distinct control vectors.
  - Checks:
    - The closed-form case comes first: the tree learned from 1,000 sampled initial states is "almost identical" to the closed-form policy (p. 20).
    - A uniqueness probe: with three random costate initializations per instance, the trajectories were identical or the run failed to converge (p. 21).
    - Robustness to parameter error: on fisheries with n = 10, T = 1, parameters are perturbed within ±Γ. The maximum PMP-Gap grows with Γ but stays below 5% for small perturbations (pp. 24–25).
    - On machine maintenance (n = 10, T = 1), three other parameter ranges give accuracy 1.00 and zero gap (Table B1, pp. 28–29).
  - Limits printed:
    - Some shooting runs failed, with no count given (p. 21).
    - The shooting runtime climbs fast: at T = 5, epidemic control takes 1,547 s for n = 10 and 2,880 s for n = 12 (Tables B3–B4, p. 30).
  - Not compared: Whittle's index, S051's LP index, Larrañaga et al.'s fluid index, reinforcement-learning methods or a neural network, though all are named (pp. 2–3, 8, 13). The only comparator is the uncertified extremal that the tree imitates (p. 23).
  - Not reported: the offline cost, since data generation is excluded from the training time (p. 24).
    - From Table B3, 3,000 solves of the epidemic case with n = 10, T = 5 would take about 4.6 × 10⁶ s, some 54 days of sequential computing, before the first query is answered.
    - This is my own arithmetic. It assumes the test-instance average applies and one converged initialization per instance.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) When the Hamiltonian is linear in a bounded control with a budget coupling Σuᵢ ≤ m, write the pointwise maximization as an LP and read the top-m index rule off its dual (pp. 7–8).
- (D4) Before writing "optimal", test the standard sufficiency condition (concavity of the derived Hamiltonian) on your model class. If it fails, prove the failure and call the solutions extremal (pp. 8–9).
- (D4) In a shooting method for bang-bang controls, propagate state and costate in closed form between switches, and start a new interval when the index ranking changes. This keeps discretization error out of the root-finding (pp. 10–12).
- (D2) To let a linear-split tree learn curved switching boundaries:
  - derive the switching index's functional form within one interval;
  - add exactly those terms as features;
  - prune the terms that the observed control patterns never activate (pp. 14–17).
- (D5) Validate the learning pipeline first on a model whose optimal policy has a closed form, and check that the tree recovers the switching time (pp. 17–20).
- (D5) Report imitation accuracy and the decision-level gap on fresh instances side by side. Say what the speed-up and training-time figures exclude (pp. 23–24).
- (D5) For uncertified solutions, rerun the root-finder from several random initial guesses and report whether the runs agree or fail (p. 21).
- (D5) Test for misspecification: draw each test parameter uniformly within ±Γ of its training value, and plot the worst gap against Γ (p. 24).

### S914 · A General Purpose Local Search Algorithm for Binary Optimization (working paper n.d., no DOI or arXiv)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–28, the whole paper including the references (p. 28).
- The appendix (pp. 25–28) continues the toy example step by step and was read as a trace.
- The extraction flattens binomial coefficients and norms in the displayed bounds, so they were read through the prose.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Dan Iancu and Dmitriy Katz (MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1). SKILL.md lists Iancu in his RO student lineage. · **Version and date**:
- An undated author PDF from his homepage's Optimization folder (the URL in works.json).
- Its newest reference is from 2007 and its benchmark is CPLEX 9.1 (pp. 1, 22, 28), so it was written in 2007 or later [inferred].
- It shares its three authors and a close title with S328 (*A new local search algorithm for binary optimization*, INFORMS J. Computing 25(2), 2013, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1110.0496). The index has no open text for S328, so how the published version differs is unknown.

**Position in trajectory**: an HS work.
- It sits between the 1993 simulated-annealing review (S005), which found the method weak in theory, and his group's use of simulated annealing as a working tool from 2009 (Tension T2).
- SKILL.md's Heuristic 3 cites the IJOC version as its local-search fallback.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + HS}:
  - The entry is the success, and the limits, of the exact codes.
    - Major codes (CPLEX, "EXPRESS") now solve binary problems that "a decade ago were out of reach" (p. 1).
    - The credit goes to computing speed, to cutting planes and to heuristics, and the authors judge cutting planes the larger contributor (p. 1).
    - Yet "especially dense" binary problems remain unsolved (p. 2).
    - Practice wants feasible solutions that improve on current practice, even without a proof of optimality, and few general-purpose methods exist (p. 2).
  - The problem: max c′x subject to Ax ≤ b, x ∈ {0, 1}ⁿ, with integer data (eq. (1), p. 1). The method must use no special structure (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Search the one-flip neighbours, keeping *interesting* solutions as well as feasible ones (pp. 3–4). A solution is interesting when three conditions hold:
    - (A1) it violates no constraint by more than one unit;
    - (A2) its trace (the violation vector plus the loose-constraint indicator) lies within ℓ₁-distance Q of the incumbent's trace;
    - (A3) it beats every examined solution whose trace hashes to the same box.
  - Each improvement of the incumbent clears the list and the boxes (Algorithm 1, p. 5).
  - Q is the neighbourhood depth, an explicit dial between quality and run time (p. 2).
  - The toy example shows the search passing, under this control, through infeasible and non-improving solutions (pp. 5–6, 25–28).
  - Hurkens–Schrijver's set-packing bound is a by-product of a theorem about set packing. This method is general-purpose and comes close to that bound (pp. 2, 15).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The model: pure binary programs with integer A, b and c (p. 1). The run-time bound is pseudo-polynomial in ‖c‖₁ (p. 2).
  - The guarantee needs unweighted w-set packing with exactly w ones per column, a FIFO list and an injective h (pp. 9–10).
  - Admitted for practicality: hashing and a capped list, which forfeit the guarantee (pp. 16–18).
  - The data are random instances from the authors' own generator script; no library instances are used.
    - Set covering: m = 1,000 and n = 2,500, with w = 3, 5 or random in 3–7 ones per column, unweighted and with weights in [400, 500] (p. 23).
    - One larger set-covering instance: m = 4,000 and n = 10,000 (pp. 23–24).
    - Set packing: m = 1,000 and n = 2,500 (p. 24).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1, the run time: O(‖c‖₁² · n · C(2m, Q) · max(m, n)) for fixed Q and an injective h (p. 6). This is pseudo-polynomial in general and strongly polynomial when c = e (p. 9).
  - Lemma 1, a counting device (p. 7):
    - the traces of interesting solutions are binary 2m-vectors within distance Q of the incumbent's (eq. (4));
    - each trace box can improve at most ‖c‖₁ + 1 times, because objective values are integers in a bounded range.
  - Lemma 2, incremental evaluation: Ay − b = Ax − b ± Aᵢ gives each neighbour's trace in O(m) (pp. 7–8).
  - Theorem 2: Z*/Z_H ≤ w²/(2w − 1) for Q = w², w > 1, with a FIFO list and an injective h (p. 10). The proof has two parts.
    - Lemma 3 describes a poor local optimum (pp. 10–12). The optimum's variables share no constraint pairwise (13). Counting tight constraints gives R > Z*/w (14). Swap pairs (vᵢ, pᵢ) exist (15), and so does an improving exchange (16).
    - Lemma 4 shows that the FIFO search reaches that exchange (pp. 13–14). Each solution gets a "generation", and solutions are examined in increasing generation. If Q ≥ T·w, a solution T generations along the exchange path is in the list. The exchange needs T = w, hence Q = w² (pp. 14–15).
    - The bound is slightly weaker than Hurkens–Schrijver's w/2 + ε (p. 15).
  - Implementation costs, analysed:
    - with an injective h, memory is O(n · C(2m, Q)); solutions are bit arrays and traces sparse arrays (p. 16);
    - with N_TB boxes and N_H hash functions, the run time becomes O(‖c‖₁² · N_TB · n · max(m, n·N_H)), and the guarantee is lost (eq. (25), pp. 17–18);
    - a priority queue adds O(log N_TB) per operation but is reported to cut wall-clock time (p. 21).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup:
    - The opponent is CPLEX 9.1 (pp. 1, 22), with the same 6 GB of memory as the heuristic (p. 23).
    - The machine is a dual-core Intel Xeon 5050 (3.00 GHz) with 8 GB of RAM, running Ubuntu (p. 22).
    - The heuristic's parameters are fixed at N_H = 2, N₁ = 2, N₂ = 2 and N_r = 5 (p. 21).
  - Staged runs, each starting from the previous output, with the times summed (p. 22):
    - Q = 4 with 10 MB, then 50 MB;
    - Q = 6 with 100 MB, then 250 MB;
    - Q = 10 with 1 GB, then 2 GB;
    - two final 6 GB runs, with Q = 15 and Q = 20.
  - Checkpoints: 5, 10 and 20 hours, and 10, 20, 100 and 200 hours for the large instance (Tables 1–5, pp. 23–24).
  - At 20 hours, by my tally of the 14 comparisons in Tables 1–5, the heuristic is better in 9, CPLEX in 3, and 2 are ties (pp. 23–24). CPLEX wins:
    - unweighted covering with w = 5 (229 against 231, at every checkpoint);
    - weighted packing with w = 3 (147,877 against 147,604);
    - the large weighted covering instance (393,540 against 432,562).
  - Two cases go against the headline:
    - On weighted packing with w = 3 the order reverses over time: the heuristic leads at 5 hours, CPLEX at 10 and 20 hours (Table 5, p. 24).
    - The large weighted instance needs 100 hours before the heuristic leads (367,516 against 381,087; Table 3, p. 24).
  - The text's verdict: the heuristic outperforms CPLEX "after approximately 20 hours (and in some cases earlier)", while CPLEX has an edge at 1–2 hours (p. 23). It rests on "the eight examples presented here and several other runs" (p. 23).
  - Not reported (pp. 2, 22–24):
    - CPLEX's settings, bounds or gaps;
    - the starting solution z₀;
    - run-to-run variation;
    - any comparison with the heuristics the paper cites (feasibility pump, pivot-and-complement, pivot-cut-and-dive).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The frame is the success of exact MIP codes, with cutting planes ranked above heuristics (p. 1).
  - The paper places itself among the few general-purpose binary heuristics (p. 2):
    - lift-and-project (Balas–Ceria–Cornuéjols 1993);
    - pivot-and-complement (Balas–Martin 1980);
    - the feasibility pump (Fischetti–Glover–Lodi 2005);
    - pivot-cut-and-dive (Eckstein–Nediak 2007);
    - Aarts–Lenstra (1997) as the review.
  - Hurkens–Schrijver (1989) is credited with a slightly stronger bound, but one specific to set packing (pp. 2, 15).
  - The reference list has eight entries (p. 28). None is on simulated annealing, not even his own 1993 review with Tsitsiklis (S005).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The five-item contribution list opens with "genuinely general purpose". It ends with the CPLEX comparison, flagged "Most importantly", as in the abstract (pp. 1–2).
  - A three-variable set-packing instance is traced through every step and finished in the appendix (pp. 5–6, 25–28).
  - Hedges: "we believe" (p. 1), "very encouraging results" (p. 2), and "practical promise" shown in "preliminary computational tests" (p. 25).
  - Slips:
    - the set-packing text says w = 3, but Tables 4–5 have rows for w = 3, 5 and 3–7 (p. 24);
    - the introduction motivates "especially dense" problems (p. 2), but the implementation is sparse (p. 16) and the conclusion targets "large, sparse" problems (p. 25).
  - The headline "after approximately 20 hours" generalizes over exceptions that the paper's own tables print (pp. 2, 23–24): Tension T6.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: an algorithm, a complexity bound, an approximation guarantee and a computational comparison (pp. 2, 25).
  - The limits are concrete:
    - the guarantee does not survive the hashed implementation (p. 18);
    - CPLEX leads on short runs (p. 23);
    - without hashing, memory grows as n·C(2m, Q) (p. 16);
    - the tests are preliminary and use random instances (p. 25).
  - Future directions: none stated (p. 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Let the local search store solutions with bounded infeasibility, indexed by their signature of violated and loose constraints, and keep the best objective for each signature. Clear the store whenever the incumbent improves (pp. 3–5).
- (D4) To prove a local search pseudo-polynomial, count the signatures reachable within the depth bound. Multiply by the number of distinct integer objective values that each signature's record can take (Lemma 1, p. 7).
- (D4) For an approximation ratio of a local search, use two lemmas (pp. 10–15):
  - characterize any poor local optimum and exhibit an improving exchange (Lemma 3);
  - prove that the search order (FIFO by generation) reaches it within the depth budget (Lemma 4).
- (D4) Update the constraint slacks incrementally (Ax − b ± Aᵢ), so that each neighbour costs O(m) (p. 8).
- (D5) Run a heuristic in stages of rising depth and memory, each warm-started by the previous output, and report the total wall-clock time (p. 22).
- (D5) Duel a commercial solver with matched memory at several wall-clock checkpoints. Print every checkpoint, including those the solver wins (pp. 23–24).
- (D7) Trace a three-variable instance through every step of the algorithm, with the full trace in an appendix (pp. 5–6, 25–28).
