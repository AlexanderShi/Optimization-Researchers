### S083 · Where to locate COVID‐19 mass vaccination facilities? (Naval Research Logistics 2022, DOI 10.1002/nav.22007; arXiv 2102.07309)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v2 (arXiv:2102.07309v2 [q-bio.PE], 18 Jul 2021, stamp on p. 1): main text pp. 1–30 in full; appendix Tables 3–6 (pp. 34–37) read; references (pp. 30–34) skimmed. Figures 5–9 read from their labels, captions and the text that reports them. The NRL print was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: V. Digalakis Jr., A. Jacquillat, M. L. Li, A. Previero (MIT Sloan School of Management and Operations Research Center, p. 1); feedback from T. Greg McKelvey Jr. acknowledged (p. 30) · **Position in trajectory**: a COVID-19 item of the 2019–2026 health-care period {AP DA}. It extends the group's DELPHI forecaster (Li, Tazi Bouardi, Skali Lami, Trikalinos, Trichakis and Bertsimas, medRxiv 2020; pp. 2, 6–8, 33) and calibrates seven DELPHI parameters from the clinical-outcomes database of the group's "From predictions to prescriptions" paper (p. 7; reference on p. 31). It also cites his influenza-vaccine-composition paper with Bandi (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + DA; IO for site selection; HS for the simulate–optimize loop}: The entry is a live decision with a named owner. The new Biden administration was opening federal mass vaccination sites, which raised the question of where to put them (abstract, p. 1; p. 2), and politics requires at least one site per state (p. 2). Two gaps are named. The operational vaccine literature centres on predictable epidemics such as seasonal influenza and is "not readily applicable to a new disease such as COVID-19" (p. 4). Methodologically, epidemic models have been wrapped in scenario analysis, enumeration, simulation or simple heuristics, apart from a chance-constrained model (Tanner et al. 2008) and a two-stage stochastic one (Yarmand et al. 2014); "In contrast, this paper directly embeds SEIR dynamics into an optimization model to support vaccine distribution" (p. 5). Problem: choose N = 100 sites among the 500 most populous cities, then allocate doses across sites, states and age groups, to minimize deaths over February–April 2021 (pp. 6, 11, 18). Left out by choice: pharmacies and other small sites, for lack of public data (p. 3), and the political, economic and social considerations (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Embed the predictor in the prescriptive model. DELPHI is an SEIR extension with 11 states, under-detection, a policy-response function γ(t) and a declining mortality rate m(t) (pp. 6–7). It becomes DELPHI–V, with age risk classes and four vaccination states S′, E′, I′ and M (pp. 8–10). Its ODEs, discretized by forward differences, become constraints of DELPHI–V–OPT, a location–allocation model with three objectives: deaths, people still exposed at the horizon (so that doses are not wasted late in the horizon), and population-weighted distance (pp. 11–14). The solver is a coordinate descent with two modules. Simulate runs the discretized ODEs for a fixed allocation in O(MKT) operations. Optimize fixes the infected totals Î at their simulated values, which makes the bilinear constraints (28)–(31) linear. The loop stops when the relative change of the objective falls below ε = 0.1% (pp. 16–17, Algorithm 1; p. 21). Three interpretable baselines that satisfy all constraints serve both as starting points and as benchmarks (pp. 15–18). Changed against the closest work: optimization replaces the enumeration or simulation of candidate strategies (p. 5). Kept: DELPHI's fitted parameters (p. 18). A second, smaller bilinear program reconciles the two mortality data sources (pp. 18–21).
- **D3 Model and data**: Class: a nonconvex mixed-binary program, bilinear in the infection terms, with binary site variables x and binary county-to-site assignments W (pp. 12–15, eq. (39)). Data: DELPHI fitted per state on New York Times case and death counts; census age breakdowns; CDC cases and deaths by age to the end of January 2021 (pp. 18–19, Figure 4); 51 "states", 3,006 counties and six age groups (p. 18); base values of 90% effectiveness and 1 million doses a day (p. 21). Assumptions the authors flag:
  - vaccinated people can still transmit, assumed "conservatively" and relaxed in §6.2 (pp. 8, 27);
  - vaccine effects are instantaneous, all-or-nothing and single-dose (p. 9);
  - r^I, r^d and r^D are uniform across risk classes (p. 12); the robustness test then varies infection rates by age (p. 27);
  - one representative vaccine of average effectiveness, justified by assuming the same vaccine mix at every site (p. 12);
  - people are vaccinated only in their own state (called conservative), but doses can move between sites within a state (called optimistic) (p. 14).

  Adopted without comment: the initial susceptible, exposed and infected populations are split across age classes in proportion to class size (p. 18), and recovery states are dropped as irrelevant to deaths (p. 9). Not stated: the values of λE, λD, θS, ΘL, θV and θP, beyond "small values" for the objective weights (p. 14) and "tight values" for the fairness parameters (p. 23).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Embedding device: the discretized dynamics enter as one-sided inequalities (28)–(36), with equalities only for deaths and immunity (37)–(38) (pp. 13–14). [my reading] The paper does not discuss whether these inequalities bind at the optimum.
  - Intractability argued by counting: 2m(K + 1)T nonconvex constraints with 2(K + 1) bilinear terms each, so 4mT(K + 1)² terms in all, "nearly 900,000" for 51 states, 6 age groups and 90 days (p. 15) [my computation: 899,640]. Gurobi 9.0's nonconvex quadratic solver is judged "limited to small-scale instances" (p. 15). No timed attempt on (P) at any size is reported (pp. 15–29).
  - Recovering a solvable class by alternation: fix the coupling term at its simulated value, so each Optimize step is linear (p. 16). [my reading] The text calls this step "a linear program" solved by barrier (pp. 16, 21), yet (P) keeps x and W binary (eq. (39), p. 14). It does not say whether the binaries are relaxed or the step is a MILP.
  - No convergence or quality guarantee. The conclusion concedes that the discretization and the coordinate descent "do not yield theoretical guarantees on solution quality" (p. 30).
  - Data reconciliation. Reference rates (40) keep the CDC ratios between age classes and rescale them to DELPHI's regional mean (p. 20). A per-region program (41)–(48) then fits rates by region, age and day, with a bilinear identity m·X = D (44) and monotonicity in time (45) and across risk classes (46). Decoupling by region lets Gurobi 9.0's nonconvex solver reach a 1% gap "within minutes" (pp. 20–21; Table 1).
  - Baselines as small integer programs: population-based and case-based apportionment minimize the absolute deviation between share × N and the site count y_j, subject to Σ y_j = 100 and y_j ≥ 1 (pp. 17–18).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Gurobi 9.0 on a 2.3 GHz 4-core processor; barrier with tolerance 10⁻⁶; Δt = 1 day, chosen "After extensive experimentations"; ε = 0.1% (p. 21).
  - Main comparison, lives saved against no vaccination (Table 2, p. 23): top-cities 19,045 (base); population-based 19,709 (+3.5%); case-based 21,037 (+10.5%); optimized locations with uniform allocation 23,622 (+24.0%); fully optimized 25,615 (+34.5%); proposed, with fairness constraints, 23,000 (+20.8%).
  - [my computation] The headline of 20% and 4,000 lives (pp. 1, 3, 24, 30) is measured against the weakest benchmark. The proposed solution gains about 9.3% over the case-based benchmark (23,000 against 21,037) and about 16.7% over the population-based one. Against the fully optimized solution, the fairness constraints cost 2,615 lives, about 10% of its benefit. The text reports both rows but never states this difference (pp. 23–24).
  - Equity by state (Figure 6, pp. 24–25): against the population-based benchmark, Texas gains 1,450 lives, New York 440, Florida 380 and Iowa 290, and no state loses more than 100. Pennsylvania's 85 extra deaths are called "well within the margin of error of DELPHI–V" (p. 25). [my reading] That margin is given nowhere in the paper.
  - Robustness protocol (pp. 26–29). Each perturbed model evaluates three solutions: the top-cities benchmark, a re-optimized solution, and the proposed solution with its sites fixed and its allocations re-optimized.
    - Effectiveness 0.8–0.95 and budget 0.5–2 million doses a day (Figure 7): the proposed solution matches the re-optimized one in all but one case; at 500,000 doses it is within 2% (p. 27).
    - Age-dependent infection rates, and no transmission from vaccinated people: fewer than 50 extra deaths (p. 27).
    - Fifty scenarios with independent ±20% uniform perturbations per state: gains of 12.5–30% under perturbed infection rates and 17.5–25% under perturbed mortality rates (p. 28; Figure 9).
  - Not reported [my reading after the full read]: run times or iteration counts of the coordinate descent, although the contributions promise "short runtimes" (p. 4); any bound or gap for (P); the λ and θ values, and the sensitivity analyses on them announced on p. 21.
  - Field check (pp. 25, 34–37): FEMA opened 24 sites in 13 states instead of 100 (Table 4). Within those 13 states, the realized and proposed allocations lie on the same side of the population share in 9 states for sites and 7 for doses (Tables 5–6). The text calls this agreement "in the majority of cases" (p. 36).
  - [my reading] Internal inconsistency: the text gives the four largest states "6 to 10" sites each (p. 22), matching Figure 5's largest labels (10, 8, 6, 6). Appendix Table 3 instead lists Texas 11, California 8, Florida 6 and New York 5 (p. 35), and Table 3 sums to 100 sites [my computation].
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in two literatures: OR on vaccine supply chains (composition, production, stockpile allocation, delivery; p. 4, including Bandi–Bertsimas 2020), and the epidemiology of allocation across risk classes and regions (p. 5). Prior work is characterized by its method (simulation, enumeration, simple heuristics), not criticized directly (p. 5). Three advances are claimed: allocation across regions and risk classes with data-driven rates; a COVID-specific SEIR model; and formal optimization "as opposed to relying on enumeration, simulation or simplified heuristics" (p. 5). Closest works: Matrajt et al. 2020 (whom to vaccinate first within one location) and Grauer et al. 2020 (spatiotemporal strategies tested by SEIR simulation) (p. 5). DELPHI's credentials are the group's own: inclusion in the CDC ensemble, use in choosing Johnson & Johnson's Phase III trial sites (p. 2), and a "mean average percentage error among the lowest of the CDC ensemble forecast" (p. 8). [cross-reference] The skill's Method 5 checkpoint cites DELPHI's relative WIS of 0.99 against the naive baseline's 1.00 in the field's own metric [05 §5.2]; this paper reports MAPE only.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2, D4) To embed a bilinear dynamic model in a planning model, alternate: simulate the dynamics under the current decision; fix the coupling factor (here the infected totals) at its simulated trajectory; solve the resulting linear model; repeat until the relative change of the objective is below a tolerance. Start from a feasible, interpretable rule (pp. 16–17, 21).
- (D2) Build every baseline so that it satisfies all of the model's constraints; each then serves as both initialization and benchmark (pp. 17–18).
- (D3) Add a small end-of-horizon penalty on people still exposed, so that a death-minimizing model with a finite horizon does not waste doses near its end (p. 11).
- (D4) Reconcile a granular but aggregated source with a coarse but reliable one by an optimization that keeps the ratios of one and the totals of the other, under monotonicity constraints. Decouple it by region so that a global nonconvex solver reaches a stated gap (pp. 19–21).
- (D5) Test robustness with three arms per perturbation: the benchmark re-evaluated; the proposed solution with strategic decisions fixed and tactical ones re-optimized; a full re-optimization. The gap between the last two is the cost of committing now (pp. 26–29).
- (D5) Put the unconstrained and the fair solutions in the same table, so that the price of the fairness constraints can be read off (Table 2, p. 23).
- (D5) After deployment, compare realized and proposed allocations state by state, by the sign of their deviation from the population share (Tables 5–6, pp. 35–37).

### S092 · Online mixed-integer optimization in milliseconds (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2022, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2022.1181; arXiv 1907.02206)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v4 (arXiv:1907.02206v4 [math.OC], 23 Mar 2021; dated March 24, 2021 on p. 1): pp. 1–36 in full; references (pp. 37–42) skimmed. Figures 2–5 and 7–8 read from their captions, axis labels and the text that reports them; their values are not tabulated. Neither v1 nor the IJOC print was read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Bartolomeo Stellato (p. 1, no affiliations printed; computations on Princeton Research Computing resources, p. 36; a former postdoc of Bertsimas according to the skill's note 04) · **Position in trajectory**: the second paper of the Bertsimas–Stellato "Voice of Optimization" line ([BS21], Machine Learning 2021). [BS21] aimed at interpretability, this paper aims at speed, and a robotics application followed ([CCS+20], p. 8). It is the source paper the skill cites for Heuristic 5.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + IO}: The entry is a standing belief plus a gap. MIO "is still considered harder to solve than convex optimization and, therefore, it is more rarely applied to online settings" (p. 2), although its computational power grew "over a trillion times" in 25 years according to [Bix10] (p. 2). Online optimization must answer within hard time limits on limited hardware. But only the parameters change between solves, so data from earlier solves can be reused (p. 2). Each rival line is faulted for one gap:
  - learning to branch and reinforcement learning ignore the parametric nature of the problems (pp. 5–6);
  - active-set learning for convex QPs either does not predict the sets [MRN19] or has no probabilistic guarantee and is tailored to MPC [KKK19] (p. 6);
  - embedded solvers (CVXGEN, OSQP, qpOASES, ECOS) handle only continuous convex problems (pp. 6–7);
  - explicit hybrid MPC needs memory exponential in the dimension (p. 7);
  - heuristics (feasibility pump, ADMM with rounding) do not use the data of repeated solves (p. 7);
  - warm-starting branch-and-bound can fail to help, for three listed reasons (pp. 7–8).

  Statement: for parametric MIQO, learn the map from parameters to the optimal "strategy", so that the online step is one NN evaluation plus one prefactorized linear solve (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A strategy is the set of tight constraints plus the optimal integer values, s(θ) = (T(θ), x*_I(θ)) (pp. 9–10). Given the strategy, the reduced problem is continuous, convex and smaller (p. 10). For MIQO it is an equality-constrained QP, solved through its KKT system (pp. 17–18). Changes from [BS21] (pp. 3–4): MIQO in place of general mixed-integer convex problems; fast but non-interpretable feedforward NNs in place of optimal classification trees; no convex solve online; KKT factorizations cached offline per strategy; strategy pruning; a flops count. Online: rank strategies with the NN, decode the top k in parallel, and keep the feasible one with the best objective (pp. 12, 18, 21). Offline: sample 100,000 parameters, solve each with Gurobi to high accuracy, and read off the tight constraints; check a Good-Turing stopping rule; prune; train with depth, width, learning rate, batch size and epochs tuned by Optuna (pp. 13, 20, 22). Software: MLOPT, on GitHub (p. 20).
- **D3 Model and data**: Class: parametric MIQO (8) with P positive semidefinite; MILO is the case P = 0 (p. 17). Caching applies only when θ enters q and b, not P or A (p. 18), which rules it out for the portfolio example (p. 28). Assumptions: LICQ bounds the number of tight constraints by n; without it, they are in practice still far fewer than m (p. 9). Tight constraints can be imposed as equalities only when g is linear in x (p. 10). The Good-Turing bound treats the samples as independent draws (p. 12). [my reading] The nonsingularity of the KKT matrix (10) is not discussed, though LICQ may fail (p. 9).
  - Fuel-cell energy management: parameters from [FDM15] and a random load profile; samples drawn uniformly in balls of radius 0.5 around a 10,000-step closed-loop trajectory (pp. 23–25).
  - Sparse portfolio trading: S&P 100 data 2008–2013 and a 15-factor risk model; return forecasts are realized returns plus noise, and the authors say they "are not real" (pp. 27–28); samples within 0.001 times the parameter magnitude (p. 28).
  - 2-D motion planning with obstacles: T = 60 and τ = 0.1 s; initial positions uniform in the box (pp. 31–32).

  Splits: 80% training and 20% validation; 10,000 test samples; tolerances ε_inf = ε_sub = 10⁻⁴ (pp. 20, 22–23).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Strategy encoding: once T(θ) is known, every other constraint is redundant at the optimum; with x_I fixed as well, the reduced problem (3) is convex and continuous (pp. 9–10).
  - Decoding: the KKT system (10) has size q = n + |T(θ)| + d (11). A sparse permuted LDLᵀ factorization costs O(q³) and each solve O(q²); MINRES is the alternative, at O(q²) per step (pp. 17–18).
  - Matrix caching: one offline factorization per strategy seen in training, so online decoding is forward–backward substitution (p. 18).
  - Complexity (12): O(n_M n_{L−1} + (n + |T(θ)| + d)²), independent of the parameter values and free of any cube of the dimensions, so cheaper than one dense factorization (p. 19). [my reading] (12) counts one decoded strategy. Decoding the top k and checking their feasibility and objective repeats k times (in parallel) and is not in (12), although the authors say strategy evaluation is "almost all" of MLOPT's measured time (pp. 18, 21).
  - Missing-strategies bound (Theorem 4.1, imported from [BS21]): with confidence 1 − β, the probability of an unseen strategy is at most G + c√((1/N) ln(3/β)), where G is the Good-Turing estimator and c = 2√2 + √3. Sampling stops when this bound falls below ε (pp. 12–13). The paper proves no new theorem.
  - Strategy pruning as a MILO (7): minimize the number of strategies kept so that every sample's reassigned cost is within a relative ε of its optimum (p. 14). The MILO is ruled out by size: 100,000 samples times 2,000 strategies means 200 million reduced problems, and "millions of binary variables" (p. 14). A frequency heuristic replaces it, halving α until all reassigned samples meet the tolerance (Algorithms 2–3, pp. 14–16). On small problems the heuristic "always" gives a similar number of strategies to the MILO, by the authors' account, with no numbers shown (p. 15).
  - No online guarantee [my reading]: feasibility and near-optimality rest on the coverage bound and on measured test statistics (pp. 12–13, 22–23).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Platform: Princeton's PICSciE facility, with 16 Broadwell e5-2680v4 cores for data collection and an NVIDIA P100 GPU for training; online, both methods run on CPUs (p. 22). Not stated: the Gurobi version (only the 2020 reference manual is cited, pp. 21, 40) and the online thread counts, both for Gurobi and for MLOPT's parallel top-k evaluation.
  - Baselines: Gurobi warm-started from the previous parameter's solution, run at default settings and in a "heuristic" mode (TimeLimit = 1 s, MIPFocus = 1) (p. 21). [my reading] The order of the test parameters, which decides what the warm start reuses, is not stated.
  - Metrics: normalized infeasibility; relative suboptimality against Gurobi's optimum; and an accuracy that counts a prediction as correct when it is feasible within 10⁻⁴ and suboptimal by less than 10⁻⁴, whatever its strategy label (pp. 22–23). Average and maximum times are reported.
  - Maximum times (Tables 1–3, pp. 25, 29, 35):

    | Example | MLOPT | Gurobi | Gurobi heuristic |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Fuel cell, T = 10–60 | 0.0096–0.0130 s | 0.0089–114.81 s | 0.0097–1.87 s |
    | Portfolio, c = 10–50 | 0.0113–0.0137 s | 19.67–2,529.39 s | 1.47–2.37 s |
    | Motion planning, 2–10 obstacles | 0.0263–0.2213 s | 3.53–809.34 s | 1.01–1.05 s |

    In motion planning the Gurobi heuristic returns no feasible solution within 1 s, except with 2 obstacles (p. 33).
  - Pruning: from 8,604 to 1,362 strategies at T = 40 (p. 25). No reduction for the portfolio, where reassignments turn infeasible or suboptimal (p. 29), and almost none for motion planning (p. 32; Table 3).
  - [my computation] Ratios of maximum times, Gurobi to MLOPT, run from 0.9 (T = 10, where Gurobi is faster) to about 185,000 (c = 20).   - Failures the text does print: motion-planning accuracy "most of the times less than 10%", with "marginal violations of the obstacle boundaries" (pp. 32–33); Figure 7's infeasibility axis spans 10⁻¹ to 10⁰ (p. 34). **Method links**:
- Method 4: evidence ✅ (pp. 20–23, 25, 29, 35). The incumbent is the commercial solver, run warm-started both at default settings and in a time-limited heuristic mode. Suboptimality and infeasibility are measured against its optima on 10,000 test samples, and the code is released (p. 20). Cases where the method does not win stay visible in the tables (T = 10, Table 1).
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 12–13, 20, 22–23, 36). The exact solver moves offline, as the teacher that labels strategies. Online there is no certificate, only a Good-Turing coverage bound and measured statistics. The paper never calls its online answers certified, and it states its own limit: no use where Gurobi needs hours (p. 36).
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 6). The speed-up figure ("over a trillion times") is borrowed from [Bix10], not rebuilt, and is used to concede that MIO remains too slow online. The operative *why now* is the data produced by repeated parametric solves plus fast NN classifiers. Data point for the skill's figure series (200 billion → 450 billion → 800 billion → 2 trillion): this 2021 text cites "over a trillion" from a 2010 source.
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 9–10, 17–19). The class-keeping move is turned around. Fixing the integers and the tight constraints reduces the online problem to a linear system, so no solver runs online at all; and the structure that remains (θ entering only q and b) is what makes caching possible.
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (pp. 22–23, 29). Predictions are scored as decisions: a strategy counts as accurate when its decoded solution is feasible and near-optimal, whatever its label. But there is no owner or current rule, and the motivating closed-loop uses (control performance, portfolio backtests, p. 29) are not evaluated.
- Method 3: variant ⚠ (pp. 19, 25–26, 29–30, 34–35) [my reading]. This is a price-of-speed curve. The knob k trades time against suboptimality, infeasibility and accuracy (Figures 2, 4, 7), and the flops bound does not depend on the solution. The bad cases (T = 10; 8 obstacles) go uncommented.
- Method 6: evidence ✅ (pp. 2–3, 8, 20). A programme line with software: [BS21] (interpretability) → this paper (speed) → [CCS+20] (robotics), carried by MLOPT.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) For a parametric MIQO that is solved repeatedly, label each sampled instance with its strategy (tight constraints and integer values) and learn the map from parameters to strategy as multiclass classification. Decode by fixing the integers and solving the KKT system of the equality-constrained QP (pp. 9–10, 17).
- (D4) When the parameters enter only the linear cost and the right-hand side, factor each strategy's KKT matrix offline and cache it; the online step becomes forward–backward substitution (p. 18).
- (D2) Hedge classifier errors: decode the top-k classes in parallel and keep the best feasible one. Plot time, suboptimality, infeasibility and accuracy against k (pp. 12, 18; Figures 2, 4, 7).
- (D3) Stop sampling when the Good-Turing bound on the probability of an unseen strategy, G + c√((1/N) ln(3/β)), falls below a target ε (pp. 12–13).
- (D4) Prune classes with a frequency heuristic: keep the strategies that cover a 1 − α fraction of the samples, reassign the rest, and halve α until every reassigned sample is within a relative ε of its optimum. Check against the exact MILO on small instances (pp. 14–16).
- (D5) When several labels give equally good solutions, define accuracy by decision quality (feasible, and within 10⁻⁴ suboptimality), not by label match (pp. 22–23).
- (D5) Report maximum, not only average, solve times per instance size against the application's hard deadline. Benchmark against both the default solver and a time-limited heuristic mode, both warm-started (pp. 21, 25, 29, 35).
- (D7) Count the online flops as a function of the dimensions, to show that run time does not depend on the parameter values (p. 19).
