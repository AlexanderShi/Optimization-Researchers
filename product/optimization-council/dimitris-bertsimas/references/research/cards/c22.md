### S095 · Sparse Regression: Scalable Algorithms and Empirical Performance (Statistical Science 2020, DOI 10.1214/19-sts701; arXiv 1902.06547)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v2 (arXiv:1902.06547v2 [stat.ME], 29 Feb 2020): main text pp. 1–31 in full; Appendix A (pp. 31–33) in full, including the proof of Theorem 4; Appendices B–C (pp. 33–42) read for all prose and captions, plot-axis residue skipped; references (pp. 43–45) skimmed. Not read: the Statistical Science print, its discussions and the rejoinder (DOI 10.1214/20-STS701REJ).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jean Pauphilet and Bart Van Parys (both MIT Operations Research Center, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the ML programme's answer after Hastie, Tibshirani and Tibshirani's 2017 comparison (cited as [29], pp. 10, 21, 23, 44). The best-subset estimator of BKM 2016 (card S010 in c16; [6] here) gains a ridge term and the group's cutting-plane solver ([5] Bertsimas–Van Parys, [7] Bertsimas–Pauphilet–Van Parys, both "under revision" in 2017, p. 43), and the paper doubles as a review for practitioners. It is the response paper that SKILL.md's Heuristic 2 cites; research note 05 §1.3 already lists its concessions, so this card adds the protocol details and the headline-vs-body checks.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO; CP as the relaxation device}: The entry is the practitioner's confusion, not an intractability belief: the abstract says the profusion of research "might have provided little guidance to practitioners" (p. 1; again p. 2). The target is reframed from prediction to selection: accuracy A (share of true features selected) and false discovery rate FDR (share of selected features that are false) (p. 2). Prediction is called a derivative measure that depends on more than the selected features (p. 10). Gaps named: Lasso recovers supports only under "admittedly strong assumptions" and systematically adds irrelevant features (Su et al.; pp. 2, 8); theory finds regimes where Lasso fails although recovery is information-theoretically possible (p. 2); nonconvex penalties are rarely adopted because they lack convexity (p. 2). The exact problem is "NP-hard [38] and believed to be intractable in practice" (p. 4). The paper disclaims theory: "Our goal is not to provide a theoretical analysis" (p. 2). Five methods are compared: the ℓ2-regularized cardinality-constrained estimator (CIO), its Boolean relaxation (SS), Lasso/ENet, MCP and SCAD. Stepwise is excluded for its many variants and iterations (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Two contributions in one paper. (i) A benchmark with a stated yardstick: a good selector shows a "two-fold convergence", A → 1 and FDR → 0 as n grows (abstract, p. 1; p. 30). (ii) A dual projected sub-gradient algorithm for the Boolean relaxation (5) of the ridge-regularized cardinality problem (Algorithm 2.2, pp. 6–7). For fixed α the objective is linear in s, so minimizing over s is a sort that keeps the k smallest components; α takes one projected sub-gradient step; the support is recovered by partial minimization at the averaged dual iterate, not by averaging s, so the output stays binary and k-sparse (p. 7). What changed: Pilanci–Wainwright–El Ghaoui's relaxation now scales "two orders of magnitude larger" (p. 6). Relative to BKM 2016, the estimator gains a ridge term, justified by robustness and by the instability of subset selection (Breiman) (p. 4), and the exact solver becomes outer approximation on a convex integer reformulation (pp. 4–5). What stays: enforcing sparsity "explicitly" by a cardinality constraint (pp. 16, 30).
- **D3 Model and data**: Class: a convex loss plus (1/2γ)‖w‖² subject to ‖w‖0 ≤ k (3), equivalent to the convex integer problem min over s ∈ {0,1}^p, eᵀs ≤ k, of max over α of f(α, s), linear in s and concave in α through the loss's Fenchel conjugate (Theorem 1, cited from [7], pp. 4–5). Losses: least squares, logistic and hinge (Table 1, p. 2); six in the package (Table 9, p. 32). k and γ are cross-validated, with γ doubled from γ0 ≈ 1/max‖xi‖² (Chu et al.) (pp. 5–6). Assumptions adopted for tractability: the ridge term (p. 4); tightness of the relaxation when a saddle point has an unambiguous top-k set (Theorem 2, from Pilanci et al.), a condition that "seems often fulfilled in real-world applications" (p. 6); uniformly bounded sub-gradients for the rate (Theorem 3, p. 7). Data:
  - Gaussian designs with Toeplitz Σij = ρ^|i−j| (mutual incoherence holds), ρ ∈ {0.2, 0.7}, SNR ∈ {6, 1, 0.05}, and (p, k) = (20,000, 100), (10,000, 50), (2,000, 10) for regression (Table 2, p. 11); half those p for classification (Table 5, p. 25). w_true ∈ {−1, 0, 1}^p with k_true nonzeros; SNR mapped to PVE = SNR/(1 + SNR), from 85.7% down to 4.8% (p. 10).
  - A "hard" Σ that violates mutual incoherence (Loh–Wainwright), with θ = 1/(2k) + 1/(2√k) and w_true = (1/√k, …, 0) (p. 16).
  - A real design: TCGA lung cancer, n = 1,145 patients and p = 14,858 genes, with synthetic responses at 10 SNR values (Table 4, p. 21) and real tumour-type labels (594 adenocarcinoma, 551 squamous) for classification (p. 28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Convex integer reformulation via Fenchel duality; for least squares the inner maximum is closed-form, c(s) = ½Yᵀ(I + γXsXsᵀ)⁻¹Y (p. 5).
  - Outer approximation (Duran–Grossmann) on the convex c(s), implemented as lazy constraints in a single branch-and-bound tree; cuts are reused across k because c does not depend on k (Algorithm 2.1, pp. 5–6). Stop at ε = 10⁻⁴ or at the time limit (p. 5).
  - Tightness: Sion's minimax theorem plus linearity in s give a top-k construction; the relaxation is tight iff a saddle point has unambiguous k largest components (Theorem 2, Pilanci et al., p. 6). The authors' gloss: "randomness breaks the complexity of the problem" (p. 6).
  - Rate: O(1/√T) in objective value for the averaged dual (Theorem 3, from Bertsekas, p. 7), called "disappointingly slow" (p. 7); the defence is empirical recovery of the primal support (p. 7). A Polyak-type adaptive step (p. 8); per-iteration cost O(n‖s‖0) for α and O(np + p log p) for s (p. 7).
  - The paper's own theorem: for the cardinality-penalized variant (9)–(11), the relaxation is tight if a saddle point makes every entry of (λ − (γ/2)ᾱᵀXjXjᵀᾱ)j nonzero; proof by Slater's condition (strong duality) and uniqueness of the minimizer in s (Theorem 4, pp. 32–33).
  - No support-recovery theory for CIO or SS: none like Loh–Wainwright exists "to the best of our knowledge" (p. 16).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: CIO in Julia/JuMP with Gurobi 7.5.0; SS in Julia; glmnet (R and Julia; version 1.9–5 in reference [22], p. 44) for Lasso/ENet; ncvreg (R, no version) for MCP and SCAD; Xeon @2.3 GHz, 1 CPU, 16 GB (p. 10). Table 10 ran on 1 CPU with 250 GB (p. 32).
  - Budgets: CIO 60 s for regression and 180 s for classification (pp. 5, 39); 600 s in the hyper-parameter study (p. 33). SS: 150 iterations according to p. 11, but Tmax = 200 according to p. 12 and Figures 1–7, and Tmax = 150 in Figures 8–10, 13, 19–20 [my reading: the budget varies by figure].
  - Protocols: k fixed at k_true, so A = 1 − FDR (p. 11); k cross-validated on validation MSE (pp. 12, 16); true-vs-false-feature curves over one common sparsity range, with λ calibrated and linearly interpolated for penalty methods (p. 12); 10 data sets per point (captions, pp. 13–20); time reported relative to "a Lasso estimator with glmnet in the same language and on the same data" (p. 11).
  - Results printed against the authors: noise equalizes the methods (p. 12). With cross-validated k in high correlation "no method clearly dominates", and in high noise and high correlation ENet and SCAD "seem to clearly dominate" (p. 12). On the real design, ENet dominates in very noisy regimes and MCP makes "fewer incorrect guesses" (p. 21). In classification CIO and SS "converge significantly slower" in accuracy (p. 25), and MCP "slightly dominates" at given k (p. 39). Stopping SS early fails in classification (pp. 12, 39). On real labels, the exact method's AUC is 0.9798, against 0.9821 (Boolean relaxation), 0.9814 (Lasso) and 0.9806 (ENet), with 87.5 genes against 65, 114 and 398.5 (Table 7, p. 28).
  - Not reported [my reading]: whether CIO reached ε = 10⁻⁴ within 60 s, or any gap, although the authors note that the solver spends most of its time proving optimality (p. 5). Also unreported: whether the glmnet reference time is for one λ or a path (p. 11), while Table 8 credits Lasso with the "Whole regularization path at no extra cost" (p. 31). Classification pits CIO and SS with hinge loss against the others with logistic loss "For implementation considerations" (p. 23). The scale claim of n = p = 100,000 or n = 10,000 with p = 1,000,000 "within minutes" (p. 3; again pp. 31–32) exceeds Table 10, which stops at n = 50,000 (with p = 100,000) and p = 500,000 (with n = 10,000) (p. 32).
- **D6 Positioning**: Lasso is credited with three ingredients (fast algorithms, off-the-shelf code, theory under strong assumptions), then its selection deficiencies are documented from statisticians' own work (Su–Bogdan–Candès, Fan–Song, Wainwright, Wang–Wainwright–Ramchandran, Gamarnik–Zadik) (p. 2). ℓ1 is recast as primarily a robustness device that shrinks coefficients, citing Bertsimas–Fertis and Xu–Caramanis–Mannor, so that its feature-selection ability "ought to be challenged" (p. 9; again pp. 23, 30). Nonconvex penalties are credited with the oracle property and support recovery without incoherence (Loh–Wainwright) (pp. 2, 9, 16). His own exact algorithm is claimed as "the most scalable method providing provably optimal solutions" (p. 2) and "the only method which scales to instances of such sizes, while provably solving such an NP-hard problem" (p. 4). HTT 2017 supplies the evaluation frame (the PVE formula, p. 10; the SNR grid "as in Hastie et al. [29]", p. 21) and is conceded in part (p. 23). Hazimeh–Mazumder's coordinate-descent method for the exact problem is cited among the advances (p. 2) but not benchmarked [my reading]. Pilanci et al. are credited for the relaxation and its tightness theorem (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Framed as a review "with an application-oriented eye" (abstract, p. 1), with a coined criterion (two-fold convergence, pp. 1, 30; its two halves stated separately on p. 3), a "Summary and guidelines" list (pp. 21, 23) and a pros-and-cons table (Table 8, p. 31). Opinion markers: "In our view" (p. 9; lower-case on p. 21), "In our opinion" (pp. 16, 30, 33), "we believe - and observe -" (p. 30). Priority phrases: "To the best of our knowledge" (pp. 2, 4, 16). Headline vs body [my reading]:
  - The abstract's "all methods terminate in times comparable with the glmnet package" (p. 1) means a factor of 10–100 in the body (pp. 12, 36).
  - The conclusion says the exact formulation "clearly outperforms all other methods, in all settings, and both for regression and classification, with the lowest false detection rate" (p. 30). The body has exceptions: ENet and SCAD in high noise and high correlation (p. 12), MCP's fewer false guesses on the real design (p. 21), MCP's accuracy edge in classification (p. 39).
  - "MCP and SCAD provide the sparsest classifiers" (p. 28), yet in Table 7 the Boolean relaxation (65 genes) is sparser than SCAD (79.5) (p. 28).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Stated contributions: a unified treatment of five methods; the sub-gradient algorithm with a convergence rate; the SubsetSelection package; a comparison on accuracy, FDR and time (p. 3). Stated limits: CIO needs a commercial solver and extra time, and SS is a heuristic (Table 8, p. 31); the O(1/√T) rate (p. 7); no theory for CIO or SS (p. 16); whether cross-validation can select the "best" model is an open question (p. 21). Recommendations: screen with Lasso or ENet, then run a nonconvex selector "if time permits" (p. 23); "we highly recommend the use of cardinality-constrained formulation or its relaxation, depending on available computing resources" (p. 30). Unstated [my reading]: the certification status of the time-limited CIO runs; the direction of the bias from unmatched classification losses.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Judge a feature-selection method by two curves along n at fixed noise and correlation: accuracy should reach 1 and FDR should reach 0. Fix k = k_true for one experiment (so A = 1 − FDR) and cross-validate k in another (pp. 11–12, 16, 30).
- (D5) Compare codes written in different languages by time relative to the incumbent's implementation in the same language on the same data (p. 11).
- (D5) Add a design where the rival provably fails (Σ violating mutual incoherence) to check that your experiment reproduces known theory before trusting it elsewhere (pp. 16, 22).
- (D5) Use ROC-style true-vs-false-feature curves over one common sparsity range; for penalty methods, calibrate λ to hit each k and interpolate linearly where it cannot (p. 12).
- (D4) Solve a min-max relaxation by dual sub-gradient ascent, recovering the primal by partial minimization at the averaged dual (a sort that keeps k) rather than by averaging, so the output stays binary; track the best c(s^t) as an upper bound and stop on the duality gap or a stall count (pp. 6–8).
- (D4) Implement outer approximation as lazy constraints in one branch-and-bound tree, and reuse the cuts across k when the convex function does not depend on k (pp. 5–6).
- (D4) Prove tightness of a Boolean relaxation by showing that the minimizer in s at a saddle point is unique, hence binary (Theorem 4, pp. 32–33).
- (D7) When answering a critic, adopt the critic's grid and formula (SNR 0.05 to 6 via PVE = SNR/(1 + SNR)), state which of their findings survive, and release code for the methods that had none (pp. 3, 10, 21, 23).

### S144 · Machine learning and natural language processing methods to identify ischemic stroke, acuity and location from radiology reports (PLoS ONE 2020, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0234908)
Read level: **full**: the published article, pp. 1–16, including all prose, Tables 1–3, figure captions, author contributions and references. The S1 File (S1 Appendix, S1–S6 Tables, S1–S3 Figs) is not in the file and was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Charlene Jennifer Ong (first and corresponding author; Boston University School of Medicine, Boston Medical Center, Harvard Medical School and MIT ORC); Agni Orfanoudaki, Rebecca Zhang and Francois Pierre M. Caprasse (MIT ORC); Hutch, Ma, Fard, Balogun, Miller, Minnig, Saglam, Prescott, Greer and Smirnakis; Bertsimas last (MIT ORC and Sloan) (p. 1). Bertsimas's listed roles: conceptualization, funding acquisition, project administration, resources, supervision, and review and editing; formal analysis and software are by Orfanoudaki, Zhang and Caprasse, and methodology does not list him (p. 14). · **Position in trajectory**: the 2019–2026 health-care row {AP DA ML}: a clinician-led paper in a clinical journal with Orfanoudaki (in SKILL.md's health lineage) as analyst, where the group's OCT and OCT-H are two of seven classifiers (pp. 6–7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + ML}: The current rule is billing codes. ICD-9/10 codes "can misclassify ischemic stroke events and do not distinguish acuity or location" (abstract, p. 1); of 1,359 reports from patients with stroke billing codes, only 925 (68%) reported an ischemic stroke (p. 10). Named uses: triaging critical reports, finding patients eligible for time-sensitive interventions, and building research cohorts (pp. 1–2); MCA stroke is chosen as the subtype actionable by thrombectomy (p. 3). Hypothesis: counting-based featurization (BOW, tf-idf) "may not sufficiently capture the language describing stroke features", and embeddings might (p. 2). Aims: extend NLP to location and acuity, and test whether a neurovascular GloVe beats BOW and tf-idf (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: Domain-specific word vectors. GloVe is trained on a neurovascular corpus (UpToDate neurology articles, a stroke textbook, a neuroradiology textbook and 10,000 held-out radiology reports from 2010–2017) and released as "the first neuroradiology specific set of vector representations" (pp. 5–6). It is crossed with seven classifiers over three featurizations and three binary tasks (Table 2, p. 8), and only the two extremes are validated externally: interpretable BOW with logistic regression, and complex GloVe with an RNN (p. 6). The tasks are kept separate, not merged into "acute MCA stroke", so the system can extend to other stroke types (p. 10).
- **D3 Model and data**: 60,564 CT, CTA, MRI and MRA reports of 17,864 adults with ICD-9 ischemic-stroke codes, 2003–2018, from Massachusetts General and Brigham and Women's via the RPDR (p. 3). 1,359 hand-labelled reports from 297 patients (883 CT or CTA, 476 MRI or MRA); each was labelled twice, discrepancies were adjudicated by attending neurologists, and indeterminate findings were labelled absent. Agreement was 91%, 87% and 93% on a 10% sample, and 97%, 95% and 98% against images for 300 reports (pp. 3–4). Derivation prevalences: 925 stroke, 350 MCA, 522 acute (p. 8). External cohort: 500 reports from 424 patients at Boston Medical Center, 2016–2018, a safety-net hospital with a different population (p. 3); 266 stroke, 90 MCA, 106 acute (p. 8). Preprocessing removes headers and signatures, joins n-grams and lower-cases (p. 5). GloVe settings: dimension 100, 50 iterations, window 10, learning rate 0.05 (p. 6). Five random 75/25 splits ("bootstrapping"), with the whole external cohort scored for each (p. 6). Whether splits keep a patient's reports together (297 patients, 1,359 reports) is not stated [my reading].
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a. The work is purely empirical: OCT and OCT-H appear as off-the-shelf classifiers, described as "OCT considers all splits at once" (Table 1, p. 7), with no formulation, solver, time limit or gap reported.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol: grid search with 10-fold cross-validation (logistic regression λ with 1,000 iterations and tolerance 0.01; k ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20} for k-NN; tree minimum bucket and maximum depth in 1–10; Random Forest with 200 trees; a two-layer LSTM) (p. 6). The threshold is set for sensitivity above 90% with the highest specificity; calibration curves; a McNemar test between the best and second-best models (pp. 6–7); bootstrap CIs; R 3.5.2, scikit-learn and TensorFlow (p. 8). OCT's implementation, version and solve settings are not stated [my reading].
  - Internal AUCs (Table 2, p. 8): GloVe/RNN 0.961, 0.976 and 0.925 (stroke, MCA, acuity); BOW/logistic regression 0.951, 0.959 and 0.898. GloVe pairs badly with logistic regression and single trees (p. 9). Random Forest's calibration declines at high predicted probabilities (especially for MCA), and GloVe/RNN appears best calibrated (p. 9).
  - The group's own trees [my count from Table 2, p. 8]: axis-aligned OCT has a lower AUC than CART in all 9 featurization × task cells, with non-overlapping 95% CIs in 4 (e.g. BOW: 0.805 vs 0.889 for stroke, 0.867 vs 0.949 for MCA). OCT-H beats CART in 6 of 9, ties once and loses twice. The text never discusses OCT. One OCT-H interval does not contain its estimate (GloVe stroke, 0.767 with CI 0.775–0.834).
  - External (Table 3, p. 12): BOW/logistic regression 0.892, 0.857 and 0.797 against GloVe/RNN 0.92, 0.893 and 0.925. The text reports specificities falling for stroke and MCA (0.75 and 0.70, against 0.87 and 0.91 internally) (p. 9). General-purpose (Wikipedia) embeddings with an RNN give 0.74, 0.75 and 0.693 (pp. 12–13).
  - Arithmetic checks [my computation]: Table 3b's accuracies (0.828, 0.862, 0.866) and its "Precision" (0.764, 0.932, 0.916) are reproduced to three decimals only if its Sensitivity and Specificity columns are read swapped and "Precision" is read as negative predictive value, at the external prevalences of p. 8 (266, 90 and 106 of 500). As printed, the accuracies would be 0.839, 0.736 and 0.737. Read swapped, the external sensitivities are 0.75, 0.70 and 0.69, below the more-than-90% target that the text says was kept (p. 9). In Table 2b, the acuity precision of 0.935 does not fit its own sensitivity, specificity and the 522/1,359 prevalence, which give about 0.65 (p. 8).
  - Inference [my reading]: McNemar is used "to validate the hypothesis that the two predictive models are equivalent" (p. 7). Results with p = 0.023 and p = 0.031 are reported as "equivalent performances" (p. 9) although 0.05 was the stated significance level (p. 7). The χ² values do not match these p-values under one degree of freedom (14.78 gives p ≈ 0.0001) [my computation]; they may be split averages (S5 Table, not read).
- **D6 Positioning**: The results agree with Zech et al. (simple featurization matches deep learning for binary head-CT findings) and Kim et al. (a single tree beat SVMs) (pp. 2, 12). Garg et al.'s kappa is cited from a different task (p. 12). Interpretability is graded per method in Table 1 (High, Medium, High, High, Low, Low; p. 7), but the discussion and conclusion favour the low-interpretability RNN (pp. 11, 13). ### S204 · Learning mixed-integer convex optimization strategies for robot planning and control (IEEE CDC 2020, DOI 10.1109/cdc42340.2020.9304043; arXiv 2004.03736)
Read level: **full**, arXiv v2 (arXiv:2004.03736v2 [cs.RO], 11 Apr 2022): all 11 pages, including Algorithms 1–2, the three experiments, figure captions and references. The CDC print (pp. 1698–1705) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Abhishek Cauligi and Preston Culbertson (equal contribution), Mac Schwager and Marco Pavone (Stanford), and Bartolomeo Stellato (Princeton ORFE); Bertsimas is fourth of six (MIT ORC); funded by NASA fellowships (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the DA line *learning to optimize* (team.json DA scope; SKILL.md Heuristic 5). It extends Bertsimas–Stellato's MLOPT ([1] "Online mixed-integer optimization in milliseconds", arXiv 1907.02206; [13] "The voice of optimization", Machine Learning 2020; pp. 1, 9–10) to robotics, carried by a Stanford robotics lab.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + DA; AP (robotics) as testbed}: The entry concedes the speed-up and says it is not enough. MICP has seen "several orders of magnitude solve time speedups compared to 25 years ago" (abstract, p. 1; the introduction cites Bixby [10] for these advances, p. 1), yet its "solution times are still too slow for online applications" (p. 1). Planning and control need milliseconds while solves take seconds to minutes, and multithreaded commercial solvers do not run on embedded platforms (p. 1). The closest method, MLOPT, fails on robotics MICPs: big-M implication and OR constraints give several optimal integer assignments for one continuous optimum, so the labels are ill-posed, and there are too many strategies to classify (pp. 1–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: CoCo, "Combinatorial Offline, Convex Online". Offline: solve sampled instances, store each optimum as a logical strategy (the integer assignment plus the set of tight big-M constraints), keep one class per distinct strategy, and train a multiclass feedforward network (Algorithm 1, p. 4). Online: rank strategies with the network, fix the integers of the top n_evals in turn, solve the convex problem, and return the first feasible solution, else report failure (Algorithm 2, pp. 4–5). Changes from MLOPT: logical strategies remove redundant labels (p. 3); task-specific strategies split separable logic (one obstacle at one time step) into sub-formulas, with one classifier for all of them queried by a one-hot index (pp. 4, 7); continuous active sets are no longer labelled, because robotics MICPs beyond MIQP and MILP cannot be solved from KKT conditions (p. 3). Unchanged: the offline/online split, strategy classification and a convex solve online (pp. 1–3).
- **D3 Model and data**: A parametrized MICP with convex objective and constraints, where big-M constraints are the only link between x and δ (P(θ), p. 3), with Mi(θ) = sup gi (p. 3). Assumptions: problems are well-posed (a unique continuous minimizer; several δ* allowed) (p. 3); ill-posed cases with several optimal paths are neglected as rare (p. 4); θ is drawn from a known distribution p(Θ) "representative of the problems encountered in practice" (p. 3). Instances (Table I, p. 5):
  - Cart-pole with soft walls: MIQP, N = 10, 40 binaries, θ ∈ R¹³ (pp. 5–6).
  - Free-flying robot with 8 axis-aligned obstacles: MIQCQP, N = 5, 160 binaries, θ ∈ R⁴⁴ (pp. 6–7).
  - Task-oriented grasp: MISOCP, 30 candidate points, a four-finger hand, 30 binaries, 4,500 training problems, and task weights as the softmax of N(0, 10I) draws (pp. 7–8). Fig. 6 shows a five-finger hand (p. 7).
  - A 90/10 train/test split (p. 5).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Big-M modelling of logic after Bemporad–Morari (p. 3). A big-M constraint is defined as tight when only the solution's δ value satisfies it, which makes a strategy unique for a given continuous solution (pp. 3–4). A conjunction F1 ∧ … ∧ Fℓ splits into sub-formula strategies "without loss of generality" (p. 4), with the trade-off stated: all ℓ predictions must be right to recover the global strategy, so the decomposition has "diminishing returns" (p. 4). No theorem, guarantee or complexity bound: "CoCo forgoes the optimality guarantees" of branch-and-bound, defended by receding-horizon control (p. 5); guarantees are future work (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Julia 1.2, JuMP, PyTorch with ADAM; three-layer ReLU networks with 32 or 128 neurons per layer; Gurobi and Mosek, with presolve and multithreading disabled (except for the grasp problem) "to better approximate the computational resources of an embedded processor"; code on GitHub (p. 5). Not stated [my reading]: hardware, solver versions (the reference is a 2020 manual, p. 10), n_evals, and the size of the grasp test set.
  - Baselines: the exact solver; the Masti–Bemporad regressor with the same architecture (p. 5); MLOPT without decomposition (free-flyer only, p. 7); the most common training mode and random modes (grasp, Fig. 9, p. 9).
  - Metrics (Figs. 3, 5, 8): % feasible, time (forward pass plus convex solves), number of convex solves, and cost relative to the global optimum.
  - Results: cart-pole, 10,000 test problems: 99% feasible against 58% for the regressor; one QP for 95% of problems against about 50 QPs in Gurobi's branch-and-bound; 99% of feasible solutions globally optimal (p. 6). Free-flyer: 516 strategies against about 81 thousand for MLOPT; 92% feasible against 18% (regressor) and 35% (MLOPT); one QCQP for 85%; optimal 92% of the time (p. 7). Grasp: globally optimal for 99%; tens of milliseconds against seconds for Mosek (pp. 8–9).
  - Slips and unshown claims [my reading]: the free-flyer text says "92% of the training set" while scoring ten thousand test problems (p. 7). Figure 9's ratios use "1000 problems in the training dataset" (p. 9). The claim that CoCo beats branch-and-bound "terminated after a fixed number of iterations" (p. 5) has no truncated branch-and-bound arm in Figs. 3, 5 or 8. Speed-ups are "1 to 2 orders of magnitude" in the abstract and conclusion (pp. 1, 9) but "two to three" for grasps (p. 9, Fig. 8 caption). The time comparisons are against solvers with presolve and threads disabled (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: MLOPT is both base and foil (pp. 1–3, 7). Learned warm starts for QP, SQP and indirect methods [15–19] "only consider the setting of continuous optimization" (p. 2). Learning to branch [22, 23] still runs branch-and-bound online (p. 2). Masti–Bemporad's regressor was demonstrated only on an MIQP with 14 binaries (p. 2) and is benchmarked here. LVIS [5] still solves an MIQP online (p. 2). Hogan et al. [8] are closest but handle a narrower class (p. 2). Explicit MPC and meta-learning are named as inspirations (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Conference format with a statement of contributions (p. 2) and claim-bearing captions (Fig. 3 claims near-total feasibility without sacrificing optimality, p. 6). The single limitations paragraph argues from deployment: rare suboptimality is tolerable if fast execution keeps behaviour safe (p. 5). The "globally optimal" percentages are measured against offline exact solves, so the word is earned on test data rather than certified online (pp. 6–9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: logical strategies, task-specific strategies, three robotics benchmarks and released code (pp. 2, 5). Stated limits: no online optimality guarantee (p. 5); ill-posed problems neglected (p. 4); diminishing returns of decomposition (p. 4); possible failure (Algorithm 2, p. 4). Future work: theory on sampling the strategy space and bounding the online optimality gap; mixed-integer nonconvex problems such as task planning (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 2: variant ⚠ (p. 1). The tool-change argument is turned around: the speed-up since the 1990s is granted qualitatively (from Bixby) and judged insufficient for millisecond control on embedded hardware, so the exact solver moves offline to label data. No figure is rebuilt.
- Method 4: variant ⚠ (pp. 5–9). The certificate moves offline: exact solves label the training data and score each online answer against the global optimum (99%, 92%, 99%). Online answers carry no certificate, and the paper says so (p. 5). The duel is against the exact solver on time and against rival learners on feasibility and cost, not against an incumbent heuristic.
- Method 1: variant ⚠ (pp. 2–3, 5–8). The robotics MICPs are borrowed from that literature. The class is kept by learning rather than by reformulation: once the predicted integers are fixed, the online problem is a QP, QCQP or SOCP (pp. 2, 5–8).
- Method 5: variant ⚠ (Figs. 3, 5, 8–9; pp. 6–9). The learned predictor is scored by the decisions it produces (feasibility, cost relative to the global optimum, solves needed), not by classification accuracy. There is no owner or current rule, and the parameter distribution is synthetic (p. 3).
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted): Heuristic 5 evidence: one MICP family is solved repeatedly online and its strategy is learned offline, with claims narrowed (forgone guarantees, p. 5; gap bounds as future work, p. 9). Tension T1: uncertified online, but optimality measured offline. The Taste quick-check on keeping the class is met by learning.
- New-pattern candidate: **canonical strategy labels** (p. 3): define the label as the integer assignment plus the tight big-M constraints, so equivalent optima share one class.
- New-pattern candidate: **one shared classifier for repeated sub-decisions** (pp. 4, 7): decompose separable logic and query one network with a one-hot index; about 81 thousand strategies become 516.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Before learning a solver's output, canonicalize the labels: map every optimal integer assignment to the integer values plus the set of constraints that only those values satisfy, so equivalent optima share one class (p. 3).
- (D4) When the logic is a conjunction of repeated, separable sub-formulas (one per obstacle per step), train one classifier for the sub-decision with a one-hot index of the instance, and state the trade-off: all ℓ predictions must be right (pp. 4, 7).
- (D2) Online fallback: try the top-n_evals predicted strategies, each a convex solve with the integers fixed; return the first feasible one and report failures explicitly (Algorithm 2, p. 4).
- (D5) Score a learned optimizer by decision outcomes (% feasible, time, convex solves, cost relative to the offline optimum) against the exact solver, a rival learner and trivial baselines (most common mode, random) (Figs. 3, 5, 8–9).
- (D5) Run the exact baseline under the deployment platform's limits (no presolve, one thread) and say so (p. 5).

- "Thus, some suboptimality in relatively rare instances can be tolerated if fast execution enables safe behavior and, in some cases, a well-tuned terminal cost can also account for long horizon behavior." (p. 5)

- **D2 Core idea**: A four-step chain from descriptive to prescriptive analytics, in which each step's outputs parametrize the next (Fig. 1, p. 2):
  1. A cohort-level database built from 160 clinical studies (pp. 2–3).
  2. Mortality and infection calculators (pp. 4–5).
  3. DELPHI, an 11-state SEIR extension with undetected states and a government-response factor γ(t) = (2/π) arctan(−(t − t0)/k) + 1 (p. 6); then DELPHI-presc, a regression tree from policy classes to γ(t), used to simulate reopening scenarios (p. 7).

- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Database: 160 studies from December 2019 to May 2020 covering 133,600 patients (3.13% of global cases on May 12, 2020; Europe 81,207, Asia 19,418, North America 23,279).   - Calculators: ASST Cremona and HM Hospitals. Mortality cohort 2,831 (711 deaths); infection cohort 3,135 (1,661 positive), Cremona only because HM had no negative cases (pp. 3–5). First-day labs and vitals only; kNN imputation; features missing for more than 40% of patients dropped; XGBoost capped at 20 features; SHAP for transparency (pp. 4–5).
  - DELPHI: seven parameters fitted per state by nonlinear optimization minimizing in-sample error on NYT county counts, with a state included after 100 cases (p. 7). States are assumed independent, ignoring inter-state travel (p. 7).
  - Ventilators: implemented on April 15; 25% of hospitalized patients ventilated; worst-case demand via a buffer, "for robustness" (pp. 8–9). Formulation and assumptions are in the appendix (not read).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: DELPHI's ODE uses the arctan response, read as three phases of government response, with t0 the start and k the strength (p. 6); the per-state fit minimizes in-sample error (p. 7; details in the appendix, not read). The policy model is a regression tree, chosen "For simplicity and interpretability", over seven mutually exclusive policy classes (p. 7). Ventilator allocation is a bi-objective MIO (shortages, projected and worst-case; inter-state transfers), traced as a Pareto frontier over the maximum share of supply a state may pool (pp. 8–9); mixed-integer optimization is chosen for "modeling flexibility" (p. 8). No proofs, bounds or solve times appear in this file.
