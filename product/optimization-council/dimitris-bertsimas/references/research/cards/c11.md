### S160 · Persistence in discrete optimization under data uncertainty (Mathematical Programming 2006, DOI 10.1007/s10107-006-0710-z)
**Read level**: full. All 24 file pages were read:
- introduction and contributions (pp. 1–4);
- review of moment cones (pp. 4–6);
- the marginal moment model, with Theorem 1, Lemma 1 and their proofs (pp. 6–10);
- the two-step extension, with Theorem 2 and its proof (pp. 10–14);
- project-management experiments (pp. 15–18);
- spanning-tree experiments and conclusions (pp. 18–23).

The reference list (pp. 23–24) was skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Karthik Natarajan and Chung-Piaw Teo, both at the National University of Singapore (p. 1); SKILL.md Academic Lineage lists Natarajan under CP and Teo under IO among his students · **Place in trajectory**: the moment/SDP line of SW2, carried from bounds into combinatorial optimization. Received 17 May 2004, accepted 15 April 2005 (p. 1). It builds on the authors' own marginal-moment bound for expected optimal values ([4], pp. 2, 9–10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP + IO; a worst case over a moment ambiguity set, as in RO; SD-type probabilistic combinatorial optimization}: The target is the *persistency* of a 0-1 variable: the probability that it equals 1 in the optimal solution when the objective coefficients are random (Definition 1, p. 2). The entry point is a gap: moment methods give tight bounds, but there has been little research on the structure of solutions under uncertainty (p. 2).
  - The gap is made concrete with Van Slyke's 8-activity project (Table 1, p. 3). CPM/PERT names activities 1 and 2 as critical, with an expected duration of 20.2. Simulation gives 22.98, and puts activity 3 on the longest path with probability 0.715.
  - For a given distribution, persistency is NP-hard in general: two-point independent costs already need 2^n solves (p. 3). Distributions are also rarely known (p. 3), so the model uses only ranges and marginal moments (p. 3).
  - Uses: finding critical activities in project management, and pruning arcs before repeated spanning-tree or route-guidance solves (p. 3).
  - Transferable: open with a small classical instance on which the practitioner's deterministic rule gives the wrong answer once variability enters, and give one number for the damage (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Bound E[Z_max(c̃)] from above by its supremum over all joint distributions with the given marginal moments (eq. (2), p. 6).
  - Lift each x_i to conditional moment vectors w_i(k) = E[c̃_i^j | x_i(c̃) = k]·P(x_i(c̃) = k), for k ∈ {0, 1} (p. 7). The expectation becomes Σ_i w_i1(1) (eq. (3), p. 7).
  - Constraints (Theorem 1, eq. (6), p. 7): w_i(1) + w_i(0) = m_i; (w_10(1), …, w_n0(1)) ∈ CH(X); every w_i(k) in a closed moment cone.
  - At the optimum, w*_i0(1) is the persistency under an extremal distribution that attains the bound, exactly or in the limit (Lemma 1, p. 8).
  - Against [4], the bound is unchanged; what changes is which side is read. The primal moment form replaces the dual polynomial form (7), because the primal reveals persistence (pp. 9–10).
  - The same device extends to two-step problems: inner 0-1 problems linked by an outer 0-1 selection. These cover multiobjective choice and a capacitated portfolio of optimization problems (pp. 10–12; Theorem 2, p. 12).
  - Transferable: when a bound is known in dual form, write its primal over conditional moments. The primal variables are then the structural quantities, here the probability that each decision is on (pp. 7–10).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: a 0-1 problem max c̃'x over X ⊆ {0,1}^n with random c̃, ties assumed to have measure zero (p. 1). Only the support and the first j_i marginal moments of each c̃_i are given (p. 6).
  - Cross moments and dependence are left out on purpose. The authors grant that estimates may be imprecise for correlated coefficients (pp. 3–4) and that persistency is sensitive to correlation (p. 16).
  - Tractability needs a polynomial-size description Ax ≤ b of CH(X) (p. 6; Remark (a), p. 9).
  - Instances:
    - Van Slyke's 8-activity project (pp. 2–3, 15–16);
    - Kleindorfer's project with 40 activities and 51 paths, with triangular, two-point and moment data (Table 3, pp. 16–17);
    - random complete graphs with 15 and 30 nodes in [0, 10]²: means are Euclidean distances, σ is drawn from [0, μ/3], ranges are μ ± 3σ (p. 21).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Duality of moment cones and nonnegative polynomials, with a closure for moments that only a limit of measures attains (pp. 4–6).
  - Hankel-matrix PSD representations for supports ℝ, ℝ+ and [0, 1] (Proposition 1, p. 5). With two moments, the equivalence of 2×2 PSD and a second-order cone gives an SOCP (Proposition 2, p. 6).
  - Conditioning on x_i(c̃) linearizes the expected optimum (pp. 6–7).
  - Tightness by construction (Lemma 1, pp. 8–9):
    1. Write the optimal persistence vector as a convex combination, with weights λ*_p, of extreme points of CH(X).
    2. Pick solution p with probability λ*_p.
    3. Draw each c̃_i from w*_i(1)/w*_i0(1) or from w*_i(0)/w*_i0(0).
    4. Check that the marginal moments add back to m_i, and that the expected optimum is at least the bound.
  - Theorem 2 repeats the construction as a nested decomposition, first over y and then over x (pp. 12–14).
  - Remark (a) gives polynomial-time persistency for longest path on DAGs, assignment, shortest path and spanning tree (p. 9).
  - Transferable: to show a moment relaxation is tight, build its extremal distribution from a convex decomposition of the optimal point into feasible 0-1 solutions (pp. 8–9).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Runs used SeDuMi 1.05 with MATLAB 6.5 on a 2.4 GHz Pentium IV under Windows XP (p. 15). The reference list cites v. 1.03 (p. 23).
  - Van Slyke project (Table 2, p. 16): PERT, Monte Carlo (20,000 samples, from normals with σ13 ∈ {−1, 0, 1} and from a triangular law) and the marginal moment model (MMM) are compared. MMM's worst-case expected duration is 26.30, against 22.56–23.33 simulated. Its persistency for activity 3 is 0.655, against 0.675–0.810 simulated. It runs in under 5 CPU s.
  - Kleindorfer project (pp. 16–18, Table 4): MMM is compared with the complete-marginal-distribution model (MDM) of Meilijson–Nadas and Klein Haneveld, solved by 20-piece linearization.
    - Expected durations: PERT 53.667; MDM 62.047 (triangular) and 63.910 (two-point); MMM 64.344.
    - MMM runs in under 0.5 s.
    - Simulation takes 942 CPU s for 20,000 samples. It finds 20 persistent activities against MMM's 15, and the extra ones have probability below 0.08 (p. 18).
  - Spanning tree: Algorithm B keeps the L most persistent arcs before 20,000 Prim solves (p. 20). Results (Table 5, p. 23):

    | Nodes | Arcs kept (L) | CPU saved | Objective increase |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | 15 | 66 | 19.8% | +0.042% |
    | 15 | 21 | 44.6% | +6.8% |
    | 30 | 191 | 28.9% | +0.6% |
    | 30 | 37 | 53.5% | +14.9% |
  - Only independent normal costs are tested in the spanning-tree study (p. 21), and no variances are reported.
  - Transferable: report a pruning step as the whole curve (kept fraction → time saved → objective lost), bad end included (Table 5, p. 23).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - It separates its persistency from Adams–Lassiter–Sherali's LP persistency, which has a different motivation (footnote, p. 2).
  - It extends the authors' own moment-bound line, on options, probability inequalities, order statistics and expected combinatorial optima ([2]–[5]), from bounds to structure (p. 2).
  - CPM/PERT is the classical baseline (pp. 2–3).
  - The marginal-distribution approach is dismissed as "cumbersome due to lack of precision" because of its piecewise linearization (p. 16).
  - The pruning idea is borrowed from Yaman–Karasan–Pinar's robust spanning tree (p. 20).
  - The reference list gives [4] and [5] the same title (p. 23).
- **D7 Writing**: It opens with a small instance and a striking number, with an exclamation mark (p. 3). A numbered list gives structure and contributions (p. 4). Theorems, a lemma and remarks carry their proofs inline (pp. 7–14). Empirical claims are hedged: "extensive experiments seem to indicate" (p. 4); the results "suggest the potential" (p. 21) and "justify the potential" (p. 22).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution: theory and an algorithm (a polynomial-time convex model when the hull is compact), with an empirical illustration (pp. 4, 22).
  - Stated limits: dependence is ignored (p. 4); persistency is sensitive to correlation (p. 16); a compact hull is required (p. 9).
  - The conclusion gives no future directions (p. 22).
  - Untested: correlated costs in the spanning-tree study, and problems whose X has no compact hull (pp. 20–21).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. Criticality, a quantity owned by PERT and simulation, becomes an SDP/SOCP that stays polynomial when CH(X) is compact (pp. 6–9). The boundary is printed: persistency is NP-hard for a given distribution (p. 3), and a compact hull is needed (p. 9).
  - Nuance on step 4 of Method 1 (dualize): the authors leave their dual formulation [4] for its primal, because the primal moment variables carry the structure. The extremal distribution is built from the primal solution (pp. 8–10).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant. Persistence pruning is priced on a curve with one knob: L arcs kept, against CPU saved and objective lost, with the bad end printed (Table 5, p. 23). But the knob trades computation, not a property anyone wants protected, and no bound ties L to the loss.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The practitioner's rule (PERT/CPM) is the baseline (pp. 2–3, 16–17). But the score is agreement with simulated criticality and duration, not a decision outcome for the project manager.
- New-pattern candidate: **solution structure, not only bounds**. The target is which variables are on. It is read from the primal of the authors' own dual bound, through an explicit extremal distribution (pp. 2, 8–10).
- New-pattern candidate: **shrink-then-solve with a printed quality/time knob**. A cheap convex model prunes the variables of a problem that is solved many times, and the trade-off curve is published (pp. 20–23). S073 is a second instance (clustering before the MIO).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Lift a 0-1 decision under random costs to conditional moment vectors with Hankel-PSD moment-cone constraints; its persistency is w_i0(1) (pp. 7–8).
- With two moments, replace the 2×2 Hankel PSD constraint by a second-order cone, so the model stays an SOCP (p. 6).
- Prove tightness through an explicit extremal distribution, built from a convex decomposition into extreme points (pp. 8–9).
- Prune variables by persistence before many re-solves, and publish the full curve of kept fraction, time and objective (pp. 20–23).

**Quotes**:
- "Using a deterministic approach would imply that the project manager focuses on the wrong path 71.5 percent of the time!" (p. 3)
- "The advantage in the primal formulation is that we obtain important insights into the persistence of decision variables of discrete optimization problems." (p. 10)

### S073 · Classification and regression via integer optimization (Operations Research 2007, DOI 10.1287/opre.1060.0360)
**Read level**: full. All 20 file pages were read:
- introduction and the geometry of the approach (pp. 1–5);
- the classification MIO, with clustering, outliers and polyhedra (pp. 5–8);
- the regression MIO, with polyhedra and variable transformations (pp. 8–11);
- all experiments and tables (pp. 11–19);
- the shortcomings section and conclusions (pp. 16–18);
- endnotes (pp. 19–20).

The reference list (p. 20) was skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Romy Shioda (University of Waterloo, p. 1), the first student in the ML lineage of SKILL.md Academic Lineage · **Place in trajectory**: received November 2002, revised April 2005 and April 2006, accepted April 2006 (p. 1); note 06 counts these 41 months. It was handled in the Financial Services review area (p. 1), with support from the Singapore-MIT Alliance (p. 20). SKILL.md dates the MIO-for-statistics line from this submission (Method 2; SW4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO}: Continuous optimization has shaped statistics, but integer optimization has had "very limited impact in statistical computation" (p. 1).
  - Statisticians knew that classification and regression can be written as integer programs. A belief formed in the early 1970s held such programs intractable (p. 1), so the approach "has not been seriously investigated" (pp. 1–2).
  - The *why now* is qualitative: advances in integer optimization over the past decade make large problems solvable in practical times (pp. 1–2).
  - The contribution has two parts: alert statistics and data mining that IO can matter, and show that CRIO matches and often beats the state of the art (p. 2).
  - The starting point was a globally optimal version of CART (p. 4).
  - Transferable: date the belief you overturn, and split the contribution into one claim for the community and one for the method (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: CRIO is a three-stage pipeline (pp. 2–4; §§3.5, 4.6):
  1. Cluster the points, to cut the number of binaries.
  2. Solve one MIO that assigns clusters to K groups and removes outliers.
  3. Turn the groups into polyhedra with continuous programs: SVM-like QPs for classification, LPs for regression. Further LPs delete redundant faces.

  Details by task:
  - Classification groups the Class-1 points so that no Class-0 point lies in a group's convex hull. By Farkas' lemma this becomes one separating-hyperplane system per (Class-0 point, group) pair. Assignment binaries switch each system on through big-M, which is rescaled to M = 1 (problems (1)–(6), pp. 5–6). Violation margins let the model ignore outliers (problem (10), pp. 7–8).
  - Regression assigns clusters to K groups, each with its own coefficients, under absolute loss. It uses a big-M assignment and an outlier group capped at a fraction ρ of the points (problems (15) and (17), pp. 8–10).
  - A new point that falls in no region gets the region of the majority of its F nearest neighbours (pp. 3, 11).
  - The authors found that hyperplane partitions can be modelled "far more tractably" than CART's univariate splits (p. 4).
  - Transferable: encode the condition that no opposite-class point lies inside a group's convex hull with Farkas' lemma: one separating hyperplane per (point, group), switched on by assignment binaries (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: two-class classification, and regression with absolute loss. K is chosen by the user and kept small to avoid over-fitting (pp. 2, 5, 8–9).
  - Hierarchical clustering uses a statistical distance (endnote 3, p. 19). Clusters holding under 1% of the points are dropped as outliers (pp. 7, 11).
  - Synthetic data: 11 Gaussian-mixture sets "generated to favor SVMs with Gaussian kernels", and 11 polyhedral sets generated to favour CRIO and multivariate trees. In both, 5% of the labels are flipped (p. 11).
  - Real data: UCI Cancer, Liver, Diabetes and Heart for classification (pp. 11, 15). For regression: Boston, Abalone (categorical variable dropped), Auto and nine Friedman sets (p. 16).
  - Splits are 50/30/20, repeated 10 times. The test part is kept aside until the parameters are fixed (pp. 11, 16).
  - Text and tables disagree slightly. Cancer has 682 points in the text and 683 in Table 7; Diabetes has 7 variables in the text and 8 in the table; Auto has 5 and 7 (pp. 11, 15, 16, 19).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Farkas' lemma for convex-hull membership (p. 5).
  - Big-M rescaled to 1 by homogeneity in the classification model (p. 6). The runs use M = 1,000 and 10,000 (pp. 11, 16).
  - Size accounting:
    - Problem (6) has K·m1 binaries and K·m0 + K·m0·m1 + m1 rows; clustering cuts the binaries to K·K1 (p. 6).
    - The point-level regression model (15) is hard to solve "in reasonable time" already at n ≈ 200 (p. 9).
    - Problem (17) has K·L binaries instead of K·n, and L is named as the knob between quality and time (p. 10).
  - LP tests for merging clusters (problem (7), p. 6) and for redundant faces (problem (14), p. 8).
  - A pair of soft-separation LPs, one with p'e ≥ 1 and one with p'e ≤ −1, rules out the trivial hyperplane (problems (18)–(19), p. 10).
  - Nonlinear transforms (x², log x, 1/x) are chosen by one-dimensional regressions (p. 10).
  - There is no optimality or generalization theorem. "Globally optimal" refers to the MIO over cluster assignments (pp. 4, 18).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: CPLEX 8.0 on a Linux desktop (p. 11; endnote 6, p. 20).
  - Baselines, all tuned on the validation set (pp. 11, 16):
    - logistic regression and neural networks (MATLAB);
    - SVMfu, with linear, polynomial and Gaussian kernels;
    - CRUISE, with univariate and multivariate trees;
    - the boosting codes GBM and TreeNet;
    - least squares and MARS for regression.

    Salford's CART was dropped because CRUISE matched or beat it in every case (endnote 5, p. 20).
  - Classification wins (p. 14):

    | Opponent | Gaussian sets won | Polyhedral sets won |
    |---|---|---|
    | SVM | 7 of 11 | 9 of 11 |
    | Neural networks | 10 of 11 | not tallied |
    | Multivariate trees and both boosting codes | 11 of 11 | not tallied |
  - The text says CRIO beat every method on all four real sets (p. 15). Table 7 shows GBM's Liver test accuracy at 0.720 against CRIO's 0.717, within one standard deviation (p. 15).
  - CRIO chose K = 1 on most classification sets, and K = 2 only on G6, G8, P6 and Diabetes (pp. 14–15). With K = 1 the MIO reduces to an LP, and 80–99% of the time goes to the QPs (p. 15).
  - No MIO gap or solve status is reported anywhere (pp. 11–19).
  - Regression is "not as convincing" (p. 16). Its run time "explodes" because of big-M (p. 16):
    - about 148 s against 7.7 s for MARS on Abalone (Table 14, p. 19);
    - 3,029–3,307 s against under 4 s for MARS on the Friedman sets with n = 4,000 (Table 11, p. 18).
  - TreeNet times are given only as bounds (endnote 7, p. 20).
  - Failures are printed in a section titled "Discussion on Shortcomings" (pp. 16–18).
  - Transferable: build one synthetic family that favours your strongest rival and one that favours your model, and say which is which (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - CRIO sits between the heuristics of statistical inference (CART, MARS) and the machine-learning and mathematical-programming tradition (SVM) (p. 1).
  - CART is faulted for its "fundamentally greedy approach" (p. 1), and its over-splitting is shown on a checkerboard (Figure 9, pp. 4–5).
  - Credit goes to Arthanari–Dodge for IO formulations of statistical problems, to Bennett–Blue for optimal trees of fixed structure, and to Mangasarian for LP separation (p. 1).
  - Implicit branching (Bienstock; Bertsimas–Shioda 2004) is proposed to remove big-M (p. 17).
  - Cross-reference with note 05 §2.1. The later OCT manuscript judged that CRIO did not reach provable optimality on moderate problems and gained little over CART. Yet S073's own Table 7 puts CRIO above CRUISE's univariate trees on all four real sets (p. 15). Either the verdict concerns a different comparison (perhaps the abandoned univariate attempt, p. 4) or it is a later re-judgement; these texts cannot settle which.
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract and the conclusion close on a hope that statisticians will re-examine IO (pp. 1, 18).
  - A geometric overview comes before the mathematics (§2, pp. 2–5).
  - Wins are counted ("seven out of the 11 cases", p. 14).
  - The conclusion hedges ("more testing is clearly needed", p. 18), yet still claims splits made "in a globally optimal manner" (p. 18).
  - A whole section is given to shortcomings (pp. 16–18).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution: a method, a software package and a computational study (pp. 1–2, 18).
  - Stated limits (pp. 16–17):
    - regression is discontinuous at region boundaries, so a continuous model may suit continuous functions better;
    - CRIO is slower than trees, though faster than boosting in almost all cases and sometimes faster than SVM;
    - it is not interpretable, with "not much that can be done" about it;
    - big-M is weak.
  - Future work (pp. 16–17): a tailored QP solver, implicit branching, stopping at a 5–10% gap or a time limit, continuity constraints and a tighter M.
  - The intended users are those who value accuracy over computation time, for instance in "medical and genetic research" (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (early form). The belief is dated to the early 1970s and conceded as the reason for neglect (pp. 1–2). But the *why now* is a qualitative appeal to the past decade's IO progress, with no speed-up figure rebuilt for this paper (pp. 1–2). The paper was submitted in November 2002 (p. 1), twelve years before the first figure SKILL.md records (2014).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence, qualified. Classification and regression, owned by statistics, are rewritten as MIO plus QP/LP (§§3–4, pp. 5–11). The boundary is printed: problem (15) is too slow at n ≈ 200 (p. 9), and big-M blows up regression run times (p. 16).
  - Qualified because heuristic clustering runs before the MIO, and K = 1 in most runs reduces the MIO to an LP (p. 15).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (the duel with the incumbent) (pp. 11, 14, 20):
  - baselines are tuned on validation data;
  - the stronger tree code is kept (CRUISE over CART);
  - 10 random splits come with standard deviations;
  - one synthetic family is built to favour the strongest rival.
- Method 4 ✗ contradiction (the certificate). No gaps or solve status are reported. The authors propose stopping branch-and-bound at a 5–10% gap or at a time limit, because provable optimality "is not critical in this context" (p. 17). Yet the conclusion claims globally optimal splits (p. 18). This is Tension T1 already in 2007.
- Also in the skill's terms:
  - Heuristic 4 ✅: negative results appear in the body (pp. 16–18).
  - Tension T4 ✅: big-M is named as the regression bottleneck (p. 16).
  - Tension T6 ⚠: the claim to be best on all four real sets is contradicted by the Liver row of Table 7 (p. 15).
  - Research Taste mark 5 ⚠: interpretability is conceded as an unfixable weakness and traded for accuracy (p. 17), the reverse of his later Optimal Trees stance.
- New-pattern candidate: **outlier removal inside the integer model, with a budget**. Classification uses violation margins; regression has an outlier group capped at ρN points (pp. 7, 9–10).
- New-pattern candidate: **shrink-then-solve with a printed quality/time knob**. Clustering with LP-checked merges runs before the MIO, and the number of clusters L is named as the knob (pp. 6, 9–10). S160 is a second instance.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Exclude points from convex hulls through Farkas' lemma, with assignment binaries and big-M rescaled to 1 (pp. 5–6).
- Cut the number of binaries by clustering with LP-checked merges, and expose the number of clusters as the knob between quality and time (pp. 6, 10).
- Put outlier removal inside the assignment MIO, with a budget ρ on the points removed (p. 9).
- Avoid the trivial separating hyperplane: solve one LP with p'e ≥ 1 and one with p'e ≤ −1, and keep the better (p. 10).
- Generate one synthetic family that favours the strongest rival and one that favours you (p. 11).
- Write a shortcomings section that names who should use a competitor instead (pp. 16–17).

**Quotes**:
- "the belief was formed in the early 1970s that these methods are not tractable in practical computational settings." (p. 1)
- "In addition, because a provably optimal solution is not critical in this context, we can prematurely terminate the branch-and-bound procedure, say at 5% to 10% relative optimality gap or by a time limit." (p. 17)

### S106 · A learning approach for interactive marketing to a customer segment (Operations Research 2007, DOI 10.1287/opre.1070.0427)
**Read level**: full for the article. All 16 file pages were read:
- introduction (pp. 1–3) and related work (pp. 3–4);
- the model and the value-of-information results (pp. 4–7);
- the approximate dynamic programming method (ADP) and its extensions (pp. 7–11);
- the Interval heuristic (pp. 11–12);
- simulations (pp. 12–16) and future research (pp. 14–15).

The online companion is not in the file and was not read. It holds Appendix A (proofs of Propositions 2–4), Appendix B (convexity and upper bound of the relaxed value) and Appendix C (evidence on how to choose λ).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Adam J. Mersereau (UNC Kenan-Flagler, p. 1). The work is part of his MIT ORC PhD thesis (p. 16), and note 04 lists Mersereau (2003) among the PhD students on the homepage · **Place in trajectory**: received December 2003, revised in 2005 and 2006, accepted December 2006 (p. 1). Endnote 2 gives earlier working titles (p. 16). It belongs to the SD bandit thread that SKILL.md Method 2 step 6 follows to 2026.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD + AP (marketing); DA}:
  - Interactive marketing (direct mail, catalogues, banner ads, paid search) needs automated, data-driven decisions about which message to send (p. 1).
  - Offline experiments are costly, slow and unrepresentative, so learning has to happen during operations (p. 1).
  - Decisions come in batches, because of simultaneous contacts, delayed responses, offline database updates or system-wide constraints. The classic bandit samples one at a time (pp. 1–2).
  - The literature has "very little work" on this and "no consensus" on a framework (p. 1).
  - The problem: M messages, N customer encounters per stage, independent Bernoulli responses with beta priors, T stages; maximize expected successes (p. 2).
  - Transferable: name the operational reasons the textbook model breaks (batching, delays, offline databases, budgets) before you extend it (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The exact dynamic program over beta-posterior counts (§3.2, pp. 5–6) has about 10^8 states for N = 10, T = 10, M = 3, and 10^34 for N = 10, T = 5, M = 10 (p. 7). The paper gives two approximations.
  - **ADP**:
    - Relax the per-stage constraint Σ_m x_m = N with a Lagrange multiplier, so that the dynamic program separates by message (pp. 7–8).
    - Keep the multiplier constant in time and set it by bisection, typically seven iterations, so that the decoupled choices nearly meet the constraint (pp. 8–9).
    - Restore feasibility with a one-stage problem (11), re-solved each period over the remaining horizon (pp. 8–9).
    - Approximate the subproblems with a lookahead of H = 2, blocks of B = N/10 and value functions interpolated from a few samples (pp. 9–10, 12).
  - **Interval**: Lai's (1987) asymptotic upper-confidence rule, extended to batches. Customers are assigned one by one, and each interval widens within the batch without moving its midpoint (pp. 11–12).
  - The ADP also extends to budgets across segments and to customers who migrate between segments, through value adjustments (pp. 10–11).
  - Transferable: decouple a weakly coupled dynamic program by pricing the coupling constraint with one scalar, chosen by bisection so that the decoupled decisions are nearly feasible (pp. 7–9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Customers are pre-segmented (RFM variables, contact histories, demographics) into homogeneous, independent segments. This separates customer modelling from message optimization (p. 1).
  - Messages are independent given the decisions. These independence assumptions are named the "key assumptions ensuring tractability", and they are conservative when messages are in fact dependent (p. 2).
  - Rewards are Bernoulli with beta priors, read as counts from offline pre-experiments (p. 4). N is constant and exogenous (p. 5). Migration matrices are assumed known "as an approximation for tractability" (p. 11).
  - Data are simulated only: true success probabilities p_m are drawn from beta(s̄, f̄), and priors are strengthened by a_m preliminary experiments (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Lemma 1: splitting a batch into two sequential batches leaves the distribution of outcomes unchanged (by Vandermonde's identity; proof omitted) (p. 6).
  - Proposition 1, the value of information, by backward induction. It uses the convexity of the final-stage value, a maximum of linear functions, and Jensen's inequality (pp. 6–7).
  - Propositions 2–4: the optimal reward is superadditive in N and in T, and for a fixed total, more and smaller batches do better (p. 7). The proofs are in the online appendix, not read.
  - The relaxed value is convex in λ and an upper bound (p. 8; proof in Appendix B, not read).
  - Proposition 5: for λ ≤ 0 or λ ≥ 1 the method reduces to greedy, so only λ ∈ (0, 1) matters (p. 9).
  - Counts: O(T³N²) states and about T³N⁴ operations per subproblem (p. 9).
  - Transferable: before approximating, prove the exact model's structural facts and turn them into design advice, such as using more and smaller batches (pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Benchmarks (p. 12):
    - Ideal, an oracle upper bound;
    - Exact, for tiny cases;
    - Greedy (play-the-leader);
    - GGreedy, which ranks messages by Gittins index (discount 0.90);
    - Interval and ADP.
  - On tiny exact cases (two messages, up to 8 stages, up to 12 customers), Greedy, ADP and Interval match Exact "in most of the simulations" (p. 13).
  - There are 13 large configurations, each run on 2,000 simulated problems, with batches up to 1,000 (Tables 1–2, pp. 13–14). ADP's regret reduction ranges from −6.0% to 38.3% against Greedy, and from −6.4% to 3.2% against Interval (Table 2, p. 14). The bold marks for 95% significance in Table 1 are lost in the extraction.
  - Gains are largest when prior information is weak (p. 13; Figure 2(b), pp. 14–15).
  - ADP trails Interval for long horizons and small batches, which the authors attribute to its limited lookahead (p. 13).
  - A lookahead of H = 2 beats H = 1, and larger H adds little (p. 14; Table 3, p. 15).
  - CPU per stage on a 2.4 GHz Xeon: Interval below 0.005 s and 0.02 s; ADP with H = 2 0.53–0.69 s (Table 4, p. 16).
  - A variant in which the method also chooses batch sizes gave little gain beyond three periods (p. 5).
  - There is no real data and no field test (pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable: benchmark against an oracle bound, an exact solver on tiny instances and the greedy rules, and report regret reductions with their sign (pp. 12–14).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The marketing work is sorted into three groups (pp. 2–3): myopic choice models (Bult–Wansbeek, Rossi et al., Ansari–Mela); lifetime-value dynamic programs (Bitran–Mondschein, Simester et al., Gönül–Shi); and two learning studies, one of them "not implemented or tested".
  - On the methods side (pp. 3–4): Gittins indices; Lai–Robbins and Lai's asymptotics; Anantharam et al. on multiple plays, with at most one sample per arm; and clinical-trial designs with few stages.
  - The decomposition lineage is credited (p. 7): Meuleau et al., Castañon, Yost–Washburn, Hawkins' thesis and Whittle's restless bandits, then later Caro–Gallien and Adelman–Mersereau.
  - Earlier work is restated rather than attacked.
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The problem is "difficult to solve but simple to state" (p. 2).
  - A paragraph sets out the modelling sacrifices made for tractability (p. 2).
  - The authors claim to be "among the first to propose a workable solution" (p. 14).
  - The results end in a practical rule for choosing a method: Greedy may be enough; Interval when speed matters; ADP when extensions matter (p. 14).
  - The future-research section is concrete (pp. 14–15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution: a framework, two methods and structural insights (p. 3).
  - Limits:
    - the customer model is simpler than marketing's choice models (pp. 2, 14–15);
    - Interval does not extend to features that span segments (pp. 11–12);
    - ADP's lookahead fails for long horizons with small batches (p. 13);
    - Propositions 3–4 need the undiscounted case (p. 6).
  - Future work (pp. 14–15): dependence among messages and segments; customer heterogeneity; approximations of Bayes updates; non-Bayesian or asymptotic optimality; and field tests, which need decision control that marketers rarely cede.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant {SD}. The exact model comes first (a dynamic program), then a relaxation that keeps a decomposable structure. The multiplier reads as a price whose only meaningful range is (0, 1) (pp. 5–9). The authors prefer the ADP over the faster heuristic because, being built on a mathematical programming formulation, it extends to budgets and migration (pp. 10–11, 14).
  - It is a variant because the class is a dynamic program, not LP, conic or MIO, and the only solve-time evidence is CPU per stage (Table 4, p. 16).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. Decisions are scored by successes and regret against the myopic play-the-leader and Gittins-greedy rules (pp. 12–14), which follow the logic of the existing myopic marketing models (p. 2). But this happens only in simulation. The field test is named as the missing step, together with its obstacle (pp. 14–15).
- Method 6 ✅ weak evidence. A joint paper drawn from a student's thesis (p. 16) opens the bandit thread that SKILL.md follows to 2026.
- Also in the skill's terms: Heuristic 4 ✅. The paper prints ADP's losses to Interval, and in two configurations to Greedy (Table 2, p. 14). It also prints the weak batch-size variant (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: **Lagrangian decomposition of a weakly coupled stochastic dynamic program, with one multiplier found by bisection** (pp. 7–9).
- New-pattern candidate: **structural propositions of the exact model as design advice, before approximating** (pp. 6–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Price the per-stage coupling constraint with one multiplier, constant in time and found by bisection; restore feasibility with a one-stage re-solve each period (pp. 8–9).
- Prove the meaningful range of the multiplier: outside (0, 1) the method collapses to greedy (Proposition 5, p. 9).
- Approximate subproblem value functions by linear interpolation on a few samples, after plotting their typical shapes (p. 9; Figure 1, p. 10).
- Batch a one-at-a-time UCB rule: assign customers sequentially within the batch, widening each interval without moving its midpoint (p. 12).
- Benchmark against an oracle upper bound, an exact method on tiny instances and the greedy rules, and report regret reductions with their sign (pp. 12–14).

**Quotes**:
- "The fact that the ADP approach is based on a mathematical programming formulation makes it extendable." (p. 14)
- "Testing an adaptive learning model requires not just data but also decision-making control in a real-world system." (p. 15)

### S050 · Algorithmic prediction of health-care costs (Operations Research 2008, DOI 10.1287/opre.1080.0619)
**Read level**: full for the article. All 11 file pages were read: introduction, data and error measures, methods, results and conclusions. The reference list (pp. 10–11) was skimmed. The online Appendices A–D (variable lists, details of the trees and the clustering) are not in the file and were not read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors** (p. 1): Margrét V. Bjarnadóttir (Stanford GSB); Michael A. Kane (MIT Medical); J. Christian Kryder and Rudra Pandey (D2Hawkeye, Waltham); Santosh Vempala (Georgia Tech); Grant Wang (MIT EECS). The acknowledgment thanks D2Hawkeye's engineering team for data support (p. 10). The paper states no relationship between Bertsimas and D2Hawkeye (pp. 1, 10). Notes 03 and 06 record a board seat in 2003–2009 (from the CVs) and a claim to have co-founded it (from the oral history) · **Place in trajectory**: received January 2007 and accepted July 2008, in a special issue on OR in health care (p. 1). It anchors the 2001–2013 health-data row of SKILL.md and Method 5 step 2.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP (health care) + ML; DA as a first step}: Health-care costs are rising, and accurate prediction is "a significant first step" (p. 1). The gap has two parts:
  - Validation. Claims-based prediction has used regression and heuristic rules since the 1980s without validation on unseen populations (p. 1). Most earlier studies report no out-of-sample predictability (p. 2).
  - The yardstick. R² is a relative measure, dominated by outliers and not comparable across classes of method (p. 2).

  The problem: predict third-year cost from two years of claims and cost data (pp. 1–2).
  - Transferable: enter an applied field by questioning its validation practice and its yardstick before proposing a method (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The methods are off the shelf; the work goes into features, targets and error measures. No new algorithm is proposed.
  - Methods: classification trees (p. 6), and a spectral clustering adapted from EigenCluster. It clusters first on monthly costs, weighting later months more, and then on medical data within each cost cluster (pp. 6–8).
  - Features: grouped codes, risk factors, the cost trajectory, and a "spike" indicator that separates acute from chronic cost profiles (pp. 3–4).
  - Target: one of five cost buckets, each holding the same total dollars (p. 4).
  - Measures: hit ratio, an asymmetric penalty, absolute prediction error (APE) and a median-anchored measure R (pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable: when the cost level alone mixes up acute and chronic cases, encode the shape of the cost trajectory (spike or plateau) as one indicator (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - 838,242 commercially insured members from 2,866 employers. The 24-month observation window runs 8/1/2004–7/31/2006, and the 12-month result window 8/1/2006–7/31/2007 (p. 2).
  - Only members with full coverage in the result window are modelled, "in the interest of simplicity", although including the partial-coverage population improves the error measures (p. 2).
  - Random equal thirds serve for learning, validation and test. The test set is held back to the end (p. 2).
  - Codes are grouped (p. 3; Table 1): over 13,000 diagnoses into 218 groups, over 22,000 procedures into 180 and over 45,000 drugs into 336. With over 700 risk factors, there are 1,523 variables in all.
  - Buckets run from under $3,200 (83.9% of members) to over $50,000 (0.5%, or 1,175 members in the learning sample). Each bucket holds about $117 million (p. 4; Table 2).
  - 8% of members carry 70% of the cost (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for optimization models and proofs; the analysis concerns error measures.
  - R²'s denominator, broken down by bucket: the top bucket (0.5% of members) contributes 27.9% of it. Truncating costs at $100,000 cuts this to 16% (Table 4, p. 5).
  - A median-anchored measure, R = 1 − Σ|t − f| / Σ|t − m|, which is 0 for the median forecast (p. 5).
  - Relative reductions in the error sums, bucket by bucket (Tables 13–14, pp. 9–10).
  - Transferable: before reporting R², break its denominator down by segment, and add an absolute-error analogue anchored at the median (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - The baseline carries the last 12 months' cost forward. Overall results (Table 6, p. 6; Table 11, p. 9):

    | Method | Hit ratio | Penalty | APE |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Baseline (last 12 months) | 80.0% | 0.431 | $2,677 |
    | Classification trees | 84.6% | 0.386 | $2,243 |
    | Clustering | 84.3% | 0.374 | $1,977 |

    Against the baseline, APE improves by over 16%, the penalty by 10.5% and the hit ratio by about 5% (p. 8).
  - In the top bucket, the hit ratio rises from 19.3% to 36.9% with trees and to 42.7% with clustering. APE falls by over 50% and by over 58% (p. 8; Table 11, p. 9).
  - Models that use cost alone do as well in the low buckets (Table 12, p. 9; p. 8).
  - A separate tree is tuned for each error measure (p. 7).
  - Comparison with other studies, "as an indication only" (p. 8): APE of 78.8% and 89.4% of the mean, against 93% and 98%; R² of 0.162 and 0.180, against 0.099–0.154.
  - The baseline's R² is negative, because it fails on the top bucket (p. 9; Table 13, p. 10).
  - There is one random split, and no confidence intervals or tests.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper argues against regression-based risk adjustment (Ash, Zhao, Cumming, Powers et al.) and its habit of reporting R² (pp. 1–2).
  - It acknowledges doubts about claims data (Jolins et al., Dans) as well as evidence that they are useful (p. 1).
  - The clustering comes from co-authors' work (EigenCluster, Kannan et al.) (p. 6).
  - The text does not name the tree software, but the reference list includes CRUISE binaries (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing**: Three key findings, (a)–(c), come first, in the abstract and the introduction (pp. 1–2). Positions are marked as such ("In our view", p. 2), and the cross-study comparison is hedged (p. 8). Finding (c) is worded three ways:
  - abstract: medical information helps only for high-cost members (p. 1);
  - introduction: medical information helps the clustering, while trees do as well on cost alone (p. 2);
  - conclusion: cost is the best predictor for all but the highest-cost patients (p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution: an application, plus a way of evaluating it (error measures aligned with the decision); no new algorithm (pp. 1, 9–10).
  - Limits: comparisons across data sets are not fully valid (p. 8); the population is restricted to full coverage (p. 2).
  - Future work: reimbursement and insurance pricing, which need integration with health economics and system design; patient intervention (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence.
  - The baseline is the owner's naive rule (last year's cost), much stronger than random (p. 5).
  - The error measures follow the intervention decision (p. 4). Under-forecasts are penalised twice as much as over-forecasts, and the buckets map to wellness, disease-management and case-management programmes.
  - The data come from a partner company whose staff co-author the paper (pp. 1, 10).
  - One nuance: predictions, not decisions, are scored, and prescription is left as the next step (p. 1).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. The out-of-sample protocol has a sealed test set and a strong naive incumbent (pp. 2, 5–6). But there is no exact model, warm start or certificate (trees and clustering are heuristics), and the comparison with published studies is "as an indication only" (p. 8).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant. Here the company comes before the paper: data and two co-authors come from D2Hawkeye (pp. 1, 10). This reverses Method 6's order, in which a company comes last, after the course and the book. Note 06 dates the company tie to 2001–2003.

**Transferable techniques**:

### S109 · The air traffic flow management problem: An integer optimization approach (IPCO 2008, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-68891-4_3)
**Read level**: full. All 13 file pages were read: introduction and literature (pp. 1–3), the model and valid inequalities (pp. 3–8), regional and national experiments (pp. 8–12), conclusions and references (pp. 12–13).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Guglielmo Lulli (Milano-Bicocca) and Amedeo Odoni (MIT), his PhD adviser per SKILL.md Academic Lineage (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: IPCO 2008, LNCS 5035 (p. 1). It continues the air traffic flow management (ATFM) line, which runs from Odoni (1987) through Vranas–Bertsimas–Odoni (1994), Bertsimas–Stock (1998) and Bertsimas–Stock Patterson (2000) (pp. 1–2, 12–13). It sits in the AP air-transportation folder.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO + AP (air transportation); LO for the relaxation}:
  - Congestion and delay: about one US flight in four was delayed or cancelled in 2000, and delays cost US airlines $5.9 billion in 2005 (p. 1).
  - ATFM had focused on airports and ground delays (p. 1). En-route sector capacity, however, is a lasting limit. It calls for a network model with more options: ground and airborne holding, speed control and rerouting (p. 2).
  - The gap lies in the authors' own earlier models (p. 2):
    - Bertsimas–Stock (1998) computes efficiently, thanks to facet-defining constraints, but fixes each flight's route.
    - Bertsimas–Stock Patterson (2000) reroutes but does not reach real instance sizes.
  - Rerouting is called "one of the greatest challenges" of the field (p. 2). The goal is to combine the "flexibility" of the 2000 model with the mathematical properties of the 1998 one (p. 2).
  - Transferable: frame a new model as the union of two predecessors' strengths, and name which strength comes from which (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep the 1998 time-indexed variables: w = 1 if flight f has reached sector j *by* time t (p. 5). Then add rerouting without new variables (pp. 3–6; constraints (4)–(6)):
  - Each origin–destination route is a maximal chain of an acyclic sector digraph (a poset).
  - Local conditions make each flight follow one route. A flight enters a sector only after spending the minimum time in one predecessor, and leaves to exactly one successor.

  The objective weighs airborne delay α > 1 times as much as ground delay. Super-linear tardiness costs (t − a_f)^{1+ε} spread delay for equity (p. 5). Three classes of valid inequalities (fork, joint, antichain), plus a condition that turns constraints (5) into equalities, strengthen the relaxation (Propositions 1–4, pp. 6–8).
  - Transferable: replace path variables by local precedence conditions on a DAG of alternatives. Routes become maximal chains, and antichains give clique-type cuts (pp. 3–4, 7).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model (pp. 4–6): deterministic capacities per period for airport departures, airport arrivals and sectors; minimum times in each sector; time windows; turnaround links between continued flights. The route digraph is taken to be acyclic "without loss of generality" (p. 3).
  - Instances (p. 8): randomly generated, with sizes "comparable to realistic ones".
    - The airspace is a grid of equal sectors with equal minimum times.
    - Airports are hubs and regionals, with no flights between two regionals.
    - Demand is uniform, at 15 flights per period in peaks and 8 off-peak.
    - A bad-weather front cuts the capacity of three sectors for five periods, then moves on.
  - Route sets: the sectors of the shortest route, plus the neighbours of any sector above 80% of capacity. This gives about 3 forks per origin–destination pair, and up to 121 routes with 20 forks (p. 9).
  - No real flight data are used.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Cumulative "by" variables: a flight's presence in a sector is the difference of two variables, and monotonicity in time is a chain of inequalities (pp. 5–6).
  - The poset encoding: routes are maximal chains, and a single route is enforced by local conditions (pp. 3–4).
  - The fork and joint inequalities are proved by two-case arguments on constraints (4), (5) and (8) (Propositions 1–2, pp. 6–7). The antichain inequality follows directly from the poset (Proposition 3, p. 7).
  - Proposition 4 gives the condition under which constraints (5) hold as equalities (p. 8).
  - There are no facet proofs for the new inequalities and no complexity results.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: CPLEX 9.0 MIP through AMPL, on a four-processor 3 GHz AMD-Xeon with 8 GB of RAM under Linux. A 1% optimality gap is accepted, and clique cuts are generated at a moderate level (p. 9).
  - Regional instances (pp. 9–11; Table 1):
    - Size: 20 airports (10 hubs), 113 sectors (about a third of the US airspace), 20 periods of 15 minutes, about 3,000 flights, with 50% to 90% of flights connecting.
    - After preprocessing: about 270,000 constraints and 150,000 variables.
    - Mean time: 241 s over the 41 feasible instances (recomputed from Table 1).
    - One instance takes over 10 minutes (724 s). It is the only one that branched (68 nodes).
    - The 5 lowest-capacity instances are infeasible (Table 1, p. 10).
    - Unlike the 1994 model, this one does not slow down near the "infeasibility border" (p. 11).
  - National instances (pp. 11–12; Table 2):
    - Size: 30 airports, 145 sectors, 22 periods and 6,475 flights (5,180 connecting); about 570,000 constraints and 305,000 variables; a 3,600 s limit.
    - The stated mean and median times are 987 s and 710 s. The ten times listed in Table 2 give 1,032 s and 773 s, and the text does not say which runs were averaged.
    - At 50% capacity no 1% solution was found within 3,600 s (gap 1.05%, 332 nodes). With a 3% tolerance, the gap is 2.58% after 569 s.
  - The value of rerouting at 10% capacity (p. 12): ground delay falls from 949 to 733 and airborne delay from 150 to 126, with 220 flights (3.4%) rerouted. The stated "almost 30%" and 19% are relative to the rerouted values (216/733, 24/126). Relative to the baseline without rerouting they are 22.8% and 16.0%.
  - Larger weather fronts and other front speeds were tested but not reported, "for the sake of brevity" (pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable: list per instance the time, gap, cut counts by family and simplex iterations; keep the infeasible instances in the table; name the instances that branched (Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Odoni (1987) is credited as the first to formalize ground holding (p. 1).
  - Helme's multicommodity flow model was weak computationally, and Lindsay et al. built disaggregate 0-1 models (p. 2).
  - The paper positions itself against the authors' own 1998 and 2000 models (p. 2), and against the 1994 model's slow-down near infeasibility (p. 11).
  - Four of the ten references are the first author's own (pp. 12–13).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract promises times of "less than 15 minutes" at US scale and a "main engine" for US air traffic (p. 1). The conclusion repeats this, as something that "makes us optimistic" (p. 12).
  - The body has national runs above 15 minutes: the stated mean of 987 s, and runs of 988 s and 3,600 s (Table 2, p. 11).
  - The outline promises "indications for future research" in Section 4 (p. 3), which gives none (p. 12).
  - Each inequality is illustrated on a toy digraph (Figures 2–4, pp. 7–8).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution: a formulation (rerouting without new variables), valid inequalities, and computation on synthetic instances of US size (pp. 2, 12).
  - There is no limits section. Visible limits (pp. 8–12):
    - the instances are synthetic;
    - capacities are deterministic;
    - a gap tolerance stands in for optimality;
    - the benefit of rerouting is measured against the same model without rerouting, not against current ATFM practice.
  - No future work is stated (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence. Rerouting is added inside the same 0-1 class with no new variables, and the poset inequalities keep the LP relaxation strong: only one of the 41 feasible regional instances branched (pp. 2–8, 10–11). The boundary is printed: the infeasible instances, and the national instance at 50% capacity (pp. 10–12).
