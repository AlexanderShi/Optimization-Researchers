### S078 · An analytics approach to designing combination chemotherapy regimens for cancer (Management Science 62(5), 2016, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.2015.2363)
Read level: **full**: the Articles in Advance version; body and Appendices A–B read in full (pp. 2–21), reference list skimmed (pp. 21–22); title and all four authors confirmed on p. 2. The electronic companion (the list of 72 drugs, p. 8) is not in the file and was not read.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Allison O'Hair (Stanford GSB), Stephen Relyea (MIT Lincoln Laboratory), John Silberholz (MIT Sloan and ORC) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: health-care analytics {AP DA} at the start of the 2014–2019 period; received 1 October 2014, accepted 23 September 2015 (p. 2); supported by NSF and Lincoln Laboratory; the acknowledgments credit semiautomated extraction forms (Natasha Markuzon) and "over 20 MIT undergraduate students in the early stages of this research" (p. 18): the data asset behind Heuristic 6.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP+DA+IO}: Most phase II trials in advanced gastric cancer test a new regimen (89.4%), and most of these test new combinations of drugs already tested (84.6%) (p. 2); combinations are many, a trial costs more than $10 million (phase II) or $20 million (phase III), and phase II populations are unrepresentative (p. 3). The entry is the gap left by three neighbours: patient-level prediction rules cannot predict unseen regimens; meta-regressions explain existing randomized trials with few drug variables (three in Hsu et al. 2012) and no out-of-sample test; preclinical models use no patient outcomes (pp. 3–4). Stated problem: predict trial outcomes before the trial is run and select the regimens to test in phase II and III, with the central objective of better phase III regimens than current practice (p. 3). Gastric cancer is chosen as the third cause of cancer death, with no standard regimen and many trials (pp. 4–5).
- **D2 Key idea**: Three linked stages: build the trial-arm database (pp. 5–7); predict an arm's median overall survival (OS) and its share of patients with a dose-limiting toxicity (DLT) from earlier trials with regularized regressions (pp. 8–10); then select. For phase II, a MIO picks a never-tested regimen of at most three drugs that maximizes predicted OS plus an exploration bonus Γ·u_d, u_d = t_d^(−1/2) with t_d the number of prior trials of drug d, subject to predicted DLT ≤ t (model (1), pp. 12–13). For phase III, previously tested phase II regimens are ranked by predicted OS in a typical phase III population instead of by their raw phase II results, which favour small and healthy trials (the top five by reported data average 37 patients against 51 for the top five by model, pp. 14–15). Toxicity enters as a constraint, not an objective, because no phase III trial in the database lists toxicity as its primary objective (p. 12).
- **D3 Model and data**: Inclusion: phase I/II, II and III trials for advanced or metastatic gastric (including gastroesophageal-junction and esophageal) cancer published in English by March 2012; exclusions: sequential treatments, radiotherapy, curable stages, GIST (p. 5). A single person screened the PubMed and Cochrane results (350 trials), a reference search added 64, giving 414 trials and 495 arms, and a single person extracted the data (p. 5). Efficacy is median OS, the primary objective of 60% of phase III trials (p. 5), reported for 93.5% of arms (p. 6); toxicity is the share of patients with at least one DLT, with DLT definitions read from the protocols of 20 combined phase I/II trials and the 0.5 threshold taken as the median of the 33–67% range used in them (pp. 6–7). Covariates: 20 demographic and study variables, and for each of 72 drugs a binary, an instantaneous dose and an average weekly dose (216 variables) (pp. 7–8). Linear models with error variance σ²/n_i, hence weights n_i/n̄ (p. 8). Missing covariates are mean-filled or imputed (p. 7; App. A.1, p. 19); Grade 4 blood toxicities were imputed in 230 arms (46.5%) (p. 19). Admitted simplifications: performance status is the only treatment-selection factor controlled (p. 6); trastuzumab is excluded because it is indicated only for HER2-positive patients (pp. 6, 13); interaction terms were tried and dropped (p. 10). The MIO's feasible set: at most one drug per class, five disallowed class pairs, new drugs forced in, doses restricted to values seen in some trial (p. 13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: (i) MIO (1): binaries b_d with continuous instantaneous and average doses; the ridge predictors are linear in all of them, so objective and toxicity constraint are linear; cardinality N = 3, which covers 89.1% of the database and all NCCN-preferred regimens; class constraints Ab ≤ c; a novelty constraint (b, i, a) ∉ P; dose sets Ω_d (pp. 12–13). How (1d) and (1e) are linearized is not shown. (ii) The exploration bonus is motivated by the standard deviation of a sample mean, which scales as t_d^(−1/2) (footnote 11, p. 12). (iii) The DLT composite: the share of patients with any DLT cannot be computed from the marginal toxicities, so five combination rules are compared, from the max rule (a lower bound) to the sum rule (an upper bound), by correlation with the reported total Grade 3/4 toxicity on the 36 arms that report it; the grouped-independent rule wins with 0.893 (pp. 19–20). (iv) Imputation chooses a linear or quadratic fit by residual sum of squares (p. 19). No theorems.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Prediction is tested chronologically: arms are sorted by publication date, each is predicted only from earlier trials and never from another arm of the same trial, and testing starts after the first 98 arms (20%), giving 347 survival and 307 toxicity test points (p. 9). Models: unregularized least squares, ridge, lasso, random forests (500 trees, node size 5) and RBF-kernel SVMs, tuned by 10-fold cross-validation (p. 8), in R 3.0.1 (p. 9); the baseline is the mean of earlier outcomes, because oncologists publish no predictions to compare with (pp. 9–10). In the last four-year window, ridge reaches out-of-sample R² 0.56 (bootstrap 0.53 [0.50, 0.57]) and RMSE 2.50 against 3.75 for the baseline; lasso's out-of-sample R² is 0.58; toxicity AUC is 0.83 for ridge and 0.87 for the SVM (Figures 2–3, p. 10). Ridge is carried forward as accurate enough, cheapest and simplest (pp. 9–10). Flagging: 10% of high-toxicity trials can be flagged while flagging 0.4% of acceptable ones, 20% at 1.9%; for efficacy, 10% at 0.8% and 20% at 3.1% (p. 11). Imputation check: AUC 0.817 on the 265 arms without imputed outcomes against 0.828 on all (p. 19). Regimen selection is evaluated from 1997 on over the 27 phase III experimental arms, twice: a simulation metric (40 bootstrap ground-truth coefficient sets × 9 settings t ∈ {0.3, 0.4, 0.5}, Γ ∈ {0, 2, 4}, simulated outcomes fed back into training; pp. 15–16, App. B p. 20) and a matching metric (each suggestion scored by the most similar future trial, with penalties 0/1/10/100 for dose, drug and class mismatch; pp. 15–16). Results: simulated median OS is higher in all 360 runs, by 2.8 months (95% CI 0.9, 5.0) to 4.7 months (2.6, 6.5); the high-DLT rate does not differ significantly; differences between parameter settings all have CIs that include zero (p. 16). Matching: +2.3, +3.5 and +3.9 months, with +0.02, +0.05 and +0.12 in the share of high-toxicity arms, for t = 0.3, 0.4, 0.5 (p. 17); suggestions match all drugs of their matched trial in 56–59% of cases (p. 18). Reported ways in which the model's choices differ from practice: 100% of phase II and 98% of phase III suggestions use exactly three drugs, against 31% and 37% in practice; the newest drug in the model's phase III picks had a median of 5 prior trials, against 22; phase II suggestions with new combinations are 14–37% against 41% in practice (pp. 16–17). Toxicity AUC dips at the end of the period as new drugs enter (p. 9). No MIO solver, version or solve time is reported; the only mention of solving is a pseudocode line for model (1) (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Contrasts itself with clinical prediction rules, meta-analysis and meta-regression, and preclinical models (pp. 3–4), and presents itself as a complement to the last: in vitro tests could vet suggested regimens, and the models could score regimens designed by other means (p. 4). Claims three firsts: the database, out-of-sample prediction for arbitrary drug combinations, and optimization-based regimen design (p. 3). Exploration methods deferred to future work: the knowledge gradient (Frazier, Powell and Dayanik 2008) and Q-learning (Dearden et al. 1998) (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing**: An aspiration paragraph, then bulleted contributions each closing on a "to our knowledge" first (p. 3); Table 1 pairs each model with its evaluation technique (p. 4); a glossary of clinical-trial terms (Table 2, p. 5). Each surrogate metric is followed by a paragraph on which direction its bias runs, and results are read asymmetrically: favourable results merit a trial, unfavourable ones end the line (pp. 15–16). The abstract is hedged ("might improve", "merit further testing", p. 2) but says toxicity outcomes are unchanged, which rests on intervals that include zero while the matching point estimates reach +0.12 at t = 0.5; the discussion softens it to "without major changes" (pp. 2, 17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Data asset, prediction models, phase II design and phase III selection models, and two preclinical evaluation metrics (pp. 3, 17). Stated limits: no trial was run (p. 17); the simulation assumes the ridge structure is true and may score biologically infeasible regimens, a bias in the model's favour; the matching metric scores a regimen in another population, a bias either way (pp. 15–16); outcomes of new drugs cannot be predicted (p. 4); the HER2-positive subgroup is left out (p. 13). Future: a single-arm trial of an optimized regimen, then a randomized trial against standard design; preclinical constraints and drug–drug interaction terms; other diseases and subgroups (p. 18). Not discussed: single-person screening and extraction (p. 5) with no second-reader check reported; the three-drug signature, which the authors trace to "the nature of the formulation" and set against researchers' wish to test a range of sizes, without changing the model (pp. 16–17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (pp. 3, 14–18): the owner is the trial planner; the baseline is the regimens oncologists actually chose for the 27 phase III experimental arms; decisions are scored by outcomes (simulated and matched OS, high-DLT share), not by prediction accuracy; the paper says what its evaluation cannot show and asks for a single-arm trial, then a randomized one. This is the step-6 instance the skill already cites.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (pp. 12–13, 20): fitted linear predictors keep regimen design inside a MIO, but the stop rule's measured solve time is missing: no solver, version or run time.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (pp. 9, 15–17): no certificate or gap is reported for model (1); the duel is against practitioners' historical choices, not a warm-start heuristic, and the prediction duel is against a running mean.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pp. 13, 16–17): two knobs (toxicity limit t, exploration weight Γ) on a 3 × 3 grid with a published table of survival gain and toxicity change; no knob–property bound, and differences between settings are within noise.
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted): Heuristic 6 ✅ evidence: the field lacked the data (p. 3); the authors built it (pp. 5–7) with more than 20 undergraduates early on (p. 18) and split prediction (§3) from selection (§4); the 2008 start year given in Heuristic 6 is not stated in this paper. Tension T6 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 17).
- new-pattern candidate: **surrogate evaluation with stated bias direction** (two metrics with different failure modes, read asymmetrically) (pp. 14–16).
- new-pattern candidate: **counterfactual replay against the historical decision-maker** (history rerun from 1997: the model chooses where oncologists chose, simulated outcomes are fed back) (pp. 15–16, 20); also [card S143, p. 4] and, as matched imputation, [card S079, p. 5].

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Chronological out-of-sample testing for anything built from historical cases: sort by date, predict each case only from earlier ones, keep sibling records (other arms of the same trial) out, and start after a burn-in share (p. 9).
- (D5) When a decision cannot be field-tested yet, evaluate it with two surrogates whose biases run in different directions (a simulator over bootstrap-plausible ground truths; matching to the closest real future case), state each bias, and let only favourable results justify the next, costlier test (pp. 14–16; App. B, pp. 20–21).
- (D4) Put an exploration bonus proportional to (prior tests)^(−1/2) of each component into the optimization objective, with a single weight Γ swept in the evaluation (pp. 12, 16).
- (D3) Restrict continuous decisions to values observed in the data (doses used in some trial), so the optimizer cannot propose unprecedented quantities (constraint (1e), p. 13).
- (D3) Read thresholds and composite definitions off the domain's own documents (DLT lists and the 0.5 cut-off from phase I protocols) instead of inventing them (pp. 6–7).
- (D4) Bracket an aggregate that cannot be computed from marginals between a max rule (lower bound) and a sum rule (upper bound), and pick a rule in between by correlation on the cases that report the aggregate (pp. 19–20).
- (D5) Report how the optimizer's decisions differ in kind from practice (regimen size, age of drugs, novelty share), not only in outcome (pp. 16–17).

**Quotes**:
- "such a database did not exist before this study" (p. 3)
- "would suggest that no further evaluation is warranted" (p. 15)

### S081 · OR Forum—An algorithmic approach to linear regression (Operations Research 64(1), 2–16, 2016, DOI 10.1287/opre.2015.1436)
Read level: **full**: pp. 2–16 read in full, including Tables 1–13 and the authors' biographies (p. 16); reference list (pp. 15–16) skimmed. Title and both authors confirmed on pp. 1–2.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Angela King (MIT ORC PhD 2015 under Bertsimas, thesis on regression under a modern optimization lens; p. 16) · **Position in trajectory**: opening of the statistics-under-a-modern-optimization-lens programme {ML IO}; the biography calls the paper "part of his 2013 Morse lectureship" (p. 16), where the skill dates the Morse lecture to 2014; an OR Forum paper received and accepted in September 2015 (p. 2); it rests on the best-subset paper (cited as forthcoming in AoS) for warm starts and big-M estimation (pp. 5, 8); partly funded by the MIT–Accenture alliance (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+IO}: Regression models are built "through trial and error" to balance competing goals: predictive power, interpretability, significance, robustness to error in data, sparsity (abstract, p. 2). The authors consulted four regression textbooks and found the goals treated one at a time with little guidance on how to balance them; they adopt the six-step procedure of Chatterjee et al. as "the standard approach", whose authors call model building an art; two modelers with the same data end with quite different models (p. 3). The task "lends itself naturally" to MIQO but has not been modelled so because of the belief that MIQO is intractable at scale (abstract, p. 2). Aspiration: an algorithm that meets all properties jointly and, when they cannot all hold, certifies that they cannot (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: One MIQO (5) with a binary indicator per coefficient, big-M linking and a cardinality bound, plus a constraint family per property: group sparsity (equal indicators), pairwise multicollinearity (z_i + z_j ≤ 1 for pairs correlated above ρ), nonlinear transforms (at most one of x, x², √x, log x), modeler expertise (forced inclusion); an ℓ1 term Γ‖β‖₁ in the objective as robustification, justified by the robust least-squares equivalence that recovers the Lasso (Theorem 1, cited from Bertsimas–Fertis 2009 and Xu et al. 2009); an optional least-median-of-squares objective for outliers (Table 1, p. 3; pp. 5–8). Properties that are not linear constraints (bootstrap significance, condition number) are checked on the three best supports and enforced by cutting those supports off and resolving (Stage 3, p. 8). Kept from current practice: out-of-sample validation (p. 8). Changed: the properties are handled jointly, more variables than a person can handle, a set of models instead of one (p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Linear model y = Xβ + ε (p. 2); error in the data modelled by a norm-bounded uncertainty set on X (p. 6); significance by bootstrap, because distribution-free, high-dimensional and regularized settings void the least-squares assumptions (pp. 6–7); pairwise correlation as a surrogate for multicollinearity, backed by a global condition-number check (above 15 suspect, above 30 severe) (p. 7). Defaults: a 50/25/25 split, ρ = 0.8, ten log-spaced Γ values, maximum condition number 30, three Stage-3 rounds (pp. 7–8); big-M estimated from data (p. 8). Synthetic data: x ~ N(0, Σ), Σ_ij = ρ^|i−j|, k = 10 equally spaced unit coefficients, SNR = var(x′β)/σ² (p. 10). Real data: twelve public sets (UCI, Porto, Florida, the lars package, an HIV study) (p. 10); Croq'Pain (60 stores, from the Bertsimas–Freund teaching case) and Ames housing (2,271 × 315 after dummy expansion) (pp. 8–10); Concrete (1,030 observations, 8 variables) and Energy Efficiency (768 observations) (pp. 11, 13–14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: No new theorem; Theorem 1 is cited (p. 6). Devices: big-M linking −Mz_l ≤ β_l ≤ Mz_l (pp. 7–8); a no-good cut Σ_{l∈S_i} z_l ≤ |S_i| − 1 that removes one corner of the binary hypercube (p. 8); a feasibility precomputation, k_max = maximum stable set of the high-correlation conflict graph, itself a small MIO (p. 7); warm starts by discrete first-order methods (p. 5); a Γ grid from 0 to the value that zeroes β (p. 7). The promised certificate (a suboptimality guarantee even when stopped early, and infeasibility as proof that the properties are incompatible, p. 3) concerns each MIQO for fixed k and Γ; the final choice over k, Γ and the Stage-3 cuts is by validation R² (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Dell Precision T7600, Xeon E5-2687W 3.1 GHz, 16 cores, 128 GB; Gurobi 6.0.0 through JuMP 0.7.0 in Julia 0.3.3; GLMNet 0.0.2 for the Lasso and grplasso 0.4–4 in R for group Lasso (p. 10). Time limit per MIQO 20 s (n > p) or 40 s (n < p), with optimality "often" reached earlier (p. 11); the number of MIQOs per data set is k_max × (number of Γ values) × (Stage-3 rounds) (p. 11); wall times, given in hours and explicitly not a benchmark, run from about 0.3 to 2.1 h per synthetic experiment (p. 11; Tables 3–8, p. 12; Table 13, p. 14). Baselines: the Lasso with Γ tuned on the same validation set (p. 11), group Lasso (p. 14), the manual or published models (pp. 9–10). Metrics: K*, true positives, test R², maximum pairwise correlation, condition number (p. 11). Synthetic: MIQO finds the true support with one or two extra variables where the Lasso adds about 24 (n > p) or 45 (n < p), at similar R², and its models have half or less of the Lasso's maximum correlation and no severe condition numbers (p. 11). Real data: all MIQOs solved to optimality except on Diabetes and HIV (20 s limit) (p. 11); fewer variables than the Lasso at similar R² (Table 9, p. 13), but the Lasso's test R² is the higher on 7 of 12 sets: by 0.192 on Pyrimidines, which the text discusses as the price of interpretability (p. 11; with ρ = 1, MIQO 0.375 against 0.367, Table 10, p. 13), and by 0.047 on Diabetes, which it does not discuss. Croq'Pain: in under a minute the MIQO returns the same five variables as the manual procedure, validation R² 0.80 (p. 9). Ames: 20 significant variables and test R² 0.920, against 92% for the data set author's 36-variable model (p. 10). Concrete: allowing transforms raises test R² from 0.609 to 0.823 with six variables, against the Lasso's 0.834 with twelve, at an unchanged 1–1.5 min (p. 13). Energy: three variables and R² ≈ 0.88 and 0.85, against group Lasso's 13 of 14 variables and ≈ 0.91 and 0.86; the MIQO model uses neither categorical group, so the reported solution does not show the group constraint at work (p. 14). Combined example (n = 100, p = 1,000): MIQO has the higher R² in eight of nine cells of Table 13, but in the last cell (first parameter 2, ΔX ~ U(0, 2)) the Lasso leads, 0.368 against 0.245, with 5.8 true positives against 4; the text only says that Table 13 presents the results (pp. 14–15). Anomaly: in the extracted text, Table 7 (robustness, ΔX ~ U(0, 2)) repeats every entry of Table 3 (sparsity, ΔX = 0) with the SNR rows in reverse order, except the Time column, and in Table 3 test R² falls as SNR rises (0.716 at SNR 6.32, 0.975 at 1.58) (p. 12); the text discusses the two tables as separate experiments (p. 11). No per-MIQO gaps are reported.
- **D6 Positioning**: Against the textbook standard approach (p. 3). Credits the leaps-and-bounds method of Furnival and Wilson as an earlier MIQO-based best-subset method, accurate only for p ≤ 30 (p. 5). Against the Lasso (regularity conditions hard to verify and failing under correlation, p. 5; its predictive power reattributed to robustness, p. 6), group Lasso (focused on recovery, whereas a feasible MIQO solution guarantees the group property; no earlier MIQO group-sparsity work known, p. 5), principal-components and partial-least-squares regression (uninterpretable features) and shrinkage (does not decorrelate) (p. 5); analytic post-selection inference (Javanmard–Montanari, Lockhart et al.) set aside in favour of the bootstrap (p. 7). Speed-up history from Bixby 2012 and Nemhauser 2013 (p. 4). The devices come from the group's own papers: best subset, Bertsimas–Fertis, Bertsimas–Copenhaver, Bertsimas–Mazumder, Bertsimas–Weismantel (pp. 4–6).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract follows the programme template: practice (trial and error) → belief (MIQO intractable) → the figure (450 billion) → the method (p. 2); "astonishing" appears four times on pp. 2–4. A section titled "The Aspirations of the Work" divides labour between human intuition and machine computation (p. 2); Table 1 maps each property to its section and model line (p. 3); two worked examples, both teaching cases, precede the experiments (pp. 8–10). "The only methodology we are aware of" appears twice (pp. 3, 15). Judgements are marked as such ("In our opinion", p. 13). The conclusion is one paragraph, with no limits and no future directions (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A unified MIQO framework and a three-stage algorithm, with real and synthetic evidence that it produces models "in realistic timelines" (p. 15). Limits visible but not stated: MIQOs stopped at the time limit on Diabetes and HIV (p. 11); hours of wall time per synthetic experiment (pp. 11–12); lower R² than the Lasso on more than half the real sets (p. 13); the Table 3/Table 7 duplication (p. 12); the infeasibility certificate never exercised in an experiment. No future directions (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (pp. 2, 4): the abstract leads with practice, belief and figure; the belief is dated to the 1970s and 1980s and called justified at the time; the figure is rebuilt from version-on-version solver tests (CPLEX 1.2 → 11, more than 29,000; Gurobi 1.0 → 6.0, more than 27; 780,000 combined) times Top500 hardware (about 570,000), about 450 billion. This is the 450-billion figure the skill dates to AoS 2016, here in a second paper. (Card S010 in c16, read in its arXiv v1 version of May 2015, records 200 billion with a hardware factor of about 320,000.)
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (p. 4): the factors are for mixed-integer linear problems; the MIQO factors are asserted "similar" without a source, as the skill's checkpoint says. The same 570,000 hardware factor recurs in S011, where the total becomes 800 billion through the solver factor alone [card S011, p. 6].
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (pp. 3, 7–8): a statistics-owned modelling process written as one MIQO that keeps its class.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence for the protocol (pp. 5, 7–11): the trial-and-error loop replaced by one MIQO, warm starts, stated limits, solver, machine and software versions, a duel with the Lasso tuned on the same validation set, synthetic data with known truth plus real data.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant for the certificate (pp. 3, 11): promised, but no gaps are reported, two real sets end at the time limit, and infeasibility is never demonstrated.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (pp. 11, 13): the price of interpretability is named and its knob ρ handed to the analyst with two points of the curve (Pyrimidines, Tables 9–10), but no solution-independent bound is given.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (pp. 8–10, 16): the paper sits in a named programme and a student's thesis line, and its examples are teaching cases (the authors' own Croq'Pain case; the Ames class project).
- Heuristics and tensions (not counted): Heuristic 1 ✅ evidence at full-text level: the Lasso's predictive power is credited to robustness (p. 6). Anti-pattern *regression as an art* ✅ (p. 3). Heuristic 9 ✅ (p. 2). Taste mark 7 (a proof of infeasibility also counts) stated (p. 3) but not exercised.
- new-pattern candidate: **lazy no-good cuts for properties checked outside the model** (pp. 8, 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Turn each rule of thumb of a modelling craft into a constraint on support indicators: equal indicators for groups, z_i + z_j ≤ 1 for conflicting pairs, Σ z ≤ 1 over a variable's transforms, z = 1 for expert-mandated variables (pp. 7–8).
- (D4) For properties that can only be checked after fitting (bootstrap significance, condition number), check the best few incumbents, cut each failing support off with Σ_{l∈S} z_l ≤ |S| − 1, resolve, and cap the rounds (p. 8).
- (D4) Before sweeping a cardinality k, compute the largest feasible k as a maximum stable set on the conflict graph, so that no infeasible MIQO is solved (p. 7).
- (D1) Read the field's standard textbooks to define the current procedure step by step, then test against it on the textbooks' own teaching data sets (pp. 3, 8–10).
- (D5) Report interpretability measures (K*, maximum pairwise correlation, condition number, true positives) beside test R², so a sparser model's price is visible next to its gain (pp. 11–13).

**Quotes**:
- "Fitting regression models has long been viewed as an art" (p. 3)
- "This is the price of insisting on interpretability" (p. 11)

### S143 · Inventory management in the era of big data (Production and Operations Management 25(12), 2006–2009, 2016, DOI 10.1111/poms.2_12637)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–4 read in full, including the three notes and the references (p. 4); the three figures survive only as captions (pp. 3–4), so their plotted values were not read. **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jack Dunn (MIT ORC) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: SW4 {ML IO}; received 17 September 2015, accepted 3 March 2017 (p. 3), the same September in which S081 was received and accepted; it extends the "re-examination of statistics under a modern optimization lens" after best subset, King's regression papers and least quantile regression (p. 6); the acknowledgments thank Andrew Mason for comments on "our earlier MIO formulation" (p. 37).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+IO}: CART, C4.5 and ID3 grow trees greedily, split by split, optimize impurity rather than the misclassification they are judged on, and need pruning because strong splits can hide behind weak ones (p. 4). The entry is a concession by the incumbent's own founders: Breiman et al. called their procedure one-step optimal and an overall optimal tree infeasible with the computers of 1984 (quoted, p. 5), so top-down induction reflected a practical limitation, not a belief in its superiority (p. 5). Earlier one-step heuristics (linear and continuous optimization, dynamic programming, genetic algorithms, an SGD upper bound) never produced certifiably optimal trees in practical times, and the enumeration methods T2, T3 and T3C give non-binary trees of depth at most 3 (p. 5). The tree problem is "best posed" as a MIO (p. 5); the statistics community's belief in MIO intractability was true in the early 1970s (p. 5) and no longer holds (p. 6).
- **D2 Key idea**: Write CART's own objective (misclassification plus α times the number of splits, with minimum leaf size N_min; problem (1), p. 9) as one MIO over the maximal tree of depth D (the OCT model (24), pp. 13–14), so that OCT solves exactly the problem CART approximates (pp. 9, 27). Relaxing the integrality of the split coefficients to a_t ∈ [−1, 1]^p turns the same model into hyperplane trees (OCT-H) with the same number of binaries (pp. 17–19). Warm starts: CART trimmed to depth D, and solutions from lower depths and smaller split budgets (pp. 14–16). Tuning: α is replaced by an integer split budget C = 1, …, C_max, dominated solutions are removed, and the midpoint of the winning solution's α interval is used (pp. 15–17). Changed: global rather than greedy, misclassification rather than impurity, no pruning. Kept: CART's objective, parameters and depth limits (p. 9).

The synthetic study follows Murthy and Salzberg: random ground-truth trees, uniform x, labels from the tree, 10 trees × 10 training–test pairs, test size 2^D·(p − 1)·500, varying depth, training size, label noise, feature noise and dimension (pp. 20–25). Real data: 53 UCI data sets with n from 47 to 5,456 and p up to 484 (Tables 7 and 11, pp. 28–29, 33–34), 50/25/25 splits repeated five times (p. 26). Admitted limit of scale: data sets with tens of thousands of points were tried, and the time needed was prohibitive (p. 25).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: Devices: (i) maximal-tree indexing with left and right ancestor sets (p. 10); (ii) a split switch d_t: with a_t = 0 and b_t = 0 every point goes right, so a node can stop splitting without new variables (p. 11); (iii) strict inequalities replaced through a per-feature ε_j, the smallest nonzero gap between sorted values of feature j, with big-M constants read off the scaling (M1 = 1 + ε_max, M2 = 1) (p. 12); (iv) leaf misclassification linearized with label indicators and M = n (p. 13); (v) the loss normalized by the baseline error L̂, so that α does not depend on data-set size (p. 13); (vi) for OCT-H, |a_jt| linearized, M = 2, and a fixed µ = 0.005 for the strict inequality, a compromise between numerical trouble and lost feasible region; indicators s_jt so that α penalizes the variables used (pp. 17–18). The index sets of the leaf-assignment and split constraints switch between T_B and T_L across (6)–(14), (24) and (28) (pp. 11–14, 17–19), and (24) writes b_t where (13) has b_m (pp. 12–13).
