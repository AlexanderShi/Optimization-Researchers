- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + ML; DA-adjacent through the automate-or-defer decision; no optimization model}:
  - The entry is an application bottleneck:
    - EHR data are mostly unstructured, so registries depend on manual abstraction, which "demands significant institutional resources" (p. 2);
    - broader registry use is held back by human effort, incomplete entry, inconsistency across institutions and cost (p. 3).
  - The exemplar is the STS ACSD (p. 2): abstracted by trained staff to standard specifications, audited annually, and covering 95% of US non-federal cardiac surgery programs.
  - The gap (p. 3):
    - AI extraction has worked for single domains (cancer registries; selected cardiac surgery variables). Comprehensive abstraction for surgical registries "with hundreds of data elements" is unmet.
    - Published systems rely on proprietary GPT-3.5/4 models and narrow note types, which raises concerns about reproducibility, transparency and generalizability.
    - Fewer than 10% of clinical AI models are externally validated, and few are tested against gold-standard registries or across independent systems.
    - Commercial abstraction services pair human reviewers with software that "may lack independent, published, peer-reviewed validation".
  - The problem as stated: populate STS ACSD v4.20.2 automatically from multimodal EHR data at the registry's accuracy standard. Models are developed at MGB and validated externally at HHC (pp. 2–3, 11).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - One independent pipeline per target variable, with identical patient-level splits across variables (p. 12).
  - Each variable gets 30 base models: ten sources (seven note types; labs, medications, diagnosis codes) times three featurizations (pp. 2, 12–13):
    - ClinicalBERT fine-tuned per source, with a classification head on [CLS] (p. 12). It is fine-tuned for three epochs only, to avoid catastrophic forgetting (p. 13).
    - Sentence-BERT summarization (pp. 12–13). Sentences are ranked by similarity to the target, "guided by the STS data abstraction manual", and ClinicalBERT is fine-tuned on the top sentences. This fits long notes into the token limit.
    - TF-IDF features with standard classifiers (p. 13).
  - A meta-learner trained on the validation set stacks the 30 probabilities and weights the sources per variable (p. 13). Fig. 1 labels it "e.g. Random Forest" (p. 4).
  - A dual-threshold rule sorts each prediction into negative, positive or deferred to human review. A variable-level accuracy gate sends whole variables back to manual review (pp. 13–14; Fig. 4, p. 14).
  - Hierarchy rules fill 434 child variables from AI-populated parent fields (pp. 6, 12, 14).
  - Changed vs the closest prior systems (pp. 3, 8–10):
    - small on-premise models instead of proprietary or frontier LLMs;
    - coverage of hundreds of variables;
    - validation against the registry's own labels at a second site.
  - Unchanged: the learners are standard supervised models, and the deferral design is "inspired by" refs. 59–60 (p. 13).
  - Why this design (pp. 8–10):
    - HIPAA and PHI governance prohibit sending raw notes to cloud LLM APIs.
    - On-premise frontier models such as Llama-3 70B need multi-GPU clusters, "often financially and operationally out of reach for most hospital systems".
    - Frozen generative LLMs often match or trail traditional ML unless fine-tuned (refs. 46–48).
    - Each component can be replaced without retraining the whole system.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Problem class:
    - per-variable binary classification of binary or binarizable targets (p. 11);
    - multiclass parent–child variables filled by deterministic rules (pp. 12, 14);
    - 40 deterministic variables filled by institution-specific rules, with unit harmonization (p. 11).
  - Labels: the records each hospital submitted to STS, abstracted by trained nurses under standard specifications and audited annually. They are taken as the gold standard (p. 11).
  - Cohorts (pp. 4, 11):
    - MGB: 8,515 patients, 1 Jan 2015 to 30 Sep 2021 (derivation and internal validation);
    - HHC: 3,116 patients, 1 Jul 2020 to 31 Dec 2023 (external validation);
    - excluded: transfers, missing STS records, and missing or inconsistent timestamps.
  - The sites differ (Tables 1–2, pp. 5–6):
    - cardiology notes average 241 words at MGB and 1,369 at HHC;
    - visit notes exist only at MGB;
    - anesthesia notes are nearly twice as long at MGB.
  - Variable funnel (pp. 5–7; Fig. 2, p. 7):
    - 1,069 STS variables;
    - 311 excluded, being populated in under 0.15% of patients;
    - 758 candidates;
    - 111 not completed, because they failed the quality standards;
    - 647 completed at least partially: 40 deterministic, 173 AI-derived, 434 parent/child.
  - Homogenization (p. 12): parseRPDR cleaning; structured fields serialised as text with TabText and also kept in structured form; ICD codes mapped to clinical categories; labs and medications rendered in natural language.
  - Assumptions admitted for tractability:
    - variables are modelled independently (p. 13);
    - on-premise GPUs "precluded the use of much larger frontier models" (p. 13);
    - transferred patients are excluded (pp. 10–11);
    - labels carry the human abstractors' errors and biases (p. 10).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for formulations, bounds and proofs. Design devices:
  - Threshold calibration, stated in words: t1 and t2 are chosen on the validation set so that sensitivity and specificity both exceed 96%, "with the objective of maximizing automated completion rates" (p. 13).
  - Variable gate: each AI-derived variable must reach at least 96% accuracy on automated predictions in the testing set. Otherwise it "reverted entirely to manual review" (p. 13).
  - Operating point: chosen on the accuracy–completion trade-off to meet the STS 99% standard (p. 14).
  - The width of the defer band doubles as a quality measure: better-separated variables send fewer predictions to review (Fig. 4 caption, p. 14).
  - Hierarchy enforcement: a negative parent sets all children negative; a positive or deferred parent leaves the children with their own predictions (p. 14).
  - Two-stage evaluation (pp. 7, 14):
    - first, AUC, precision, recall and specificity on the AI-derived variables;
    - then pipeline accuracy (agreement with the manually abstracted values) and completion rate;
    - accuracy is the pooled metric because most parent–child variables are multiclass.
  - Blinded adjudication: an expert clinician re-reviews AI–registry disagreements without seeing the AI output or the registry value (pp. 14–15).
  - [My reading] The validation split does three jobs: it trains the meta-learner, selects its model type and sets the thresholds. The test split both gates variables and produces every reported metric (p. 13).
    - Gating on the test set biases the reported accuracy of the retained variables upward, by an unknown amount.
    - The paper does not discuss it.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol: a patient-stratified 70/20/10 split at MGB, with all metrics on the held-out test set; HHC is the external site; computing was on-premise at both sites (pp. 11, 13).
  - Not described in the main text (pp. 11–14):
    - how the MGB models and thresholds reached HHC (retrained, recalibrated or frozen);
    - how the visit-note source, absent at HHC, was handled;
    - which HHC records were scored.
  - Headline (p. 7; Fig. 3, p. 9):
    - overall accuracy above 99% on the extracted outputs at both sites;
    - completion 49.5% at MGB and 43.2% at HHC;
    - intraoperative 61.5% and 52.6%; preoperative 35.0% and 31.4%; CABG 53.5% and 46.3%.
  - The 99% pools deterministic, AI-derived and parent/child variables (p. 7). AI-derived variables are held only to 96% (p. 13); their own accuracy is in Table S5, which is not in the file.
  - Discrimination is reported only on "the subset of records that the pipeline completes post-thresholding" (p. 6):
    - AUC, recall and specificity are "well above 90%" for most variables;
    - precision is lower for some, because of class imbalance;
    - [my reading] removing the uncertain middle band tends to raise these figures.
  - The trade-off: relaxing 99%+ to 94% would raise MGB completion from 49.5% to about 75% (pp. 7, 10).
  - The human-label check covered two variables in the MGB test set (p. 8):
    - diabetes: 3.3% of cases were discordant. In 17.9% of these the AI was right and the registry likely wrong.
    - CABG: 1.2% discordant, with the AI right in 9.1%.
    - Errors in deterministic variables were classed as entry mistakes, temporally wrong values (an intraoperative A1C entered for the preoperative one) and missing data.
  - Not reported (pp. 4–10):
    - confidence intervals;
    - any other extraction system run on the same data (an LLM, a commercial service or an earlier NLP tool);
    - the pipeline's runtime, compute or cost;
    - abstractor hours saved.
  - Failures printed: 111 variables are never automated, and rare conditions complete less often under the strict thresholds (pp. 7, 10).
  - [My reading; figure labels as extracted] Figure 3's case-mix shares (CABG 43% and 54%) differ from Table 1 (45% and 50%). The figure thus seems to use evaluation subsets, but the text does not say which (pp. 5, 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Put a three-zone rule (negative / defer / positive) on any classifier whose errors are costly (p. 13).
  - Choose the two thresholds on validation, to meet floors on sensitivity and specificity while maximizing the automated share.
  - Send whole targets that miss a per-target accuracy floor back to humans.
  - Keep that gate off the test set [my reading of p. 13].
- (D5) Report the pair (accuracy on automated outputs, share automated) at the owner's standard, plus one alternative point on the curve, so that the owner can pick the risk level (pp. 7, 10, 14).
- (D5) When the baseline is also the ground truth, run blinded adjudication of the disagreements and report how often the label, not the model, was wrong (pp. 8, 14–15).
- (D2) Fit long documents into a transformer by ranking sentences against the target's definition in the owner's abstraction manual (S-BERT similarity), then fine-tune on the top sentences (pp. 12–13).
- (D2) Use the data standard's own hierarchy (parent negative, so children negative) to propagate predictions and keep the outputs logically consistent (p. 14).
- (D3) Document site heterogeneity (note types, counts and lengths per site), so that an external validation shows what it actually tests (Table 2, pp. 5–6; p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + ML; DA only nominally, through a prescriptive-tree tool used as a classifier; no optimization model}:
  - The entry is the incumbent tool's cost and coverage (pp. 1–2):
    - the Frailty Index needs manual chart abstraction and a questionnaire administered by a geriatrician;
    - manual assessment has inter-rater reliability problems;
    - automated approaches use one data modality;
    - "current tools predict limited outcomes (typically mortality alone)".
  - The FI predicts mortality well, but not falls or hospitalization consistently, and it is a static snapshot (p. 2).
  - The problem as stated (pp. 1–2):
    - predict six outcomes after inpatient visits: 3- and 6-month all-cause mortality and readmission, 6-month falls, 1-year recurrent falls;
    - use structured EHR, clinical notes and CT;
    - assign each visit to one of four vulnerability tiers by transparent rules.
  - The four tiers deliberately mirror the FI's four levels, which "directly inform prognosis and care planning" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Stage 1, HAIM-style fusion (Fig. 1a, p. 3; pp. 5–6): tabular features, Clinical-Longformer note embeddings, and Merlin abdominal/pelvic-CT and I3D head-CT embeddings are concatenated, screened and fed to XGBoost, one model per outcome.
  - Stage 2 (pp. 2, 8; Fig. 1b, p. 3):
    - Each visit gets a 7-dimensional vector: five predicted probabilities, age and comorbidity count. Recurrent falls are left out, because inpatient instances were sparse.
    - The vectors are z-scored and clustered by k-means with k = 4 on the validation set.
    - An OPT trained on the unnormalized vectors reproduces the cluster labels as threshold rules.
    - The clusters are named Non-, Pre-, Moderately and Severely Vulnerable from their profiles (p. 8).
  - Changed vs HAIM (S023): the targets are geriatric multi-outcome ones, and predictions are turned into classes, "bridging the gap between predictions and clinical decision-making" (p. 3). Unchanged: fusion followed by boosting.
  - The design choices are justified unevenly:
    - trees, because decision paths are "transparent and easy to interpret" (p. 2);
    - k = 4, to mirror the FI's levels (p. 3);
    - a policy tree rather than a classification tree, and with which rewards: not stated (pp. 2, 8).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - One health system: MGB, i.e. MGH, BWH and partner hospitals (pp. 1, 4).
  - Cohort (p. 4):
    - patients with at least one FI evaluation from 2018 (8,519);
    - data from January 2015 to March 2024;
    - inpatient visits from January 2017 to March 2024, so that two years of history exist;
    - age 65 or older; visits ending in death excluded.
    - Result: 6,763 patients, more than 19,000 inpatient visits and more than 1.13 million encounters. Predictions are per encounter.
  - Labels:
    - Mortality comes from a three-step rule (p. 14): recorded death dates; "Expired" discharge dispositions; an age-gap rule (≥0.75 and ≤−0.25 years for 90 days, ±0.5 for 180 days). Undeterminable visits are dropped.
    - Falls come from ICD-9 E880–E887 and ICD-10 W00–W19 and R29.6. Recurrent falls are predicted on any visit with a fall, not only inpatient ones (p. 4).
    - Event rates run from 5.21% (3-month mortality) to 60.04% (recurrent falls) (Table 2, p. 15).
  - Features (pp. 4–5):
    - seven look-back windows per feature: current visit, 30, 60, 90 and 180 days, 1 and 2 years;
    - over 18,000 ICD codes mapped by CCSR to 530 categories, and CPT codes to 248;
    - six high-risk drug classes plus the Beers criteria, and polypharmacy at ≥5, ≥10 and ≥15 drugs;
    - notes embedded in 768 dimensions;
    - abdominal CT through Merlin's image encoder (2048 dimensions), fine-tuned per outcome down to 128;
    - head CT through an I3D ResNet152, down to 128.
  - Missing data: median imputation fitted on training. Tabular data are the only required modality; missing text or imaging is imputed (p. 5).
  - Screening: an elastic net (strength 0.1, L1 ratio 0.1 by 3-fold CV, balanced class weights) keeps coefficients above 0.01. This reduces over 12,000 features to 80–400 (p. 5).
  - Splits: patient-level 80/10/10, with the same patients across outcomes. Imaging models are tuned on an internal 80/20 split of the training set (p. 4).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a for formulations and proofs. Design devices:
  - Out-of-sample second stage: clustering and the OPT are fitted on the validation set, because training-set probabilities would give "overly optimistic risk estimates" (p. 8).
  - Tier validation: one-hot tiers enter an untuned logistic regression, trained on validation and scored on test (p. 8; Table 1, p. 9).
  - Matched comparison with the incumbent (pp. 7–8; Appendix B, p. 19):
    - the FI is scored only on visits where it was recorded, which avoids stale scores;
    - an extra HAIM model is trained and tested on those same visits.
  - Reporting threshold: argmin over t of |TPR(t) − TNR(t)| on validation (Table 8 caption, p. 18).
  - Absent: confidence intervals, significance tests and calibration analysis (pp. 1–24).

- New-pattern candidates:
  - **Re-pose the target at the clinicians' decision strata** (recurrence; S678 in c41). Four tiers mirror the FI's four levels (pp. 3, 8).
  - **Cluster-then-tree phenotyping** (p. 8; Table 1, p. 9).
    - Cluster the vector of predicted multi-outcome risks into a few tiers.
    - Make the tiers assignable by an interpretable tree.
    - Validate them by their downstream prognostic value on held-out data.
  - **Second stage fitted on held-out first-stage outputs**. The clusters and the tree are fitted on validation-set predictions (p. 8); S618 trains its meta-learner on validation-set probabilities (S618 p. 13).

  - **Frozen modular extractors producing one patient vector** (recurrence; S023 in c23, S678 in c41). Per-modality embeddings are concatenated for one boosted learner (pp. 3, 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3) Recover incomplete death outcomes with an explicit rule cascade (p. 14): recorded dates, expired discharges, an age-gap rule with asymmetric margins, and exclusion of the visits that stay undetermined.
- (D3) Build every feature over nested look-back windows (current visit, 30, 60, 90 and 180 days, 1 and 2 years) to separate acute from chronic risk (p. 4).
- (D4) Fit any second stage (clusters, a tree, a meta-model) on the validation split's out-of-sample predictions, never on training-set predictions (p. 8).
- (D5) Judge a discretization by its downstream prognostic value (p. 8; Table 1, p. 9): put one-hot tiers in an untuned logistic model, trained on validation and scored on test, beside the incumbent's AUC.
- (D5) Give the incumbent its strongest footing: score it only where it was actually recorded, and retrain your model on that same cohort (pp. 7–8; Appendix B, p. 19).
- (D5) Print training, validation and test AUC for every modality and every note type, failures included (Tables 2–7, pp. 15–17).
- (D5) Pit a foundation-model image embedding against a simple domain-feature model (body composition) before crediting the foundation model (Table 5, p. 16).
