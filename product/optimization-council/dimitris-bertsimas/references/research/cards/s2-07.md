### S747 · Note on the Reformulation for Static Robust Problems for Convex Uncertainty Sets (unpublished note 2015, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–5 (the opening statement, the three revised proofs, references). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Goyal and Lu match; the note is dated May 17, 2015 (p. 1). The work list's venue string (`Rn 500, 1`) does not appear in the file [my reading].
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Vineet Goyal, Brian Y. Lu (p. 1; no affiliations given) · **Position in trajectory**: an erratum-type note on a published adaptive-RO paper. It cites Lemma 4 (page 291), Theorem 3 (page 298), Lemma 6 (page 304) and Lemma 8 (page 318) (p. 1). These pages fall inside MP 150(2) pp. 281–319, the print range of S176 in the index, and the notation T(U, h) matches the S176 card in s2-06 [my inference].

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+LO}: The original proofs "assume that the uncertainty is a polytope", while the results are stated for convex, compact, down-monotone uncertainty sets (p. 1). The note gives revised proofs for that general case (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace the extreme-point argument by the duality between support functions and gauges:
  1. The row constraints b_jᵀy ≤ 1 for all b_j ∈ U_j hold iff bᵀy ≤ 1 over conv(∪U_j), and conv(∪U_j) = conv T(U, h) by Theorem 3 of the paper (p. 1).
  2. The static value is then the support function of C = (conv T)° ∩ Rⁿ₊. Rewritten as the Minkowski functional of C°, it becomes min{λ : λb ≥ d, b ∈ conv T(U, h)}, because d ≥ 0 (pp. 1–2).
  3. Lemmas 6 and 8 are reduced to Lemma 4 by lifting y to v = (y, µ + 1) (pp. 2–4).
- **D3 Model and data**: The static robust packing LP max{dᵀy : By ≤ h for all B ∈ U, y ≥ 0} with h > 0 (p. 1). Lemma 6 adds objective uncertainty through a product set U^B × U^d (p. 2). Lemma 8 makes (B, h) uncertain together; the case h_j = 0 is trivial (pp. 3–4). No data.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - polar calculus ((S°)° = S, (Rⁿ₊)° = Rⁿ₋) and Proposition 3.2.5 of Hiriart-Urruty–Lemaréchal for the support-function/gauge duality (p. 2);
  - for uncertain right-hand sides, each row is normalized by h_j, a step called "very similar to the perspective function", which carries convexity over (p. 4);
  - the substitution θ_j = (µ_j/h_j)/Σ_i µ_i/h_i gives min{λt : λb ≥ d, (b, t) ∈ conv T(U^{B,h}, e)} (p. 5).
  - [my reading] The identity printed as (S1 ∩ S2)° = S1° ∪ S2° (p. 2) holds for closed convex sets only after the closed convex hull of the union is taken. The final form λb ≥ d is what that hull gives.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: none.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Three proofs repaired, so that the stated results cover general convex, compact, down-monotone sets (p. 1). The note does not say whether any published statement changes. Whether it was issued as a formal erratum cannot be told from the file (not checked).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1–5): the static robust value is read off a dual object, a gauge over conv T. The note extends the reformulation from polytopes to the convex sets the theorems claim.
- new-pattern candidate: *proof-repair note*: when a published proof silently uses a stronger assumption than its theorem states, circulate revised proofs that keep the statements (p. 1). Workflow E's correction, narrowing the claim after an error, works the other way.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To robustify a packing LP over a convex, non-polyhedral uncertainty set, write the robust feasible set as the polar of conv(∪ row sets) intersected with the orthant. Its support function is then a gauge: min{λ : λb ≥ d, b ∈ conv T} (pp. 1–2).
- To add objective or right-hand-side uncertainty to such a result, lift the variables (v = (y, µ + 1)) so that the new case is the old lemma in one more dimension (pp. 2–4).

**Quotes**:
- "The original proof in the paper assume that the uncertainty is a polytope." (p. 1)
- "Note that the last maximization problem can be viewed as the support function of the set" (p. 1)

### S088 · Reformulation versus cutting-planes for robust optimization: A computational study (Computational Management Science 2016, DOI 10.1007/s10287-015-0236-z)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–23 (abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1–12, figure captions, references). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Dunning and Lubin match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Iain Dunning (corresponding) and Miles Lubin, both MIT ORC (p. 1); Lubin was funded by a DOE CSGF fellowship (p. 23) · **Position in trajectory**: computational RO. Received March 2014, accepted July 2015 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+IO+LO}: RO problems are solved either by a duality-based reformulation or by an iterative cutting-plane method, and "There is little guidance in the literature" on which to use (p. 2). The one direct study (Fischetti–Monaci 2012) covered only polyhedral sets, tested no significance and tried no cutting-plane variants (p. 2). The paper reframes the question for parallel computing: run both and stop at the first, as Gurobi does with simplex and barrier for LO (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - A precise cutting-plane method for RMIO: cuts at the root relaxation until it is robust feasible; then lazy constraints at each integer solution; plus a repair heuristic that fixes the integers and solves the remaining RLO by cuts (pp. 5–7).
  - Benchmark both methods and their variants with statistics (pp. 10–11), then evaluate a hybrid that takes the faster method on each instance (pp. 16–20).
- **D3 Model and data**: Linear RO with constraint-wise sets: the Bertsimas–Sim budget set, in which at most Γ coefficients deviate [my reading: the Γ glyph is dropped], and Ben-Tal–Nemirovski ellipsoids (pp. 3, 8–9). Instances: NETLIB and Mittelmann LPs; MIPLIB3 and MIPLIB 2010 "easy" MIPs, those whose nominal version solves within an hour (pp. 9–10). A coefficient is uncertain unless it looks like a simple rational; deviations are 2%; equality rows stay certain. 183 LO and 40 MIO instances remain (p. 10). Γ ∈ {1, 3, 5}, reported for Γ = 5 because run times did not change (p. 11).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - problem class by route (Table 1, p. 8): reformulation keeps LO/MIO for the budget set but turns ellipsoids into SOCP/MISOCP; the cutting-plane master stays LO/MIO for both;
  - separation: partial sorting in O(n + Γ log Γ) for the budget set (p. 8); a closed-form KKT maximizer in O(n) for ellipsoids (p. 9);
  - termination: finite for polyhedral sets (finitely many extreme points), not guaranteed for ellipsoids (p. 9);
  - a one-variable example (max x, x ≤ 1.5, ux ≥ 1 for all u ∈ [0.9, 1.1], x integer) shows that a robust-feasible root does not make integer solutions robust (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi 5.6 with C++ cut logic, one thread, 1,000 s limit (pp. 4, 10); metrics are % fastest, median and geometric mean of the run time divided by the per-instance best, bootstrap 95% CIs, and performance profiles (pp. 10–11). RLO with budget sets: cutting planes are fastest on 75.5% of instances, but the best-of-both reformulation has the lower geometric mean, 1.39 against 1.52, with overlapping CIs (Table 2, p. 12). RLO with ellipsoids: reformulation wins, 1.38 against 1.76 (Table 3, p. 13). RMIO: no significant winner for either set; for budget sets this contradicts Fischetti–Monaci's verdict that cutting planes are "significantly worse" (Tables 4–5, p. 14). Without root cuts the median ratio on infeasible instances is about 23 (budget set) and 20.5 (ellipsoids) (Table 7, p. 16). The repair heuristic does not help on average (p. 16; Table 8, p. 18). The hybrid takes 50–75% of any single method's time (pp. 18, 22). [my reading] Not stated: how instances that hit the limit enter the ratios, and whether code is released.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Four contributions are listed (p. 3). Printed limits:
  - the hybrid is not implemented as a tool (p. 2), and its evaluation assumes that simultaneous single-thread runs do not interfere (p. 20);
  - thread allocation needs instance-specific study (p. 21);
  - MISOCP solvers were still young (p. 16);
  - a proximal bundle method was tried without gain (p. 5).

  Future work: tougher MIO instances, stabilization, warm starts, presolve, block structure (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 8–9, 18–22): the class-keeping device is the cutting-plane master, which stays LO/MIO even for ellipsoids. The choice of route is settled by measured run times, not by class.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 10–20): a duel between two exact methods on public libraries, with solver version, threads, time limit and confidence intervals stated. There is no heuristic incumbent, and the hybrid is computed as a minimum over separate runs, not run as one solver.
- Heuristic 4 · evidence ✅ (not a Method): negative results are printed in the body (the repair heuristic, p. 16; the bundle method, p. 5).
- new-pattern candidate: *race the rival methods instead of ranking them* (pp. 2, 16–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Report benchmark run times as ratios to the per-instance best, summarized by median and geometric mean with bootstrap CIs, beside performance profiles; do not average ratios arithmetically (pp. 10–11).
- When no method dominates, evaluate the portfolio that runs them in parallel and stops at the first finisher, and report its time as a ratio to each single method (pp. 16–19).
- In a cutting-plane MIO with semi-infinite constraints, separate at the root until the relaxation is feasible, then only at integer candidates (lazy constraints); analyze the infeasible instances separately (pp. 6, 15–16).

**Quotes**:
- "There is little guidance in the literature about which method should be used for any given RO problem instance." (p. 2)
- "This is a clear win, and suggests that a hypothetical robust optimization solver should follow this hybrid strategy." (p. 22)

### S201 · Unified optimization of traffic flows through airports (Transportation Science 2016, DOI 10.1287/trsc.2015.0590)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–18 (abstract, §§1–5, Proposition 1, Tables 1–7, figure captions, references). The displayed constraints (2a)–(2n) and (7a)–(7o) are flattened and were read through their prose explanations (pp. 7–8, 11). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Frankovich match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael Frankovich (Aviation Edge, LLC) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the air-transportation line with Odoni and MIT Lincoln Laboratory, which gave the grant and the data (p. 17).
- It moves from the runway-configuration model S120 (card in s08), whose reliance on capacity envelopes it calls "heavy machinery" (p. 3), down to decisions for each flight.
- It reuses the "by" variables of Bertsimas–Stock-Patterson 1998 (S015) and the inequalities of Bertsimas–Lulli–Odoni 2008 (pp. 10–12).
- Received June 2012, accepted November 2014 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+AP}: Delays cost US airlines $8.3 billion in 2007, and infrastructure alone will not keep pace (p. 2). ATFM research optimizes network flows, and airport studies treat one subproblem at a time; solving them in isolation "may lead to overall solutions which are grossly suboptimal, or indeed infeasible" (pp. 2, 4). The problem: choose runway configurations, runway assignments and sequences, gate holding, and taxi and terminal-airspace routes together (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two binary IPs (pp. 5, 9):
  - P1 treats gates, taxiways and airspace as non-binding and optimizes configurations, runway assignments and sequences. Minimum separation depends only on the flight type (weight class × arrival or departure), so the sequencing variables are indexed by type, not by flight: O(CRT + FR) variables instead of O(FRT), and O(C²RT) constraints instead of O(F²RT) (p. 6). Cumulative counting constraints (2i) link type slots to flights (pp. 7–8).
  - P2 fixes P1's runway assignments and orders and routes each flight on a capacitated node network with "by" variables. Separation is enforced only for the pairs P1 makes adjacent (pp. 9–11).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Deterministic inputs over a one-hour horizon in 20-second periods (pp. 4–5, 12). Close parallel runways are modelled as one runway, "(only slightly) conservative" (p. 7). P2 is feasible when takeoff and landing times have no strict deadlines (pp. 5, 9).
  - Data: one historical day at DFW (November 2, 2009) and one at BOS (September 28, 2010), with METAR weather, ASDE-X tracks and ASPM OOOI times (p. 12). Release times are actual pushback times and touchdown times less a constant (pp. 12, 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Proposition 1 gives valid inequalities for conv(X) that the LP relaxation violates; the proof is in the online appendix (p. 9). P2 adds fork, joint and antichain inequalities (p. 12). Its separation constraints are dominance constraints between "by" variables, which Bertsimas–Stock-Patterson showed to be facet-defining for a similar problem (p. 11). The loss from the two-phase split is bounded empirically, not proved: Tables 1–2 report the P1–P2 difference as "a Bound on the Optimality Gap" (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi 5.0 on an Intel i7-860 with 16 GB, 1,200 s per phase (p. 12); eight one-hour instances per airport, 153–175 flights at DFW and 59–91 at BOS. P1–P2 differences are at most 2.1% and P1 gaps are 0.0; P2 runs to about its 1,200 s limit in two instances, one of them with a 1.0% gap (Tables 1–2, p. 13) [my decoding]. Totals run 161–1,465 s: "five to 10 minutes" typically and always under half an hour (pp. 13, 17). Against the historical days, average surface times fall 5–14% at DFW and 7–25% at BOS, while arrival taxi times rise (p. 13). Gate holding gives 10.1 against 10.5 minutes at DFW, against 11.3 historically (p. 17). Objective weights, configuration-change counts and gate holding are varied (Tables 5–7, pp. 15–17). [my decoding] The text says that, apart from the largest difference (2.1%), the others are at most 1.1% (pp. 12–13), but BOS row 6 of Table 2 has 3,877.1 against 3,928.3, a 1.3% difference (p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Claimed as "the first truly unified and tractable" approach, applicable to any airport (p. 4). The authors list the limits (pp. 4, 15, 17):
  - deterministic data, so the optimum has no queues at runways;
  - no fairness between airlines;
  - a central decision-maker, against collaborative ATM;
  - decisions on different time scales.

  Only a subset of the decisions, such as configuration sequences, is implementable under current practice (pp. 4, 17). The comparison covers surface times only, because fix times were unavailable (p. 13). Future work: uncertainty and fairness (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 4, 6, 8, 13, 17): a problem practice splits into pieces is written as binary IPs, with a device (type-indexed variables) that keeps them solvable at real scale. Tractability is shown as solve time on historical days, and the paper names what it leaves out (pp. 4, 15).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 12–13): solver gaps and run times are printed for each phase, and the phase difference serves as a gap for the decomposition. There is no heuristic incumbent.
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 12–14): judged against what happened on the historical days. But the optimizer knows the actual pushback and touchdown times, the metric leaves out air time, and nothing is field-tested.
- Taste mark 3 · evidence ✅ (not a Method): joint optimization replaces subproblems solved separately (pp. 3–4).
- new-pattern candidates: *type-indexed variables when the coupling depends only on type* (pp. 6, 8); *relax-then-restrict decomposition, with the difference of the two phases reported as an optimality bound* (pp. 5, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When pairwise constraints (separation, setups) depend only on a few types, index the time-slot variables by type and link them to individual jobs with cumulative counting constraints. The constraint count falls from O(F²RT) to O(C²RT) (pp. 6–8).
- Decompose by the bottleneck: solve the bottleneck problem with the other capacities relaxed, fix its sequence, solve the full routing, and print the difference of the two optimal values as a bound on the loss (pp. 5, 9, 13).
- In time-indexed routing, use cumulative variables equal to 1 once a flight has reached node j by time t, so that precedence and separation become dominance constraints between them (pp. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "a naïve attempt to solve this overall problem would be far from computationally tractable" (p. 4)
- "Indeed, this modeling technique may be applied for general bounded-TSP type problems." (p. 6)

### S216 · Decomposable Markov decision processes: A fluid optimization approach (Operations Research 2016, DOI 10.1287/opre.2016.1531)
Read level: **partial**:
- Read: pp. 1–9 (abstract, §§1–3, Propositions 1–4, Theorem 1, Algorithm 1, the limitation on action spaces, the opening of §4 and Assumptions 1–3); pp. 11–14 (Theorem 2 and its remarks, Corollaries 1–2, Theorem 3, §4.5, the opening of §4.6, §§5.1–5.3, Propositions 8–9, Corollaries 3–4); pp. 15–20 (Tables 2–5, §§5.4–5.5, §6, acknowledgments, references).
- Not read: the ALO, CLR and ALR formulations of §§4.1–4.3 beyond the assumptions (pp. 9–11); §4.6 and Theorem 4 (pp. 12–13); the electronic companion with all proofs, which is not in the file.

First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Mišić match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Velibor V. Mišić (UCLA Anderson at publication, p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: SD in the achievable-region lineage (SW1): the paper compares its fluid LP with the Bertsimas–Niño-Mora performance-region model (pp. 5, 13–14). Received February 2014, revised three times, accepted May 2016 (p. 2). The acknowledgments credit the referees with the link to Adelman–Mersereau (p. 19).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+LO}: Many MDPs are products of components, so their state space grows exponentially, while their data come per component (pp. 2–3). Approximate LP (ALO, de Farias–Van Roy) still has one constraint per system state, and Lagrangian relaxation (Hawkins; Adelman–Mersereau) needs an action space that decomposes (pp. 4–5). Performance-region models rest on conservation laws and are "fragile" without them (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: An LP whose variables are the marginal frequencies x^m_ka(t) of component m being in state k while system action a is taken at time t (pp. 6–7). Conservation constraints act per component, and consistency constraints tie all components to the same action frequencies A_a(t). A finite version models T periods exactly and aggregates the tail with discounting (pp. 7–8). The policy re-solves the LP from the current state and takes arg max_a A_a(1) (Algorithm 1, p. 8).
- **D3 Model and data**: Independent components, additive rewards, and any action allowed in any state (p. 6). Instances are multiarmed bandits, regular and restless (pp. 15–17):
  - M = 5 bandits with n = 4 states (1,024 system states) for exact comparisons;
  - M and n up to 20 for simulation.

  Rewards are identical across bandits, so a method must read the transition structure. One family has deterministic transitions and is called "potentially pathological" (p. 16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - J* ≤ Z*_T, with Z*_T non-increasing in T (Proposition 4, p. 8). A counterexample shows that the bound need not be tight (p. 7, EC.2).
  - If the LP solution is achievable by some policy, the policy greedy in A(1) is optimal (Theorem 1, p. 7).
  - The alternate Lagrangian relaxation, which relaxes action consistency, equals ALO in bound and value function (Theorem 2, p. 11). For bandits, CLR, ALR and the Bertsimas–Niño-Mora model coincide (Propositions 8–9, p. 14).
  - The chain J* ≤ Z*_T ≤ … ≤ Z*_1 ≤ Z*_ALO = Z*_ALR ≤ Z*_CLR (Theorem 3, Corollary 2, p. 12); proofs are in the e-companion (not read).
  - The size is linear in components and actions, but larger than CLR and ALR (Table 1, §4.5, p. 12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: With exact J* from value iteration on 1,024 states, the fluid bound is closer than ALR on every instance and discount factor, and gains little beyond T = 5–10 (Tables 2–3, pp. 15–17; only the first instance of each set is shown, p. 16). In simulation (500 steps, 100 random initial states, β = 0.99) the fluid policy beats greedy, ALR and the Bertsimas–Niño-Mora primal-dual heuristic. At M = n = 20 the mean gap to the best bound is about 1.2%, against about 11.2% (greedy), 12.0% (ALR) and 36.2% (primal-dual) [my decoding of Table 5, p. 18], at about 2.6 s per action (p. 19). [my reading] No solver or hardware is named.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the formulation, the ordering of bounds and the bandit evidence (pp. 3, 19). Stated limits:
  - exponentially large system action spaces, a regime "we do not consider" (p. 9);
  - an LP solution that may not be achievable by any policy (p. 7);
  - a formulation that is not the smallest (p. 12).

  Future work: a robust or adaptive fluid model for uncertain data, and posterior sampling for learning (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 6–8, 11–14): a DP problem is written as an LP linear in the number of components. Equivalence theorems place the rival relaxations, and the boundary is printed (action spaces, achievability, size).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 17–19): each simulated policy is scored by its gap to the best available upper bound and dueled against greedy, ALR and his own 2000 primal-dual heuristic. There is no warm start, and no solver or machine is named.
- new-pattern candidates: *one chain of bounds for all rival relaxations* (Corollaries 2 and 4, pp. 12, 14); *place your own earlier method in the new hierarchy and beat it*: the performance-region bound equals CLR and ALR, and its heuristic loses (pp. 5, 14, 18–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a product-form MDP, write an LP over per-component state–action frequencies with an action-consistency constraint; take the action with the largest first-period frequency and re-solve every period (pp. 6–8).
- Keep T periods disaggregated and aggregate the tail by a discounted conservation constraint. The bound then improves monotonically in T, a dial between size and quality (pp. 7–8).
- Score heuristic policies by the gap between their simulated reward and the tightest available upper bound, so every policy gets a certificate-like number (p. 18).

**Quotes**:
- "This alternate Lagrangian relaxation is significant because it extends the scope of the Lagrangian relaxation approach to problems that do not have a decomposable system action space." (p. 4)
- "For such problems, the fluid formulation (3) will be harder to solve; we do not consider this regime in the present paper." (p. 9)

### S327 · Scalable robust and adaptive inventory routing (Optimization Online 2016, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–29 (abstract, §§1–6, Theorem 1 with Algorithm 1 and its proof, the route-generation model, Tables 1–3, Appendix A); references (pp. 29–35) skimmed. Figures 2–5 carry axis text only. The file is a manuscript "Submitted to Transportation Science" (p. 1). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Gupta and Tay match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Swati Gupta (Georgia Tech ISyE), Joel Tay (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: robust and affinely adaptive MIO for a routing application. The uncertainty set comes from the robust-probability programme (Bandi–Bertsimas 2012, S065, card in s09) (pp. 10, 30). A local heating-oil company, Lorden Oil Co, set the objective and supplied the data behind the parameters (pp. 2, 28).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+IO+AP}: Finite-horizon inventory routing under uncertain demand, for example residential heating oil (p. 1). Exact approaches solve "only up to around a hundred customers", and heuristics shorten the horizon (p. 3); the robust inventory-routing papers cited report 30 customers over 7 periods (Solyali et al.) and 50 customers (Aghezzaf) (p. 7). A typical New England company has about 10,000 customers (p. 3). The company's main objective was a smaller fleet with schedules robust to demand (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - A route is a neighborhood with a fixed visiting cost, because most travel cost lies between the depot and the neighborhoods (pp. 8, 18). The model can then start from the company's known routes (p. 8).
  - Demand depends on temperature through a convex piecewise-linear curve. CLT-style sets bound temperature and customer noise, and the resulting demand set is non-convex (pp. 10–11).
  - The algorithm Opt-Temp computes the worst case exactly, so the robust model becomes a deterministic MIO with 2NT extra constraints (pp. 12–16).
  - The adaptive model makes delivered quantities affine in past observed demand and is solved by cutting planes, with Opt-Temp as the separation oracle (pp. 16–18).
- **D3 Model and data**: N customers, M vehicles of capacity S, tours θ with costs c_θ (p. 9). Γ is set so that each set holds the realized temperatures with probability 99% for large T (p. 11). Projecting the set onto the first t periods "weakens the theoretical probabilistic guarantees", but lowers cost (p. 16). Data (pp. 20–22, 28–29):
  - TSPLIB locations from eil51 to rl5915 (51–5,915 customers) and a single depot;
  - generated family sizes, initial levels and demand;
  - Boston temperatures for November 2013–March 2014;
  - 50 training and 50 test scenarios;
  - T = 151 days, with costs counted for the first 141.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: Theorem 1 states that Opt-Temp maximizes an affine combination of convex non-increasing functions of temperature over the budget set (p. 13). The proof is an exchange argument: some optimal solution has at most one temperature strictly inside its ±3σ bounds, and a sorted greedy then finds it (pp. 14–15). Routes are improved by a set-cover model over schedules within ±3 days of the nominal ones, solved by column generation on its LP relaxation with tabu-style moves (pp. 19–20). The implementation cuts the affine rule to three lag terms (pp. 21–22).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Gurobi 6.0.0 via JuMP on a 16-core Xeon with 128 GB, two hours per model, warm-started nominal → robust → adaptive (pp. 21–22). Stockouts fall from 160–225% of customers (nominal) to under 9% (robust); the reduction is at least 94% in every scenario and over 96% on average, and the adaptive model removes another 0.5–1% of customers (p. 22). Robust-to-nominal cost ratios are 0.425–0.860, but the Gurobi gaps are 1.61–77.3% (nominal), 16.4–74.3% (robust) and 18.9–75.5% (adaptive) (Table 1, p. 25). Twenty-four-hour runs of the two smallest nominal models brought their gaps below 5% without changing the solutions; the larger cases did not improve (pp. 24–25). At N = 575 the robust and adaptive models cut the fleet from 11 to 8 vehicles with no more stockouts, which the nominal model cannot do (Table 2, pp. 26–27). [my reading] The saving above 100 customers is "44% or more" on p. 24 and "at least 56%" on p. 27; Table 1 (CR/CN = 0.560 at N = 200) supports 44% (p. 25).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: robustness, adaptability, scalability to about 6,000 customers over 151 days within two hours, and solution quality (pp. 3–4, 27–28). Admitted limits:
  - the neighborhood-cost simplification does not suit routing whose cost lies in individual links (p. 8);
  - route generation is heuristic and "not the main focus" (p. 18);
  - the projected sets weaken the probabilistic guarantees (p. 16);
  - the lower bounds remain weak (pp. 25, 28).

  Future work: better provable lower bounds (along Bertsimas–de Ruiter), emergency-refuelling routing, dynamic quantities (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 12–18): a non-convex uncertainty set is handled by an exact worst-case oracle. The robust model stays a deterministic MIO and the adaptive model a cutting-plane MIO; scale is stated in customers, days and hours (p. 4).
- Method 4 · evidence ✅, with a caveat (pp. 21–25): warm starts chain nominal → robust → adaptive; solver, limit and gaps are printed even when large; the 24-hour check is reported together with its failure on larger cases. The cost claims, though, rest on solutions with gaps of 16–77%.
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11, 21, 26–27): the robustness knob Γ is set by a coverage target and tuned on training scenarios, not published as a curve. The knob the company cares about, fleet size, gets a price table with its bad case (five vehicles or fewer).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 2, 20–22, 28): the owner set the objective, but the baseline is the nominal model on synthetic data, not the company's current schedules.
- new-pattern candidates: *coarsen the decision until the uncertainty layer scales* (routes as neighborhoods, pp. 8, 18); *exact worst case over a non-convex set by an exchange argument* (pp. 13–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To robustify against a common driver (temperature) plus individual noise, put CLT-style budget sets on both and compute the worst case of each constraint by a sorted greedy with one interior coordinate (pp. 10–15).
- Warm-start each richer model with the previous model's solution so that it is feasible from the start: the robust quantities become the adaptive base amounts, with zero affine terms (p. 22).
- When gaps stay large, rerun the smallest instances for 24 hours and report whether the solutions changed (pp. 24–25).

**Quotes**:
- "Our robust and adaptive formulations are tractable for ∼6000 customers." (p. 1)
- "This weakens the theoretical probabilistic guarantees that we can make, because the Central Limit Theorem might not apply in small cases." (p. 16)

**Quotes**:

### S164 · Robust product line design (Operations Research 2017, DOI 10.1287/opre.2016.1546)
Read level: **partial**:
- Read: the prose of pp. 1–19 (abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1, 2 and 5 as quoted in the text, captions of Figures 1–11).
- Not decoded: the flattened displayed formulas and the bodies of Tables 3 and 4 beyond the values quoted in the text. The references (p. 20) were skimmed.

First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Mišić match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Velibor V. Mišić (UCLA Anderson) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: RO carried into marketing science (area of review: Marketing Science, p. 2). Received September 2014, accepted June 2016 (p. 2). The idea of structural uncertainty comes from a prostate-screening paper with Silberholz and Trikalinos (pp. 3, 6), S249 in the index (not carded). A later paper with Mišić solves the nominal first-choice problem exactly (S093, card in c2-04).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+IO+AP; ML for estimation, HS as solver}: Product line design (PLD) assumes a known choice model, but the model is estimated from small conjoint samples (p. 2). Two uncertainties follow: the parameter values and the model structure itself (p. 3). Product line decisions are hard to reverse, which argues for protection (p. 3). The closest prior work is a post hoc robustness test (Belloni et al. 2008, revenues 2–5% lower) and robust MNL assortment (Rusmevichientong–Topaloglu), which is tied to the MNL model (pp. 4–6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Maximize the worst-case expected revenue over an uncertainty set of choice models, plugging the worst-case objective into the standard divide-and-conquer heuristic (pp. 8–9). Three kinds of uncertainty set (pp. 9–11):
  - finitely many versions of one model: bootstrap re-estimates, posterior samples or EM local optima;
  - continuous sets; for first-choice models with polyhedral segment weights (box or aggregate deviation) the worst case is an LP;
  - a few structurally distinct models that fit about equally well.

  A weighted objective α·worst case + (1 − α)·nominal traces an approximate Pareto frontier (p. 11).
- **D3 Model and data**: First-choice, latent-class MNL and hierarchical-Bayes mixture MNL models (pp. 6–7). Data: the Toubia et al. (2003) conjoint study of laptop bags for Timbuk2 (p. 12):
  - 330 respondents and 10 attributes, giving 3,584 candidate products;
  - lines of 5 products, with three competitor bags as the no-purchase option;
  - the revenue structure of Belloni et al.

  Uncertainty sets come from B = 100 bootstraps (p. 13), from MCMC with 100,000 iterations, thinned (p. 15), and from varying K (p. 16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The robust PLD is "still, in general, a difficult problem to solve to provable optimality" and is solved heuristically (p. 8). The ordering (robust beats nominal in the worst case, nominal beats robust in the nominal case) is derived from optimality (p. 9). [my reading] The experiments use heuristic solutions from 10 random starts (p. 12), so the ordering is not guaranteed there. No bounds are given.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Julia, a custom EM implementation and R's bayesm; divide-and-conquer from 10 random starts, with no solver gaps or run times (p. 12). Metrics: the worst-case loss (WCL) of the nominal line and the relative improvement (RI) of the robust line (p. 12). Parameter uncertainty: WCL up to 23.02% and RI from 0.36% to 13.82% across K (Table 2, pp. 13–14); box sets on first-choice weights give WCL 2.38% at ε = 0.2 and 10.11% at ε = 2 (p. 13). Structural uncertainty (one first-choice and two LCMNL models): WCL 23–37% and RI 13.7–54.6%, while the robust line earns $63.38–$63.69 under all three (Table 5, p. 18). A frontier for K = 8 shows lines close to the nominal revenue with much better worst cases (Fig. 4, p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A robust PLD framework for several choice models and two kinds of uncertainty, with empirical evidence (pp. 3, 19). Limits [my reading]: one data set, heuristic solutions only, and continuous MNL sets left out as hard (p. 11). Future work: uncertainty in costs and competitive responses in the no-purchase option (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 11–15, 18): robustness is priced with two metrics, a set-size knob ε and an α-weighted frontier, and the weak case is printed (RI 0.36% at K = 4, p. 13). There is no solution-independent bound, and the curve is computed by a heuristic.
- Method 4 · contradiction ✗ (pp. 8, 12): an IO problem is solved only heuristically, with no gap or certificate. The exact MIO came later, for the nominal first-choice case (S093, c2-04).
- Method 1 · variant ⚠ (pp. 8, 10): the robust version keeps the nominal problem's heuristic, and the worst case is an LP for first-choice models with polyhedral weights. What is kept is a heuristic's class, not a solver's.
- new-pattern candidate: *structural uncertainty*: an uncertainty set made of structurally distinct fitted models, imported from a health paper (pp. 3, 6, 11, 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the uncertainty set from the estimation itself: bootstrap re-fits, posterior draws or EM local optima. The worst case over a finite set is the minimum of B evaluations (pp. 9–10).
- When model-selection criteria disagree (AIC against CAIC), optimize against the worst of the "good enough" models instead of picking one (pp. 16–18).
- Report the WCL of the nominal solution and the RI of the robust one, and trace the nominal-against-worst-case frontier with a weighted objective, so the decision-maker can pick a point (pp. 11–12, 15).

**Quotes**:
- "Almost all approaches to PLD tacitly assume that this choice model is known precisely and is beyond suspicion." (p. 2)
- "In summary: the robust solution will perform better than the nominal solution in the worst case, while the nominal solution will perform better than the robust solution in the nominal case." (p. 9)
