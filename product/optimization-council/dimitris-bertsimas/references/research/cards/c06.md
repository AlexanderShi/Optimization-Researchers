### S043 · The traffic flow management rerouting problem in air traffic control: A dynamic network flow approach (Transportation Science 2000, DOI 10.1287/trsc.34.3.239.12300)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17 read, including Tables I–V and the references; figures read through their captions and the text.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Sarah Stock Patterson (Fuqua School of Business, Duke, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: continues his own air-traffic line: Vranas, Bertsimas and Odoni 1994a, b on multi-airport ground holding (1994a = S036, carded in c02) and Bertsimas and Stock Patterson 1998 on flow management with en-route capacities; this paper adds rerouting (p. 3; references pp. 16–17). Funded by Draper Laboratory and NASA (p. 16). Received August 1998, accepted August 1999 (p. 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP+IO+LO}:
  - The entry is an application gap. When weather cuts sector or airport capacity, the Air Traffic Command Center and each airline's operations center reroute and reschedule through an iterative collaborative process, and no formal optimization model is used at any stage of it (pp. 1–2).
  - Urgency comes from European figures: EUROCONTROL projects traffic growth by a factor of 1.4 by 2002; a 5% traffic increase gave a 26% increase in delays; congestion sits mostly in the airspace, not at airports (p. 2).
  - The gap is claimed as a first: dynamic rerouting has not been addressed in the literature (p. 3). Earlier work, including his own, controlled release times (ground holding) and airborne speeds on capacitated airspace, but not routes (pp. 2–3).
  - Problem: choose reroutes, ground delays and speed changes to minimize delay cost (fuel, safety costs, taxes) under time-varying sector capacities (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open with the owner's current process, state that it uses no formal model, and give one number that makes the cost of doing nothing concrete (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Key idea**:
  - Model the airspace as a time-expanded network whose nodes are sector crossing points and airports, with self-loops of travel time one for delay; commodities are origin–destination pairs, later also per airline (pp. 3–4, 6).
  - Aggregate flights into commodity flows to beat dimensionality, and add flight-level binaries only for continued flights (an aircraft that must fly a later leg), which costs one extra constraint per continued flight (pp. 4–5).
  - The *Lagrangian Generation Algorithm* (pp. 7–9): (1) relax the sector-capacity constraints (7) with multipliers and drop integrality, so each subproblem is an uncapacitated multicommodity dynamic network-flow LP, solved by CPLEX's network solver; (2) randomized rounding: random walks along positive-flow arcs decompose the fractional flow into flight paths, which join a path pool; (3) a set-packing IP picks one path per flight subject to capacity and aircraft continuity and updates the upper bound; (4) stop when the upper bound is within ε of the LP lower bound, else update the multipliers.
  - What is new relative to standard Lagrangian methods, by the authors' account: the combination with a randomized rounding heuristic (p. 3). The Lagrangian iterations do not tighten the bound, since the Lagrangian optimum equals the LP relaxation (p. 7); they exist to produce many different fractional solutions, hence many candidate routes (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Class: a multicommodity, integer, minimum-cost dynamic network flow whose capacity constraints are bundled over commodities, arcs and time periods, with side constraints (8) and non-flow binaries (pp. 5–6).
  - Airport gadget: four nodes separate landings, aircraft retiring for the day, turnaround for continued flights (r(i) minutes) and departures with ground holding (p. 4, Fig. 3).
  - Data model: sector capacities C_i(t) are known, deterministic inputs (p. 4); the weather forecast's uncertainty is not modelled.
  - Tractability choices the authors admit: aggregation over flights (p. 4); continued flights tied to the commodity of a predecessor rather than to a specific flight, because the alternatives need many side constraints (p. 5); no explicit cancellations, which are left to the airline after a run (p. 5).
  - Several airlines: commodities indexed by airline; fairness proposed as packing constraints capping each airline's share of total delay, not implemented (pp. 6–7).
  - Instances: four airports (Denver, Phoenix, Las Vegas, Salt Lake City) and 42 sectors, with FAA travel and turnaround times; 71 FAA-scheduled flights over 8 hours in 5-minute periods; capacities simulated (uniform random in Scenario I, deterministic moving fronts in II and III); Scenario III has 200 flights with capacities scaled up, because the earlier capacity levels would have made it infeasible (pp. 9–10, 12, 14).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Formulation (1)–(10) with a five-term objective; the rerouting cost appears as actual minus scheduled flying cost (p. 5). Every flow-conservation constraint is explained in words, node by node (p. 6).
  - Multiplier update after Everett (1963): multiply up by (1 + α) when the constraint is violated and down by (1 − α) otherwise; α grows while a constraint stays violated and shrinks when it oscillates; only the sign of the violation is used, never its size (p. 7).
  - Randomized rounding draws among positive-flow arcs with equal probability, to favour alternative paths; flow-proportional probabilities are proposed but not used (p. 8). Paths satisfy flow conservation only; capacity and continuity are left to the packing IP, which gives the pool more flexibility (p. 8).
  - Packing IP (11)–(13) over path/time pairs; constraint (13) keeps a continued flight from departing before an aircraft of the right commodity has arrived and turned around (p. 9).
  - No theorem of any kind (convergence, approximation or complexity); quality is certified empirically by the gap to the LP relaxation (p. 7, pp. 10–15).
  - Transferable: let generated columns satisfy only the easy constraints and leave the coupling constraints to the packing master (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Platform: Sun Sparc 20 with 48 MB; CPLEX for the network LPs, the LP relaxation and presolve (p. 7, p. 10). Parameters α1 = 2, α2 = 0.33, starting multipliers 10 and steps 0.8, picked after a few trials, not tuned extensively (p. 10, p. 12).
  - Scenario I: LP relaxation with 24,509 constraints and 61,912 variables, 181 s, value 2498.5; the algorithm takes 330 s and returns 2509, within 0.4% of the bound; the final packing IP has 2209 constraints and 145 variables, 424 and 125 after presolve (p. 10; Table I, p. 11).
  - Scenario II: LP 59 s, value 2387; solution 2418, within 1.2%, in 116 s; a 100-iteration run reaches 2389 (0.08%) by iteration 7 and nothing better later (p. 12; Tables II–III, p. 14).
  - Scenario III, 200 flights: LP with 25,881 constraints and 66,489 variables, 86 s, value 6513.5; solution 6574 (0.92%) in 169 s; continued runs reach 6520 (0.09%) (pp. 14–15; Tables IV–V, p. 16).
  - Metrics: gap to the LP bound, run time, and total delay split into ground, airborne, rerouting and late-aircraft minutes (pp. 10, 12, 14–15); route maps and flight time lines illustrate single decisions (pp. 11–14).
  - No baseline is run: neither the current command-center procedure, nor his 1998 model without rerouting, nor a simple rounding heuristic. The value of rerouting itself is therefore not measured. The one reported limit is Scenario III's capacity increase to avoid infeasibility (p. 14).
  - Transferable: print a per-iteration table (Lagrangian time and value, number of violated constraints, presolve and IP time, IP value) and split the objective into its operational parts (pp. 10–16).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Continues the ground-holding literature (Terrab–Odoni, Richetta–Odoni, Vranas–Bertsimas–Odoni, Andreatta and co-authors) and the en-route models (his 1998 paper, Helme, Lindsay and co-authors) (pp. 2–3).
  - Sets aside the dynamic network-flow literature (Ford–Fulkerson; surveys by Aronson, Bookbinder–Sethi, Powell–Jaillet–Odoni) as not directly relevant, because his problem is multicommodity, integer and side-constrained (p. 3).
  - Frames the model as a tool for the FAA's collaborative decision making (pp. 1–2) and claims priority twice (p. 3; p. 16).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract compresses the whole pipeline into one paragraph with a single headline: solutions within 1% of the lower bounds (p. 1). The body is slightly weaker: Scenario II stops at 1.2% (p. 12), and the sub-0.1% figures come from runs past the stopping rule (pp. 12, 15). The abstract also speaks of real problems (p. 1), while the capacities are simulated (p. 10). Small instances of Tension T6, headline ahead of caveat.
  - Worked flight time lines make the output concrete for practitioners (pp. 11–12, Fig. 8).
  - The conclusion generalizes with a hedge: the design may be useful in other problems, and dynamic flows in manufacturing, telecommunications and ground transport are envisioned (p. 16).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a model (rerouting, ground holding and speed control in one dynamic network flow), an algorithm, and computations on FAA-based instances with gaps near or under 1% (pp. 1, 16).
  - Stated limits: no cancellations (p. 5); fairness among airlines only sketched (pp. 6–7); parameters untuned (p. 10).
  - Visible limits: deterministic capacities although weather is the motivating uncertainty (p. 4); simulated capacities; one region with four airports; no comparison with current practice (pp. 9–15).
  - Future: other applications of the model and of the algorithmic design (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: formulation first. Rerouting, handled today by an iterative human process with no formal model (pp. 1–2), is written as a multicommodity integer dynamic network flow (pp. 3–6), and the LP class is kept as the engine (network-flow subproblems, p. 7). But tractability comes from aggregation and a heuristic decomposition, not from an exact class-keeping reformulation, and no boundary (complexity or size limit) is printed.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: every reported solution carries an honest gap to the LP relaxation, and the stopping rule is the gap (pp. 7, 10, 12, 14–15). The exact IP enters only as the final packing step over heuristically generated columns; there is no incumbent heuristic to duel and no time limit beyond the ε-stop.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the decision is the object (routes and delays), the owner's network data are used and run times are reported (pp. 10–15), but the yardstick is the LP bound, not the owner's current rule: no comparison with the command center's process (pp. 1–2) or with ground holding alone, and no field test.
- New-pattern candidate: *relax–randomize–pack*: produce many fractional relaxation solutions, decompose them by randomized rounding into a column pool, choose a feasible combination with an exact packing IP, and certify it with the relaxation bound (pp. 7–9, 16). S051 shares the core (a solution extracted from a relaxation and certified by it).
- New-pattern candidate: *aggregate, then disaggregate only where coupling needs it* (commodity flows plus flight binaries for continued flights; pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use the LP relaxation for the lower bound and a Lagrangian multiplier sweep only to diversify fractional solutions; decompose each into candidate columns by random walks on positive-flow arcs; select with a set-packing IP; stop at an ε gap to the LP bound (pp. 7–9).
- Let generated columns satisfy only the easy constraints (flow conservation) and leave the coupling constraints (capacity, aircraft continuity) to the packing master (p. 8).
- Aggregate identical units into commodity flows and add unit-level binaries only where coupling demands them, here one constraint per continued flight (pp. 4–5).
- Model a transfer hub as a four-node gadget (arrivals, exits, turnaround, departures) with holding self-loops (p. 4, Fig. 3).
- Update multipliers multiplicatively from the sign of each violation alone, enlarging the step on persistent violation and shrinking it on oscillation (p. 7).
- Run past your stopping rule once and report what the ε-stop left on the table (p. 12, p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "The problem of dynamically rerouting aircraft has not been addressed to the best of our knowledge in the literature." (p. 3)
- "The idea of extracting solutions using randomization and combining these solutions using integer programming may be useful in other problems as well." (p. 16)

### S051 · Restless bandits, linear programming relaxations, and a primal-dual index heuristic (Operations Research 2000, DOI 10.1287/opre.48.1.80.12444)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–11 read, including Table 1 (the heuristic), Table 2 (results) and the references.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: José Niño-Mora (Universitat Pompeu Fabra; the work is part of his MIT Operations Research Center PhD dissertation, p. 11) · **Position in trajectory**: the performance-region (achievable-region) programme of SW1: after Bertsimas–Paschalidis–Tsitsiklis 1994 and Bertsimas–Niño-Mora 1996 on conservation laws and indexable systems (p. 2), the approach is taken to a PSPACE-hard problem. Presented in 1994 (TIMS/ORSA Boston) and 1995 (INFORMS Applied Probability), received March 1997, accepted April 1998, printed 2000 (p. 1, p. 11); funded by his PYI award with Draper matching funds (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+LO, with IO devices}:
  - The entry is a contrast between two fields. Combinatorial optimization learned that solving well depends on strong formulations; stochastic optimization lacks a unified practical method, meets dynamic programs of prohibitive size, falls back on ad hoc techniques, and judges heuristics against other heuristics, which says little about their suboptimality (p. 1).
  - The paper presents itself as one step of a larger programme, the performance region approach (Coffman–Mitrani 1980 onwards), whose two problems are generating constraints on the region and designing near-optimal policies from relaxations (pp. 1–2).
  - Target: Whittle's restless bandit. N projects, exactly M active each period, discounted rewards, each project Markov under an active and a passive transition matrix. It is PSPACE-hard (Papadimitriou–Tsitsiklis), whereas the classical bandit is solved by Gittins indices (pp. 2–3).
  - Applications listed: clinical trials, aircraft surveillance, worker scheduling, police control of drug markets, make-to-stock production (p. 3).
  - Transferable: open with what the neighbouring field learned that yours has not, and name the evaluation practice it implies is missing (bounds, not heuristic-versus-heuristic) (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea**:
  - Write the restless bandit exactly as an LP over the polytope of discounted state–action frequencies (exponential size), then approximate that polytope by projections of polynomial-size lifted polytopes, after the projection idea quoted from Lovász–Schrijver (p. 2, p. 5).
  - First-order relaxation = Whittle's relaxation (M active on average) rewritten as an LP with O(N|S_max|) variables and constraints (pp. 5–6). Second-order relaxation, new: variables for project pairs, pair-MDC flow constraints, counting identities and consistency with the first-order variables, of size O(N²|S_max|²) (pp. 6–7). k-th order relaxations on k-tuples give P1 ⊇ P2 ⊇ … ⊇ PN = P; the N-th is exact (p. 7).
  - Primal-dual heuristic from the first-order LP: activate projects whose active primal variable is positive; if fewer than M, add those with the smallest active reduced costs; if more, keep the M with the largest passive reduced costs (p. 8; Table 1, p. 9). Under a connectivity (mixing) assumption it is a priority-index rule whose index is the active minus the passive reduced cost (pp. 8–9).
  - Kept from Whittle: his relaxation. Changed: the index comes from LP duals and is defined for every instance, whereas Whittle's index applies only to indexable problems, a property that may be hard to check (p. 3); it depends on all other projects and does not reduce to the Gittins index on classical bandits (p. 9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Discrete time, finite state spaces, discount factor in (0, 1), known initial states, exactly M of N projects active at every epoch, Markovian policies (pp. 2–4).
  - Assumption 1 (each project's active/passive transition graph connected) is used only for the index interpretation (p. 8).
  - Instances: seven synthetic problems. Two random with (N, |S_n|, M) = (10, 7, 1); one random (5, 3, 1); one (5, 3, 1) built so that greedy performs poorly, reused with M = 2, 3, 4 (p. 10). Discount factors 0.2, 0.5, 0.9 and 0.95 (Table 2, p. 10).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1: the performance region of a discounted MDP equals the flow-conservation polytope P, and P's vertices are achieved by stationary deterministic policies; this removes d'Epenoux's (1960) positivity assumption on the initial distribution. Proof: a vertex is the unique maximizer of some linear objective; restricting the LP to the vertex's support shows exactly one positive action per state, which defines a deterministic policy; other points are reached by randomization (pp. 4–5).
  - Propositions 1–2: complete formulations of the first- and second-order projected polytopes, by Theorem 1 applied to single-project and project-pair MDCs (pp. 5–6).
  - Valid identities (8)–(10) by counting passive–passive, active–passive and active–active pairs at every epoch; (11)–(12) tie pair variables to single-project variables (p. 6).
  - The dual (D1) and its reduced costs, read as rates of objective decrease per unit increase of each state–action frequency (Murty), motivate the heuristic (pp. 7–8). Proposition 3: under Assumption 1, every vertex of P1 has at most one project-state with both actions positive, which yields the index form (p. 8).
  - Boundary: polynomial for fixed order k, exponential as k grows; the N-th relaxation is the MDP's standard LP (p. 7).
  - Transferable: to prove that a polytope is a performance region, show that each vertex is achieved by a stationary deterministic policy (pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Heuristics and simulation in C; LPs in GAMS with CPLEX; a SUN 10 workstation (p. 10).
  - Reported per instance: simulated values of the greedy and primal-dual heuristics, Z* (only for the small instances), Z2 and Z1 (p. 10). Simulation truncates the horizon where the discount weight falls below 1e-10 and stops when successive running averages differ by less than 1e-5; no confidence intervals (p. 9).
  - Results: the primal-dual heuristic is essentially optimal on Problems 3–7, slightly better than greedy on Problems 1–2, and never worse; greedy deteriorates as the discount factor approaches 1 (Problem 4 at 0.9: greedy 65.1, primal-dual 75.1, Z* 75.15) (pp. 10–11).
  - The second-order bound improves markedly over the first-order one and is often close to Z*. On Problem 1 a wider gap remains, attributed to the bound by an argument (greedy is expected to be near optimal on random instances), not by computation (p. 10). Second-order LPs took at most 10 minutes (p. 11).
  - Not compared: Whittle's index heuristic, the incumbent named in the paper's own review (p. 3). The authors stopped at greedy, judging the heuristic adequate for realistic sizes (p. 10); the largest instance has 10 projects with 7 states.
  - Reported negatives: Z* is not monotone in M because exactly M must be active (p. 11); the second-order relaxation is not exact on classical multiarmed bandits (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Continues Coffman–Mitrani, Federgruen–Groenevelt, Shanthikumar–Yao and his own conservation-law work (pp. 1–2). Reformulates Whittle's relaxation rather than attacking it, and faults Whittle's index only for needing indexability, which may be hard to check (p. 3).
  - Imports polyhedral-combinatorics devices (lift-and-project after Lovász–Schrijver; primal-dual heuristics) into stochastic optimization (p. 2, p. 11).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The first paragraph states the field contrast; the second names the programme (p. 1). Contributions form a two-item list (p. 2).
  - Claims rise from the abstract's hedged wording (experiments suggest near-optimality, p. 1) to exceptionally accurate (p. 2) and performed exceptionally well (p. 10).
  - Slips: the PSPACE-hardness result is dated 1999 on p. 2 and 1994 on p. 3; in the extracted Table 1 both dual-stage branches carry the same comparison sign, whereas the prose distinguishes p < M from p > M (p. 8, p. 9).
  - The conclusion generalizes the lesson from discrete to stochastic optimization (p. 11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: theory (Theorem 1, the relaxation hierarchy, the new second-order relaxation), an algorithm (the primal-dual index heuristic) and computations; together, a feasible policy with a suboptimality bound that tightens at extra computational cost (p. 11).
  - Limits: exact optima only for tiny instances; the second-order bound can be loose (Problem 1); no comparison with Whittle's index; simulation without error bars (pp. 9–11).
  - Future: the same ideas for other stochastic optimization problems (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a PSPACE-hard problem owned by the dynamic-programming tradition is written exactly as an LP over the performance region (Theorem 1), approximated inside the LP class by polynomial-size relaxations, its policy is read off the dual (indices from reduced costs), and the boundary is printed (polynomial for fixed order, exact only at order N) (pp. 4–9). Unlike SW1, where policies from relaxations are recorded as ad hoc, the relaxation here yields a principled policy.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the paper pairs its policy with a certificate (the relaxation bound) and faults heuristic-versus-heuristic evaluation (p. 1, p. 11), but its duel is with greedy, not with Whittle's index (pp. 3, 10).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: explicitly one step of a larger programme (p. 1), carried by a student's PhD dissertation and presented twice before submission (p. 11).
- New-pattern candidate: *policy from the relaxation, certified by the same relaxation*: the primal support and reduced costs give the policy; Z1 and Z2 bound its suboptimality (pp. 7–11). Compare S043.
- New-pattern candidate: *order-k lifting hierarchy*: relaxations indexed by interaction order, trading bound strength for size, exact at the top (pp. 6–7, 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write a discounted MDP's achievable region as the flow-conservation polytope of state–action frequencies, and prove equality by showing every vertex is achieved by a stationary deterministic policy (pp. 4–5).
- Lift a hard polytope with variables for k-tuples of subsystems; add identities by counting how many tuples of each action pattern exist at every epoch; link the orders by marginalization constraints (pp. 6–7).
- Turn a relaxation into a policy in two stages, activating on positive primal variables and ranking the rest by reduced costs; then check whether it collapses to a priority-index rule under a mild assumption (pp. 8–9).
- Tabulate heuristic values, the exact optimum where computable and a hierarchy of bounds side by side across the parameter that controls difficulty (here the discount factor), and include one instance family on which the naive rule is known to fail (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Furthermore, the quality of proposed heuristics is usually assessed by comparing their empirical performance with that of alternative heuristics, which gives little information about their degree of suboptimality." (p. 1)
- "We have proposed an approach that provides a feasible policy together with a guarantee for its suboptimality for the restless bandit problem." (p. 11)

### S080 · When is time continuous? (Journal of Financial Economics 2000, DOI 10.1016/s0304-405x(99)00049-5)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–32 read, including Appendix A (proof of Theorem 1, pp. 27–30) and the references.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Leonid Kogan (Wharton), Andrew W. Lo (MIT, corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: finance with Lo at MIT's Laboratory for Financial Engineering, the probabilistic companion of the ε-arbitrage paper S069: it cites that work's 1997 working paper for reading prices as production costs (p. 7, p. 30), and S069 cites this paper (S069 p. 2). Funded by the LFE, NSF and his PYI award; presented widely, including the 1997 INFORMS Applied Probability Conference (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+AP}:
  - The entry is a philosophical question made operational: continuous-time processes only approximate physical phenomena, so when is a continuous-time model a good approximation? It is answered for derivatives through the replication error of delta hedging carried out in discrete time (pp. 1–3).
  - Gap in prior work: Boyle–Emanuel studied local errors over one interval; Toft's closed-form variance for calls and puts under geometric Brownian motion is pages long and hard to analyse; Leland and the transaction-cost literature study the trade-off between error and cost in special cases (pp. 4–5).
  - Problem: the asymptotic distribution of the tracking error of Merton's delta hedge rebalanced N times over a fixed horizon, for general diffusions and payoffs (p. 5, pp. 8–9).
- **D2 Key idea**:
  - Continuous-record asymptotics: N → ∞ with the time span fixed (p. 2, p. 8).
  - Result: √N times the tracking error converges in distribution to a stochastic integral G driven by a Brownian motion independent of the price's, so the limit is symmetric; RMSE = g/√N + O(1/N) (Theorem 1, pp. 9–10).
  - The leading constant is named *temporal granularity*, a property of a (payoff, price process) pair, and turned into a rule: about g²/d² trades for a target RMSE d, so halving the error takes four times the trades (p. 3, pp. 11–12).
  - No optimization: the hedge is the standard delta hedge; S069 optimizes it.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions: (A1) frictionless markets, (A2) zero interest rate (by renormalization, which rules out stochastic rates), (A3) a diffusion with volatility bounded below and regularity ensuring a complete market, (A4) N equally spaced trading dates (pp. 6–7).
  - Theorem 1 needs a payoff six times differentiable with bounded derivatives plus derivative bounds (2.10) on drift and volatility (p. 9). The authors note that this excludes calls and puts, which Theorem 2 covers (continuous piecewise-linear payoffs) under an extra smoothness condition (pp. 10–11).
  - With discrete trading, the pricing function is reinterpreted as the production cost of an approximate replication rather than an equilibrium price (p. 7).
  - Simulation design: geometric Brownian motion with drift 0.1, volatility 0.3, P0 = 1 and strike 1; N = 10, 20, 50, 100; 250,000 paths per setting; then P0, volatility and drift varied; an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck model with mean reversion 0.1 and 3.0 (pp. 15–20).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Proof of Theorem 1 (Appendix, pp. 27–30): (a) bound the pricing function's derivatives through the martingale representation and Friedman's theorems; write H(t, P_t) as the solution of an SDE system; read the discrete delta-hedge portfolio as an approximation scheme for that system; comparison with the Euler scheme gives one-step orders of accuracy, and Milstein's theorem lifts them to mean-square order 1/2 over the whole interval. (b) Comparison with the Milstein scheme isolates the leading error term, a sum of (ΔW)² − Δt weighted by half of σ²P² times the option's gamma; a functional CLT turns the normalized sum into an independent Brownian motion; Duffie–Protter's lemma closes the argument and Slutsky's theorem removes the O(1/N) remainder. (c) The isometry of stochastic integrals gives g (p. 30).
  - Theorems 2–4 (piecewise-linear payoffs; closed forms for geometric Brownian motion and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) are stated with proofs available on request (pp. 11, 13, 14, 27).
  - Comparative statics: g is zero at zero volatility, rises, then decays to zero as volatility grows; with zero drift g is unimodal in P0/K with its maximum at exp(σ²/2); a straddle has twice the granularity of a put or call (pp. 13–14).
  - Extensions (§5): weak convergence of the whole error path by tightness (Burkholder, Hölder) plus the continuous mapping theorem, used for American options (pp. 21–22); joint limits: tracking errors are asymptotically uncorrelated with every asset, and for derivatives with convex prices two tracking errors are asymptotically positively correlated even when the stocks' returns are negatively correlated (pp. 22–23); a Feynman–Kac PDE for g² (p. 24); L^p norms and general loss functions by the delta method (pp. 24–25).
  - Transferable: read a discretely implemented continuous-time policy as a numerical scheme for an SDE and import the scheme's accuracy theorems (pp. 27–30).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Monte Carlo only checks the asymptotics: the simulated densities of the normalized error are practically indistinguishable from the limit density already at N = 10 (p. 16, Fig. 1).
  - Tables 1–3: the relative error of g/√N against the simulated RMSE ranges from 0.04% to 8.9%; the largest values occur at N = 10 away from the money (pp. 16–18).
  - Findings: RMSE is highest near the money, but RMSE per dollar of option is highest out of the money; with strong mean reversion the peak moves to where exp(α) is near the strike (pp. 19–20).
  - The PDE method's numerical results are called preliminary and encouraging but are not shown (p. 24). No market data are used.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Generalizes Grannan–Swindle, who first derived the RMSE formula in the Black–Scholes case: this paper adds the asymptotic distribution and covers more general strategies, including stochastic time deformations (footnote 2, p. 10).
  - Reconciles its symmetric limit with Boyle–Emanuel's skewed local errors: the aggregation of local errors over the option's life is what makes the limit symmetric (p. 10).
  - Separates itself from the transaction-cost literature by studying only one side of its trade-off, which buys generality (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - A question as title; a philosophical opener set aside in one sentence for a practical question (p. 2).
  - The usable rule and the counterintuitive finding (granularity is not monotone in volatility, with the economic intuition) both appear in the introduction (p. 3).
  - Each theorem is followed by intuition paragraphs (pp. 10, 11–12, 13); only Theorem 1 is proved in print (p. 27).
  - The conclusion turns a limitation into a stance: the measure is not invariant to the contract and the process, and the authors call that a feature (p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: an asymptotic theory of discrete-hedging error (distribution, rate, constant), closed forms for two processes, simulation evidence and several extensions (p. 26).
  - Limits: frictionless, complete markets; equally spaced rebalancing; only two closed forms; proofs of Theorems 2–4 not published; no market data; the cost side of discrete trading set aside (pp. 5–7, 11, 13–14).
  - Future: only the hope that granularity is one useful way to tackle the issue (pp. 26–27); the optimized hedge is S069.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (loose): one interpretable constant, g, prices the cost of discrete time, and the paper publishes the knob curve (N ≈ g²/d²) together with its bad cases (near the money; non-monotone in volatility) (pp. 3, 12–14, 19). But g is a descriptive asymptotic constant, not a knob in an optimization model, and there is no solver class to keep.
- Tension T3 ✅ evidence (optimization versus probability): a co-authored paper built on continuous-record asymptotics, a functional CLT and weak convergence, with no optimization formulation at all (pp. 8–11, 27–30).
- New-pattern candidate: *graded measure of a binary property*: replace a yes/no notion (continuous or discrete time) by the leading constant of an error expansion (pp. 3, 11–12, 26). S069 does the same for market completeness.
- New-pattern candidate: *check the asymptotics by simulation at small N* (pp. 14–18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To get the error of a discretely implemented continuous-time policy, read the implementation as a numerical scheme for an SDE system and import the scheme-accuracy theorems (Euler for the rate, Milstein for the leading term), then apply a functional CLT (pp. 27–30).
- Name the leading constant of an error expansion and turn it into a sizing rule (N ≈ g²/d²) (p. 3, p. 12).
- Check every asymptotic formula by Monte Carlo at the smallest practical N and tabulate its relative error (pp. 15–18).
- Give the constant a Feynman–Kac PDE representation so that it can be computed without simulation (p. 24).
- Prove weak convergence of the whole error process and use the continuous mapping theorem to get path functionals (maximum error, error at an exercise time) at no extra cost (pp. 21–22).

**Quotes**:
- "If one derivative-pricing model has twice the granularity of another, it would require four times as many delta-hedging transactions to achieve the same RMSE tracking error." (p. 12)
- "But we regard this as a positive feature of our approach, not a drawback." (p. 26)

### S069 · Hedging derivative securities and incomplete markets: An ϵ-arbitrage approach (Operations Research 2001, DOI 10.1287/opre.49.3.372.11218)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–26 read, including the appendix (proof sketches, pp. 22–24) and the references; figures read through their captions and the text.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Leonid Kogan (Wharton), Andrew W. Lo (MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the optimization half of the pair with S080, which it cites (p. 2); its working-paper version dates from 1997 (S080 p. 30). Received May 1997, accepted July 2000 (p. 1); funded by MIT's Laboratory for Financial Engineering, NSF and his PYI award (p. 24).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+AP}:
  - The entry is what dynamic spanning leaves open. Exact replication needs nontrivial conditions; with stochastic volatility, jumps, frictions, market closures or discrete trading it is impossible, and equilibrium pricing leaves the replication question unanswered (p. 1).
  - Problem: for an arbitrary European payoff and a set of fundamental securities, find the self-financing strategy whose terminal value is closest to the payoff in mean square (p. 1, p. 4).
  - Practical motive: dealers can write illiquid over-the-counter derivatives only because they can synthesize them by dynamic trading (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**:
  - Solve replication by stochastic dynamic programming with a quadratic terminal loss over a vector Markov state (P, Z), where Z holds the state variables that cannot be hedged (Assumption 3) (pp. 3–5).
  - Theorem 1 (discrete time): the cost-to-go is quadratic in the portfolio value V, the optimal stock holding is linear in V, and the coefficients follow backward recursions; the least-cost initial wealth V0* = b0 and the minimal error ε* = √c0 follow (pp. 5–6). Theorem 2 is the continuous-time analogue, a PDE system from the HJB equation (pp. 6–7).
  - ε* measures the degree of market incompleteness, a gradient instead of a yes/no property; V0* is a minimum production cost and explicitly not a price (p. 2, p. 7).
  - Against Black–Scholes delta hedging: in the GBM case the first term of the optimal rule is the delta and the second, a wealth correction, vanishes in the continuous-time limit; delta hedging therefore need not be optimal in discrete time (p. 6).
  - Path-dependent payoffs are made Markov by adding a running maximum, a running average or a knock-out indicator to the state (p. 5).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Assumptions: frictionless markets; zero riskless rate (by renormalization); a finite-dimensional Markov state not perfectly correlated with traded prices, the only source of incompleteness, in reduced form; known fixed trading times (pp. 3–4).
  - Tractability choices, admitted: mean-squared error first for tractability, second for symmetry (a dealer quoting bid and ask does not know which side the client takes) (pp. 7–8); no constraints on holdings, because forcing a nonnegative terminal value would make the problem intractable (p. 4).
  - Numerical setting: a European put with strike 1, six-month maturity, 25 trading periods of length 1/50; GBM with drift 0.07 and volatility 0.13; Merton's jump-diffusion with at most three jumps per interval (a necessary evil, after Ball–Torous); a stochastic-volatility model in discretized form, with transition densities approximated by Monte Carlo; a sell-at-the-high path-dependent option (pp. 12–17).
  - Each parameter is varied by ±25% and ±50% with the others fixed (pp. 12, 15–16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Theorem 1: backward induction with first-order conditions; convexity in the holding follows from the positivity of the quadratic coefficient (Appendix A.1, p. 22). Theorem 2: verification in the HJB equation, with the algebra done in Maple (Appendix A.2, pp. 22–24). Both proofs are sketches; a detailed appendix is offered on request (p. 22).
  - Consistency checks against known theory: the GBM continuous-time limit recovers the Black–Scholes PDE and zero error, and the optimal rule coincides path by path with the Black–Scholes hedge ratio although its functional form differs (pp. 8–9).
  - Known exact-replication conditions recovered as special cases: for the jump-diffusion, zero jump probability, zero jump size or zero diffusion (Merton 1976) (pp. 10–11); for stochastic volatility, deterministic volatility or perfectly correlated Brownian motions (p. 12).
  - Perturbation in a small jump amplitude: for convex payoffs the replication cost rises above the Black–Scholes price, and the replication error is of fourth order in the amplitude (p. 11).
  - For stochastic volatility the problem is posed in continuous time from the start, because the discrete-time algebra is too involved (p. 11).
  - Transferable: validate a new method by recovering the known special cases before showing new ones (pp. 8–12).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Numerics: the recursion coefficients are stored on a spatial grid with piecewise-quadratic interpolation, and expectations are computed by Gauss–Hermite quadrature (p. 12). Grid sizes and run times are not reported.
  - Baseline: the practitioner's rule, Black–Scholes delta hedging, for an at-the-money put on 1,000 shares of a $40 stock over 25 periods. On one illustrative path the optimal strategy has the larger error, on a path with two large moves the much smaller one (the terminal errors' decimal points are lost in the extraction); over 250,000 paths the average error is $241.2 against $248.0 (pp. 2–3, Table 1).
  - Sensitivity figures: replication cost, error and relative error against P0 under each model (Figs. 1–12, pp. 13–22).
  - Table 2: 1,000 simulated paths for each of six stochastic-volatility parameter sets moving toward GBM; realized errors track the theoretical ε*, which falls from 0.0086 to 0.0060 (pp. 16–19).
  - §5: three models calibrated to the same expected return and volatility; hedging costs nearly identical for GBM and the jump-diffusion; the stochastic-volatility model has the largest error for these parameters, with the caveat that the order can be reversed (pp. 18–21; Table 3, p. 24). Finding: with discrete trading, the relative errors of complete and incomplete models are of comparable size, so discrete trading is as important a source of incompleteness as missing instruments (p. 21).
  - Transferable: calibrate competing models to the same first two moments before comparing their hedging errors (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Complements Schweizer's mean-variance hedging: concedes that the approach is a special case of his and claims sharper results and an implementable procedure from the Markov assumption (p. 2).
  - Complements the transaction-cost literature (Leland onwards), which solves special cases; this handles arbitrary payoffs under vector Markov dynamics (p. 2).
  - Separates replication from pricing: preferences (Duffie–Jackson, Duffie–Richardson) or an equilibrium are needed for a price (p. 7); bounds on ε* over all equivalent martingale measures are predicted to be extremely wide without further structure (p. 8). The next year S056, with another co-author, takes the bounds route for prices, with moment and option-price information as the extra structure.
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Opens with Merton's insight and asks how close one can come (p. 1); coins *ε-arbitrage* as the brand.
  - The introduction shows two hand-picked paths, one on which the method loses, labels them illustrative rather than conclusive, and then gives the 250,000-path averages (p. 2).
  - Objections are answered under their own headings: why V0* is not a price, and why mean-squared error (pp. 7–8).
  - A modest close: one of many possible measures, with no claim of generality (p. 21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a DP solution of mean-square optimal replication, with exact recursions in discrete time and PDEs in continuous time, a measure of incompleteness, and numerical studies of jumps, stochastic volatility and a path-dependent option (pp. 21–22).
  - Limits: a symmetric quadratic loss; unconstrained holdings, so the terminal value can be negative; a Markov state; no frictions; proofs sketched; numerical details (grid, timing) missing; a small average gain over delta hedging in the GBM example (pp. 2, 4, 7–8, 12, 22).
  - Future: other implications of dynamic replication, unspecified (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: a problem owned by financial economics is written as an optimization problem whose structure is kept by the device: quadratic loss plus a Markov state keep the value function quadratic in wealth and the policy linear, giving exact recursions; the boundary is printed (nonnegativity constraints would make it intractable) (pp. 4–6). The class kept is a quadratic DP recursion, not LP, conic or MIO.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (simulated): the hedging decisions are scored against the practitioner's current rule (Black–Scholes delta hedging) by the decision metric, the replication error, including a path on which the method loses and averages over 250,000 paths (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: *graded measure of a binary property*: market completeness becomes ε*, which ranks models by their degree of incompleteness, something equilibrium models cannot give (pp. 2, 8, 17–19, 21). S080 does the same for continuous time.
- New-pattern candidate: *objections under their own headings* (pp. 7–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a tracking or hedging problem, use a quadratic terminal loss with a Markov state so that the value function stays quadratic in wealth and the policy linear; then minimize the quadratic in initial wealth for the least-cost start (pp. 5–6).
- Make path-dependent objectives Markov by adding a running statistic to the state (p. 5).
- Validate a new method by recovering the known special cases: the complete-market continuous-time limit and the classical exact-replication conditions (pp. 8–12).
- Expand around the complete-market solution in a small incompleteness parameter to get closed-form corrections (p. 11).
- Implement backward recursions on a grid with piecewise-quadratic interpolation and Gauss–Hermite quadrature (p. 12).
- Show the incumbent rule beating you on one path before showing the average over many paths (p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "Perfect replication is impossible in dynamically incomplete markets, but how close can one come, and what does the optimal-replication strategy look like?" (p. 1)
- "a gradient for market completeness seems more natural from an empirical and a practical point of view." (p. 2)

### S056 · On the relation between option and stock prices: a convex optimization approach (Operations Research 2002, DOI 10.1287/opre.50.2.358.424)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–17 read, including the appendix (proof of Proposition 1, p. 16), the endnote and the references.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ioana Popescu (INSEAD) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the finance application of the moment-problem programme with Popescu (SW2): it cites their 1999 working paper on optimal inequalities in probability as the modern treatment of the underlying problem (p. 3, p. 16). Received July 1999, accepted September 2000 (p. 1); funded by NSF and the MIT–Singapore Alliance (p. 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP+LO+AP}:
  - The entry is a long-standing finance question: what follows about option prices from no-arbitrage alone, with no model of the price dynamics (Cox–Ross, Harrison–Kreps, Ross, Rubinstein, Lo 1987, Grundy 1991, Boyle–Lin 1997) (pp. 1–2).
  - Five numbered questions organize the paper: bounds on an option given moments; bounds on moments given option prices; bounds on other options given option prices; multi-asset bounds with partial moment and correlation information; the effect of transaction costs (p. 1). Grundy (1991) had posed several of them as open problems (p. 2).
  - All are instances of one problem: maximize or minimize E[φ(X)] over distributions with given generalized moments (problem (2), p. 2).
  - Transferable: number the field's open questions in the introduction and show that one optimization problem contains them all (pp. 1–3).
- **D2 Key idea**:
  - No-arbitrage means a martingale measure exists, so price bounds are moment problems over probability distributions (p. 2).
  - Dualize (strong duality by Isii): the dual constraint says a polynomial lies above the payoff, i.e., polynomial nonnegativity on intervals; nonnegative univariate polynomials are sums of squares, i.e., PSD Gram matrices (Proposition 1, inspired by Ben-Tal–Nemirovski) (pp. 2–4). So one SDP gives the best bound on a call given n moments (Theorem 1), and on any piecewise-polynomial payoff (Theorem 2) (pp. 4–5).
  - Bounds from other option prices become LPs over discretized distributions with closed-form solutions (Theorem 4): a call-price function is valid if and only if it is decreasing and convex, so the bounds interpolate between neighbouring strikes (pp. 5–8).
  - Variance bounds from call prices: the upper bound in closed form (Theorem 5); the lower bound through a separable quadratic network-flow problem solved by a one-dimensional DP (Theorem 6) (pp. 9–11).
  - Transaction costs: bid–ask intervals replace the equalities; there is no arbitrage if and only if a convex decreasing function fits between bids and asks, an easy arbitrage test (Theorem 7, pp. 12–13).
  - Several assets: relax the support from the nonnegative orthant to all of R^m to get polynomial-time SDP bounds (Theorem 8), and prove the exact problem NP-hard (Theorem 9) (pp. 13–15).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - One economic assumption, no arbitrage; zero interest rate without loss of generality (p. 2); moments or option prices taken as known.
  - Variance bounds need a finite upper bound K on the price at maturity, introduced as a call with zero price (p. 9); strong duality for several assets needs the moment vector to lie in the interior of the feasible moment set (endnote, p. 16).
  - Tractability choice, admitted: optimizing over martingale measures on R^m rather than R^m_+ gives weaker, non-optimal bounds that can be computed (p. 13).
  - Data: Wall Street Journal closing quotes of 7 July 1998. Microsoft July '98 calls (Fig. 1, p. 6); S&P 100, S&P 500, Yahoo and Amazon calls (Fig. 2, p. 7); Microsoft January '99 calls with strikes 80 to 140, riskless rate 0.0557, half a year to maturity, and an assumed upper bound K = 160 (p. 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**:
  - Proposition 1: a nonnegative polynomial has real roots of even multiplicity and conjugate complex pairs, hence is a sum of squares, hence a PSD Gram form; the interval cases reduce to the whole line by the substitutions x = a t²/(1+t²) for [0, a], x = a(1+t²) for [a, ∞) and x = a + (b − a) t²/(1+t²) for [a, b] (Appendix, p. 16).
  - A new duality proof of Lo's closed-form bound (Theorem 3): the optimal dual quadratic is tangent to the payoff line, with two cases according to where its minimum lies (p. 5).
  - Closed forms through discretization: the LP restricted to a finite support has the same value as the continuous problem (weak duality on both sides plus LP strong duality), so an extremal discrete distribution exists; the closed form comes from enumerating which dual constraints bind at a basic optimal solution (pp. 6–8).
  - Theorem 6 turns a geometric tangent construction into a quadratic network-flow problem; a DP over the initial value d0 solves it, with d0 = 0 recommended as a heuristic start (pp. 10–11).
  - Theorem 8: nonnegativity of a quadratic over R^m is one PSD condition (part a); for piecewise-quadratic data over a polyhedral partition, a separation routine (Algorithm A: a negative eigenvalue yields a violating point, otherwise d convex QPs are solved) plus the equivalence of separation and optimization gives polynomial time (part b) (pp. 13–15).
  - Theorem 9: over R^m_+ the separation problem is copositivity testing, which is NP-hard (Murty–Kabadi), so the optimal bound is NP-hard (p. 15).
  - Transferable: get closed forms by discretizing the support, proving by duality that nothing is lost, and enumerating the binding constraints of a basic optimal solution (pp. 6–8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Illustrative only. Market quotes yield nonconvex call-price functions, so some computed upper bounds fall below the lower bounds; the authors attribute this to closing prices that were not simultaneous and to the transaction costs ignored (p. 7, Fig. 2).
  - Microsoft January '99: given the Black–Scholes mean, the standard deviation lies in [26.6677, 26.9851], an implied volatility of 0.3223 to 0.3259 against the Black–Scholes 0.3241; with only the option-implied interval for the mean, [97.6829, 111.8755], the standard deviation lies between 23.7660 and 49.2101, and the bounds are tight for each fixed mean (Table 1, p. 12).
  - No SDP is solved in the paper and no run time is reported; efficient means polynomial time, supported by citations to interior-point theory and the SDPA solver (p. 3). The claim that the d0 = 0 start performs very well in practice carries no data (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Generalizes Lo (1987), Grundy (1991) and Boyle–Lin (1997) (p. 3); traces the moment problem to Scarf (1958, inventory), Isii, Karlin–Studden and Smith (1995) (pp. 2–3).
  - Claims the method for the whole question: convex optimization is the natural way to address it (pp. 1–2, p. 15); calls the link to semidefinite optimization unexpected (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - Five numbered questions as the roadmap (p. 1); the simplest case (a call bound from mean and variance) is posed first as an optimization problem (p. 2).
  - The contributions list mislabels the questions: §3 is said to answer Question (2) and §4 Question (3), the reverse of the introduction's numbering, and §5 (transaction costs) Question (3) instead of (5) (p. 3, against p. 1). A typo in a theorem heading (Thoerem 4, p. 6).
  - Geometric figures carry the intuition (Fig. 1, p. 6; Fig. 3, p. 11). The conclusion restates the abstract almost word for word (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: a unified convex-optimization treatment of model-free option bounds: an SDP for moment-based bounds, closed forms for option-based bounds, variance bounds, transaction costs, polynomial multi-asset bounds and an NP-hardness boundary (p. 3, p. 15).
  - Stated limits: multi-asset bounds are not best possible, and best possible ones are NP-hard to find (p. 13, p. 15).
  - Visible limits: no computational study of the SDPs; a heuristic start in the variance lower bound's DP; the market illustration shows the frictionless bounds violated (pp. 7, 11, 12).
  - No future work is stated (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a question owned by financial economics is written exactly as a moment problem; the device keeps it in a solvable class (dual polynomial nonnegativity becomes an SDP; option-based bounds become LPs with closed forms); structure is read off the dual (extremal discrete distributions; the convexity test for arbitrage); the boundary is printed (NP-hard over R^m_+, polynomial over R^m) (pp. 2–15). *Tractable* here means polynomial time without any solve time, consistent with the Method 1 limitation that the word meant complexity class in his early work.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the why-now is a tool change, SDP being solvable in theory and in practice (Nesterov–Nemirovski, Vandenberghe–Boyd, the SDPA solver), but it is stated qualitatively, with no figure (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *closed form from an LP's basic solution* (discretize the support, prove equivalence by duality, enumerate the binding constraints) (pp. 6–8).
- New-pattern candidate: *relax the support to regain polynomial solvability, then prove the unrelaxed problem NP-hard* (pp. 13–15), a specific form of Method 1's step 5.

**Transferable techniques**:
- For best-possible bounds on E[φ(X)] given moments: dualize, write the dual constraint as polynomial nonnegativity on intervals, map each interval to the real line by a substitution, and impose PSD Gram matrices (Proposition 1, pp. 3–4, 16).
- Get closed forms by solving over a discrete support, showing with weak and strong LP duality that nothing is lost, and enumerating the binding dual constraints at a basic optimal solution (pp. 6–8).
- Characterize valid pricing functions by shape (decreasing and convex), and turn bid–ask data into an arbitrage test: can a convex decreasing function pass between them? (p. 6, p. 12)
- When the exact problem is NP-hard through copositivity, relax the domain to R^m for an SDP bound, and prove the hardness through the equivalence of separation and optimization (pp. 13–15).
- Illustrate the bounds on public market quotes and compare them with the incumbent model's number, here the Black–Scholes implied volatility (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "This result leads to an unexpected connection between finance and semidefinite optimization." (p. 3)
- "We have demonstrated that convex optimization is the natural way to address the relation between option and stock prices without making distributional assumptions for the underlying price dynamics, using only the no-arbitrage assumption." (p. 15)
