### B001 · Introduction to Linear Optimization (Bertsimas, Tsitsiklis; Athena Scientific 1997): Preface, as served on the publisher's web page (book material 1997, http://www.athenasc.com/linoptpreface.html)
Read level: **full**: the whole web preface, one file page, every paragraph.
- Fetched 2026-10-29; HTTP Last-Modified 1999-11-04.

**Role**: core (book material: preface) · **Coauthors**: John N. Tsitsiklis. The preface speaks in the first-person plural throughout and attributes nothing to either author alone. · **Position in trajectory**: the LO textbook of the 1995–2002 row (S002, abstract-level card in a3-01). It was "developed while we took turns teaching a first-year graduate course at M.I.T., for students in engineering and operations research" (¶ course). His 2014 oral history gives the same origin (01 §1.9; oral history p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {LO + IO, with finance (AP), stochastic programming, HS and AP case studies as topics}:
  - Purpose: "a unified, insightful, and modern treatment of linear optimization, that is, linear programming, network flow problems, and discrete linear optimization". It covers classical topics and the state of the art, with "special attention to theory" but also applications and case studies (¶ purpose).
  - Main objective: to help the reader become "a sophisticated practitioner of (linear) optimization, or a researcher". This means four abilities, in this order (¶ purpose):
    1. formulate fairly complex optimization problems;
    2. appreciate "the main classes of problems that are practically solvable";
    3. describe the available solution methods;
    4. understand the qualitative properties of the solutions.
  - Domain codes:
    - LO: chapters 1–9 and 12. IO: chapters 10–11.
    - Excursions: stochastic programming (Benders, ch. 6); finance (duality and the pricing of financial assets; options pricing in the exercises); HS (local search and simulated annealing, ch. 11); AP (the ch. 12 case studies, titles in B004) (¶ topics; ¶ Chapter 6, 11, 12).
  - Transferable technique: name the reader's target role, and the abilities that make it up in order of priority, before any content (¶ purpose).
- **D2 Core idea (book design)**:
  - *Philosophy*: "insight matters most" (Quote 1). This "necessarily requires a geometric view", while algorithms "can only be described algebraically". So the focus is "the beautiful interplay between algebra and geometry": understanding is built from figures and geometric arguments, then translated into formulas and algorithms (¶ philosophy).
  - *"comprehensive, but economical"*: all the principal ideas, but not encyclopedic, and not every detail of every algorithm. Once the principles are mature, the details come with little effort (¶ economical).
  - *State of the art*: interior point methods, large-scale optimization, and case studies "that stretch the limits of currently available algorithms and computers" (¶ state of the art).
  - *The art chapter*: the last chapter is "a critical part of this book", for two reasons (¶ success):
    - success depends on large and important problems (Quote 2);
    - progress needs "both problem specific insight, as well as a deeper understanding of the underlying theory".
  - *Three departures from other textbooks*:
    1. The full simplex tableau is only "a useful device for working out numerical examples" (¶ simplex).
    2. LP is treated in general form, not standard form. Concepts such as extreme points are defined in the general form; the standard form is used only for algorithms (¶ departure).
    3. "we separate the structural understanding of linear programming from the particulars of the simplex method": duality theory is derived without relying on the simplex method (¶ departure).
  - *Topics "not commonly covered"* (¶ topics):
    - column geometry and the simplex method's efficiency;
    - duality and the pricing of financial assets;
    - a unified view of delayed column generation and cutting planes;
    - stochastic programming and Benders decomposition;
    - the auction algorithm;
    - theoretical implications of the ellipsoid algorithm;
    - a thorough treatment of interior point methods;
    - a whole chapter on practice;
    - and, in the exercises: Leontief systems, strict complementarity, options pricing, von Neumann's algorithm, submodular function minimization, and "bounds for a number of integer programming problems".
  - *A cross-chapter theme*: "the modeling, complexity, and algorithms for problems with an exponential number constraints" [sic]. Modeling is in §10.3, complexity in §8.5, algorithms in ch. 6 and §8.5, and a case study in §12.5 (¶ theme).
  - *Chapter map*, as the preface describes it (¶ Chapter 1–12; titles and pages in B004):
    - ch. 1: LP, with examples and linear algebra;
    - ch. 2: polyhedra and extreme points, "emphasizing the interplay betwen the geometric and the algebraic viewpoints" [sic];
    - ch. 3: the simplex method, from "a high-level and geometrically motivated derivation" to the revised simplex, ending with the tableau; Phase I, anticycling and column geometry;
    - ch. 4: duality, "first obtained as a corollary of the simplex method", then derived from a separating hyperplane theorem developed from first principles;
    - ch. 5: sensitivity analysis and parametric programming; dual optimal solutions as subgradients of an optimal cost function;
    - ch. 6: delayed column generation and cutting planes as complementary ideas (cutting stock, Dantzig–Wolfe, stochastic programming, Benders);
    - ch. 7: network flows, with one representative per type of algorithm (primal descent, dual ascent, approximate dual ascent) and the focus on the main ideas, "rather than on the refinements that can lead to better complexity estimates";
    - ch. 8: complexity, the ellipsoid method, "a proof of the polynomiality of linear programming", separation and optimization, exponentially many constraints;
    - ch. 9: interior point methods: geometry, computational issues, convergence proofs and complexity analysis;
    - ch. 10: IP formulations, with "some intuition as to what constitutes a ``strong'' formulation";
    - ch. 11: IP methods: exact methods, approximation algorithms and "heuristic methods (local search and simulated annealing)", plus a duality theory for IP;
    - ch. 12: the art: modeling, exploiting structure and tuning algorithms; interior point methods against simplex variants "in a realistic large scale setting"; "some indication of the size of problems that can be currently solved".
  - Transferable technique: state each departure from the standard treatment with its reason. For example, the standard form "can hinder the deeper understanding of the subject" (¶ departure).
- **D3 Prerequisites and scope**:
  - Prerequisite: "a working knowledge of linear algebra", in fact a small subset of it (subspaces, linear independence, rank), "sometimes used in subtle ways". Mathematical maturity helps (¶ course).
  - Scope: linear and discrete linear optimization (¶ purpose). No nonlinear or conic topic is named.
- **D4 Theory and proof policy** (as the preface names them):
  - duality proved twice, from the simplex method and from a separating hyperplane theorem (¶ Chapter 4);
  - dual solutions as subgradients (¶ Chapter 5);
  - polynomiality of LP via the ellipsoid method, and the equivalence of separation and optimization (¶ Chapter 8);
  - convergence proofs and complexity analysis for interior point methods (¶ Chapter 9);
  - a duality theory for IP (¶ Chapter 11);
  - for network flows, the main algorithmic ideas but not the complexity refinements (¶ Chapter 7).
  - Transferable technique: derive a structural result apart from the algorithm that first yields it, so readers know which properties hold in general (¶ departure).
- **D5 Numerics, exercises, cases**:
  - Ch. 12 compares interior point methods with simplex variants in a realistic large-scale setting and indicates the size of problems that can currently be solved (¶ Chapter 12). The preface reports no numbers.
  - The exercises are "intended to deepen the understanding of the subject, or to explore extensions of the theory in the text, as opposed to routine drills". Some are numerical, the starred ones are fairly hard, and a solutions manual is offered to qualified instructors (¶ exercises).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The book sets itself against "many other textbooks" on the tableau and on the standard form (¶ simplex, ¶ departure), and lists topics "not commonly covered" (¶ topics).
  - References: "we make no attempt to provide a comprehensive bibliography". The sources cited are original references of historical interest, recent texts, and recent journal articles where a topic touches current research (¶ references).
  - Acknowledgements (¶ acknowledgements):
    - Colleagues Dimitri Bertsekas and Rob Freund are thanked for discussions and for reading parts of the manuscript. This is the Bertsekas whom the skill keeps apart from Bertsimas; here he is a thanked colleague. Freund later co-authored S128.
    - Fifteen students, colleagues and friends are named for reading, criticism and work on the exercises (card's count).
    - Seven of the fifteen are Bertsimas coauthors in works.json: Chryssikou, Gamarnik, Mourtzinou, Paschalidis, Perakis, Sethuraman and Stock (S015, the 1998 air-traffic paper).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The objectives come in sequence, in the first-person plural ("Our general philosophy", "Another of our objectives", "Our last objective").
  - Each departure gets its own paragraph, with its reason.
  - A chapter-by-chapter description is followed by modularity guidance and course plans.
  - There is one hedge: "It will, we hope, convince the reader" (¶ success).
- **D8 Contribution and stated limits**:
  - Four course designs, two of them tried at MIT (¶ course suggestions):
    1. most of ch. 1–7 plus a few topics from ch. 9–12;
    2. the same, with ch. 9 covered fully in place of ch. 7;
    3. a broad overview built from ch. 1, §§2.1–2.4, 3.1–3.5, 4.1–4.3, 5.1, 7.1–7.3, 9.1, 10.1, the easier parts of ch. 11 and one application from ch. 12;
