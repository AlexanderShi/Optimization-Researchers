# Batch s2-15 · Dimitris Bertsimas · supplement (round 2)

**Batch intent**: Eight works from 2022–2023. In most of them a partner (a company, a hospital group, a clinical trial, a professional society) sets the problem, and the method serves it. I read them to see what the lens keeps and what it drops in that setting. Four strands:
- prescriptive analytics scored against a partner's current rule: S339 (OCP's sales and supply-chain MIO, deployed through Dynamic Ideas), S353 (an anti-sedentary message policy for the HeartSteps trial) and S370 (antihypertensive treatment from Boston Medical Center EHR data);
- ML methods under the optimization lens: S365 (stable classification by robust optimization over training subsets) and S459 (SparClur: sparse regression with one support shared by all tree leaves);
- position pieces in clinical journals: S364 (an editorial on ML and automated extraction for the STS registry) and S430 (a surgical perspective on interpretability and bias);
- D019, a two-page publisher correction to S274.

Questions asked of each work:
1. Does the formulation keep a solver class, and does the paper print where tractability ends (Method 1)?
2. Is interpretability or stability priced against accuracy with one knob, and is the bad case printed (Method 3)?
3. Which incumbent is dueled, at what budget, and are certificates or gaps reported (Method 4)?
4. Is the decision scored against the owner's current rule, and who computes the counterfactual (Method 5)?
5. Do the headline numbers match the tables (Tension T6)?

### S339 · Toward global food security: Transforming OCP through analytics (INFORMS Journal on Applied Analytics 2022, DOI 10.1287/inte.2021.1111)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–19: the INFORMS cover, abstract, introduction, central problem, analytics approach with scalability and deployment, impact, concluding remarks, appendix with the model, and author biographies. Figures 1–5 and A.1–A.2 are captions only. First-page check: the title matches, and Dimitris Bertsimas is the third of ten authors (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**:
- Adrian Becker, Steve Kokkotos and Georgia Mourtzinou (Dynamic Ideas LLC);
- Kassem Benabderrazik, Nada Chtinna, Nada El Majdoub, El Miloudi Mahboubi and Ilyas Rakhis (OCP);
- Driss Lahlou Kitane (OCP and MIT) (p. 2).

**Position in trajectory**: the applied write-up of a deployment carried by Dynamic Ideas, the consulting company that Bertsimas cofounded with Mourtzinou (p. 3; biography p. 18). The keywords include "Edelman Award" (p. 2). It is the first OCP paper; S307 (c30) followed.

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + IO; LO and CP (second-order cone storage constraints) as engines}:
  - Scale: OCP extracts about 40 million tons of ore a year from more than 45 fronts at eight mining locations (p. 3). It sells 22 grades of rock, 9 qualities of phosphoric acid and 43 grades of fertilizer (p. 5).
  - Entry: before 2006 the departments worked in silos (p. 6):
    - sales favoured the products with the highest unit gross margin;
    - mining maximized extracted volume;
    - chemical processing worked with whatever rock it received.

    The company was in financial distress, with a $3.8 billion long-term liability (p. 4).
  - Problem: one multiperiod MIO from the mines to the port (pp. 6–7):
    - it maximizes the margin on variable cost over the horizon;
    - secondary objectives cover line switching and the profitability of inventories;
    - alternative primary objectives are available.
- **D2 Core idea**: Put all major operational decisions on one decision platform, so that the departments plan against one corporate objective (p. 3).
  - Three features are said to require "a novel modeling approach" (p. 6):
    - several objectives;
    - substitute products that can fill a contract;
    - complex extraction, blending, transport and production constraints, with nonlinear joint-venture dynamics.
  - Two guiding principles shape the formulation (p. 15):
    - any number and composition of products;
    - general decision variables, so that managers can test new connections, capacities and inventories.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The model is deterministic. Demand enters as contracts with a minimum volume, a maximum volume and a price. The model picks the volume and may substitute products (pp. 7, 15).
  - A product at the port is assumed to meet demand at once (p. 7).
  - Data: in 2011, people on the same team could not agree on one data set. The team alternated between improving the data and updating the model. The resulting database now also feeds reports and KPIs (pp. 7–8).
  - The start of the collaboration is dated 2010 on pp. 3 and 8, and 2011 on pp. 6–7 [my reading].
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Binary devices in the appendix (pp. 16–17):
    - semicontinuous production: minVol·δ ≤ extract ≤ δ·maxVol;
    - a cap on the number of products per facility;
    - switch indicators linked by 1 + δswitch ≥ βprod(t) + βprod(t − 1), whose wasted capacity is charged inside the capacity constraint;
    - stack-level binaries for storage halls;
    - joint-venture tolling and profit sharing.
  - The storage dynamics use second-order cone constraints, which the appendix leaves out (p. 15).
  - Sizes and times (p. 8):
    - monthly model: about 6,500 rows, 6,000 columns (440 integer) and 20,000 nonzeros;
    - multiperiod model: 18,000 rows and 16,000 variables (1,200 integer);
    - a single-period run takes 0.5 s of optimization and 31 s including reports, and stays under a minute with switching.
  - The daily port-loading model has 600,000 rows, 500,000 columns and 60,000 binaries, with longer runs and "larger" gaps (p. 8). Three remedies are named:
    - formulations with tighter relaxations;
    - heuristic starting solutions (boats pre-assigned to gates, coarser time intervals);
    - Gurobi callbacks that shift effort between feasibility and optimality.

    No gap value is printed [my reading]. There is no theory.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: Impact is estimated, not observed.
  - For 2020, each capability is compared in the model with the old rule (pp. 10–11):

    | Comparison | 2020 impact |
    |---|---|
    | Sales arbitrage against a higher-unit-margin-first portfolio | $200 million |
    | Multiperiod against single-period planning (the authors say this overestimates the single-period solution) | $130 million |
    | Optimized blending against manual paths (about 5% more output) | $60 million |
    | Tactical scheduling of bottlenecks | $60 million |
    | Supply-chain design changes found from shadow prices | $40 million |
    | **Total** | **$490 million** |

  - The other years (pp. 9, 11):
    - 2017–2019 use the same method;
    - 2015 rests on a confidential consulting report;
    - 2016 applies the 23.6% average ratio of the other years.
  - Table 1 totals $2,330 million against $9,855 million of EBITDA (p. 11).
  - Two numbers disagree [my computation]: the introduction says 23.2% (p. 4), and 2020's 24.0% in Table 1 should be 490/2,017 = 24.3% (p. 11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Claimed (pp. 2, 11, 13):
    - over $2.3 billion of EBITDA in 2015–2020, and at least 20% of EBITDA in each year;
    - a $20 billion CAPEX programme prioritized by the bottlenecks the model exposed;
    - fertilizer formulas growing from 4 in 2007 to more than 40 in 2020;
    - a culture of collaboration;
    - an MIT executive-education class with 100 students in 2021.
  - Extensions under test (pp. 8–9):
    - extraction scheduling;
    - boat scheduling;
    - a daily tactical model;
    - a robust and adaptive version that scales to the same sizes as the deterministic one;
    - a CAPEX model.
  - Limits are not discussed [my reading]:
    - the counterfactuals come from the authors' own model;
    - 2015 and 2016 are not computed;
    - the authors are the consultant and the client.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 6, 8–11): the baseline is the owner's own pre-2006 rule (higher-margin-first sales, siloed operations), and the score is a decision metric, EBITDA. The model has been the primary decision platform since 2015, and OCP teams run it without the consultant (pp. 6, 8). The counterfactual is the model's re-run, not an observed outcome.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 13–14): adoption is carried by Dynamic Ideas, a company he cofounded. Bertsimas teaches the case in an MIT Sloan executive class with OCP's chief executive (p. 13). The paper also claims portability to a steel maker and to Morocco's national airline (pp. 13–14).
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3, 8–9): the class stays MIO with second-order cone constraints, solve times are printed, and the robust and adaptive extension is said to scale to the same sizes as the deterministic model (p. 9).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (p. 8): heuristic starts and tighter formulations are used for the daily model, but its gaps are only called "larger" and are not quantified.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Attribute a deployed model's value to separable capabilities. Switch one capability off at a time against the owner's old rule, sum the parts, and say which comparison flatters the model (pp. 10–11).
- When a large daily MIO stalls at the root relaxation, build starting solutions from a coarser time grid or a pre-assignment. Use callbacks to move the solver between feasibility and optimality (p. 8).
- Start with a spreadsheet LP covering one mine, one treatment plant and one chemical facility, as the 2008 proof of concept did. Then grow the MIO module by module and deploy it progressively: components from 2011, the fully functional version in 2015 (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "We had to experiment with different formulations to identify those that yield tighter relaxations and with heuristics to create appropriate starting solutions so that Gurobi would avoid long delays at the root relaxation." (p. 8)
- "we compared the revenue generated through the multiperiod comprehensive planning of the current model with the revenue that would have been generated under the siloed approach that the company followed before the transformation." (p. 9)

### S353 · Data-driven interpretable policy construction for personalized mobile health (2022 IEEE International Conference on Digital Health (ICDH) 2022, DOI 10.1109/ICDH55609.2022.00010)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–10: the abstract, §I–VIII, Algorithms 1–4, Tables I–II, the text layer of the Fig. 3 tree, and the references. Figs. 1–2 are captions only. First-page check: the title matches, and Bertsimas is the first of four authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Predrag Klasnja (University of Michigan, School of Information), Susan Murphy (Harvard, Department of Statistics), Liangyuan Na (MIT ORC) (p. 1). The HeartSteps trial papers it cites are by Klasnja, Murphy and coauthors (refs. 7, 8, p. 10). · **Position in trajectory**: a DA paper. It applies Optimal Policy Trees (ref. [15], Amram, Dunn and Zhuo, arXiv 2012.02279) to micro-randomized trial data, and imputes with OptImpute (ref. [40], S030, c18) (pp. 3, 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {DA + ML + AP (health)}:
  - In HeartSteps V2/V3, a user who has been sedentary for 40 minutes may receive an anti-sedentary message. The current algorithm samples eligible times uniformly, aiming at 1.5 messages a day, and makes no attempt to maximize the treatment effect (abstract; p. 1).
  - Goal: a policy that shortens the time until the user is non-sedentary while sending fewer messages. Behavioural scientists must be able to read it and draw theory from it (pp. 1–2).
  - Constraints of the setting (p. 1):
    - 51 users;
    - noisy data;
    - too many messages risk disengagement.
- **D2 Core idea**: A batch off-policy pipeline in three steps:
  1. Doubly robust counterfactual outcomes, from XGBoost outcome models and the trial's known randomization probabilities (pp. 4–5).
  2. A threshold rule (Algorithms 1–2, pp. 5–6). The nth message of the day is sent when the estimated effect falls below a threshold. The threshold is learned per returning user, or pooled for new users, by a grid search over 25 values per message (−120 to 0 in steps of 5).
  3. OPT+ (Algorithm 3, p. 6). An Optimal Policy Tree is trained on outcomes penalized by λ per message, with λ fixed or λ_i = αΓ_i per user. A message is sent only when the tree and the threshold rule agree and the message is feasible. Parameters are selected on validation days by user-daily metrics (Algorithm 4, pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Model: a contextual bandit per user. A message's effect is assumed to last at most two hours, and longer-term effects (disengagement) are handled by a daily budget N ∈ {1, 2} (pp. 1, 3, 5).
  - Data (pp. 3–4):
    - 94 V2/V3 users, of whom 63 had all three data sources;
    - 51 remained (25 V2, 26 V3) after dropping users with no available time steps or less than a week of data;
    - up to 80 days per user and 103 features.
  - Outcome: minutes until the user is non-sedentary, capped at 120 (p. 4).
  - The feasibility indicator is printed as min{I_it, r_it, N − n_it}, where r_it = 1 if a message was sent in the past two hours (pp. 3–4). Read literally, it allows a message only if one was just sent, which contradicts the two-hour rule; 1 − r_it seems meant [my reading].
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: The policy itself has no optimization model and no theory. The paper's devices concern evaluation:
  - Separate outcome models, trained on the test set only, serve as ground truth for every policy, including the current policy's historical actions. The evaluator therefore shares no model with the learner (p. 5).
  - Points with no message now but a message within two hours are dropped from outcome training (p. 5).
  - The budget enters tree training as a per-message penalty, and is enforced as a hard constraint only at execution (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Test sets: the later days of returning users, and all days of new users (Table I, p. 4).
  - Per average user and day (p. 7):

    | Policy | Sedentary minutes | Messages |
    |---|---|---|
    | Current policy | 148.02 | 1.62 |
    | Fixed-threshold rule, budget 1 | 103.98 | 0.79 |
    | OPT+ with a fixed λ | 108.56 | 0.76 |

  - Personalized thresholds give no benefit over fixed ones (p. 7).
  - Stability: 99 more trees were trained with slightly different parameters. The top seven of the final tree's 15 split variables appear in at least half of the 100 trees (Table II, p. 8).
  - Runs used Julia on a MacBook Pro, and no run times are given (p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Claimed contributions (p. 2):
    - two batch off-policy methods;
    - an end-to-end mHealth pipeline;
    - an interpretable policy that names known and new moderators.

    Heart rate in sitting users is read as stress, and offered as a hypothesis to test with intraday stress measures (p. 8).
  - Stated limits (p. 9):
    - 51 of 94 participants, a possible source of bias;
    - a deterministic binary policy, which hinders later off-policy analysis;
    - a batch proof of concept.

    An online version with warm-started retraining is proposed (p. 9).
  - Not stated [my reading]: every number, including the current policy's, is a model estimate. Nothing was field-tested.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 5, 7, 9): the owner's current algorithm is the baseline, and the scores are decision metrics (sedentary minutes, messages per day). The policy is built for the trial's scientists to read. The paper calls itself a proof of concept to be tested online (p. 9).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 6–7): the message budget is the knob (N, λ, α), and Fig. 1 plots sedentary time against message count. But only six discrete variants are shown, and the price of interpretability is stated as OPT+ being "comparable" to the threshold rule, though OPT+ is 4.6 minutes worse [my computation].
- Also Heuristic 4: a negative result is printed in the body, namely that personalization brings no benefit (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Score every policy with outcome models fitted only on the test fold. That includes the incumbent's logged actions. The evaluator then shares nothing with the learner (p. 5).
- Enter a resource budget into tree training as a per-action penalty λ, or a user-scaled λ_i = αΓ_i, and enforce the hard budget only at execution (p. 6).
- Act only when an interpretable tree and a personalized threshold rule agree. This makes the policy more conservative and keeps it readable (p. 6).
- Audit an interpretable policy's stability: retrain about 100 trees with perturbed parameters and report how often each split variable recurs (Table II, p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "HeartSteps uses an algorithm to uniformly spread out the daily message budget over time, but does not attempt to maximize treatment effects." (p. 1)
- "We observe that personalization does not bring a benefit over fixed thresholds." (p. 7)

### S364 · Improving quality in cardiothoracic surgery: exploiting the untapped potential of machine learning (The Annals of Thoracic Surgery 2022, DOI 10.1016/j.athoracsur.2022.06.058)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–6: the whole editorial, Figure 1's caption, the disclosures and the references. First-page check: the title matches, and Bertsimas is the last of eight authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Agni Orfanoudaki (Saïd Business School, Oxford; corresponding), Joseph A. Dearani (Mayo Clinic), David M. Shahian (MGH), Vinay Badhwar (West Virginia University), Felix Fernandez (Emory), Robert Habib (The Society of Thoracic Surgeons), Michael E. Bowdish (Cedars-Sinai) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: an editorial that announces a programme with The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS). STS funds the work, and Bertsimas and Orfanoudaki collaborate with it on automated data extraction (p. 5). It cites the group's mitral-valve paper (S296 in the index), its coronary-artery paper (S174, s12) and POTTER (S041 in the index) as early evidence (refs. 13–15, p. 6).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP + ML; DA as the aim}:
  - The STS National Database covers about 97% of US adult cardiac programmes, with 96–97% accuracy in audits (p. 1). Its Adult Cardiac Surgery Database holds nearly 8 million records (p. 3).
  - Tension: better ML risk models need more variables of higher dimension. Participants will not accept a heavier manual collection burden, and STS has already cut the required variables (pp. 1, 3).
  - The question, in one sentence: can the burden fall while the data grow (p. 3)?
- **D2 Core idea**: Use NLP to extract registry fields automatically from structured and unstructured EHR data, with data managers reviewing each hospital file (pp. 3, 5). The registry could then add variables at little or no extra cost, and feed nonlinear risk models, multimodal data and the search for new risk factors (pp. 3–5; Figure 1, p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**: There is no model. The data are the STS registry and hospital EHRs. The editorial grants that EHRs differ across organizations, have missing or inaccurate elements, and keep much in free text (pp. 2–3).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis**: n/a.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**: None. The editorial reports two findings from the literature:
  - ML has improved cardiothoracic risk prediction only marginally, plausibly because the data elements are few and of low dimension (p. 3);
  - early ensemble and tree-based studies improved risk estimation (p. 4, refs. 10–14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution: an agenda for STS (pp. 3–5):
    - automated extraction;
    - nonlinear models, including committee classifiers;
    - interpretable or SHAP-explained models;
    - multimodal data.
  - Perils named (p. 5):
    - few studies show gains in accuracy, practice or cost;
    - opacity;
    - EHR errors propagating into the models;
    - bias;
    - the need for external validation across several centres.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 5): the programme is carried by a professional society, its registry and four academic medical centres, with STS funding, rather than by a course or a company. The editorial names the paradigm it hopes to set for other national registries (p. 3).
- Method 5 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 3–4): the owner (STS) and its current rule (manual curation of a reduced variable set) are named. But the targets are prediction metrics (discrimination and calibration, p. 4), not decisions.
- Also Heuristic 6 (build the data asset when a field lacks the data to decide): this is the editorial's central proposal (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the cost of collecting data blocks richer models, automate the extraction and keep a human review of each file. Variables can then be added at near-zero marginal cost (pp. 3, 5).
- In the piece that launches a programme, list its perils too: error propagation from the source data, bias, and external validation across centres (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "Is there a way to minimize the data collection burden while at the same time collecting even more extensive data?" (p. 3)
- "Despite numerous publications that highlight the potential opportunities and benefits derived from the deployment of ML in cardiac surgery, few studies have demonstrated substantial improvements in risk model predictive accuracy, clinical practice, or cost." (p. 5)

### S365 · Stable classification (Journal of Machine Learning Research 2022, no DOI; JMLR 23(296), paper 20-667)
Read level: **full** (main text):
- pp. 1–21: abstract, §1–7, references;
- Appendix A, selected tables: Tables 12–13 (SVM accuracy and output stability, pp. 22–23), Table 16 (LR accuracy, p. 26), Table 20 (RF accuracy, p. 30) and Table 24 (OCT accuracy, p. 34).

The remaining appendix tables (pp. 24–25, 27–29, 31–33, 35–53) were not read. First-page check: the title matches, and the authors are Bertsimas, Dunn and Paskov (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jack Dunn (Interpretable AI), Ivan Paskov (MIT ORC) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: it extends Bertsimas–Paskov's stable regression (JMLR 2020; S312 in the index) from linear regression to classification. It places itself in the "optimization versus randomization" theme of chapters 15–18 of the 2019 book (pp. 2–3). Submitted 6/2020, revised 10/2022, published 11/2022 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + RO; LO and CP as engines}:
  - Problem: instability, meaning that small changes in the training data change the model and its predictions. It arises from the training/validation split and from new data over time (pp. 1–2).
  - Entry: Breiman asked whether a more stable single-tree version of CART exists, and the paper says it answers in the affirmative (p. 2). RF and LR are also unstable in their own ways (p. 2). No prior work stabilizes RF or SVM, the paper says, because both are believed to be stable already (pp. 3–4).
  - A synthetic sepsis-alert example shows two LR approaches. Their fold accuracies have the same mean and spread, but their coefficient volatility differs widely (Table 1, p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Treat the choice of training set as the uncertainty. Minimize, over models, the worst-case loss over all subsets of size k (Problem 2, p. 8).
  - Three ways to solve it (pp. 9–12):
    - a robust counterpart, by LP duality of the inner maximization (SVM and LR only);
    - a cutting-plane method that adds the most violated subset;
    - a Monte Carlo version that solves once over ζ sampled subsets, after Calafiore and Campi's scenario approach.
  - Stability is measured three ways over s = 100 bootstrap fits (pp. 6–7):
    - output: the variance of predicted probabilities;
    - structural: the spread of coefficients or variable importances;
    - hyperparameter: the spread of the tuned values.
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Losses: hinge for SVM, logistic for LR, and 1 minus the predicted probability of the true label for OCT and RF (Table 2, p. 8).
  - The worst case is chosen over an average case mainly for computational efficiency (p. 9).
  - Not stated [my reading]: the k used in the experiments, and how the min–max is trained for RF and OCT, beyond Table 2.
  - Data: 30 UCI data sets (p. 12).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - The inner maximum over {Σ z_i = k, z ∈ {0, 1}^n} equals the maximum over its convex hull. Its dual turns the problem into min kθ + Σ u_i subject to θ + u_i ≥ f(m, x_i, y_i) and u ≥ 0 (Problems 4–5, pp. 9–10). The stable SVM is therefore an LP, and the stable LR a convex program (Problems 6–7, p. 10).
  - [my reading] The objective is the sum of the k largest training losses, which the paper does not name.
  - The convergence of the cutting planes is credited to Fletcher–Leyffer (p. 11). The Monte Carlo version's probabilistic feasibility is credited to the scenario literature, not proved here (p. 12).
  - Table 2 lists OCT as "Solved to Optimality" (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Protocol (pp. 12–13):
    - a 90/10 split and 100 bootstrap fits;
    - tuning by a 70/30 split or by 5-fold cross-validation;
    - ℓ2-regularized SVM and LR, an RF of 100 trees, OCT, and ζ = 20.
  - Results, averaged over the 30 data sets (Tables 3, 5, 7, 9, pp. 14–16):
    - SVM: accuracy 0.773 → 0.807 with the robust counterpart.
    - LR: stability improves, accuracy does not (0.672 → 0.668).
    - RF: changes are small. Accuracy goes 0.847 → 0.849 (Monte Carlo) and output stability 0.016 → 0.014 (cutting planes), but structural stability worsens, 0.004 → 0.012 (cutting planes).
    - OCT: the original is best on accuracy and hyperparameter stability, and the Monte Carlo version on output stability (0.029 → 0.026).
  - Run times relative to the original (Table 11, p. 17): the robust counterpart takes 0.31 (SVM) and 0.49 (LR); the cutting planes take up to 8.9 (RF). Hardware and software: Core i9-9900K, Julia 1.3.1, Ipopt 3.13.2 and Gurobi 9.0.0 (p. 16).
  - Missing [my reading]: significance tests, and CART or DRO baselines.
  - Per-data-set reading of Table 12 (p. 22) [my computation]:
    - the robust counterpart beats the original SVM on 6 data sets, loses on 7 and ties on 17;
    - the five largest gains come on data sets where the original scored 0.500–0.629 (banknote-authentication 0.556 → 0.957, acute-inflammations-2, spect-heart, spectf-heart, connectionist-bench-sonar);
    - those five gains sum to more than the net gain over all 30.
  - On six data sets, all four SVM variants have output-stability values of order 10^-12 or smaller (Table 13, p. 23). This suggests nearly constant predictions [my reading].
  - Table 16's caption repeats the SVM averages (0.773, 0.761, 0.795, 0.807). Its LR column means are 0.672, 0.666, 0.657 and 0.668, as in Table 5 (p. 26) [my computation].
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Claimed (pp. 4, 18–19):
    - a robust-optimization framework that stabilizes any classifier;
    - three algorithms;
    - better stability, and sometimes accuracy, at comparable or lower run times.
  - The conclusion suggests that current training sits at an interior point of the performance/stability Pareto curve (p. 19).
  - Limits and future work are not discussed [my reading].

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 9–10): the worst-case set is chosen because it keeps the class. The stable SVM is an LP, the stable LR stays convex, and the paper prints that the counterpart exists only for these two (p. 10).
- Method 3 · variant ⚠ (pp. 8–9, 19): stability is the property and k the knob, but k is never varied and no price curve is drawn. The trade-off claim rests on averages over 30 data sets.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 3, 8, 13–17): the duel is only against each method's own nominal version [my reading]. There is no comparison with CART, which Breiman's question is about, or with the DRO methods the paper says it beats in efficiency (p. 3). OCT is listed as "Solved to Optimality" without a reported certificate (Tension T1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To be robust to the training split, minimize the worst case over all k-subsets. The dual of the inner LP gives kθ + Σ u_i with θ + u_i ≥ loss_i, which keeps a linear or convex loss in its class (pp. 9–10).
- Where no counterpart exists (trees, forests), solve once over ζ ≈ 20 sampled subsets. Plot the metrics against ζ to show that they settle (pp. 12–13, 18).
- Report stability three ways over 100 bootstrap fits on one fixed test set: the variance of predicted probabilities, the spread of coefficients or variable importances, and the spread of tuned hyperparameters (pp. 6–7).

**Quotes**:
- "Our choice to minimize over the worst-case training error rather than an average-case is primarily motivated by computational efficiency" (p. 9)
- "perhaps the current approaches to training algorithms have been operating at an interior point with respect to the performance/stability Pareto curve." (p. 19)

### S459 · Sparse regression over clusters: SparClur (Optimization Letters 2022, DOI 10.1007/s11590-021-01770-9)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–16 of the Springer-template manuscript of December 28, 2020 (p. 1). Figures 3.1–3.5 keep only their axis labels. First-page check: the title matches, and the authors are Bertsimas, Dunn, Kapelevich and Zhang (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Jack Dunn, Lea Kapelevich and Rebecca Zhang (all MIT ORC) (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: it puts the Bertsimas–Van Parys exact sparse regression (ref. [4], S061, c21) into the leaves of Optimal Regression Trees (Dunn's thesis, ref. [10]) (pp. 2–3). The glucose data follow the diabetes study (ref. [6], S079, c17) (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML + IO; AP (health) as the testbed}:
  - The linear models in ORT leaves are fitted heuristically, by Lasso, which selects many features outside the true support (p. 3).
  - Entry: when leaves are fitted separately, a variable such as past HbA1c may matter in some leaves and not in others, which the paper calls medically implausible (p. 2).
  - Problem (1.1): ridge-regularized least squares in each of J clusters, with ||w_j||_0 ≤ q and one support common to all clusters (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - One binary support vector s is shared by all leaves (p. 4).
  - The problem becomes a minimization over s of a sum of J kernel terms, convex in s (Lemma 1, p. 4).
  - It is solved by outer approximation, with cuts from closed-form duals and subgradients added by lazy callbacks (Algorithm 1, pp. 5–6).
  - A convex relaxation supplies the warm starts. It swaps the min and the max, and its inner problem is solved by taking the top q indices (p. 5).
  - For J = 1 the method reduces to Bertsimas–Van Parys (p. 3).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Synthetic data (pp. 6–7):
    - Gaussian X with n = 60–800 and p = 2,000;
    - J ∈ {1, 2, 5, 10, 20};
    - 10 support features with coefficients ±1, and SNR 20;
    - five data sets per point.

    A variant gives the clusters partly different supports (pp. 7–9).
  - Real data (p. 11):

    | Data set | Observations × features |
    |---|---|
    | Glucose, Boston Medical Center patients | 10,806 patients, 85 features |
    | Framingham, time to stroke | 1,266 × 40 |
    | Framingham, blood-pressure change | 91,955 × 41 |
    | King County house prices | 21,613 × 18 |

  - The partition comes from a cross-validated ORT with point predictions (p. 12).
  - γ was picked by the "highest MSE" on held-out data (p. 7); the lowest seems meant [my reading].
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**:
  - Lemma 1 is proved "by the same argument" as Lemma 1 of [4] (p. 4).
  - The dual variables α_j = (I + γK_j)^{-1} y_j give the cut (eqs. 2.5–2.6, p. 5), and the capacitance matrix avoids the n × n inverse (p. 5).
  - There is no new theory. Correctness and phase transitions are shown empirically.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Setup: Julia/JuMP, CPLEX 12.10, AMD Ryzen 9 3950X (p. 6).
  - Phase transition: with five leaves, about 300 observations suffice, against 5 × 120 = 600 for separately fitted leaves (p. 7).
  - Scale: n up to 100,000 and p up to 50,000 (J = 10) take 1.7–19.9 s, with full recovery and no false positives (Table 3.1, p. 9).
  - Real data (pp. 12–14): a two-minute CPLEX limit, with the incumbent used when the limit is hit (p. 12); five folds; depths 0–5.
    - Lasso has the strictly highest out-of-sample R² in 21 of 24 depth rows and ties in one more. Exact SparClur trails it by at most 0.016 (Tables 4.1–4.4, pp. 12–13) [my computation].
    - SparClur uses 8.8 features per glucose leaf, all shared across leaves; Lasso uses 21.2 per leaf (Table 4.5, p. 14).
  - Baseline handicap: the uncoordinated sparse baseline gets the convex relaxation, because the exact method would take over a week (p. 12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Claimed (p. 15):
    - the method is correct and scalable;
    - it beats uncoordinated sparse regression when observations are few;
    - interpretability improves substantially at a very small cost in accuracy.
  - The code is released (GitHub, p. 4; Zenodo, ref. 14, p. 16). The medical data cannot be shared, so a public house-price data set is added (pp. 4, 15).
  - The bad case is printed: when the clusters' supports differ, a small q costs accuracy (pp. 9–10).
  - Stroke prediction gained nothing from depth (p. 15). No future work is given.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–6): a medical requirement for interpretability (the same variables in every leaf) becomes one binary support vector in an MIO that is convex in s. It is solved by outer approximation, reusing the J = 1 device of [4].
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 9–10, 12–15): interpretability is priced. The knob is q, kept to at most 10 because clinicians can read that many and tuned by cross-validation (p. 12). The price is the R² gap to Lasso and to separately fitted sparse leaves, and the bad case of differing supports is printed (pp. 9–10).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 5, 12, 14): the exact solve starts from the convex relaxation and runs under a stated time limit. But no gap is reported, and the uncoordinated rival gets the relaxation instead of the exact solver, so the budgets differ.
- Method 2 · evidence ✅ (p. 3): the introduction repeats [4]'s point that phase transitions put in question the belief that exact algorithms cannot match heuristics at scale. No speed-up figure is rebuilt.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When the models of several segments must use the same variables, impose one binary support vector across all segments. In the kernel form the objective stays convex in s, so outer approximation applies (pp. 2–5).
- Get warm starts from the min–max convex relaxation, whose inner maximization is solved by picking the q largest scores (p. 5).
- Report interpretability beside accuracy (Table 4.5, p. 14): features per leaf (minimum, maximum, mean), and how many features recur in more than half of the leaves.
- Use the phase transition as a design rule: compute how many observations each segment needs before the exact method becomes fast (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "the price to be paid for imposing additional structure that favors this interpretability is small." (p. 14)
- "This is because training and cross-validating for appropriate parameters using uncoordinated sparse regression, which involves building linear models within each leaf separately, would take over a week of computational time for our deepest trees, which we consider impractical." (p. 12)
