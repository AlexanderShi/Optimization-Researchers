### S230 · Balancing efficiency and fairness in liver transplant access: tradeoff curves for the assessment of organ distribution policies (Transplantation 2020, DOI 10.1097/tp.0000000000003017)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–7 (abstract, introduction, Materials and Methods, Results with Tables 1–2 and Figures 1–3, Discussion, references). The Supplemental Digital Content (links.lww.com/TP/B831), which holds the policy definitions, the optimization behind the optimized districts and the parameter grids (pp. 2–3), is not in the file and was not read. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Papalexopoulos, Trichakis, Wang, Hirose and Vagefi match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Theodore Papalexopoulos, Nikolaos Trichakis, Yuchen Wang (MIT ORC), Ryutaro Hirose (UCSF surgery), Parsia A. Vagefi (UT Southwestern surgery) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the fairness line with Trichakis (S026, c13; S044, c14) carried into liver allocation in a clinical journal. The contribution statement credits Bertsimas with problem definition, approach, writing and editing, and Papalexopoulos, Trichakis and Wang with implementation (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP+DA; IO only in the unread SDC}:
  - The 1998 Final Rule asked that a candidate's location not be a major determinant of access; two decades later the geographic disparity in liver access persisted (p. 1).
  - A July 2018 lawsuit and the 2017 court-ordered lung policy change led DHHS to ask for a liver proposal without DSAs or regions (pp. 1–2).
  - OPTN's three frameworks (fixed distance, optimized boundaries, continuous distribution) had no consensus. The entry: compare them all on efficiency–fairness tradeoff curves (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Parameterize each concept: acuity circles (radii and MELD bands), optimized districts (number of regions and DSA groupings "generated through optimization"), and a continuous score MELD − λ·distance (p. 2).
  - Sweep the parameters, simulate each policy in SRTR's LSAM, and plot deaths and the SD of median MELD at transplant (σMMaT) against average transport distance (pp. 3–4).
  - Compare each reference policy with the continuous-score (CS) policy whose λ gives the same transport distance (Table 1, p. 4).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - LSAM version 2014 on SRTR data 2007–2011; 20 runs for each of 2010 and 2011 (p. 3).
  - Metrics: deaths, transplants, share transplanted locally, MMaT and σMMaT across the 58 DSAs, group discrepancies, transport distance and time, share flown (p. 3).
  - Urgency by MELD, MELD-Na or OPOM, the group's ML mortality predictor (p. 3; ref. 10, p. 7).
  - Admitted limits: LSAM may overestimate deaths averted and cannot model changed listing or acceptance behaviour; only a linear distance term is tried (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: n/a in the article. The optimization that generates districts and policy parameters is in the SDC (pp. 2–3), not read. No theorem or bound.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Current 11-region policy: 2,510 deaths a year at 244.7 nm. Matched CS (λ = 0.022): 2,406, a difference of −104 (−140, −68), and σMMaT −29.8% (Table 1, p. 4).
  - Against AC 250/500, approved by OPTN in December 2018 (p. 3): −30 (−53, −7). Against AC 300/600: −24 (−58, 10) (Table 1, p. 4). The intervals are one SD (p. 4). [my reading] The last includes zero, but the text reports the reduction without qualification (p. 4).
  - OPOM instead of MELD saves about 500 lives a year across all concepts (p. 6); 418, and 556 with CS (p. 7).
  - Rounding distances to 10–50 nm leaves CS outcomes almost unchanged (pp. 4–5).
  - Marginal grafts: discards trend up with distance (Table 2, p. 6). [my reading] At matched distance CS discards more grafts from donors over 70 than the current policy (31.7% against 28.7%); the text attributes the trend to distance only.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contribution: tradeoff curves as a decision aid; CS gives the fewest deaths and the lowest σMMaT at every transport distance (pp. 4, 7).
  - Stated limits: the simulator and a single linear implementation; Snyder et al.'s nonlinear scores are left out (p. 7).
  - Future: continuous distribution for all solid organs; curves for other metrics (pp. 1, 7). The UNOS board endorsed continuous distribution for future policies in December 2018 (p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2–4, 7): fairness and efficiency priced on curves indexed by one interpretable knob (λ, score per nautical mile). The single policy is left to "the transplant community" (p. 7). Steps 3 and 5 are thin: no bound, and the weakest comparison is not flagged.
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 3–4, 7): the owner's current rule (11 regions) and its newly approved rule (AC 250/500) are the baselines. Fairness is scored on the owner's accepted metric, MMaT, accepted by the OPTN committee in March 2013 (p. 7). No field test; the simulator's limits are printed.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (pp. 1, 7): the Trichakis fairness programme reaches a clinical journal, with implementation by his collaborators and Bertsimas on problem and approach (p. 1). The concept returns with UNOS in 2025 (S476, c2-07).
- New-pattern candidate: *matched-cost comparison on a frontier* (Table 1, p. 4): the new family is tuned to the incumbent's cost before outcomes are compared.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Tune the new policy family's knob until a cost metric equals the incumbent's (here transport distance), then report outcome differences with intervals (Table 1, p. 4).
- Answer a stakeholder objection to a continuous score (two hospitals across town) by coarsening its input and showing outcomes do not move (pp. 4–5, 7).
- Publish the whole frontier as an interactive site (livervis.github.io) so others can examine metric pairs the paper does not plot (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Continuous scoring policies dominated all others considered and allowed for the greatest reduction of deaths across all transport distances." (p. 4)
- "Limitations of this study include that estimating number of deaths averted using LSAM may represent an overestimation, and that LSAM cannot account for changes in practitioner listing or acceptance behavior" (p. 7)

### S262 · Bus routing optimization helps Boston public schools design better policies (INFORMS Journal on Applied Analytics 2020, DOI 10.1287/inte.2019.1015)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–14 (cover, abstract, all sections, figure notes, references, author biographies). First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas, Delarue, Eger, Hanlon and Martin match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Arthur Delarue and Sébastien Martin (MIT ORC PhD students), William Eger and John Hanlon (Boston Public Schools; Hanlon its chief operating officer) (pp. 2, 14) · **Position in trajectory**: the practice write-up of the PNAS methods paper (S137, c2-04), a 2019 Edelman finalist (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP+IO; HS}:
  - BPS spent $120 million on transport in 2017 (about 10% of its budget), growing 5–7% a year against revenue growth of 2–4%; each bus costs about $100,000 a year (p. 4).
  - Ten officers drafted routes by hand in about 3,000 people-hours, so no policy could be costed (p. 4). A 2016 change to one school's start time cost over $1 million unexpectedly (p. 5).
  - Vendors had no ready tool, so BPS ran a two-part, 60-day crowdsourced challenge; the MIT team won (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - BiRD (biobjective routing decomposition): stop assignment by IO; insertion-generated trips joined by a set-cover IO; several scenarios per school on the Pareto frontier of buses against ride time; one scenario per school chosen by a network-flow IO; bus scheduling by IO for morning and afternoon (pp. 6–7).
  - Start-time selection as a generalized quadratic assignment problem (GQAP) with pairwise routing-affinity costs built from BiRD scenarios. It is solved by local improvement: re-solve a random subset of schools by MIO with the rest fixed, and repeat (p. 9).
  - Policy goals (high-school starts after 8:00, elementary dismissals before 4:00, special education, savings) enter as weighted GQAP-representable terms (p. 11).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - Challenge data: approximate home addresses, school locations, vetted stops (p. 5); 25,000 riders, 5,000 of them door-to-door; a heterogeneous fleet of over 700 buses (p. 4).
  - Google Maps car travel times, admitted to misroute large buses and to differ from bus times (p. 8).
  - A community survey of preferred times (pp. 10–11), later judged unrepresentative and framed without the current times (p. 12).
  - Tractability choices admitted: GQAP formulations were intractable even on small instances (p. 9); compatibility-cost parameters set by cross-validation (p. 9).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: decomposition into IO subproblems bridged by joint scenario selection (Fig. 3, p. 7); the start-time problem as a GQAP (p. 9). Near-optimality of one-school subsets with restarts is cited from synthetic tests in the PNAS paper (p. 9). No proofs.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Challenge data: up to 20% fewer buses (p. 7).
  - Deployment in 2017: 75 fewer buses (12%) in the algorithmic plan and 50 (7%) after BPS added buses back for caution; an estimated $5 million and 20,000 pounds of carbon emissions a day (p. 8).
  - First-week on-time performance was significantly worse than the year before; by November it surpassed the prior year's (p. 8).
  - Start times: 450 buses against 600 when optimizing transport alone (pp. 9–10); a satisfaction–cost curve (Fig. 5a, pp. 10–11). The chosen plan cut high-school starts before 8:00 from 74% to 6% and elementary dismissals after 4:00 from 33% to 15%, with $12 million reinvested (p. 11).
  - It changed 85% of schools' times (median one hour) and was not implemented (pp. 2, 12). No gaps or solve times are reported.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: an algorithm used for two years, a way to cost policies, a start-time model (p. 2).
  - The paper diagnoses its own non-adoption: the survey did not show options against current times, start times were not mapped to end times, loss aversion, unrepresentative engagement, and after-school plans that were general rather than school-specific (p. 12).
  - Afterwards: a school committee policy with quantifiable objectives, a Boston Globe interactive, testimony in Rhode Island (p. 12); Dynamic Ideas software in talks with almost 30 districts and partnering with 3 (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 · evidence ✅ (pp. 2, 4, 7–8, 12): owner, decision, data and current rule (manual routing, three bell tiers) come first. The deployment report includes the poor first week, and the plan not adopted is called not implemented, as the Workflow D checkpoint asks. This is the group's own account of the episode behind Method 5's correction [05 §5.1]: the district saw the curve (pp. 10–11), but school-level changes surfaced only after the plan was proposed (p. 12).
- Method 3 · evidence ✅ (pp. 10–11): community satisfaction priced in buses and dollars on a trade-off curve; policy weights are the knob (p. 11).
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (p. 9): the exact GQAP stalls, so local improvement with random restarts is used (Heuristic 3). No gap or certificate is given for the deployed solutions.
- Method 6 · evidence ✅ (p. 13): a company, Dynamic Ideas, "started by Dimitris Bertsimas", carries the method to other districts; one applied write-up per deployment.
- New-pattern candidate: *publish the adoption post-mortem* (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Generate several Pareto scenarios per component (school) instead of one, then pick one per component jointly by IO; this joins single- and multi-component subproblems (pp. 6–7).
- When an exact quadratic assignment model is intractable, re-solve a random subset of assignments by MIO with the rest fixed, from several random starts (p. 9).
- Report both the algorithmic and the deployed savings, and plan for the operator to add capacity back when inputs may be optimistic (p. 8).
- In a stakeholder survey, show each option against the current state and map coupled consequences (start time to end time); the paper names their absence as a cause of failure (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "Each additional point of community satisfaction in Boston costs dozens of additional buses and tens of millions of taxpayer dollars." (p. 10)
- "Ultimately, the district decided that such an important decision should not be implemented without deeper community engagement, and the new bell times did not become a reality." (p. 12)

### S312 · Stable regression: On the power of optimization over randomization (Journal of Machine Learning Research 21(230) 2020, no DOI)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–22 (abstract, §§1–9, Tables 1–8, figure captions); the appendix error-bar Tables 9–15 (pp. 22–24) skimmed. First-page check: the title (in full "Stable Regression: On the Power of Optimization over Randomization in Training Regression Problems") and the authors Bertsimas and Paskov match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ivan Paskov (MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the theme of the 2015 experimental-design paper (S124, c15), cited as its origin (p. 2), applied to training splits; optimal classification trees interpret the result (p. 17). Submitted May 2019, published November 2020 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {ML+RO; LO and convex QP as engines; IO for support recovery}:
  - The random training/validation split is standard practice; coefficients, supports and hyperparameters can change with the split, most when data are scarce (pp. 1–2).
  - The authors know of "no other work" that chooses splits by other means; Garbade (1977) only tests stability (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - Replace the random split by min over β of max over binary z with Σz = k of Σ z_i|y_i − x_iᵀβ| plus a penalty: the model is trained on the hardest training set of size k (eq. 1, p. 5).
  - The inner problem is linear in z, so z relaxes to its convex hull; LP duality of the inner maximum gives an LP (Lasso or no penalty) or a convex QP (ridge, elastic net) (eqs. 2–3, pp. 5–6).
  - The model is thus robust to every subpopulation of size k; the hardest set is explained by an optimal classification tree (pp. 2, 16–17).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - ℓ1 loss throughout; ℓ2 reruns are said to be similar (p. 7).
  - 10 UCI data sets and two synthetic ones (n = 30,000, p = 1,000; n = 1,000, p = 30,000; 20% of coefficients nonzero, ±1) (p. 7; sizes in Table 1, p. 8).
  - 1,000 repetitions, each with a random 10% test set (p. 7).
  - Support recovery adds an L0 constraint with big-M binaries (eqs. 4–5, pp. 13–14).
  - Interpretation: a diabetes data set with over 100,000 observations and a BIDMC emergency-department data set (p. 17).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: convex-hull relaxation and LP duality of the inner maximum (pp. 5–6). [my reading] The dual is the usual sum-of-the-k-largest-residuals form. No statistical theorem: the case is empirical. The discussion explains the shrinking gain by the number of possible splits, largest at k = n/2 (p. 21).
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - Average MSE gains over random splits: 15.12% at 50/50 down to 1.00% at 90/10 without penalty; 10.82% down to 0.31% with penalty; 0.75% and 0.43% against 5- and 10-fold CV (Tables 1–2, pp. 8–9). The SDs of prediction error, coefficients and hyperparameter follow the same pattern (Tables 3–7, pp. 10–13).
  - Support recovery: higher accuracy and lower false-alarm rate at every n with s known; with s estimated by CV, randomization leads slightly at small n (Figs. 1–2, pp. 15–16).
  - Optimal trees on the hardest set: 90% accuracy (diabetes) and 71% (emergency department) (pp. 17–18).
  - Run time 2–3 times a random split's at 50/50–70/30, about 1.02–1.03 times at 80/20 and 90/10 (Table 8, pp. 20–21).
  - [my reading] "In all cases" refers to averages; some cells go the other way, e.g. Computer Hardware's prediction-error SD at 70/30–90/10 (Table 3, p. 10) and its hyperparameter SD at every split (Table 7, p. 13). The text does not say which optimization column the CV columns are compared with (Table 2, p. 9).
  - No solver, version or machine is named ("commercial optimization software", p. 6); the text calls the timing table Table 7 (p. 20).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: lower error and variability, better support recovery, subpopulation robustness with interpretable hardest sets, an efficient algorithm (pp. 3, 22).
  - Stated limits: a smaller edge against CV (pp. 1, 21); the hardest-set examples were chosen where experts could check them (p. 19).
  - Future: apply the method where no prior intuition exists (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 · evidence ✅ (pp. 5–6): a step owned by statistics, the random split, is written as a robust problem, and LP duality keeps it in the LP or QP class of the nominal regression.
- Method 4 · variant ⚠ (pp. 7–16): the incumbent (random splits; 5- and 10-fold CV, added as "a fairer comparison", p. 7) is dueled on real data and on synthetic data with known truth. Certificates are moot for LP and QP, but the MIO support-recovery runs report no limit or gap, and no solver or machine is named.
- Method 2 · variant ⚠ (pp. 1, 13–14): the abstract opens with the field's belief (Heuristic 9), but MIO is called "nowadays highly solvable" with no figure.
- New-pattern candidate: *optimization over randomization* (pp. 2, 22): with S357 in this batch and S124 (c15). SKILL already lists it as Taste mark 3, not as a Method.
- New-pattern candidate: *explain the adversary* (pp. 16–19): fit an interpretable model to the worst-case indicator to learn which subpopulation is hardest.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a procedure picks a subset at random (a split, a sample), write the choice as an inner maximum over z with Σz = k, relax z to its hull and dualize; the problem stays in the nominal class (pp. 5–6).
- Fit an optimal classification tree with the worst-case indicator as the label to describe the hardest subpopulation, and check the tree with domain experts (pp. 17–19).
- Report stability (SDs over repeated test splits of predictions, coefficients and hyperparameters), not only mean error (Tables 3–7, pp. 9–13).

**Quotes**:
- "We investigate and ultimately suggest remediation to the widely held belief that the best way to train regression models is via random assignment of our data to training and validation sets." (p. 1)
- "When compared to cross-validation, the optimization approach still has an advantage, but it is markedly smaller." (p. 21)

### S344 · Relative robust and adaptive optimization (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2020, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2018.0860)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–20 (cover, abstract, §§1–6, figure captions, the start of Appendix A); the rest of Appendix A (pp. 20–21) skimmed; references not read. First-page check: the title and the authors Bertsimas and Dunning match (pp. 1–2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Iain Dunning (MIT ORC) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the adaptive-RO line (affine and finite adaptability, p. 3) reopened for relative objectives. Received April 2016, accepted August 2018 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO; LO and IO as engines}:
  - Early robust discrete optimization (Kouvelis and Yu 1997) used relative criteria; the past decade used the absolute worst case, held to be more tractable (abstract, pp. 2–3).
  - Absolute RO is conservative: one bad realization can dominate, so the solution may do poorly on most scenarios (p. 2).
  - Problem: competitive ratio (ΠCR) and regret (ΠREG) against the clairvoyant value Π(ξ), for robust LO problems including affine adaptability (eqs. 3–5, pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea**: split by the curvature of Π(ξ) (pp. 4–8).
  - Π concave (right-hand-side uncertainty, LP offline): substitute the offline LP into the inner problem, merge the two minimizations and dualize. Regret stays a robust LP (polyhedral set) or becomes an SOCP (ellipsoid), only slightly larger and in the same class (p. 5). CR uses an epigraph variable (p. 6). A MILO offline problem is handled by cutting planes with lazy constraints (p. 6).
  - Π convex (objective uncertainty): the inner problem is bilinear and nonconvex. For the budget set, Theorem 1 restricts deviations to vertices, γ ∈ {−1, 0, 1}; binaries and an exact linearization of binary×continuous products make the inner problem a MILO for a cutting-plane method (pp. 6–8).
  - Pareto blends: divide the absolute objective by ΠRO to make it dimensionless (fNR) and optimize λ·fCR + (1 − λ)·fNR. A tiny λ selects the best-CR solution among the RO optima, instead of Iancu and Trichakis's hand-picked scenario (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Model and data**:
  - The offline problem is feasible and bounded for every ξ (p. 4); Π(ξ) > 0 for CR (p. 6); finite upper bounds and a budget set in the convex case (p. 7); polyhedral or ellipsoidal sets (p. 4); linear decision rules for recourse (pp. 4, 13, 15).
  - Synthetic instances: layered and dense minimum-cost-flow graphs, a 10-period inventory problem, and two-stage facility and stock location with n = 5–25 (pp. 10–17).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis (brief)**: LP duality of the merged inner problem (pp. 5–6); Theorem 1, a biconvex maximum over polytopes is attained at vertex pairs, reproduced from the literature (p. 7); exactness of the linearization checked by enumerating the two binary cases (p. 8); cutting-plane upper and lower bounds (p. 6). [my reading] The conclusion says "we showed that, in general, this problem is intractable" (p. 19), but the text argues only nonconvexity (pp. 6–7); no hardness proof is given.
- **D5 Computational and empirical evaluation**:
  - JuMP and Gurobi 6.5.0 on an Intel 2.8 GHz Core i7-4960HQ; cuts until no violation exceeds 10⁻³ (p. 10).
  - Layered graph: CR better at the median (13.2 against 15.6) and in relative cost at nearly all percentiles; RO better only at the extreme tail (16.8 against 17.1 at the 99th) (p. 11). Dense graph: roughly the opposite; RO is better in absolute cost at the median and the 99th percentile (p. 11).
  - Time: RO 50–100 times the deterministic LP, CR 10–20 times RO; at n = 50 the medians are 3.6 s and 0.3 s (p. 11).
  - Inventory: λ from 0 to 1 traces a frontier (CR 0.43–0.69, absolute 85–123), each solve under 5 s; in simulation λ = 0.6 is called "an excellent trade-off" (pp. 13–14).
  - Facility location: the regret reformulation solves as fast as RO; cutting planes take 80 s against 2 s at n = 20 and were stopped beyond 100 s at n ≥ 25 (pp. 17–18). RO stocks 781 units against 990 (CR) and 1,045 (regret); it protects the worst 25% of scenarios and leaves profit elsewhere (pp. 17–19).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**:
  - Contributions: tractability for concave Π, a MILO cutting-plane method for the budget set, Pareto blends, three case studies (pp. 3, 19–20).
  - Stated limits: integer problems may offer few intermediate Pareto solutions (p. 9); problem-specific cuts could speed the method (p. 11).
  - Future: choose among Pareto solutions by simulation, back-testing and the decision-maker's risk preferences (p. 20).
