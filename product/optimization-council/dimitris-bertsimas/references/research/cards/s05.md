### S341 · Analysis of LP relaxations for multiway and multicut problems (Networks 34(2) 1999, DOI 10.1002/(sici)1097-0037(199909)34:2<102::aid-net3>3.0.co;2-x)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–13 (all text, including every proof and the 16/15 and 10/9 gap constructions); reference list p. 13 skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Chung-Piaw Teo (NUS), Rakesh Vohra (Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: IO approximation period. A preliminary version appeared at IPCO 1995, and the paper was received in 1996 (p. 1). The same rounding device recurs in two IPCO 1996 papers with Teo (p. 2, refs [3], [15] p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {IO+LO}: Multiway cut is NP-hard for k ≥ 3 (p. 1). Chopra–Owen's extended formulation was supported only by computational results and was never analysed for tightness (p. 2). Entry: bound theoretically how far these relaxations can lie from the integer optimum, using a new randomized rounding argument (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: The paper writes multiway cut as a continuous nonlinear program with linear constraints whose continuous relaxation is already exact (Theorem 1, p. 3). Its linearizations give EF1 (Chopra–Owen's formulation), EF2 and a new convex EF3 with absolute values (pp. 3–4), which are all equal under nonnegative costs (Theorems 2–3, pp. 4–5). Projecting them recovers the path and tree inequalities (Theorem 4, pp. 5–6). A map to maximum-weight stable set on an auxiliary graph I(G) gives a stronger relaxation, IND (Theorem 5, p. 8). IND is solvable in polynomial time (Theorem 6, p. 9) and is integral when I(G) is perfect, which covers trees and new polynomial cases (Theorems 7–10, pp. 9–10). Rounding EF3 gives E[Z_H] ≤ 2(1 − 1/k) Z_EF3 (Theorem 11, p. 11). A k = 4 relaxation attains 4/3 (Theorem 12, pp. 11–12), and a multicut relaxation M2 attains 2α(H)(1 − 1/|V(H)|) (Theorems 13–14, pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Model and data**: The input is a graph with edge weights, assumed nonnegative from p. 4 on so that EF1 = EF2 = EF3, and k terminals. Multicut adds a demand graph H with independence number α(H) (p. 12). No data are used.
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): (i) NLF is shown exact by randomization: node u is assigned to terminal j with probability ȳ_j(u) (p. 3). (ii) One uniform threshold U is shared by all nodes, so that P{(u,v) ∈ δ(S_j)} = |ȳ_j(u) − ȳ_j(v)|, the very term EF3 constrains (p. 11). The rounding is derandomized by conditioning or by threshold minimum cuts (p. 11). (iii) Lower bounds on the gap come from Cunningham's 16/15 example (p. 6) and from a generalized family that reaches 10/9 asymptotically (pp. 6–7). (iv) Perfect graphs and clique-gluing give the integrality results (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: none. Chopra–Owen's experiments are cited (p. 6).
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper reads its result as sharpening Dalhaus et al. (so spelled in the paper). The factor 2(1 − 1/k) is the same, but it is stated against the LP value rather than the integer optimum (p. 11). Contributions are listed section by section (p. 2), and a footnote concedes that one corollary may already be known (p. 8).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are at the level of formulations: one source for known relaxations, a stronger relaxation, new polynomial cases, and LP counterparts of combinatorial bounds. Two questions stay open: the gap between the upper bound 2 and the 10/9 example, and whether IND improves the bound (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (IO): one exact formulation generates the rival family's relaxations, and the paper prints where its analysis stops: 2 against 10/9 (pp. 2–7, 13). The class kept is an LP relaxation, not a robust or learned model.
- new-pattern candidate: probabilistic reading of a relaxation solution. LP values are read as assignment probabilities, both to prove exactness (p. 3) and to round (pp. 10–11).
- new-pattern candidate: relaxation-centred IO. A combinatorial approximation algorithm becomes an LP relaxation with the same worst-case bound (pp. 11–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To bound the integrality gap of a relaxation, design a rounding whose per-edge cut probability equals the term the relaxation constrains (here |ȳ_j(u) − ȳ_j(v)| ≤ 2x(u,v)); the factor then follows line by line (pp. 4, 11).
- Before strengthening a family of formulations, write one exact nonlinear model with linear constraints. Each valid linearization of its products is then a relaxation, and comparing formulations becomes algebra (pp. 3–5).
- Pair the upper bound on a gap with an explicit instance family that forces a lower bound, and state the remaining interval as open (pp. 6–7, 13).

**Quotes**:
- "We also introduce a new randomized rounding argument to study the sharpness of these formulations." (p. 1)
- "However, our result is a little stronger in that the bound on the heuristic solution is in terms of the linear programming relaxation and not the optimal integer solution." (p. 11)

### S368 · Bounds and policies for dynamic routing in loss networks (Operations Research 47(3) 1999, DOI 10.1287/opre.47.3.379)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–16 (all text, Theorems 1–5, Tables I–VIII, insights, conclusions); reference list skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Thalia Chryssikou (MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: achievable-region programme (SW1). The paper extends Bertsimas–Paschalidis–Tsitsiklis 1994 from multiclass queueing networks to loss networks (p. 2) and was received in April 1996 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+LO}: The problem is admission control and dynamic routing in multiclass loss networks. Even the blocking probabilities of one fixed policy are #P-complete to compute (p. 1). The existing bounds are Gibbens–Kelly's max-flow LP and Kelly 1994 (p. 2), while practice runs variants of DAR with trunk reservation (pp. 1–2). Entry: derive the bounds and the policies from the same relaxations (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: The variables are steady-state probabilities of partial state descriptions: x = P{state, accept} and y = P{state}. The probability-flow balance equations of aggregated Markov chains give LPs whose feasible regions contain the achievable region (pp. 2–3, 5). The relaxations form a hierarchy:
  - first order: per-link totals, O(NRC) variables (Theorem 3, pp. 4–5);
  - second order: direct against aggregate alternative flow, 3NC² + NR variables (Theorem 4, p. 6);
  - third order: specific alternative routes, 2NRC² variables (Theorem 5, pp. 7–8).

  Enlarging the aggregated state further would recover the exact MDP (p. 8). Two policies are read off the third-order LP. The dynamic policy accepts a call with probability x/y and, for an alternative route, uses the average (A+B)/2 of the two links' estimates, which worked best empirically (p. 10). The static policy uses marginal acceptance probabilities and needs no state information (p. 11).
- **D3 Model and data**: The network is a complete directed graph whose alternative routes have one intermediate node (p. 2). Arrivals are Poisson and holding times exponential with a common rate μ (p. 2). Policies are Markovian and judged under the stationary distribution (p. 2). The experiments set μ = 1 and C_ij = 10 (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Balance equations serve as LP constraints (proofs of Theorems 1, 3 and 5, pp. 3, 5, 8). The single-link case shows the whole ladder (the max-flow bound with O(1) variables, the relaxation with O(C), Miller's exact LP with O(C²)) and shows that only the relaxation, not the max-flow bound, yields a policy (pp. 3–4). Symmetric networks reduce to O(1), O(C) and O(C²) LPs (pp. 8–9). Large capacities are handled by scaling circuits into blocks (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Bounds come from CPLEX (no version given) and policies from simulation with standard deviations (p. 12). The instances are five-node symmetric and asymmetric networks plus one larger network that adds LBA (Tables I–VIII, pp. 6–14). The text reports the second-order bound equal to Kelly's and the third-order bound slightly better (pp. 12–13). Best bound/best policy is within 0–2.5% under symmetric rewards and 4–10% under asymmetric rewards (p. 13). The static policy has the best simulated reward in 27 of the 34 instance columns of Tables I, II, IV, V and VII, and DAR in 3 (pp. 6–13). In the asymmetric-capacity columns 5–6 of Tables IV–V, however, the tabulated second-order bound (8) lies below both Kelly's bound and the third-order bound (9) (pp. 10, 12), although the text calls (9) the tightest (p. 13).
- **D6–D7** (one line): The second-order relaxation is presented as Kelly's dynamic-programming bound in LP form, "close in spirit" (p. 8), and a numbered list of qualitative insights explains why the policies beat DAR (p. 14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The new bounds improve only slightly on Kelly's, more so as asymmetry grows (p. 15). DAR's trunk-reservation parameters are reused for asymmetric rewards although that notion "has to be rethought" (p. 12). More realistic settings are left as a necessary continuation (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (SD): the control problem is rewritten as LPs over performance vectors that contain the achievable region, with variable counts printed for each level (pp. 2, 5–8). This is the SW1 programme.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the paper duels the practice heuristics (DAR, and LBA on the larger network) and gives each instance a gap from the bound (best bound/best policy), but it relies on simulation and has no exact solve (pp. 12–14).
- new-pattern candidate: relaxation hierarchy by interaction order (pp. 4–8).
- new-pattern candidate: policy from the relaxation, certified by the same relaxation (pp. 10–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a ladder of LP relaxations by aggregating an intractable MDP to partial state descriptions of growing order. Write only balance, capacity and offered-traffic constraints, and tabulate the variable count of each rung (pp. 3–8).
- Read a randomized policy off a relaxation as conditional probabilities x/y. When two marginal estimates disagree (the two links of a route), test simple combinations (min, average) empirically (p. 10).
- Report suboptimality as best bound / best policy, so that each instance carries its own gap (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "To the best of our knowledge, there is no systematic approach to derive policies from relaxations." (p. 2)
- "The suboptimality guarantees defined as best bound/best policy range from 0 to 2.5% under symmetry conditions" (p. 1)

### S123 · Performance of multiclass Markovian queueing networks via piecewise linear Lyapunov functions (Annals of Applied Probability 11(4) 2001, DOI 10.1214/aoap/1015345407)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–29 were read: abstract, introduction and contributions, model, the §3 method (Lemma 1, Theorem 1 and its proof), §4 lower bounds including virtual stations, §5 upper bounds, §6 examples, §7 conclusions and the opening of Appendix A. The proofs of Appendices A–B (pp. 29–44) were not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: David Gamarnik (IBM T. J. Watson), John N. Tsitsiklis (MIT) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: stochastic-networks programme. The paper turns the global-stability results of Bertsimas–Gamarnik–Tsitsiklis [2] and Dai–Vande Vate [8] into performance bounds (pp. 2–3). It was received in March 2000 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+LO}: Performance analysis of multiclass networks is largely open and at least as hard as stability (p. 2). Methods based on quadratic Lyapunov functions and LPs give only numerical bounds on expectations, and they can be inconclusive on globally stable networks (p. 4). Entry: turn stability tools into performance tools (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea**: The core is a general result for countable Markov chains. A Lyapunov function with drift γ, exception level B and bounded jumps yields geometric tail bounds and an expectation bound, and a lower Lyapunov function yields matching lower bounds (Lemma 1, Theorem 1, pp. 8–11). The paper applies it to networks in four steps:
  - Linear lower Lyapunov functions give closed-form lower bounds of order Σ_j 1/(1 − ρ_σj) under any policy (Proposition 2, Theorem 2, pp. 11–13). Virtual stations extend them to priority policies (Theorem 3, p. 16).
  - A smoothed max of linear functions, built from any feasible solution of the Down–Meyn stability LP, is a Lyapunov function with an explicit B (Proposition 7, Theorem 4, pp. 19–21).
  - For two-station reentrant lines, a Dai–Vande Vate LP with slack ε ≥ 1 − ρ* gives E L = O(1/(1 − ρ*)²) under any work-conserving policy (Lemma 2, Theorem 5, pp. 22–25).
  - Feedforward networks get the same order, improving the O(1/(1 − ρ*)^{J0}) obtained with quadratic functions (Theorem 6, pp. 26–27).
- **D3 Model and data**: Arrivals are Poisson, services exponential, routing deterministic, buffers infinite, and customers do not renege (pp. 4–5). Only Markovian preemptive policies are covered; FIFO is excluded, although the authors believe the results hold for it (p. 5). Uniformization yields a discrete-time chain (p. 6), and stability is assumed a priori (p. 7).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): The tail recursion comes from the stationarity identity applied to max(c, Φ) (Lemma 1, proof from p. 29; used on pp. 9–11). The LP solution is perturbed uniformly to smooth the max of linear functions near the intersections of their hyperplanes. This follows Down–Meyn but with explicit constants (p. 20). The drift is bounded below through the LP slack in terms of ρ* (pp. 23–25).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: none. The worked examples are the Lu–Kumar network and a 3-station, 6-class reentrant line (pp. 17–19, 27–28).
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper claims the first explicit geometric upper and lower tail bounds at this generality, where earlier results gave numerical bounds on expectations only (pp. 1, 29). It re-establishes Down–Meyn's result with explicit constants (p. 20) and points to a heavy-traffic singularity at ρ2 + ρ4 + ρ6 = 2 not observed before (p. 19).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The upper bounds are numerical, through an LP, except for two-station reentrant lines (p. 3). Whether the bounds are finite exactly when the fluid network is globally stable is left open for the stochastic network (p. 29). Removing the square in O(1/(1 − ρ*)²) is also left open (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (SD): an LP is the device, and its feasible solution becomes the certificate for a probabilistic statement. The statement is a performance bound, not an optimal policy (pp. 19–25).
- new-pattern candidate: stability certificate reused as a performance bound (pp. 3, 19–21).
- new-pattern candidate: print the looseness of your own constants and the conjecture that would close it (p. 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If a Lyapunov function with bounded jumps proves stability, apply its drift inequality to max(c, Φ) under the stationary law. This gives a geometric recursion for P(Φ > B + 2ν_max m); the same template with a lower Lyapunov function gives lower bounds (pp. 8–11).
- Smooth a piecewise linear Lyapunov function by averaging over uniform perturbations of its LP coefficients, and carry the box sizes into an explicit exception constant (pp. 20–21).
- For lower bounds, use linear functions whose coefficients are tail sums of class loads. Only arrivals then raise the function, and the drift parameter is γ_j = 1 − ρ_σj (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "The goal of this paper is to turn some of the stability analysis tools into useful performance analysis tools." (p. 3)
- "It would be interesting to strengthen this result, perhaps by removing the exponent 2." (p. 29)

### S205 · From fluid relaxations to practical algorithms for job shop scheduling: the makespan objective (Mathematical Programming Ser. A 92(1) 2002, DOI 10.1007/s101070100272)
Read level: **partial**. Read: pp. 1–12 (abstract, introduction, related work, fluid relaxation, the FSA definition and Fig. 1); pp. 17–20 (motivation of nominal start times, the potential function, Lemmas 2–3); pp. 26–27 (Theorem 4 and the necessity remark); pp. 27–28 (the model with arrivals); pp. 33–41 (all computational results and the conclusions). The worked example (pp. 13–16) was skimmed. The proofs on pp. 20–26 and 28–33 were not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Jay Sethuraman (Columbia; the work was done at the MIT ORC) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: fluid-relaxation programme. The paper follows Bertsimas–Gamarnik [3] (p. 2) and has a companion on the holding-cost objective with Gamarnik [4] (p. 1). It was received in September 1999 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {SD+IO}: Job-shop makespan J||Cmax is NP-hard and notoriously hard even when small: a 10×10 instance stayed open for over 20 years (p. 2). Approximation algorithms classify complexity, but none "seems practical" (p. 5). Entry: the high-multiplicity regime (N jobs per type), where a fluid relaxation borrowed from multiclass queueing control is solvable in closed form (pp. 2, 7–9).
- **D2 Key idea**: The algorithm combines two communities (p. 2). From queueing control comes the fluid relaxation. Its optimum Cmax, the largest machine congestion, is reached by a processor-sharing-like allocation u_{i,k} = p_{i,k} x_i / Cmax (Proposition 2, pp. 7–9). From networking comes fair queueing based on start times (pp. 2, 17–18). At each machine epoch, FSA schedules the available job with the smallest nominal start time, which is the fluid start time corrected for discrete precedence (pp. 10–11, 17). This is a dynamic version of ordering jobs by LP start times, with the "LP start times" computed on the fly (p. 3). Theorem 4 gives Cmax ≤ C* ≤ C_D ≤ Cmax + max_i Σ_l (2P + U). The additive error is therefore at most (I + 2)Pmax Jmax, and FSA is asymptotically optimal as N grows (pp. 26–27). The result extends to deterministic arrivals (§3, pp. 27–33).
- **D3 Model and data**: Processing times are integers, schedules are non-preemptive, a machine processes one task at a time, and stages run in order (pp. 2, 5). The numbers of job types and stages are fixed; only multiplicity grows (p. 3). §3 adds deterministic arrivals at rates λ_i over a finite horizon (p. 28).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Congestion gives the lower bound (Propositions 1–2, pp. 6–8). A potential φ_r(t) = max{T^c_r(t) − T^d_r(t), −p_r} measures how far the discrete schedule lags the continuous one (p. 19). It yields DS ≤ NS + (P + U), in the spirit of Greenberg–Madras and Parekh–Gallager (p. 18). Completion times are chained from fluid to nominal to discrete (p. 18). A flow-shop example shows that an additive (Jmax − 1)Pmax is necessary for any comparison with the fluid bound (p. 27).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **ft10 with N = 1 to 1000**: the gap to N·Cmax stays between 491 and 653, against a theoretical bound of about 5,000. The relative error is 0.88 at N = 1 and below 1% at N = 100 (Table 10, pp. 33–34).
  - **82 OR-library instances** with 5–20 machines and 5–50 job types, N up to 500 (Table 11, pp. 33–36). Where the gap is 0 (for example la06, la12, la14 at every N), the lower bound certifies FSA's schedule as optimal; this is read from the table, and the text does not remark on it (p. 35).
  - **Eight dispatch rules** on swv01–10: at N = 1, STT beats FSA on all ten instances (Table 12, p. 37). From N = 10 on, FSA has the smallest error except on swv01, where STT is about 500 time units better (Tables 13–16, pp. 37–38).
  - **Shifting bottleneck**: it is better wherever it finishes, but on three of the four instances it fails to finish at N = 10 or N = 20 (out of stack space, hours of run time) (Tables 18–21, pp. 39–41).
  - Every Table 11 instance runs in under five seconds on an Ultra 10 Sun (p. 41). All gaps are measured to the trivial bound, not to the optimum (p. 34).
- **D6–D7** (one line): Against Sevast'janov, the paper claims a smaller additive bound when I is small and simpler implementation (p. 4); against approximation schemes, practicality (p. 5). The title has the form "From X to Y".
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The tightest additive error remains open; the conjecture is (Jmax − 1)Pmax (pp. 27, 41). FSA is offered as "a candidate for practical application" when jobs have multiplicity (p. 41). At N = 1 its error is large (Table 10, p. 34).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the paper duels the incumbent heuristics (eight dispatch rules of similar running time, and the shifting bottleneck) and prints where FSA loses (N = 1, swv01, small N against the shifting bottleneck). Each instance gets a gap to a lower bound, but there is no exact solve or certificate beyond that bound (pp. 37–41).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (SD → IO): the queueing-control relaxation supplies both the bound and the schedule (pp. 7–11).
- new-pattern candidate: asymptotic optimality as the warrant for a simple heuristic (pp. 3, 26–27, 33–34).
- new-pattern candidate: policy from the relaxation, certified by the same relaxation (pp. 9–11, 26–27).
- new-pattern candidate: From-X-to-Y titles naming the transplant (p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a hard combinatorial problem with repeated job types, solve the fluid relaxation in closed form. Then discretize by scheduling in order of "nominal" start times that track the fluid schedule and are corrected for precedence, and prove an additive error that does not depend on multiplicity (pp. 9–11, 26–27).
- To prove that a discrete schedule tracks a continuous one, use a potential that charges lag and credits at most one job of lead (p. 19).
- Test an asymptotic heuristic across multiplicities N = 1 to 1000 on standard benchmarks. Report its error against the lower bound beside the theoretical bound, and report where a slower incumbent wins and where it stops running (pp. 33–41).
- Show that incumbent rules are not asymptotically optimal with a two-machine family whose makespan stays a constant factor above the congestion bound (STT: 12k + 2 against 9k, p. 39).

**Quotes**:
- "the results of this paper imply that as the number of jobs increases, the combinatorial structure of the problem is increasingly less important, and as a result, a fluid approximation of the problem becomes increasingly exact." (p. 3)
- "Our experiments show that FSA is competitive although slightly worse than the shifting bottleneck heuristic, whenever such a comparison is possible." (p. 41)

### S350 · Joint network pricing and resource allocation (Working paper, MIT, June 2002, no DOI)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–21 were read: abstract, introduction, literature review, contributions, model and structural results, solution algorithm, the three multi-period heuristics, bounds, numerical experiments, the nested-inventory example and conclusions. The appendix proofs (pp. 22–28) were not read. Most numbers in Tables 1–10 are lost in the OCR text layer (pp. 16–21).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Sanne de Boer (MIT ORC student) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: revenue-management programme. Bertsimas–de Boer papers on simulation-based ADP and nested booking limits are cited (pp. 2, 21), as is Bertsimas–Popescu (p. 2). DPM follows an idea "succesfully applied in Bertsimas et al. (2001)" (p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {AP+SD}: Airline revenue management optimizes seat inventory at given prices. Joint pricing and seat allocation is rarely addressed, and the single-leg treatments do not scale (pp. 2–3). Newsvendor pricing models are single-product (p. 3). Entry: a multi-product, capacity-constrained, priced, multi-period newsvendor, motivated by airline pricing (pp. 1, 4).
- **D2 Key idea**: Demand is multiplicative, D = μ(p)Z (p. 5). Price is optimized out product by product. The expected revenue at the optimal price, R(x), is concave when log f is concave or Z is lognormal (Theorems 1–2, citing Zabel and Young, p. 6), even though revenue is not jointly concave in (x, p) (example, pp. 6–7). Piecewise-linearizing the concave R(x) turns the network problem into an LP (pp. 7–8). For multiple periods, three heuristics are re-solved on a rolling horizon (pp. 9–12):
  - DAM aggregates future demand into one period.
  - DDM sets separate allocations per period.
  - DPM fixes each product's total allocation by an LP over one-dimensional DP value functions, then prices dynamically.
- **D3 Model and data**: Production happens at the start of the period, a product's mean demand depends only on its own price, and excess demand is lost without salvage (p. 4). Assumption 2 is admitted to be necessary for the tractable structure (pp. 4–5). Inventory control is partitioned (p. 13). The data are synthetic: single-hub airline networks with Erlang demand, CV² = 33% and linear expected demand, parameters following Weatherford (1997) (p. 15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): A bound ladder J_LB ≤ J_static ≤ J_dynamic ≤ E[F(z)] ≤ F(E z) = J_UB follows from perfect foresight and Jensen's inequality. The lower bound depends on the coefficient of variation (Theorems 3–7, pp. 13–14). Under multiplicative demand the CV does not shrink with scale, so static deterministic pricing need not be asymptotically optimal, unlike under Poisson demand (pp. 14–15). Concavity of DPM's value functions was observed in every test but not proved (pp. 11–12). A Normal-noise counterexample marks where the concavity result stops (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Each policy is evaluated on 1000 simulated demand runs (p. 16). Network-wide pricing gains up to 22% over the benchmark p∞, which mimics points of sale pricing on their own (p. 17). DAM is marginally better than DPM when price elasticity is constant over time; DPM is clearly better only when elasticity varies (pp. 17–18). The stochastic model beats the deterministic one, and the upper bound "is not very tight" (p. 19). Scaling the problem by factors from 1 to 10 changes nothing significant (p. 19). The table values themselves are unreadable in this text.
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper disputes Gallego–van Ryzin (1997) and Paschalidis–Tsitsiklis (2000), attributing their asymptotic optimality of static deterministic pricing to Poisson demand (pp. 4, 14–15, 19). It calls DPM "an elegant combination of linear and dynamic programming" in both the abstract and the conclusions (pp. 1, 21).

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Dessislava Pachamanova (MIT ORC student, later at Babson) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: robust-finance programme. - **D1 Problem and entry point** {LO+RO+IO; AP}: Firm-value and intensity-based credit models need assumptions about default and recovery that are not always easy to validate (pp. 2–3). Entry: extract information from bond prices assuming only no-arbitrage (p. 3).

Minimizing and maximizing over that set bounds each π_j and the price of a test bond (Theorem 2, pp. 9–10). An unknown recovery rate is scanned in steps of 0.1, and rates at which the market price leaves the bounds are excluded (p. 10). Errors in the discount factors are handled by a robust counterpart over polyhedral sets, including a budget Γ normalized by standard deviations (Theorem 3, pp. 12–14). Simulated data use Poisson defaults with λ = 0.02 and bid-ask noise U[0, 0.01] or U[0, 0.03] (p. 18). Real data are Southwestern Bell, Bell Telephone–New Jersey and Delta Air bonds on July 2, 1999, with the Treasury curve from Bloomberg (pp. 26–27, 29).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Farkas' lemma turns infeasibility into an explicit arbitrage portfolio (pp. 6–7). LP duality gives the robust counterpart (pp. 12–13), and the Freund–Vera LP gives the smallest data perturbation (p. 16). A big-M-style link |π^i − v^k| ≤ (1 − z_ik)e ties each bond to its class curve (p. 17).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Bounds tighten with more bonds: five bonds give a price range within 5%, with little gain beyond twenty (pp. 18–19). A test bond whose maturity lies outside the data has wider bounds (p. 19).
  - An apparent recovery-rate effect is traced to the way the spreads were generated (pp. 21–22).
  - Robust bounds are tighter than nominal ones, because the robust feasible region is smaller (p. 24).
  - Classification separates default intensities that differ by 0.001 (p. 26).
  - On real data, the test bond lies inside the bounds for every recovery rate except those in [0.75, 1] (p. 27). AA is separated from BBB−, but not AA from AA− (p. 29).
  - Every problem solves in seconds (p. 29). No rival credit model is run.
- **D6–D7** (one line): The bounds are positioned as a data-driven complement to factor-based stochastic credit models (pp. 2–3, 29). Contributions are listed section by section (pp. 3–4).

- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the budget Γ appears (p. 13), but here robustness narrows the bounds (p. 24) and no price curve is reported.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a market's no-arbitrage condition is linear in unknown probabilities, compute the min and max of each unknown over the price-consistent set instead of fitting a stochastic model. The Farkas certificate of infeasibility is then an arbitrage trade (pp. 6–10).
- Calibrate an unobserved parameter (here recovery) by scanning it and excluding the values at which an observed price leaves the computed no-arbitrage bounds (p. 10).
- When real data make the feasibility system infeasible, compute the minimum-norm perturbation that restores feasibility instead of deleting observations (pp. 15–16).

### H011 · Optimal mean-variance bounds on the expectation of the highest order statistics (homepage manuscript 2004, no DOI; the file is the April 2004 preprint "Tight Bounds On Expected Order Statistics", a version of S154, DOI 10.1017/s0269964806060414)
Read level: **full**: pp. 1–20 (all text, Tables 1–4, Figures 1–3, references).

**Text identity**: Page 1 carries the title "Tight Bounds On Expected Order Statistics", the same three authors and the date April 2004 (p. 1). The homepage file papers/MomentProblems/orderstatistics.pdf is listed under this entry's title with the date May 2004 (works.json entry for H011). The computing platform, the Andreasen example and SeDuMi recur in S154's 2006 PEIS text (S154 pp. 13–16), which has a core card in c10. This is therefore a card for an earlier version; cite S154 for the published wording.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Karthik Natarajan (Singapore-MIT Alliance), Chung-Piaw Teo (NUS) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: moment-problems programme. It builds on Bertsimas–Natarajan–Teo [6], "Probabilistic combinatorial optimization: moments, semidefinite programming and asymptotic bounds" (pp. 4, 19).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {CP+AP}: Classical bounds on expected order statistics either assume independence (Gumbel; Hartley–David) or are not tight under general means and variances (Arnold–Groeneveld, Aven) (pp. 2–3). Entry: compute the tight bound sup E[x_{k:n}] over all distributions with the given moments (Definition 1, p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea**: The inequality x_{n:n} ≤ z_{n:n} + Σ(x_i − z_i)^+ gives a convex reformulation (Theorem 1, after Meilijson–Nadas, p. 4). The inner supremum has the closed form ½(μ_i − z + √((μ_i − z)² + σ_i²)) (Proposition 1, p. 5). An exchange argument reduces the problem to a strictly convex univariate minimization, solved by bisection (Theorem 2, pp. 5–6; p. 8). An n-atom extremal distribution attains the bound (Theorem 3, pp. 6–7), and the bisection endpoints give two closed-form bounds (Theorem 4, p. 9). With covariances, the dual moment problem becomes an SDP (pp. 11–12). For k < n, bounding x_{k:n} by the average of the top n − k + 1 order statistics gives a bound at least as tight as Arnold–Groeneveld's, and tight for identical moments (Theorems 5–7, pp. 12–14).
- **D3 Model and data**: Only means and variances are known (optionally covariances), with no independence assumed (p. 1). The σ_i are assumed positive for simplicity, relaxed in §2.3 (pp. 4, 10). Q ≻ 0 is needed for the SDP to be exact (p. 11). Nonnegative asset prices are not modelled (p. 15).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): The tightness certificate pairs the primal inequality with a matching extremal distribution (pp. 4–7). Cauchy–Schwarz gives the univariate inner supremum (p. 5). Global nonnegativity of a quadratic becomes an SDP constraint (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: The lookback-option example from Andreasen has n = 10, r = 5%, T = 1 and strikes K = 70 to 140. At K = 70 the tight bound is 75.38, against 327.97 (Boyle–Lin), 81.20 (Arnold–Groeneveld) and 77.79 (Aven) (Table 2, p. 16). On 100 random moment sets with n = 30, closed form (15) errs 7.73% on average, against 22.86% for Arnold–Groeneveld and 16.91% for Aven (Table 3, p. 17). For the second order statistic, (33) errs 1.04% against 3.13% (Table 4, p. 18). Everything runs in under a minute on a Pentium II, with SeDuMi for the SDP (pp. 14, 16).
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper recovers Arnold–Groeneveld's identical-moment bound as a special case (pp. 8, 13) and extends Lai–Robbins (p. 6). It concedes that there are constructed cases where the closed forms lose to the classical bounds (p. 18).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Tight bounds for general k under mean-variance (and covariance) information are left to future research (p. 19). For k < n, the bisection bound is not guaranteed tight (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (CP): a probability question is written as a convex program. The worst case is read from an extremal distribution and, with covariances, from the dual SDP (pp. 4–7, 11–12).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the incumbent closed-form bounds are run on a published example and on random moment data. The certificate is tightness through an extremal distribution, not a gap (pp. 15–18). This matches the link on S154's c10 card.
- new-pattern candidate: tight-or-nothing: bounds certified by extremal distributions (pp. 6–8, 13).
- new-pattern candidate: Subsume the incumbent as a special case (pp. 6, 8, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For sup E[max_i x_i] under marginal moment information, bound the max by z + Σ(x_i − z)^+, take the univariate worst case of each term in closed form, and minimize over the scalar z (pp. 4–6).
- Turn the endpoints of the bisection bracket into closed-form bounds. Compare them, together with the classical bounds, against the exact value on random instances (pp. 8–9, 17–18).
- Certify tightness by writing down the extremal discrete distribution built from the optimal z (pp. 6–7).

**Quotes**:
- "All our results are distribution-free with no explicit assumption of independence made." (p. 1)
- "While cases can be constructed for which both the bounds are weaker that either of Arnold and Groeneveld and Aven's bounds, the results suggest that the bounds are useful." (p. 18)

### S167 · Robust discrete optimization under ellipsoidal uncertainty sets (Manuscript, MIT, March 2004, no DOI)
Read level: **partial**: pp. 1–21 were read: the complexity result, the structural theorem, the piecewise-linear approximation, the Frank–Wolfe-type algorithm and its gap bound, the generalized budget model, the experiments and the conclusions. The numbers in Tables 2–3 are lost in the OCR text layer (pp. 20, 22); Table 1 is partly readable (p. 11). References (pp. 22–23) were skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Coauthor**: Melvyn Sim (NUS) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: RO period. The paper extends Bertsimas–Sim budgeted robust discrete optimization [5, 6] (pp. 2–3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point** {RO+IO}: Kouvelis–Yu scenario robustness can make polynomial problems NP-hard, while the Bertsimas–Sim budget sets preserve complexity; for ellipsoidal sets on discrete problems no complexity results existed (p. 2). Entry: does ellipsoidal cost robustness change the complexity of a discrete problem, and can it be solved fast in practice (p. 2)?
- **D2 Key idea**:
  - **Correlated ellipsoids**: the robust problem is NP-hard for shortest path, assignment, resource scheduling and spanning tree. The proof rewrites Kouvelis–Yu's two-scenario maximum as c'x + ½√(x'(c₁ − c₂)(c₁ − c₂)'x) (Theorem 1, pp. 4–5).
  - **Uncorrelated ellipsoids**: min c'x + f(d'x) with f concave reduces to nominal problems with costs c + η(w)d, one for each attainable value w of d'x (Theorem 2, pp. 5–6). Identical variances need n + 1 nominal solves (Proposition 1, p. 6). The link to parametric discrete optimization gives polynomiality over matroids (pp. 6–7).
  - **General d**: a piecewise-linear under-approximation of Ω√w with error ε gives an ε-optimal solution from k nominal solves (Theorems 3–4, Algorithm 1, pp. 7–10). That is not polynomial in bit size, but the readable cells of Table 1 show k = 25 to 447 (pp. 10–11).
  - **Local search**: a Frank–Wolfe-type method has improvement, monotonicity and local-optimality properties (Theorem 5, pp. 11–13). Two local optima found from the two ends of the subgradient range give a gap bound (Theorem 6, pp. 14–16).
  - **Generalized budget model**: Γ = f(e'x) depends on the solution, and the problem stays polynomial (Theorem 8, pp. 16–18).
- **D3 Model and data**: X ⊆ {0,1}^n is accessed through a nominal linear-optimization oracle (pp. 3, 9). Only the objective is uncertain (p. 3). The algorithms assume a diagonal covariance (p. 5). The budget model's probability bound assumes independent costs distributed symmetrically on [c − d, c + d] (Theorem 7, p. 16).
- **D4 Formulation and analysis** (brief): Hardness comes from a reduction of a known NP-hard robust problem via an algebraic identity (pp. 4–5). The concave function is linearized by subgradients over the finite range of d'x (pp. 5–6). Breakpoints are placed for a given maximum error (Proposition 3, pp. 8–9). The generalized budget model uses duality of the inner maximization (p. 17).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: For the robust knapsack (|N| = 200, b = 20,000, Ω = 1 to 5), the text reports the optimum reached in few iterations and a very small gap where two local minima arise (Ω = 3, 4) (Table 2, p. 20). For the uniform matroid (|N| = 200 to 20,000, Ω = 1 to 3, 100 instances each), most runs are optimal within fewer than 10 nominal solves, and the iteration count barely grows with |N| (Table 3, pp. 21–22). No exact solver or parametric algorithm is run as a baseline, although the matroid case is strongly polynomial by parametric programming (p. 21). The table values are unreadable in this text.
- **D6–D7** (one line): The paper frames its question as "the price (in increased complexity) of robustness" (p. 4) and calls its first result unfortunately negative (p. 4).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The complexity under a diagonal covariance is left open (p. 21). Algorithm 1 is not polynomial in bit size (p. 10). The generalized budget model costs more computation (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (RO+IO): class preservation is tested and its boundary printed. Budget sets and identical-variance ellipsoids keep the nominal class (n + 1 nominal solves), while correlated ellipsoids do not (NP-hard) (pp. 4–6, 21).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the price of robustness is measured in complexity rather than in objective value, and Γ is made solution-dependent to reduce conservatism (pp. 4, 16–18).
- new-pattern candidate: robust = nominal on modified data (structure inheritance) (pp. 5–6).
- new-pattern candidate: two-sided local search brackets the optimum with a computable gap (pp. 13–16, 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To show that a robust version is hard, rewrite a known hard robust problem (here two-scenario min-max) as a special case of your uncertainty set through an algebraic identity (pp. 4–5).
- For min c'x + f(d'x) with f concave over 0–1 sets, enumerate the attainable values w of d'x and solve the nominal problem with costs c + f'(w)d. If those values are few, complexity is preserved (pp. 5–6).
- Run the same local search from both ends of the subgradient range. If the two local optima differ, Theorem 6 gives a certified gap, and bisecting the range closes it (pp. 13–16, 19).

**Quotes**:
- "Our first result is unfortunately negative. Under ellipsoidal uncertainty sets with general covariance matrices, the price of robustness is an increase in computational complexity." (p. 4)
- "A message of the present paper is that the complexity of robust discrete optimization is affected by the choice of the uncertainty set." (p. 21)
