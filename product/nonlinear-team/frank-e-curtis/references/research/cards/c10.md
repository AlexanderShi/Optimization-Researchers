### S064 · Globally convergent primal-dual active-set methods with inexact subproblem solves (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2016, DOI 10.1137/140993314)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole text including Appendix A (proof of Theorem 3, pp. 19–21) and the reference list.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Zheng Han (Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lehigh-era work on large-scale QP subproblem solvers. It follows the COR@L technical report 12T-013 by Curtis, Han and Robinson [13] (p. 22) and presents itself as an enhancement of the PDAS method of Hintermüller, Ito and Kunisch [28] (p. 1). Received October 2014, accepted August 2016 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a cost bottleneck in a fast method. PDAS methods change the active-set estimate arbitrarily at each iteration and "often converge in few iterations" (abstract, p. 1). Each iteration requires the solve of a reduced linear system, which is "the main computational expense" (p. 2). In large-scale settings these systems are solved by Krylov methods, so the question is how much inexactness a globally convergent PDAS method can tolerate (pp. 1–2). The problem class (QP) has equality constraints plus upper bounds on a subset of variables (p. 3). Assumption 1 requires A_EF invertible and a positive definite reduced Hessian R (p. 3). The intended applications are discretized optimal control problems where R has M-matrix-like structure (p. 2, §5). The authors deliberately do not reduce the QP to a bound-constrained QP (BQP). Their reason is that the BQP reduced Hessian can be dense when the QP data are sparse (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: before using a reformulation that makes the theory easier (here: eliminate free variables to get a BQP), check whether it destroys the sparsity the large-scale solver depends on. If it does, keep the original data and pay the extra analysis cost (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The iterates are partitions (A, I) of the bounded indices. Theorem 2 states that a partition is optimal if and only if the violated sets V_P ∪ V_D are empty (p. 4). The generic Algorithm 1 moves any nonempty subsets C_P ⊆ V_P and C_D ⊆ V_D (p. 5). The key insight is identity (7): the exact subspace solution equals the inexact one plus residual terms involving R_II^{-1} and Schur complements of Q (p. 7). Bounds α_I and β_A proportional to the residual therefore certify a subset of the *exact* violated sets without computing the exact solution (Lemma 4, (8)–(10), pp. 7–8). There are three algorithms:
  1. Algorithm 2 uses an explicit upper bound γ ≥ ‖R_II^{-1}‖, obtained from an H-matrix procedure (12) and turned into α and β by Hölder's inequality (13) (pp. 8–10). Two optional extra conditions are given: a relative-residual test (14) and a consistency test (16) with parameter θ. The authors state that they are "not necessary to ensure convergence" but help in practice (pp. 10–11).
  2. Algorithm 3 replaces the explicit bound by a dynamic γ. It multiplies γ by δ_γ > 1 whenever the KKT error at a partition update is not below κ times the maximum of the last j̄ such errors (step 13, p. 12).
  3. Algorithm 4 is the "straightforward heuristic" of a residual tolerance ζ that shrinks as the run proceeds (pp. 13–14).

  What is kept from [28]: the partition update and the subspace solution. What is changed: the solve may be inexact at every partition except optimal ones (abstract p. 1; p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: derive the inner solver's stopping test from what the outer step must certify. Here the test is "a nonempty subset of the true violated indices is identified", not a forcing-sequence bound on the residual (Lemma 4, pp. 7–8).
  - Transferable technique: when the constant the test needs is expensive (‖R_II^{-1}‖), replace it by a parameter that grows only when a progress monitor fails. The proof then argues that, if progress never comes, the parameter eventually exceeds the true constant (Algorithm 3, Theorem 8, pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumption 1 (p. 3). The finite-termination theorem (Theorem 3) needs one of two conditions (p. 5): (a) R is a P-matrix with ‖[R_II^{-1}R_IA]_+‖_1 < 1 plus a positivity condition; or (b) R = M + E with M an M-matrix and ‖E‖_1 "sufficiently small". The authors flag that the convergence guarantees "require strengthenings" of Assumption 1 (p. 3). Solves with A_EF are assumed exact, which they call reasonable because A_EF does not depend on the partition (p. 6). Theorem 5 needs either residuals → 0 under repeated solves, or at most J solves per partition until the residual is exactly zero (p. 8). The bound procedure (12) needs R = M + E (Lemma 7, p. 10). Algorithm 4 is guaranteed only with ζ = 0, which is called "significantly weaker" (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Finite termination by a potential over finitely many partitions** (Theorem 12, p. 20). Each update gives [Δx]_{A+} ≤ 0 and [Δz]_{I+} ≥ 0, and these are not both zero. Hence eᵀx_B strictly decreases while x is determined by one of finitely many partitions. Theorem 13 handles R = M + E by an O(‖E‖_1) perturbation of the same inequality (p. 21). Both generalize [28, Theorems 3.3–3.4] from BQPs to (QP) and to partial updates (p. 19).
  - **Continuity argument** for Lemma 4: at a suboptimal partition, every strictly violated index stays detectable once ‖(r̃, t̃)‖ ≤ ε_i, and ε is the minimum over the violated indices (p. 8).
  - **H-matrix bound**: ‖B^{-1}‖_1 ≤ (min_i [Bw]_i)^{-1} for any w in P(B) (Lemma 6, from Varga [53], p. 9). It is computed by iteratively solving M(R_II)w ≈ e until M(R_II)w > 0, and the bound is exact when R is an M-matrix (Lemma 7, pp. 9–10). The Ahlberg–Nilson–Varah bound is the diagonally dominant special case (p. 10).
  - **Contradiction on a growing parameter** (Theorem 8, pp. 12–13). If infinitely many updates occur with KKT errors bounded away from zero, then γ → ∞ and eventually γ ≥ γ̄ = max_I ‖R_II^{-1}‖_p (17). From then on Theorem 5 applies.
  - **Small counterexamples that justify the expensive mechanism**: Example 9, a 3×3 R with ‖R^{-1}‖_1 = 31/4 < 8 = ‖R_II^{-1}‖_1, shows that a global bound cannot initialize γ safely (p. 13). Example 11 shows that the naive Algorithm 4 can need a residual that is exactly zero to guarantee a correct nonempty update, while Algorithms 2–3 succeed for |r̃_3| < 3 (pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable technique: when you claim a cheap heuristic cannot have the stronger guarantee, give a 3×3 example in which it provably needs an exact solve (Example 11, pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Software: all variants live in one Python 2.7.3 package using cvxopt and SciPy's MINRES, with no preconditioner. The authors justify this by the purpose of the experiments (pp. 15–16). The comparator SSN, a semismooth Newton method with inexact solves from [30], is reimplemented in the same package (p. 15). Algorithm 1 is the "exact" baseline: the same method with a tight residual tolerance (pp. 15, 17).
  - Engineering details: (13) is checked only every 10 MINRES iterations because of its cost (p. 15). Algorithm 3 gets an extra γ update from MINRES intermediate vectors (p. 15).
  - Problems: the two 2-D elliptic optimal control problems of [28], (18) and (19), on grids g ∈ {4, …, 64}, up to n = 8192, m = 4096 (Table 1, p. 17). Termination uses mesh-scaled condensed optimality conditions "to avoid mesh-dependent performance" (pp. 16–17).
  - Setup: parameters in Table 2 are "merely ones that worked well in our experiments" (p. 17). All algorithms start from the same initial partition (p. 17).
  - Measures: PDAS iterations and total Krylov iterations. CPU time is not reported, but is said to be proportional to Krylov iterations (p. 18).
  - Basic instances (Tables 3–4, p. 18): iteration counts are nearly equal. Krylov totals drop, e.g. for (18) at g = 64: Algorithm 1 29911 against Algorithm 2 16269, Algorithm 3 16261, SSN 16308. "Algorithm SSN is also competitive" (p. 18).
  - Degenerate instances, built following [4] with half of the optimal bound multipliers zero (p. 18; Tables 5–6, p. 19): for (18) at g = 64, Algorithm 4 needs 7512 Krylov iterations against 16797 for Algorithm 1 and 11958 for SSN.
  - The bound procedure (12) needed only one Krylov iteration per PDAS iteration because of diagonal dominance (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper is framed as an enhancement of [28] (p. 1) and contrasted with classical primal or dual active-set methods "plagued by slow adaptation of the active-set estimate" (pp. 1–2). The appendix theorems are presented as generalizations of [28] (p. 19). The comparison with Hintermüller–Stadler's inexact SSN [30] is gentle: SSN is competitive, "though we believe" the productivity guarantees "can be advantageous" (p. 19). Test (14) is credited to inexact Newton methods (Dembo–Eisenstat–Steihaug [16], p. 11). The authors' own earlier technical report [13] is cited (p. 2, p. 22).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is "Our contributions in this paper are twofold" (p. 2). The introduction then answers the two obvious objections as "two important remarks to make at the outset": why not reduce to a BQP, and why not simply shrink a tolerance (p. 2). Every algorithm is followed by prose on what it buys and what it costs, e.g. the two "key disadvantages" of Algorithm 4 (p. 14). The conclusion is hedged ("we believe", p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: put the two objections a referee will raise first into the introduction as "remarks at the outset", each with a one-sentence answer and a pointer to where it is proved (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is theory plus algorithms plus a research prototype. The limits are stated concretely:
  - the class requires P-matrix or perturbed M-matrix reduced Hessians (p. 5);
  - the third algorithm's guarantee is weak and its parameters are hard to choose (p. 14);
  - there is no preconditioner (p. 16);
  - parameters are untuned, and "it would be ideal to tune these parameters for individual applications" (p. 17).

  The experiments use two optimal-control problems only and no CPU times (pp. 17–18). The conclusion names no explicit future direction (p. 19).

**Method links**: Method 1 ✅ evidence: the inexactness test is derived from what the active-set update must certify, and inexactness is allowed "at all partitions except optimal ones" (pp. 1, 7–9). Method 2 ⚠ variant: the dynamic γ in Algorithm 3 is a parameter raised by a progress monitor on the KKT error (p. 12). It follows the "update rule on observed progress" shape, but controls inexactness, not the objective–feasibility trade-off. Method 3 ✅ evidence (weak): intentionally degenerate instances are reported separately (pp. 18–19), but they are built from [4], not by a new mechanical rule. Method 5 ✅ evidence with ⚠: the exact baseline differs only in solve accuracy and the rival SSN lives in the same software (p. 15). Parameters are hand-picked with the tuning effort unreported (p. 17), which matches the ⚠ already in Method 5. New-pattern candidates: "counterexample that justifies the expensive mechanism" (Examples 9 and 11, pp. 13–15); "unknown-constant estimator that only grows when progress stalls" (Theorem 8, pp. 12–13; also S002 σ_k, p. 8).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Inner stopping test = "the outer step is certified productive" (identify a subset of the true violated sets), with residual bounds from a computable norm bound on the inverse of the reduced matrix (pp. 7–10).
- Replace an expensive problem constant by a monotone dynamic parameter. Raise it only when a windowed progress test fails (KKT_j ≥ κ max of last j̄), and prove it eventually dominates the constant (pp. 12–13).
- Compute a certified bound on ‖R^{-1}‖_1 by iteratively solving M(R)w ≈ e until M(R)w > 0, then applying Varga's bound (Lemma 6, (12), pp. 9–10).
- Report total inner (Krylov) iterations next to outer iterations when the claim is about inexact inner solves (Tables 3–6, pp. 18–19).
**Quotes**:
- "a simpler heuristic method that merely decreases a dynamic accuracy tolerance as the optimization process proceeds will not ensure convergence unless one imposes strong requirements on the employed linear system solver" (p. 2)
- "the purpose of our numerical experiments is merely to illustrate the convergence behavior of our algorithms despite inexactness in the subspace solutions" (p. 16)

### S077 · Accelerating convergence to competitive equilibrium in electricity markets (2016 IEEE Power and Energy Society General Meeting (PESGM) 2016, DOI 10.1109/pesgm.2016.7741162)
**Read level**: full (TR version). Read the MERL technical report TR2016-095 (July 2016): the cover sheet (p. 1) and all five paper pages (pp. 3–7), including the references. The published IEEE version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Arvind U. Raghunathan (MERL, first author), Yusuke Takaguchi, Hiroyuki Hashimoto (Mitsubishi Electric Advanced Technology Center) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: An industrial collaboration with MERL in which Curtis is the second author. It applies the semismooth-equation approach of the Curtis–Raghunathan MERL report "Solving Structured Quadratic Optimization Problems as Nonsmooth Equations" [13] (pp. 3, 7) to market clearing. It belongs to the same 2014–2016 thread of semismooth and active-set Newton methods for structured QPs as S064. The voice is a joint industrial paper, probably led by the first author (inferred from author order only).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is an application gap. Electricity-market research "has focused largely on aspects of electricity market design" with "little work on algorithmic and computational aspects" (p. 3). The future grid (ARPA-E NODES) calls for decentralized algorithms that "scale with network size and have little overhead in communication" (p. 3). Agents (GenCos and DSOs) are price-taking and keep their cost functions private. The ISO therefore uses a subgradient price update, which converges slowly and requires "significant numbers of message communications" (p. 3). The problem is to compute a competitive (Walrasian) equilibrium: agent problems (1)–(2), the ISO problem (3) and the equilibrium conditions (a)–(c) (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: in an applied venue, open with what the field has *not* studied (algorithms rather than design) and tie it to a concrete systems requirement, here communication rounds (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The protocol stays the same (the ISO sends prices, the agents solve privately), but each agent also returns the sensitivity ∂P_i*/∂λ_i of its solution to its price (p. 3). These sensitivities are analytic for the agents' one-dimensional bound-constrained problems: 1/c_i″ or −1/u_i″ in the interior, 0 at a bound ((14)–(15), p. 6). The ISO's KKT conditions with P = P*(λ) form an implicit complementarity problem (ICP) (6)–(7) (p. 5). It is solved by a Fischer–Burmeister reformulation (8)–(9), semismooth Newton steps (11), and a backtracking line search on Ψ = ‖Φ‖² (13) (pp. 5–6). The generalized Jacobian H_k is built from diagonal matrices and the sensitivity matrix B_k (16) (p. 6). Algorithm 1 is on p. 6. Compared with dual decomposition, only the ISO's update and one extra scalar per agent change.
  - Transferable technique: to accelerate a decomposition loop, ask each subproblem to return the derivative of its solution with respect to the coordinating signal. The coordinator can then take (semismooth) Newton steps instead of subgradient steps, with the information pattern unchanged (pp. 3, 6).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: GenCo costs strictly convex (Assumption 1) and DSO utilities strictly concave (Assumption 2), so the agent solutions are unique (p. 4). Further modelling assumptions (p. 3): DC power flow; each DSO is a single node with deferrable loads; agents are price takers who do not share cost functions. Strict complementarity of the agent problems is assumed "for simplicity", with the general case deferred to [13] (p. 6). F is only piecewise C¹ (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: There is one proved result, Theorem 1: a competitive equilibrium is equivalent to a solution of the ICP. The proof runs both directions through the KKT conditions of the convex ISO problem (p. 5). Local superlinear convergence is cited, not proved here: Theorem 2 from Qi–Sun [18] for C¹ F, and [13] for PC¹ F (p. 5). The line search is described as promoting global convergence, with no theorem (p. 5). The H_k formula with the z-vector for degenerate indices β^k comes from De Luca–Facchinei–Kanzow [20] (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Cases: IEEE 9-, 14-, 30-, 39-, 57- and 118-bus networks. GenCo costs are quadratic from the data files, DSO utilities are random quadratics, and demand bounds are 0.8–1.2 times the file demand (p. 6).
  - Baseline: a subgradient method with step 1/(k+1) (17) that replaces steps 7–9 of Algorithm 1. No tuning of this step is reported (p. 6).
  - Protocol: MATLAB on a 3 GB desktop (p. 6). Each figure is the average of 10 runs with randomly generated costs, utilities and demands; tolerance 10⁻⁶; an iteration cap of 100000 (p. 7).
  - Results (Table I, p. 7): the subgradient method hits the cap on 5 of 6 cases (43250 iterations on the 39-bus case). Semismooth Newton needs 5–10 iterations, 16–83 function evaluations and 0.01–1.50 s, against 1.42–29.5 s for the subgradient method. The authors add that the Newton update "is more expensive" per iteration (p. 7). Fig. 1 plots error against iteration, and the observed rate is said to be superlinear "as predicted by Theorem 2" (p. 7). The figure itself is not in the text layer.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the equilibrium-modelling literature: Hobbs–Helman, Hobbs–Metzler–Pang, Baldick, Weber–Overbye, Wang et al., and AC-model papers (p. 3). It does not criticize that literature; it points out that the literature lacks algorithmic work (p. 3). The only algorithmic rival is Shor's subgradient method [12] (pp. 3, 6). The method itself is credited to the authors' own MERL report [13] (pp. 3, 5).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: A short IEEE format with sections A "Our Focus" and B "Our Contribution". The modelling assumptions (a)–(d) are listed up front (p. 3). The headline claim, "several orders of magnitude speedup", is in the abstract (p. 1, p. 3) and is quantified in the conclusion as "about 4 orders of magnitude fewer function evaluations" (p. 7). "Robust" is claimed on six network cases (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an application-level algorithm transfer. The stated limits are the lumped DSO model and the absence of distributed generation (p. 7). The stated future directions are to model DSO networks with distributed generation, AC power flow, and the SDP relaxation [10] (p. 7). Not discussed in the text (reader's observation): the cost of the extra sensitivity messages, and a tuned or accelerated first-order baseline.

**Method links**: Method 1 ⚠ variant: the inner solvers (the agents) are asked to return exactly what the outer Newton iteration needs, namely solution sensitivities (pp. 3, 6). This is the "inner serves outer" logic applied to information content rather than to solve accuracy. Method 5 ⚠ variant: the results average 10 randomized runs (p. 7), but the baseline is an untuned 1/(k+1) subgradient method stopped by an iteration cap (pp. 6–7). There is no favoured or tuned baseline, unlike Method 5 steps 5–6. New-pattern candidate: "sensitivity-returning decomposition", i.e. turn a price-coordination loop into a semismooth Newton method on the coordinator's complementarity system (pp. 3, 5–6).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the coordinator's optimality conditions with the agents' best responses substituted in, as an implicit complementarity problem, then apply a Fischer–Burmeister semismooth Newton method with a merit line search (pp. 5–6).
- For one-dimensional bound-constrained agent problems, the solution sensitivity is 1/(second derivative) in the interior and 0 at a bound: cheap to communicate and exact (p. 6).
- When the baseline is a first-order coordination method, compare oracle calls (agent solves) as well as iterations and time, because communication rounds are the real cost (Table I, p. 7).
**Quotes**:
- "we impose that the agents also return the sensitivity of their solution to changes in the price" (p. 3)
- "Still, the number of function evaluations is 4 orders of magnitude smaller than for the subgradient method." (p. 7)

### S002 · A trust region algorithm with a worst-case iteration complexity of O(ε^{-3/2}) for nonconvex optimization (Mathematical Programming 2017, DOI 10.1007/s10107-016-1026-2)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole text including the Appendix "Subproblem solver" (pp. 31–32) and the reference list. The version read is the Springer online-first text (received October 2014, accepted May 2016, p. 1). The later corrigendum to Lemma 3.19 (mentioned in SKILL.md) was not read, so the Lemma 3.19 noted below is the original statement.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel P. Robinson (Johns Hopkins AMS), Mohammadreza Samadi (Lehigh ISE, Curtis's student) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Curtis's entry into worst-case complexity for nonconvex smooth optimization. It is the TRACE line, later extended to inexact subproblems (per SKILL.md, not this text) and later criticized by Curtis himself (regional complexity; SKILL.md Method 4).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a gap between two traditions. Methods with "global and fast local convergence guarantees" were the field's aim for decades, while worst-case complexity bounds "have typically been overlooked" for nonconvex problems (p. 2). ARC attains O(ε^{-3/2}), which is optimal for a class of second-order methods, whereas a traditional trust-region method only guarantees O(ε^{-2}) (p. 3). The stated purpose is a trust-region method with ARC's bound that keeps the classical global and local guarantees (p. 3). The measure is iteration count, justified because the methods use at most one function, gradient and Hessian evaluation per iteration (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when a newer framework beats a classical one on a theoretical measure, ask whether the classical framework can be modified to match it while keeping its own advantages. Here TR keeps the possibility of unregularized Newton steps, which ARC never takes (pp. 3, 11).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The method starts from a diagnosis. ARC's analysis rests on two properties of accepted steps (2.4): decrease ≥ c₁σ_k‖s_k‖³ and ‖s_k‖ ≥ (c₂/(σ_max + c₃))^{1/2}‖g_{k+1}‖^{1/2}, and traditional TR does not guarantee them (p. 6). TRACE changes three things (p. 7):
  1. acceptance uses ρ_k = (f_k − f(x_k + s_k))/‖s_k‖³ (2.5) instead of actual/predicted reduction;
  2. a step with good decrease but λ_k > σ_k‖s_k‖ (too small for its dual variable) is rejected *and the region is expanded* (Algorithm 1 steps 14–17, p. 9);
  3. a contraction may set the new radius implicitly as ‖s‖ for a regularized subproblem Q_k(λ) with a chosen λ, so the radius may decrease sublinearly (contract, p. 9).

  An auxiliary σ_k estimates an upper bound for λ_k/‖s_k‖. It enters only the acceptance test, never the subproblem (p. 8). A nondecreasing cap Δ_k bounds expansions (p. 8). What is kept: the quadratic model solved globally inside a trust region (pp. 4–5). The name stands for Trust Region Algorithm with Contractions and Expansions (p. 8). The authors chose to present a specific contraction procedure rather than abstract conditions because it is "closer to how the algorithm would be implemented" (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: to import a rival method's complexity, list the inequalities its proof uses for accepted steps (here (2.4)) and redesign your own acceptance and parameter-update rules until every accepted step satisfies an analogue (pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f is C² (Assumption 2.1, p. 4). Assumption 3.1 adds: f bounded below; g Lipschitz on a set containing iterates and trial points; nonzero, bounded gradients (p. 12). Assumption 3.15 asks for a Lipschitz Hessian on the path of iterates and steps; it is needed for complexity (p. 20). For the local result, Assumption 3.29 asks for a subsequence converging to x* with H(x*) ≻ 0 and a locally Lipschitz Hessian (p. 28). Exact Hessians and globally optimal subproblem solutions are assumed "For simplicity", and the authors admit these requirements are "impractical in some large scale settings" (pp. 3, 30).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - **Iteration taxonomy**: accepted A (split into A_σ and A_Δ), contraction C, expansion E (p. 12). Lemma 3.7 shows that C ∪ E cannot be followed by E (pp. 14–15), so at most one expansion occurs between accepted steps (Lemma 3.22, p. 24).
  - **Global convergence**: if only contractions occur from some point, then δ_k → 0 and λ_k → ∞ (Lemma 3.9, pp. 15–16), so a large dual variable forces ρ_k ≥ η (Lemma 3.8, p. 15) and A is infinite (Lemma 3.10, p. 16). lim ‖g_k‖ = 0 then follows from a standard argument on two interleaved subsequences (Theorem 3.14, pp. 19–20).
  - **Bounded σ_k**: Lemma 3.17 bounds λ_{k+1}/‖s_{k+1}‖ after any contraction (pp. 21–22), and Lemma 3.18 bounds σ_k (p. 22). Lemma 3.19 gives the step-norm lower bound ‖s_k‖ ≥ (H_Lip + σ_max)^{-1/2}‖g_{k+1}‖^{1/2} (pp. 22–23).
  - **Counting template**:
    - Good accepted steps decrease f by at least η(H_Lip + σ_max)^{-3/2}ε^{3/2}, so there are at most K_σ(ε) of them (Lemma 3.20, p. 23).
    - Steps capped by Δ decrease f by at least ηΔ₀³, so there are at most K_Δ of them (Lemma 3.21, p. 24).
    - Contractions between accepted steps are at most K_C = 1 + log(σ_max/σ)/log(min{γ_λ, γ_c^{-1}}). The reason is that the ratio λ/‖s‖ grows geometrically during contractions and is capped (Lemmas 3.23–3.24, pp. 25–26).
    - Total: K(ε) = 1 + (K_σ(ε) + K_Δ)(1 + K_C) = O(ε^{-3/2}) (Theorem 3.25, p. 26).
  - **Second order**: λ_k ≥ −ξ_{k,1} gives decrease ≥ ηε³σ_max^{-3}, hence O(max{ε^{-3}, ε^{-3/2}}) (Lemma 3.27 and Theorem 3.28, pp. 27–28).
  - **Local rate**: eventually all steps are accepted Newton steps (Lemma 3.31, p. 29), so the rate is Q-quadratic (Theorem 3.32, p. 30).
  - **Eigen-decomposition bound**: the lower bound on ‖ŝ‖ in contract goes through the eigen-decomposition of H_k (Lemma 3.12, pp. 17–18).
  - Transferable technique: for any adaptive method, bound (#good steps) by (f₀ − f_min)/(guaranteed decrease per good step). Then bound (#bad steps between good ones) by a constant, using a ratio that grows geometrically on bad steps and is bounded above (pp. 23–26).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The paper has no numerical section: its organization is algorithm, analysis and conclusion (p. 4). The Appendix argues on cost instead. Contract can be folded into the safeguarded Newton solve of the secular equation, so an iteration is "at most as expensive as" a traditional TR or ARC iteration (pp. 10, 31–32). After a contraction the next subproblem avoids the "hard case" (p. 32).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds directly on ARC (Cartis–Gould–Toint [4, 5, 6]), Nesterov–Polyak [12] and Griewank [9] (pp. 2–3). It thanks Cartis, Gould and Toint for discussions "that were inspirational" (p. 30). ARC is not criticized, only distinguished from TRACE: cubic regularization in ARC "is never 'off'" (p. 3), and ARC "never computes Newton steps" (p. 11). The claimed novelty is "significant departures" from traditional TR acceptance and update rules (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution is stated in the third paragraph of the introduction (p. 3). A whole subsection (§2.1) explains *why* the change is needed before the algorithm is given (pp. 5–6). The three modifications are listed as bullets (p. 7). Each odd step gets prose ("Perhaps the most intriguing aspect of our algorithm is the calculation stated in Step 30", p. 10). The conclusion leaves sharpness explicitly open (p. 30).
  - Transferable technique: give a "Motivation" subsection that names the exact inequalities the rival's proof relies on, so readers see the design target before the algorithm (§2.1, pp. 5–6).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and theoretical. The limits are stated concretely: exact second derivatives and global subproblem solutions (p. 30); sharpness of the bound not confirmed, because Newton steps and the dynamic choice between contraction and regularization prevent copying ARC's lower-bound example (p. 30); no numerical evidence (D5). Future work: inexact variants, "the subject of current research" (p. 30).

**Method links**: Method 4 ⚠ variant: here Curtis adopts the worst-case iteration-complexity yardstick as the design target and justifies iterations as the measure (p. 2), without auditing it. This is the 2017 side of the tension that SKILL.md records for his later "grain of salt" stance. Method 2 ⚠ variant: as in steering, the contribution is a parameter-update rule (radius contraction and expansion, σ_k) driven by observed step quality (λ_k/‖s_k‖, ρ_k) (pp. 7–9). The trade-off is step size against complexity, not objective against feasibility. Research Taste mark 2 (keep the classical guarantees) is evidenced: TRACE "maintains the global and fast local convergence guarantees of a traditional trust region algorithm" (p. 30). Heuristic 2 (count the safeguard's cost) is evidenced by the Appendix cost argument (pp. 31–32). New-pattern candidates: "port the rival's proof-critical inequalities" (pp. 6–7); "unknown-constant estimator that grows only when needed" (σ_k, p. 8; Lemma 3.18, p. 22; cf. S064 Theorem 8).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure decrease relative to ‖s‖³ instead of predicted reduction when the target is O(ε^{-3/2}) complexity. Near a strict minimizer this test still accepts Newton steps, because {s_k} → 0 (§2.4, p. 11).
- Use the trust-region dual variable λ_k as a signal: when λ_k/‖s_k‖ is too large, reject and expand rather than accept a step that is too short (pp. 7–8, 10).
- Set a contracted radius implicitly as ‖s(λ)‖ for a chosen regularization λ, which allows sublinear radius decrease and keeps the next subproblem out of the hard case (pp. 7, 9, 32).
- Complexity counting template: (#good steps) ≤ (f₀ − f_min)/(decrease per good step), times (1 + bounded #bad steps between good ones) (Theorem 3.25, p. 26).
**Quotes**:
- "there are situations in which our algorithm may reject a step and expand the trust region" (p. 3)
- "We expect that this is also the case for our algorithm, but this has not yet been confirmed." (p. 30)

### S004 · A BFGS-SQP Method for Nonsmooth, Nonconvex, Constrained Optimization and its Evaluation using Relative Minimization Profiles (Optimization Methods and Software 2017, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2016.1208749)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole text: the T&F cover sheet (p. 1), the article (pp. 2–29), the notes and references (pp. 29–31), and the Appendix A figure captions (pp. 32–35). All figures (Figs. 1–7, A1–A8) are images, so only their captions and the prose describing them were read. The version read is the online-first article (received 23 June 2015, accepted 23 June 2016, p. 2).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Tim Mitchell (Max Planck Magdeburg, corresponding author), Michael L. Overton (Courant/NYU) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: The successor of SQP-GS (Curtis–Overton 2012, [13]). It drops gradient sampling and keeps BFGS plus Byrd–Nocedal-style penalty steering. It introduces the GRANSO code (p. 24) and relative minimization profiles (RMPs). It is one of SKILL.md's signature works (Methods 4 and 5; Heuristic 5).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Two entries:
  1. Application pull. The HIFOO controller-design toolbox handles constrained nonsmooth problems with a sequential fixed penalty parameter (SFPP) restart scheme around BFGS, "because of the prohibitive cost of using gradient sampling" (p. 4). The authors hope "to replace the SFPP method currently implemented in hifoo" (p. 4). SQP-GS is the only method with guarantees for (1), but it needs O(n) gradients per iteration (p. 3). Lewis–Overton showed BFGS to be efficient on unconstrained nonsmooth problems (p. 3). The stated aim is "to eschew a costly gradient sampling approach entirely" (p. 3).
  2. Evaluation gap. Performance and data profiles need binary success criteria that "must typically be chosen arbitrarily" and are sensitive to stopping parameters (pp. 4–5). For nonconvex constrained problems, objective quality, feasibility and speed "are often at odds with one another" (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: when your method has no convergence theory, make the evaluation methodology part of the contribution and say so explicitly ("we rely upon our new benchmarking tool to justify our algorithm's performance", p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Step**: BFGS-SQP replaces the BFGS direction on the exact penalty φ = μf + v by the penalty-SQP QP (7) with a BFGS matrix H_k, solved through its dual (8)–(9) (pp. 6–7).
  - **Steering** (Procedure 1, p. 8), adapted from Byrd et al. [11, 12] (p. 6): if the predicted linearized violation reduction l_δ(d_k) < c_v v(x_k), compute the feasibility-only reference direction d̃_k (μ = 0) and shrink μ by c_μ until l_δ(d_k) ≥ c_v l_δ(d̃_k), with a capped loop (p. 8).
  - **Stopping**: the stationarity test reuses gradients from the l most recent nearby iterates in a QP (12)–(13) that imitates gradient sampling without new evaluations (pp. 8–9).
  - **Line search**: an inexact Armijo–weak-Wolfe search from Lewis–Overton (p. 9). Full pseudocode in Procedure 2 (p. 10).
  - **Implementation choices**: c_v = 0.1 and c_μ = 0.9; at most 10 steering loops; steering only at infeasible iterates (p. 24); μ₀ = 16, always halved, to avoid floating-point inconsistency (note 6, p. 29).
  - **Evaluation tool**: an RMP plots, against a relative-residual tolerance γ, the fraction of problems on which a method's best feasible objective lies within γ of a target ((16)–(17), pp. 17–18). The targets are the best feasible values found by any method (19) (p. 18). β-RMPs restrict each method to β times a per-problem budget b_i. The budget is set from the method under study (22), and the targets are re-set ("rolling") for each β (26) ((20)–(26), pp. 19–22).
  - Transferable technique: obtain a cheap stationarity certificate for nonsmooth problems from the gradients of recent iterates within a radius. The certificate is the minimum-norm element of their (penalty-weighted) convex hull, computed by a QP dual (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: f and c are only assumed continuously differentiable almost everywhere (p. 2). Equality constraints are omitted "for a clearer and more concise exposition" (p. 2). The paper makes no convergence claim: "We make no claims that BFGS-SQP has theoretical convergence guarantees" (p. 9). The test problems are spectral-radius problems (non-Lipschitz at multiple eigenvalues, Remark 4.3, p. 11) and pseudospectral-radius problems (locally Lipschitz, Remark 4.7, p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: n/a for convergence: the paper proves no theorem. The analytic design work is definitional:
  - relative differences rather than ratios for objective quality, with the reason given (p. 19);
  - the equivalence of r^{m,β} and r^m for β ≥ 1 when the budgets come from method m (pp. 20–21);
  - the rolling target set, which maximizes separation in each β-RMP (p. 22).

  §5.3 discusses the measurement error of β-RMPs: CPU-time variability, quantization over discrete iterates, and missing per-iterate timing (pp. 21–22).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - Test set: 200 problems, 100 spectral-radius (14) and 100 pseudospectral-radius (15), built from a stated random recipe with 2–5 plants and N ≤ 20 or ≤ 8. The sizes were kept small "in order to compare to SQP-GS" (pp. 23–24).
  - Rivals: SNOPT 7.5, SFPP (HANSO BFGS, 20 iterations per penalty value), SQP-GS 1.1, and BFGS-SQP (GRANSO), with Mosek 7 as the QP solver (p. 24). The authors state that SNOPT, the only compiled code, "has a significant advantage in terms of speed" (p. 24).
  - Stopping: all tolerances are set to machine precision or zero violation, with 500 iterations, to "decouple" the evaluation from each code's stopping rule (pp. 22, 24).
  - Starting points: all runs start at the feasible origin. 20% of the problems were rerun from random infeasible starts, with "minor differences" and the details omitted (p. 24).
  - Budgets: per-problem budgets come from BFGS-SQP's time, with β ∈ {1, 5, 10, ∞}. An average time per iterate serves as cost proxy because SQP-GS and HANSO give no per-iterate timing (p. 24).
  - Spectral set (Fig. 5, pp. 25–26): at ∞-RMP, SQP-GS finds 78% of the best optimizers against BFGS-SQP's 22%, but takes 26.6 times longer. At β = 10, 5 and 1 the split is 55/45, 40/59 and 7/92. Panels relative to the faster SNOPT and SFPP are omitted but summarized: BFGS-SQP still finds about 70% and 86% at β = 1 (p. 26).
  - Pseudospectral set (Fig. 6, p. 26): BFGS-SQP finds 59% against SQP-GS's 34% even at β = ∞, while being 14.4 times faster.
  - Ablation (§6.4, Fig. 7, pp. 27–28): BFGS-SQP was rerun with condition-number caps 10^{2j}. Any regularization hurts on the spectral set; moderate caps (10⁶–10⁸) help on the pseudospectral set; the effect is called "problem dependent".
  - Single-problem plots: iteration plots of objective and violation and the final spectral configurations precede the benchmark (Figs. 1–4, pp. 12–16).
  - Reproducibility: code and test set are public (p. 25). MPBNGC was excluded with a stated reason: no Matlab interface and no iterate history (note 2, p. 29).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Algorithm: continues Lewis–Overton [35] (BFGS for nonsmooth problems), the authors' own SQP-GS [13], and the steering rules of Byrd–Nocedal–Waltz [11] and Byrd–Lopez-Calva–Nocedal [12] (pp. 3, 6). It is careful about [35]: their argument that ill-conditioning is beneficial is set against SQP-GS's eigenvalue bounds (p. 3), and this contrast is tested in §6.4 (p. 27).
  - Benchmarking: the paper praises performance profiles before criticizing them ("The ingenuity of performance profiles", p. 16; "A beautiful property", p. 20). It draws on Moré–Wild's data profiles and on Birgin et al. for the arbitrariness of success criteria (pp. 4–5, 17).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The contributions are "threefold": algorithm, test set, RMPs (p. 4).
  - The absence of guarantees is admitted in the abstract (p. 2).
  - Results language is strong: BFGS-SQP "completely outclasses all the other methods" at β = 1 (p. 26), a "remarkable outcome" (p. 26), and "completely outperform" in the conclusion (p. 28). It sits next to an honest report of SQP-GS's wins at β = ∞ on the spectral set (pp. 25–26).
  - Methodological prescriptions are written as general advice to evaluators (p. 18).
  - Transferable technique: report the rival's best case first and with numbers (78% vs 22%, 26.6× time), then show the budgets at which the ranking flips (pp. 25–26).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution has three layers: algorithm and software (GRANSO), test set, and evaluation methodology (p. 4). Limits stated:
  - no convergence guarantees (pp. 2, 9);
  - small problem dimensions forced by SQP-GS's cost (p. 23);
  - one application domain, with extension to other problem classes left "for future work" (p. 28);
  - the effect of regularization is unexplained, and "more investigation into this matter would be a worthwhile pursuit" (p. 28);
  - the β-RMP cost axis is only a coarse approximation (pp. 21–22).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ evidence: steering is moved into nonsmooth BFGS. The SFPP contrast is drawn in prose: under a fixed schedule the penalty "simply remained too high for too long", while BFGS-SQP "immediately made progress towards finding feasible points" (pp. 6–8, 12). Method 4 ✅ evidence: performance and data profiles are audited for their binary success criterion, and RMPs are built to show the objective, feasibility and budget trade-off (pp. 4–5, 16–22). Method 5 ✅ evidence: the compiled-code advantage of SNOPT is stated (p. 24); extra panels use the faster rivals' budgets (p. 26); the rival's wins are printed (pp. 25–26); there is a one-component regularization sweep (pp. 26–28). ⚠ The parameters c_v and c_μ are given without a tuning account (p. 24), and the 20% infeasible-start check is summarized without data (p. 24). Method 3 ⚠ variant: hard instances come from a randomized application generator chosen "for their challenging properties" (p. 4, pp. 23–24), not from mechanical infeasible or degenerate variants of a standard set. Heuristic 5 is evidenced: no guarantees, with the guarantee-carrying SQP-GS kept as comparator (pp. 2, 26). Heuristic 8 is evidenced: single-run iteration plots before the benchmark (Figs. 1 and 3, pp. 12, 15). New-pattern candidate: "budget-sliced comparison", i.e. rerun the comparison at budgets equal to multiples of the new method's own per-problem cost, so a slower but better rival is judged at equal cost (β-RMPs, pp. 19–22, 25–26).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Steering for a nonsmooth penalty method: compute the μ = 0 reference QP direction only when the current direction's predicted violation reduction is below c_v v(x_k), and shrink μ until the direction achieves a c_v fraction of the reference reduction (Procedure 1, p. 8).
- RMP protocol: run every code with tolerances at machine precision, record full iterate histories, define targets as the best feasible values found across codes, and plot the fraction within relative difference γ on a log axis, including γ = ∞ (pp. 18, 22–24).
- β-RMP panel: per-problem budget = the new method's own cost. Always include β = 1 and β = ∞, add a few intermediate values, and re-set the targets for each β (pp. 21–22).
- When one rival is much faster (SNOPT, SFPP), add panels whose budgets come from *that* rival (p. 26).

- "the fact remains that SQP-GS actually took 26.6 times longer to run to obtain these better results" (p. 26)
