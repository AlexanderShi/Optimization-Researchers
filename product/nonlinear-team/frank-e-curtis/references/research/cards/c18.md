### S020 · Trust-region Newton-CG with strong second-order complexity guarantees for nonconvex optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2021, DOI 10.1137/19M130563X; arXiv:1912.04365)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–22 read (abstract, §§1–7, every lemma and proof, Algorithms 1–4, acknowledgments). References pp. 22–25 skimmed. Figure 1 (p. 23) is an image: only its caption and the prose about it (pp. 21–22) could be read; the profile curves were not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel P. Robinson (Lehigh), Clément W. Royer (Paris-Dauphine), Stephen J. Wright (UW-Madison) · **Place in trajectory**: follows TRACE (ref. [11], cited p. 3 and used as a comparator, p. 21) and brings the Royer–Wright line-search Newton-CG complexity machinery ([29, 30], p. 3) into the classical trust-region Newton-CG (Steihaug–Toint) framework. It sits in his 2014–2024 "complexity of practical methods" line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is unconstrained min f(x) with f twice Lipschitz continuously differentiable and possibly nonconvex (eq. (1), p. 1). The target is an (ε_g, ε_H)-stationary point (eq. (3), p. 2). The paper enters by naming a gap: methods with the best complexity bounds "have often been designed primarily with complexity guarantees in mind and, as a result, represent a departure from the algorithms that have proved to be the most effective in practice" (abstract, p. 1). Other schemes "have not demonstrated such good performance in practice against a state-of-the-art trust-region Newton-CG algorithm" ([1, 6], p. 2). The question is therefore whether the workhorse, trust-region Newton-CG, can be given the best known iteration and operation complexity with "fairly minor modifications" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: frame a complexity paper as "make the method practitioners already use reach the best known bound" and not "design a new method with a better bound". Commit in the introduction to checking that the changes do not degrade the measures practitioners care about (iterations, function, gradient evaluations; p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Two methods are given. Algorithm 1 (exact) solves a trust-region subproblem whose model is regularized by ½ε_H‖s‖² (eq. (6), p. 5). The minimum eigenvalue is computed only for the termination test, when ‖g_k‖ ≤ ε_g, and "does not influence the iterate sequence" (p. 4). The rest is "identical to a traditional trust-region Newton method" (p. 4). One rule is not classical: after a rejected step the radius becomes γ1‖s_k‖ (line 20, p. 5). Algorithm 4 (inexact) uses a truncated CG (Algorithm 2, p. 12) on the model regularized by 2ε (eq. (26), p. 10). Three things differ from Steihaug–Toint (pp. 10–11). (i) The residual test is ‖(H+2εI)s+g‖ ≤ (ζ/2) min{‖g‖, ‖s‖} (eq. (27)), which borrows the ‖s‖ term from optimal-complexity Newton methods. (ii) CG exits on curvature ≤ ε for H+2εI, that is, curvature ≤ −ε for H, and moves to the boundary (flag bnd-neg). (iii) An optional iteration cap kmax = min{n, ½√κ ln(4κ^{3/2}/ζ)} is derived from CG convergence bounds (line 4, p. 12). CG returns one of four termination flags (bnd-neg, bnd-norm, int-res, int-max), and these flags decide whether a randomized minimum-eigenvalue oracle (MEO, Algorithm 3, p. 14) is called. It is called only on int-max, or on int-res with ‖g_k‖ ≤ ε_g (lines 8–11, p. 15). Implementation Strategy 4.1 stores a negative-curvature direction found by the MEO and reuses it until the next successful iteration (p. 18). In practice the MEO is randomized Lanczos with an adaptive stop (λ_{l−t} − λ_l ≤ 10⁻⁵), and CG uses n̄ = min{n+2, 1.2n} in place of n because of loss of conjugacy (p. 20).
  - Transferable: let the inner iterative solver return a typed exit flag, and route the outer method's expensive safeguard (here the eigenvalue oracle) only on the flags whose meaning the analysis needs. A cap exceeded is itself a certificate (Lemma 7: if the cap is exceeded then H ≼ −εI, p. 11).
  - Transferable: present an exact-subproblem variant first and prove its complexity. Then build the inexact variant so that each lemma has an inexact counterpart with changed constants (Lemmas 1→9, 2→10, 3→11, 4→12, 5→13; pp. 6–18).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1: {f_k} is bounded below, and f is C² with Lipschitz gradient (L_g) and Hessian (L_H) on an open set containing the segments [x_k, x_k+s_k] (p. 3). Assumption 2: the MEO needs at most min{n, 1 + ⌈C_meo ε_H^{−1/2}⌉} Hessian-vector products, with C_meo logarithmic in the failure probability ξ (p. 14). Randomized Lanczos and CG on a random right-hand side satisfy it ([29], p. 14). Implementation Strategy 4.1 is called a "common-sense rule" and is assumed for the probabilistic results (p. 18). The authors admit that the regularization is there for the analysis: it "makes for a relatively straightforward complexity analysis", with the "possible downside" that practical behavior may depend on the tolerance ε_H, "which is not the case for a traditional trust-region framework" (p. 4). The factor 2 in (26) is "intentional", for consistency in the termination condition (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Model decrease ≥ ½ε_H‖s_k‖² follows from the trust-region optimality conditions (9) (Lemma 1, p. 6). For CG steps the bound comes from monotone CG model decrease and, at a negative-curvature exit, from the conjugacy and orthogonality identities (31a–d) (Lemma 8, pp. 11–13). (b) Small steps succeed: rejection implies ‖s_k‖ > 3(1−η)ε_H/L_H, which gives a radius floor δ_min (Lemma 2, p. 6). (c) Decrease per successful interior step: a quadratic inequality in ‖s_k‖ from the Hessian-Lipschitz bound (4b), turned into a min{‖g_{k+1}‖ε_H^{−1}, ε_H} lower bound by the scalar inequality (12) taken from Royer–Wright (Lemma 3, p. 7). (d) Counting: the successful iterations are split into S_L (small gradient, which forces a boundary step), S_GG and S_GL, with |S_GL| ≤ |S_L| + 1 (Lemma 4, p. 8). Unsuccessful runs are bounded through geometric shrinkage of the radius against the floor (Lemma 5, pp. 8–9). The result is Theorem 1: |S| = O(max{ε_H^{−3}, ε_H^{−1}, ε_g^{−2}ε_H}), which is O(ε_g^{−3/2}) for ε_H = ε_g^{1/2} (p. 10). (e) Operation complexity: CG on a matrix with condition number κ reaches (27) within J(M,ε,ζ) = Õ(ε^{−1/2}) iterations (Lemma 6, p. 11). Theorem 3 gives min{n, ε_g^{−1/4}}·Õ(ε_g^{−3/2}) Hessian-vector products, that is Õ(ε_g^{−7/4}) (pp. 19–20). (f) Probability: induction on the number of MEO calls, with P_k ≤ ξ(1 − Σ P_i), gives success with probability ≥ (1−ξ)^{K̄_S} (Theorem 2, pp. 18–19). The acknowledgments credit Yue Xie with "most of the proof of Theorem 2" (p. 22).
  - Transferable: to bound the iterations of a nonconvex method to a second-order point, partition the successful iterations by gradient size before and after the step (S_L, S_GG, S_GL). The awkward "gradient becomes small after this step" class is then charged to the next S_L iteration (p. 8).
  - Transferable: prove a probability-of-success bound for a randomized certificate by induction on the number of oracle calls. Use an implementation rule (reuse the stored direction) to cap the calls by the number of successful iterations (pp. 18–19).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Seven Matlab variants (p. 21): TR-Newton (Algorithm 1 with Moré–Sorensen), TR-Newton-CG-explicit (capped), TR-Newton-CG (uncapped), each also run without the ε_H regularization ("no reg."), plus TRACE [11]. The uncapped no-reg variant is "the most similar to traditional trust-region Newton-CG with Steihaug-Toint stopping rules" (p. 21). Parameters: γ1 = γ2⁻¹ = 0.5, ψ = 0.75, η = 0.1, ζ = 0.25, δmax = 10²⁰, δ0 = 10, regularization 2ε_H for all six (p. 21). Test set: CUTEst (downloaded 1 July 2020), each problem at the size option closest to [100, 1000], 233 problems; only the 109 with n ≥ 100 are reported (p. 21). The filter is by size and set in advance, not by outcome. Measures: performance profiles of iterations, gradient evaluations and Hessian-vector products, with τ capped at 10, for (ε_g, ε_H) = (10⁻⁵, 10^{−5/2}) and (10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁵) (pp. 21, 23). Limits: 10⁴ iterations and 10⁴n Hessian-vector products (p. 22). Every algorithm solved at least 101 of the 109 problems at both tolerances, "a reliability of about 93%" (p. 22). Iterations and gradient evaluations are similar. The no-reg variants use fewer Hessian-vector products, and the paper says so and notes they lack the guarantees (p. 22). How much this matters depends on the cost of a gradient relative to a Hessian-vector product (p. 22). The MEO was called only at the last iteration on all but three problems (p. 22). No CPU times are reported, and TRACE's showing is not discussed in the prose (p. 22).
  - Transferable: ablate the theory-motivated modification itself. Run each variant with and without the term the proof needs, report the measure where it hurts (Hessian-vector products) next to those where it does not, and state the cost ratio at which the difference matters (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: log how often the expensive safeguard actually fires (the MEO was called only at the last iteration on all but three problems, p. 22) as evidence that the worst-case device is cheap in practice.
- **D6 Positioning**: The literature review is short and covers four lines (pp. 3–4). Cubic regularization reaches O(ε_g^{−3/2}) (Nesterov–Polyak, Cartis–Gould–Toint). Agarwal et al. [1] and Carmon et al. [6] reach Õ(ε_g^{−7/4}) operations with high probability, and Carmon et al. [5] reach it deterministically for first-order points. Royer–Wright line search [29, 30] reaches the same bounds. On trust regions: O(ε_g^{−2}) [19], shown sharp [8]; TRACE [11] matches cubic regularization; trust-region and cubic hybrids [12, 14, 15, 2, 3]. Trust-region subproblem solvers are also reviewed ([16, 20, 33, 4, 18], p. 4). The paper says its method is "inspired in part by the line search method proposed in [30]" (p. 4), and its results "correspond to the results obtained for the line search method in [30, Theorem 5, Theorem 6]" (p. 10). It sets aside the hard case: it "does not present a serious challenge" because CG or the MEO handles it (p. 4). Prior complexity-first methods are criticised in one sentence, for not having shown practical performance against trust-region Newton-CG (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution sentence comes early: "Our chief contribution is to show that a trust-region Newton-CG method can be modified to have state-of-the-art operation complexity properties" (p. 2). Admissions are candid and specific: the downside of the regularization (p. 4); two "unsavory" MEO failure modes, one of which can stop the method at a point that is not even ε_g-stationary (p. 20); and a choice made "for simplicity of description at the expense of a (small) probability of failure" (p. 20). The conclusion is softer than the data: "the practical effects of including these modifications can be relatively minor" (p. 22), while the same section reports a "noticeable effect on practical performance, mostly in terms of Hessian-vector products" (p. 21). Theorems are followed by plain-language remarks, for example after Theorem 1 (p. 10) and Theorem 3 (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory and algorithm: the first trust-region Newton-CG with O(ε^{−3/2}) iterations and Õ(ε^{−7/4}) Hessian-vector-product complexity to (ε, ε^{1/2})-points (Theorems 1–3, pp. 10, 18–19; conclusion p. 22). Limits stated: the MEO can fail with probability ξ per call (p. 20); the regularization costs Hessian-vector products (p. 22); the explicit cap is "triggered only in rare cases" (p. 20). Future direction: an implicit CG cap [29] would give a deterministic Õ(ε_g^{−7/4}) bound for ε_g-stationary points, but "the resulting method is significantly more delicate to implement" (p. 22). Limits visible but not stated: problems are of moderate size (n ≥ 100, default sizes near [100, 1000]); there are no timings; and the claim of matching practical behavior rests on iteration and gradient profiles.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): the paper measures what its own complexity device costs. The no-reg variants beat the guaranteed ones in Hessian-vector products, "recall that these variants do not possess optimal complexity guarantees" (p. 22). The regularization is called a convenience for the analysis with a practical downside (p. 4). This is the Taste warning "worst-case complexity sold as practical gain" applied to his own method, though the conclusion's "relatively minor" (p. 22) softens it (Inner Tension 1).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅): all variants share one Matlab framework, and exactly one component is toggled (the ε_H regularization; cap on or off) (p. 21). The in-house TRACE [11] is the rival with optimal complexity, and the incumbent's win in Hessian-vector products is printed (p. 22). Against Method 5: no external production trust-region Newton-CG code and no timings.
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the inner CG stopping rule (27), the negative-curvature threshold and the cap come from what the outer complexity argument needs: decrease ≥ ¼ε_H‖s‖² and a gradient bound at the next iterate (Lemma 11, pp. 16–17). The "outer need" here is a worst-case decrease inequality, not a merit-function model reduction (pp. 10–11).
- Heuristic 10 (evidence, in the card only): the exact-subproblem twin is analyzed first and "sets the stage" for the inexact one (pp. 4, 13).
- new-pattern candidate: **Equip the workhorse**. Make minimal changes to the method practitioners already use so that it reaches the best known complexity, then ablate those changes to show what they cost (pp. 2, 4, 21–22). See also TRACE in the SKILL's Signature Work Anatomy.
- new-pattern candidate: **Typed inner-solver exits route the outer safeguard**. CG flags bnd-neg, bnd-norm, int-res and int-max decide when to call the eigenvalue oracle, and an exceeded cap is a curvature certificate (pp. 10–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Partition successful iterations into S_L, S_GG and S_GL, with |S_GL| ≤ |S_L| + 1, to get second-order iteration bounds (p. 8).
- Use the scalar inequality −a + √(a² + bt) ≥ (−a + √(a² + b)) min{t, 1} to turn a quadratic lower bound on ‖s‖ into a decrease bound of min-type (p. 7).
- Reuse a rejected negative-curvature direction until the next success. This caps oracle calls by |S| and gives the (1−ξ)^{K̄_S} success probability (pp. 18–19).
- Ablate the analysis-motivated term in every variant and report the measure it inflates (pp. 21–22).

**Quotes**:
- "The variants with no regularization term in the subproblems outperform the others in this respect; recall that these variants do not possess optimal complexity guarantees." (p. 22)
- "we expect the “cap” of Algorithm 2 to be triggered only in rare cases, due to the conservative nature of the CG convergence bounds that gave rise to this cap." (p. 20)

### S037 · Inexact sequential quadratic optimization for minimizing a stochastic objective function subject to deterministic nonlinear equality constraints (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv:2107.03512)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–25 read (cover sheet, abstract, §§1–5, all lemmas and proofs, Algorithm 1, Tables 1–2). References pp. 25–27 skimmed. Figure 1 (box plots, p. 23) is an image: only its caption and the prose were read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel P. Robinson, Baoyu Zhou (all Lehigh ISE, p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: the inexact rung of the stochastic SQP line. It extends the exact stochastic SQP of Berahas–Curtis–Robinson–Zhou [3] and the rank-deficient variant [2] (p. 3) with the inexact SMART-style termination tests of his 2008–2010 inexact SQP work ([6, 7, 12], p. 7). The batch list keeps it separate from S045 (INFORMS J. Optim. 2024); the file read is COR@L TR 21T-015 = arXiv v1 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is min f(x) = E[F(x, ω)] s.t. c(x) = 0, with deterministic smooth constraints and only stochastic objective gradients (eq. (1), p. 2). It is motivated by a list of applications: PDE-aware CNNs, physics-constrained learning, reduced-order PDE inverse problems, portfolio selection, optimal power flow, and constrained MLE (pp. 2–3). The entry is a gap: only three stochastic methods [2, 3, 24] guarantee convergence for the constrained problem, and "All of these methods must factorize a matrix during each iteration, which may not be tractable for large-scale problems" (p. 3). Identifying inexactness conditions for SQO is described as "a challenging task with few solutions" ([4, 6, 7, 18, 19], p. 3).
  - Transferable: open by listing what every existing method in the new setting still requires (here a factorization per iteration), and make dropping that requirement the contribution (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The step is decomposed (p. 4). The normal step v_k ∈ Range(J_kᵀ) only needs to satisfy a Cauchy-decrease condition (6) on ½‖c_k + J_k v‖², so any Krylov method that stays in the range space qualifies (p. 5). The tangential and dual pair (u_k, δ_k) comes from an iterative solve of the KKT system (11) that uses the stochastic gradient g_k, with residuals (ρ_k, r_k) (12) (p. 6). Two termination tests decide when the inner solve may stop (p. 7). Termination Test 1 requires residual bounds relative to the current and previous KKT residuals (14), residual bounds proportional to the step-size sequence, ‖ρ_k‖ ≤ κ_ρβ_k and ‖r_k‖ ≤ κ_rβ_k (15), a tangential-step condition (16), and the model reduction condition (13) at the current merit parameter. Termination Test 2 replaces (13) by a linearized-feasibility condition (17), and the merit parameter τ is then updated by (18)–(19) so that (13) holds. The merit function is φ = τf + ‖c‖₂, and the choice of norm "is not essential" (p. 5). The step size can be anything in [α_k^min, α_k^max] (pp. 8–9). α_k^min uses an adaptively estimated ratio ξ_k = ∆l/(τ‖d‖²) (eq. (23)), and α_k^max = min{α_k^φ, α_k^min + θβ_k²} caps the excursion that stochastic gradients may cause (eq. (28)). Dual updates may be any y_{k+1} that does not increase the dual residual (29) (p. 9).
  - Transferable: bound the inner-solve residuals by a multiple of the outer step-size sequence β_k (15). The inexactness error then enters the analysis at the same order as the stochastic step-size terms, O(β_k) in bias and O(β_k²) after multiplication by α_k (Lemmas 11–14, pp. 15–19).
  - Transferable: define the admissible step size as an interval [α^min, α^max] with α^max − α^min ≤ θβ_k². The implementation can then take longer steps (here up to (1.1)^t·α^suff) without leaving the analysis (pp. 9, 21–22).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1: f and c are C¹ with Lipschitz, bounded derivatives on an open convex set containing the iterates, and the singular values of J are uniformly bounded below, i.e. LICQ everywhere (pp. 4–5). The authors note that X need not be bounded (p. 5). Assumption 2: unbiased gradients with bounded variance M_g (p. 6). Assumption 3: H_k is independent of g_k, bounded, and uniformly positive definite on Null(J_k) (p. 6). Assumption 4: the inner iterative solver converges to the exact solution (p. 10). Assumption 5: c_k ≠ 0 or g_k ∉ Range(J_kᵀ), justified as "unlikely" to fail for a stochastic gradient; two practical remedies are offered: resample, or add Gaussian noise (p. 10). Assumption 6: bounded duals and residuals, called "mild" (p. 13). The convergence theorem is conditioned on the event E_τ,low: τ eventually constant and small enough, ξ_k bounded below (eq. (38), p. 18). The other two merit-parameter events (τ → 0, or τ too large) are not analyzed, on the ground that "for practical purposes, they can be ignored for the same reasons as are claimed in [3]" (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Well-posedness by cases on ‖c_k‖ (Lemma 1, pp. 10–11). If c_k ≠ 0, Termination Test 2 eventually holds. If c_k = 0, Termination Test 1 eventually holds, using u_*ᵀ(g + Hv) + ½u_*ᵀHu_* < 0 from (31) (p. 11). (b) Step-size interval: the strongly convex function φ(α) (26), built from the piecewise-quadratic merit upper bound (21), satisfies φ(0) = φ(α^φ) = 0. So any α ∈ [α^min, α^max] ⊆ [0, α^φ] gives φ(α) ≤ 0 (Lemma 4, pp. 12–13). (c) A deterministic floor ξ_min for the stochastic ratio sequence ξ_k, from ∆l ≥ κ_lτ(‖u‖² + ‖c‖) and ‖d‖² ≤ ω_4(‖u‖² + ‖c‖) (Lemmas 7–9, p. 14). (d) Null-space formulas (34)–(35) for u_k − u_k^true, with the projector bound ‖I − Z(ZᵀHZ)⁻¹ZᵀH‖ ≤ 1 from "eigenvalues of AB equal those of BA" (eq. (36), p. 15). (e) Bias and variance bounds: ‖E_k[u_k − u_k^true]‖ ≤ ω_5β_k (Lemma 11, pp. 15–16) and |E_k[∇fᵀd^true − gᵀd]| ≤ ω_6β_k + ω_7β_k√M_g + ζ⁻¹M_g (Lemma 12, pp. 16–17). (f) Splitting on the sign of ∇fᵀ(d_k − d_k^true), using α^max − α^min ≤ θβ_k², controls the correlation between the random step size and the random direction (Lemma 14, pp. 18–19). (g) Theorem 1, as for SG: with constant β_k the expected average of the stationarity measure ∆l(x, τ, ∇f, d^true) is O(β); with Σβ = ∞ and Σβ² < ∞ the weighted average tends to 0 (pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable: when the step size is random and correlated with the search direction, condition on the sign of the error term. Bound the positive part with α^max, the negative part with α^min, and pay only the gap θβ_k² (Lemma 14, pp. 18–19).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab (p. 21). Normal step by CG on J_kᵀJ_k v = −J_kᵀc_k to a relative 0.1 tolerance. Tangential step by MINRES (the [10, 27] implementation) until (46) and a termination test hold (p. 21). The two arms differ only in the MINRES tolerance κ: SISQO (κ = 0.1 on CUTEst, 10⁻⁴ on control) and SISQO exact (κ = 10⁻⁷). Both use the same normal step "since it limits the variation attributable to the different calculations" (p. 22). The comparison is matched on work. SISQO stops when ‖c‖∞ ≤ 10⁻⁶ and the stationarity measure with least-squares multipliers is ≤ 10⁻². SISQO exact gets SISQO's total MINRES count as its budget, and its best feasible iterate is chosen afterwards (p. 22). CUTEst: 138 equality-constrained problems were cut to 11 by four filters, two of which depend on outcomes: "the objective function remained above −10^50 over the sequences of iterates generated by runs of our algorithm" and LICQ at every iterate of every run (p. 23). Noise g_k ~ N(∇f_k, (ε_N²/n)I) with ε_N ∈ {10⁻⁴, 10⁻², 10⁻¹} (p. 23). L and Γ are estimated each iteration by finite differences at a random nearby point. The Hessian of the Lagrangian is modified by convex combination with I (ι_k = 10^{−j}) until no modification is triggered (p. 23). Five runs per problem are shown as box plots (p. 23). Two optimal control problems are adapted from Hintermüller–Ito–Kunisch to equality constraints and finite-sum objectives, with N = 3, ten runs, and Tables 1–2 (pp. 24–25). SISQO needs more outer iterations (e.g. 8.9 vs 6.9) but reaches lower feasibility error at the same MINRES count (e.g. 2.41×10⁻⁷ vs 3.86×10⁻⁵) (Table 1, p. 25). The text says SISQO attains "better average feasibility and stationarity errors" (p. 24). At ε_N = 10⁻² in Table 1, however, SISQO exact's stationarity error is lower (1.95×10⁻³ vs 2.09×10⁻³; p. 25).
  - Transferable: compare an inexact and an accurate variant at equal inner-solver work. Give the accurate arm the inexact arm's total Krylov-iteration count as a budget and judge both by quality measures computed outside the algorithm (least-squares multipliers) (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: Penalty and augmented-Lagrangian methods are contrasted with SQO. Line-search SQO is called "state-of-the-art for solving deterministic equality constrained optimization problems" (p. 3). The paper places itself as the inexact extension of [2, 3], and contrasts with Na–Anitescu–Kolar [24], which uses an exact augmented Lagrangian merit function (p. 3). The termination tests are "inspired by the sufficient merit approximation reduction termination tests developed in [6, 7, 12]" (p. 7). Lemma 5 is borrowed from [12, Lemma 3.5] with "small straightforward modifications" (p. 13). The result is called "comparable" to [3] and to SG [5] (p. 25). External baselines are not run; the only rival is the in-framework exact arm (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contributions are enumerated (i)–(iii) on pp. 3–4. The paper repeatedly defends its assumptions: Assumption 5 "we contend that it is unlikely" (p. 10), Assumption 6 "mild" (p. 13), the excluded events "we contend that, for practical purposes, they can be ignored" (p. 17). Each condition comes with a short motivation paragraph, for example the derivation of α^suff from (21) (p. 8). Remarks marked "for purposes of our analysis only" separate analysis quantities (d^true, τ^true,trial) from computed ones (pp. 6, 18). The conclusion is two sentences of summary with no future directions (pp. 24–25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and theoretical: the first inexact (matrix-free) stochastic SQO with convergence in expectation matching [3] and SG (Theorem 1, p. 20; conclusion p. 25). Limits stated or visible: the theory covers only E_τ,low; the other events are dismissed by reference to [3] (p. 17). Assumption 1 requires full-rank Jacobians everywhere (p. 5). The test set is small and filtered on outcomes, 11 problems with n + m ∈ [500, 10000] (p. 23). L and Γ are estimated, not known (p. 23). No complexity bound is given. No future work is stated (pp. 24–25).

**Method links**:

### S048 · Practical optimal control of a wave-energy converter in regular wave environments (Renewable Energy 2021, DOI 10.1016/j.renene.2021.02.134; arXiv:2012.08049)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–24 read (abstract, §§1–6, Appendix A on the functional form of the control cost). References pp. 19–21 skimmed. Figures 2–18 are images: only captions and prose were read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Mertcan Yetkin (first and corresponding author, Lehigh ISE), Sudharsan Kalidoss and Arindam Banerjee (Lehigh Mechanical Engineering), Lawrence V. Snyder (Lehigh ISE); Curtis is third of five authors (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an applied, multi-department paper (NSF CMMI-1400164 and CCF-1442858, p. 19). In the SKILL's publication survey it is one of the "Large multi-institution or applied teams" papers (01-publications). The voice is the team's; nothing in the text attributes a specific part to Curtis.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is time-domain optimal control of a single two-body heaving point absorber (Muliawan et al.) (pp. 1–3). The entry is a modeling flaw in the literature: "little attention has been given to some issues that arise when employing control strategies in practice, such as the cost of applying a control force, inefficiencies in the power conversion system, and long-term damage to the device" (p. 2). The flaw is shown on a toy case. With no incident wave (F_ex ≡ 0), the standard model (11)–(14) has an optimal objective ≈ 10⁵, "a strictly positive energy extraction" from no wave, so models that omit the control cost overstate energy and can even predict gains where there are losses (p. 6).
  - Transferable: test a model on the input where the true answer is known trivially (here: no wave, so no net energy). A nonzero optimum exposes a missing term in the objective (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The contribution is the formulation; no new algorithm is proposed. The basic discrete-time problem uses forward-Euler dynamics (13), a trapezoidal energy objective (11) and a hard force bound (14) (p. 5). Four things are added to it. (i) A soft bound: a penalty ρ(|u| − γη)₊ written linearly with auxiliary α_k (eqs. (16), (20)–(21), pp. 5, 7). (ii) A displacement bound (17) (p. 6). (iii) A control-cost term λ1u² (p. 7, derived in Appendix A). (iv) A smoothness term λ2(Δu)² (eq. (18), p. 7). The complete model is (19)–(21) (p. 7). The paper stresses that "our contribution lies in the modeling and solution approach, rather than in the specific form used for this cost" (p. 7). The acceleration term is approximated by ξz(t), using the negative correlation of position and acceleration under a sinusoid (eq. (9), p. 4). System coefficients A, b, c come from a three-step least-squares identification on WEC-Sim runs: free decay for A, free floating for c, then sinusoidal control policies over all phase shifts for b (pp. 8–9). Real-time use is by receding horizon (Algorithm 1, p. 10), with the wave-prediction step skipped for regular waves (p. 10).
  - Transferable: identify a linear surrogate's coefficient blocks in sequence, each from its own experiment (free decay → A; free floating → c; controlled → b), so that each block is fitted with the others fixed (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linear wave theory, no viscous drag (p. 3). Mooring force negligible; an "ideal" power take-off that "can exert any desired force within some limits" (p. 4). Regular waves only, which removes wave prediction and coefficient updates (pp. 10, 19). A quadratic control cost "as a proxy", calibrated from Klamo's magnetic-damping data and a regression on Muliawan's power data (pp. 6–7, 21–24). The same component takes power out and feeds it in, and λ1 also absorbs conversion inefficiency (p. 8). Admitted: the cost may have another form for other power take-off systems (p. 7), and coefficients for a new wave period are "not so clear" to obtain (p. 8). The abstract says the discretization "is a quadratic optimization problem that can be solved efficiently" (p. 1). The conclusion says the QP has "a non-convex objective function" (p. 18). It is solved locally by IPOPT (p. 11), with multi-start agreement offered as a hint of global optimality (p. 13).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a, the paper has no theorems or proofs. Its analytical content is modeling derivation: the approximation of the acceleration term (p. 4), the linearization of the soft-bound penalty (p. 7), and the order-of-magnitude chain b ↑ ⇒ ż² ~ O(1/b) ⇒ |F| ~ O(√b) ~ O(√P) ⇒ P ~ O(F²) from a fitted model ż² = 1330.2/(b + 9158.7), chosen by largest R² among exponential, logarithmic and polynomial families (Appendix A, pp. 22–24).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The models are written in AMPL and solved with IPOPT. All reported runs use the 6 m / 4 s wave. Runs on other waves were made but not shown, "since the main conclusions are similar" (p. 11). Section 5.1 chooses λ1 and λ2 by a physical criterion: the smallest values for which the average period of the velocity is within 5% of the wave period (λ1 = 10⁻⁷, λ2 = 10⁻⁹ for this wave). The largest values over all waves (10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁶) are then fixed, because "in a realistic setting, the parameters λ1 and λ2 are likely to be fixed" (p. 11). A misspecification experiment follows: with true λ* = (10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁶), it plots f_λ*(x(λ))/f_λ*(x(λ*)). Underestimating the cost coefficients can make the ratio negative; overestimating keeps it positive (Figure 7, p. 13). Section 5.2 covers a 50 × 50 grid of (η, ρ) (2500 instances), all solved to local optimality. Each instance is solved from several initial points with "the same optimal solution" (p. 13). The objective decreases with ρ and with smaller η (Figure 8, pp. 13–14). CPU time roughly quadruples as η → 0 (Figure 9, p. 14). Section 5.3 runs the receding horizon (T = wave period, Δt = 0.01, T_c = period/10, K = 100) under three (η, ρ) settings. For η = 1 the solve time is about 0.14 s against an update horizon of 0.4 s, so real time holds (p. 16). For η = 0.1 it is about 0.5 s, not real time (pp. 16, 18). No other control strategy (latching, phase control, MPC from the literature) is run as a baseline. Latching and phase control are argued against in text: "too simplistic" (p. 6).
  - Transferable: calibrate an unobservable cost weight by a physical consistency criterion, the smallest weight that keeps the controlled system's period within 5% of the forcing period. For robustness across conditions, take the largest such weight over the conditions, and justify the asymmetry with a misspecification-ratio plot (pp. 11–13).
  - Transferable: when a nonconvex model is solved by a local solver, report multi-start agreement as evidence for, not proof of, global optimality (p. 13).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself among constrained optimal control and MPC methods for wave-energy converters: Falnes, Hals et al., Wang et al., Zou et al., Bacelli et al., Cretel et al., Richter et al.'s NMPC, and Nielsen et al. on losses and fatigue (p. 2). Its two-part claim is that the cost of control and device damage are under-modeled (p. 2), and that the cost is only "implicitly" accounted for in latching and phase control, which are "too simplistic" (p. 6). It cites Falnes for the need to feed energy back, which its negative-objective intervals confirm (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contributions are three bullets on p. 3. The rhetoric leans on a counterexample: the "energy from no wave" example is the core argument for the cost term (p. 6). Sanity checks are announced as such: "As a sanity check" and "As a further sanity check" (both p. 11). The limits are stated plainly in the conclusion (p. 19). There is an inconsistency between abstract and conclusion on convexity (pp. 1, 18), and typos ("Not that", p. 4; "the the", p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a model (control cost, hard and soft bounds) plus a real-time demonstration, at the application layer (pp. 18–19). Limits stated: regular waves only; irregular waves need prediction and coefficient updates. Control of multiple converters needs dynamic coefficient estimation. A better device-specific control-cost form is needed (p. 19). Limits visible: one wave shown out of three; no baseline controller; the "real-time" claim holds only for η = 1 (pp. 16, 18); no validation in WEC-Sim of the optimized control (only the regression trajectories are compared, pp. 9–10).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (variant ⚠): a degenerate instance (zero incident wave) is built on purpose to break the standard model (p. 6). The thing broken here is the problem formulation, not a solver.
- Method 4 (variant ⚠): the paper audits the objective used across the literature (energy captured without control cost) and shows it rewards infeasible physics (pp. 2, 6). The misspecification-ratio plot shows the hidden asymmetry (p. 13). This is a modeling yardstick, not an evaluation one.
- Method 5 (contradiction ✗): no rival controller is run; latching and phase control are dismissed by argument (p. 6), and results for two of the three waves are withheld (p. 11).
- new-pattern candidate: **Known-answer input as a model test**. Run the model where the physical answer is trivially zero (no forcing) to reveal a missing cost (p. 6).
- new-pattern candidate: **Physically anchored weight calibration**. Choose penalty weights by a measurable consistency criterion and take the conservative maximum across conditions, backed by an asymmetric misspecification study (pp. 11–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Zero-forcing sanity instance to expose a missing objective term (p. 6).
- Sequential least-squares identification of the surrogate dynamics, one coefficient block per experiment type (p. 8).
- Misspecification-ratio plot f_true(x(λ))/f_true(x(λ_true)) across a parameter sweep, to decide which way to err when choosing a weight (p. 13).
- Report solve time against the controller update horizon for each parameter regime, so that "real-time" is a measured claim with stated regime boundaries (pp. 14, 16, 18).

**Quotes**:
- "Any optimization model that does not incorporate the cost of control will suffer from this same sort of error." (p. 6)
- "We see from this experiment that it is arguably much worse to underestimate the cost coefficients than to overestimate them." (p. 13)

### S056 · An accelerated communication-efficient primal-dual optimization framework for structured machine learning (Optimization Methods and Software 2021, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2019.1650361; arXiv:1711.05305)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–18 read (abstract, §§1–5, Lemmas 1–3, Theorem 4 with full proof, Theorem 5, Corollary 6, §3.2, all experiment prose). Figures 1–6 read from the text layer (axis labels and legends only; curves not readable). References pp. 18–20 skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Chenxin Ma (first author, Lehigh ISE), Martin Jaggi (EPFL), Nathan Srebro (TTI-Chicago), Martin Takáč (Lehigh ISE); Curtis is third of five (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: part of the 2013– stochastic/ML line that started with Lehigh colleagues Scheinberg and Takáč (SKILL Research Trajectory). The work is funded by NSF CCF-1618717 (p. 1), the grant also acknowledged in S020 (p. 1). It is Takáč's group's CoCoA line; nothing in the text attributes a specific part to Curtis.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is the primal-dual pair (A) min f(Aα) + Σg_i(α_i) and (B) min f*(w) + Σg_i*(−A_iᵀw), which cover Lasso, SVM and logistic regression (pp. 1–2). The setting is distributed, where communication rounds dominate (pp. 1–2). The entry: CoCoA and CoCoA+ balance computation and communication but give only O(1/t) for the general convex case (p. 3). Accelerated single-machine methods reach O(1/t²) (p. 3). Generic acceleration through Catalyst is set aside because "it does not allow the local solver to produce randomized solutions which might only have sufficient quality in expectation" (p. 3). The nearest accelerated rivals [11, 5] need strongly convex subproblems (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a generic wrapper (Catalyst) would give the rate, say exactly which property of your setting it cannot handle (here randomized local solvers whose quality holds only in expectation), and build the structure-specific variant around that property (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: AccCoCoA+ (Algorithm 1, p. 6) adds Nesterov-style coupling sequences (y_t, z_t) and the scalar recursion θ_{t+1} = (√(γ²θ_t⁴ + 4θ_t²) − γθ_t²)/2, which is computed identically on every node. Only w_t = ∇f(Ay_t) is communicated each iteration (pp. 4–6). The local subproblem G_k (7) keeps CoCoA+'s data-dependent regularizer (λθ_tσ′/2)‖A(z − z_t)‖², with σ′ ≥ γ max‖Aα‖²/Σ‖Aα^[k]‖² measuring how strongly the partitions are correlated (eq. (8), p. 5). The choice σ′ = γK is "safe" (p. 5). Local solvers may be inexact, with additive error ε_t in expectation (Assumption 2, p. 5): "Our theory holds for arbitrary local solvers used on the worker machines" (p. 1).
  - Transferable: keep everything of the base framework (subproblem, partition-aware regularizer, one vector communicated per round) and add only scalar sequences that every node can update without communication (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1: f and g_i closed convex, ∇f Lipschitz with constant λ, each g_i* L-Lipschitz, and ‖A_i‖ ≤ 1 "without loss of generality" (p. 2). The same symbol λ is used for the regularization parameter in (2) (p. 2). The data are partitioned into disjoint blocks (p. 4). Inexactness: E[G_k(z_{t+1})] ≤ G_k(z*_{t+1}) + ε_t (eq. (9), p. 5). The analysis does not need strongly convex subproblems, "This is only possible due to the special structure of our objective function OA" (p. 8). L-Lipschitz g_i* implies dom g_i lies in a ball of radius L, which is used to bound R ≤ max_k 2Ln_k (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) The θ_t recursion: θ_t is positive and decreasing, with (1 − γθ_{t+1})/θ_{t+1}² = 1/θ_t² and θ_t ≤ 2/(tγ + 2), by induction on a quadratic in 1/θ (Lemma 1, pp. 6–7). (b) α_t is a convex combination Σρ_t^l z_l with explicit recursive weights (Lemma 2, p. 7). This defines the upper bound ĝ_t = Σρ_t^l g(z_l) ≥ g(α_t) by convexity (eq. (23), p. 9). (c) Each subproblem is strongly convex in the A-seminorm: G_k(u) ≥ G_k(u*) + (λθσ′/2)‖A(u − u*)‖² (Lemma 3, pp. 7–8). (d) Theorem 4 follows the randomized estimate-sequence argument of Fercoq–Richtárik [5], extended to subproblems that are not strongly convex. The Lyapunov quantity (1/θ²)φ_t + (γλσ′/2)r̃_t² is unrolled (pp. 8–13). The inexactness enters through C1 and C2, bounded by (29) and Jensen, which gives the error terms E_j in (22b) (pp. 11–12). (e) The suboptimality rate is converted to a duality-gap rate through Dünner et al. [4, Theorem 4] (Theorem 5, p. 13). (f) Corollary 6: γ = 1 gives T ≤ √(8L²/(λε)) iterations, against CoCoA+'s 8L²/(λε) (p. 14). Constants are tabulated in data-correlation quantities σ̃², σ_k², σ², R (Table 1, p. 14). (g) Inexact local solves: with ε_t = a_tθ_t, constant a_t gives O(1/t), and a summable a_t (e.g. r/t^p, p > 2) keeps O(1/t²) (§3.2, p. 15).
  - Transferable: to accelerate a method whose subproblems are not strongly convex, prove strong convexity in the seminorm induced by the data matrix (Lemma 3) and run the estimate-sequence argument in that seminorm. The ‖α* − α_0‖² term of standard accelerated bounds then becomes ‖A(α* − α_0)‖², which can be zero even when α_0 ≠ α* (pp. 7–8, 14).
  - Transferable: schedule the inner-solver error as ε_t = a_tθ_t and read off which summability condition on a_t preserves the accelerated rate (p. 15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: C++ with MPI on Amazon EC2 m3.xlarge instances, K = 4 nodes, SDCA as local solver (p. 16). Code on GitHub (p. 4). Datasets: url, rcv1.test, covtype, epsilon (Table 2, p. 16). news20 also appears in Figures 1 and 4 (pp. 17, 19) but is not in Table 2. Comparisons are against CoCoA+ only, at γ ∈ {1/K, 1}. Other distributed solvers (quasi-Newton, ADMM) are delegated to [10, 17] (p. 16). Measures: duality gap against iterations and elapsed time for hinge-loss SVM (Figure 1); λ ∈ {10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁷} on url, where the gain grows as λ shrinks (Figure 2, pp. 16–17). Lasso suboptimality on url and epsilon (Figure 3, p. 18). A sweep of the inner iteration limit H shows that the time-optimal H depends on the dataset's communication cost; on news20 larger d favours more local work (Figures 4–5, pp. 16–17, 19). The Figure 4 caption speaks of "σ′ = 1/K and σ′ = 1" while its legends vary γ (p. 19).
  - Transferable: sweep the inner-work knob H and plot both outer iterations and wall time to show the computation/communication trade-off. The best H for time is dataset-dependent, and the paper explains this through d (communication size) (pp. 16–17).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds directly on CoCoA [6], CoCoA+ [10, 17] and DisDCA [20] (p. 3). It contrasts with Newton-type distributed methods (DiSCO [21], DANE [16]), where each CG step costs a communication round (p. 2), and with asynchronous shared-memory methods (Hogwild, Liu–Wright) that are "terribly inefficient" in distributed settings (p. 2).
