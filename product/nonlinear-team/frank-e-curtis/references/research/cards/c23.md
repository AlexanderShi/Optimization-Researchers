### S021 · A stochastic sequential quadratic optimization algorithm for nonlinear-equality-constrained optimization with rank-deficient Jacobians (Mathematics of Operations Research 2024, DOI 10.1287/MOOR.2021.0154; arXiv 2106.13015)
**Read level**: full. Read file pp. 1–31 in full: cover, abstract, §§1–6, and Appendix A, the deterministic analysis (pp. 29–31). Appendix B, the total-probability proof (pp. 31–40), was skimmed: I read the tree construction, the lemma statements and how the Chernoff bound is used, but did not check each inequality. Acknowledgments and references (pp. 40–42) were read. Many formula symbols were lost in extraction, so some inequalities are rebuilt from the surrounding text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Albert S. Berahas (Michigan), Michael J. O'Neill, Daniel P. Robinson (Lehigh) · **Place in trajectory**: the "rank deficiency" rung of the stochastic-SQP ladder. It builds directly on the 2021 SIOPT stochastic SQP ([1] = Berahas, Curtis, Robinson, Zhou) and on the deterministic matrix-free rank-deficient method ([5] = Curtis, Nocedal, Wächter, SIOPT 2009) (pp. 3, 41).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the stochastic SQP of [1] assumes that the singular values of the constraint Jacobians are bounded below, so LICQ holds at every iterate. This paper removes that assumption, because rank deficiency can be unavoidable in practice and would ruin the convergence of any method not designed for it (p. 2). The authors claim that no other algorithm is designed for stochastic equality-constrained optimization with possibly rank-deficient Jacobians (p. 3). The problem is min f(x) = E[F(x,ι)] s.t. c(x) = 0. Constraint values and derivatives are exact, and only stochastic objective gradients are available (eq. (1), p. 4). The target covers the feasible case and, when the constraints are infeasible, convergence to an infeasible stationary point of ‖c‖₂ (p. 3; eq. (4), p. 5).
  - Transferable: enter a new setting by naming the one assumption of your own predecessor paper that fails in practice (here LICQ at every iterate), and build the new paper around dropping it.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: it uses the Byrd–Omojokun step decomposition. The normal step v_k is any vector in Range(J_kᵀ) that is feasible for a trust-region-like least-squares subproblem (5) and satisfies a Cauchy-decrease condition (6), so a single Cauchy step or truncated CG/GLTR is enough (pp. 5–6). The tangential step solves a null-space QP (7) through the consistent KKT system (9). Krylov methods tolerate the singular matrix, while Bunch–Kaufman can fail (pp. 6–7). The merit function is φ = τf + ‖c‖₂ with the τ_trial/τ_k update (11)–(12) (p. 7), as in [1]. New: the adaptive sequences χ_k and ζ_k classify each direction as "tangentially" or "normally dominated" (15)–(16) (pp. 7–8), and a ratio estimate ξ_k uses a different denominator in each case (17)–(18) (p. 8). The step size is a Lipschitz-based α_suff (21) projected onto an interval whose ends depend on the direction type (22). For tangentially dominated steps the interval scales with τ_k, so they can shrink to zero when τ_k → 0, while normally dominated steps keep a step bounded away from zero (p. 9). The authors state that without this distinction the method would lose its guarantees even in the deterministic setting (p. 9). No line search is used, unlike [5] (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a merit parameter may vanish, split the step-size rule by which component dominates the direction. Let the objective-driven component shrink with the merit parameter, and keep the feasibility-driven component's step bounded away from zero.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1 requires f and c to be C¹ with Lipschitz derivatives, and f, ∇f, c, J to be bounded over an open convex set containing the iterates; the iterates themselves need not be bounded (p. 4). The authors concede that this boundedness is "admittedly not ideal" for a stochastic method and attribute it to the adaptivity of the merit parameter (pp. 4–5). Assumption 2: g_k is unbiased with bounded variance M (p. 6). Assumption 3: H_k is chosen independently of g_k, bounded, and uniformly positive definite on Null(J_k) (p. 6). Stronger noise assumptions are added only per event: in the vanishing-τ case (Assumption 5), ‖g_k − ∇f(x_k)‖ is bounded deterministically (p. 19), and this is justified because the algorithm is then moving to the deterministic task of minimizing constraint violation (p. 3). The analysis needs L and Γ (Algorithm 1, p. 10). The experiments estimate them by gradient differences near x₀ (p. 26).
  - Transferable: when your analysis needs an unusually strong assumption, give two explicit justifications: why the constrained setting forces it, and that no rival offers comparable coverage under weaker assumptions (pp. 4–5).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) A deterministic twin comes first. Theorem 1 (p. 10; proof in Appendix A, pp. 29–31) states what the method achieves with g_k = ∇f(x_k), and these results fix the "aims" for the stochastic analysis (pp. 10–11). (ii) Lemmas 2–3 show that the adaptive χ, ζ, ξ sequences become constant after finitely many iterations, with bounds that are uniform over all runs, that is, deterministically (pp. 11–12). (iii) Lemma 4 writes u_k = Z_k w_k, which makes the tangential step conditionally unbiased even under rank deficiency (p. 12). (iv) The analysis splits on the limit of τ_k: E_τ,low (constant, small enough; Theorem 2 and Corollary 1, pp. 14–18), E_τ,zero (τ_k → 0 with bounded noise; Theorem 3, liminf of ‖J_kᵀc_k‖ = 0, p. 21), and E_τ,big (constant but too large). For E_τ,big the probability over a single run is zero (Proposition 1, p. 24), and the total probability over all runs is zero (Theorem 4 informal, p. 24; Theorem 5 in Appendix B). The proof builds a tree of signatures of the merit-parameter decrease indicators and applies the multiplicative Chernoff bound (p. 24; pp. 31–40). (v) The authors name an uncovered case, diminishing β_k with only tangentially dominated directions and Σβ_kτ_k < ∞, and argue that it is detectable in practice and can be fixed by switching to constant β_k (p. 22). (vi) The remaining events E_τ,zero,bad and E_τ,big,bad are dismissed: the first as of theoretical interest only (it cannot occur for finite sums with uniformly bounded gradient deviations), and the second because its effect is observable through ‖J_kᵀc_k‖ and would be monitored anyway (pp. 24–25). (vii) They recommend resetting β_k whenever an adaptive parameter changes, rather than prescribing β_k in advance (p. 18).
  - Transferable: organise a stochastic-algorithm analysis as a case split on the limiting behaviour of each adaptive parameter (settles small / vanishes / settles too large / pathological). Prove the important cases, show the "too large" case has probability zero, and for what is left either argue it is observable in computed quantities or name it as open.
  - Transferable: to pass from "probability zero in each run" to "probability zero over all runs", organise the run histories as a tree of finite signatures and bound the bad leaves with a Chernoff bound on independent Bernoulli trials (p. 24, Appendix B). The paper itself explains that a single run is like one draw from a continuous distribution, so per-run probability zero says nothing about the union (p. 24).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab code, publicly released (GitHub StochasticSQP; p. 25). Baselines: a stochastic subgradient method on the exact penalty function (10) and, where it applies, stochastic projected gradient. Na et al. [15] is left out because it refines gradient estimates inside each iteration and so belongs to a different regime (p. 25). Results are reported at the best iterate, chosen by a feasibility threshold and then the stationarity error. That error uses least-squares multipliers from the true gradient, which the algorithm itself never uses (pp. 25–26). The same Lipschitz estimates are used for all algorithms (p. 26). CUTEst: 67 equality-constrained problems with n + m + 1 ≤ 1000 and non-constant f. To make every instance degenerate, the last constraint is duplicated, which leaves the feasible region and the stationary points unchanged (pp. 26–27). Gaussian gradient noise is added at several levels between 10⁻⁸ and 10⁻¹ (the full list is partly lost in extraction), with 10 seeds per problem, giving 670 runs per noise level (p. 27). Budget: 1000 iterations for SQP with β_k = 1. The subgradient method gets 10,000 iterations and per-instance tuning over 11 τ values × 4 β values, which comes to 440 times the SQP iterations (p. 27). The results are box plots (Fig. 1, p. 27). The event τ_{k−1} ≤ τ_trial,true held in about 98 % of iterations and in all of the last 50 iterations of every run (p. 27). Logistic regression: 11 LIBSVM datasets with 12 ≤ n ≤ 1000 and 256 ≤ N ≤ 100000. There are 10 random linear constraints plus a duplicated one, so LICQ fails at every iterate (p. 28). Batch sizes are 16 and 128, with 5 seeds and 5 epochs. The baselines are tuned and effectively get 16× and 11× the epochs. Tables 2–3 give means with 95 % confidence intervals (pp. 28–30). In the tables some rows favour the baseline, e.g. heart and madelon with batch 128 in Table 3 (p. 30), but the text says only that SQP "regularly outperforms" (p. 28). The event held in about 97–98 % of iterations (p. 29).
  - Transferable: make every test instance violate the assumption your method removes, using a transformation that changes neither the feasible set nor the stationary points (duplicate a constraint), and apply the same transformation to both the benchmark library and the application problems.
  - Transferable: for each analysed event, report how often it held over all iterations and over the final window of every run, and say what that implies for the theory.
- **D6 Positioning**: it continues [1] (same merit update; Lemmas 5 and 16 adapt [1]'s Lemma 3.9 and Proposition 3.16; pp. 12, 23) and [5] (Lemmas 1 and 6 follow [5]; pp. 11, 13). It separates itself from Na, Anitescu and Kolar [15] point by point: [15] assumes LICQ at every iterate and uses an adaptive line search that may need accurate estimates (p. 3), and its exact merit function is not viable without LICQ at all iterates (p. 5). Penalty-based stochastic (proximal) gradient methods are dismissed because they lack stationarity guarantees for the constrained problem, and stochastic Frank–Wolfe because it needs a convex feasible region (p. 3). The tone is restatement plus differentiation, not attack.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the contribution sentence comes in the abstract and is tightly framed: the method is unique in that it both allows stochastic gradients and has guarantees when the Jacobians may be rank deficient (abstract, p. 2). Theorem 1 is used as a roadmap: the paper lists which stochastic result reproduces each deterministic limit, with forward page references (pp. 10–11). Weak points are stated openly: "admittedly not ideal" (p. 4), an "unfortunate case not covered by Theorem 3" (p. 22), and an "open question" (p. 25). Parenthetical remarks give design motivation, e.g. that the χ and ζ sequences are new compared with [1] (p. 7). The ratio of intuition to proof is high in §§3–4.
  - Transferable: state the deterministic-twin theorem first and phrase each stochastic result as "the in-expectation counterpart of limit (24)/(25)/(26)" so the reader always knows the target.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: the contribution is algorithmic and theoretical, with a software release. Limits stated by the authors: the boundedness assumptions (pp. 4–5); the uncovered diminishing-β case (p. 22); the open question for E_τ,zero,bad (p. 25); and the warning that τ_min may be arbitrarily close to zero in different runs, so practitioners should be wary (pp. 18–19). The conclusion repeats the claims and gives no explicit future-work list (p. 29). Further directions are mentioned in passing: larger step-size rules via a one-dimensional search, covered by the analysis but not tested (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. One algorithm moves from stochastic optimization to minimizing constraint violation through the adaptive merit parameter, and there is no separate phase. The analysis is organised around what τ_k does (pp. 5, 7, 19, 22–25). Infeasible stationary points are a built-in termination (Algorithm 1, line 2, p. 10).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. Degenerate variants are made mechanically by duplicating the last constraint, both for CUTEst and for the logistic-regression constraints (pp. 27–28). Unlike PIPAL, the paper does not state how narrow this construction is: duplicating a constraint breaks LICQ but leaves the feasible set unchanged, and no infeasible instances are tested.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. Per-instance tuning and 440× the iterations for the baseline, with the handicap given as a number (p. 27); 16× and 11× the epochs (p. 28); the same Lipschitz estimates for all codes (p. 26); a rival excluded for a stated regime mismatch (p. 25). ⚠ The text claims SQP "regularly outperforms" even though some table rows favour the baseline (p. 30).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The normal step only has to satisfy a Cauchy-decrease condition, so any number ≥ 1 of CG/GLTR iterations is acceptable (p. 6). The inexactness is set by what the merit analysis needs, but no iterative-solver termination test is derived.
- Heuristic 1 (not a Method) ✅: the event frequency is logged (pp. 27, 29). Heuristic 10 ✅: deterministic twin (Theorem 1) and the rank-deficiency rung (pp. 3, 10).
- New-pattern candidate: "event-split analysis of adaptive parameters". Prove the important cases, show probability zero for the bad but likely-looking case, and argue that the rest is observable or open (pp. 11, 14, 19, 22, 24–25).
- New-pattern candidate: "self-tuning monotone estimates of unknown constants". The adaptive χ, ζ, ξ (and τ) sequences stand in for problem constants that are known to exist but cannot be computed, and are proved to settle after finitely many iterations with bounds that hold in every run (pp. 7–8, 11–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Duplicate a constraint to make every instance degenerate without changing the feasible or stationary sets (p. 27).
- Prove a tangential/normal-dominated split in the step-size projection so that the feasibility step survives a vanishing merit parameter (p. 9).
- Use a tree of signatures and a Chernoff bound to show a total-probability-zero result over uncountably many runs (p. 24; Appendix B).
- Reset the step-size sequence whenever any adaptive estimate changes, so that a constant or diminishing schedule is followed between changes (p. 18).
- Report the best iterate (most feasible first, then least stationary), using true-gradient least-squares multipliers that the algorithm never sees (pp. 25–26).

**Quotes**:
- "One finds that it is the adaptivity of our merit parameter (see (10) later on) that necessitates the aforementioned boundedness assumptions that we use in our analysis." (p. 5)
- "This provides evidence for our claim that the merit parameter not reaching a sufficiently small value is not an issue of practical concern." (p. 27)

### S030 · Sequential quadratic optimization for stochastic optimization with deterministic nonlinear inequality and equality constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2024, DOI 10.1137/23M1556149; arXiv 2302.14790)
**Read level**: full. Read file pp. 1–27: cover, abstract, §§1–6, acknowledgements and references. In §4 all statements and the surrounding discussion were read. The proofs of Lemmas 7–11 and 14–20 were followed for structure, but each inequality was not checked, and many formula symbols were lost in extraction. The version read is arXiv v1 (1 Mar 2023), not the published SIOPT text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel P. Robinson (Lehigh), Baoyu Zhou (Chicago Booth) · **Place in trajectory**: the "inequalities" rung of the stochastic-SQP ladder. It extends [1] (Berahas, Curtis, Robinson, Zhou 2021) from equality constraints to equality plus bound constraints, and through slacks to general inequalities (pp. 3–5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the paper has two entry points. First, the literature on stochastic algorithms for nonlinear (nonconvex) inequality constraints is almost empty: the only exceptions listed are two active-set SQPs and one momentum augmented Lagrangian (p. 3). Second, a structural obstacle: with inequalities, the stochastic search direction is automatically a biased estimate of the "true" direction, while [1]'s analysis relies on conditional unbiasedness (p. 3). The problem is min E[F(x,ω)] s.t. c(x) = 0, x ≥ 0. This is general because any inequality cI(x) ≤ 0 becomes cI(x) + s = 0 with s ≥ 0 (eq. (1), pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable: when extending your own method to a harder constraint class, find the exact property of the old proof that the new class destroys (here, unbiased directions) and make it the stated reason the paper is needed.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: the iterates stay feasible for the bounds. Step 1 solves a strongly convex QP (7) for v_k = u_k + ∇c(x_k)w_k: it minimizes linearized violation plus a regularization μ_k‖u‖² on the null-space part, subject to x_k + u + ∇c w ≥ 0. Then v_k = 0 exactly when x_k is feasible or an infeasible stationary point (p. 6; Lemma 1, pp. 8–9). Step 2 solves QP (8) with a stochastic gradient: it reaches the same linearized feasibility as v_k and keeps x_k + d ≥ 0 (p. 6). The merit-parameter and ratio-parameter updates are as in [1] and [12] (pp. 7–8). The step size is any α in [α_min, α_max]. Here α_max is the largest root of a strongly convex function ϕ_k that bounds the merit decrease, and α_min comes from Lipschitz constants (p. 7). The implementation searches with factors 1.1^t inside the interval (p. 24). Subproblems are solved with Gurobi (p. 24).
  - Transferable: compute a feasibility-only step first with a regularized QP whose zero solution certifies "feasible or infeasible-stationary". Then constrain the optimality step to match that step's linearized progress. One subproblem acts as both a progress target and a stationarity certificate.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1 requires f and c to be C¹ with Lipschitz, bounded derivatives on a set that contains the iterates almost surely, and also that ∇cᵀ has full row rank over X (p. 5). This is a step back from S021's rank-deficient setting, and it is what makes the solution of (7) unique (p. 6). Assumption 2: unbiased gradients with variance ≤ ρ_k, where ρ_k is prescribed (p. 5). Assumption 3: H_k is SPD, with ζI ⪯ H_k ⪯ κ_H I. The authors note that the guarantees are therefore of the kind expected for a first-order-type algorithm (p. 6). The key analysis assumption is Assumption 4: the event E, in which the merit parameter settles at a constant ≥ τ_min > 0 (p. 16). They argue that its other parts are "not a shortcoming" (p. 16). For the diminishing-step result, the variance must vanish (ρ_k ≤ ιβ_k²). The authors say this is stronger than in [1] and is needed because of the bias (p. 19).
  - Transferable: when an extension forces a stronger noise condition, state where it is used and which new difficulty forces it (p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Subproblem-based stationarity measures. Lemmas 1–5 show that the solutions of (7) and (8) vanish exactly at infeasible-stationary or KKT points, and that their norms are stationarity measures under strict complementarity and full-rank conditions (pp. 8–12; Remark 1, p. 12). (ii) Lemmas 6–13 hold in every run: τ_k and ξ_k are nonincreasing and drop by a fixed fraction when they decrease, and either τ_k → 0 or it becomes constant (pp. 12–16). (iii) Projection view of the direction (Lemma 12, p. 15): d_k is the H_k-projection of the unconstrained QP minimizer onto the feasible set of (8). The obtuse-angle lemma then gives ‖d_k − d_true‖ ≤ constant × ‖g_k − ∇f‖ (Lemma 14, p. 17). This replaces the unbiasedness argument of [1]. (iv) A filtration of trace σ-algebras F_k = G_k ∩ E conditions everything on the event (p. 16). (v) Theorem 1: with constant β, the limsup of averaged expected stationarity measures is O(β); with diminishing β and ρ_k ≤ ιβ_k², they vanish (p. 21). (vi) Theorem 2: if the gradient errors are uniformly bounded, then τ_k does not vanish (p. 23). The "too large τ" event is not proved to have probability zero here; the reader is sent to [1]'s Proposition 3.16 (p. 23). (vii) Proposition 1, for the deterministic algorithm, is stated without proof, citing space (p. 23).
  - Transferable: when a constrained stochastic subproblem makes the step a biased estimate, write the step as a projection in the H-norm and use non-expansiveness (the obtuse-angle lemma) to bound the error of the step by the error of the gradient, instead of trying to restore unbiasedness.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab with Gurobi, compared with the authors' Julia code for Na et al.'s active-set SQP [31] (AdapGD option, default parameters), with Sen Na acknowledged for consultation (pp. 24–25). Test set: CUTEst inequality-constrained problems with m ≤ n ≤ 1000, f(x_k) ≥ −10²⁰ at all iterates in all runs of the authors' own algorithm, and no Gurobi errors, giving 323 problems (p. 24). Both codes use the same x₀ and the same noise model, N(∇f, ε_g(I + eeᵀ)), with ε_g ∈ {10⁻⁸, 10⁻⁴, 10⁻², 10⁻¹}, and 5 runs per problem (p. 24). Budget: 10⁴ stochastic gradient samples or 12 h CPU (p. 24). L and Γ are re-estimated every 100 iterations by differences of stochastic gradients at ten samples (p. 24). Best iterate: the minimum feasibility error if no iterate reaches 10⁻⁴, otherwise the minimum KKT error with true-gradient multipliers. The same formula is applied to the rival's iterates and multipliers (p. 24). Because the Julia code cannot handle simple bounds, only 57 problems are compared head-to-head. The other 266 are shown for the authors' method only (p. 24; Fig. 1, p. 25). The verdict, "comparatively good", is hedged (p. 24). Diminishing-step results are left out, citing the page limit (p. 24). Unlike S021, the event frequency is not logged.
  - Transferable: when the rival code covers only part of your test set, split the figure: show the head-to-head comparison on the shared subset, and report your method alone on the rest.
- **D6 Positioning**: the paper places SQP against penalty methods (ill-conditioning and sensitivity to the penalty-update scheme) and interior-point methods (best in deterministic practice) (pp. 2–3). It admits that interior-point methods often beat SQP in the deterministic setting and says no stochastic interior-point method exists for this setting (p. 3). It lists the follow-ups to [1] and [32]: rank deficiency [3] = S021, inexact solves [12], trust region [17], noisy and biased estimates [5, 35], variance reduction [2, 4], sketching [33], and complexity [13] (p. 3). The nearest rival is [31], which refines estimates within an iteration and so gives near-deterministic guarantees, whereas this paper settles for convergence in expectation (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: a roadmap paragraph at the start of §4 previews every subsection and why it matters (p. 8). Assumption 4 is defended in a pre-emptive paragraph that opens by saying it is "not a shortcoming" of the analysis (p. 16). Simplifications are flagged as such: nonnegativity is used for notational simplicity (p. 5), and a looser H_k condition is skipped because it would obscure the analysis without adding much (p. 6). The empirical claims are hedged: "can outperform" (abstract, p. 2; conclusion, p. 25).
  - Transferable: after each restrictive assumption, add a short paragraph that takes its parts one by one and says why each is harmless or where the paper itself rules out the alternative (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: the contribution is an algorithm with in-expectation convergence theory (no software release is mentioned in this version). Limits: full-rank Jacobians are assumed (p. 5); the theory needs Lipschitz constants, which are estimated in practice (pp. 24–25); the probability-zero result for the "too large τ" event is not proved here (p. 23); the deterministic proposition is unproved (p. 23); the head-to-head comparison covers 57 problems; diminishing-step experiments are omitted (p. 24). Future work is general: the assumptions might be loosened further (p. 25). They also suggest that an almost-sure version of Theorem 1(b) may be possible (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence. One algorithm with an adaptive merit parameter, a built-in infeasible-stationary termination (Algorithm 1, line 3, p. 8), and a diagnosis tied to τ: if τ_k becomes very small while μ_k stays bounded below, Theorem 2 implies the stochastic gradient estimates have grown large, and more accurate estimates may help (p. 23).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant. The rival is the authors' own external code run with default parameters, same x₀, same noise and the same best-iterate rule (p. 24), and the authors were consulted. But there is no baseline tuning, no handicap stated as a number, and only 57 of 323 problems are shared.
- Method 3: no link (no constructed degenerate or infeasible instances).
- Heuristic 10 (not a Method) ✅: the inequality rung on the same skeleton (pp. 3, 7). It breaks Heuristic 7 (not a Method) ✗: problems are kept only if f(x_k) ≥ −10²⁰ at all iterates in all runs of the authors' own algorithm (p. 24), which filters on outcomes of those runs. Heuristic 1 is not followed: the event the analysis needs (Assumption 4) is described as likely behaviour in practice (p. 8), but it is not measured.
- New-pattern candidate: "event-split analysis of adaptive parameters" (pp. 8, 16, 21–23); same pattern as S021.
- New-pattern candidate: "projection non-expansiveness instead of unbiasedness" (Lemmas 12 and 14, pp. 15, 17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use a regularized feasibility QP whose zero solution certifies feasibility or infeasible stationarity, and require the optimality QP to match its linearized progress (pp. 6, 8–9).
- Bound ‖d_k − d_true‖ by the gradient error through the H-norm projection and the obtuse-angle lemma (pp. 15, 17).
- Choose a step size inside an interval with a computable upper end (the largest root of a strongly convex merit-bound function), searching geometrically from the lower end (pp. 7, 24).
- Apply one shared best-iterate rule to your iterates and the rival's (feasibility threshold first, then KKT error) (p. 24).

**Quotes**:
- "We remark in passing that interior-point methods often outperform SQP methods in the deterministic setting, but as far as we are aware there exists no interior-point method designed for the stochastic setting that we consider." (p. 3)
- "This discussion is meant to provide confidence to a user that our algorithm is based on one that has state-of-the-art convergence properties under common assumptions in the deterministic setting." (p. 8)

### S069 · Using synthetic data to mitigate unfairness and preserve privacy in collaborative machine learning (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2409.09532)
**Read level**: full. Read file pp. 1–14 in full (abstract, §§1–5, Algorithm 1, Tables 1–2, references), arXiv v2 of 23 Jan 2025. Figures 2–3 are only available as captions in the text. The first page lists Chia-Yuan Wu, Frank E. Curtis and Daniel P. Robinson, and the title matches the work list (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Chia-Yuan Wu (Lehigh), Daniel P. Robinson (Lehigh) · **Place in trajectory**: an application paper in ML fairness and privacy, outside NLP-solver design. Its link to the bilevel line (S072) is not established (work-list note).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: in collaborative ML and federated learning, fairness-aware aggregation methods (FairFed, FAIR-FATE, FedGAN variants) need frequent parameter exchange. They also send client statistics such as dataset size or fairness metrics to the server, which costs communication and can leak information (pp. 2, 5). The entry point is to move fairness out of the aggregation and into client-side synthetic data, with a single transmission (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: when a constraint is costly to enforce at the aggregator, ask whether it can be built into what the clients send, so that the aggregator solves an unconstrained standard problem.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: there are two stages per client (Fig. 1, p. 2; Algorithm 1, p. 10). Stage 1 is a bilevel problem (9). The inner problem trains a regularized logistic model on synthetic features. The outer problem chooses the synthetic features so that the inner model has low loss on the real client data and satisfies decision-boundary-covariance fairness constraints (pp. 6–7). The constraints are replaced by a penalty with fixed ρ_o, a choice the authors attribute to computational efficiency and convenience (p. 8). The outer gradient comes from the chain rule plus implicit differentiation of the inner optimality condition, which reduces to solving a linear system (p. 8). The inner problem is solved by L-BFGS with a strong Wolfe line search (100 iterations) and the outer one by Adam (pp. 8–9). Stage 2 feeds the fair synthetic data into DP-CTGAN, or any DP generator, to obtain (ε, δ)-DP synthetic data (pp. 6, 9, 12). The server trains a standard regularized model on the pooled synthetic data (eq. (16), p. 9).
  - Transferable: regularize the inner problem of a bilevel formulation explicitly so that its minimizer is unique and the implicit-differentiation gradient is well defined (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: one binary sensitive feature, binary labels, a linear predictor d_θ(a) = aᵀθ, and the Zafar et al. decision-boundary covariance as a convex surrogate for SP and EO (pp. 4, 6). Privacy is inherited entirely from DP-CTGAN through post-processing (pp. 6, 12). There are no convergence or privacy-accounting assumptions for the bilevel solve.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a, the paper has no theorems. The only analysis is a communication-cost comparison: O(K·t·|θ|) for standard FL against O(K·|D|) + O(K·|θ|) for the one-shot scheme, so the scheme wins when t·|θ| is large relative to |D| + |θ| (p. 9).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: one dataset (Law School, 20,798 points, race as the sensitive feature) split into two clients, 80/20 train/test, on one workstation using Python multiprocessing (p. 9). The penalty is swept over ρ_o ∈ {0, 10, 100, 1000, 10000}. The baseline is ρ_o = 0, i.e. the real data (pp. 9–10). Accuracy, covariance and |SPD|/|EOD| are reported for stage-1 data, stage-2 data at 100 % size and stage-2 data at 10 % size (Figs. 2–3, p. 11; Tables 1–2, p. 12). The trends are not monotone: for example, EOD with ρ_o = 100 drops stage-2 accuracy to 73.77 % (p. 12). The authors pick ρ_o by reading the test-set tables (ρ_o = 10 on p. 11; ρ_o = 1000 on p. 12). There are no repeated runs, no confidence intervals and no comparison with FairFed or other fairness-aware FL methods. The authors expect, without testing, that the results carry over to K ≫ 0 (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: the paper says it extends synthetic-data FL (Goetz and Tewari; FedSynth), which targets efficiency, by adding fairness (pp. 5–6). It criticises the aggregation-based fair-FL methods for communication and leakage (p. 5). It claims, "to the best of our knowledge", to be the first collaborative-ML approach with a single transmission that also addresses unfairness (p. 3). It takes its fairness and DP tools from the ML literature, and no optimization-methods literature is cited apart from Nocedal–Wright and Adam (pp. 13–14).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: the contributions are a bulleted list (p. 3), and a flexibility bullet lists three user-controlled knobs (ε_SP/ε_EO, N_s¹/N_s², DP parameters) (p. 3). Design decisions are stressed with "We stress that" sentences, about the server's plain problem (p. 8) and about the DP stage being only one part of the pipeline (p. 6). The empirical claims are moderately hedged ("without sacrificing too much accuracy", p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: the contribution is a framework with an application demonstration. The limits are not spelled out. The paper says the client count is kept small for demonstration (p. 9) and that only a single sensitive feature and binary classification are treated "for simplicity" (p. 6). The conclusion gives no future directions (p. 13). Not addressed: convergence of the bilevel solve, the effect of DP noise on fairness (stage-2 covariances are "not monotonically decreasing", p. 12), and selection on test data.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction. There is no external baseline, although competing fair-FL methods are discussed (p. 5); there is one dataset with two clients; there are no repeated runs; and the penalty value is chosen from test-set results (pp. 9–12). This is far from the fair-fight protocol of the NLP papers.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. Here the penalty parameter is a fixed user knob swept over a grid, presented as control over the fairness/accuracy trade-off (pp. 3, 8–10). It is not steered adaptively. This is a different role (a modelling knob, not a globalization device).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant. The full tables are shown so the reader can weigh accuracy against fairness (Tables 1–2, p. 12), in line with "present the data, let the reader decide", but the authors then make the choice on the test data themselves.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The inner solve is a fixed 100 L-BFGS iterations, and the implicit gradient assumes inner optimality (pp. 8–9). The accuracy of the inner solve is not linked to the needs of the outer method.
- New-pattern candidate: "push the constraint into the data" (fairness built into the synthetic data so the server solves a plain problem; pp. 3, 8). One paper only.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the communication cost of a one-shot scheme against the iterative baseline and state the crossover condition (t·|θ| against |D| + |θ|) (p. 9).
- Report every stage of a pipeline separately (stage 1, stage 2 at full size, stage 2 at 10 % size) against a no-intervention baseline, so the cost of each stage is visible (Tables 1–2, p. 12).

**Quotes**:
