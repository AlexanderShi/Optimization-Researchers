### S015 · A stochastic trust region algorithm based on careful step normalization (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2019, DOI 10.1287/IJOO.2018.0010; arXiv:1712.10277)
**Read level**: full. Read the arXiv v3 / COR@L 17T-17-R1 version (last revised 27 June 2018, p. 2), pp. 1–24: all of §§1–5, every theorem and proof in §3, the experiment narrative and figure captions in §4, and the acknowledgements. References (pp. 24–25) skimmed. Citations that need pages should use the version form [S015 arXiv p. N].
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Katya Scheinberg, Rui Shi (both Lehigh ISE at the time, p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: his first stochastic trust-region-type algorithm, written right after the SIAM Review survey with Bottou and Nocedal (ref. [2], which supplies the SG baselines for the theory, pp. 9–10). It is described as "a first step toward designing new classes of first- and second-order trust region methods" for stochastic problems (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: SG steps "possess no natural scaling": convergence guarantees need stepsizes chosen in a problem-dependent way (∝ 1/L), unlike Newton's unit step (p. 2). The paper claims only to "reduce somewhat this dependence" through normalized steps (p. 2). The entry is a gap in the literature. Hazan–Levy–Shalev-Shwartz normalize every step and need strict-local-quasi-convexity and Ω(ε⁻²) mini-batches (p. 3). Stochastic trust-region methods ([16], [5]) accept or reject steps and need fully linear models (p. 3). TRish accepts every step, like SG, and needs only second-moment bounds (p. 3).
  - Transferable: state the motivation as a weakness the incumbent shares with its deterministic ancestor (no natural scaling), then claim only a partial fix ("reduce somewhat") (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: TRish (§2.1, p. 4) takes the trust-region step −α_k g_k/‖g_k‖ only when ‖g_k‖ ∈ [1/γ_{1,k}, 1/γ_{2,k}]. Below that interval it takes the SG step −γ_{1,k}α_k g_k, above it −γ_{2,k}α_k g_k. The step norm is a continuous function of ‖g_k‖ (Figure 1, p. 5). The trust-region step solves the linear-model subproblem (2.3) (p. 4). What is changed from classical trust region: no ratio test, no rejection (p. 3). What is kept from SG: every step is accepted. Why the hybrid: Example 2.1 (p. 4) builds an unbiased two-point gradient distribution (6 w.p. 1/3, −3/2 w.p. 2/3) for which the normalized step is "twice as likely" to ascend as to descend. With (γ1, γ2) = (1, 1/2) or (1/4, 1/6) the TRish step descends in expectation (p. 5).
  - Transferable: before you propose a normalized or clipped stochastic step, build a two-point unbiased distribution on which the pure version ascends in expectation, then show your safeguard's parameters remove the failure (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: L-smooth f bounded below (Assumption 3.1, pp. 5–6). Unbiased g_k with E_k‖g_k‖² ≤ M1 + M2‖∇f‖² (Assumption 3.2, p. 6, called "standard"). Some results add the Polyak–Łojasiewicz condition (Assumption 3.3, p. 7; the authors place it relative to error bounds, quadratic growth and others), variance ∝ α_k (Assumption 3.4, p. 12, met by mini-batches growing like α_k⁻¹, p. 12), or geometrically decreasing variance (Assumption 3.5, p. 14, met by geometrically growing samples). The parameter conditions bound the ratio γ1/γ2 (p. 5; Theorems 3.1–3.5). The authors say the theory's parameter values are "often too conservative in practice" (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Lemma 3.1 (pp. 6–7) splits each iteration into the three step cases and conditions on the event E_k = {∇f(x_k)ᵀg_k ≥ 0}. Every case is bounded by the same expression, which gives a single expected-decrease inequality (3.3) with an extra term (γ1 − γ2)α_k P[E_k]E[∇fᵀg | E_k]. (ii) Lemma 3.2 (p. 8) bounds that extra term by h1 + h2‖∇f‖² with Jensen's inequality and a ≤ ½(1 + a²). The acknowledgements say an anonymous referee suggested this proof (p. 24). (iii) Worked Gaussian Examples 3.1–3.6 (pp. 8–9, 13, 14) show that the bounding quantity is reasonable. (iv) Six theorems mirror the SG results of [2]. Theorem 3.1: PL, constant parameters, linear convergence to a neighborhood (pp. 9–10). Theorem 3.2: PL, sublinear rate with γ1 − γ2 ∝ α_k, by induction (pp. 11–12). Theorem 3.3: constant γ's under Assumption 3.4 (p. 13). Theorem 3.4: linear rate with geometric variance decay (p. 15). Theorem 3.5: no PL, bound on the average squared gradient (p. 16). Theorem 3.6: no PL, diminishing steps, liminf of expected gradient → 0 (pp. 16–17). (v) A comparison paragraph after Theorem 3.1 sets the TRish neighborhood against the SG bound of [2, Thm 4.6]. It finds a residual term that does not shrink with α unless γ1 − γ2 is tied to α (pp. 9–10), and that observation motivates Theorem 3.2.
  - Transferable: when a hybrid step has several regimes, condition on the regime and on the sign event of gᵀ∇f, and bound every regime by one common inequality, so the remaining analysis is the SG analysis plus one extra term (pp. 6–7).
  - Transferable: put each theorem next to the incumbent's theorem under the same assumptions, and read off the parameter coupling (here γ1 − γ2 = O(α)) that makes your bound match (pp. 9–10, 14, 23).
- **D5 Experiments**: Logistic regression on LIBSVM a1a, w1a and rcv1 (pp. 18–21), and two-convolutional-layer CNNs in TensorFlow on mnist and cifar-10 (pp. 21–23). Metrics are training loss and testing accuracy against epochs. Protocol: G (the average stochastic-gradient norm) is computed from an independent SG run. TRish gets 60 (α, γ1, γ2) settings, and SG gets 60 stepsizes spanning the same range of effective stepsizes "so that neither algorithm had an advantage" (pp. 18–19; 36 settings for the CNNs, p. 22). Tuning picks the best average testing accuracy over the last ten iterations (p. 19). Each tuned configuration is run 10 times from the same start (pp. 19–23). Case frequencies are reported for every dataset: e.g. a1a has case 2 in ≈99% of iterations (p. 19), rcv1 27%/73%/0% (p. 20), mnist 62%/37%/≈0% (p. 22). There is a two-epoch retuned rcv1 run (p. 20). Honest limits are stated: on convex problems the gains are "somewhat limited" (p. 20) and cifar-10 reaches only ≈60% accuracy (p. 22). One inconsistency: the text says the mnist losses were not averaged over the 10 runs because the problem is nonconvex (p. 22), while the Figure 6 caption says "Average training loss" (p. 22).
  - Transferable: give the baseline a grid that covers the same range of effective stepsizes as your method's (product of α and γ ranges), with the same number of settings (pp. 18–19, 22).
  - Transferable: for a method with several step regimes, report per-dataset regime frequencies so readers can see that the new regime (here normalization) is actually active (pp. 19–23).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on SG theory as summarized in the authors' own review [2] (pp. 5, 9–10). It differs from [12] by not normalizing every step and argues that full normalization "cannot lead to general convergence guarantees" (p. 3). It is set apart from stochastic trust-region methods [16], [5] as "quite distinct" (p. 3) and related to AdaGrad-style and normalized-online methods [7], [21] (p. 3). The criticism is technical and polite: it explains why [12] needs extra assumptions instead of dismissing it.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The main claim is hedged twice in the introduction ("does not completely overcome", "for practical purposes, reduce somewhat", p. 2). Each theorem is followed by a paragraph comparing it with SG (pp. 9–10, 12, 14, 16). The authors explicitly do not recommend the parameter settings used in the comparison (p. 10). The conclusion attributes the gains to normalization "which one can argue lessens its dependence on problem-specific quantities" (p. 23). The theorem-to-intuition ratio is high on theorems, but every example is worked numerically.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are an algorithm, convergence theory matching SG in six settings, and evidence on ML test problems. Limits are stated concretely: a "more substantial numerical study ... beyond the scope", stepsize-decrease schedules not studied (p. 23), and theory values too conservative (p. 17). Future work: Hessian approximations, acceleration, momentum (p. 23), and tying the (γ1, γ2) adjustment to α (p. 23). Not stated by the authors: the tuning criterion is the evaluation metric (testing accuracy, p. 19), applied symmetrically to both methods.

**Method links**: Method 5 ✅ (evidence: matched tuning effort "did not require more effort" and matched effective-stepsize ranges, pp. 18–19, 22; 10 runs per configuration). Method 3 ⚠ (variant: the deliberately broken case is a constructed noise distribution in the theory, Example 2.1, p. 4, not a constructed test set). Method 2 ⚠ (variant: one algorithm whose step rule moves continuously between SG and trust-region regimes, with no separate phase, p. 5. But nothing is updated from predicted progress: the γ's are fixed or scheduled). Also practises Heuristic 1 (regime frequencies logged on every dataset, pp. 19–23) and Heuristic 10 (keep the SG skeleton, change only the step scaling, p. 3). New-pattern candidate: "match the incumbent's theorem, win in the experiments" (pp. 3, 9–10, 23).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a two-point unbiased gradient distribution that breaks the "pure" stochastic version of a deterministic device, and use it to justify the safeguard (pp. 4–5).
- Make a piecewise step rule continuous in ‖g‖ so that the analysis can treat the regimes uniformly (Figure 1, p. 5).
- Condition each regime on the event gᵀ∇f ≥ 0 and fold all regimes into one expected-decrease inequality (pp. 6–7).
- Couple the regime-width parameter to the stepsize (γ1 − γ2 = O(α)) to recover the incumbent's sublinear rate (pp. 11–12, 23).
- For the baseline, cover the same effective-stepsize range with the same number of settings, then report regime frequencies (pp. 18–23).
**Quotes**:
- "We argue in this paper that employing normalized steps in all iterations cannot lead to general convergence guarantees, which perhaps explains the additional assumptions required for convergence by [12]." (p. 3)
- "We took care to make sure that the tuning procedure for TRish did not require more effort than the tuning used for the SG method that we have for comparison purposes." (p. 18)

### D001 · Adaptive Stochastic Optimization (CoRR 2020, arXiv:2001.06699)
**Read level**: full. Read arXiv:2001.06699v1 (dated 22 January 2020), pp. 1–16: §§1–6 in full, references (pp. 13–14) skimmed, and the supplementary analysis for §§4.1, 4.2 and 4.4 (pp. 15–16) in full. Many exponents in the complexity formulas are cut off in the text layer (e.g. "Tε = O(ε", pp. 4, 5, 6). Those exponents are not reported here. The batch note says this is probably the preprint of S031 (IEEE Signal Process. Mag. 2020). That was not checked, because S031's text is not in this batch.
**Role**: core (position/survey article) · **Coauthors**: Katya Scheinberg · **Place in trajectory**: a magazine-style synthesis of the Lehigh stochastic-adaptive line. It cites his own TRish [13] and TRACE [11], Scheinberg's STORM [10], the supermartingale analysis [2], and the line search with Paquette [23] (pp. 9–14). It names stochastic TRACE as an open problem (p. 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a practical cost, not a theorem. SG and its variance-reduced variants are "nonadaptive" in the sense that batch sizes and stepsizes are prescribed (p. 2). Tuning them for each application costs weeks or months on large systems, with an energy analogy from [1] (p. 1). Adaptive methods (trust region, line search, regularized Newton) are the deterministic answer and are "significantly more difficult to design for the stochastic regime" (p. 1), because comparing noisy function values can lead to bad decisions whose combined effect is hard to control (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open a methods paper with the cost of the incumbent's tuning loop in units the reader feels (CPU-days, repeated solves), then present adaptivity as a way to remove that loop (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A single analysis framework. Algorithm 1 (deterministic, p. 4) and Algorithm 2 (stochastic, p. 7) have the same model–step–ratio-test–update skeleton, with the stepsize parameter α_k scaled by γ up or down. The analysis does not count successful and unsuccessful iterations. It uses a potential Φ_k = ν(f(x_k) − f*) + (1 − ν)·(a stepsize term) that decreases in every iteration (p. 3). Condition 4.1 (p. 3) then bounds T_ε by Φ_0 and Θh_ε(α_ε). The formula, eq. (1), is not legible in the text layer. The potential is instantiated for trust region (§4.1, p. 4), line search (§4.2, p. 5), cubic regularization (§4.3, pp. 5–6), and second-order, convex and strongly convex variants (§4.4, p. 6). The stochastic Condition 5.1 (pp. 7–8) asks for success with probability 1 − δ > ½ when α_k is small, and expected decrease Θh_ε(α_k). The bound on E[T_ε] is Theorem 5.2, taken from [2] (p. 8). What is kept is the classical adaptive skeleton. What changes is only the accuracy requirement on the model and the estimates (§5.1, pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable: before analysing a stochastic adaptive method, rewrite the deterministic analysis with a potential that mixes the objective gap and the stepsize parameter, so that every iteration (successful or not) reduces it. The stochastic version then only needs the reduction in expectation (pp. 3, 7–8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Deterministic: ∇f Lipschitz, f* > −∞, and models matching f to first order (p. 3). An extra condition α_k ≤ τ‖∇f(x_k)‖ is imposed "for consistency" with the stochastic case, although it is not needed deterministically (p. 4). Stochastic: model accuracy holds with probability 1 − δ1 and function estimates are accurate with probability 1 − δ2 (Algorithm 2, p. 7). Three accuracy families are compared (pp. 8–9). Taylor-like bounds in Δ_k can be met by sample averages. The gradient-norm condition is "an impractical condition" because it needs ‖∇f‖. The stochastic-gradient-norm condition with Δ_k = α_k‖g(x_k)‖ needs no unknown quantity. The line-search analysis adds a variance bound on the function estimates through a control sequence {Δ_k} (p. 11). The authors note that this bound is stronger than (5a) and less tolerant to outliers (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) A potential function decreasing on all iterations, with a weight ν close to 1, chosen so that the successful-iteration decrease dominates the stepsize-term increase (pp. 4–6). (ii) The cubic-regularization case needs progress measured at x_{k+1}, so the stopping time is redefined as the first k with ‖∇f(x_{k+1})‖ ≤ ε (p. 6). (iii) In the stochastic case: the four-way good/bad model × good/bad estimate case analysis (Figure 3, p. 10), with the key step "possible decrease of this measure is proportional to any possible increase" when both are bad (prob ≤ δ1δ2) (p. 10). (iv) A diagnosis of why the optimal-complexity analysis does not transfer: with random s_k, x_{k+1} is not F_k-measurable, so T_ε is not a stopping time (p. 12). (v) The supplementary analysis derives the convex and strongly convex bounds through reciprocal and log potentials (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: when a deterministic proof does not carry over to the stochastic case, check whether the quantity the proof needs is still a stopping time (or measurable) with respect to the algorithm's filtration (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: One illustration (§3, pp. 2–3, Figure 1): ℓ2-regularized logistic regression (λ = 10⁻⁴) on MNIST, digit 5 vs rest, mini-batch 64, 10 epochs. SG with α = 1 reaches ≈98% testing accuracy, and with α = 0.01 it stagnates near 90% (p. 2). The adaptive method checks the mini-batch objective, shrinks α and grows the batch on failure, and does the reverse on success. It "competes with that attained by the best SG run" from every initial stepsize (p. 2). The text does not say how many runs were made, or whether the extra mini-batch objective evaluations are counted on the horizontal axis.
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits Cartis–Scheinberg [8] and Blanchet–Cartis–Menickelly–Scheinberg [2] for the stochastic framework (p. 7) and claims the deterministic framework as "new for the deterministic optimization literature" (pp. 2–3). The criticisms are direct. Stochastic cubic methods [28, 30] use ε in place of Δ_k, so they "essentially reduce to sample average approximation" and "are not adaptive enough to be competitive" (p. 12). The interpolation assumption of [29] is "unreasonably strong" and would imply zero generalization error (p. 11). Exponentially growing batches are prescribed and "not adaptive in our desirable sense" (p. 8). The probabilistic Wolfe line search [18] "possesses no known theoretical guarantees" (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Magazine register: a "dirty secret" opening and an energy analogy (p. 1), an illustration before the framework (§3), and figures instead of proofs for the stochastic cases (Figure 3, p. 10). The proofs of the deterministic cases are moved to a supplement (pp. 15–16). Open questions are numbered by obstacle (stopping time, the TRACE multiplier, γ asymmetry, work complexity) (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a unifying analytical framework (deterministic part new) and a survey of stochastic TR and LS instances. Open questions are specific: a stochastic method with optimal deterministic complexity, the stopping-time definition (p. 12), stochastic TRACE without exact multipliers (p. 12), error bounds that improve work complexity, not iteration complexity (p. 12), separate γ's for increases and decreases (p. 12), and practical batch-size and stepsize rules (p. 12). The framework is presented "avoiding some technical details" (p. 7), so complete statements are delegated to [8, 2].

**Method links**: Method 5 ✅ (evidence, stated: tuning effort is part of the cost of a method; "problems cannot be solved once", p. 2). Method 4 ✅ (evidence: audits rivals' assumptions and analyses for whether they measure what practice needs: the interpolation assumption (p. 11), and accuracy-for-every-iteration analyses that "reduce to" the deterministic case (p. 12)). Method 5 ⚠ (variant: the article's own illustration is a single dataset and does not state run counts or whether the adaptive method's extra data accesses are charged, p. 2). Also practises Heuristic 10 (the deterministic framework is built first "as a first step", p. 2, then generalized, p. 7). New-pattern candidate: "every-iteration potential (objective gap + stepsize term)" as the analysis device for adaptive methods (pp. 3–6; also used in the stochastic framework, pp. 7–11).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Analyse adaptive methods with Φ_k = ν(f − f*) + (1 − ν)·h(α_k, ‖∇f‖) and pick ν so that Φ decreases on unsuccessful iterations too (pp. 3–6).
- Classify the possible accuracy conditions on stochastic estimates by whether they need an unknown quantity (‖∇f‖) and therefore can or cannot be enforced, and prefer conditions stated in α_k‖g_k‖ (pp. 8–9).
- For a stochastic trust-region proof, enumerate the four good/bad combinations of model and estimate, and show that the bad-bad case increase is proportional to the possible decrease (p. 10).
**Quotes**:
- "Tuning these parameters means that problems cannot be solved once; they need to be solved numerous times" (p. 2)
- "By contrast, in the framework presented here, the analysis is structured around a measure in which progress is made in all iterations." (p. 3)

### S005 · Gradient sampling methods for nonsmooth optimization (Numerical nonsmooth optimization: State of the art algorithms 2020, DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-34910-3_6; arXiv:1804.11003)
**Read level**: full. Read arXiv:1804.11003v1 (29 April 2018, "Submitted to: Special Methods for Nonsmooth Optimization"), pp. 1–16: §§1–7 and Appendices A–B in full. References (pp. 16–18) skimmed. The published chapter may differ, so citations that need pages should use [S005 arXiv p. N].
**Role**: core (review chapter, joint voice of five authors) · **Coauthors**: James V. Burke, Adrian S. Lewis, Michael L. Overton, Lucas E. A. Simões · **Place in trajectory**: a review of the gradient-sampling (GS) line, including Curtis's own SQP-GS [CO12], adaptive sampling with Que [CQ13, CQ15], and BFGS-SQP with relative minimization profiles [CMO17] (pp. 9–12). Which author wrote which section is not stated. The SQP-GS, adaptive-sampling and BFGS-SQP passages describe Curtis's work, but the voice is joint.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The aim is to review GS and to emphasize "the simplicity of gradient sampling as an extension of the steepest descent method" (p. 1). The philosophy: nonsmooth functions of interest (locally Lipschitz or semialgebraic) are differentiable almost everywhere, so a gradient oracle at random points is enough (p. 1). The motivating failure is that steepest descent can converge to nonstationary points of nonsmooth f (p. 2, footnote 1 citing recent inexact-line-search examples). GS stabilizes the direction with the min-norm element of the convex hull of sampled gradients (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm GS (p. 4). Sample m ≥ n + 1 points uniformly in B(x_k, ε_k), compute the min-norm g_k in the convex hull of the gradients by a QP, then do a backtracking line search with the nonnormalized direction −g_k. Sampling radius and stationarity target are reduced when ‖g_k‖ ≤ ν_k, and the next iterate is perturbed if f is not differentiable there (Steps 8–10). The review states five deliberate differences from [BLO05] and [Kiw07] (p. 3). Examples: Kiwiel's min{t_k, ε_k} rule, which removes a compact-level-set assumption; nonnormalized directions, which keep the number of line-search function evaluations from growing; and requiring only differentiability, not continuous differentiability, at sampled points (pp. 3–4). The QP is shown to be "essentially the same operation required in every iteration of a bundle method" through a primal-dual pair (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is locally Lipschitz and continuously differentiable on an open set D of full measure (Theorems 3.1, 3.2 and Corollary 3.1, pp. 5–6). The review corrects the original papers. [BLO05] and [Kiw07] assume only that D is open and dense, but their proofs need full measure (p. 5, footnote 3, and Appendix A). The algorithm's requirement is also weakened from continuous differentiability to differentiability at iterates and sampled points (Appendix B, pp. 15–16). The authors argue that in practice the full-measure assumption "usually holds" for semialgebraic or tame f (p. 15). The non-Lipschitz case (e.g. the spectral abscissa) is outside the theory (§3.3, p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Global convergence results are cited and adapted (Theorem 3.1 ≈ [Kiw07, Thm 3.3]; Theorem 3.2 from [Kiw07, Thm 3.5]), and the necessary modifications are listed (pp. 5–6). (ii) Appendix A proves Property 1 (∂̄f(x) ⊆ G_ε(x) under full measure) with Clarke's characterization that excludes null sets plus Carathéodory (pp. 13–14). Example 1 builds a Lipschitz f from the indicator of an open dense set of small measure (a union of intervals around the rationals) and shows that Property 1 fails without full measure (pp. 14–15). (iii) The authors say "we do not have a counterexample to convergence of GS in the absence of this assumption" (p. 12): the counterexample is to the proof's lemma, not to convergence. (iv) The necessity of the differentiability check is shown with the [HSS16] max-type function, where landing on a kink has positive probability (p. 8, Figure 2). (v) Carathéodory explains why m ≥ n + 1 gradients are needed (p. 9).
  - Transferable: when a published proof uses an assumption that is weaker than what a key lemma needs, isolate the lemma as a named "Property", prove it under the stronger assumption, and build a one-dimensional example showing that the lemma fails without it. Say plainly whether the example also breaks the theorem (pp. 12–15).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a. The review runs no new experiments. It reproduces Figure 1 from [BLO02b] (spectral abscissa, steepest descent vs gradient bundle, p. 3) and reports others' findings in prose. For example, in [CMO17] BFGS-SQP was "much faster" than SQP-GS, but SQP-GS with enough time often found better solutions as judged by relative minimization profiles, more so on non-Lipschitz problems (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: Strong continuity with [BLO02b], [BLO05] and [Kiw07]. Kiwiel's changes are adopted and credited one by one (p. 3). The oversight in the founding papers is corrected openly, and the people who found it are named (p. 5, footnote 3). Extensions are surveyed neutrally: Riemannian GS [HU17], SQP-GS [CO12] and its feasible two-phase variant [TLJL14], derivative-free variants [Kiw10, HN13] and manifold sampling (pp. 10–11). Applications are drawn from hanso/hifoo and robot path planning (pp. 11–12).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A single theme repeated at the start and the end: "the foundation of the approach is fundamentally simple in nature" (pp. 2, 12). The algorithm differences are given as a numbered list with reasons (pp. 3–4). Technical corrections are pushed to appendices so that the main text stays readable (pp. 5–6, 13–16). The weakness is stated bluntly ("Its main weakness is its per-iteration cost", p. 6) and immediately followed by the enhancements that address it (§4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a consolidated algorithm statement, a corrected theory (full-measure assumption, differentiability-only requirement), and a map of enhancements (trust-region-restricted line search, avoiding the differentiability check, adaptive sampling with a constant number of gradients, quasi-Newton and BB variants; §4, pp. 7–10) and extensions (§5). Open questions: finite termination with a fixed radius (p. 6), non-Lipschitz semialgebraic f (pp. 7, 12), why GS does well on spectral-abscissa problems with high multiplicity (p. 7), and links to stochastic-gradient analysis of smoothed objectives and to Davis–Drusvyatskiy model-based methods (pp. 12–13). A caution: stochastic analyses use predetermined stepsizes, while GS's line search is "crucial to its practical success" (p. 13).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ (evidence: SQP-GS updates the penalty parameter so that either the penalty settles and limits are feasible and stationary, or it goes to zero and limits are stationary for a constraint-violation measure, inside one algorithm, p. 10). Method 4 ✅ (evidence: the BFGS-SQP vs SQP-GS verdict is given by relative minimization profiles, not by speed or termination, p. 12). Method 3 ⚠ (variant: the deliberately built "breaking" object is a counterexample to a proof property, Example 1, pp. 14–15, not a constructed test problem). Also: Heuristic 2 (the cost of m ≥ n + 1 gradients is named as the main weakness, p. 6, and the next target is a constant number of gradients per iteration with warm-started QPs and reused gradients, p. 9). Heuristic 5 (the fast BFGS phase without guarantees is kept beside the guarantee-carrying GS, pp. 11–12). New-pattern candidate: "correct the founding proof in public" (footnote 3 p. 5; Appendices A–B; Conclusion p. 12).
**Transferable techniques**:

### S018 · ADMM for multiaffine constrained optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2020, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2019.1683553; arXiv:1802.09592)
**Read level**: full, with skimming stated. **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Wenbo Gao and Donald Goldfarb (Columbia IEOR). Curtis (Lehigh) is third author (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an outlier in his body of work. It is an ADMM convergence paper with nonconvex, nonsmooth objectives and multiaffine (bilinear and higher) constraints. Curtis's support is listed as DOE DE-SC0010615 and NSF CCF-1618717 (p. 1). His individual contribution is not stated in the text, so this card is weak evidence for his personal habits.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Existing ADMM theory, including the nonconvex multiblock results, assumes linear constraints (p. 2). But if all variables except one are fixed, a multiaffine constraint is still affine in the remaining variable, so ADMM "would behave similarly" (p. 2). Applications motivate the class. The NMF bilinear formulation of Boyd et al., whose convergence "appears to have been an open question" (pp. 2–3); dictionary learning and convolutional DL (pp. 4–5); nonconvex reformulations of convex problems such as RPCA and Burer–Monteiro max-cut (p. 5); risk-parity portfolios (pp. 5–6); and neural-network training through the splitting of [53], which has no convergence proof (p. 6).
  - Transferable: find a structural property that the existing theory actually uses (here, affine in each block when the others are fixed) and widen the problem class to everything that has that property (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Plain multiblock ADMM (Algorithm 2, p. 7) on problem (P) with variables X (blocks X0…Xn) and a final block Z = (Z0, Z1, Z2) that enters through injective or "absorbing" linear maps Q1 and Q2 (p. 4). The algorithm itself is not changed. The contribution is the problem structure (P) and the analysis. Modelling moves supply the structure: slack variables penalized by a smooth function (p. 10) and splittings that make every subproblem closed-form (Appendix D, pp. 32–33). Section 4.2.6 shows that the extra constraint X_ℓ = Z3 is equivalent to adding a proximal term, which links the assumptions to proximal ADMM (p. 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1 (p. 7): subproblems attain their optimum for large ρ (A1.1); Im(Q) ⊇ Im(A) (A1.2); φ coercive on the feasible set; ψ(Z) = h(Z0) + g1(Z_S) + g2(Z2) with h convex lsc, g1 strongly convex and g2 Lipschitz differentiable (A1.3). Assumption 2 (p. 8) adds the split f = F(smooth) + Σ f_i and, for each block, either "strengthened convexity" (Definition 5.13, p. 13, weaker than strong convexity, e.g. ‖x‖³, p. 13) or an injective linear appearance. The authors admit the assumptions "are admittedly long and somewhat involved" (p. 9) and tabulate them in Appendix C (pp. 31–32). Exactness is lost: exact penalties such as λ‖Z‖₁ fail A1.3, so the slack reformulation is not exact (p. 10). The authors suggest this "may be a limitation inherent to ADMM" (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) A three-level layering of general ADMM facts (§6, pp. 15–20): smooth constraints and general f; multiaffine constraints, giving the exact augmented-Lagrangian change through the block-linear term C_U (Lemma 6.8, pp. 16–17; Remark 6.9 explains why this fails for general nonlinear constraints); then separable f. (ii) Lemma 6.12 / Condition 6.13 (pp. 17–18): an "absorbing" sub-block Y1 that can restore feasibility and is smooth in ψ gives a lower bound on the augmented Lagrangian for ρ large. (iii) A sufficient-decrease argument: the Z-update decrease dominates the W-update increase for ρ large (Lemma 7.5, pp. 21–22), then summability gives ‖Z − Z⁺‖ → 0 and feasibility of limit points (Corollary 7.8, p. 23). (iv) The KŁ property via Attouch–Bolte–Svaiter (Theorem 5.23, H1–H2) for convergence of the whole sequence (Theorem 4.5, pp. 9, 24–25). (v) Tightness. Theorem 4.6 (p. 9; proof A.1, p. 28): min x² + y² s.t. xy = 1 makes ADMM go to (0, 0) with w → −∞ when A1.2 fails, even though the objective is strongly convex. The authors also cite [57]'s divergence example for nonsmooth final blocks (p. 10). (vi) An audit of others' assumptions. Remark 6.6 (p. 16) shows that "successive differences → 0", which [29, 58] assume, is by itself enough for stationarity of limit points, so it is "a very strong assumption".
  - Transferable: for each assumption in your main theorem, either give a small example where dropping it breaks convergence (two variables are enough here: xy = 1) or say explicitly that it is "not tight" and why you kept it (simplicity and checkability) (pp. 9–11).
  - Transferable: test a rival analysis's assumption by proving that it alone implies the conclusion; if it does, the assumption is doing the work (p. 16).
- **D5 Experiments**: Appendix E (pp. 33–37), labelled a "Numerical Demonstration". Synthetic spherical blind deconvolution with circular convolution. Two formulations are compared: ADMM-exact (SBD0, which violates A1.3) and ADMM-slack (SBD1, which satisfies the assumptions) (p. 34). Three noiseless instances (Figures 2–4) and three noisy ones (Figures 5–7) show the objective over 60 s and snapshots of the recovered kernel (pp. 34–37). Result: the two are similar on noiseless data, and the empirical rate is sublinear (p. 34). On noisy data ADMM-exact "eventually begins to diverge" as the multipliers grow, while ADMM-slack converges (p. 36). There is no external baseline, no repeated-run statistics, and the instance sizes and parameter values do not appear legibly in the text layer.
  - Transferable: when your theory forces a reformulation (here a slack), test the theory-compliant and the "natural" formulation on data where the natural one's exactness assumption is violated (noise), so that the reformulation's cost and benefit are both visible (pp. 34–36).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends nonconvex linearly-constrained ADMM [57, 33, 28, 25]. It claims assumptions "no stronger than" those used there (p. 7) and that the basic content of the assumptions is "fundamentally the same as in the linear case" (p. 9). It cites the 3-block divergence example [9] as the reason A1.2 is "close to indispensable" (p. 9). The criticism of [6] is specific: its conditions do not guarantee that subproblems have solutions, with the counterexample in [11] (p. 9). [53] is criticized for having no convergence proof (p. 6). The paper does not compare against Bolte–Sabach–Teboulle's monitored Lagrangian methods [4], which are mentioned only (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Main results first (§4) with a long "Discussion of Assumptions" that goes through them one by one and says for each whether it is tight (§4.2, pp. 9–11). Technical material is quarantined ("The reader may wish to proceed directly to Section 6", p. 12). There are worked modelling examples for every application (§3, Appendix D). Hedges are calibrated ("we suspect that AL/M1 is tight", p. 10; "not tight", pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory (convergence to constrained stationary points, and to a single point under KŁ) for a new problem class, plus modelling recipes with closed-form subproblems. Limits are stated concretely: A1.2 and A1.3 are needed, so exact penalties are excluded (p. 10); ρ must be "sufficiently large", with an explicit sufficient bound (Remarks 4.2 and 4.4, pp. 7, 9); ReLU networks need smoothing or the Appendix B formulation (p. 6); and the alternative assumptions are not tight (pp. 10–11). The §6 results are offered as tools for other structured problems (pp. 9, 15). No rate theory is given beyond the sublinear observation in Appendix E (p. 34).
