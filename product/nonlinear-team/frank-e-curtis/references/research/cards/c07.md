### S029 · An inexact sequential quadratic optimization algorithm for nonlinear optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2014, 10.1137/130918320)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–34: introduction, Algorithms A, 1, 2, all of §3 including the proofs of Lemmas 3.4–3.28 and Theorem 3.29, §4 experiments, conclusion; reference list skimmed). Figure 1 exists only as a caption in the text layer (p. 31), so the plot itself was not read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Travis C. Johnson (Northwestern ESAM), Daniel P. Robinson (Johns Hopkins AMS), Andreas Wächter (Northwestern IEMS) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Curtis at Lehigh ISE (p. 1); received April 24, 2013, accepted April 11, 2014 (p. 1). It extends the inexact SQP line for equality constraints (refs [7, 8, 14], p. 2) and the inexact interior-point line ([13, 15], p. 2) to equality and inequality constraints in an SQP framework, built on the exact two-QP infeasibility-detecting SQP of Burke–Curtis–Wang ([5], p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is that classical SQO methods solve the QP subproblem exactly in every iteration (p. 1). The paper asks which loose conditions on an inexact primal-dual QP solution still guarantee global convergence, "so that some amount of inexactness is always allowed (at suboptimal primal-dual points)" (pp. 1–2). A second requirement is that the method keeps the automatic transition to the feasibility problem (FP) when (NLP) is locally infeasible, which the authors say is "important in any modern optimization algorithm" because it gives useful information when models or data are inconsistent (p. 2). The gap is stated against the authors' own prior method: [5] "requires exact QP solutions (in order to ensure rapid infeasibility detection)" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: enter by taking your group's own exact method and asking for the weakest per-iteration accuracy test that keeps every one of its guarantees, including the infeasible case.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The paper presents two algorithms on purpose. Algorithm A (p. 8) is an exact SQO method with at most two QPs per iteration: a penalty QP (PQP) and, when needed, a feasibility QP (FQP) whose step serves as a reference for achievable linearized feasibility progress (pp. 4–5). Three scenarios A–C decide the step and the penalty update (pp. 6–7). Scenario C takes a convex combination τd_PQP + (1−τ)d_FQP and decreases μ, which the paper calls "reminiscent of" steering (p. 7). The inexact method (iSQO, Algorithm 2, p. 14) replaces exact solves by four termination tests on the QP residual ρ_k: an inexact-Newton-type relative residual bound (2.17), multipliers kept in [−e, e] and [0, e], and a model-reduction condition (2.19) (p. 9). Test 2 tightens the tolerances before the penalty parameter may change (p. 9). Test 3 allows a dual-only update when x_k is stationary for the penalty function (p. 10), and Test 4 governs the FQP (p. 10). These tests feed six scenarios (pp. 11–12). Scenarios 1, 5 and 6 are new: they give null primal steps with dual and penalty updates, "required in order to have a well-posed and globally convergent algorithm when inexact QP solutions are allowed" (p. 12). The convexification (H ← H + ξI whenever ½dᵀHd < θ‖d‖², Algorithm 1 step 4, p. 13) also goes inside the QP loop. What is unchanged: the ℓ1 exact penalty merit function, the Armijo backtracking on it (2.14), the FJ-function/penalty-parameter μ framework, and the "up to two QPs" structure of [5] (pp. 2, 7). The authors intend Algorithms 1 and 2 to be "fully integrated", with the tests checked inside the QP solver (p. 13).
  - Transferable: write the exact algorithm first as a scaffold with named scenarios, then list exactly which extra scenarios the inexact version needs and why (here: never require an exact solve at a point that is stationary for the penalty function, p. 8).
  - Transferable: separate multiplier estimates for the optimality problem and the feasibility problem, so each stationarity residual is measured on its own (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, c, c̄ are C¹ on an open convex set containing the iterates and trial points (Assumption 3.1, p. 14). For global convergence, functions and first derivatives are bounded and Lipschitz (Assumption 3.15, p. 19) and Hessian approximations are bounded (Assumption 3.16, p. 19). The QP solver is an assumption, not an algorithm. Assumptions 3.2–3.3 (pp. 14–15) only require that repeated inner iterations with fixed H drive ρ_k arbitrarily small with an objective value no worse than d = 0; the authors call this "a reasonable assumption for many QP solvers" (p. 15). MFCQ enters only in the outcome classification (Definition 3.27, Lemma 3.28, p. 27). No second-order or local-rate assumption, and no local convergence result.
  - Transferable: state the inner solver's requirements as a short assumption on its iterates (residual → 0 with objective no worse than zero) so that any solver meeting it plugs in.
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: Well-posedness comes first (§3.1). Lemma 3.4 shows that convexification triggers finitely often (p. 15). Lemmas 3.5, 3.7 and 3.8 show that each termination test is reachable in each relevant case (pp. 15–17). Lemma 3.9 shows that every call to the inner solver terminates (p. 17), and Lemma 3.10 that exactly one scenario occurs, which defines the index sets K_i (pp. 17–18). The penalty update keeps μ positive and nonincreasing (Lemma 3.11, p. 18). −Δl_k serves as a surrogate for the directional derivative of φ (Lemma 3.12, cited to Burke [3, 4], p. 18), so the line search is well-posed (Lemma 3.13, Theorem 3.14, pp. 18–19). Global convergence (§3.2) uses a step-size lower bound α_k ≥ ωΔl_k (Lemma 3.18, p. 20). A shifted penalty function ϕ(x, μ) = μ(f(x) − f̲) + v(x) is monotone across penalty decreases (Lemma 3.19, p. 21), which gives Δl_k → 0 and d → 0 (Lemma 3.20, p. 21). The workhorse is a generic sequence lemma (Lemma 3.21, pp. 21–22): if r_{k+1} ≤ κr_k + e_k on an infinite set K, r_{k+1} ≤ max{r_k, r̄_k} off K, and e_k, r̄_k → 0, then r_k → 0. It is used to drive both the feasibility residual (Lemma 3.22, pp. 22–24, a two-case proof with a contradiction subcase built on ζ₁ = (ζ+1)/2) and the penalty residual to zero (Lemma 3.23, pp. 25–26). Two further steps classify the outcomes when μ → 0: limit points are all feasible or all infeasible (Lemma 3.24, p. 26), and a nonzero multiplier at a feasible FJ point means MFCQ fails (Lemma 3.28, p. 27). Theorem 3.29 lists three outcomes: KKT or infeasible stationary point with μ bounded away from zero, infeasible stationary point with μ → 0, or FJ points where MFCQ fails (p. 27). Corollary 3.30 gives KKT points under MFCQ (pp. 27–28). Some test conditions are designed backwards from the proof: conditions (a), (b) of Test 2 and (2.27) of Test 3 exist so that μ_k → 0 with v_k → 0 forces MFCQ failure (p. 10).
  - Transferable: Lemma 3.21's "contract on an infinite subsequence, don't grow elsewhere" sequence lemma, for residuals that only some iteration types reduce.
  - Transferable: the shifted penalty ϕ = μ(f − f̲) + v, whose monotonicity survives decreases of μ (Lemma 3.19).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: A Matlab prototype (p. 28). No iterative QP solver met the requirements or offered an interface for the tests (p. 28). So inexactness was emulated: exact bqpd solutions were perturbed by 0.5^j·u, with u uniform in (−1, 1), for the first j at which a test holds (eq. 4.2, p. 29). The unperturbed run is the exact comparator in the same code (p. 29). Ipopt's problem scaling is adopted ([44, §3.8], p. 28), as is the τ bisection from [5] (p. 28). The infeasibility exit (4.1c) additionally requires μ_k ≤ μ̲, because otherwise the code would stop early as infeasible on problems it could still solve (pp. 28–29). The test set is 309 CUTE AMPL problems with at least one free variable and one general constraint, n + m ≤ 20,000, and no bqpd failure in any of the four variants (p. 29). dallasm and dallass were dropped for AMPL evaluation errors (footnote, p. 29). Presolve was off (p. 29). Table 2 gives exit counts: optimal 289/291/293/293 and infeasible 8/7/6/6 for exact and κ = 0.01/0.1/0.5, about 96% success (pp. 29–30). Table 3 measures how inexact the accepted solves really were. The minimum and the geometric-mean residual ratio κ_I per problem are binned: for κ = 0.01 the per-problem minimum lay in [1e−3, 0.01) for 277 problems (p. 30), against about 1e−16 for exact bqpd solves (p. 30). Iterations are compared with the log₂ outperforming factor of Morales [39] (p. 30): all but 13 problems lie within a factor of two, and almost 100 have identical counts (p. 31). Fig. 1 orders problems by size and finds the factor "independent from problem size" (p. 31). No CPU time is reported and no cost per QP is measured. The claimed saving is conditional: "may be an overall reduction in computational costs" in "a more sophisticated implementation" (p. 28).
  - Transferable: when no inner solver supports your tests yet, emulate inexactness by perturbing exact solutions geometrically until the test is just met, and run the exact solution in the same code as the comparator.
  - Transferable: report a distribution of the accepted inexactness (the κ_I bins), so readers can see that the tests really allowed loose solves.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in three lines: inexact Newton for equations ([16, 42]), inexact Newton for constrained optimization ([29, 31, 37, 41]), and ℓ1 exact penalty SQO ([20, 27, 28, 43] and recent [10, 11, 24–26, 40]) (p. 2). It also scopes out inexact-derivative SQO ([2, 17, 30, 32, 45, 46]), "not in the scope of this paper" (p. 2). Its own earlier inexact methods ([7, 8, 14, 13, 15]) are named as the technique's precursors (p. 2). The closest predecessor, [5], is credited for the two-QP structure and the penalty update and differs only in requiring exact QP solutions (p. 2). The steering literature ([5, 9, 11, 12, 22]) is cited for the convex-combination condition (p. 7). No rival inexact-SQP code is compared numerically.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The novelty sentence is in the abstract and repeated in the introduction and conclusion ("The novel feature ...", pp. 1, 31). The exact algorithm is introduced explicitly as motivation: "Algorithm A still represents the foundation for our iSQO method, so it has been presented as motivation and for reference" (p. 8). Each test is followed by a paragraph on why it exists, often pointing to the lemma that needs it (pp. 9–12). Hedging is dense in the numerical section ("preliminary implementation", "suggest", "may", pp. 28, 31–32), and the claim is kept to reliability and iterations.
  - Transferable: after each algorithmic condition, add one sentence that names the lemma requiring it. Readers then see which conditions are for the proof and which are for practice.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and theoretical (loose termination tests, well-posedness, global convergence including infeasible and MFCQ-failure outcomes), with a proof-of-concept experiment (pp. 31–32). Limits are visible in the text rather than discussed. Inexactness is simulated, not produced by an iterative QP solver (pp. 28–29). No timing is reported (p. 28). There is no local convergence analysis (not stated as a limit). The method "does not entirely reduce to Algorithm A if exact QP solutions are computed" (p. 8). Future directions: real-time optimal control with hot-started QPs [34], where Jacobian-vector products are cheap, and large-scale problems with QP solvers that give good inexact solutions quickly (p. 32).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): the QP solver's termination tests are derived from the ℓ1-penalty model reduction and residual ratios the outer method needs (pp. 9–10), with the explicit design intent that "the QP solver iterates until the needs of the (outer) algorithm are met" (p. 13); convexification is also moved inside the inner loop (p. 13).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): steps 4–6 of Method 1 are not done here. The baseline is exact-versus-perturbed in one code, not relative-residual stopping rules, and no production integration or timing is attempted. Inexactness is emulated by perturbation because no suitable QP solver existed (pp. 28–29).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): one algorithm moves automatically to minimizing ℓ1 infeasibility when (NLP) is locally infeasible (p. 2). The penalty update is a test on model-predicted progress against an FQP reference step (Scenario C/4, (2.12)–(2.13), pp. 7, 12).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the comparator differs only in the inner-solve accuracy, with the exact solution in the same code (p. 29), and Ipopt's scaling is adopted (p. 28).
- Heuristic 10 ✅ (evidence): the exact twin (Algorithm A) is built first, and only the broken component (exact QP solves) is replaced (pp. 3, 8).
- Heuristic 7 ⚠ (variant): the test set excludes problems on which bqpd failed in any variant, an outcome-based but symmetric cut, with no pre-filter count given (p. 29).
- new-pattern candidate: "Scenario-partitioned iteration analysis": each iteration falls into exactly one named scenario, a lemma proves exhaustiveness, and the analysis runs over the index sets K_i (pp. 6–7, 11–12, 17–18).
- new-pattern candidate: "Emulated inner inexactness": an exact inner solution is perturbed until the test is just satisfied, to test the theory's tests before an inner solver supports them (p. 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Design the inexact method as "exact algorithm + extra scenarios that make an exact solve never necessary" and say which scenarios would be vacuous under exact solves (pp. 8, 12).
- Use the generic sequence lemma (Lemma 3.21, pp. 21–22) for any residual that is contracted only on some iteration types.
- Make the infeasibility exit require a small penalty parameter as well, so the code does not stop early on problems it could still solve (pp. 28–29).
- Report per-problem minimum and geometric-mean inner residual ratios in bins (Table 3, p. 30) to show that the inexactness was real.
- Compare iteration counts with the log₂ outperforming factor, ordered by problem size, to check whether inexactness hurts more on larger problems (pp. 30–31).

**Quotes**:
- "In this manner, the QP solver iterates until the needs of the (outer) algorithm are met." (p. 13)
- "We disabled the AMPL presolve feature to maintain the idiosyncrasies of each formulation." (p. 29)

### S008 · A quasi-Newton algorithm for nonconvex, nonsmooth optimization with global convergence guarantees (Mathematical Programming Computation 2015, 10.1007/S12532-015-0086-2)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–28: introduction, Algorithms 1–5, Lemmas 2.2–2.8, the full proof of Theorem 3.1, §4 implementation, test set, results with Tables 4–5, conclusion; reference list pp. 28–30 skimmed). Performance-profile figures (p. 26) exist only as axis labels in the text layer, so the curves were not read; statements about them come from the authors' text (p. 25).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Xiaocun Que (Lehigh ISE; corresponding author, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received 27 May 2014, accepted 5 May 2015 (p. 1). It follows Curtis–Que's adaptive gradient sampling ([14], p. 2) and the Curtis–Overton SQP-GS line. It restores guarantees for the BFGS-on-nonsmooth practice of Lewis–Overton ([35], p. 2), the step SKILL.md Heuristic 5 already names (DOI 10.1007/s12532-015-0086-2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is an observed practice that lacks theory: "many have witnessed good performance of BFGS when solving nonsmooth problems [24,25], despite the fact that global convergence guarantees for the algorithm in this context are rather limited [35]" (p. 2). Two deficiencies of plain BFGS are named: no termination test tied to a stationarity measure, and inverse Hessians that may tend to singularity near nonsmooth solutions (p. 2). Gradient sampling (GS) has guarantees, but each iteration "is significantly more expensive than a BFGS iteration" and lacks variable-metric curvature (pp. 2–3). The problem class is locally Lipschitz f, C¹ on an open dense set D (Assumption 2.1, p. 4).
  - Transferable: enter where practitioners already trust a method without theory, and name the exact defects (no certificate, possible singular metric) the theory must repair.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The method is a hybrid that is "an unadulterated BFGS strategy for the majority of the iterations" and adds adaptive GS only when monitored conditions fail (p. 2). The direction minimizes a piecewise-quadratic model over sampled gradients with metric W_k. It is computed from the dual QP as d_k = −W_kG_ky_k, so W_k⁻¹ is never needed. With one sample point it reduces to the BFGS step (2.2) (pp. 5–6). The sampling radius is reduced only when ‖G_ky_k‖_W ≤ νε_k, the curvature test (2.6b) holds and α_k > 0 (2.6, p. 7). A weak-Wolfe line search drops the curvature condition after J̲ trials and returns α = 0 if the sample set is still small (Algorithm 2, p. 9). An iterate-perturbation step keeps iterates in D (Algorithm 3, p. 9). The sample set resets to {x_{k+1}} when the curvature test and α_k ≥ α̲ hold, and otherwise grows by random points in the ε-ball (Algorithm 4, p. 11). The Hessian update is damped BFGS in good iterations and otherwise a damped, update-skipping L-BFGS rebuilt from w_kI with a provably bounded condition number (Algorithm 5, pp. 12–13; Lemma 2.8, p. 14). What is unchanged: the BFGS formula and weak-Wolfe search in good iterations. What is borrowed: the adaptive GS machinery and bounded L-BFGS from [14] (pp. 5, 13).
  - Transferable: make the safeguard dormant. The algorithm reduces to the trusted fast method whenever monitored tests (curvature bound, large enough step) pass, and adds sampling only when they fail.
  - Transferable: when the metric must be bounded for the proof, swap to a freshly initialized, update-skipping L-BFGS matrix only in the iterations where boundedness matters (Algorithm 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Locally Lipschitz f that is continuously differentiable on an open dense D (Assumption 2.1, p. 4). Clarke ε-stationarity is the target (p. 4). The theory requires the curvature threshold ξ to satisfy the bound (3.1), which depends on the L-BFGS parameters (p. 15). The implementation knowingly violates it: "our model curvature threshold ξ does not satisfy the upper bound in (3.1)" (p. 20). The implementation also never checks membership in D (p. 20). Lemma 2.4, which drops the line-search truncation, needs weak lower semismoothness, an assumption the authors "do not wish to make" (p. 10).
  - Transferable: when the code departs from the analyzed algorithm (parameter outside the proved range, unchecked set membership), say so in the implementation section next to the parameter table.
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: Well-posedness: Lemma 2.2 gives descent and ‖G_ky_k‖_W = ‖d_k‖_{W⁻¹} (p. 7). Lemma 2.3 shows the line search and perturbation terminate with probability one (p. 10). Lemma 2.7 and Lemma 2.8 give eigenvalue bounds for each damped L-BFGS update and for m updates (2.20) (pp. 13–14), proved by induction over the m updates (p. 14). Lemma 3.2 collects the well-posedness facts (p. 15). Lemma 3.3 gives infinitely many positive steps, by contradiction with sample-set growth (p. 16). The main Theorem 3.1 says: with probability one either f → −∞, or ε_k → 0 and every cluster point is stationary (p. 15). Its proof splits into Case 1, infinitely many "good BFGS" iterations, where sufficient decrease forces d_k → 0 (p. 17). In Case 2 the bounded L-BFGS matrices make (2.6b) hold always (p. 17). Assuming ε_k stays fixed, the iterates form a Cauchy sequence (p. 18). The two subcases (x̄ ε-stationary or not) are then shown to be probability-zero events, using Lemma 3.5 (an approximate least W-norm element lemma, p. 16), Lemma 3.6 (a nonempty set of good sample sets near x̄, cited to [14, 32], p. 17) and Lebourg's mean value theorem (pp. 18–19). The final step, ε_k → 0 implies stationary cluster points, is "exactly that of [14, Theorem 4.2, Case 2]" (p. 19). No rate or complexity result.
  - Transferable: prove "the radius is eventually reduced" by assuming it stays constant and showing that the needed bad event recurs on infinitely many independently resampled sets, so it has probability zero (pp. 18–19).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: C++ code bfgs-gs (armadillo, a specialized active-set QP solver adapted from Kiwiel [31]) on a stated machine (p. 20). Parameters are in Table 2 (p. 20). Several were chosen because they "worked well in our tests": p̄ = 100, ε₀ = 0.1, and p_{k+1} ← 5 (pp. 20–21). Rivals: hanso-bfgs (plain BFGS), hanso-default (BFGS then GS) and lmbm (limited-memory bundle). All run at defaults except iteration limits set "on par" (pp. 21–22). The test set has 26 scalable problems with n = 50 (n = 64 for EIG_PROD), each from 10 starting points, giving 260 runs (pp. 22–23). CPU time is ignored because the codes differ in language. The measures are iterations and function and gradient evaluations, with an argument that per-iteration costs are similar (pp. 23–24). The paper reports termination-flag counts by type (Table 4, p. 25): bfgs-gs(1e−4) hits its stationarity test 253/260 times, while lmbm stops on "other" 240/260 times. Profiles treat all runs as successful, because flag-based success would favour the authors' own code (p. 25). Solution quality is judged independently: for every final iterate, 10³ fresh uniform points in a 10⁻²-ball give the minimum-norm element of the convex hull of gradients, and geometric means over the 10 runs are reported per problem (Table 5, pp. 26–27). The authors use fresh random points rather than algorithm iterates to obtain "a fairer measure" (pp. 26–27). Their own code is run at two tolerances (ε_f = 1e−4 and 1e−6) to match the quality of lmbm and HANSO respectively, instead of retuning everyone (p. 24). Losses are printed: for "a few starting points, our solver performs poorly on problem 20" (p. 28). Table 5 shows bfgs-gs(1e−6) far worse than HANSO on problems 6, 20 and 22–24 (p. 27).
  - Transferable: certify solution quality by an external measure computed identically for every solver, from fresh random samples around each final iterate, and use geometric means over repeated starts (pp. 26–27).
  - Transferable: when rivals' stopping rules are incomparable, run your own code at two tolerances that bracket the rivals' quality, rather than tuning the rivals (p. 24).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Lewis–Overton's BFGS for nonsmooth problems ([35]), presented as encouraging results that motivate the design (p. 2). Also builds on Burke–Lewis–Overton GS ([7]) and Curtis–Que adaptive GS ([14]) (pp. 2–3). GS is distinguished from bundle methods because GS subproblems involve no cutting planes "even when the objective function is convex" (p. 3). HANSO's two-phase design is described as an algorithm "that switches from BFGS to a GS method", with guarantees similar to the new method, while plain BFGS has guarantees "limited to only a few types of simple problems" (p. 21). Predecessors are credited by restatement; there is no direct criticism.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The design intent is stated as a feature ("This feature is intentional", p. 2), and each subroutine's motivation precedes its algorithm box (pp. 8–12). Hedging in the results is moderate and comparative. The results are framed as "versatility" (p. 24), and the conclusion says "competitive with—and in some ways outperforms" (p. 28). Methodological caveats are argued in the text (why flags are not trusted, why CPU is ignored, why random points), not put in footnotes (pp. 24–27).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic, theoretical (convergence with probability one) and software (C++ code) (pp. 1, 28). Stated limits: proofs rely on the bounded L-BFGS fallback (p. 28), the implementation violates the ξ bound (p. 20), and some problems and starts go badly (problem 20, p. 28). Tuning effort on the 26-problem set is not reported (pp. 20–21). Future direction: let ξ and μ̲ tend to zero and μ̄ to infinity with the sampling radius while keeping the guarantees. This was not proposed because it is more complicated and "we did not see any benefits of such a strategy in any numerical experiments that we performed" (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ (evidence): termination flags are explicitly not trusted as success indicators, because they would favour the authors' own code (p. 25). Solution quality is judged by an algorithm-independent ε-stationarity estimate built from fresh random samples (pp. 26–27). This is the 2015 journal source of the passage SKILL.md quotes from "Que thesis 2016" in Method 4 step 4.
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): repeated runs (10 starts per problem) and geometric means (pp. 22, 27); losses printed (problem 20, p. 28; Table 5, p. 27); iteration limits set on par for all codes (p. 22).
- Method 5 ⚠ (variant): rivals run at defaults while the authors' parameters were chosen on the evaluation set ("worked well in our tests", pp. 20–21), and the tuning effort is not reported. This matches the 03 PE13 pattern SKILL.md already flags.
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant, nonsmooth analogue): one algorithm that adds sampling inside BFGS iterations when monitored tests fail, against hanso-default's BFGS-then-GS switch (pp. 2, 21). The "steer, don't switch" preference carried to unconstrained nonsmooth optimization, where there is no penalty parameter.
- Heuristic 5 ✅ (evidence): a fast method without guarantees (BFGS on nonsmooth problems) is kept as the comparator (hanso-bfgs), and guarantees are restored by a hybrid (pp. 2, 21).
- Heuristic 6 ✅ (evidence): "we ignore CPU time and focus on the performance measures of iterations, function evaluations, and gradient evaluations required until termination" (p. 24); SKILL.md attributes this wording to the Que thesis 2016, and it already appears here.
- new-pattern candidate: "Dormant safeguard": the guaranteed method reduces to the unadulterated fast method whenever monitored tests pass (p. 2; Algorithms 4–5, pp. 11, 13).
- new-pattern candidate: "Disclosed theory–code gap": the implementation section names where the code leaves the proved parameter range or skips a check (ξ outside (3.1), no D test; p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Count termination flags by type in a table before showing profiles, and say why flag-based success would bias the profile (Table 4, p. 25).
- Independent ε-stationarity certificate for every solver's output: 10³ uniform points in a radius-10⁻² ball, min-norm element of the gradient hull, and geometric mean over runs (pp. 26–27).
- Bracket rivals' solution quality by running your own code at two tolerances (p. 24).
- Swap to a bounded, update-skipping L-BFGS matrix only when the proof needs boundedness (Algorithm 5, Lemma 2.8, pp. 13–14).

**Quotes**:
- "A feature critical to the practical performance of our algorithm is that, when it is applied to solve many problem instances, the algorithm reduces to an unadulterated BFGS strategy for the majority of the iterations." (p. 2)
- "if we only considered a termination flag of type (1) to be the indicator for a successful run, then the profiles would be skewed in favor of the codes that yielded such a flag most often" (p. 25)

### S019 · An adaptive augmented Lagrangian method for large-scale constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming 2015, 10.1007/S10107-014-0784-Y)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–43: introduction, §2 basic AL, §3 algorithm and well-posedness proofs line by line (pp. 6–18), global-convergence lemmas (pp. 18–32), Theorem 3.14 and discussion (pp. 32–33), all of §4 and the conclusion; references skimmed). Lemmas 3.8–3.13 (pp. 21–32) were read for statements and case structure. Their algebra was skimmed, not checked line by line. Figs. 1–2 (Lancelot and AAL-TR output for CATENA, pp. 36, 38) are only captions in the text layer. Figs. 3–6 are reduced to axis labels. Statements about them come from the authors' text (pp. 37, 39, 41).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Hao Jiang, Daniel P. Robinson (Johns Hopkins AMS) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received 10 December 2012, accepted 15 April 2014 (p. 1). It transfers the steering technique of exact penalty methods (Byrd–López-Calva–Nocedal [7], Byrd–Nocedal–Waltz [8], Mongeau–Sartenaer [34]; p. 2 and reference list pp. 43–44) to augmented Lagrangians. SKILL.md Method 2 lists it under "Practice" (DOI 10.1007/s10107-014-0784-y).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The AL revival rests on matrix-free implementation and local convergence under weak assumptions (p. 2). The entry point is a practical defect: with a poor initial penalty parameter or multipliers, "little or no progress is made in the primal space", and effort is wasted, "especially in early iterations" (p. 2). The paper narrows its own claim at once: with well-scaled constraints "basic AL methods are quite efficient", so the enhancements help most "for difficult and/or poorly scaled problems" (p. 2). The defect is made concrete on a generic Algorithm 1 (p. 5). The penalty parameter changes only after a full (nonlinear) AL minimization, so an iteration may achieve nothing but a decrease of μ (p. 5).
  - Transferable: write the incumbent as a one-page generic algorithm and point to the exact line (here line 18 to 21) where effort is wasted.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The paper moves the penalty decision from the outer (major) loop into each trust-region iteration. A steering step r_k minimizes a Gauss–Newton model of the constraint violation in a radius θ_k ∝ ‖F_FEAS‖ (eqs. 3.1, 3.4–3.5, p. 6) and only needs Cauchy decrease (Algorithm 2, p. 7). The trial step s_k approximately minimizes the AL model (3.8) in a radius Θ_k = Γ_k·min{δ_k, δ‖F_AL‖} (p. 8). μ is decreased inside the iteration until the condition (3.11c) holds: Δq_v(s_k) ≥ min{κ₃Δq_v(r_k), v_k − ½(κ_t t_j)²} (p. 9). The second term lets the trial step increase violation when the current violation is well within the target t_j (p. 9). Multipliers are updated only when ‖c‖ ≤ t_j and a stationarity target T_j is met, with safeguarded bounds Y_j (pp. 10–11). The penalty parameter is only ever decreased; allowing increases "would be a heuristic and not theoretically interesting" (p. 9). What is unchanged: the AL function, bound-constrained subproblems, matrix-free capability (abstract; p. 2), trust-region acceptance (p. 10). The steering step's cost is called "minor" because it can be computed in parallel with s_k and needs only Cauchy decrease (p. 9).
  - Transferable: transfer a parameter-update rule from one method family to another by redefining "progress toward feasibility" with that family's own model (here the Gauss–Newton model q_v) and asking only for Cauchy decrease of the reference step.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Problem min f s.t. c(x) = 0, l ≤ x ≤ u, f and c C² (p. 3). Well-posedness needs only C² at x_k (Assumption 3.1, p. 12). Global convergence assumes the iterates lie in a convex compact set where f, c are C² (Assumption 3.2, p. 18), plus bounded multipliers via Y_j (p. 18). The multiplier-convergence corollary adds an LICQ-type full-row-rank assumption on free variables (Assumption 3.3, p. 28). The authors admit the result is weaker than standard AL theory. When μ_k → 0 and the iterates are feasible, they cannot guarantee first-order points "even under the assumption that the MFCQ holds" (p. 33).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**: Well-posedness rests on the limit Lemma 3.4: as μ → 0 the AL model tends uniformly to the feasibility model, ∇_xL → Jᵀc, and the AL Cauchy step converges to the feasibility Cauchy step (3.18a–d, pp. 13–15). This happens because the Cauchy backtracking index l_{α,μ} eventually equals l_β (pp. 14–15). So the steering condition (3.11c) is eventually satisfied by Cauchy steps (Theorem 3.6, pp. 17–18). The Γ_k ∈ (1, 2] and ε_k devices in Algorithm 2 exist to make that matching work (pp. 7–8, 13). Lemma 3.5 bounds the Cauchy decrease using a curvature bound Ω, citing Moré [35, Thm 4.4] (pp. 16–17). Global convergence uses summability of AL decreases across changing μ, with Dirichlet's test for the series Σμ_k(f_k − f_{k+1}) and Σμ_k y_kᵀ(c_{k+1} − c_k) (Lemma 3.7, pp. 18–21). It then splits four ways: whether the multiplier-update set Y is finite or infinite, and whether μ is bounded away from zero or tends to zero (Lemmas 3.8, 3.9, 3.10, 3.13, pp. 21–32). The finite-Y cases use trust-region radius-bounded-away-from-zero arguments (pp. 22, 25). Lemma 3.12 is a projection scaling inequality, ‖P[x + ξv] − x‖ ≤ ξ‖P[x + v] − x‖ for ξ > 1 (pp. 30–31). Theorem 3.14 has three outcomes: infeasible stationary limit points; μ bounded away from zero with a first-order-optimal subsequence; or μ → 0 with feasible limits, where optimality needs an extra condition on how often μ decreases between multiplier updates (p. 32).
  - Transferable: to show a parameter-reduction loop terminates, prove that the problem's own model and step converge to the feasibility model and step as the parameter goes to zero (Lemma 3.4). The loop then terminates by continuity.
  - Transferable: use Dirichlet's test to sum μ-weighted decreases when μ_k decreases monotonically to zero (Lemma 3.7).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab AAL-TR (Algorithm 4) against BAL-TR, a trust-region version of the basic Algorithm 1 whose components "were mostly identical", with the same radius update and step acceptance (p. 34). For equality problems both use Steihaug–Toint CG, as Lancelot does, "for a fair comparison" (p. 34). For inequality problems both use Cplex with the same convexification (adding 10·I until convex), "used for both" (p. 34). Lancelot is excluded from the inequality comparison because its subproblem solver differs strikingly and the comparison would not "isolate the effect that steering has" (pp. 34–35). Lancelot's options were changed to match (no nonmonotone descent, no magical steps, ℓ2 trust region, inexact subproblems, same initial penalty; Table 2, pp. 36–37). Termination: the infeasibility exit also requires μ_k ≤ μ_min, to avoid stopping early at slightly infeasible points (p. 35). Problem functions are prescaled to gradient norm ≤ G (p. 35). The paper first shows one iteration-level run on CATENA (15 variables, 4 constraints, pp. 35–38). In Lancelot, iterations that only decreased the penalty "accounted for ≈79% of the iterations" (p. 37). The AAL-TR log shows μ decreasing exactly when the trial step predicts increased violation (column Dqv(s), p. 37). Equality CUTEr set: 91 problems, with every exclusion named and reasoned (pp. 38–39). Excluded are problems too large for Matlab, problems Lancelot treats as having no objective, nonlinear equations Lancelot solves without a penalty, and three problems where all solvers converged to infeasible stationary points, with both codes' iteration counts given (p. 39). Profiles in iterations and gradient evaluations (Figs. 3–4, pp. 39–40) first show that BAL-TR performs like Lancelot. This is a sanity check that the in-house baseline is not a straw man (p. 39). AAL-TR then beats both (p. 39). Table 3 tabulates final penalty values (p. 40). Inequality Hock–Schittkowski set: 64 problems (p. 41). Exclusions include simple QPs, nonsmooth problems, one LICQ failure, problems where both codes reached infeasible stationary points, and problems where "both BAL-TR and AAL-TR did not reach the desired optimality tolerances" (p. 41). Two observations of my own from the text layer. First, hs76 is listed both as eliminated and in the final 64 (p. 41). Second, the column sums of Table 3 check (91 each), and Algorithm 4 ends with μ < 10⁻² on 51 problems against 34 for Lancelot (my reading of the text-layer table, p. 40). The text nevertheless says the adaptive rule "generally does not drive the penalty parameter smaller than does Lancelot" (p. 40).
  - Transferable: before claiming a gain over a mature code, show that your in-house version of the incumbent algorithm performs like the mature code on the same set. Then compare your method to your in-house incumbent, which differs in only one component.
  - Transferable: open the experiments with one small problem's iteration log for both the incumbent and your method, with a column that shows the mechanism (here the predicted violation change Dqv(s)).
- **D6 Positioning**: AL history is credited to Hestenes and Powell (p. 2). Matrix-free AL ([1, 4, 9, 32]) and ADMM successes ([6, 38, 41]) are cited as the reason for the revival (p. 2). The method is "inspired by" exact-penalty steering [7, 8, 34] (p. 2). Stabilized SQO and AL-merit-function methods are pointed to as related, not compared (p. 3). Lancelot is described respectfully as "very mature", with "a variety of advanced features from which the implementation of our methods could also benefit" (p. 36). - **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The novelty statement ("The novel feature ... is an adaptive update for the penalty parameter") is in the abstract (p. 1) and repeated in the conclusion (p. 42). The theoretical weakness is stated plainly and defended with numerical evidence (p. 33). Fixes the authors could have made are listed with reasons for not making them, namely practical complexity and cost of multipliers in every iteration (p. 33). The experimental philosophy is stated as isolation of one mechanism (p. 34). Claims about penalty values ("does not drive the penalty parameter unnecessarily small", p. 41) are stronger than the tables appear to support (see D5).
  - Transferable: when your theory is weaker than the incumbent's, list the modifications that would recover it and say why you did not make them.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic (adaptive penalty update for AL, matrix-free compatible) plus global convergence and Matlab experiments (pp. 42–43). Stated limits: no guarantee that μ stays bounded away from zero even under MFCQ (p. 43); the Cauchy steering step may give slow convergence on infeasible problems (p. 43); weaker convergence in case (iii) than other AL methods (p. 33). Proposed remedies: switch to a traditional AL method once consistent feasibility progress is seen, to inherit its theory (pp. 33, 43); compute more accurate steering steps for infeasible problems (p. 43); parallel computation of r_k and s_k (p. 43). The claim of "large-scale" rests on matrix-free compatibility. The experiments are Matlab on CUTEr and HS problems, and the largest ones (aug2dc, dtoc3) were dropped as "too large for our Matlab implementation" (p. 38).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): the penalty update is a test on model-predicted feasibility progress inside each iteration, against a steering reference step (3.11c, pp. 9–10), not after a failed AL minimization (p. 5). It is instrumented by tabulating final penalty values (Tables 3–4, pp. 40, 42), as Method 2 step 5 prescribes.
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): the authors propose, as a remedy for their weaker theory, to "transition from our algorithm to a traditional AL approach" near solutions (pp. 33, 43). This is a switch, used to inherit guarantees, though it is proposed and not implemented.
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence): the steering subproblem needs only the Cauchy decrease the convergence proof requires (3.11b; Algorithm 2, pp. 7, 9). Both subproblems may be solved inexactly to keep the method matrix-free (pp. 7–8).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the rival basic method lives in the same framework with mostly identical components (p. 34). The mature code's settings are aligned (Table 2, pp. 36–37). The mature code is left out where subproblem solvers differ, so that the steering effect stays isolated (pp. 34–35). The in-house baseline is checked against Lancelot (p. 39).
- Method 3 ⚠ (variant): no infeasible or degenerate variants are built. Problems where codes converge to infeasible stationary points are removed from the profiles, though named, with iteration counts given (pp. 39, 41).
- Heuristic 8 ✅ (evidence): CATENA iteration logs for Lancelot and AAL-TR come before the benchmarks (pp. 35–38).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Make the parameter-reduction loop well-posed by proving that the subproblem model and Cauchy step converge to the feasibility model and step as the parameter goes to zero (Lemma 3.4, pp. 13–15).
- Let the steering condition allow an increase of linearized violation when the current violation is already well inside its target (the second term of (3.11c), p. 9).
- Calibrate your reimplemented incumbent against the mature code before claiming gains (p. 39).
- Name every excluded test problem with its reason, and give both codes' iteration counts for the problems you remove (p. 39).
