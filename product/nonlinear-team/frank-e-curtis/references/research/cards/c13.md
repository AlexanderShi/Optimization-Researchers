### S001 · Optimization methods for large-scale machine learning (SIAM Review 2018, DOI 10.1137/16M1080173; arXiv:1606.04838)
**Read level**: full. Read: pp. 1–84 in full (all of §1–§9 and Appendices A–B, including all proofs). Bibliography (pp. 85–95) scanned for self-citations and positioning only.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Léon Bottou (Facebook AI Research), Jorge Nocedal (Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Curtis is listed at Lehigh ISE with DOE and NSF support (p. 1). It is a survey and position paper, not a research article. It comes before the stochastic SQP papers that SKILL.md lists under Heuristic 10, and it is the most-cited item on his list (5921 cites in the batch metadata). The 164-entry bibliography (pp. 85–95) cites no Curtis paper: the only "Curtis" in the file is the author line (p. 1). The co-authors' own lines are cited (Bottou [18]–[25], [88], [89]; Byrd–Chin–Nocedal [28]–[31]; Nocedal [97], [113], [114], [163]). So the survey speaks from Bottou's learning-theory line and Nocedal's line, not from Curtis's constrained-optimization work.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a mismatch between communities. Large-scale machine learning is "a distinctive setting in which traditional nonlinear optimization techniques typically falter", so these techniques "should be considered secondary to alternative classes of approaches that respect the statistical nature of the underlying problem" (p. 3). The paper asks three questions (p. 3): how optimization problems arise in ML and why they are hard; which methods have succeeded and why; which recent advances and open questions exist. Two case studies answer the first question (p. 3–4). Text classification gives convex problems: regularized logistic loss (2.3), the RCV1 encoding with d = 47,152 (p. 5–6). Deep neural networks give nonconvex problems (2.5) with tens of millions of parameters (p. 7–8). The formal problem moves from misclassification risk (2.1), (2.6) to expected risk R(w) = E[f(w; ξ)] (3.4) and empirical risk Rn (3.6) (pp. 5–15). Structural risk minimization and early stopping link optimization accuracy to generalization (pp. 11–13).
  - Transferable technique: open a cross-community survey with two contrasting case studies, one convex and well understood, one nonconvex and "enigmatic" (p. 3). Use them to motivate the problem class before any algorithm appears.
- **D2 Core idea & organization**: SG (Robbins–Monro) is the workhorse. Every other method is placed by its relation to SG on a two-dimensional map (Fig. 3.3, p. 20): one axis is noise reduction (dynamic sampling, gradient aggregation, iterate averaging; §5, Fig. 5.1, p. 40), the other is second-order information against ill-conditioning (Hessian-free Newton, stochastic quasi-Newton, Gauss–Newton, natural gradient, diagonal scaling; §6, Fig. 6.1, p. 51). The batch Newton method sits in the corner, which points to combinations of both axes (p. 21). The authors give three arguments for SG over batch methods (§3.3, pp. 16–19):
  - intuitive: data redundancy, e.g. ten copies of a set (p. 17);
  - practical: SG against batch L-BFGS on RCV1 (Fig. 3.1, p. 18);
  - theoretical: total work is n log(1/ε) for batch gradient and 1/ε for SG (pp. 18–19).

  The analytical core is one generic algorithm (Algorithm 4.1, p. 22). It needs only three tools: a seed ξk, a stochastic vector g(wk, ξk) and a stepsize αk (p. 21). The direction may be a stochastic gradient, a mini-batch gradient or a scaled direction Hk·(mini-batch gradient) (4.2), and the results hold "as long as the expected angle between g(wk, ξk) and ∇F(wk) is sufficiently positive" (p. 22). The authors hedge the map themselves: the schematic "should not be taken too literally", and iterate averaging and momentum do not fit it cleanly (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: state the method with the search direction as an abstract random vector under moment conditions, so that one analysis covers plain, mini-batch and scaled (quasi-Newton) directions (pp. 21–24).
  - Transferable technique: organize a crowded literature on a map whose origin is the workhorse and whose axes name the deficiency each family fixes (Fig. 3.3, p. 20). State the map's limits in the next paragraph (p. 21).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Assumption 4.1: ∇F is L-Lipschitz (p. 23). Assumption 4.3 (p. 24) has three parts:
  - (a) F is bounded below on the region the iterates visit;
  - (b) sufficient descent in expectation, ∇F(wk)ᵀE[g] ≥ µ‖∇F(wk)‖² and ‖E[g]‖ ≤ µG‖∇F(wk)‖ (4.7);
  - (c) variance at most M + MV‖∇F(wk)‖² (4.8).

  Together these give the second-moment bound (4.9). The authors say all of §4 holds if (4.9) is assumed directly, and that the split form was chosen only to ease §5 (p. 24). Assumption 4.5 is strong convexity (p. 26). The authors flag several choices as made for convenience or relevance:
  - the ξk are independent "to avoid requiring certain machinery" from stochastic processes; an adapted process would also work (fn. 4, p. 22);
  - results are in expectation, not almost sure, because martingale arguments "do not provide significant additional insights" (Inset 4.2, p. 25);
  - convexity results are stated only for the strongly convex case, for three reasons: regularized models in practice, local strong convexity near minimizers, and extensions available elsewhere (Inset 4.2, p. 25);
  - sampling is with replacement; incremental (without-replacement) analysis is outside the scope (fn. 3, p. 21).

  §4.4 uses the asymptotic equivalence E(n, ε) ∼ 1/n + ε, "the fastest rate that remains compatible with elementary statistical results" (p. 36). §7 and §8 assume full gradients first and discuss stochastic variants afterwards (pp. 68, 75). Appendix A explains why smoothness was preferred to convexity: it covers nonconvex objectives and scaled directions, at the cost of treating nonsmooth models separately in §8 (pp. 82–83).
  - Transferable technique: when an assumption is there only for convenience, say so in a footnote and name the weaker version that would also work (fn. 4, p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**: All SG results come from two lemmas (§4.1, pp. 23–25). Devices:
  - **Lemma 4.2** (p. 23): the descent lemma in expectation, E_ξk[F(wk+1)] − F(wk) ≤ −αk∇F(wk)ᵀE[g] + ½αk²L·E[‖g‖²] (4.4). It follows from the L-smooth upper bound (4.3), proved in App. B (p. 83).
  - **Lemma 4.4** (pp. 24–25): with Assumption 4.3 this becomes −(µ − ½αkLMG)αk‖∇F‖² + ½αk²LM (4.10b). The authors read it as "Markovian": the right-hand side is deterministic given wk (p. 25).
  - **Theorem 4.6** (fixed step, strongly convex; pp. 26–27): the PL-type inequality 2c(F − F*) ≤ ‖∇F‖² (4.12, App. B p. 84) turns (4.10b) into a recursion. Subtracting the fixed point ᾱLM/(2cµ) gives a pure contraction (4.15). Result: linear convergence to a noise floor (4.14).
  - **Stepsize-halving sketch** (pp. 27–28, Fig. 4.1): halve the step when the gap reaches twice the floor. By (4.18) each halving needs about log(3)/(αrcµ) iterations, i.e. twice as many, which yields an effective O(1/k) rate. A practical schedule is derived from the fixed-step theorem.
  - **Theorem 4.7** (diminishing αk = β/(γ + k); pp. 28–29): induction on the bound ν/(γ + k), with ν defined in (4.22) so that the residual term is nonpositive. It closes with k̂² ≥ (k̂ + 1)(k̂ − 1).
  - **Theorem 4.8** (fixed step, nonconvex; p. 32): telescope the total expectation of (4.10b) and use Finf to bound the average squared gradient (4.28).
  - **Theorems 4.9–4.10** (pp. 33–34): weighted sum Σαk E‖∇F‖² < ∞ under Σαk = ∞, Σαk² < ∞. Corollary 4.11 (p. 34): Markov's inequality gives convergence in probability at a randomly chosen iterate. Corollary 4.12 (App. B, p. 84): split the increments of G(w) = ‖∇F(w)‖² into positive and negative sums S⁺, S⁻ and use convergent-sum arguments to get lim E‖∇F‖² = 0.
  - **Theorem 5.1** (p. 41–42): if the variance decays geometrically (5.1), induction gives linear convergence with ρ = max{1 − ᾱcµ/2, ζ} (5.4).
  - **Theorem 5.3** (pp. 43–44): a work count. Mini-batch sizes ⌈τ^(k−1)⌉ summed as a geometric series show that dynamic sampling reaches ε with O(1/ε) sample gradients, the same as SG.
  - **§4.4 error decomposition** (pp. 35–37): approximation + estimation + optimization error (4.31); a "macroscopic" problem, minimize E(n, ε) subject to T(n, ε) ≤ Tmax (4.32); Table 4.1 compares batch and stochastic (p. 37). The authors tie it to the Ω(1/k) lower bound for noisy oracles (pp. 36–37).
  - **Mini-batch trade-off** (pp. 30–31): the theorem constants show that mini-batching divides M by nmb but does not allow an nmb-times larger step, because α1 ≤ µ/(LMG) ≤ 1/(µL).
  - Others: descent test (5.11) and its proof (p. 45); Theorem 7.1 for randomized coordinate descent (p. 72); Theorem 8.1 for proximal gradient using (8.5) (pp. 75–76); regret-bound derivation (Inset 4.3, p. 39).

  Complexity form: expected optimality gap or expected average squared gradient. The constants are explicit, and the authors point at the ratios L/c and M/c (4.24, p. 30), which return in the ill-conditioning commentary (pp. 37–38).
  - Transferable technique: after the one-step expected-decrease lemma, subtract the fixed point of the recursion to get a pure contraction (4.15, p. 27). Use an induction with a constant ν that absorbs the noise term for diminishing steps (4.22, p. 29).
  - Transferable technique: to judge a method with growing per-iteration cost, convert its rate into total sample-gradient evaluations to ε (Theorem 5.3, pp. 43–44).
- **D5 Experiments & evidence**: The authors decline a numerical comparison on purpose, because it "might bias our review toward a limited test set and implementation details" (p. 4). The paper has one experiment of its own (Fig. 3.1, pp. 17–18):
  - setup: RCV1 binary logistic regression, batch L-BFGS against SG with fixed α = 4, over the first 10 epochs;
  - x-axis: accessed data points, i.e. sample-gradient evaluations, not iterations or time;
  - caveat stated in the text: SG had to be run "repeatedly using different choices for the stepsize α until a good choice was identified for this particular problem" (p. 17);
  - caveat stated in the text: with more epochs the batch method would "eventually overtake the stochastic method" (p. 19).

  Other figures report other groups' results: DNN word error rates (Fig. 2.2, p. 8) and ILSVRC error history (Fig. 2.4, p. 9). The practical claims in §5–§6 are attributed to cited work ([12], [19], [89], [162]; pp. 65–67). Failure cases are reported mainly through cited examples:
  - [105]: β too small after overestimating c leaves the distance above 10⁻² after 10⁹ iterations (p. 29);
  - SGD-QN's curvature estimate "ruins" from correlated noise ([19], p. 65);
  - Adagrad's steps decrease too aggressively ([162], p. 67).
  - Transferable technique: under a demonstration plot, state the tuning effort that produced the favourable curve and the regime in which the loser overtakes (pp. 17, 19).
  - Transferable technique: plot against accessed data points so that batch and stochastic methods are charged in the same unit (Fig. 3.1, p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: The survey translates between communities:
  - R-linear convergence ("geometric" in ML) (p. 18);
  - stochastic approximation and sample-average approximation as the counterparts of stochastic and batch methods (p. 16);
  - back propagation as algorithmic differentiation (p. 7).

  It continues Robbins–Monro (Inset 3.1, p. 16), the Bottou–Bousquet trade-off analysis ([22, 23], p. 35) and the Nemirovski–Yudin oracle lower bounds ([1, 106, 128], pp. 36–38). It qualifies the ML consensus without dismissing it: "many in the machine learning community" view SG as ideal; "We argue, however, that this is far from settled" (p. 40). Gradient aggregation methods "should not be regarded as clearly superior to SG" (p. 49). On convex analysis it departs from the ML literature on purpose, choosing strongly convex over merely convex results (p. 25). Self-citation is limited to Bottou's and Nocedal's lines. No Curtis paper is cited (references pp. 85–95).
  - Transferable technique: when writing for a neighbouring community, map your vocabulary onto theirs on first use (R-linear = geometric, SA = SG, SAA = batch; pp. 16, 18).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The theme is stated in the abstract and again on p. 3. Three numbered questions structure the whole paper (p. 3). Theorems are followed by plain-language "what can be learned" paragraphs with run-in headings: "Role of Strong Convexity", "Role of the Initial Point", "Trade-Offs of (Mini-)Batching" (pp. 29–31). Boxed insets carry history and side views (Robbins, p. 16; analysis perspectives, p. 25; regret bounds, p. 39). "Commentary" subsections (§4.5 p. 37, §5.3.3 p. 49, §8.2.2 commentary p. 81) temper the preceding theory: §4.5 opens with "it would be premature to conclude that SG is a perfect solution" and a "large gap between asymptotical behavior and practical realities" (p. 37). Hedging is frequent and specific:
  - "we suspect that the practical technique presented here might merely serve as a starting point" (p. 45);
  - "At present, it is not known how useful gradient aggregation methods will prove to be" (p. 49);
  - "the full potential ... is not yet known" for stochastic quasi-Newton (p. 58);
  - "Theoretical guarantees about the inclusion of momentum in stochastic settings are elusive" (p. 69).

  Small worked examples do expository work:
  - Bertsekas's region of confusion (Example 3.1, p. 17);
  - a Hessian-vector product for exp(w1w2) (Example 6.1, p. 52);
  - a two-variable function where Newton scaling gives a worse direction than the gradient (p. 66);
  - |w|, which shows that the minimum-norm subgradient is a discontinuous accuracy measure (fn. 9, p. 80).
  - Transferable technique: after each theorem, write one short paragraph per constant or parameter saying what it does in practice (pp. 29–31).
  - Transferable technique: when you reject a standard measure, give the smallest example that breaks it (|w| for the minimum-norm subgradient, fn. 9, p. 80).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is expository and analytical. It offers a unified, short convergence and complexity theory for a general SG (§4), a work-complexity argument for SG in the big-data regime (§4.4) and a taxonomy of the next methods (§5–§8) (p. 82). The authors state these limits:
  - parallel and distributed computing is "neither discussed nor analyzed at length", and SG "may not be the best suited method for emerging computer architectures" (p. 82);
  - ADMM and EM are omitted (p. 82);
  - nonconvexity strategies for Hessian-free Newton are not "totally satisfactory or universally accepted" (p. 55);
  - safeguards for sᵀv > 0 in stochastic quasi-Newton "have not been fully developed" (p. 58);
  - batch normalization's noise is "poorly controlled" (p. 68);
  - there is no proof of a faster-than-linear rate with subsampled Hessians (p. 54).

  Future directions:
  - methods that start like SG and pass into reduced variance (p. 45);
  - combinations of noise reduction and second-order schemes (Fig. 3.3, p. 21; p. 58);
  - distributed settings where per-iteration overhead matters less than communication (p. 38).
  - Transferable technique: end each method family with a commentary that names the regime where it would lose (κ ≪ n vs. very large n for aggregation methods, p. 49). Do not end it with a verdict.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (inner solve serves the outer solver) — evidence ✅. The stochastic and regularized analogues are spelled out:
  - Hessian-free Newton-CG stops at ‖rk‖ ≤ ρ‖∇f_Sk‖ or maxcg (Algorithm 6.1, p. 53), and the outer rate is set by the residual accuracy, {‖rk‖/‖∇F‖} → 0 for superlinear (p. 54);
  - the Hessian sample can be smaller than the gradient sample because "the iteration is more tolerant to noise in the Hessian estimate than it is to noise in the gradient estimate" (p. 53);
  - proximal Newton subproblems use an ISTA-step-norm test together with model decrease, solved loosely at first and more accurately near the solution (pp. 79–80).

  These ideas belong to the Byrd–Nocedal line ([29], [31]), and the survey is joint, so this is evidence of the shared school, not of Curtis alone.
- Method 4 (audit the yardstick) — evidence ✅:
  - §4.4 replaces "rate of convergence" with expected error under a time budget, and the slower SG wins (pp. 34–37);
  - §5.2 asks "whether it is meaningful to describe a method as linearly convergent if the per-iteration cost increases without bound" and answers with total work (p. 43);
  - §5.3.3 repeats the move for aggregation methods (p. 49);
  - fn. 9 rejects the minimum-norm subgradient as an accuracy measure (p. 80);
  - §4.5 warns about the gap between asymptotics and practice (p. 37).
- Method 5 (fair-fight benchmarking) — variant ⚠. The worry about test-set bias is the same (p. 4), but the response is to avoid a benchmark altogether and argue from trade-offs and theory. The one plot (Fig. 3.1) uses a tuned SG on one dataset, with the tuning disclosed (p. 17) and the batch method's later win admitted (p. 19). The tuning effort is not counted.
- Method 2 (steer, don't switch) — variant ⚠. The same single-algorithm, monitored-transition ideal appears in an unconstrained, sampling form (p. 45). The adaptive sample size is raised by a monitored test (5.12), with a geometric backup (p. 45). There is no penalty or feasibility trade-off here.
- Heuristic 1 (computable safeguards): evidence. The exact descent test (5.11) needs ∇F, so it is replaced by a sample-variance estimate (5.12) plus a preset geometric growth as backup (p. 45).
- Heuristic 10 (deterministic twin, then the stochastic version): evidence. §6.2.1 "Deterministic to Stochastic" lists what breaks when L-BFGS gets noisy gradients: the rate, the cost per iteration, and the curvature pairs (pp. 56–57). §7 and §8 introduce each method with full gradients and then discuss stochastic variants (pp. 68, 75).
- Taste 5 (the workhorse is a target, not an endpoint): evidence. Everything is placed relative to SG (Fig. 3.3, p. 20), and the paper argues the SG consensus "is far from settled" (p. 40).
- New-pattern candidate: **abstract stochastic direction with moment bounds**. One generic algorithm with g(wk, ξk) constrained only by (4.7)–(4.8) covers SG, mini-batch and scaled directions (pp. 21–24).
- New-pattern candidate: **map the field around the workhorse**. A 2-D map with the incumbent at the origin and axes named by the deficiency each family fixes, hedged at once (pp. 20–21, 40, 51).
- New-pattern candidate: **total-work accounting**. Convert rates to evaluations to ε, or to error under a time budget, before declaring a winner (pp. 34–37, 43–44, 49). This may belong under Method 4 rather than as a new method.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Analyse a generic method whose direction is an abstract random vector with first- and second-moment conditions (4.7)–(4.8), so that one proof covers plain, mini-batch and quasi-Newton-scaled steps (pp. 21–24).
- For fixed steps, subtract the noise floor ᾱLM/(2cµ) to turn the expected-decrease recursion into a contraction (4.15, p. 27). For β/(γ + k) steps, close the induction with a constant ν chosen to make the residual term nonpositive (4.22, p. 29).
- Derive a practical step-halving schedule from the fixed-step theorem: halve when the gap reaches twice the floor and keep each step about twice as long (4.17)–(4.18), Fig. 4.1 (pp. 27–28).
- Judge growing-cost or slower-rate methods by total sample-gradient evaluations to ε (Theorem 5.3, pp. 43–44) or by error under a time budget (4.32), Table 4.1 (pp. 35–37).
- Replace an uncheckable condition by an estimate plus a guaranteed fallback: the sample-variance test (5.12) with geometric sample growth as backup (p. 45).
- For inexact subproblem solves, use an accuracy measure that is zero exactly at the solution and continuous (the ISTA step norm), add a model-decrease condition, and tighten the tolerance ηk → 0 for a superlinear rate (pp. 79–80).
- Under a favourable demonstration plot, state the tuning that produced it and when the other method overtakes (pp. 17, 19). Plot against accessed data points (p. 17).
- Follow each theorem with "Role of …" paragraphs that say what each constant means in practice (pp. 29–31).
