### S033 · A penalty-interior-point algorithm for nonlinear constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming Computation 2012, 10.1007/S12532-012-0041-4)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–29, all text incl. references; the contents of Table 1 (input parameters, p. 22) and Table 3 (final penalty values, p. 24) and Figs. 1–4 (pp. 23, 25–27) are not in the text layer, so only their captions and the prose about them were read).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: none (sole author; thanks R. A. Waltz for "aiding in the software development" and J. Nocedal for presentation, p. 28) · **Position in trajectory**: first Lehigh solo algorithm paper (received 26 April 2011, p. 1); joins the 2008 steering rules [9] and the 2009 adaptive barrier rules [28] in one method (p. 13), and cites the 2010 infeasibility-detection SQP paper [5] for the fast infeasible-rate idea (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two families each have a known weakness: penalty methods regularize constraints and detect infeasibility but often need LP/QP subproblems "numerous times", "intractable for many large-scale applications" (p. 3); interior-point methods scale through linear solves but "often lack a sophisticated mechanism for regularizing the constraints" and "can perform poorly on problems with difficult constraint sets" (p. 3). The paper wants both large scale and regularization, and fast convergence to an infeasible stationary point of v(x) = Σ max{c_i(x), 0} (1.2) when the problem is infeasible, since for many methods "the rate of convergence to such points is extremely slow" (p. 2). The stated novelty is the parameter updates, not the subproblem (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable: enter by pairing two method families whose strengths and weaknesses are complementary, then declare that the contribution is the parameter-update rule, not the (already known) merged subproblem.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Penalty-interior-point subproblem (1.5): min ρf(x) − μΣ(ln r_i + ln s_i) + Σs_i s.t. c(x) + r − s = 0 (p. 3). Two design moves. (i) A "slack reset": for fixed x the slack problem (3.3) is convex and separable with closed-form solution (3.4), applied every iteration, so the constraints of (1.5) always hold and no third merit parameter is needed; Δx from the Newton system (3.1) is a descent direction for the reduced merit φ̃(x; ρ, μ) (Theorem 3.2, pp. 8–9). (ii) Adaptive updates of ρ and μ inside every iteration (Algorithm 2, p. 17): candidate sets R_k, M_k of current and smaller values; at an infeasible iterate an admissible pair must give linearized feasibility progress proportional to the ρ = 0 direction (3.16), sufficient descent in a quadratic merit model (3.17), and ρ ≤ ‖KKT residual of the feasibility problem‖² (3.18); the largest admissible ρ is chosen first, then the largest μ that approximately minimizes a quality function (3.20)–(3.21) (pp. 13–16). The approximate (not exact) minimization of the quality function departs from [28] because exact minimization drove μ to zero "too quickly due to only marginal gains" (p. 16). Kept: a Fiacco–McCormick conservative variant (Algorithm 1 = PIPAL-c, p. 12) as baseline and fallback (if no pair is admissible, PIPAL-a takes PIPAL-c's direction, pp. 18–19).
  - Transferable: when a merit function would need an extra weighting parameter, look for a variable block that can be eliminated in closed form each iteration (slack reset, (3.4)), so the merit becomes a function of the primal variable alone.
  - Transferable: make a parameter update a choice among a few candidate values tested against model-reduction conditions in the same iteration, with the conservative choice as the fallback when no candidate passes.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, c continuously differentiable (Assumption 2.1, p. 5); inequality form (1.1), equalities handled by c_i(x) + a_i − b_i = 0 with penalty a_i + b_i, chosen because splitting into two inequalities destroys MFCQ and an extra slack adds degrees of freedom (p. 20). Assumption 3.1 (reduced Hessian positive definite on the null space, p. 9) is enforced in practice by an exponentially growing identity shift until the inertia is right (p. 19). No global or local convergence theorem for Algorithm 2 is stated: the abstract promises only to "motivate the convergence properties" (p. 1), and the fast infeasible rate is pursued "with the hope of achieving this behavior" by importing the ρ = o(distance) condition from [5] (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Section 2 lists regularity facts without proofs ("straightforward to prove and have appeared various times in the literature. Thus, formal proofs are not provided.", p. 5): MFCQ holds at all feasible points of (1.3) and (1.5) (Theorem 2.2, p. 5); limits of (1.5) solutions as μ → 0 solve (1.3) with multipliers in [0, 1] (Theorem 2.5, p. 6); exact-penalty threshold ρ ∈ (0, 1/‖λ*‖∞] (Theorem 2.8, p. 7). The one proof given is the directional-derivative identity (3.6) under the slack reset (Theorem 3.2, pp. 9–10). The computational device is linearity of the Newton right-hand side in (ρ, μ): (4.3) builds the direction for any pair from three solves with one factorization (p. 18). Second-order correction reuses the factors (one extra forward/backward solve, p. 19).
  - Transferable: if the parameters you want to choose enter only the right-hand side of a linear system, freeze the matrix for the iteration and express every candidate's step as a linear combination of a few solves ((4.3), p. 18); the cost of trying several parameter values becomes a few triangular solves.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab code; PIPAL-c, PIPAL-a and IPOPT 3.9stable (MA27) on AMPL CUTEr models with presolve off "so as to test the algorithms on difficult constraint sets" (p. 22). Filters stated: models with ≥ 20,000 nonzeros in the initial Newton matrix (Matlab memory) and unconstrained models removed; 438 models; problems no algorithm solved removed, leaving 417 (pp. 22–23). Engineering adopted from IPOPT "for the purpose of providing a fairer comparison": bound relaxation, initial-point modification, gradient-based scaling, minimum parameter values, evaluation-error handling (pp. 17, 20–21). Measure: iteration counts only, shown as pairwise "positive/negative" bar profiles after Morales [27] plus a Dolan–Moré log profile (pp. 22–23). Results: on the full set "PIPAL-a and especially IPOPT have an edge in terms of efficiency" (p. 24); on the 132 problems where PIPAL-c had to decrease ρ, PIPAL-c is weakest and PIPAL-a "more competitive with" IPOPT (p. 24). Constructed sets from 125 Hock–Schittkowski models: degenerate (add −c_i(x)² ≤ 0, 120 kept) where IPOPT "lags slightly in terms of robustness" (p. 25), and infeasible (add c_i(x)² ≤ −1, 105 kept) where IPOPT "fails for many of the remaining problems" (pp. 26–27). Final penalty values tabulated (Table 3, p. 24). One ablation reported without numbers: dropping condition "(3.19)" at infeasible points gave worse efficiency (p. 27); as printed this names the feasible-case condition, so it probably means (3.18) (p. 27 vs. Algorithm 2, p. 17; not resolvable from the text). Input parameters were "those values that we found to yield the best results in our numerical experiments" (p. 21).
  - Transferable: after the full-set profile, re-run the comparison on the subset where the baseline actually needed the mechanism under test (here the 132 problems where PIPAL-c reduced ρ, p. 24); state that the subset is defined by the baseline's run.
  - Transferable: import the incumbent's preprocessing and safeguards into your prototype before comparing, and list each imported feature with its source section (pp. 20–21).
- **D6 Positioning**: Lists penalty-interior-point relatives (modified barrier [24,30,31], [1], [3,4], [10,11], [18], [32]) and says they "differ in detail" but share the regularization motivation (p. 4); classifies their parameter updates as "conservative" (Fiacco–McCormick-like, or monitoring over many iterations) and argues each new ρ value may cost "a complete run of a standard interior-point algorithm" (pp. 11–12). Claims first combination of steering [9] and adaptive barrier [28] rules (p. 13). Concedes the incumbent's efficiency in the conclusion (p. 28).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Hedged contribution sentences ("we believe that we have designed an approach that is both intuitively appealing and yields solid preliminary performance results", p. 4; "novel and sound", p. 28). Design rationales come with the failure seen when the alternative was tried (quality function, p. 16). Results are written as "the picture is much different" for each constructed set (pp. 25, 27). The conclusion frames the result as two strengths plus one concession, then names MPCC and MINLP branch-and-bound as the contexts where it matters (p. 28). An acronym footnote explains "pipal" (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and computational: a penalty-IPM with in-iteration (ρ, μ) updates and a Matlab prototype. Limits stated: more degrees of freedom than a pure IPM "may result in slower convergence" (p. 28); a "more sophisticated implementation" is needed, with the open challenge of keeping ρ from being "reduced much lower than required" (p. 24); the degeneracy construction covers one type only (p. 25). Limits visible but not stated: no convergence theorem for the adaptive algorithm (pp. 1, 5, 15); iteration counts only, no CPU time; parameters tuned on the test runs (p. 21); the ablation has no numbers (p. 27). Future: general-purpose solver; MPCC and MINLP (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ✅ evidence: the contribution is "the design of updates for the penalty and interior-point parameters" (p. 1), made inside every iteration from model-predicted feasibility progress ((3.16)–(3.18), pp. 14–15; Algorithm 2, p. 17), with final ρ values tabulated (Table 3, p. 24). ⚠ variant on step 4: the fast infeasible rate is imported from [5] as a hope, not proved here (p. 15).
- Method 3 (Break it on purpose) ✅ evidence: degenerate and infeasible HS variants by mechanical rules, presolve off, the narrowness admitted (pp. 22, 25–26).
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✅ evidence: IPOPT's engineering adopted (pp. 20–21); in-framework conservative rival PIPAL-c (p. 12); incumbent's efficiency win printed (pp. 24, 28). ⚠ variant: parameters tuned on the evaluation runs (p. 21); ablation reported without numbers (p. 27).
- Heuristic 2 (count a safeguard's extra solves) ✅ evidence: the (ρ, μ) search costs one factorization and at most three sets of forward/backward solves (p. 18).
- Heuristic 7 (name filters, report pre-filter counts) ✅ evidence: 438 → 417, 125 → 120 and 105, removal criteria stated (pp. 22, 25–26).
- new-pattern candidate: one factorization, many parameter values (linearity of the right-hand side in (ρ, μ), (4.3), p. 18).
- new-pattern candidate: mechanism-activated subset (compare on the instances where the baseline needed the mechanism, p. 24).
- new-pattern candidate: cheap default with guaranteed fallback (PIPAL-a falls back to PIPAL-c's direction when no pair is admissible, pp. 18–19; see also S036).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Eliminate slack variables in closed form at every iterate ((3.3)–(3.4), p. 8) so the constraints always hold and the merit function needs no balancing parameter (p. 9).
- Freeze the KKT matrix within an iteration and get the step for any (ρ, μ) by linear combination of three solves ((4.3), p. 18).
- Choose the largest μ whose quality value is within a factor of the best, not the minimizer, to avoid driving μ to zero for marginal gains ((3.21), p. 16).
- Build the comparison in three layers: full set, the subset where the mechanism fired, then constructed degenerate and infeasible sets (pp. 22–27).

**Quotes**:
- "After all, in the context of Algorithm 1, each distinct value for the penalty parameter ρ potentially requires the computational efforts of a complete run of a standard interior-point algorithm." (p. 12)
- "Our experiments support the claim that when an interior-point method is performing at its best, it is difficult to outperform such an approach in terms of efficiency." (p. 28)

### S036 · A note on the implementation of an interior-point algorithm for nonlinear optimization with inexact step computations (Mathematical Programming, Series B 2012, 10.1007/S10107-012-0557-4)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–19, all text incl. tables and references; Figs. 1–2 are captions only in the text layer; the motivations "omitted" here and deferred to the full-length report [10] (p. 3) were not read).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Johannes Huber (Basel), Olaf Schenk (Lugano), Andreas Wächter (Northwestern, corresponding author) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the implementation paper of the 2010 inexact interior-point algorithm [13] (Curtis, Schenk, Wächter, SIAM J. Sci. Comput.), closing the inexact SQP/Newton series [8,9,12,13] (p. 3); received 12 April 2011 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Large-scale problems for which "most interior-point methods, have proved to be impractical due to large numbers of variables/constraints and significant fill-in during the factorization" (p. 2); PDE-constrained optimization as the prime class (p. 2). The paper's purpose is "a practical implementation, including enhanced algorithmic features" of [13] (p. 2): an implementation note, not a new theory.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Ipopt's interior-point framework (Algorithm 1, p. 5) with iterative solves of the primal-dual system under SMART termination tests (Algorithm 2, p. 7): three tests built on the model reduction Δm_k of an exact-penalty merit function ((2.9)–(2.10), pp. 6–7), with a residual target κ = 10⁻² or 10⁻³ but "the actual tolerance is dictated by the entirety of the termination criteria" (p. 7). Algorithm 3 (p. 8) adds a normal/tangential decomposition with a trust-region normal step (2.15) satisfying Cauchy decrease, needed for global convergence when Jacobians lose rank (pp. 7–9). Main change from [13]: use the single-system Algorithm 2 by default and switch to Algorithm 3 only when the line search returns α_k < 10⁻³, because the analysis of Algorithm 2 uses full-rank Jacobians exactly to keep α_k bounded away from zero (pp. 9–10). Other changes: Hessian modification triggered by the tests' curvature condition rather than by inertia (p. 11), plus a pre-emptive modification when the previous line search cut α_k (p. 11); a flexible penalty function [11] accepting sufficient decrease for any π in an interval (3.1) (p. 11); an adaptive refinement of the PARDISO multilevel preconditioner (drop tolerances divided by 3 after 1,500 SQMR iterations, up to 4 attempts, then fall back to Algorithm 3, p. 10).
  - Transferable: when a cheap method's convergence proof needs a quantity bounded away from zero (here the steplength), monitor that quantity in the implementation and switch to the guaranteed method only when it collapses (pp. 9–10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Problem (1.1) with f, c_E, c_I "sufficiently smooth (e.g., C2)" (p. 2); infeasible instances return a stationary point of the ℓ2 violation (1.2) (pp. 2–3). Global convergence is inherited from [13] for Algorithm 1 + Algorithm 3 "under common assumptions" (p. 9). The primal-dual barrier Hessian used in practice is covered by the primal analysis of [13] as long as it stays between νμS⁻² and ν̄μS⁻² (ν = 10⁻¹⁰, ν̄ = 10¹⁰), enforced by adjusting the dual variables (pp. 10–11). Termination, initial point and μ update identical to Ipopt [28] (p. 5).
  - Transferable: to use the practical variant of a component (primal-dual Σ) while keeping a proof written for the analysable one (primal Σ), sandwich the practical choice between multiples of the analysed one and enforce the sandwich (pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No new theorems; the paper relies on [9,12,13]. Devices restated: the bound Dφ_k ≤ −Δm_k that makes Δm_k a surrogate for the merit directional derivative (p. 6); Cauchy decrease for the inexact normal step (p. 9); consistency of the reformulated system (2.16) because it stipulates A_k d_k = A_k v_k (p. 9).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Ipopt revision 1954 (branches/parallel) with PARDISO 4.1.1 SQMR and a multilevel incomplete-factorization preconditioner; finite elements with libmesh and PETSc (pp. 11–12). Robustness: on 617 CUTE problems the implementation solves 89 % against 96 % for default Ipopt, while always using Algorithm 3 as in [13] gives 84 %; 142 problems triggered the switch (p. 12; details deferred to [10]). Speed: two 3-D PDE problems at three mesh sizes, against default Ipopt with direct factorization: boundary control with radiation (up to 670,153 variables, Table 1, p. 13) with speedups 4.34, 3.63, 7.85 (Table 2, p. 13); server-room cooling with a nonconvex pointwise gradient constraint (up to 663,886 variables, Table 3, p. 17) with speedups 2.64, 6.94, 17.89 (Table 4, p. 17). Iteration counts and final objective values match Ipopt's (Tables 2 and 4, pp. 13, 17). The weak case is reported: near the end of the boundary-control run the preconditioner needs tighter tolerances, fill-in near 10 and over 1,000 SQMR iterations, up to 35 min per step computation against under 4 min early (pp. 14–15). Discretized constraints are scaled by factors such as 10⁻²/h so their gradients do not vanish as h → 0 (pp. 13, 16).
  - Transferable: report a speedup table per mesh level with iteration count, final objective, total and per-iteration CPU time, so readers see that the gain comes from cheaper iterations at equal solution quality (Tables 2 and 4, pp. 13, 17).
  - Transferable: when solving discretized PDE constraints, scale each constraint family by a power of h so its gradient does not vanish under refinement (pp. 13, 16).
- **D6 Positioning**: Places the method among nonlinear elimination, reduced-space and full-space PDE-optimization approaches, as full-space but unique "in its ability to attain strong theoretical convergence guarantees with great computational flexibility" (p. 2); cites the convex-case alternative of terminating on the relative residual alone [14] and explains why nonconvexity needs more (p. 6). Credits IBM collaborators for the server-room example (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A short "note" that states what is omitted for space and where it lives (full-length report [10], p. 3). Parameter values are listed in full in the text (pp. 7, 9–11). Claims are tied to tables ("For the largest problem, the speedup is a factor of 7.85", p. 14). The conclusion restates the design (single system ideally, decomposition as fallback, p. 18) and names one open problem.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Software and algorithm engineering: the inexact algorithm made usable inside Ipopt with a switching rule and preconditioner refinement. Limits stated: preconditioner effectiveness decreases as μ → 0; whether the log-barrier ill-conditioning can be handled inside the incomplete factorization is left to future research (p. 18); robustness below default Ipopt (89 % vs 96 %, p. 12). Limits visible: general-purpose preconditioner only (p. 3); two PDE examples; no comparison with other inexact or PDE-specific solvers.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ✅ evidence: SMART tests from the merit model set the iterative solver's stopping point (pp. 6–7); Hessian modification triggered by the tests, not inertia (p. 11; Method 1 step 3); production code timed at scale (pp. 12–17; Method 1 step 6).
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ⚠ variant: the flexible penalty interval (p. 11) steers the merit parameter, but the step computation does switch between two algorithms on a steplength trigger (pp. 9–10); the switch stays inside one outer algorithm.
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✅ evidence: the guaranteed-but-always-regularized variant of [13] is run as an in-framework rival (84 % vs 89 %, p. 12); default Ipopt's higher robustness is printed (p. 12); the worst preconditioner behaviour is described (pp. 14–15).
- new-pattern candidate: cheap default with guaranteed fallback, triggered by the quantity the cheap method's proof needs (α_k < 10⁻³, pp. 9–10).
- new-pattern candidate: price the guarantee (run the always-guaranteed variant to measure what the guarantee costs: 84 % vs 89 %, p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Default to the one-system inexact step, keep the decomposition step as fallback, and switch on a small accepted steplength (threshold 10⁻³, p. 10).
- Escalate inside the inner solver before changing the outer algorithm: tighten drop tolerances (÷3), retry up to four times, then switch step algorithm, then accept the last inexact solution without guarantees (p. 10).
- Trigger a Hessian modification at the start of an iteration if the previous line search had to backtrack for sufficient decrease, to shorten steps and raise accepted steplengths (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "Specifically, the computation time for the largest instance with more than 600,000 variables was reduced from more than 6 days to 8.2 h, a speedup by a factor of 17.89." (p. 17)
- "As mentioned at the end of Sect. 4.1, we have observed a decrease in effectiveness of the preconditioner as the barrier parameter approaches zero." (p. 18)

### S012 · An adaptive gradient sampling algorithm for nonsmooth optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2013, 10.1080/10556788.2012.714781)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–24, all text incl. the Appendix on the QO solver (pp. 23–24) and references; Figs. 1–3 are axis labels and legends only in the text layer).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Xiaocun Que (Lehigh ISE, supported by the same NSF grant DMS-1016291, pp. 2, 22) · **Position in trajectory**: follows the SQP-GS paper with Overton [8] (p. 3) and starts the student line with Que that later produced the termination-flag remark the skill quotes from Que's thesis (Method 4 step 4); received 7 October 2011 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Gradient sampling (GS) is robust and has probability-one guarantees for nonsmooth nonconvex f (p. 2), but has three practical limitations listed as numbered remarks: n + 1 or more fresh gradients per iteration, a QO subproblem built afresh each iteration, and at best steepest-descent behaviour (p. 3). The paper addresses all three while keeping GS's guarantees.
  - Transferable: open with a numbered list of the incumbent's practical limitations and map each design element to the remark it fixes (remarks (1)–(3), p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Adaptive sampling: keep recently computed gradients that still lie in the current ε-ball and add only p̄ new samples per iteration (O(1) in theory, p̄ = n/10 in practice) up to a cap p (Algorithm 2.1 step 2, p. 5; p. 18). Gradients active in the previous QO enter the initial active set, giving a warm start (p. 3). If fewer than p samples are held, the line search is "incomplete": at most u backtracks, else α_k = 0 and more samples are collected (step 6, p. 5). Variable metric: H_k reinitialized each iteration to μ_k I and updated from sample-point information, either by L-BFGS on sampled directions with skip rules (9a)–(9b) (pp. 6–7) or by a new rank-one "overestimation" update that raises the model value at sample points toward f (pp. 10–11), which is inert for convex f (Theorem 3.8, p. 12). μ_k is doubled or halved according to whether α_k < 1 (p. 18). GS is the special case p̄ = p ≥ n + 1, H_k = I (p. 5).
  - Transferable: make the incumbent a parameter setting of your method (GS = AGS with p̄ = p, H_k = I, p. 5), so every comparison is one code with one knob turned.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f locally Lipschitz and continuously differentiable on an open dense set D (Assumption 4.1, p. 12); uniformly bounded positive definite H_k (Assumption 3.1, p. 6), proved for both updates (Theorem 3.3/Corollary 3.4, p. 9; Theorem 3.6/Corollary 3.7, pp. 11–12). The authors note the bounds are worst-case and "typically would not be tight in practice", and that ill-conditioned approximations may help on nonsmooth problems [25], so variants violating Assumption 3.1 are also tested (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Theorem 4.2: with probability one, f(x_k) → −∞ or ε_k → 0 and every cluster point is Clarke stationary (p. 12). The proof "follows closely that of Kiwiel" [22], redone "for the sake of completeness" because adaptive sampling and variable metrics change details (pp. 13, 15). Devices: (i) the QO's KKT conditions give ∇f(x_k)ᵀd_k ≤ −d_kᵀH_kd_k, so d_k is a descent direction (Lemma 4.3, p. 13); (ii) incomplete line searches cannot repeat forever because the sample count grows to p (Lemma 4.4, p. 13); (iii) a decrease bound per unit distance travelled (Lemma 4.5, p. 14); (iv) Carathéodory's theorem gives an open set T of sample sets whose QO reduction is ω-close to the ideal (Lemma 4.7, p. 15); uniform sampling hits T eventually, so staying outside T is a probability-zero event (Cases 1a–1b, pp. 16–17). For the matrices: a per-update bound lemma (Lemma 3.2, pp. 7–8) iterated over at most p + 1 updates plus Rayleigh–Ritz for the inverse (p. 9); for the overestimation update, ρ_k,i ∈ [0, √(2ρ) − 1] gives (2ρ)^(p+1) conditioning (pp. 10–11).
  - Transferable: to keep a quasi-Newton matrix inside the hypotheses of a convergence theorem, reinitialize it each iteration and cap the number of updates and their curvature ratios (skip an update if dᵀy < γε² or ‖y‖² > σε², (9), p. 7); the bound then follows by iterating a one-update lemma.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab with a specialized active-set QO solver (ASQO, after Kiwiel [21], Appendix pp. 23–24). 26 scalable nonsmooth problems (10 from Haarala et al., 10 from TEST29, 6 others; convex and nonconvex), n = 50 (64 for EIG_PROD), each run 10 times from x₀ and nine random starts in a ball of radius ‖x₀‖ (pp. 19). Six variants in one code differing only in sampling and Hessian strategy (Table 1, p. 18). Measures: final sampling radius when a budget of 100n gradient evaluations is exhausted, and average QO iterations per nonlinear iteration; profiles over 260 runs (p. 19). Results: AGS beats GS on both (Figure 1, p. 20); both Hessian strategies beat AGS, AGS-over better on radius, AGS-LBFGS on QO iterations (p. 20); the ill-conditioned variants without guarantees bring no gain, and AGS-LBFGS-ill is worse on radius, with two candidate explanations offered (pp. 20–21). Parameters "were chosen as those that yielded the best overall results in our experiments" (p. 18). No bundle-method or other external comparator.
  - Transferable: for methods whose termination is not comparable, fix an evaluation budget and compare the progress measure reached (here the final sampling radius, floored at 10⁻¹², p. 19), and add a second measure that isolates the mechanism (QO iterations per iteration for warm starting).
  - Transferable: run the variant that drops the theory's safeguard (here the bounds on H_k) to test whether the safeguard costs performance (AGS-*-ill, pp. 18, 20–21).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds explicitly on Burke–Lewis–Overton [3,4] and Kiwiel [22,23], with differences listed point by point (no adaptive sampling in [22], no exact gradients in [23], no Hessian approximations in either) and credit given ("the works of Kiwiel have been inspirational", p. 3). Bundle methods are named as the competitor GS may one day match "in terms of theoretical might and practical performance" (p. 2); Wolfe's conjugate subgradient method is discussed as a forerunner with similarities and differences (p. 21).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions stated in the abstract with the efficiency claim tied to a measure (progress "within a given number of gradient evaluations", p. 2). Unexpected results are reported as such ("We did not expect to see a reduction in average QO iterations", p. 20) and negative results given explanations labelled as possible (pp. 20–21). Proofs that follow a predecessor say so and say why they are repeated (pp. 13, 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic plus theory: an adaptive, variable-metric GS framework with the same probability-one guarantee, two bounded Hessian updates and a warm-startable QO solver. Limits: n = 50 only; parameters tuned on the test set (p. 18); no external comparator; the μ_k value is called "critical for the performance" yet set by a heuristic (pp. 6, 18). Future: merging GS and bundle methods (p. 21); borrowing previous search directions as in Wolfe's method (pp. 21–22).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✅ evidence: the incumbent (GS) is a parameter setting of the new code; a one-component ablation ladder GS → AGS → AGS-LBFGS / AGS-over → -ill variants (Table 1, p. 18); 10 runs per problem (p. 19). ⚠ variant: parameters picked for "the best overall results" on the evaluation set (p. 18) and no external state-of-the-art code.

### S022 · A sequential quadratic optimization algorithm with rapid infeasibility detection (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2014, 10.1137/120880045)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–34, all text incl. all proofs of §4, Tables 1–4 and references; Figs. 1–2 are axis labels only in the text layer).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: James V. Burke (University of Washington), Hao Wang (Lehigh ISE) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the second rung of the infeasibility-detection line after Byrd–Curtis–Nocedal 2010 [6], called "the immediate predecessor" (p. 3); received 6 June 2012, revised March 2014 (p. 1). It is the SQuID paper cited in the skill's Heuristic 2.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Many methods "either fail or require an excessive number of iterations and/or function evaluations" before detecting infeasibility (p. 1); slow verdicts are a bottleneck in MINLP branch-and-bound and parametric optimization (p. 2). Penalty-SQO methods put "all of the responsibility for driving constraint violation minimization" on the penalty parameter and converge slowly on infeasible problems if it is not driven down fast (p. 2). The predecessor [6] has the fast infeasible rate but needs at least three QO solves per iteration near an infeasible stationary point (p. 3). Goal: the same guarantees with at most two QO solves, without feasibility restoration (p. 2).
  - Transferable: enter by counting the predecessor's per-iteration subproblem solves in the regime you care about, and set the bound you will meet as the headline (three or more vs. at most two, p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Each iteration: QO (3.7) minimizes a quadratic plus the linearized ℓ1 violation with the objective ignored, measuring the best local feasibility progress (p. 5); if the iterate is infeasible and that progress is at most θv(x_k), then before the second QO the penalty ρ is cut to at most κ_ρ R_inf² and the optimality multiplier estimate is pulled toward the feasibility one ((3.12)–(3.14), p. 7); QO (3.9) then minimizes the penalized objective model, keeping linearly satisfied constraints satisfied and relaxing the others (p. 6); the step is the convex combination d(w) with the smallest w giving at least a fraction β of the feasibility progress ((3.10)–(3.11), pp. 6–7); a second ρ update (3.18)–(3.19) secures the model-reduction condition (3.17) before an ℓ1 line search (3.20) (pp. 7–8). Two separate multiplier estimates are kept, one for the optimization and one for the feasibility problem (p. 3). Implementation: skip (3.7) at nearly feasible points (pp. 9, 29); declare infeasibility only if also ρ_k ≤ ρ̄, accepting extra iterations to avoid false verdicts (p. 29).
  - Transferable: place the parameter update between two subproblem solves, where the first solve's information (attainable feasibility progress) can change the second solve's objective in the same iteration (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, c_E, c_I twice continuously differentiable (p. 4); infeasibility certificate = Clarke stationary point of the ℓ1 violation v (3.2) (p. 4). Well-posedness assumes positive definite QO Hessians (Assumption 4.1, p. 9); global convergence adds bounded Lipschitz functions and uniformly bounded H (Assumption 4.7, p. 12); local results assume full-rank active Jacobian, strict complementarity, second-order sufficiency, exact Hessians and unit steps for large k (Assumptions 4.19–4.20, pp. 20–21). The authors call these strong, explain that exact indefinite Hessians are hard because nonconvex QO is NP-hard and unit steps may fail through the Maratos effect, and point to Hessian augmentation, watchdog or second-order correction as remedies (pp. 20–21); the classical assumptions were "the conscience decision" (sic) "to avoid unnecessary distractions in the analysis" (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Well-posedness: model reductions are surrogates for directional derivatives (Lemma 4.2, pp. 9–10); ρ stays positive and the merit model reduction dominates the feasibility one after the updates (Lemma 4.5, pp. 11–12). Global: bounded directions by comparing with the feasible zero step (Lemma 4.9, p. 13); steplength bounded below by a multiple of the model reduction (Lemma 4.10, p. 14); the shifted penalty ϕ = ρ(f − f̲) + v is monotonically decreasing even though ρ changes (Lemma 4.11, p. 14); model reductions and directions vanish (Lemmas 4.12–4.13, pp. 14–16); limit points stationary for v (Theorem 4.14, p. 16); KKT if ρ stays bounded away from zero (Theorem 4.15, p. 16); if ρ → 0, limit points are all feasible or all infeasible, and feasible ones are Fritz John points where MFCQ fails (Theorem 4.16, pp. 17–19); summary trichotomy (Corollary 4.17, pp. 19–20). Local: implicit-function path x_ρ at distance O(ρ) from x* (Lemma 4.22, pp. 22–23); identification of the optimal partition by both QOs (Lemma 4.23, pp. 23–24); Newton analysis via Dennis–Schnabel Thm 5.2.1 (Lemma 4.24, pp. 24–25); a perturbed-Newton error bound with O(ρ) + O(multiplier-modification) terms, so ρ = O(distance²) gives quadratic convergence to an infeasible stationary point and o(distance) gives superlinear (Theorem 4.25, pp. 25–27); quadratic convergence to KKT points with ρ constant (Theorem 4.28, p. 28).
  - Transferable: when a penalty parameter only decreases, prove monotonicity of the shifted merit ρ(f − f̲) + v, which absorbs the parameter changes (Lemma 4.11, p. 14).
  - Transferable: for rates at infeasible points, write the step as a Newton step on a perturbed system and bound the error by C‖e_k‖² + O(perturbation); the parameter update rule is then read off from the perturbation size (Theorem 4.25, pp. 25–26).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Prototype Matlab code with quadprog; gradient scaling at the initial point; scaled termination tests (5.1)–(5.2); eigenvalue-based Hessian shifts (p. 29); parameters in Table 1 (p. 30). 123 Hock–Schittkowski AMPL models (hs068, hs069 excluded for a missing external function) and infeasible copies made by adding x₁ ≤ 0 and x₁ ≥ 1 (p. 30). Feasible set: 110 of 122 solved (90.16 %), 11 failures, 1 wrongly declared infeasible (hs112x excluded for an evaluation error) (Table 2, p. 30); infeasible set: 111 of 123 (90.24 %), 12 failures, none declared feasible (Table 3, p. 31). Failures attributed to strong nonconvexity and excessive Hessian modification (p. 31). Rate evidence: log₁₀ of the optimality and infeasibility residuals over the last ten iterations of every solved run; slopes below −1 and −2 read as superlinear and quadratic (Figs. 1–2, p. 31). Predecessor comparison on the six toy problems of [6], with parameters changed to match [6] (initial ρ = 1): iterations and QO solves per problem; SQuID uses fewer QO solves except on robot (55 vs 34), where it also needs more iterations (27 vs 13) (Table 4, p. 32). No comparison with an external solver. Theory–implementation gaps are stated: ω = 1 − 10⁻¹⁸ makes the w_k ≥ ω branch of (3.19), called "important in our convergence analysis" (p. 8), inconsequential in the runs (p. 30), and (3.7) is skipped near feasibility (p. 29).
  - Transferable: plot the log of the optimality (or infeasibility) residual for the last ten iterations of every solved instance and read off slopes as empirical rates (p. 31).
  - Transferable: when comparing with a predecessor, rerun with the predecessor's parameter settings and report the per-problem cost unit you claim to reduce (QO solves, Table 4, p. 32).
- **D6 Positioning**: A full literature-review section (§2, pp. 2–3): penalty-SQO methods [19] criticised by mechanism (the penalty parameter carries all the burden, p. 2); [6] credited and then counted (p. 3); steering [10,11] said to share [6]'s disadvantage (p. 3); multiphase active-set SQO [8,9,13,17,18,21,29] distinguished because they predict active sets and solve equality-constrained second phases, while SQuID solves up to two inequality-constrained QOs (p. 3); the predictor/Cauchy/accelerator methods [22,23] distinguished as trust-region methods (p. 3); Burke's earlier methods [3,4] closest in feasibility-step design (pp. 3–4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Two novelties named in the introduction (single approach without restoration; at most two QO solves, p. 2), and the paper's focus fenced explicitly against general assumptions (p. 2). The abstract calls it "A two-phase strategy" though both phases run inside every iteration (p. 1). Prototype results are framed modestly ("strong for a prototype implementation", p. 30). The conclusion restates the two regimes in parallel sentences (near an optimum / near an infeasible point) and leaves inexact QO solves and negative curvature to future work (p. 32).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory-led algorithm: global convergence plus quadratic local rates to both KKT points and infeasible stationary points with at most two QO solves per iteration. Limits stated: QO solver efficiency, and Hessian modifications that "are cumbersome in large-scale settings and may inhibit superlinear convergence" (p. 32); strong local assumptions (pp. 20–21). Limits visible: HS problems only, Matlab quadprog prototype, no external solver; the infeasible construction (two contradictory bounds on x₁) is a single, easy type of infeasibility (p. 30). Future: inexact subproblem solves and negative curvature (p. 32).
