### S066 · Solving nearly-separable quadratic optimization problems as nonsmooth equations (Computational Optimization and Applications 2017, DOI 10.1007/s10589-017-9895-8)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–42 read in full, including §§1–7, all ten random-QP tables and Tables 11–13, and the whole appendix (stationarity, differentiability, regularity, QP vs ICP lemmas). Reference list (pp. 42–44) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Arvind U. Raghunathan (Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories) · **Place in trajectory**: a 2016-submitted structured-QP paper (received 23 March 2016, p. 1) that builds on Raghunathan's earlier work with Bai on network utility maximization (p. 5, ref. [1]) and on a joint electricity-market paper (ref. [44], p. 33). It sits next to Curtis's SQP and globalization work, but here the "outer" iteration is on the coupling multipliers, not on the primal variables.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is a QP that is separable except for linear coupling constraints Σ A_i x_i ≤ b, with polyhedral X_i (p. 2, p. 5). It is motivated by network utility maximization, nonlinear network flows and two-stage stochastic programs (p. 2). The paper enters by criticising dual decomposition on two counts. First, subgradient and bundle methods on the nonsmooth dual "often require many iterations" and have O(1/ε²) complexity and "poor local convergence properties"; the smoothing methods are O(1/ε) (p. 3). Second, all the cited methods except [30] treat only coupling equalities, use slacks for inequalities (which destroys strict convexity), exclude nonconvex problems and maximize the dual (p. 4). A further, practical complaint: dual (sub)gradient methods recover the primal only as an ergodic average, which converges sublinearly (p. 4). The authors observed this in their own runs.
  - Transferable: open with a two-class taxonomy (linear-algebra decomposition, which needs solver internals, versus problem decomposition, which treats subproblem solvers as black boxes; p. 2). Place yourself in one class and name the one property every competitor in that class shares and you drop (here: reliance on convex duality and dual maximization; p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Replace "maximize the dual function" by "solve the implicit complementarity problem (ICP)" 0 ≤ F(λ) ⊥ λ ≥ 0 with F(λ) = b − Σ A_i x_i(λ) (eq. (1.5), p. 4). A solution gives a KKT point of the full QP without convex duality (p. 4), and the primal solution comes directly as x(λ*) (p. 4). The ICP is written as a semismooth equation Φ(λ) = 0 with the Minimum or Fischer–Burmeister operator (eqs. (2.1)–(2.4), pp. 6–7) and solved by semismooth Newton (2.5) (p. 7). The paper keeps Fischer–Burmeister because its step "only requires solving a linear equation", whereas the Minimum operator's step needs an optimization problem (p. 11). The Newton step comes from one block linear system that uses the subproblems' active sets and the sensitivity of x_i(λ) (eqs. (3.1)–(3.2), pp. 11–12). Separability is kept: each evaluation of F is N independent subproblem solves (1.2i) (p. 3). Globalization is a choice between two mechanisms. One is the merit function Ψ = ½‖Φ‖² with backtracking condition (3.4) (pp. 12–13). The other is a multidimensional filter adapted from Gould–Leyffer–Toint, with Φ split into q blocks (eqs. (3.5)–(3.8), pp. 13–14). The filter is chosen because full Newton steps are accepted more often, which cuts F evaluations, each "expensive, requiring the solution of several optimization problems" (p. 13). Algorithm 1 falls back to a normalized direction built from H^k and Φ(λ^k), described as "an approximation for the steepest descent direction" of ½‖Φ‖², when the Newton system is not solvable or the accepted step is below ρ_small (pp. 14–15). Algorithm 2 handles convex and indefinite cases. It adds a proximal term (μ/2)‖x_i − x̄_i‖² to each subproblem (1.2'i) (p. 15). It updates (μ, κ, x̄) only when the proximal term is not yet negligible and ‖Φ‖ ≤ κμ (p. 16; Algorithm 2 step 6, p. 17). In the convex case μ is reduced; in the indefinite case μ0 > −min eig(Q_i) is kept and κ is reduced (pp. 16–17). The update is "inspired by the Fiacco-McCormick-type barrier parameter update strategy" (p. 16). In the filter variant the filter is reset after a parameter change (p. 17).
  - Transferable: when a dual method needs convexity only to link subproblem solutions to the original problem, write the linking condition (primal feasibility of the coupling constraints plus complementarity) as an equation in the multipliers and solve it with a Newton-type method. Duality theory is then not needed for the reduction (pp. 4, 41).
  - Transferable: gate updates of the proximal or penalty parameter on "‖residual‖ ≤ κ·μ", so that parameters are not changed using information from points far from a solution (p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: X_i = {x : B_i x ≤ c_i}, nonempty (p. 5). Local theory (Theorem 2.2, p. 9) assumes three things: (1.1)-LICQ, strict complementarity in every subproblem (β_i = ∅), and the strong second-order sufficient condition for every subproblem. The coupling constraints may be non-strictly complementary (summary, p. 11). The step computation assumes x_i(·) is C¹ near λ^k, which holds under (1.2i)-LICQ, β_i = ∅ and SSOSC by Lemma 7.1 (pp. 11, 40). Semismoothness of Φ follows from x_i(·) ∈ PC¹ (Ralph–Dempe, Lemma 7.2, p. 41) and closure under composition (p. 7). The authors admit the assumption gap for globalization: the NCP literature assumes F ∈ C¹ or LC¹, which "is not guaranteed for (1.5)" (p. 5). Global convergence is "not straightforward to obtain, since in our context the function F is only semismooth" (p. 12). In the nonconvex case, solving (1.5) gives a second-order KKT point only if the subproblem solutions are second-order KKT points and Q is PSD on a critical cone (pp. 6, 42). In the indefinite experiments the subproblems are made convex by μ0 even though the full QP is not (p. 29).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The local theory is a chain of reductions to Qi's theorem that semismooth Newton converges superlinearly at a BD-regular root (Theorem 2.1, p. 7). (a) Lemma 2.2: three KKT-type matrices are nonsingular. The proof is by contradiction: from Qu + B^Tv + A^Tw = 0, u^TQu = 0 and SSOSC give u = 0, then LICQ gives (v, w) = 0 (pp. 8–9). (b) Lemma 2.3: the principal submatrices of ∇F are written as a Schur-complement-type product through the subproblem sensitivity system (7.15) (p. 9). (c) Theorem 2.2(i): nonsingularity of every ∇F[σσ] gives b-regularity, hence BD-regularity of Φ_min by De Luca–Facchinei–Kanzow (Lemma 2.1, p. 7) (p. 10). (d) Theorem 2.2(ii): ∇F[αα] is nonsingular, and the Schur complement ∇F[ββ]/∇F[αα] is shown positive definite through a sequence of equivalences ending at u^TQu > 0 on the null space of active constraints. As a symmetric P-matrix this gives R-regularity, hence BD-regularity of Φ_FB (pp. 10–11). Global convergence is argued only informally: "it is easily seen" that steps bounded away from zero on a subsequence drive Ψ → 0 (p. 13), with a similar remark for the filter (p. 14). No theorem covers Algorithm 2 (pp. 16–17). Appendix Lemmas 7.3–7.4 (QP ⇐ ICP) have one-line proofs; Lemmas 7.5–7.6 (QP ⇒ ICP) are stated "without proof" as straightforward (p. 41).
  - Transferable: to get a superlinear rate for a semismooth reformulation whose inner map is an argmin, (i) derive the Jacobian of the argmin by implicit differentiation of the subproblem KKT system, (ii) express the needed regularity (b-/R-regularity) as nonsingularity or positive definiteness of a reduced KKT matrix, and (iii) prove that by the standard "u^TQu = 0 ⇒ u = 0 by second-order sufficiency, then LICQ kills the multipliers" contradiction (pp. 8–11).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The implementation is in MATLAB, with Neumaier's MINQ as subproblem solver and all parameters listed (ρ = 0.9, γθ = 10⁻⁴, ρ_small = 10⁻⁸, μ_small = κ_small = 10⁻⁴, δ = 0.5, λ0 = 0; p. 18). The test problems are random QPs with a planted primal-dual solution. Active and inactive bounds and their multipliers are chosen first to enforce strict complementarity. Q_i is built from range and null-space bases so that it is strictly convex, convex, or indefinite with SSOSC. Coupling rows are built from null-space bases, and q_i is set so that the planted point is stationary (pp. 18–19). There are 100 instances per configuration, nN = 100–400 (p. 19). Three globalization arms are compared: merit, filter-2 and filter-5 (p. 19). Metrics: number solved, average iterations, average function evaluations (= rounds of subproblem solves), average residual and CPU time (Tables 1–10, pp. 21–31). The baselines are a normalized diminishing-step subgradient method, proximal Jacobian ADMM (Deng et al.) with the penalty parameters "also employed by the authors in their experiments", and the proximal center algorithm of Necoara–Suykens. Limits are 5000 iterations or 900 s (p. 20). Results: Algorithm 1 takes about 10 or fewer iterations on strictly convex problems (p. 19). subgrad and prox-cent solve 0 instances. prox-admm solves 18/100, 0/100, 100/100 and 0/100 across the four strictly convex inequality configurations, with far more iterations (p. 23). The convex baselines solve essentially none (pp. 20, 27–28). The paper reports its own failures. filter-5 is "slightly worse" on small equality-coupled problems, attributed to the filter being "too flexible" (p. 20). The indefinite results include failures, down to 0/100 for filter-5 in two configurations (Table 10, p. 31). The authors note that raising the time limit would solve more problems (p. 29). Baseline results on indefinite problems are not reported because those "methods are not designed to solve nonconvex problems" (p. 29). Applications: (i) electricity-market social welfare on MATPOWER case9–case118, 10 runs each, against subgradient; the method is "2-3 orders of magnitude faster" (p. 33). (ii) Water-network analysis (hanoi, ..., modena) for a = 1 and a = 1.857, 10 runs, with no baseline in Table 12 (p. 35). (iii) A nonconvex newsvendor with Nr = 10 and 100 scenarios over 100 runs, where the objective is checked against IPOPT (Table 13, p. 36). No parallel or distributed timing is reported (§§5–6 read).
  - Transferable: a planted-solution generator for structured QPs. Fix x*, ξ*, λ* and the active sets first, build Q_i from range and null-space bases to set the curvature class, build the coupling rows from null-space bases, then solve for q_i. Each instance then has a known stationary point and known regularity (LICQ, SSOSC, strict complementarity), and the number of active coupling constraints m_a is a controllable knob (pp. 18–19).
  - Transferable: when one function evaluation means N subproblem solves, report function evaluations next to iterations, and justify a globalization choice (here the filter) by evaluations saved (pp. 13, 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the problem-decomposition class, against Schur-complement interior-point decomposition (Gondzio–Grothey, Zavala–Laird–Biegler). Those methods need solver internals, and the approach "can be less effective" in active-set methods (p. 2). It reviews first-order dual methods (Nesterov smoothing, excessive gap, path-following) and second-order ones (Necoara–Suykens barrier; Frasch–Sager–Diehl semismooth Newton on the dual for strictly convex QPs with equality coupling). It distinguishes itself on three points: coupling inequalities, nonconvexity, and not maximizing the dual (pp. 3–4). Relative to the NCP literature (De Luca–Facchinei–Kanzow, Munson et al.), its formulation is "somewhat more straightforward" because both complementarity components are functions of λ (p. 7). The C¹/LC¹ assumption of that literature does not hold here (p. 5). The filter is credited to Fletcher–Leyffer, Gould–Leyffer–Toint and Kanzow–Petra (p. 13). The paper says it "builds on and generalizes" Bai and Raghunathan (ACC 2013) (p. 5). Prior work is criticised directly but briefly, not reformulated. Frasch–Sager–Diehl, the nearest second-order rival, is cited (p. 4) but not run in §5.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution sentence comes at the start of §1.2: "In contrast to existing approaches that employ dual maximization as in (1.4), we consider solving the implicit complementarity problem" (p. 4). The organization paragraph gives page pointers to Algorithms 1 and 2 (p. 6). After Theorem 2.2 a plain-language paragraph lists the key assumptions and what they allow (p. 11). Hedging is concentrated on globalization: "we claim that, under reasonable conditions, these mechanisms should offer such guarantees" (p. 12). The results text is direct about weak spots (filter-5, indefinite failures, time limit; pp. 20, 29). The conclusion is short, restates the contributions and ends on "superior performance ... compared to a classical subgradient approach" on the three applications (p. 36). There are small slips: refs [31] and [32] are identical (p. 43), and the indefinite section says "Algorithm1 does not update the proximal parameter μ" where Algorithm 2 is meant (p. 29).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution has three layers: theory (local Q-superlinear rate of semismooth Newton on the ICP under subproblem LICQ, strict complementarity and SSOSC; Theorem 2.2, p. 9), algorithms (Algorithm 1 for strictly convex problems, Algorithm 2 with proximal updates for convex and indefinite ones; pp. 15, 17), and applications (pp. 29–36). Limits stated in the text: no global convergence proof (p. 12). In nonconvex problems the method may stop at points that are not second-order KKT points of (1.1) (p. 6). The indefinite runs fail on some instances and depend on the time limit (p. 29). filter-5 is less robust (pp. 20, 29). Limits visible but not stated: test sizes are small (nN ≤ 400, p. 19); there is no theory for Algorithm 2 (pp. 16–17); there is no comparison with a second-order decomposition rival; and the decomposition and privacy motivation (p. 32) is not backed by parallel timings. The only future direction given is that the edge-wise network decomposition "can be readily extended to decomposition of a large network into sub-networks" (p. 34). The conclusion lists no open problems (p. 36).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (variant ⚠): Algorithm 2's proximal-parameter rule updates (μ or κ, x̄) only when ‖Φ‖ ≤ κμ and the proximal term is not yet negligible, so the update depends on measured progress and is not scheduled (pp. 16–17). Two differences from Method 2: the rule trades proximal regularization against equation residual, not objective against feasibility, and it carries no proof. Algorithm 1's Newton-to-gradient fallback is a monitored switch inside one algorithm (pp. 14–15).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): the merit, filter-2 and filter-5 globalizations are run as arms of one algorithm on identical instances (p. 19). prox-admm is run with the parameter values its own authors used (p. 20). The paper's own weak arm (filter-5) and the indefinite failures are printed (pp. 20, 29–31). Against Method 5: the external rivals are first-order methods under 5000-iteration or 900-s caps; the closest second-order rival (Frasch–Sager–Diehl, p. 4) is not run; and the application comparisons use only subgradient (p. 33), no baseline (p. 35) or an IPOPT objective check (p. 36).
- Method 4 (contradiction ✗): success in §5 is "average residual below 10⁻⁶" of the semismooth system, the proposed method's own optimality measure. How the residual is computed for the baselines is not stated (pp. 19–20). An independent quality check, the objective against IPOPT, appears only for the newsvendor (p. 36).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the inner/outer coupling here goes through sensitivities, not termination tests. The outer Newton step needs only the subproblem solutions, their active sets and the KKT-based sensitivity system (3.2) (pp. 11–12), and the design explicitly avoids "access to internal routines" of the subproblem solver (p. 2). Subproblems are solved exactly by MINQ (p. 18); inexactness is not considered.
- Heuristic 10 (evidence, in the card only): the same skeleton climbs from strictly convex (Algorithm 1) to convex and indefinite (Algorithm 2, which adds only the proximal term and its update rule) (pp. 6, 14–17).
- new-pattern candidate: **Reformulate to shed a convexity requirement**. Replace dual maximization by an equation in the multipliers so that the reduction to KKT points needs no convex duality (pp. 4, 41).
- new-pattern candidate: **Prove the local rate, argue the globalization**. The superlinear local theory is fully proved, and global convergence is claimed as "should" hold with informal arguments (pp. 9–14).
- new-pattern candidate: **Planted-solution instance generator** with controllable regularity and active-set counts (pp. 18–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Recast a dual-decomposition fixed point as an implicit complementarity problem in the multipliers and solve it with semismooth Newton on the Fischer–Burmeister reformulation. Each F evaluation is still N separable subproblem solves, and the primal comes out directly as x(λ*), with no ergodic averaging (pp. 4, 6–7, 11–12).
- Prove BD-regularity by reducing it to b-/R-regularity, then to nonsingularity of reduced KKT matrices built from subproblem sensitivities, using the "SSOSC forces u = 0, LICQ forces the multipliers to 0" contradiction (pp. 7–11).
- Choose between two semismooth operators by the cost of their step: a linear system (Fischer–Burmeister) against an optimization problem (Minimum) (p. 11).
- Gate parameter updates on ‖residual‖ ≤ κμ, in the Fiacco–McCormick style, and reset the filter after each parameter change (pp. 16–17).
- Build random structured test problems from a planted primal-dual solution with prescribed LICQ, SSOSC, strict complementarity and number of active coupling constraints (pp. 18–19).
- Compare globalization mechanisms (merit vs filters with different block counts) as arms of the same algorithm, count function evaluations as the expensive unit, and report where the more permissive arm fails (pp. 13, 19–20, 29–31).
