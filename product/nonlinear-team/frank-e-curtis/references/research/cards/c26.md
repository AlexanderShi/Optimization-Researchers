### S044 · A stochastic-gradient-based interior-point algorithm for solving smooth bound-constrained optimization problems (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2025, DOI 10.1137/23M1569460; read as arXiv:2304.14907 v3)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–28 read in full; references pp. 28–30 skimmed). The text read is the arXiv v3 preprint (COR@L TR 23T-018), not the journal version. First page checked: title and "Frank E. Curtis" as first author (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Vyacheslav Kungurtsev, Daniel P. Robinson, Qi Wang · **Place in trajectory**: the first interior-point rung of the stochastic line. The equality-constrained stochastic SQP papers ([3, 4, 5, 14, 15], p. 3) come first; this paper isolates the obstacle those methods would meet with inequalities: barrier derivatives "are not Lipschitz continuous in the interior of a set of bound constraints" (p. 3). S059 cites it as [10] (p. 3 of S059).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap claim: no interior-point method for nonconvex problems "has been shown rigorously to offer convergence guarantees" when only stochastic gradients are available (p. 2). The one prior stochastic-approximation IPM, Carbonetto et al. [12], is said to have an asymptotic claim that "overlooks a critical issue related to the step sizes" (p. 3; worked out in Remark 3.3, p. 15). The scope is deliberately narrowed to bound constraints "for a straightforward presentation" (p. 3). The paper argues that the hard part of any general extension is exactly the bound-constrained barrier problem (p. 3). Problem (1): min f(x) over B = [l, u], f possibly nonconvex (p. 5).
  - Transferable: when the general problem is too much, find the one obstacle every extension must cross (here the non-Lipschitz barrier gradient), and solve the smallest problem class that contains it. State in the introduction that this is why the scope is narrow (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (p. 6) keeps the log-barrier but removes the three components that need exact information (contributions (i)–(iii), p. 3). (i) The barrier parameter follows a prescribed sequence {µk} ↘ 0 rather than a stationarity test, since such tests cannot be computed in the stochastic setting (p. 3). (ii) The fraction-to-the-boundary rule is replaced by a prescribed inner neighborhood N[l,u](θk) = {l + θk ≤ x ≤ u − θk} with {θk} ↘ 0 (eq. (5), p. 6). (iii) There is no line search: the step size follows an ℓ⁻¹-type rule whose denominator is a computable local Lipschitz bound of the barrier gradient (Parameter Rule 3.1, p. 9). The direction is dk = −Hk⁻¹qk with diagonal Hk and qk the (stochastic) barrier gradient (eq. (7), p. 6). Kept from classical IPMs: the log barrier, continuation in µ, and the KKT recovery through yk = µk/(xk − l) (p. 13). The whole design reduces to a balance among three sequences (barrier, neighborhood, step size), with exponents µk = µ1k^tµ, θk−1 = θ0k^tθ and a step-size factor k^tα (p. 9). Non-diagonal Hk, path projections and a primal-dual variant are discussed and left out because they "would only obfuscate our analysis" (Remarks 2.1–2.2, pp. 6–7).
  - Transferable: when the information model makes an adaptive test uncomputable (stationarity, fraction-to-boundary, Armijo), replace each test by a prescribed sequence and make the admissible region of sequence exponents the theorem. Plot that region (Figure 1, p. 17).
  - Transferable: make the step-size denominator a computable upper bound of a local Lipschitz constant that is valid along the step actually taken (the αk,pre / γ̄k construction, pp. 9–10). Use the same tighter bound in analysis and code: "we make these choices to have consistency between our analysis and numerical experimentation" (Remark 3.1, p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.1: f is C¹ on an open superset of B, bounded below and above on B, with a Lipschitz and bounded gradient (p. 5). Upper boundedness is flagged as "the nonstandard aspect", added for extreme cases in the stochastic setting (p. 5). Assumption 3.1: iterate distances to finite bounds are bounded by some χ > 1, unknown to the algorithm (p. 7). Stochastic Assumption 3.2: unbiased estimates with errors bounded almost surely by known σ2, σ∞ (p. 16). The authors name this as the limit of the work: "we assume that the errors of the stochastic gradient estimators are bounded by a known value", which is looser than [9] in the unconstrained case (p. 4). The rules also need problem constants (ℓ∇f,B, κ∇f,B,∞, σ∞), which the authors note can be estimated (pp. 3–4, 24).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (1) A shifted barrier ϕ̃ (log((x−l)/χ)) is bounded below by f_inf and decreases as µ increases, so decreases across changing µ telescope (Lemma 3.1, pp. 7–8). (2) A point-pair Lipschitz bound ℓ∇f,B + µ/a(x, x̄) + µ/b(x, x̄) that blows up as θ ↘ 0 (Lemma 3.2, p. 8; Remark 3.2, p. 10). (3) A lower bound on the neighborhood step fraction γk by case analysis on the sign of ∇if, the quadratic formula and the mean value theorem on √· (Lemma 3.6, pp. 11–13). (4) The deterministic theorem uses summability of γkαk‖∇ϕ̃‖² against unsummable γkαk to get a liminf (Theorem 3.1, p. 13). KKT recovery uses bounded auxiliary multipliers µk/θk−1 and Bolzano–Weierstrass (pp. 13–14). (5) The exponent bookkeeping in Corollary 3.1 is deliberately written out to show where tµ ≥ tθ and where tµ ≤ tθ is needed, forcing tµ = tθ (pp. 14–15). (6) In the stochastic case, step sizes are random and correlated with the gradient error. Lemma 3.10 splits on the sign of Pk = ∇ϕ̃ᵀH⁻¹(∇ϕ̃ − Qk) by total expectation and pays only the "buffer" part of the step size (p. 18). (7) The noise term is O(k^2tµ) + O(k^(tµ+2tα)), summable if tµ < −1/2 and tµ + 2tα < −1 (Remark 3.4, pp. 16–17). This gives liminf ‖∇ϕ̃‖ = 0 almost surely (Theorem 3.2, p. 21). The authors explain why they get no lim result: the barrier gradient is not globally Lipschitz over B (p. 21). (8) §4 is a negative-result section. For a projected variant on a strongly convex f, the natural recurrence uk+1 ≤ vkuk + ek has ek/(1 − vk) tending to a nonzero constant, so the standard argument fails (Propositions 4.1–4.3, pp. 22–23).
  - Transferable: when random step sizes correlate with gradient noise, split the expectation on the sign of the cross term and bound only the part of the step size above its deterministic minimum, the "buffer" (Lemma 3.10, p. 18). Then require the buffer sequences to decay fast enough (Parameter Rule 3.3, p. 16).
  - Transferable: add a short "obstacles for a simplified algorithm" section that shows exactly where the obvious simpler method's proof breaks (§4, pp. 22–23). It answers the reviewer question ("Upon seeing our algorithm, a reader may wonder if a simpler variant has convergence guarantees", p. 3) before it is asked.
  - Transferable: write out a redundant proof step when it shows why a parameter coupling (tµ = tθ) is forced (Corollary 3.1 preamble, p. 14).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab on the Lehigh cluster (p. 24). The test set is the 43 LIBSVM binary-classification datasets of at most 8 GB (p. 24), each with logistic regression and a one-hidden-layer tanh network with cross-entropy loss (p. 24). The bounds l = −1, u = 1 are chosen so that "many bounds" are active at a solution (p. 24). The problem constants ℓ∇f,B and κ∇f,B,∞ are estimated by a 500-iteration true-gradient pre-run, and σ∞ from 100 stochastic gradients at x1 (p. 24, Table 1 p. 26). The rival is PSGM, a projected (stochastic) gradient method, with step sizes using "the same initial and final step sizes" schedule as SIPM "in order to have a direct comparison" (p. 25). PSGM with a fixed step was not tried because SIPM is only defined for diminishing steps (p. 25). The simplified §4 algorithm was also run and judged "qualitatively identical" to PSGM relative to SIPM, but not shown (p. 24). Schedules are tied to the budget maxiter (pp. 24–25). The buffer formulas were picked after trying others: "Other formulas ... were tested; the values above worked best for our experiments" (p. 25). The measure is a per-problem relative difference in [−1, 1] of the final objective and the projected-gradient norm, the latter computed with true gradients (p. 25). The stochastic runs use 10 runs per problem with box plots, 1 and 1000 epochs, and test loss where test sets exist (26 datasets) (pp. 25, 27). Reported outcome: SIPM wins at small budgets and is "as reliable" at large budgets. One problem where PSGM "significantly outperformed SIPM" at maxiter = 100 is named (p. 25).
  - Transferable: when comparing a diminishing-step method with a projected-gradient baseline, give the baseline the same step-size schedule endpoints so that only the constraint-handling mechanism differs (p. 25).
  - Transferable: estimate the problem constants your step rule needs with a short, fixed pre-run, and publish them per problem in a table (Table 1, p. 26).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper continues the authors' stochastic SQP line ([3, 5, 14, 15], p. 3) and the SIAM Review survey for unconstrained stochastic-gradient theory ([9], pp. 4, 21). It names deterministic IPMs (Byrd–Hribar–Nocedal [11], Wächter–Biegler [27]) as the source of the fraction-to-the-boundary rule it drops (p. 3). Randomized IPMs for linear objectives ([2, 19]) are set aside as a different setting (p. 3). Carbonetto–Schmidt–de Freitas [12] get a pointed technical correction, not a dismissal (Remark 3.3, p. 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions come as five numbered items, each phrased as what the algorithm does not rely on (i–iii) or what the analysis gives (iv–v) (pp. 3–4). A limitation paragraph follows immediately (p. 4). Remarks are used heavily to fence off generalizations (Remarks 2.1, 2.2, 3.1–3.4). The deterministic setting is given a "complete investigation" because the algorithm is new there too (p. 3). Hedging stays moderate: "can outperform" (abstract, p. 2; p. 4), "as reliable as" (p. 25).
  - Transferable: phrase each design contribution as a dependency removed ("does not rely on ... stationarity tests"; "does not employ a fraction-to-the-boundary rule"; "does not rely on step acceptance criteria"), and pair each with the reason it cannot be computed in the target setting (p. 3).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic and theoretical contribution: the first stochastic-gradient IPM with deterministic and almost-sure liminf guarantees for nonconvex bound-constrained problems (pp. 3–4, 21), plus experiments. Stated limits: noise bounded almost surely by a known value (p. 4); liminf, not lim (p. 21); bounds only. Future work: pair the method with stochastic equality-constrained SQP toward general stochastic IPMs (p. 27). No rates or complexity are given (none claimed).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the objective-versus-constraint parameter (barrier µ) is still the research object, but in the stochastic setting it becomes a prescribed schedule balanced against the neighborhood and step-size schedules (pp. 3, 6, 9, 17). It is not an adaptive rule on predicted progress, because "stationarity measures cannot be computed accurately in the stochastic setting" (p. 3).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the baseline shares the step-size schedule endpoints (p. 25), stochastic runs are repeated 10 times (p. 25), and the incumbent's win is named (p. 25). But the buffer formulas were tuned on the test set ("worked best for our experiments", p. 25) with no matching tuning for PSGM, and the §4 variant's results were withheld (p. 24).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (weak): the hard feature is manufactured by a rule. Bounds l = −1, u = 1 make "many bounds" active on standard ML data (p. 24). These are active bounds, not infeasible or degenerate variants.
- Heuristic 10 evidence (not a Method; recorded as a note): the deterministic twin is developed and tested in full beside the stochastic version (pp. 3, 10–15, 25).
- new-pattern candidate: **prescribed schedules replace uncomputable tests**: once a stationarity test or step-acceptance test cannot be evaluated, replace it with a prescribed sequence and prove the admissible exponent region (pp. 3, 9, 16–17).
- new-pattern candidate: **obstacle section for the obvious simpler method** (§4, pp. 22–23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn "this test needs exact information" into "this parameter follows a prescribed sequence" and make the joint admissible region of the sequence exponents the theorem. Draw it as a figure (pp. 3, 16–17).
- Bound the cross term between a random step size and gradient noise by splitting on its sign and charging only the buffer above the deterministic minimum step (p. 18).
- Write a section proving where the simpler variant's standard analysis fails, even in the strongly convex deterministic case (pp. 22–23).
- Estimate problem constants by a short true-gradient pre-run and tabulate them per instance (pp. 24, 26).

**Quotes**:
- "This is significant since stationarity measures cannot be computed accurately in the stochastic setting that we consider." (p. 3)
- "Upon seeing our algorithm, a reader may wonder if a simpler variant has convergence guarantees." (p. 3)

### S054 · Almost-sure convergence of iterates and multipliers in stochastic sequential quadratic optimization (Journal of Optimization Theory and Applications 2025, DOI 10.1007/S10957-024-02568-2; read as arXiv:2308.03687 v1)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–22 read in full; references pp. 22–24 skimmed). The text read is the arXiv v1 preprint (COR@L TR 23T-019, 7 Aug 2023), not the JOTA version. First page checked: title and "Frank E. Curtis" as first author (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xin Jiang, Qi Wang · **Place in trajectory**: a theory follow-up to the 2021 stochastic SQP paper [3] (Berahas–Curtis–Robinson–Zhou). It upgrades the in-expectation guarantee of that paper to almost-sure statements about iterates and multipliers, for a simplified variant (pp. 3, 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For the stochastic-gradient subclass of stochastic SQP ([3, 2, 16, 17]), guarantees "have been limited to asymptotic convergence of the expected value of a stationarity measure to zero" (abstract, p. 2; p. 3). Unconstrained SG methods have almost-sure results (Robbins–Siegmund, Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis, pp. 4–5). The gap is therefore defined by analogy with the unconstrained theory. Multipliers matter for stationarity certificates, sensitivity and active-set identification. Inequality solvers call equality-constrained subsolvers, so multiplier convergence there is a prerequisite (p. 3). Problem (4): min E[F(x, ι)] subject to c(x) = 0, with exact constraints and stochastic objective gradients (p. 6).
  - Transferable: find a gap by checking which classical guarantee type (here almost-sure convergence) exists for the unconstrained ancestor but not for your constrained method. Then state the target as "bring the analysis in line with" the ancestor (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: No new algorithm. Algorithm 1 (p. 8) is a simplified version of [3]: the merit parameter τ and ratio parameter ξ are fixed at values small enough that they never need adaptive updates (Assumption 2.2, p. 7), with step sizes αk = βkτξ/(τL∇f + Γ) and {βk} unsummable but square-summable (eq. (10), p. 7). The simplification is admitted ("in order to allow our analysis not to be obscured by auxiliary details"). The authors "conjecture" the results also hold for the original variant in the event of [3, §3.2.1] (p. 7). The new idea is on the multiplier side: the multiplier error splits into a primal-distance part plus a gradient-noise part, so the noise part can be averaged away (pp. 3, 14–16). The practical version averages over a window of recent iterates within distance ε of the current one (p. 21).
  - Transferable: to analyze an adaptive method, first analyze the variant whose adaptive parameters are frozen, state the conjecture for the full method, and check in experiments that the adaptive rules stayed constant (pp. 7, 21).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.1: iterates in an open convex set where f and c are smooth and bounded, and the Jacobian has full row rank with singular values bounded below (p. 6). Loosening this to "almost surely" is declined as adding generality without substance (p. 6). Assumption 2.3: unbiased gradients with bounded conditional variance (p. 8), weaker than S044's almost-sure bound. Assumption 2.4: Hk bounded and uniformly positive definite on the null space (p. 8). Theorem 3.1 adds two conditions. First, a local PL-type inequality (20) linking the merit gap to the reduced gradient and the constraint violation (p. 11; interpreted on p. 12). Second, the conditional hypothesis limsup ‖Xk − x⋆‖ ≤ ε almost surely (p. 11). Assumption 4.1: H(x) and M(x) are locally Lipschitz near x⋆ (p. 13), justified in two bullet paragraphs that cite Byrd–Schnabel on the continuity of null-space bases (pp. 13–14). Lemma 4.1's conditions (30) are "admittedly nontrivial" and argued to be natural CLT-type conditions (p. 18). Corollary 4.2 and Theorem 4.2 assume the iterates eventually stay in the neighborhood almost surely, which Theorem 3.1 "does not directly imply" (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (1) The Robbins–Siegmund almost-supermartingale theorem is applied to Rk = ϕτ(Xk) − τf_inf. It gives an almost-surely finite limit of the merit values and Σαk Δqτ < ∞ (Lemma 1.2, p. 4; Lemma 3.1, pp. 9–10). (2) The step is decomposed into null-space and range-space parts (Dk = ZkWk + Vk), bounding the merit model reduction below by the squared reduced gradient plus the constraint violation (Lemma 3.2, pp. 10–11). (3) A constrained PL generalization (20) turns liminf Δq = 0 into convergence of the merit value and of the iterates to x⋆ (Theorem 3.1, pp. 11–12). (4) The multiplier error is split three ways: Yk − y⋆ is a constraint-residual term, plus Mk(∇f(x⋆) − Gk), plus (M⋆ − Mk)∇f(x⋆) (eq. (25), p. 14). This gives ‖Yk − y⋆‖ ≤ κy‖Xk − x⋆‖ plus a noise term (Theorem 4.1, pp. 14–15). The "true" multiplier has no noise term (Corollary 4.1, p. 15). (5) Averaged multipliers are handled with a multidimensional martingale CLT for triangular arrays plus moment convergence (Lemmas 1.3–1.4, p. 5; Lemma 4.1, pp. 16–17). The CLT is applied to ξk,i = Mi∆i/√k. The averaged noise term then vanishes in expectation, so the limsup of the averaged-multiplier error is bounded by the averaged primal distance alone (Theorem 4.2, p. 17). Almost-sure convergence follows with null-set bookkeeping (Corollary 4.3, pp. 18–20).
  - Transferable: to show that an estimator built from noisy steps (here the multipliers) converges, split its error into a "distance of the primal iterate" term and a "current noise" term. Then average the noise term with a martingale CLT on the triangular array √(1/k)·Mi∆i (pp. 14, 16–17).
  - Transferable: generalize PL to equality constraints as "merit gap ≤ µ(τ‖Zᵀ∇f‖² + ‖c‖)" locally. Check that it reduces to PL on affine constraints and to a constraint-violation bound when f is flat in the range space (pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: A "small set" of demonstrations (p. 20). Problem: constrained logistic regression with ‖x‖² = 1 plus m = 10 random linear constraints (nonconvex), on four LIBSVM datasets (a9a, australian, ionosphere, splice). These were "selected as ones that led to relatively large errors in the stochastic gradient estimates" (p. 20). Code: the public Matlab StochasticSQP package of [3] (GitHub footnote, p. 20), with Hk = I and multipliers from the linear system, not from a null-space basis (p. 20). The reference solution (x⋆, y⋆) comes from a true-gradient run checked for first- and second-order conditions, so global optimality is verified (p. 21). Mini-batch 16. {βk} was "tuned in a manner such that the primal iterates tended toward the globally optimal primal solution" (p. 21). The authors verified that τ stayed at 0.1 and ξ at 1.0 in all runs, so the runs matched the analyzed simplified variant (p. 21). Each plot shows one run of 10⁵ iterations; other seeds are said to be "qualitatively consistent" but are not shown (p. 21). Figure 1 shows the primal distance, the raw, true and fully averaged multiplier errors, and windowed averages for several ε (p. 21). The raw multiplier oscillates while the true and averaged multipliers reach much smaller errors (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: when a theorem compares estimators, plot the uncomputable oracle (true multiplier) beside the computable candidates (raw, averaged, windowed), so the reader sees how much of the oracle's accuracy the practical estimator recovers (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: when the analyzed algorithm is a simplified variant of a released code, log the adaptive parameters in every run and report that they never moved (p. 21).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Continues [3] and the authors' related stochastic SQP works ([2, 16, 17], p. 3). Other stochastic SQP methods ([1, 4, 5, 19, 26, 27, 30, 33]) are set apart because they impose "different (often stronger)" requirements on the gradient estimates (p. 3). Na–Mahoney [28] is acknowledged as comparable but with a different algorithm and assumptions (p. 3). Averaging is tied to ergodic analysis and Polyak–Juditsky, and distinguished from Nesterov's dual averaging (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The background section restates Robbins–Monro, Robbins–Siegmund and Bertsekas–Tsitsiklis, so the reader sees the unconstrained template before the constrained result (pp. 4–5). After each nonstandard assumption comes an explicit discussion, either "A few important observations and justifications are in order" (p. 13) or the "admittedly nontrivial" paragraph (p. 18). The road from result to result is announced ("Our main result of this section is Theorem 3.1 on page 11", p. 9). The roads not taken are noted (least-squares multipliers, p. 13; Hessian of the Lagrangian at the previous multiplier, p. 13).
  - Transferable: follow each strong assumption with a paragraph that (a) gives concrete algorithmic choices that satisfy it, (b) names the trap that makes it non-obvious (here, that no globally continuous null-space basis exists), and (c) cites a construction that avoids the trap (pp. 13–14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Theoretical contribution: almost-sure convergence of iterates (under local PL and an eventual-neighborhood hypothesis), of multipliers, and of a stationarity measure. Averaging removes the noise floor of the multipliers (pp. 3, 22). Limits as written: a simplified variant with a fixed merit parameter (p. 7); conditional hypotheses such as an a.s. eventual neighborhood that the theory does not supply (pp. 11, 15, 17); conditions (30) nontrivial (p. 18); the experiments use tuned step sizes and single displayed runs (p. 21). No explicit future-work paragraph (the conclusion summarizes only, p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper questions what the earlier guarantee actually measured. The quantity in the guarantee of [3], the "true" multiplier, "is not actually computed in the algorithm" (p. 15). The paper then shows that the computable multiplier carries an irreducible noise term and builds the averaged estimator that fixes it (pp. 14–16, 21–22).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the merit parameter is frozen for the analysis (Assumption 2.2, p. 7), the opposite of making its update the object. The adaptive rule is checked to stay constant in runs (p. 21).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: this is a demonstration, not a comparison. Step sizes were tuned per run toward the known solution and single runs are shown (p. 21), which departs from the "many runs" rule of Method 5 step 6.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (weak): datasets were chosen for large gradient noise, the stress the theory targets (p. 20). The constraint ‖x‖² = 1 is added to make standard data nonconvex (p. 20).
- Heuristic 1 evidence (not a Method; recorded as a note): the event the analysis needs (fixed τ, ξ) is checked in every run (p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: **freeze-to-analyze, verify-in-runs**: analyze the variant with adaptive parameters frozen, conjecture for the full method, and verify in experiments that the parameters stayed frozen (pp. 7, 21).
- new-pattern candidate: **oracle beside estimator in plots**: show the uncomputable "true" quantity next to practical estimators (p. 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Apply Robbins–Siegmund to the merit function with the true-gradient model reduction as the "P" term to get almost-sure limits from an in-expectation descent lemma (pp. 9–10).
- Bound the multiplier error by primal distance plus current gradient noise, then average to remove the noise term. In practice, average only over a window of iterates close to the current one (pp. 14–17, 21).
- Generalize PL to equality constraints with the reduced gradient plus constraint violation, and check its two special cases (pp. 11–12).

**Quotes**:
- "These datasets were selected as ones that led to relatively large errors in the stochastic gradient estimates." (p. 20)
- "The conditions in (30) in Lemma 4.1 are admittedly nontrivial, but upon closer inspection it is not surprising that conditions akin to these would be needed in order to draw any useful conclusions about an averaged Lagrange multiplier sequence." (p. 18)

### S059 · An interior-point algorithm for continuous nonlinearly constrained optimization with noisy function and derivative evaluations (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2502.11302)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–35 read in full, including Appendix A, pp. 38–39; references pp. 36–38 skimmed). First page checked: title and "Frank E. Curtis" as first author (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shima Dezfulian, Andreas Waechter (Wächter) · **Place in trajectory**: joins two lines. One is the noiseless step-decomposition IPM of Curtis–Schenk–Wächter 2010 ([15], p. 3; card S010 in batch c05). The other is the bound-constrained noisy IPM of Dezfulian–Wächter ([16], p. 3). The rung is "deterministic noise + nonlinear inequalities".

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Contemporary IPMs (Ipopt, KNITRO) assume exact function and derivative values (p. 2). The paper separates stochastic noise from deterministic noise, which is computational, bounded, and can make the noisy function nonsmooth or discontinuous (pp. 2–3). Its gap claim: algorithms with deterministic noise exist for unconstrained, bound- and equality-constrained problems, but none has been extended "to the nonlinear-inequality-constrained setting with deterministic noise" (p. 3). The bound-constrained method [16] cannot be reused directly for three reasons: Newton-type line-search IPMs can fail (Wächter–Biegler [29]), rank-deficient Jacobians make Newton subproblems ill-posed, and an infeasible method needs a merit function (p. 3). Problem (1): min f̄0(x) s.t. c̄I(x) ≤ 0 (p. 5). The algorithm aims at least at the infeasibility-minimization problem (3) when (1) is infeasible or degenerate (p. 6).
  - Transferable: before extending a method to a harder information model, list the specific mechanisms of the noiseless method that break, each with its classical failure reference (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (p. 11) solves one barrier subproblem (7) for a fixed µ. It reuses the 2010 IPM of [15]: slack formulation, scaled derivatives (p. 7), a normal step in a trust region of radius ω‖JᵀkCk‖ (eq. (12), p. 8), a tangential step from a primal-dual system (14) (p. 8), Hessian modification by inertia checks (pp. 8–9), merit ϕ = τf + ‖c‖ with the merit parameter decreased to satisfy a model-reduction condition (16)–(18) (pp. 9–10), fraction-to-the-boundary (19), and a slack reset (21) (p. 10). The noise-specific changes are three. (a) A relaxed Armijo condition with slack (2 + ζ)εk, where εk = τkϵf + ϵc (eq. (20), p. 10). (b) Termination tests at a noisy infeasible stationary point (line 2) and at a noisy stationary point (line 6) (pp. 8–9, 11). (c) The noise bounds (ϵf, ϵc) are known inputs (p. 11). The stated contribution is the merit-parameter strategy in noise: "Extending such an approach and its analysis to the noisy setting is the main contribution of this work" (p. 4).
  - Transferable: to make a line search noise-tolerant, relax the Armijo condition by a constant proportional to the known noise in the merit function (not in the gradient), so it is always satisfiable for small steps (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.1: smooth problem functions on an open set containing the iterates; noisy evaluations within known bounds ϵf, ϵg, ϵc, ϵJ (p. 5). Assumption 4.1 on Wk is strengthened to hold for noiseless quantities too "for simplicity" (pp. 12–13). Assumption 4.2 bounds the slacks. The authors note that, unlike the noiseless case, this cannot be proved because "the merit function values are not monotonically decreasing" (p. 13). Assumptions 4.3 and 4.4 require the Jacobian singular values to stay above the Jacobian noise level, and the noisy and noiseless null spaces not to differ too much (pp. 13, 20). The authors note that both "impose implicit bounds on the noise level" (p. 13). The analysis treats a single barrier subproblem only. Convergence as µ → 0 is not covered, and such an analysis "would be questionable in any case" with deterministic noise (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (1) Twin quantities: every noisy quantity has a barred noiseless counterpart (v̄k, ūk, d̄k, τ̄k) computed with the same Wk. The analysis bounds the differences εv, εu, εd, ετ (eq. (27), p. 14). (2) A perturbation bound for the normal step: a change of variables puts both subproblems on the same feasible set, and the variational inequalities are summed (Lemma 4.5, pp. 16–18). (3) Tangential steps are written as oblique (Γ-orthogonal) projections onto null spaces (Lemma 4.6, pp. 18–19) and bounded with Stewart's perturbation theory for oblique projectors ([27], Lemma 4.9, pp. 20–21). (4) The noiseless model reduction Δm̄ is shown to be a valid stationarity measure (Lemma 4.10, p. 22), and the noisy-minus-noiseless model gap is bounded (Lemmas 4.12–4.13, Corollary 4.2, pp. 23–24). (5) The noiseless merit parameter is eventually constant and positive under the Jacobian assumption (Lemma 4.19, p. 27). (6) The main theorem is a contradiction: if the stationarity measure stayed above the noise threshold, each step would decrease the noiseless merit by at least ζεk, contradicting boundedness below (Theorem 1, pp. 29–30). The resulting bound is max{ρk,1, ρk,2, ρk,3}, and its constants vanish with the noise except a τ0·g_sup term. That term is traced to the divergence of the noisy and noiseless merit parameters (Remark 4.1, p. 24; observations, pp. 30–31). (7) Appendix A gives a weaker result without Assumptions 4.3–4.4, of "less interest in practice" (p. 12; pp. 38–39).
  - Transferable: for a noisy-evaluation algorithm, define the twin iteration that would have been computed with exact values at the same iterate and the same matrix. Bound the step and model differences, then transfer the noiseless theory through those bounds (pp. 14–24).
  - Transferable: when a noise-floor bound contains a term that does not vanish with the noise, trace it to an algorithmic state (here the merit parameter's history) and give the practical fix (a small, conservative τ0) beside its cost (p. 31).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab (p. 32). Test set: CUTE/AMPL problems with at least one general constraint and l < u componentwise, at most 100 primal or dual variables: 137 problems, with the filters stated as rules (p. 32). The initial point is projected inside bounds as in Ipopt [31, §3.6]. Problems are scaled when a gradient norm exceeds 10, "as is typical for software" (p. 32). Subsolvers: Moré–Sorensen for the normal step; ldl inertia checks with identity shifts for the Hessian modification (p. 32). Settings: µ ∈ {10⁻¹, 10⁻⁴}; noise ϵf = ϵc ∈ {10⁻², 10⁻⁶} with ϵg = ϵJ = ϵH = √ϵf (p. 32). Noise is drawn uniformly from intervals or balls, and the Jacobian noise keeps the sparsity pattern (p. 33). Runs last 2000 iterations or 1 hour, with the algorithm's own termination tests disabled "in order to be confident that the algorithm has reached the highest quality solution" (p. 33). Figures 1–4 report the percentage of problems whose geometric average over the last 10 iterates meets thresholds. Two measures are used (barrier-subproblem stationarity and infeasibility stationarity), each evaluated with noisy and with noiseless values (pp. 33–35). There is no rival solver or ablation. Outcome: robust across µ, and a smaller µ gives no better final accuracy because of the noise (p. 34).
  - Transferable: when the algorithm sees only noisy values, report each stationarity measure twice, computed from the noisy values the algorithm used and from the true values. This shows that "nearly stationary" means something for the underlying problem (p. 33).
  - Transferable: inject noise into a standard test set by a stated rule (uniform in balls, Jacobian noise with the true sparsity pattern, derivative noise at √ of function noise) so others can reproduce the stress (pp. 32–33).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Built "primarily upon" Dezfulian–Wächter [16] and the step decomposition of Curtis–Schenk–Wächter [15] (p. 3). Analysis aims are "modeled on" the noiseless results of [11] and [15] (p. 12). Noisy equality-constrained methods (Oztoprak–Byrd–Nocedal [23]) are cited for the impossibility of exact convergence under deterministic noise (p. 4). The authors' stochastic IPMs [9, 10] are listed as the stochastic-noise counterpart (p. 3). Practical relevance is claimed through closeness to Ipopt and KNITRO: the techniques "can be incorporated readily into state-of-the-art software packages" (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper declares its open questions before the theory: "We mention upfront that our proposed algorithm and analysis leave certain natural questions unanswered" (p. 4). A whole subsection, §4.1 "Discussion of Aims of Convergence Analysis", sets what can be hoped for before any lemma (pp. 11–12). After the theorem come bulleted "observations" that explain each term of the bound (pp. 30–31). The barrier-decrease heuristic is left to the conclusion as a proposal (p. 35).
  - Transferable: open the analysis with a subsection that states what the theorem can and cannot say in the noisy setting, and why (§4.1, pp. 11–12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic and theoretical contribution: a noise-tolerant step-decomposition IPM for a fixed barrier subproblem. It provably reaches a point where a noiseless stationarity measure is below a noise-dependent threshold (Theorem 1, pp. 29–30; conclusion p. 35). Stated limits: fixed µ only; no theory as µ → 0 (p. 4); assumptions on Jacobian noise (p. 13); a τ0-dependent non-vanishing term (p. 31); equality constraints omitted "for simplicity" (p. 5). Future work: a computable stopping test from (51) to drive a sequence of barrier subproblems (p. 35). The experiments show feasibility of the method, not superiority (pp. 31–34).

- Method 4 ✅ evidence: every stationarity measure is reported against noiseless values as well as the noisy values the algorithm used (Figures 2 and 4, pp. 33–35). The termination flags are disabled so that the reported quality does not depend on the algorithm's own stopping decision (p. 33).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Relax the line-search sufficient-decrease test by (2 + ζ)× the known merit-function noise so it stays satisfiable, then prove the noiseless merit must drop by ζεk whenever stationarity is above threshold (pp. 10, 30).
- Bound differences between noisy and exact tangential steps through oblique-projection perturbation theory (pp. 18–21).
- Report the stationarity measures both with the algorithm's noisy values and with true values, and disable your own termination flags while benchmarking (p. 33).
