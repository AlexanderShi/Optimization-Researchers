### S061 · A decomposition algorithm for large-scale security-constrained AC optimal power flow (arXiv preprint 2021, arXiv:2110.01737)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–21 (§§1–5, all figures and captions, acknowledgment) read in full. Reference list (pp. 21–23) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel K. Molzahn, Shenyinying Tu, Andreas Wächter, Ermin Wei, Elizabeth Wong · **Place in trajectory**: the write-up of team "GO-SNIP"'s entry to ARPA-E Grid Optimization Challenge 1 (Nov 2018–Oct 2019), plus later improvements (p. 2). It is an application/engineering paper built around Ipopt as the core solver (p. 8). The index notes that the same authors published a differently titled Oper. Res. 2023 paper (S060); this preprint is kept as a separate item.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is security-constrained AC optimal power flow (SC-AC-OPF) in the formulation fixed by the ARPA-E GO Challenge 1 (abstract, p. 1). It is entered from practice, not from theory. Operators need solutions within about 10 minutes and so fall back on DC approximations that lose voltage magnitudes and reactive power (p. 2). The potential savings of AC-OPF are put at "$6 billion to $9 billion" per year in the US (p. 2). The competition fixed the hardware, scoring (objective plus penalties on slacks), time limits (10 or 45 minutes for Code 1, 2 s per contingency for Code 2) and the two-code split (p. 7). The paper states that these rules "strongly influenced our algorithmic development" (p. 7). The structural hook is that only the complementarity constraints link base case and contingency variables, so the problem is "nearly separable" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: when an external benchmark fixes the rules (time limit, scoring, required outputs), list those rules before the algorithm and derive each design choice from one of them. Here: iterative contingency selection, an approximate fast evaluation first, and per-step time budgets (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A master-problem / contingency-selection decomposition. Solve the base case with Ipopt, rank contingencies, evaluate them in parallel, add the top three non-dominated contingencies to a master problem of the form (2.8), and repeat while evaluation continues during each master solve (Figure 2, p. 8; Algorithm GO-SNIP Steps 1–10, p. 14). Five components: (i) an initial ranking from a ridge-regression model on topology and power-loss features, trained on the competition datasets with 10-fold repeated cross-validation (pp. 9–10). Apparent power loss l^s has the largest coefficient, and the model is "universal" across network sizes (p. 10). (ii) Contingency evaluation as penalty minimization (3.1), with complementarities replaced by an active-set prediction (lower/middle/upper segment) that is updated from multiplier signs, using the test λ > 0 and −s/λ < 10⁻⁶ (pp. 10–11). Active-set predictions are updated only in contingency evaluation, not in the master problem, because the latter was "too time-consuming" (p. 11). Generator contingencies are initialized by a bisection on Δk with a 1.01 loss factor (p. 11). (iii) A fast evaluation that solves a square power-flow system (3.2) with Ipopt as a Newton solver, then projects onto bounds to obtain a feasible point and hence an upper bound on the penalty. Full evaluation runs only if the bound exceeds a cutoff that "is tightened iteratively by the overall algorithm" (p. 12). (iv) "Maximum violation dominated" contingencies (Definition 3.2), a relaxation of Capitanescu et al.'s individual dominance, which the authors found "too conservative" (p. 13). (v) Preprocessing that removes degeneracy: identical parallel lines and transformers, and aggregation of reactive power of generators at the same bus (p. 13). Code 2 gives each contingency (remaining total time)/(number of unevaluated contingencies) and processes the ranking in reverse order, so that the expensive contingencies come last and get the largest budgets (p. 14). Modelling choices: flow-definition variables are kept rather than eliminated, to preserve Jacobian sparsity; norm constraints are squared to make them smooth; the logical (complementarity) formulation is used rather than the projection or MIP formulation; polar coordinates are used (pp. 6–7).
  - Transferable: a two-tier evaluator. A cheap procedure that returns a certified upper bound (a feasible point) screens candidates, and the expensive exact evaluation runs only when the bound is above an adaptive cutoff (p. 12).
  - Transferable: when per-item time is capped globally, allocate the remaining time evenly over the remaining items and process the items predicted to be expensive last, so they inherit the slack (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The formulation is the competition's own: per-unit system, Π-circuit line and transformer models (2.1)–(2.2), relaxed balance equations with penalized slacks (2.3), squared current and power ratings (2.4)–(2.5), and preventive contingency response through complementarity constraints (2.6)–(2.7) (pp. 3–5). Areas are ignored and details are simplified "that are not of consequence for the main ideas" (p. 3). No convergence assumptions are stated, and no theory is claimed. Smoothness is the stated reason for squaring the rating constraints, since IPM theory depends on it (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a. The paper has no theorems or proofs (§§1–5 read). The only formal definitions are the two dominance definitions (Definitions 3.1–3.2, p. 13). One evaluation device matters: the objective of (3.1) is recomputed explicitly instead of taken from Ipopt's report, because "the latter is influenced by internal relaxations of the bound constraints" (p. 11).
  - Transferable: when a solver relaxes bounds internally, evaluate the objective you report yourself, at the returned point, from the original model (p. 11).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The implementation is C++ with MPI. In the competition it ran as six processes with 20 pthreads each; the parent process ran only the base case and master problems (p. 15). For the paper the experiments were rerun on a single 2×Xeon server with no time limits, because "the hardware used was significantly less powerful" and to isolate each heuristic (p. 15). Data: Challenge 1 offline datasets, all scenarios for the selected networks (p. 15). Four ablations, each switching one heuristic on or off. (§4.1) Four ranking heuristics (l^p, l^s, l^c, the regression l^w) against a baseline of full penalty minimization for every contingency. The measure is the share of total penalty captured by the top three contingencies as more of the ranked list is evaluated. Figure 3 shows four networks with "One representative scenario" each, and l^w generally captures more penalty earlier (pp. 15–17). (§4.2) With and without complementarity updates: the ratio of penalties averaged over scenarios is always ≥ 1, "often" significantly (p. 16; Figure 4, p. 18). (§4.3) With and without pre-screening, at a cutoff of 2×10⁻² p.u. (the first breakpoint of the competition penalty). Fast evaluation eliminates "over half" of the contingencies on most networks, and time drops for those deemed feasible (pp. 16–18; Figures 5–7, pp. 19–20). (§4.4) Cumulative selection arms: initial ranking → +fast → +full → +dominance filtering. Each is used to build a master problem with three contingencies, then all contingencies are evaluated and the gap to the best final-round competition score is reported. The total cost decreases monotonically "In nearly all cases" (p. 20; Figure 8, p. 21). The competition result: second of 27 teams; best score on 20 % of problems, best or second-best on 53 %, top ten on > 99 % (p. 2). Measures are penalty shares, ratios, times and optimality gaps. No other team's code is run.
  - Transferable: for a pipeline of heuristics, build cumulative arms (A, A+B, A+B+C, ...) and score each against an external best-known value (here the competition's best score), so the reader sees each component's marginal contribution (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the power-systems tradition of contingency screening and ranking (Ejebe–Wollenberg 1979; Stott et al. 1987; Capitanescu 2016) and says it was "inspired by this literature" but "created a new contingency ranking heuristic" for the competition's formulation and time limits (pp. 8–9). It relaxes Capitanescu et al. 2007 dominance as too conservative (p. 13). It cites the IPM-in-power-systems lineage from the 1990s onward (p. 8). It tried Hu–Ralph's penalty reformulation of complementarity, and it failed (pp. 11–12). It does not position itself against other GO competitors' methods beyond scores (p. 2, p. 20). Criticism of prior work is brief and practical ("too conservative", p. 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper is organized as a report of "The techniques found to be most effective in terms of performance in the challenge" (abstract, p. 1). It reports failed alternatives in passing, with hedged causal attributions: warm-starting did not work, "which may be due to" IPM warm-start difficulty (p. 10); the penalty reformulation failed, attributed to negative curvature (p. 12); multiplicative features led to "similar or inferior" performance (p. 10); size as a feature did not help, "We suspect" because contingency effects are local (p. 10); the projection formulation gave "little benefit" (p. 6). Figure 1 visualizes the most penalized contingencies on Network 1 (p. 3). The contribution claims are modest and empirical. The conclusion argues only that tailored nonlinear optimization can solve SC-AC-OPF "within industrially relevant time limits" (p. 21).
  - Transferable: in an applied algorithm paper, add one sentence for every alternative you tried and dropped, with your best guess at why and labelled as a guess. This saves readers the same detours (pp. 6–7, 10–12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and engineering: a complete decomposition pipeline with five tailored heuristics, validated by the competition placing and by component ablations (pp. 2, 15–21). Limits stated in the text: polar versus rectangular formulations not evaluated comprehensively (p. 7); experiments not on the competition platform and run without time limits (p. 15). Limits visible but not stated: no theory; Figure 3 selects one "representative" scenario per network without a stated selection rule (p. 16); the ablations compare against the team's own simpler variants, not other teams' codes; the time-limit benefits of fast evaluation are argued from evaluation times (Figure 7) rather than from runs under the actual limits (pp. 18–20). Future direction: newer datasets (Challenge 2, DR POWER, GRID DATA, PGLib-OPF) for ongoing work (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): each heuristic is "switched on and off" in a single framework (p. 15), and §4.4 builds cumulative arms (p. 20). Final quality is measured against the best competition score, not the team's own flag (p. 20). Against Method 5: no external rival code is run, one representative scenario per network is plotted without a selection rule (p. 16), and the experiments drop the time limits that motivated the design (p. 15).
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): solution quality is judged by an external yardstick, the competition's scoring (p. 7, p. 20). The objective is recomputed outside the solver because Ipopt's reported value is distorted by internal bound relaxation (p. 11).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the outer loop sets how much effort each inner contingency solve gets: a penalty cutoff "tightened iteratively by the overall algorithm" decides between fast and full evaluation (p. 12), and time budgets are allocated from what remains (p. 14). The inner effort serves the outer need, but through time and penalty cutoffs, not merit-model termination tests.
- Heuristic 3 (evidence, card only): the problem is a failure users hit in practice: AC-OPF considered "intractable" at industrial scale, so operators use DC approximations (p. 2).
- new-pattern candidate: **Competition as test bed and write-up of what worked** (the external rules fix the yardstick; report surviving and discarded heuristics) (pp. 2, 6–7, 10–12).
- new-pattern candidate: **Certified cheap bound as pre-screen** (a feasible point from a reduced square system gives an upper bound that decides whether the expensive solve runs) (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Handle complementarity constraints in an NLP inner solve by an outer active-set loop. Predict a segment, solve the smooth problem, flip the prediction when a multiplier is positive and the scaled slack −s/λ < 10⁻⁶, and stop when the objective stops improving or time runs out (p. 11).
- Remove degeneracy before the solver sees it: delete redundant constraints from identical parallel elements (keeping one copy for the contingency that removes one of them) and aggregate variables of co-located generators (p. 13).
- Learn a cheap linear ranking model from the benchmark's own training instances to decide evaluation order. Report its benefit as the fraction of total penalty captured by the top-k as a function of how many candidates have been evaluated (pp. 9–10, 15–16).

**Quotes**:
- "This makes the problem nearly separable, which is a property that needs to be exploited in a solution approach for it to be efficient." (p. 5)
- "This reformulation did not work well in our experiments, which we attribute to the negative curvature that such a penalty term introduces into the objective function." (p. 12)

### S062 · Regional complexity analysis of algorithms for nonconvex smooth optimization (Mathematical Programming 2021, DOI 10.1007/s10107-020-01492-3; read: arXiv:1802.01062 v2)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–28 (§§1–7, all theorems and proofs, Tables 1–2, Figures 1–2) read in full. References (pp. 28–30) skimmed. Read version: arXiv v2 / COR@L TR 18T-003-R1 (last revised Aug 28, 2018, p. 2).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel P. Robinson (Johns Hopkins AMS) · **Place in trajectory**: follows the Curtis–Robinson–Samadi TRACE and inexact regularized Newton complexity work and the concise trust-region complexity analyses (refs [13], [15], [16], p. 29). It is the paper the SKILL.md quotes for the "anomalous objectives" warning sign.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a measure, not an algorithm. Contemporary nonconvex complexity bounds count iterations until approximate p-th-order stationarity. Under loose assumptions they are "forced to take into account exceptional objectives", and the tightness examples of Cartis–Gould–Toint use objectives "not representative of those encountered in regular practice" (p. 3). Two alternatives are dismissed: average-case analysis has no accepted definition for nonconvex objectives, and per-class analysis "limits itself to certain classes" (p. 3). The proposal is regional complexity (RC) analysis in two steps. Step 1 analyzes an algorithm over regions defined by generic derivative properties. Step 2 combines regions to obtain bounds for any function class whose search space the regions cover (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a theory seems to mismatch practice, first ask whether the measure (here: iterations to ε-stationarity over a broad class) forces the worst case onto atypical instances. Then propose a measure that separates algorithm analysis from instance class (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Regions are defined by inequalities like gradient domination and Łojasiewicz-type conditions, but pointwise and relative to a reference value f_ref. R1 = {x : ‖g(x)‖^τ ≥ κ(f(x) − f_ref) ≥ 0 for some τ ∈ [1,2]}, split into R1² (τ = 2) and R1¹ (Definition 2.1, p. 7). R2 is defined by negative-curvature domination, (λ(H(x)))₋^τ ≥ κ(f(x) − f_ref), τ ∈ [1,3], split into three subregions (Definition 3.1, p. 12). Rp is the higher-order generalization through the reduction Δp(x) of a regularized p-th-order Taylor term (Definition 6.1, pp. 23–24). The exponent ranges are justified by what goes wrong outside them (pp. 7, 12). Six demonstration algorithms are analyzed: static and adaptive regularized gradient (RG, RG-A), two trust-region radius rules (TR-G with δ = ‖g‖/ν, and TR-H, which switches to (λ)₋/ν when curvature dominates), and static and adaptive cubic-regularized Newton (RN, RN-A) (pp. 5–6). "Step 1" is stated as an instruction to the analyst: prove that an accepted step decreases f by Ω(‖g(x)‖^r) (or Ω((λ)₋^r)) at a point in the region (pp. 8, 13). The two regional instructions are presented as reusable templates, not as properties of the six methods (p. 5).
  - Transferable: state the analysis as an instruction ("prove a per-step decrease of the form Ω(measure^r) at a point of the region") so other authors can plug in their algorithm (pp. 8, 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Unconstrained min f. f is C¹ and bounded below, with gradient bounded by M1 and L1-Lipschitz on an open convex set containing the level set (Assumption 1.1, p. 5). For second-order methods, the Hessian is L2-Lipschitz (Assumption 1.2, p. 5). Higher orders are covered by Assumption 6.1 (p. 23). Algorithms are monotone descent methods (p. 5). f_ref ∈ [f_inf, ∞) is a placeholder for the limiting value, which may exceed f_inf because of nonconvexity (p. 7). The authors call f_ref one of the method's disadvantages (p. 22). The function classes in §4 are (g,H)-dominated and gradient-dominated functions of various degrees (Definitions 4.1–4.2, p. 16), with examples: strict saddle functions (Example 4.1), PL functions (Example 4.2), and convex functions over a ball with κ = 1/R (Example 4.3) (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) One scalar recursion drives everything. Normalize a_k := (f_k − f_ref)/ω with a problem-dependent ω. The region inequality and the per-step decrease give a_k − a_{k+1} ≥ a_k^q or ≥ a_{k+1}^q, and the exponent q decides linear, superlinear or sublinear behaviour (Theorem 2.1 proof, p. 9; Theorem 2.2, pp. 9–11; Theorem 3.1, pp. 13–14; Theorems 6.1–6.2, pp. 25–27). (b) The threshold κ(f_k − f_ref) < 1 is forced in the lower subregions, and the case split on whether f_k − f_ref is above or below a constant like κ³/ζ⁴ yields different rates (Theorem 2.2, pp. 9–10). (c) An m-step version (f_k − f_{k+m}) absorbs rejected steps of adaptive methods, as long as m is independent of k (pp. 8–9, 11, 14). (d) The per-method decrease properties are borrowed from earlier work: Nesterov–Polyak eq. (4.10) and §5.2; Curtis–Lubberts–Robinson Lemmas 2.5–2.6; ARC Part I/II (Corollaries 2.1, 3.1, pp. 11, 14). (e) Complete bounds for function classes are obtained by "fitting together results for different regions as if fitting together the pieces of a puzzle" (p. 15). The loosest regional bound eventually dominates, giving e.g. O(log((f0 − f_inf)/(1/κ))) + O(1/(κ√ε)) for TR-H/RN/RN-A in case (b) of Theorem 4.2 (pp. 18–20). (f) Lemma 6.1 checks that the higher-order definition reduces to ‖g‖² and (λ)₋³ up to the factor p(p+1), using the closed-form minimizer of the regularized second-order term (p. 24).
  - Transferable: to turn a per-iteration decrease into a rate, normalize the optimality gap by the constant that makes the region inequality homogeneous, and read the rate off the exponent in a_k − a_{k+1} ≥ a_k^q. Use 1/a_k^{q−1} telescoping for the sublinear cases and log–log iteration for the superlinear ones (pp. 9–11, 17–20).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a. There are no numerical experiments (§§1–7 read). Figures 1–2 are illustrations of which points of toy functions (1-D piecewise cubic-quadratic; x² − y² + 10 and x³ − y³ + 22) fall in R1 or R2 (pp. 8, 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: Closest to Nesterov–Polyak's phase analysis of cubic regularization. The difference is "most significantly in the way that we decouple the analysis of the algorithm from consideration of a particular class of functions", and several methods are analyzed at once (pp. 3–4). It claims to be "more comprehensive" than piecemeal results for gradient descent under PL-like paths (Karimi et al.), "Convex Until Proven Guilty" (Carmon et al.) and strict-saddle neighbourhoods, with the hedge "(Not to say that our work subsumes all ideas from these other papers...)" (p. 4). It reframes rather than attacks Cartis–Gould–Toint: their tight examples are valid, but "one can argue" they are not representative (p. 3). It notes that worst-case optimality results favour static methods (fixed-step gradient descent, fixed-parameter cubic regularization) "despite the fact that adaptive algorithms often perform better in practice" (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions are four bullets (p. 4), one of them phrased as a user question: "What performance can I expect if I apply an algorithm for nonconvex optimization to solve a problem that happens to be convex?" (p. 4). The two-step procedure is named on p. 3 and recalled by name ("Step 1–R1", "Step 2") wherever it is used (pp. 8, 13, 15). Tables 1–2 summarize every regional rate in one grid (p. 15). Hedging is frequent and specific: footnote 4 says a superlinear phase "should not be overstated" (p. 9), and the conclusion admits that probabilistic versions of the per-step bounds "might be a nontrivial extension" (p. 28). §5 is a balanced advantages/disadvantages section (pp. 22–23).
  - Transferable: give a new framework a short procedural name (Step 1, Step 2), include one table that lists every result in a common format, and add a section that argues against your own framework's main weak point (here the reference value f_ref; p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical and methodological: an analysis framework, regional rates for six methods, complete bounds for (g,H)-dominated and gradient-dominated classes (Theorems 4.1–4.4, pp. 16–21), and a higher-order extension (§6). Main finding: under region coverage, the trust-region method TR-H behaves much like cubic regularization, whereas the standard bound (5.1) gives TR-H the same ε⁻² as gradient descent (p. 22). The authors call that standard comparison "disappointing" (p. 22). Stated limits: f_ref may lie above f_inf (p. 22); results are for monotone methods on smooth unconstrained problems (p. 5). Future directions (p. 28): Hölder-continuous derivatives, nonmonotone methods, probabilistic and stochastic analysis (with an expected-decrease version f_k − E_k[f_{k+m}]), nonsmooth problems via proximal steps, constrained problems via projected gradient steps. Limit visible but not stated: the claim that RC results are "representative of what can be expected in practice" (p. 4) is not checked; no test measures what fraction of the iterates of practical problems fall in R1 ∪ R2.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): the paper is an audit of the complexity yardstick. It names what the standard measure rewards (worst-case anomalous objectives; static methods; pp. 3–4), decides that the gap lies partly in the measure (pp. 3, 22), and builds an alternative view in which hidden differences appear (TR-H versus TR-G versus RN; Theorems 4.2 and 4.4, pp. 18–21). This matches Method 4 step 6, "Some methods actually behave like their worst-case; others don't".
- Method 4 / Heuristic 1 (variant ⚠): the audit is theoretical only. Region membership is never measured on test problems, so the practical relevance of the new yardstick is asserted (p. 4), not shown.
- new-pattern candidate: **Analyze over regions, then plug in classes** (decouple algorithm analysis from function-class assumptions; combine regional results "as if fitting together the pieces of a puzzle"; p. 15) (pp. 3, 15–21).
- new-pattern candidate: **Let the analysis framework motivate adaptive steps** (TR-H's curvature-dependent radius; "RC analysis can be used to guide the design of new algorithms"; pp. 4, 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define a region by a pointwise inequality (measure)^τ ≥ κ(f − f_ref) with τ in a justified range, and split it by exponent into subregions. This lets one analysis give linear, sublinear and superlinear phases at once (pp. 7, 12, 24).
- When comparing methods theoretically, state all results as one-iteration ratios Δf_{k+1}/Δf_k in a common table, so the comparison does not depend on which function class is chosen (Tables 1–2, p. 15).
- Check that a proposed generalization reduces to known special cases by a short lemma (Lemma 6.1: Δ1 = ‖g‖², Δ2 = (λ)₋³ up to the scaling p(p+1); p. 24).

**Quotes**:
- "Such questions are not answered well by contemporary worst-case results, which are too conservative." (p. 4)
- "RC analysis can be used to guide the design of new algorithms." (p. 4)

### S041 · A fully stochastic second-order trust region method (Optimization Methods and Software 2022, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2020.1852403; read: arXiv:1911.06920 v1)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–25 (§§1–7, all lemmas and theorems with proofs, three experiment subsections with figure captions) read in full. References (pp. 25–27) skimmed. Read version: arXiv v1 / COR@L TR 19T-025 (Nov 2019).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Rui Shi (Lehigh ISE, student) · **Place in trajectory**: a second-order extension of first-order TRish (Curtis, Scheinberg, Shi, INFORMS J. Optim. 2019; ref [13], p. 26). It sits in Curtis's stochastic line next to Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal (ref [4]), the self-correcting variable-metric method (ref [11]) and negative-curvature exploitation (ref [12]) (p. 26).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The target is min E_ξ[F(x, ξ)] and finite sums (p. 4). The entry point is a weakness of SG: the step's variance is proportional to the stochastic gradient's variance, so "SG can take a large step even though the norm of the true gradient may be relatively small" (p. 2). First-order TRish counters this with step normalization (p. 2). The question here is whether stochastic Hessian information can be added while keeping guarantees in the "fully stochastic regime", i.e. unbiased gradients with bounded variance and Hessian estimates only bounded in norm (abstract, p. 2). The motivation is to inherit Newton-trust-region benefits: scale invariance, problem-independent step sizes near a solution, handling of nonconvexity, fast local rate. The authors concede "These properties cannot fully be attained in the stochastic regime" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Keep TRish's radius rule (3) unchanged: Δ_k = γ1α‖g_k‖ for small ‖g_k‖, α in the middle band, γ2α‖g_k‖ for large ‖g_k‖. The rule makes the step length a continuous function of ‖g_k‖ and avoids the expected-ascent example of plain normalization (p. 5). Change only the subproblem: minimize the quadratic model with a stochastic Hessian H_k, solved to at least Cauchy decrease, using Hessian-vector products (Algorithm TRish, p. 5). There is no acceptance test and no ratio test: x_{k+1} = x_k + s_k always (p. 5). If H_k = 0 the method reduces to first-order TRish (p. 5). On the analysis side, H_k need only be uniformly bounded, and "most of our analysis merely requires that {Hk} is uniformly bounded in norm" (p. 4). One theorem needs ‖H_k‖ ≤ η/(2γ1), which may require scaling H_k and so biasing it. The authors defend this by analogy with deterministic practice (p. 9).
  - Transferable: to extend a first-order stochastic method to second order, keep the step-length control that carries the guarantees, make the second-order term enter only through a bounded matrix in a Cauchy-decrease subproblem, and check that setting it to zero recovers the original method (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 4.1: f is C², bounded below, ∇f L_g-Lipschitz (p. 6). Assumption 4.2: unbiased g_k (p. 6). Seven further assumption sets match different theorems. 4.3: bounded variance M_g and ‖H_k‖ ≤ M_H, constant α ≤ γ2/(4γ1²(L_g + M_H)) (p. 12). 4.4: variance ≤ M_g α_k with α_k = a/(b+k), a strong coupling of noise to step size (p. 13). 4.5: bounded second moment, which the authors call "strong" because it implies smaller variance where ‖∇f‖ is large (p. 14). 4.6: PL condition (p. 15). 4.7: γ_{2,k} = γ1(1 − ηα_k/2) (p. 16). 4.8: geometrically decreasing variance (p. 18). 5.1: Hessian Lipschitz; E_k‖∇f − g_k‖ ≤ µ1ε/L_H and E_k‖∇²f − H_k‖ ≤ µ2√ε; subproblem solved globally; G_low ≤ ‖g_k‖ ≤ G_high (p. 19). The authors admit stricter step-size requirements than first-order TRish (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) A one-step bound that holds for any (g_k, H_k): Taylor's theorem plus error terms (∇f − g_k)ᵀs and ½(L_g + ‖H_k‖)‖s‖² (Lemma 4.1, p. 6). (b) Two Cauchy-decrease lemmas, the standard one and a variant for small ‖H_k‖ (Lemmas 4.2–4.3, p. 7). (c) The proof splits into three cases by where ‖g_k‖ falls relative to 1/γ1 and 1/γ2, matching the radius rule (p. 6). In each case the cross term is absorbed by completing a square, 0 ≤ (½‖g_k‖ − ‖∇f − g_k‖)², and the identity E_k‖∇f − g_k‖² = −‖∇f‖² + E_k‖g_k‖² (eq. (5)) converts ‖g_k‖ terms to ‖∇f‖ terms (Lemma 4.4, pp. 7–9; Lemma 4.5, pp. 9–11). (d) Telescoping gives expected average squared gradient bounds (Theorem 4.1, pp. 12–13). Robbins–Siegmund/supermartingale convergence gives liminf ‖∇f‖ = 0 almost surely (Theorem 4.2, pp. 13–14). A two-index-set contradiction (m_i, n_i) gives lim ‖∇f‖ = 0 almost surely (Theorem 4.3, p. 15). The authors state that their proof "corrects an oversight" in Wang et al.'s proof: the negation should assume failure only with positive probability (p. 14). (e) Induction proves O(1/k) for PL (Theorems 4.5–4.6, pp. 16–18) and a geometric rate under geometric variance decay (Theorem 4.7, pp. 18–19). (f) Complexity (Theorem 5.1, pp. 19–21): from the global optimality conditions (31), split on the trust-region multiplier. If υ_k ≤ √ε, then E‖∇f(x_{k+1})‖ = O(ε). If υ_k > √ε, the step is on the boundary and the conditional expected decrease is ≥ ε^{3/2}/(3L_H²). This gives O(ε^{−3/2}) "in expectation" in the nearly deterministic regime, and the authors call the result weaker than deterministic versions (p. 19).
  - Transferable: for a method whose step rule is piecewise in ‖g_k‖, do the descent lemma case by case, absorb the noise cross-term by completing a square, and convert E‖g_k‖² to ‖∇f‖² + variance with the unbiasedness identity (pp. 6–11).
  - Transferable: to get ε^{−3/2} for a trust-region step, split on the trust-region multiplier (small multiplier: gradient at the next point is small; large multiplier: step on the boundary with decrease ≥ υΔ²/2) (pp. 20–21).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Python/PyTorch. Steihaug-CG solves the subproblems with a cap of 3 CG iterations, using the same mini-batch for g_k and H_k (p. 22). Cost accounting: one Hessian-vector product is counted as one stochastic gradient, so SG and first-order TRish get more iterations per epoch (p. 22). Tuning: G is computed from an SG run with α = 0.1, and grids are α = 10^λ, γ1 = 2^a/G, γ2 = 1/(2^b G). The intervals are chosen so that "the best stepsize for SG was never at the extreme of the allowed range". The total number of settings is equalized across algorithms; for example, SG gets 4×3×3 = 36 step sizes if TRish has 36 combinations (p. 22). Hyperparameters are selected on a held-out validation split, then the method is rerun on all training data (p. 22). Problems: FashionMNIST CNN, 5 epochs, batch 128, 5 runs (pp. 22–23); CIFAR-10 CNN, 5 epochs, 5 runs (pp. 23–24); NSW2016 electricity-demand LSTM, 20 epochs, batch 100, 50 runs, log-scale losses (p. 24). Plots show mean ± one standard deviation (p. 23). Results: on FashionMNIST the first-order methods lead early and TRish overtakes in final training loss and test accuracy (p. 23). On CIFAR-10 TRish does not have the best training loss (first-order TRish does) but ends with better test accuracy (pp. 23–24). On NSW2016 TRish reaches low losses earlier while final quality is comparable (p. 24). The FashionMNIST images are described as "color image[s]" (p. 22); FashionMNIST is grayscale (factual slip in the text).
  - Transferable: equalize tuning effort by giving every method the same number of hyperparameter configurations, and widen the baseline's grid until its best value is interior (p. 22).
  - Transferable: count a Hessian-vector product as one gradient evaluation and plot against epochs, so the second-order method pays for its curvature information (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: It continues first-order TRish ([13]). The literature review sorts stochastic second-order methods into four classes, following Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal: stochastic Newton, stochastic quasi-Newton, natural gradient, diagonal scaling (pp. 3–4). The authors argue that diagonal-scaling methods (RMSprop, Adagrad, Adam) "should be viewed in this light" as emulating second-order behaviour (p. 4). They separate TRish from the stochastic trust-region methods of Chen–Menickelly–Scheinberg and Cartis–Scheinberg, which need "stronger requirements on the stochastic gradient and Hessian estimates" (p. 4). They correct an oversight in Wang–Ma–Goldfarb–Liu's proof (p. 14). The fully-stochastic guarantees are framed as "on par with those proved for the first-order algorithm", and "in turn comparable to those possessed by SG" (p. 24).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Claims are hedged and calibrated. The results are "non-trivial to obtain" though they need stricter step sizes, admitted with "Admittedly" (p. 3). Assumptions are labelled "strong" when they are (p. 14). The complexity section opens by saying the result is "not representing the behavior of the algorithm in the fully stochastic regime" (p. 19). The experiment goal is stated before the setup: better final solutions and more stability (p. 21). A negative result is printed (CIFAR training loss) with "While one cannot guarantee that such would be the behavior in general" (p. 24). The conclusion attributes the gains to "carefully chosen trust region radii and stochastic second-order information" without an ablation that separates the two (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm plus a theory that covers two regimes: fully stochastic convergence (Theorems 4.1–4.7) and nearly deterministic complexity (Theorem 5.1). The experiments are a demonstration. Stated limits: stricter step-size conditions (p. 3); Newton-TR benefits not fully attainable in the stochastic regime (p. 3); the complexity result is weaker than deterministic results (p. 19); scaling H_k may bias it (p. 9). Limits visible but not stated: three small networks and 5 epochs on the image tasks; the CG cap of 3 is fixed and not studied (p. 22); no comparison with Adam or other adaptive methods, although they are discussed (p. 4); the lower bound G_low on ‖g_k‖ in Assumption 5.1 (p. 19) fails near stationary points. No future directions are given (p. 25).

### S051 · Limited-memory BFGS with displacement aggregation (Mathematical Programming 2022, DOI 10.1007/s10107-021-01621-6; read: arXiv:1903.03471 v3)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–25 (§§1–5, the full existence proof of Theorem 3.2, implementation §3.4, all experiments) and Tables 1–2 (pp. 23, 28) read in full. Figure pages 29–30 contain captions only (plots not in the text layer). References (pp. 25–27) skimmed. Read version: arXiv v3 / COR@L TR 19T-001-R2 (last revised Aug 27, 2020).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Albert S. Berahas, Baoyu Zhou (both Lehigh ISE at the time) · **Place in trajectory**: a quasi-Newton paper next to Curtis's self-correcting variable-metric (stochastic, nonsmooth) work (refs [10], [13], p. 25) and the BFGS nonsmooth line with Que (refs [11], [12]). The SKILL.md already cites its delete-instead-of-aggregate arm as Method 5 evidence.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is a theory gap. L-BFGS gets only a local linear rate, "no better than the rate offered by a steepest descent method", whereas full BFGS is superlinear (p. 7). In addition, the number of pairs to keep is unknown a priori, and the adaptive L-BFGS of Boggs–Byrd has "not been coupled with any strengthened theoretical guarantees" (p. 3). The problem is posed as an ambitious question (an adaptive limited-memory scheme with full-memory guarantees) followed by the weaker question actually answered: a limited-memory-type scheme that reproduces full BFGS exactly while storing at most n pairs (p. 3). Figure 1 motivates it with Rosenbrock: L-BFGS(2) differs substantially from BFGS even when the last two steps span R², and information from the first pair "lingers" beyond n iterations (pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable: pose the full question you want answered, then state the weaker question you can answer as "a significant first step" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: before designing a fix, measure the folklore it relies on. Here a side computation shows that "over-writing" of old curvature by BFGS is not absolute even when the steps span the space (Figure 1, pp. 8–9).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: If a stored iterate displacement s_0 lies in the span of later ones, modify the later gradient displacements (aggregation) so that the pair (s_0, y_0) can be dropped with the BFGS matrix unchanged: BFGS(W, S_{0:m}, Y_{0:m}) = BFGS(W, S_{1:m}, Ỹ_{1:m}) (eq. (13), p. 10). The special case: if s_j is parallel to s_{j+1}, the later update completely over-writes the earlier one "regardless of the gradient displacement vectors" (Theorem 3.1, pp. 7–8). The ansatz Ỹ = W⁻¹S[A;0] + y_0[b;0]ᵀ + Y, with zero blocks motivated by Theorem 3.1 (eq. (14), p. 10), turns the matching of compact forms into three key equations (16) (Algorithm 4, p. 12). What is kept: the compact and two-loop machinery, and s_iᵀỹ_i = s_iᵀy_i > 0 (eq. (17), p. 11). What changes: the stored y's are no longer raw gradient differences, and the stored pairs need not be consecutive (Remark 3.1, p. 10; p. 17). Implementation has three cases: add the pair (independent); replace the last pair (parallel); aggregate (in span). The case is detected by attempting a rank-one Cholesky downdate of the inner-product matrix of the s's, whose breakdown yields τ (pp. 17–18). Cost is O(m²n) + O(m⁴), comparable to L-BFGS's O(4mn) for 3 ≤ m ≤ 10 (p. 20). Practical adaptive variant: project an old s onto the span of later ones and aggregate if ‖s − ŝ‖ ≤ tol·‖ŝ‖, with tol = 10⁻⁸ and a looser 10⁻⁴ for the oldest pair "to promote aggregation of the oldest pair"; otherwise drop the oldest pair as in L-BFGS (p. 22).
  - Transferable: when a limited-memory approximation discards information, look for an invariance (here: span dependence of iterate displacements) under which the information can be folded into the remaining storage exactly, then relax the invariance with a tolerance for practice (pp. 10, 22).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is C¹ for the setting (p. 4). For the superlinear rate (Theorem 2.1, from Nocedal–Wright Thms 6.5–6.6): f is C², the line search satisfies Armijo–Wolfe with unit initial step, the iterates converge to x* with a positive definite and Lipschitz Hessian (p. 7). Aggregation needs W ≻ 0, positive curvature sᵀy > 0, and linearly independent S_{1:m} (Theorem 3.2, p. 11). The authors admit that exact equivalence may require storing up to n pairs, "in which case the storage requirements and computational costs of our strategy might exceed those of a full-memory BFGS method" (p. 3). The practical variant aggregates a projected ŝ, not the actual s, so its equivalence to BFGS holds only approximately (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Theorem 3.1 by direct algebra: U_jU_{j+1} = U_{j+1} and V_jU_{j+1} = 0 when s_j = τs_{j+1} (pp. 7–8). (b) Matching the compact representations (7) term by term to obtain the key equations (pp. 10, 12). (c) Counting unknowns versus equations: (m+1)(m−1) unknowns in (A, b), and the same number of effective equations (19a)–(19c), so the system of linear and quadratic equations is square (pp. 11–12). (d) Change of variables a_j = Q⁻¹[a_{j,1}; a_{j,2}] with Q = SᵀW⁻¹S ≻ 0. This decouples the affine equations, which fix a_{j,1}, from the quadratic ones (pp. 12–13). (e) Rewriting the quadratic system as a Gram-matrix identity φ_i(a_{i,2})ᵀφ_j(a_{j,2}) = z_iᵀz_j with ZᵀZ = ωωᵀ + ΩᵀQ⁻¹Ω (eqs. (25)–(27), p. 13). (f) Reverse induction over j = m−1, ..., 1. At each step a particular solution satisfies the affine equations with norm ≤ the target, shown via a projection whose eigenvalues are in {0,1}. A nonzero null-space direction exists by a dimension count, and a strongly convex scalar quadratic in λ then attains the target norm exactly (pp. 13–17). (g) The superlinear rate is not reproved; it is inherited from BFGS because the matrices are identical (Theorem 3.3, p. 20).
  - Transferable: to prove that a square system of affine and quadratic equations has a real solution, pick a particular solution that satisfies the affine part with "norm ≤ target", then move along a null-space direction of the affine part. The resulting scalar strongly convex quadratic must cross the target value (pp. 13–17).
  - Transferable: when your scheme reproduces an existing one exactly, inherit its convergence theory by equality of iterates instead of reproving it (p. 20).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: MATLAB. (i) Accuracy of a single aggregation: (n, m) ∈ {4, ..., 128}², m ≤ n, 100 random datasets each. Quadratics come from sprandsym with condition ≈ 10⁴, and a mock optimization with noisy steepest-descent directions and exact line search builds s_0 in the span by construction. - **D6 Positioning**: It continues Nocedal's L-BFGS and the compact representations of Byrd–Nocedal–Schnabel (pp. 3, 6), with Dennis–Moré theory for superlinear rates (p. 6). The closest prior idea is Kolda–O'Leary–Nazareth's merging of pairs. Boggs–Byrd's adaptive L-BFGS is credited with empirical gains but no theory (p. 3). The authors reconnect to the classical fact that BFGS with exact line search recovers a quadratic's Hessian in n steps (the CG equivalence), and explain why running an auxiliary BFGS to compress W is impractical (pp. 9–10). The novelty claim is phrased "To the best of our knowledge" (pp. 2, 4, 24).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The novelty sentence is in the abstract and repeated in §1.1 and §5 (pp. 2, 4, 24). Expectations are lowered early: the scheme "might not always offer practical advantages" (p. 3). The motivating question is written out as an explicit question ("We are motivated by the following question", p. 3). - **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical and algorithmic: the first limited-memory-type quasi-Newton scheme with a local superlinear guarantee, as the authors frame it (pp. 2, 4, 20), plus a practical adaptive L-BFGS variant with empirical gains (pp. 21–24). Stated limits: exactness may need n pairs and cost more than full BFGS (p. 3); numerical errors in aggregation (p. 24); the practical variant has no full-memory guarantee (p. 25).
