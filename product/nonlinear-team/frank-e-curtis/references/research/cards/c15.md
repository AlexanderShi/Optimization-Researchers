### S052 · FaRSA for ℓ1-regularized convex optimization: local convergence and numerical experience (Optimization Methods and Software 2018, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2017.1415336)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–21; reference list p. 21 skimmed). Published journal version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Tianyi Chen, Daniel P. Robinson (corresponding) · **Position in trajectory**: follow-up to the SIAM J. Optim. FaRSA paper [6] (global convergence + Matlab); this one adds the local rate and the C solver (pp. 2–3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: min f(x)+λ‖x‖₁ with f convex C² (p. 2). Entry is the gap left by the companion paper: [6] gave global convergence and "preliminary numerical results" only, so this paper proves a local superlinear rate and presents a public C implementation with "extensive numerical comparisons" (pp. 2–3). Motivation for second order: first-order methods (ISTA, FISTA, SpaRSA) are "somewhat limited" on ill-conditioned problems (p. 3).
  - Transferable: split a new algorithm across two papers — (i) method + global theory + prototype, (ii) local rate + production code + large benchmark — and state the split explicitly in the second introduction (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: active-set/reduced-space method that, at each iterate, compares two optimality measures, β (zero variables) and φ (nonzero variables), and takes either a reduced-space Newton-CG step on the nonzeros (φ-step) or a reduced ISTA-type step that frees zero variables (β-step) (pp. 5–8, Algorithm 1 p. 6). The only change to the 2016 algorithm is an extra subproblem accuracy condition (4), ‖H_k d̄_k + g_k‖ ≤ min{μ_k‖g_k‖^r, ‖g_k‖²}, added because it allows local superlinear rates to be proved (p. 6); global convergence is untouched because the condition only demands a more accurate solve (p. 8). A non-standard projected backtracking line search first tries the largest step that stays in the current orthant (pp. 7–8).
  - Transferable: when you need a faster local rate, add one inexact-Newton forcing condition to the inner-solve test and argue that the global theory survives because the new condition only tightens what was already accepted (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Assumption 1.1 — f convex, C², bounded below, ∇f Lipschitz on the level set, Hessian uniformly positive definite and bounded (so strongly convex, unique x*) (p. 4); Assumption 1.2 — Hessian Lipschitz near x* (p. 4); Assumption 1.3 — strict complementarity |∇_i f(x*)| < λ on zero variables, shown equivalent to strictly positive multipliers in the (u,v) bound-constrained reformulation (p. 4). The conclusion admits strong convexity is needed and conjectures it can be removed with a distance-proportional regularization à la Yue et al. (p. 20).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (a) separation constants δ₁ (half the smallest nonzero |x*_i|) and δ₂ (half the strict-complementarity gap) define a ball in which signs agree (Lemma 3.1, p. 9) and β(x_k)=0 so only φ-steps occur (Lemma 3.2, pp. 9–10); (b) activities eventually freeze because each "ADD" iteration only zeroes components (Lemma 3.3, p. 10); (c) finite active-set identification by contradiction with strict complementarity (Lemma 3.5, pp. 10–11); (d) unit-step acceptance via the mean-value theorem + Hessian Lipschitz + eigenvalue interlacing for the reduced Hessian (Lemma 3.6, pp. 12–13); (e) reduction to classical inexact Newton theory of Dembo–Eisenstat–Steihaug on the nonzero subspace (Lemma 3.7, Theorem 3.8, p. 13; quadratic with r = 2).
  - Transferable: for a local-rate proof of an active-set method, chain "identify signs in a ball → freeze the active set → unit step accepted → it is inexact Newton on the free subspace, cite Dembo–Eisenstat–Steihaug" (pp. 9–13).
- **D5 Experiments**: ℓ1-regularized logistic regression, λ = 1/N (p. 14). C library on GitHub; default parameters from "a rough tuning procedure" (p. 14). Practical CG termination tests (16a)–(16d): stop if many entries flip orthant, if the CG iterate exceeds a trust-region-like bound from the last φ-step, if the residual decreases quadratically, or by a relaxed residual test when far from a solution (pp. 14–15). Hessian-vector products via data-matrix column subsets (pp. 15–16). 30 LIBSVM binary datasets: multi-class sets excluded, seven sets removed because they caused out-of-memory errors for FaRSA (most also for the other codes), and only the largest a9a / w8a kept (p. 16). Baselines LIBLINEAR (newGLMNET) and ASA-CG on the bound-constrained reformulation, both at default parameters with native termination at 10⁻⁶ (p. 16). Time = average CPU over 10 trials, 12-hour cap (pp. 16–17). Result: FaRSA fastest on 19 sets, second on 11; LIBLINEAR fastest on 10; LIBLINEAR and ASA-CG hit the time cap on avazu-app.tr and url_combined (pp. 17–18); Table 2 shows LIBLINEAR clearly ahead on, e.g., gisette (2.84 s vs 23.78 s) (p. 18). Table 3 logs β and φ over the final 5 iterations to check the theory: β becomes 0 on 22/30 sets and φ then decreases superlinearly on 18/22; the three linear-looking cases rerun at 10⁻¹² show superlinear behaviour (pp. 18–20).
  - Transferable: after a local-rate theorem, tabulate the theorem's two predicted signatures (the inactive measure hitting exactly 0, then superlinear decay of the active measure) over the last few iterations of every run, and rerun at a tighter tolerance where the signature is not visible (pp. 18–20).
- **D6 Positioning**: classifies second-order ℓ1 methods into proximal-Newton (LIBLINEAR/newGLMNET, coordinate-descent inner solver) vs orthant-based (OBA, CG inner solver) vs bound-constrained reformulation (ASA-CG), and presents FaRSA's inexactness conditions as accommodating both CG and CD inner solvers (p. 3). Credits Yue et al. and stabilized SQP (Gill–Kungurtsev–Robinson) for the idea that could remove strong convexity (p. 20).
- **D7 Writing**: contributions as a numbered "key features" list followed by "The key contributions of this paper are as follows. First, … Second, …" (p. 3); the algorithm is restated in full from [6] with the one changed line flagged (p. 5); every practical CG test is justified in a sentence linked to a lemma (p. 15). Hedging is light; the memory disadvantage is stated twice (pp. 16, 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic theory (local rate) + software (C, public) + benchmark. Limits are specific: larger memory because of reduced-Hessian storage (pp. 16, 20), strong convexity required (p. 20), termination conditions differ across solvers so final objective values differ on some sets (p. 17). Future: remove strong convexity (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): the subproblem conditions (2)–(4) are derived from what global convergence and the local rate need, and the CG termination tests (16a)–(16d) translate them into practical stopping rules (pp. 6, 8, 14–15).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): external solvers run at defaults with native termination, CPU averaged over 10 trials, and the incumbent's wins visible in Table 2 (pp. 16–18); but FaRSA's own parameters come from an unreported "rough tuning procedure" (p. 14), and dataset exclusion was triggered by FaRSA's own out-of-memory failures, although each dropped set is named with its reason (p. 16).
- Method 4 (evidence ✅, weak): notes that the codes' distinct termination conditions yield different final objective values and reports F-final next to time (p. 17).
- New-pattern candidate: "Check the asymptotic theorem in the run logs" (Table 3, pp. 18–20).
- New-pattern candidate: "Two-paper split: global theory + prototype, then local rate + production code" (pp. 2–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Add a forcing-sequence residual test (exponent r ∈ (1,2]) to an existing inexact subproblem test to upgrade a globally convergent method to superlinear/quadratic local convergence without redoing the global proof (pp. 6, 8, 13).
- Use eigenvalue interlacing to transfer global Hessian bounds to reduced Hessians in unit-step-acceptance proofs (p. 12).
- Design CG early-exit tests that monitor the outer method's structure (number of orthant flips, size relative to the last accepted step) in addition to residual size (pp. 14–15).
- Report a final-iterations table of the optimality measures to confirm predicted local behaviour (pp. 18–20).

**Quotes**:
- "thus indicating that the termination condition plays an important role for these two datasets." (p. 17)
- "We suspect that similar ideas could be incorporated into the FaRSA framework and would allow us to prove a superlinear convergence result without having to make a strong convexity assumption." (p. 20)

### S071 · Fast market clearing algorithms (Energy Markets and Responsive Grids: Modeling, Control, and Optimization, IMA Vol. 2018, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4939-7822-9_7)
**Read level**: full, TR version (MERL TR2017-196, December 2017, marked "IMA Workshop Volume"), pp. 1–24; reference list pp. 22–24 skimmed. Page references are TR file pages.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Arvind U. Raghunathan (MERL), Yusuke Takaguchi, Hiroyuki Hashimoto (Mitsubishi Electric) · **Position in trajectory**: application of the Curtis–Raghunathan semismooth Newton method for nearly separable QPs (COAP 2017, ref. [5]) to multi-period electricity-market clearing; extends their 2016 PES single-period paper [24] (pp. 5–6).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: decentralized clearing of a multi-period pool market with GenCos, DSOs and an ISO under a DC power-flow model (p. 5). Entry: market-design research has done little on "algorithmic and computational aspects" (p. 4); participants are price-takers who will not share cost functions, so the ISO can only send prices and receive optimal responses, and the usual subgradient method needs many communication rounds (p. 5).
  - Transferable: frame an application paper around an operational constraint the incumbent algorithm handles badly (here: privacy-forced price-only communication × slow subgradient convergence) (p. 5).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: dualize only the ISO's coupling constraints so GenCo/DSO problems separate (pp. 10–11); pose the ISO's clearing problem as an implicit complementarity problem 0 ≤ ν ⊥ F(ν) ≥ 0 whose F is evaluated through the participants' optimal responses (p. 12); reformulate with the Fischer–Burmeister function and apply a line-search semismooth Newton method on the merit Ψ = ‖Φ_FB‖² (pp. 13–14, Algorithm 2 p. 15). Sensitivities ∂P*/∂λ come from central finite differences through the existing price-query protocol (2T extra calls per participant, parallelizable), so participants change nothing — this removes the 2016 paper's assumption that participants supply sensitivities (pp. 6, 15).
  - Transferable: when the inner solver is a black box you may not modify, get the Jacobian for the outer Newton method by finite-differencing the black box's responses through its existing interface (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions**: GenCo cost strictly convex (Assumption 1, p. 7) and DSO utility strictly concave (Assumption 2, p. 8), giving unique responses; DC power flow (convex, no duality gap), DSOs as single nodes, deferrable loads, price-taking participants (p. 5). Unit-commitment binaries are set aside (convex-hull relaxation suggested) (pp. 7–8). Local theory needs nonsingular generalized Jacobians at the solution (Theorem 2, p. 14); F is only PC¹, covered by the main result of [5] (p. 14).
- **D4 Proof devices**: Theorem 1 — equivalence of competitive equilibrium and ICP solutions via the KKT conditions of the convex ISO problem (p. 13); the Fischer–Burmeister characterization φ(a,b)=0 ⇔ complementarity (p. 13); local superlinear rate imported from Qi–Sun (Theorem 2) and from [5] for PC¹ functions (p. 14). No new analysis in the chapter.
- **D5 Experiments**: MATLAB; IEEE test networks case9–case300 (Table 1.1, p. 17); random strictly convex quadratic costs and concave quadratic utilities, demands 80–120 % of MATPOWER nominal, averages over 10 runs (pp. 17–18). Single period: the subgradient method hits its 100000-iteration limit on most instances while semismooth Newton takes 5–10 iterations and is 2–3 orders of magnitude faster in CPU; function-evaluation counts include the finite-difference sensitivity calls (p. 18). The subgradient step γ_k = a/(k+1) is stated, but the value of a used is not reported (pp. 12, 17–18). Ablation of globalization: the pure Newton method of Motto et al. (no line search) hits the 1000-iteration limit on all instances of case14, case57, case118 and 9/10 of case30 (p. 19). Multi-period T ∈ {2,…,32}: iterations independent of T (Table 1.5), evaluations linear in T (Table 1.6), CPU with the share spent on sensitivities (Table 1.7), and a projected parallel speed-up formula (Table 1.8) whose gains shrink as T grows because the dense m×m Newton solve (m³ flops) dominates (pp. 19–21).
  - Transferable: report the CPU share of each algorithm component and a projected parallel speed-up, then state the regime where the non-parallel part takes over (pp. 20–21).
- **D6 Positioning**: surveys the electricity-market equilibrium literature (Hobbs, Pang, Baldick, Motto, Lavaei) (p. 4); the closest competitor, Motto et al. [20], is criticized for dual decomposition of a nonconvex AC model without zero-duality-gap guarantees and for a pure Newton strategy without global convergence guarantees (p. 6); the authors restrict to the convex DC model, allow inequality constraints and argue the problem is then "better posed" (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing**: chapter style: long motivating introduction, explicit "Our Focus" subsection with lettered modelling assumptions (p. 5); economic caveats on welfare maximization (pp. 9–10), credited to referees (p. 22). The conclusion's "about 4 orders of magnitude fewer function evaluations" (pp. 21–22) is stronger than the 2–3 orders of magnitude CPU statement of §1.5.1 (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: application + algorithm transfer, no new theory. Limits stated concretely: the Newton matrix is dense with no exploitable structure (p. 17), speed-ups fade at large T (p. 20). Future: unit commitment via convex hulls, DSO networks with distributed generation, AC power flow / SDP relaxations (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): the pure-Newton comparison is an in-framework ablation of the line search (p. 19), but the main baseline is an untuned subgradient method capped at 100000 iterations with its step constant unreported (pp. 12, 18), and the two methods stop on "distinct but equivalent" error measures (p. 18).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the outer Newton method adapts to inner solvers it may not change (the participants' optimization problems) by extracting finite-difference sensitivities through the unchanged protocol (pp. 5, 15), rather than by setting the inner accuracy.
- New-pattern candidate: "Keep the deployed interface fixed; put the acceleration in the coordinator" (pp. 5, 15, 22).
- New-pattern candidate: "Port your own structured-optimization algorithm to an industrial application with an industrial coauthor" (pp. 3, 5–6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Decentralize a welfare problem by partial Lagrangian dualization of only the coordinator's constraints, then solve the dual as a complementarity system with a Fischer–Burmeister semismooth Newton method plus a merit line search (pp. 10–15).
- Present an ablation that removes the globalization (pure Newton) to show that the line search is what makes the method robust (p. 19).
- Tabulate iterations, evaluations and CPU against a scaling parameter (time periods) in separate tables to show which cost grows with it (pp. 19–21).

**Quotes**:
- "Table 1.3 clearly shows that employing the pure Newton strategy is not robust." (p. 19)
- "However as the number of time periods increases the time involved in the step computation (1.16) dominates the overall CPU time and as a consequence the speedups are not significant." (p. 20)

### H001 · An Inexact Regularized Newton Framework with a Worst-Case Iteration Complexity of O(ε^-3/2) for Nonconvex Optimization (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2019, DOI 10.1093/imanum/dry022; arXiv 1708.00475)
**Read level**: full, arXiv version (arXiv:1708.00475v4 in IMA layout): main text pp. 1–20 read in full; Appendices A–B (pp. 20–24) theorem statements read, proofs skimmed; Appendix C table (pp. 24–29) skimmed; references pp. 29–31 skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel P. Robinson, Mohammadreza Samadi · **Position in trajectory**: generalizes TRACE (Curtis, Robinson, Samadi, Math. Program. 2017) into a framework that contains both ARC and TRACE (pp. 2, 14–16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: smooth unconstrained nonconvex min f(x) (pp. 1–2). Entry: cubic regularization (ARC) attains the optimal O(ε^-3/2) bound vs O(ε^-2) for traditional trust region; TRACE and Birgin–Martínez showed other routes to the same bound (p. 2). The paper asks what conditions on a trial step suffice, rather than proposing one more method (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when several methods share an optimal bound, enter by abstracting the step conditions they all satisfy, then show each is a special case (pp. 2, 14–16).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: subproblem (2.1) in (s, λ) with bounds σ^L‖s‖ ≤ λ ≤ σ^U‖s‖ contains trust-region, quadratic- and cubic-regularization subproblems (p. 3); the trial pair only has to satisfy Assumption 2.1 — a Cauchy-type decrease (2.3a), a curvature/residual inequality (2.3b) and a residual bound (2.3c) — which inexact Krylov solves can meet (p. 4). Steps are accepted on ρ_k = (f_k − f(x_k+s_k))/‖s_k‖³ as in TRACE (p. 4); after a rejection the bounds on λ/‖s‖ are reset or raised (Algorithm 1, p. 5). New instance (hybrid, p. 16): when σ^L = 0, try plain CG Newton steps (λ = 0) and fall back to cubic subproblems over Krylov subspaces only if CG meets negative curvature or the conditions fail.
  - Transferable: define the accepted step by inequalities rather than by the subproblem it solves; any solver that meets them inherits the theory (pp. 4, 19).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f C², bounded below (Assumption 3.1, p. 5); gradient Lipschitz on an open convex set containing iterates and trial points, g_k ≠ 0, gradients bounded (Assumption 3.2, p. 5); Hessian Lipschitz along the path of iterates and trial steps for complexity (Assumption 3.4, p. 10); subspace curvature condition (3.13) for second-order results (Assumption 3.6, p. 13). The authors admit g_k ≠ 0 is imposed only to avoid obscuring the analysis (pp. 5–6) and that ρ_k is not scale invariant (p. 4).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (a) λ_k bounded by 2‖g_k‖/‖s_k‖ + const (Lemma 3.3, p. 6); (b) if only rejections occur, λ_k/‖s_k‖ → ∞ and this eventually forces acceptance (Lemmas 3.5–3.6, p. 7); (c) global convergence via the index sets R₁, R₂ of rejected-then-accepted iterations shown finite (Lemma 3.8, pp. 8–9) and a subsequence argument borrowed from TRACE Theorem 3.14 (Theorem 3.3, pp. 9–10); (d) complexity: λ ≥ (H_Lip+κ₂+2η)‖s‖ guarantees acceptance (Lemma 3.9, p. 10) ⇒ σ^U bounded (Lemma 3.10, p. 11) ⇒ accepted steps satisfy ‖s_k‖ ≥ c‖g_{k+1}‖^{1/2} (Lemma 3.11, p. 11) ⇒ the number of accepted steps is O(ε^-3/2) (Lemma 3.12, pp. 11–12), times at most 1 + log(σ_max/σ)/log γ₁ rejections between acceptances (Lemma 3.13, p. 12) (Theorem 3.5, p. 13); second-order O(max{ε₁^-3/2, ε₂^-3}) in the subspaces searched (Theorems 3.7–3.8, pp. 13–14); (e) special-case embedding arguments for ARC (ARp, p = 2) and TRACE (pp. 14–16); (f) appendices characterize primal–dual solutions of (2.1) and show subspace solutions satisfy (2.3) (Theorems A.2, B.3, pp. 21–23).
  - Transferable: the "accepted-step count × bounded rejections between acceptances" decomposition for complexity proofs of adaptive-regularization methods (pp. 11–13).
- **D5 Experiments**: MATLAB implementations of iR Newton (hybrid) and iARC (ARC with ideas from Birgin et al.), with GALAHAD's RQS on Lanczos subspaces for the cubic subproblems (pp. 16–17). Termination ‖g‖∞ ≤ 10⁻⁶ max{‖g₀‖∞, 1}, no second-order test, 4-hour limit (p. 17). Parameters (Table 1) were chosen as values that worked well on the test set for both algorithms (p. 17); no sophisticated σ updates, to avoid extra tuning (p. 17); iARC uses an inner stopping test (5.1) stricter than (2.3c) because it is the one proposed for cubic methods (p. 17). CUTEst: 171 unconstrained problems → 152 after named removals (termination at the initial point, evaluation/memory errors, both timed out, both failed) (p. 18); three problems where only iR Newton succeeded were left out of the profiles for a fairer comparison (p. 18). Performance profiles on iterations and Hessian-vector products (Fig. 1, p. 19); the main claimed gain is in Hv-products, explained by CG recovering H_k s_k as a linear combination of products it has already computed while cubic solves cannot (p. 19). Appendix C reports per-problem %Newton steps and tridiagonal factorizations (p. 24).
  - Transferable: when your gain comes from a cheaper inner step, profile the inner-work unit (Hessian-vector products) rather than iterations, and explain the mechanism that saves it (pp. 18–19).
- **D6 Positioning**: places the work after Nesterov–Polyak and Cartis–Gould–Toint ARC, TRACE and Birgin–Martínez (p. 2). Handles the concurrent Dussault / Dussault–Orban work by listing three differences (regularization required in every step; no second-order results; focus on parameter rules) and calling the papers complementary (pp. 2–3).
- **D7 Writing**: contribution stated by negation — what the framework is not (p. 2); main theorems are short, with proofs that follow "in a similar manner" to TRACE lemmas (pp. 12–13); numerical claims hedged to "an edge" in iterations and "more drastic" in Hv-products (pp. 18–19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory (general framework with optimal first-order complexity) + one new instance + a small Matlab study. Limits: second-order guarantees hold only in the subspaces the solver explores (p. 13); the implementation omits second-order checks (p. 17); the comparison is against one in-house cubic code (pp. 16–19). Future: other instances from other solvers of (2.1) (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): the step conditions (2.3) are what the acceptance mechanism needs, and the Krylov/CG inner iteration stops as soon as they hold (pp. 4, 16, 18).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅ / variant ⚠): the rival iARC lives in the same Matlab code with shared parameters, removed problems are named with reasons, and problems won only by iR Newton are dropped from the profiles in the baseline's favour (pp. 17–18); but the parameters were picked on the test set (p. 17) and iARC used a stricter inner test (p. 17).
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): the practical gain is attributed to inexact Newton steps and measured in Hv-products, not credited to the complexity bound (p. 19).
- New-pattern candidate: "Generalize by step conditions: a framework that contains the rival optimal methods as special cases" (pp. 2, 4, 14–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Treat the regularization value λ as a variable bounded by multiples of ‖s‖ to unify trust-region and regularization subproblems in one problem (p. 3).
- Prove that existing algorithms are instances of your framework by checking that their accepted steps satisfy your conditions and that their rejection updates are consistent with yours (pp. 14–16).
- Use a hybrid that tries the cheap step first (CG Newton, λ = 0) and switches to the expensive regularized subproblem only when a test fails (p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "Our proposed framework is intentionally very general; it is not a trust region method, a quadratic regularization method, or a cubic regularization method." (p. 2)
- "Overall, we claim that the primary strength of iR Newton as compared to iARC is its ability to employ inexact Newton steps." (p. 19)

### S014 · Exploiting negative curvature in deterministic and stochastic optimization (Mathematical Programming 2019, DOI 10.1007/s10107-018-1335-8; arXiv 1703.00412)
**Read level**: full, arXiv version (arXiv:1703.00412v3, 4 Apr 2018), pp. 1–24 (references pp. 22–24 skimmed; figure axis data not interpretable in the text layer).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel P. Robinson · **Position in trajectory**: Curtis's bridge from deterministic second-order methods to stochastic/ML settings, reusing the Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal SG analysis (pp. 2, 19).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: whether following directions of negative curvature can benefit an optimization algorithm (p. 1). Entry: convergence theory for descent + negative-curvature methods exists, but there is no evidence of consistent practical gains; three reasons are listed why few methods target second-order stationarity — cost, first-order methods rarely get stuck at saddles, and lack of evidence (p. 2); in stochastic settings such methods are barely studied because benefits were not even shown deterministically (p. 2).
  - Transferable: enter a crowded theory area by asking the empirical question the theory has left open (does the theoretically motivated step actually help?) (pp. 1–2, 4).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: base decisions on upper-bounding models of f — quadratic (L) for the descent step, cubic (σ) for the negative-curvature step — so steps can, in theory, use fixed step sizes and transfer to stochastic settings where line searches are not viable (pp. 2, 8). The two-step method alternates a negative-curvature step and a descent step with fixed sizes (Algorithm 1, p. 5); the dynamic method picks whichever model predicts larger decrease and adapts L_k, σ_k, testing only the chosen step (Algorithm 2, p. 8; remarks p. 10). Stochastic two-step: SG step plus "curvature noise" ω_k d_k with ω_k uniform on [−1,1], so the curvature direction needs no descent sign (pp. 17–18, Algorithm 3). Stochastic dynamic: CG on a sub-sampled Hessian, stopping at the first nonpositive-curvature direction and using it as d_k (pp. 20–21, Algorithm 4).
  - Transferable: when moving a deterministic method to the stochastic setting, choose a globalization (fixed step from a model upper bound) that survives without exact function values (pp. 2, 20–21).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f C², bounded below; g and H Lipschitz on the path of iterates (constants L, σ) (pp. 4–5). Direction conditions (2a)–(2c), (3), (9) with constants independent of k (pp. 5, 7). No non-degenerate-saddle assumption (pp. 4, 12). Stochastic: moment conditions (26a)–(26c) on s_k and (27a)–(27b) on d_k; no expected-descent condition on d_k because it is not verifiable without the exact gradient (pp. 17–18). Second-order guarantees are not claimed in the stochastic case (p. 17).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (a) one-iteration decrease f(x_{k+1}) ≤ f(x_k) − c₁|λ_k|³ − c₂‖g(x̂_k)‖² from the cubic and quadratic upper bounds, then summability Σ|(λ_k)₋|³ < ∞ and Σ‖g‖² < ∞ (Theorem 1, pp. 6–7); (b) the dynamic method's decrease ≥ the max of the two model reductions (16) (pp. 9–10); (c) complexity by summing decreases over the index sets G(ε_g), H(ε_H): O(max{ε_g^-2, ε_H^-3}), plus a bound on inner-loop iterations that gives the same bound for function evaluations under (17) (Theorem 3, pp. 10–11); (d) stochastic: an SG-type expected-decrease inequality (Lemma 2, p. 18) fed into the arguments of Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal Theorems 4.8/4.9 for fixed and diminishing steps (Theorem 4, pp. 19–20).
  - Transferable: prove the stochastic variant by reducing it to the standard SG expected-decrease lemma so the textbook analysis applies unchanged (pp. 18–20).
- **D5 Experiments**: Matlab; CUTEst unconstrained problems with n ≤ 500 and explicit second derivatives, 97 problems (p. 13). Ablation within one code: Algorithm 2(s_k) forces the descent step every iteration vs Algorithm 2(s_k,d_k) (p. 13). Lipschitz-estimate update rule (19) given in full (pp. 13–14). Second-order stopping test (20), iteration limit 10000 (p. 14). **Method links**:
- Method 5 (evidence ✅): rivals are built in the same framework with one component switched off — descent-only Algorithm 2(s_k), and SG = NC with β_k = 0, same seeds and start (pp. 13, 22).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): the stochastic comparison is a single run, and the initial Lipschitz estimates were chosen as the smallest stable values for both methods (p. 22) — consistent with the 2019 single-run lapse noted in Method 5's say–do line.
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): the premise is that convergence theory for negative-curvature methods has not been matched by practical evidence, and the methods are judged by final objective, iterations and evaluations rather than by the bound (pp. 2, 4, 14).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the inner CG solve is used for what the outer method needs — its first nonpositive-curvature direction becomes d_k at essentially no extra cost — rather than its accuracy being set by a merit model (pp. 20–21).
- New-pattern candidate: "Pick a globalization that survives losing exact function values (model upper bounds, fixed steps)" (pp. 2, 8, 17–21).
- New-pattern candidate: "Per-problem pairwise difference plots restricted to problems where the feature fired" (pp. 14–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Choose between two candidate steps by comparing their model-predicted reductions, and test sufficient decrease only for the chosen step to save a function evaluation (pp. 8, 10).
- Update a Lipschitz estimate to the value that makes model decrease match actual decrease, clipped to [ρL_k, 10³L_k] (pp. 13–14).
- Add a randomly signed, zero-mean curvature step to SG so that the SG analysis carries over unchanged (pp. 17–20).
- Terminate CG at the first nonpositive-curvature direction and reuse it as the negative-curvature step (pp. 20–21).
