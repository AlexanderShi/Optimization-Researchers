### S053 · A subspace acceleration method for minimization involving a group sparsity-inducing regularizer (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2022, 10.1137/21M1411111; read: arXiv:2007.14951v1)
**Read level**: full, arXiv/TR version (pp. 1–28: cover, §§1–6, Algorithms 1–4, every lemma and theorem with proof in §4, Appendix A proofs of §2, the parameter table (Figure 2), Table 1, captions of Figs. 1 and 3, references). The text layer breaks displayed formulas, so the inequality chains in §4 were followed through the lemma statements and the proof prose rather than symbol by symbol. The Fig. 3 bars exist only as axis ticks; the counts are taken from the caption and text.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Yutong Dai, Daniel P. Robinson (all Lehigh ISE, p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the group-ℓ2 extension of FaRSA for ℓ1-regularized convex problems (Chen, Curtis, Robinson 2017 and 2018, refs [5], [6], pp. 3, 27); written as COR@L TR 20T-015, July 2020 (pp. 1–2). The practical step-size rule is taken from Curtis and Robinson's negative-curvature paper (ref [9], p. 22).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The model problem is min f(x) + Σ λ_i‖[x]_{G_i}‖₂ with nonoverlapping groups, which recovers ℓ1 when every group is a singleton (eq. (1), p. 2). The application motive is that ℓ1 fails when covariates come in groups: in multi-factor ANOVA, deleting a factor means deleting a group of dummy variables, which "the ℓ1-norm regularizer fails to achieve" (Yuan and Lin, p. 2). The algorithmic gap comes from a survey along three axes (p. 3). First-order methods (ISTA, FISTA, gradient projection) are "often inferior in practice" to space-decomposition and second-order methods. Coordinate descent and gglasso decompose the space "in a prescribed manner, rather than in an adaptive way". Proximal Newton methods "appear to lack good worst-case guarantees in terms of identification of the optimal solution support". Public second-order solvers target specific losses and regularizers.
  - Transferable: lay out the competitors on explicit axes (adaptive or prescribed decomposition; second-order information or not; a support-identification guarantee or not) and enter at the empty cell, saying for each rival which axis it misses (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: FaRSA-Group (Algorithm 1, p. 8). The proximal-gradient (PG) step s_k is computed every iteration, but it is used "primarily" to predict the zero/nonzero structure and to define optimality measures (p. 5). Candidate groups for Newton work are those nonzero at x_k, still nonzero after the PG step, and far from zero relative to their gradient (eq. (8), p. 5). Groups closer to zero than κ₂‖∇(f+r)‖^p are removed (eq. (9), pp. 5–6), which leaves I^cg and its complement I^pg. The measures χ^cg and χ^pg are the norms of the PG step restricted to each set (eq. (10), p. 6). If χ^pg ≤ χ^cg, a reduced-space Newton-CG direction is used (Line 8, pp. 6, 8); otherwise a PG line search runs on I^pg (Algorithm 4, p. 10). The CG direction must satisfy three conditions (pp. 6, 8): (13) descent at least that of a Cauchy-like reference direction; (14) a model value no worse than the zero step; (15) a residual ≤ μ_k‖g_k‖^q. Condition (15) is needed only for the local rate and "is irrelevant with respect to the complexity result" (p. 6). Algorithm 3 is the projected search along the Newton-CG direction (p. 9; Fig. 1, p. 7). Once the ray for group G_i enters a ball of radius ρ̄_{k,i} = min{ρ_{k,i}, sin(θ)‖[x_k]_{G_i}‖}, that group is set to zero. A step that creates a new zero group needs only no increase in the objective (Lines 44–50, p. 9). What changed from ℓ1 FaRSA: orthant geometry is replaced by these group balls. What stayed: the choice between two subspaces by comparing measures, and CG conditions "exactly the same as those used in [5]" (p. 12).
  - Transferable: use a cheap first-order step as the oracle that predicts the active set and supplies the optimality measure, and spend second-order work only on the predicted free subspace; choose the subspace each iteration by comparing the two components of that measure (Line 8, p. 6).
  - Transferable: keep every group used in the Newton step a computable distance from the nondifferentiable point (ρ_{k,i}, eq. (11), p. 6), so the regularizer's gradient is Lipschitz along the search path with constant L + λ_max/ρ_{k,min} (Lemma 4.5(ii), p. 12). The nonsmooth term then behaves like a smooth one inside the line search.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1.1 (p. 4) takes f convex and continuously differentiable, with bounded gradient and Lipschitz ∇f on the level set, and f + r bounded below. The introduction instead says f is "twice continuously differentiable" (p. 2), a mismatch in the preprint. The groups do not overlap (p. 2). The H_k are bounded and uniformly positive definite (Assumption 4.2, p. 10). The local analysis adds (p. 18): f twice continuously differentiable and strongly convex, a Lipschitz Hessian near x*, and the nondegeneracy ‖[∇f(x*)]_{G_i}‖ < λ_i for groups outside the support (Assumption 4.3), which the authors call "a strengthening" of the optimality conditions. It also fixes algorithmic choices: η < 1/2, I_k equal to the full sets, and exact reduced Hessians (Assumption 4.4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Iterations are split into four types (K^pg_→, K^pg_↓, K^cg_0, K^cg_sd, p. 10), and each type gets its own decrease lemma. Lemma 4.3 bounds α_k below and bounds the number of decreases of α_k by a log term (pp. 11–12). Lemma 4.5 derives the local Lipschitz bound from the nonexpansiveness of projection onto a ball (eq. (24), p. 13). Lemma 4.6 proves a decrease of min{c₁(χ^cg)^{1+p}, c₂(χ^cg)^{2+p}} (pp. 13–16) in two cases. Either the step is cut by the ball-hitting length τ_k, which the distance-from-zero rule bounds below by a multiple of ‖∇F‖^{p−1}, or by the Armijo condition (pp. 14–15). Theorem 4.1 sums the decreases over the "big" and "small" sets split at χ = c₁/c₂ (eq. (34), p. 16). New-zero iterations are counted combinatorially, "at most n_G" between any two other iterations, because each one zeroes a further group (p. 17). The result is O(ε^{-(1+p)}) for ε ≥ c₁/c₂ and O(ε^{-(2+p)}) otherwise (pp. 16–17); the authors note this is worse than PG's ε^{-2} (p. 17). The local analysis is a chain of identification lemmas. Lemma 4.8: the PG step zeroes groups outside the support (p. 18). Lemma 4.9: the computed sets settle on the optimal partition (pp. 18–19). Lemma 4.10: the support of x_k settles too (pp. 19–20). Lemma 4.11: all late iterations are Newton-CG sufficient-descent iterations (p. 20). Inexact-Newton theory (Dembo, Eisenstat and Steihaug, ref [11]) then gives superlinear, or for q = 2 quadratic, convergence (Theorem 4.3, p. 20). Lemma 2.4, that the PG step is bounded by the group gradient, is proved in Appendix A by case analysis and Cauchy–Schwarz (pp. 25–26).
  - Transferable: make a safeguard threshold a power p of the optimality measure (eq. (9)), so that step-length lower bounds become powers of the measure and the complexity exponent shows its dependence on p openly. Then say how to shrink the cost by taking p small (p. 17).
  - Transferable: count iterations that make no guaranteed decrease but change the structure (here, new zero groups) with a finite combinatorial budget between decrease iterations, rather than forcing a decrease bound on them (p. 17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: A Python implementation, "available upon request" (p. 21), with parameters as in Figure 2 (p. 21). Several practical deviations are disclosed (pp. 21–22). For N < n, ϕ starts at 0.8 and switches to 1, because singular Hessians gave long CG directions. κ₂ is scaled per group. H_k is the Hessian regularized with δ = 10⁻⁸. CG stops on a residual test, a trust-region-like cap ‖d‖ ≥ 10³ min{1, ‖∇F‖}, or |I_k| iterations (eqs. (46a)–(46c)). The PG parameter follows a local-Lipschitz estimate (47) that "is not covered by our analysis", with a sketch of how to cover it (p. 22). Data: LIBSVM binary classification sets, minus data too large for gglasso's memory, because gglasso does not take sparse input; only a9a and w8a from their families; 25 data sets (p. 22). Four group structures × two λ levels give 200 instances (pp. 22–23). The baseline is gglasso with "All default settings" and the same x₀ (p. 22). Under a 1000 s limit, FaRSA-Group failed on 2 instances and gglasso on 7 (p. 23). The CPU-time comparison is a bar chart of −log₂ time ratios (Morales style, ref [24]). It covers only the 109 instances on which at least one code took ≥ 1 s, and FaRSA-Group is faster on 93 of them (pp. 23–24). Final objective, with a 10⁻⁸ tolerance: 95 vs 7 wins on the 109 and 153 vs 35 on all 200. Sparsity, judged by zero-group dominance: 30 vs 7 on the 109 and 33 vs 8 on all 200 (p. 24). Not reported: iteration counts and per-data-set numbers. Also not discussed: that a Python code is timed against an R package (the gglasso footnote points to CRAN, p. 22).
  - Transferable: fix win criteria for each axis in advance (time; objective within 10⁻⁸; sparsity by zero-group dominance) and report the counts both on the nontrivial subset and on the whole set, so a reader can see what the filter did (p. 24).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues the authors' own FaRSA line ([5], [6], p. 3). The orthant-based method of Keskar et al. [18] is said to be "often outperformed by a closely related method called FaRSA" (p. 3), a self-citation. Rivals are criticized by design deficits (prescribed decomposition, exact coordinate minimization, no support-identification guarantee), not by numbers (p. 3). gglasso is called "well-cited" and "state-of-the-art" (p. 22).
  - Transferable: when your own earlier method beat a rival, cite that result as the reason for building on it, and make the new criticisms structural (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract names three ingredients ("subspace acceleration, domain decomposition, and support identification") and three theoretical results, and labels the experiments "Preliminary" (p. 2). The paper is candid that its complexity bound is worse, but it covers that with an unreferenced general claim about "well-designed second-derivative methods" (p. 17). The conclusion claims the method beat a state-of-the-art code on robustness, time, objective and sparsity, with no limitations paragraph (p. 24). Preprint slips: the lead-in to Lemma 4.6 names Algorithm 4 where Algorithm 3 is meant (p. 13), and "In s[17]" (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions are algorithmic and theoretical (worst-case complexity and superlinear local rate), with a numerical study; the software is not released (p. 21). Limits stated: the worse complexity exponent (p. 17), and the practical α update falling outside the analysis (p. 22). Limits not stated: f convex, groups nonoverlapping, logistic loss only, a single external baseline. Future directions: none stated (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (Audit the yardstick) ⚠ variant: the paper puts its own worse-than-PG complexity next to its better practical speed and lets the experiments, not the bound, carry the claim (p. 17; results p. 24). This is the mirror of Taste warning 3: worse complexity sold as no obstacle to better practice. The rhetoric leans on an unsourced general claim rather than auditing the measure.
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ⚠ variant: the CG termination conditions are derived from what the analysis needs. (13)–(14) serve the complexity proof and (15) only the local rate (pp. 6, 8). The implementation adds stopping rules (46b)–(46c) aimed at the line search's cost (p. 21). The setting is unconstrained composite rather than SQP, so there is no merit parameter.
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ⚠ variant: the rival runs with default settings and the same starting point (p. 22), and the test set is cut to what the rival can hold in memory (p. 22). The paper has no in-framework ablation (no PG-only or no-subspace arm) and no handicap disclosure. The CPU-time profile keeps only instances where some code took ≥ 1 s (p. 23), a symmetric outcome-based filter (Heuristic 7), with full-set counts reported for objective and sparsity (p. 24). CPU time is compared across a Python code and an R package without comment (pp. 21–22), against Heuristic 6.
- new-pattern candidate: first-order step as active-set oracle. The PG step predicts the support and defines the optimality measure, and second-order work runs only on the predicted free subspace, chosen by comparing the measure's two components (pp. 5–6, 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Gate Newton work on groups that are a computable distance from zero, so the nonsmooth term is locally Lipschitz-smooth along the step, and cap the step where a group's ray enters the ball (Lemma 4.5, pp. 12–13; Algorithm 3, p. 9).
- Prove complexity by iteration type: a decrease lemma per type plus a combinatorial count for structure-changing steps (pp. 10–17).
- When the practical parameter update differs from the analysed one, say so and sketch the modification that would bring it under the theory (p. 22).
- Report outcome counts on both the filtered subset and the full set (p. 24).

**Quotes**:
- "However, as is typical with well-designed second-derivative methods, although the complexity bound is worse, it typically performs better (see Section 5)." (p. 17)
- "Although this PG parameter update strategy worked better than the basic strategy in Algorithm 1 (see Line 19 and Line 21), it is not covered by our analysis in Section 4." (p. 22)

### S065 · Gradient sampling methods with inexact subproblem solutions and gradient aggregation (INFORMS Journal on Optimization 2022, 10.1287/IJOO.2022.0073; read: arXiv:2005.07822v2)
**Read level**: full, arXiv/TR version (pp. 1–27: cover, §§1–5, Algorithms 1–3, every lemma and theorem with proof, Tables 1–5, references, and the online companion A.1–A.5 with Algorithms 4–8). The file's title reads "Inexact Subproblem Solves" (p. 2), where the list has "Solutions".
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Minhan Li (Lehigh ISE, p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: continues Curtis's adaptive gradient sampling (with Que, 2013 and 2015) and the self-correcting variable-metric framework (with Robinson and Zhou, 2020) (refs [10]–[12], pp. 3, 22). It is implemented in his C++ package NonOpt (ref [8], p. 17). Original publication 15 May 2020, revised 10 Aug 2021; NSF grants CCF–1618717 and CCF–1740796 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The QP subproblem is "The most computationally expensive component" of a GS method (abstract, p. 2). Every earlier GS analysis requires "that the convex QP subproblems for computing search directions must be solved exactly in every iteration" (p. 3). A second entry is borrowed from bundle methods: subgradient aggregation shrinks later QPs (p. 3). The nearest rival, Maleknia and Shamsi [31], replaces some QPs by an "ideal" direction but still needs exact QPs near stationarity (p. 3).
  - Transferable: find the exactness requirement that every convergence proof in a line shares, and make removing it the paper (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The QP solver must produce primal-dual feasible iterates that converge (Assumption 2.1, p. 6); a dual active-set solver yields primal iterates for free through d = −WGy (eq. (7), pp. 5–6). The inner loop, Algorithm 2 (p. 8), stops in one of two ways. Either the aggregated gradient is small (eq. (10)), and the sampling radius is cut. Or a descent test (11) holds together with one of two tests: a duality-gap test (12) relative to −q, or a dual-progress test (13) with λ set by (9). Both tests imply ‖Gy‖_W ≤ (1+σ_k)‖Gy*‖_W "without knowledge of y_{k,*}" (eq. (18), pp. 8–10). The new geometric Lemma 2.2 generalizes the classical GS acute-angle lemma from the exact W-projection to points within a factor (1+ς) plus δ of it (p. 7). The inexactness parameter σ_k is cut when the line search fails to give a large step (Algorithm 1, lines 13–16, p. 7). Gradient aggregation (Algorithm 3, p. 16): after a null step (α_k = 0), the previous gradients are replaced by the single vector G_k y_k. Lemma 3.1 lifts any dual-feasible point of the aggregated QP to one of the full QP (p. 16). Aggregation "is only possible" with inexact solves (p. 15). Unchanged: the line search, iterate perturbation, sample-set update and BFGS updates from [11, 12], which are placed in the online companion (pp. 6, 23–27).
  - Transferable: state the inexact-subproblem stopping test in computable primal-dual quantities (duality gap, dual progress) that imply a bound relative to the unknown optimum (eq. (18)); the outer proof then needs only that bound (pp. 8–10).
  - Transferable: when an aggregated object replaces its pieces, prove a feasibility-lifting lemma (Lemma 3.1) so that every earlier lemma applies through the lifted dual vector (pp. 16–17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is bounded below, locally Lipschitz, and C¹ on an open set D of full measure (Assumption 1.1, p. 4). The authors separate what each part buys: differentiability on a full-measure set gives well-posedness, continuous differentiability gives the convergence guarantee, and boundedness below rules out f → −∞ (p. 4). Stationarity is Clarke stationarity (p. 4). H_k and its inverse W_k are both maintained (p. 5). The implementation assumes x_k + α_k d_k ∈ D, so the perturbation loop always stops at once (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Lemmas 2.3–2.4 are weak-duality chains showing that each stopping test gives eq. (18) (pp. 9–10). Lemma 2.5 establishes well-posedness with probability one (pp. 10–11). It also states the self-correcting BFGS property: eigenvalue bounds (24) hold on a fraction χ of the iterations, and the proof later restricts to those (pp. 11, 13). Lemma 2.7 gives a decrease bound in ‖x_{k+1} − x_k‖ (p. 12). Lemma 2.8 uses Carathéodory to find a nonempty open set of good sample sets (pp. 12–13). Theorem 2.1 argues by contradiction in two cases: the step d_k is zero infinitely often, or the radius stays fixed (pp. 13–15). A fixed radius forces a Cauchy sequence and α_k → 0. Lemma 2.2 and Lebourg's mean value theorem then show that the iteration would need its sample sets to miss an open set infinitely often, a probability-zero event because the sampling is independent of the index subsequence (pp. 14–15). Theorem 3.1 reuses the whole argument through the lifted dual vector (pp. 16–17). No rate or complexity is proved.
  - Transferable: in randomized-sampling proofs, reduce failure to the sample sets avoiding a fixed open set infinitely often along a subsequence chosen independently of the samples; that event has probability zero (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The C++ code NonOpt, with the parameters of Table 1 "used consistently across all of our experiments" (pp. 17–18). The stopping tests are checked only after (p_k+1)/4 QP iterations and then every fourth iteration, "To reduce CPU time" (pp. 17–18). Three arms differ only in the QP rule: GS-exact (KKT error 10⁻¹⁰), GS-inexact and GS-inexact-agg (p. 17). The controlled test is 15 convex max-type problems with n = 1000 and m = 500, five each for m_A ∈ {125, 250, 375}, with known minimizer x* = 0 and f* = 0 (p. 18). Each problem is run 10 times and the runs averaged, with an extra stop at f < 10⁻³ (p. 18). The effort measure is total QP iterations, called a "rough proxy", with CPU time rejected as irreproducible (p. 19). Results: GS-inexact changes QP iterations by −89.5 % to +1.4 %, one instance worse (Table 3, p. 19); GS-inexact-agg by −4.1 % to −96.9 % (Table 4, p. 20). The drop in outer iterations comes with a warning that it "might not represent behavior that one should anticipate in general" (pp. 19–20). The external test uses 20 problems "for which LMBM has been tuned", n = 1000, L-BFGS memory 50 against LMBM's 7 (p. 20). GS gets a CPU limit equal to the larger of LMBM's 10-run average time and 1 s, and its own 10 runs are averaged (p. 20). Only final objective values are compared, since iteration counts across codes "is not necessarily informative" (p. 21). Table 5 is mixed: Brown Function 2 gives 2.1e-09 for LMBM against 9.0e-01 for GS, and Test29 2 gives 0.98 for LMBM against 0.72 for GS (p. 21).
  - Transferable: build controlled test problems with a known minimizer and a tunable structural parameter (here m_A, the V-space dimension), and use convex ones so differences cannot come from reaching different local minima (p. 18).
  - Transferable: against an external code with different stopping logic, cap your time at the rival's average time, average over runs, and compare only the final objective (pp. 20–21).
- **D6 Positioning**: Sets itself apart from [31] in "two main respects": generic inexactness conditions rather than a prescribed direction, and inexactness allowed even near stationarity (p. 3). It borrows aggregation from bundle methods (Kiwiel [21]) while noting that the GS analysis is "inherently probabilistic" (p. 3). Extensions such as local rates and constrained GS are declared out of scope, but the enhancements "could be employed in conjunction with" them (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract runs two parallel sentences, one for inexactness and one for aggregation, each "proved … without the loss of theoretical convergence guarantees" and each followed by "Numerical experiments show" (p. 2). Only the new components are written out, with the inherited ones placed in an online companion (pp. 5, 23). Unexpected results are hedged (pp. 19–20). The conclusion has four sentences and no limitations or future work (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: algorithms, convergence theory and an implementation in NonOpt. No limits are written as such. From the text: no rate or complexity result; a convex synthetic test set; a proxy effort measure (p. 19); one GS-inexact instance worse than exact (+1.39 %, p. 19); LMBM ahead on several problems (p. 21). Future directions: none stated (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ✅ evidence: the QP stopping tests (12)–(13) are built to give exactly the relation (18) that the new Lemma 2.2 and the GS convergence proof need (pp. 7–10). The cost of checking them is limited by checking periodically (pp. 17–18), which matches Heuristic 2's attention to safeguard cost.
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✅ evidence: GS-exact is the same code with only the QP tolerance changed (p. 17), and the "aggregation" arm isolates one component (Table 4, p. 20). The external rival's test set is one it is tuned for, the comparison is time-matched and averaged over 10 runs, and the rival's wins are printed (pp. 20–21). This matches the Method 5 steps already in the SKILL.
- Method 4 (Audit the yardstick) ⚠ variant: the paper justifies its measure (QP iterations, a "rough proxy"), gives the reason for rejecting CPU time (p. 19), and declines to compare counts across codes with different stopping rules (p. 21). This is measure hygiene, not a critique of a community yardstick. Heuristic 6 ✅ evidence (p. 19).
- Heuristic 10 ✅ evidence: the GS skeleton is kept and only the subproblem solve is replaced, with the inherited pieces moved to the companion (pp. 6, 23).
- new-pattern candidate: known-answer controlled test problems, convex instances with x* = 0 and a dial for structure, to isolate an algorithmic change (p. 18).
- new-pattern candidate: inexactness as an enabler. Once inexact solves are allowed, a compression (aggregation) becomes admissible that exact solves would rule out (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use a primal-dual QP solver so that each inner iterate gives both a duality gap and a primal-feasible direction for free (eq. (7), pp. 5–6), and stop on gap- or progress-relative tests (pp. 8–10).
- Generalize the key geometric lemma from "exact projection" to "within a factor of the projection" (Lemma 2.2, p. 7); that one change carries the inexactness through the old proof.
- Check costly stopping tests only periodically, and say so (pp. 17–18).

**Quotes**:
- "(By employing convex, as opposed to nonconvex test problems, we can be sure that the results of our experiments are not skewed by two algorithms converging to different local minimizers and other related circumstances.)" (p. 18)
- "That said, we found it to be the best measure for comparison, as opposed to CPU time which can vary despite the algorithm being run with the same initial conditions, random number generator seeds, and so on." (p. 19)

### S094 · Hybrid Interior-Point/Active-Set SCOPF Algorithms Exploiting Power System Characteristics (Lehigh University final scientific/technical report to ARPA-E 2022, 10.2172/2323339)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–7: cover and rights-notice boilerplate pp. 1–2, public executive summary, acknowledgements, accomplishments by task, project activities, outputs, follow-on funding).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: project team Daniel Molzahn (Georgia Tech), Andreas Waechter (Northwestern), Ermin Wei (Northwestern), Elizabeth Wong (UCSD). Curtis is principal investigator (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: final report of ARPA-E award DE-AR0001073, December 2018 – July 2022, dated 29 Oct 2022 (p. 1). It funded team GO-SNIP's entries in Challenges 1 and 2 of the ARPA-E Grid Optimization (GO) Competition and the Operations Research submission "A Decomposition Algorithm for Large-Scale Security-Constrained AC Optimal Power Flow" (p. 7). This is a grant report, not a research paper; the methods are only summarized and deferred to the papers (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Application-driven. Grid operators solve OPF problems with AC power-flow equations and contingency sets, under limits of "five to ten minutes" for many real-time uses (p. 3). Industry has used linearizations, and moving to nonlinear models is said to save "$6 billion to $9 billion" a year in the US (p. 3; no source given in the report). The concrete problem, scoring and hardware are set by the GO Competition organizers (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Ipopt inside an iterative decomposition (p. 4). Preprocessing handles degeneracies and drops redundant contingencies. The base-case OPF is solved first. Contingencies are prioritized with a fast approximate evaluation followed by a full one as needed. A master problem holds only the contingencies selected so far, steps run in parallel, and dominated contingencies are not selected (pp. 4, 7). The report states plainly where the plan changed. Active-set identification ran into degeneracies, "such as flow constraints on parallel lines", and the team turned to improving the interior-point method (p. 5). For Challenge 2 the SQP solver was not pursued and crossover from IP to SQP was "not fully" developed (p. 6). An active-set treatment of the base-case/contingency complementarities beat approximations (p. 5). Inexact solution methods did not pay (p. 5). Transmission switching gave "minimal benefit" for its cost, so unit-commitment heuristics (relaxation and rounding) were prioritized (p. 6). The code "failed gracefully" by "continuously updating the output with the best-known solution" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: under a hard wall-clock limit, keep a feasible best-known output on disk at all times and overwrite it as it improves, so a timeout or crash returns the best result so far rather than nothing (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: n/a (no theorems). The mathematical models "were those provided by the GO Competition organizers" (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a (no analysis in the report; details are deferred to the submitted papers, p. 7).

### S016 · An inexact column-and-constraint generation method to solve two-stage robust optimization problems (Operations Research Letters 2023, 10.1016/J.ORL.2022.12.002; read: arXiv:2207.03291v2)
**Read level**: full, arXiv version (pp. 1–27: §§1–5, Algorithms 1–2, Propositions 1–2, Corollary 3 and Theorem 4 with proofs, Appendices A–G including Algorithms 3–4, Propositions 5–8 with proofs, references). **Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Man Yiu Tsang, Karmel S. Shehadeh (corresponding author), all Lehigh ISE (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Curtis is third author on a robust-optimization paper led by a Lehigh colleague's group; the text does not say what his part was. It carries the inexact-subproblem theme from NLP into a decomposition method for two-stage robust optimization.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In column-and-constraint generation (C&CG) the master problem is often a large MIP. Solving it to optimality is hard, and later masters are "potentially even more challenging" because scenarios accumulate (p. 4). Most work targets the subproblem; discussion of the master is "sparse" (p. 2). The one prior adaptive-tolerance scheme [13] (Tönissen et al.) improved times but gave no guarantees (p. 2). The obstacle is put precisely: a master solved to a relative gap may not give a valid lower bound (p. 4).
  - Transferable: find a practitioners' heuristic that works without a guarantee, locate the exact certificate it breaks (here the lower bound), and repair that point only (pp. 2, 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: i-C&CG (Algorithm 2, p. 6). Each master is solved to a relative gap ε^j_MP under an extra constraint cᵀx + δ ≥ L (4c), recording a lower bound L^j and an upper bound U^j (p. 5). The index ℓ marks the last iteration whose lower bound is certified (p. 5). The algorithm then sets L ← U^j, an optimistic and possibly invalid bound, which Remark 2 defends as "computationally effective" when early masters are hard (p. 5). Termination uses only the certified gap (U − L^ℓ)/U. The backtracking routine (step 3, p. 6) branches on the inexact gap (U − U^j)/U. If it is below ε̃, an exploitation step returns to iteration ℓ, resets L ← L^ℓ and multiplies ε_MP by α. Otherwise an exploration step adds the new scenario (pp. 5–6). With ε_MP = 0 the method reduces to C&CG plus constraint (4c) (Remark 3, pp. 5–6). Two variants add an exploitation frequency f_exploit, and a master time limit τ raised by β at each exploitation (§4.3, pp. 9–10; Appendix D, pp. 16–18).
  - Transferable: let an optimistic, uncertified bound drive progress, keep a pointer to the last certified bound, let only the certified bound enter the stopping test, and backtrack to it with tighter tolerances when progress stalls (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Ξ is finite or a polytope. Recourse is relatively complete. An optimal solution with finite value exists. The objective is at least K > 0, so the relative gap is defined (p. 3). The subproblem is solved by an optimality oracle that returns an extreme point of Ξ (p. 3). Two DRO models (mean-support and mean-absolute-deviation ambiguity sets) reduce to the same form under a Slater-type condition (Appendix A, p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Proposition 1, validity of L^ℓ, rests on Proposition 5: if L exceeds the optimal value, the master's value equals L (pp. 15–16). Proposition 2 bounds the certified gap at an exploitation step by (1 − ε̃)⁻¹ Π_{k=ℓ}^{j} (1 − ε^k_MP)⁻¹ − 1, through a telescoping chain U^{k+1} ≤ U^k/(1 − ε^{k+1}_MP) (eq. (8), pp. 7–8). Theorem 4 proves finite termination when ε̃ < ε/(1 + ε) in two steps (pp. 8–9). First, every exploration step adds a new scenario, since a repeated scenario would give U − U^j ≤ 0, and there are finitely many extreme points. Second, endless exploitation on a fixed scenario set is impossible, because ε_MP → 0 drives the gap bound to ε̃/(1 − ε̃) < ε. Remark 5 turns the theorem's condition into parameter advice (p. 9). No convergence result is given for the two variants (not found in the text), although the experiments use the time-limit variant (p. 11).
  - Transferable: when a tolerance enters a relative-gap certificate multiplicatively, bound the gap as a product over the iterations since the last certified point, and choose the switching threshold so that the limit of the bound sits below the target (pp. 7–9).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The main test is a distributionally robust operating-room scheduling problem from [14], whose master is a hard MILP (pp. 10–11). There are 240 instances (p. 11). Durations come from real surgery data (SINTEF): bounds at the (20th, 80th) or (10th, 90th) percentiles, |I| ∈ {20,…,25}, |R| ∈ {7, 10}, two cost weightings, five instances each. C&CG's final gaps exceed 80 % on at least 70 % of instances (p. 12). Appendix F gives the second cost weighting (pp. 21–22), with the remark that on easier instances the two methods perform similarly (p. 21). Appendix G adds a robust capacitated p-center problem with 15 instances: final gaps ≤ 8.8 % against 18.9–73.4 % (pp. 22–25). The variants are illustrated on single instances (Figs.
