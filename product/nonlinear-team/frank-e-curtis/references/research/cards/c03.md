### S096 · Inexact sequential quadratic programming methods for large-scale nonlinear optimization (Northwestern University PhD dissertation 2007, no DOI / arXiv)
**Read level**: full — pp. 1–103 read line by line (abstract, acknowledgments, Chapters 1–7); bibliography pp. 104–107 skimmed; appendix tables A–C (pp. 108–115) read row by row to check the prose claims against the raw counts.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: none (single-author dissertation; advisor Jorge Nocedal; committee Mehrotra, Fourer, Waltz; acknowledgments single out Richard Byrd, Eldad Haber, Nick Gould, p. 5) · **Position in trajectory**: the starting point. Chapters 3–4 carry the inexact SQP framework of the SIAM J. Optim. 2008 paper (SKILL Signature Work Anatomy, 10.1137/060674004); Chapter 6 is the flexible penalty function; Chapter 5 is an exact-solve precursor of the nonconvex inexact Newton work. The dissertation itself does not say which chapters are joint papers (not stated anywhere in pp. 1–103), so the chapter-to-paper mapping is inferred from titles and content.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a scale wall in one application class: PDE-constrained problems whose dimension grows with grid fineness, where factorizing the primal-dual matrix is impractical (p. 11; §3.1, p. 22). The gap is stated as a contrast between fields: inexact methods are well studied and implemented for unconstrained optimization and nonlinear equations, but not for constrained optimization, because of the paired goals of reducing the objective and satisfying the constraints (p. 12). The question is then posed as an accuracy question: how accurately must the SQP subproblem be solved to keep global convergence (p. 23). A second, separate entry (Ch. 6) is a critique of the two incumbent globalization tools: a penalty function blocks steps that temporarily increase infeasibility; a filter blocks feasibility-improving steps through historical entries and treats the problem too much like a multiobjective one (pp. 77–80). All work is framed for equality constraints only, for ease of presentation (abstract p. 3; p. 14), with inequalities deferred to a sketch (Ch. 7, pp. 99–103).
  - Transferable: state the gap as: technique X is mature in neighbouring setting A, absent in B, and one specific coupling (objective vs. feasibility) blocks the transfer (p. 12), then reduce it to one quantitative question: how inexact may the inner solve be? (p. 23).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Do not control the inner Krylov solve by a forcing sequence on the whole KKT residual (inexact Newton, Dembo–Eisenstat–Steihaug); instead accept an inexact primal-dual step when it reduces a local model of the exact ℓ2-penalty function, with primal and dual residual components handled separately (p. 23). Concretely: a model reduction condition (3.3) (p. 26), a bounded total residual (3.4) (p. 27), and two termination tests: Test I (model reduction for the current penalty value plus a loose dual-residual bound (3.5), pp. 27–28) and Test II (linearized-feasibility progress (3.6a) plus dual residual bounded by β‖c‖ (3.6b), after which the penalty parameter is raised by rule (3.7), pp. 28–29); Algorithm 3.1 (p. 29). Unchanged: line-search SQP on the exact penalty function, Armijo backtracking, the KNITRO-style penalty update of Waltz et al. (pp. 19–21, 30). Ch. 5 changes the classical inertia-correcting loop (modify W until the inertia is (n,t,0)) into a loop that accepts the step before the inertia is correct if it passes (5.8) or (5.9) (Algorithm 5.1, pp. 62–63; Algorithm 5.3, p. 66). Ch. 6 replaces one penalty parameter by an interval [πl, πu]: a step is acceptable if it gives sufficient decrease for some π in the interval; πl = πu recovers the penalty function, πl = 0 and πu = ∞ a one-element filter (p. 82). πu follows the standard predicted-progress update (6.2) (p. 86); πl rises only after a step into "region II" (feasibility gained at a large objective increase), and then only gradually by (6.7) (pp. 88–89), using (6.10), the smallest π that would have accepted the step (p. 95). The conclusion names the unifying theme: the step-acceptance model is pulled into the step computation (p. 98).
  - Transferable: when an iterative inner solver feeds an outer globalization, take the outer acceptance quantity (here the penalty-model reduction) and make it the inner solver's stopping test, splitting the residual into the components the outer analysis treats differently (pp. 23, 26–29).
  - Transferable: to reconcile two rival mechanisms, build a parameter-interval family whose endpoints recover each, then design the two endpoint updates separately because they carry different convergence duties (pp. 82–85).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Smooth f, c; equality constraints, t ≤ n (p. 14). Assumptions 3.4 (p. 33): iterates in a convex set with bounded f, c and first and second derivatives; bounded multipliers (b); full-row-rank Jacobians with singular values bounded away from zero (c); bounded Wk (d); Wk uniformly positive definite on null(Ak) (e). The author labels (b) and (c) strong, adopted to focus on inexactness, and calls relaxing them future work (p. 33); he shows (b) can be enforced by an extra residual test or a multiplier reset (pp. 45–46). Jacobian singularity is excluded deliberately as out of scope (p. 46). Ch. 5 drops (e) except at iterations where the inertia test (5.4) holds (p. 67). Ch. 6 assumes exact solves of the primal-dual system for simplicity (p. 85). Admitted design weakness: β is scale-dependent ("not ideal"), kept because (3.6b) is what bounds the penalty parameter (p. 30). Well-posedness assumes the iterative solver eventually meets (3.4) whenever (2.6) is consistent (p. 30).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) analysis-only decomposition dk = uk + vk into tangential (null-space) and normal (range-space) parts, never computed (pp. 33–34); (ii) a case split K1 / K2 by ‖u‖² ≥ γ4‖v‖² and a single progress measure Θk (‖u‖² + ‖c‖ on K1, max{‖c‖,‖r‖} on K2) (p. 36) that simultaneously bounds ‖d‖² (Lemma 3.8, p. 37), the directional derivative (Lemma 3.9, p. 38) and hence the steplength from below (Lemma 3.10, pp. 38–39); (iii) a computable surrogate gᵀd − π(‖c‖ − ‖r‖) for the directional derivative, usable in the Armijo test (Lemma 3.2 and (3.10)–(3.11), pp. 31–32); (iv) penalty-parameter boundedness by bounding each bracketed term of the model reduction by a constant times (‖c‖ − ‖r‖) using (3.6b), which is the reason β exists (Lemma 3.11, pp. 39–42); (v) telescoping the fixed-penalty merit function once π stabilizes to get ‖c‖ → 0 and ‖d‖ → 0 (Lemma 3.12, p. 42), then a contraction recursion on the dual residual, ‖g+Aᵀλ‖ ≤ max{(1−γ10)‖·‖, γ11‖c‖} + error, giving Theorem 3.13 (pp. 43–45). Ch. 5 turns the implicit K1/K2 distinction into explicit, computable tests via ‖Akdk‖/‖Ak‖ ≤ ‖vk‖ (Lemma 5.2, pp. 65–66) and re-runs the lemma chain with four index sets and Θ′k (pp. 67–72); the steplength lemma and the final theorem are only claimed there (proof details omitted, p. 70; p. 72). Ch. 6 reuses Lemmas 3.5–3.8 unchanged and proves only the parts the interval touches (Lemmas 6.3–6.6, pp. 91–94); the proof of Lemma 6.4 cites γ4, γ3 where the lemmas it invokes define γ6, γ5 (p. 92), a constant-labelling slip. Results are global first-order convergence only; local rates are discussed, not proved: linear / superlinear via a forcing sequence (3.26), with a second-order correction or nonmonotone technique against rejection of unit steps (p. 46).
  - Transferable: define one progress measure Θk per step type so that step length, merit decrease and steplength bound are all expressed in it; the global theorem then follows by telescoping (pp. 36–42).
  - Transferable: when a proof splits on a quantity you cannot observe (null/range components), derive observable sufficient inequalities for each branch and add them to the algorithm as acceptance tests (Lemma 5.2, p. 65; p. 67).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Ch. 4: stand-alone Matlab code with Kelley's GMRES, no preconditioner, on 44 equality-constrained CUTEr/COPS problems (< 10,000 variables) kept only if the exact-solve variant solved them (p. 50; p. 51); exact Hessian with an identity shift when needed (p. 50); parameters in Table 4.1 (p. 51). Baseline ires is identical except that the inner loop stops on the total relative residual (3.4) with κ = 2⁻¹…2⁻⁹ (p. 51); success rates 50/68/80/82/86 % versus 100 % for isqp (Table 4.2, p. 52; Table A.2, p. 109). Failures are classified (ascent direction for all allowable π; steplength below αmin; iteration limit), and the failure tests are deliberately naive to stress robustness (pp. 49–50). The cost of robustness is measured with a hybrid ires-isqp: on the 35 problems solved by both, 0.5 extra GMRES iterations on average over a whole run, and the 9 problems ires missed are all solved (pp. 52–53). PDE inverse problems (Haber–Hanson benchmark; Elliptic n = 8192, Parabolic n = 4608; SymQMR with a Jacobi + L-BFGS preconditioner) (pp. 53–56): a full 27-point grid over (κ, ϵ, β′) (Table 4.4, p. 56), Jacobian mat-vecs as the primary measure because the preconditioner makes iteration counts small (p. 57), best settings reported (Tables 4.5–4.6, p. 57), all settings in Table A.4 (p. 110); failures at κ = 0.1 on Parabolic attributed to ill-conditioning (pp. 57–58); recommendation to tune per application (p. 58). Ch. 5: KNITRO 5.0 Direct forced to pure line search; 36 problems = those of Ch. 4's experiments on which the inertia was wrong at least once (p. 72); metrics iterations and factorizations; performance profiles (Figs. 5.1–5.2, pp. 74–75). Ch. 6: KNITRO-Direct, 85 problems, default penalty vs πu initial values 1, 10, 100, 1000; iterations and function evaluations; profiles (Figs. 6.6–6.7, pp. 96–97). Losses are visible only in the appendices: matmod_new is worse on e.g. dtoc1nc, elec1 and elec3 and fails on orthrgds, which matmod solves (Table B.6, p. 112); pi_flex_100 fails on eigenb2 and eigenc2, which pi_default solves, and is worse on orthregc (Tables C.8–C.9, pp. 114–115). The prose says the Ch. 5 savings were consistent (p. 75) and calls the Ch. 6 results encouraging (p. 96) without naming these cases. No CPU times anywhere; Chs. 5–6 are not run on the PDE problems.
  - Transferable: to price a safeguard, run a hybrid that adds your test on top of the baseline's own stopping rule and report the extra inner iterations next to the extra problems solved (pp. 52–53).
  - Transferable: when a preconditioner makes outer and inner iteration counts uninformative, switch the headline metric to the operation the preconditioner itself consumes (Jacobian mat-vecs) (p. 57).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues and corrects inexact Newton (Dembo–Eisenstat–Steihaug): same spirit, but a whole-residual test is argued unsafe for nonconvex constrained problems because a step may move away from a minimizer or stall near a stationary point of the Lagrangian (pp. 23, 25). Distinguishes itself from inexact composite-step / trust-region SQP (Lalee–Nocedal–Plantenga; Byrd–Hribar–Nocedal; Heinkenschloss–Vicente, whose conditions are similar but tied to a composite step, pp. 23, 26–27) and from full-space line-search PDE methods (Biros–Ghattas; Haber–Ascher; Prudencio–Byrd–Cai) that lack conditions guaranteeing global convergence of inexact steps (p. 24). The penalty update is taken from Waltz–Morales–Nocedal–Orban (pp. 19, 30); matrix modification follows LOQO / IPOPT practice (pp. 61–62). Critique of filters is direct but even-handed: both penalty and filter block productive steps, shown in pictures (Figs. 6.1–6.2, pp. 78–79); nonmonotone penalty updates are dismissed as ad hoc in one sentence (pp. 77–78). Committee and acknowledgments place him in the Nocedal–Byrd–Waltz KNITRO circle, with access to KNITRO source code (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Each method chapter follows the same arc: background and motivation → algorithm → well-posedness → global analysis → numerics → final remarks with limits (Chs. 3–6; contents pp. 7–8). The core thesis appears in the abstract (p. 3) and again as the explicit major theme of the conclusion (p. 98). Frequent "we claim" for argued but unproved statements (pp. 28, 48, 52, 84, 95, 99, 100, 102), a visible marker separating claims from theorems. Geometric pictures in the ‖c‖–f plane carry the Ch. 6 argument (Figs. 2.1–2.2, 6.1–6.5, pp. 18–19, 78–88). Assumptions are labelled strong in the text rather than buried (p. 33). Evaluative words in the numerics are warm ("quite good", "impressive", p. 73; "encouraging", p. 96) while the counter-cases stay in the appendix tables.
  - Transferable: mark every unproved but load-bearing statement with an explicit "we claim" so a reader can separate argued from proved content (pp. 28, 84, 95).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution levels: algorithmic (matrix-free inexact SQP with termination tests; inertia-tolerant step acceptance; flexible penalty), theoretical (global first-order convergence for each: Theorem 3.13, pp. 43–45; outline proofs pp. 67–72, 90–94), computational (Matlab and KNITRO implementations, pp. 48, 72, 94). Limits are specific: strong Assumptions 3.4(b)(c) (p. 33); no rate guarantee without (3.26) (p. 46); singular Jacobians not analyzed (p. 46); no preconditioner in the CUTEr tests although called essential (p. 51); β scale-dependent (p. 30); parameters need tuning per application (p. 58); Algorithm 5.3 must compute the full step before testing it, which may cancel its savings when factorizing (p. 76); in an inexact setting the choice between modifying W and solving more accurately stays open (pp. 75–76). Future directions named: inexact interior-point method with slack positivity and fraction-to-the-boundary, flexible penalty in an interior-point method and in trust-region SQP (pp. 99–103), Jacobian singularities (p. 46), the Ch. 5 tests inside the inexact framework (pp. 75–76). Later items in SKILL Method 1 Practice (rank deficiency 10.1137/08072471X; inexact IPM 10.1137/090747634; Hessian modification 10.1007/s10107-008-0248-3) match these announced items (link inferred from titles, not from this text).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Baseline = your solver with only the inner stopping rule replaced by the textbook one; sweep its tolerance over a geometric grid (2⁻¹…2⁻⁹) and report the success rate per value (pp. 51–52, 109).
- Price your safeguard with a hybrid run that adds it to the baseline, and report extra inner iterations per run next to extra problems solved (pp. 52–53).
- One progress measure Θk per step type (tangential-dominant vs normal-dominant) that bounds step norm, merit decrease and steplength at once (pp. 36–39).
- Replace an unobservable proof split by observable inequalities (‖Ad‖/‖A‖ as a lower bound on the normal component) and add them to the algorithm (Lemma 5.2, pp. 65–66).
- When an acceptance rule uses a parameter range, move the lower end toward the smallest value that would have accepted the step just taken (6.10), by a fraction 0.1 with a minimum increment (6.7) (pp. 89, 95).
- Explain acceptance mechanisms by drawing the region each rival blocks in the ‖c‖–f plane (Figs. 6.1–6.5, pp. 78–88).
