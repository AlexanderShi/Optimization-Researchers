### S068 · Projected stochastic momentum methods for nonlinear equality-constrained optimization for machine learning (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2601.11795)
**Read level**: full. All 27 file pages read: abstract and §1 (pp. 2–3), §2 framework and step decomposition (pp. 3–5), §3.1 heavy-ball algorithm and analysis (pp. 5–11), §3.2 Adam algorithm and analysis (pp. 11–18), §4 informed learning (pp. 19–20), §5 experiments (pp. 20–24), §6 conclusion (p. 24), references (pp. 24–27). Figures 1–7 are plots whose content is not in the text layer; only their captions and the prose describing them were read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Qi Wang (Michigan IOE), Christian Piermarini (Amazon), Yunlang Zhu (Lehigh) (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: the stochastic-SQP line (built on Berahas–Curtis–Robinson–Zhou [2], p. 3) extended to the optimizers ML practitioners actually use; listed in SKILL.md "Latest" as student-led ML optimizers.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Momentum methods (heavy-ball, Adagrad, RMSprop, Adam) dominate unconstrained ML training (p. 2), while the stochastic Newton-SQP of [2] handles nonlinear equality constraints with a plain stochastic gradient. The entry is to transplant heavy-ball and Adam into that SQP framework with guarantees "on par" with the unconstrained ones (abstract, p. 2). A prior constrained Adam [21] exists without a guarantee, and an Adagrad-type SQP [24] exists but needs a different analysis (p. 3). The application that motivates it is informed (physics-informed) supervised learning with a small number of hard constraints imposed during training only (p. 19).
  - Transferable: enter by taking the field's most-used heuristic (Adam) and the constrained framework you already own, and ask what the minimal structural change is for the heuristic to inherit the framework's guarantees (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Each step is split into a range-space (normal) component v_k = −ρ_k J_kᵀ(J_kJ_kᵀ)⁻¹c_k and a null-space component u_k = −h_k⁻¹P_k g_k (Algorithms 2–3, pp. 5, 11). The key change is that the momentum averages (heavy-ball r_k; Adam's first and second moments r_k, s_k) are taken over the projected gradients P_k g_k rather than over g_k (pp. 3, 5), and the averaged quantity is projected again before use (d_k = v_k + P_k r_k, p. 5). Consequence used throughout: J_k d_k = −ρ_k c_k whatever the momentum, so momentum never disturbs the linearized constraint (p. 9). The step decomposition costs O(m³ + m²n) per iteration, so with few constraints the cost is close to an unconstrained step (pp. 4–5). The Adam variant borrows the modified bias correction of Défossez et al. [10] so that the effective step size is nondecreasing (p. 11). Unchanged from [2]: the SQP linear algebra; changed: no merit-function line search in the algorithm, a fixed α (pp. 5, 11).
  - Transferable: when adding momentum or averaging to a constrained method, average the null-space projections, not the raw gradients, and re-project the average; the linearized feasibility then holds exactly and the analysis separates (pp. 5, 9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f(x) = E[F(x, ξ)], c deterministic, both C¹ (p. 3). Assumption 3.1: an open convex set containing the iterates on which f is bounded below, ∥c∥, ∥∇f∥, ∥J∥ are bounded, ∇f and J are Lipschitz, and the smallest singular value of J is at least σ_min (LICQ along the iterates); the projected gradient estimate is unbiased with variance bounded by M²; ρ_k and h_k lie in fixed intervals (pp. 5–6). Assumption 3.2 adds a bound on ∥u_k∥∞ for Adam (p. 11). The full-row-rank assumption is stated as needed for the projection form (3) of first-order optimality (p. 3). Assumptions are placed on the iterate set, not proved to hold.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Lemma 3.1: Lipschitz continuity of x ↦ P(x)∇f(x) via the pseudoinverse identity for P(x) − P(x̄) and ∥J†∥ ≤ 1/σ_min (p. 6). (b) Merit function φ(x, τ) = τf(x) + ∥c(x)∥₁ used only in the analysis; one-step bound (13) (p. 7). (c) Unrolling momentum as a geometric sum r_k = Σβⁱu_{k−i} (14) and bounding E∥r_k∥² by Jensen on the normalized weights (Lemma 3.4, p. 7). (d) Splitting ∇f(x_k)ᵀd_k into a constraint term, a "fresh gradient" term with conditional expectation −h⁻¹∥P∇f∥², and a "stale gradient" drift term controlled by Lemma 3.1, the iterate distance, and Young's inequality with an i-dependent weight (Lemma 3.5, pp. 8–9). (e) Orthogonality of v_j and P_j r_j used to separate ∥d∥² (pp. 8, 10). (f) The merit parameter is not adaptive: τ is set from constants by (21) so that 1 − τκρ_max/(σ_min ρ_min) = τ, making the constraint coefficient equal the objective coefficient (Theorem 3.1, p. 10). (g) Result: the average expected value of h_max⁻¹∥P_k∇f(x_k)∥² + ρ_min∥c_k∥₁ is at most (φ(x₁) − τf_inf)/(ατK) + αC/τ, i.e. O(1/K) down to a noise floor αC/τ (pp. 10–11). (h) Adam: the analysis follows [10] term by term (Lemmas 3.7–3.8 cited, p. 12; the B2 bound from [10, Eq. (A.27)], p. 13), with a log-type bound in K and a parameter-selection argument: first β₂ near 1, then α small, then K large (Theorem 3.2, pp. 15–18).
  - Transferable: device (f). When a fixed merit parameter suffices for the analysis, choose it so the two coefficients in the descent inequality coincide; the theorem then reads as one weighted stationarity measure (p. 10).
  - Transferable: device (d). For momentum analyses, split the inner product into fresh-gradient, stale-drift and constraint terms and bound the drift by a Lipschitz property of the projected gradient, not of the gradient (pp. 6, 8–9).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: PyTorch on Colab L4 GPUs (p. 20). Four informed-learning problems: 1D damped spring (MLP 3×32, 3 ODE-residual constraints, pp. 20–21), a 1-butene isomerization ODE with a mass-balance condition (MLP 3×64, 10 constraints, pp. 21–22), 1D Burgers (MLP 3×64, 10 constraints, pp. 22–23), 2D Darcy flow with a Fourier Neural Operator (50 constraints, pp. 23–24). Compared: SQP-Heavyball(con), SQP-Adam(con), soft-constrained Adam(unc), and projection-less hard-constrained Adam(con) [21] (p. 20). Fairness device: all four use the same objective, and the hard-constrained ones add constraints (p. 20). Fixed β = 0.9, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ϵ = 10⁻⁷ for all problems (p. 20); two learning rates per problem for all methods (pp. 21–24); full-batch and mini-batch runs, mini-batch reported as mean ± one standard deviation over 5 runs (figure captions, pp. 21–24). Reported: loss over epochs and MSE; the claim that SQP-Adam(con) needs less hyperparameter tuning rests on similar results at the two learning rates (p. 21). Failures printed: on Burgers Adam(unc) and SQP-Adam(con) "were in fact comparable" and SQP-Heavyball(con) was "poorer still" (p. 23). Constraint-violation values are not reported in the prose (figures not read). The hard-constrained linear-PINN method [5] is not compared, with a stated reason (p. 19).
  - Transferable: when comparing constrained and penalized training, give every method the identical objective and let the constrained ones only add constraints, so that the comparison isolates the effect of hard constraints (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds directly on his own stochastic SQP [2] (with [1, 8]) and models both analyses on Défossez et al. [10] (pp. 2, 11). Distinguishes from [21] (no guarantee, momentum on raw gradients, pp. 3, 5) and from O'Neill's Adagrad-type SQP [24] (different analysis, p. 3). In informed learning it positions hard constraints during training against soft constraints [37] and constraint-embedding layers [4, 23] (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Priority sentence: "As far as we are aware, ours is the first paper ..." (p. 3). The "more significant contribution" is named by practical performance (Adam over heavy-ball, p. 3). Hedges: the experiments are explicitly not "huge-scale, state-of-the-art" (p. 19), yet the paper asserts that relative performance "would be similar" on larger networks (p. 19) and closes on a conjecture to the same effect (p. 24). Each theorem is followed by a paragraph explaining how to read the bound and choose parameters (pp. 11, 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic + theory + application demonstration (abstract, p. 2). Limits visible in the text: the bound has a non-vanishing αC/τ term and needs α small (p. 11); LICQ, bounded gradients and bounded ∥u_k∥∞ are assumed along the iterates (pp. 5–6, 11); experiments use small networks, two learning rates and 5 runs (pp. 20–24). The conclusion gives no future directions beyond the larger-network conjecture (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): identical objective for all four methods "To ensure a fair comparison" (p. 20), and the incumbent's tie on Burgers and the heavy-ball method's poor result are printed (p. 23). Partial: only two learning rates and no tuning protocol for the rivals (pp. 21–24).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): no steering. The merit parameter is a fixed analysis constant (21) (p. 10), the algorithm has no merit line search (pp. 5, 11), and the balance between optimality and feasibility is set by user-chosen ρ_min and h_max (p. 11). Read with Inner Tension 3: the informed-learning methodology insists that the hard-constrained problem is not solved with a penalty or augmented-Lagrangian method (p. 19).
- new-pattern candidate: "Project before you average" (momentum or adaptive scaling applied to null-space-projected gradients so that linearized feasibility is untouched; pp. 3, 5, 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Average P_k g_k, not g_k, in any momentum/Adam extension to equality constraints, and keep the normal step separate (pp. 5, 9).
- Pick the analysis-only merit parameter so the objective and constraint coefficients in the summed bound coincide (p. 10).
- Model a new constrained analysis line by line on a clean unconstrained proof (here [10]) and cite the reused lemmas explicitly (pp. 12–13).
- Same-objective protocol for hard-vs-soft constrained training comparisons (p. 20).

**Quotes**:
- "A critical feature of each extension is that the momentum terms are implemented with projected gradient estimates, rather than with the gradient estimates themselves." (p. 2)
- "Another key feature of this methodology is that one does not solve the hard-constrained training problem with a penalty-based (e.g., augmented Lagrangian) method." (p. 19)

### S087 · A Local-Linearly Convergent Algorithm for Nonconvex Equality-Constrained Optimization (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2608.12665)
**Read level**: full. All 28 file pages read: abstract and §1 (pp. 2–3), §2 assumptions, Lemma 2.1 and Theorem 2.1 with proofs (pp. 4–14), §3 progressive sampling, Assumptions 3.1–3.6, Lemmas 3.1–3.4 and Theorem 3.1 with proofs (pp. 14–27), §4 conclusion and data statement (p. 27), references (p. 28). Long displayed formulas lost symbols in extraction; their structure was reconstructed from the surrounding text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Lingjun Guo, Daniel P. Robinson (Lehigh ISE) (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: a sequel to the authors' progressive-sampling paper [1] (arXiv 2510.00417, p. 28), supplying the local rate that [1]'s improved complexity needs; listed in SKILL.md "Latest" ("a method on Fletcher's augmented Lagrangian").

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Penalty methods for equality constraints are inexact or exact; exact ones are usually nonsmooth, but Fletcher's augmented Lagrangian is exact and smooth (pp. 2–3). The Gradient-Eigenstep algorithm of Goyens, Eftekhari and Boumal [9] finds approximate second-order points with a complexity guarantee (p. 3). The entry is a missing piece: its local behaviour. The paper shows a local-linear rate near strong second-order stationary points and then uses it as the engine of a progressive-sampling method for large sample-average problems (abstract, p. 2; p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a global-complexity algorithm exists, ask what it does near a nondegenerate solution; a local rate is often the missing lemma that turns a multi-stage (warm-started) scheme into a better overall complexity (pp. 3, 14).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: No new algorithm in §2. The claim is that, with step size t small enough and penalty ρ above a threshold, [9, Algorithm 1] never triggers its eigenstep phase and its line search accepts the fixed step, so it reduces to gradient descent on F_ρ, which converges linearly in ∥∇F_ρ∥ (Theorem 2.1, pp. 9–14). §3 restates progressive sampling (Algorithm 1: sample sizes p_{k+1} = min{θp_k, N}, warm starts, K = ⌈log_θ(N/p₁)⌉ + 1 stages, p. 15) with the Gradient-Eigenstep solver as inner method (Algorithm 2, p. 15). Theorem 3.1: solving the full sample directly costs O(N max{ϵ⁻², ζ⁻³}) individual gradients and Jacobians (part (a), p. 24); progressive sampling costs p₁C₁ + T̄θ(N − p₁)/(θ − 1) + N⌈log_B(3√5 ωϵ_c/ϵ)⌉, where only the last term depends on ϵ, and only logarithmically (part (b), pp. 24–25).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f and c twice continuously differentiable (Assumption 2.1, p. 4); compact sublevel set of ∥c∥ (2.2, p. 4); singular values of ∇c bounded below on a δ-enlarged neighbourhood (2.3, p. 4); y(x) twice differentiable and Lipschitz Hessians of f + cᵀy and ∥c∥² (2.4, p. 5). Theorem 2.1 needs a start x satisfying approximate second-order conditions with positive curvature β (7), ρ > ρ_β (25), tolerance ϵ ≤ ϵ_ρ (26) and step t ≤ t_ρ (27) (p. 9). §3 adds uniform derivative bounds (3.1, p. 15), bounded sample variances (3.2, p. 16), uniform versions of 2.2–2.4 over all sampled problems (3.3–3.5, p. 16) and an (α, β)-strongly Morse property (3.6, p. 16), called "relatively mild" for problems with isolated local minima, citing [1, Remark 3.2] (p. 16). The thresholds depend on unknown constants (κ, σ_min, M_ρ).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Hessian of Fletcher's AL rewritten in null-space/range-space projections, (17), after differentiating the normal equations that define the least-squares multiplier, (13)–(15) (pp. 6–7). This gives λ_min(∇²F_ρ) ≥ min{β − η_ρϵ, 2ρσ²_min − (κ + mκ²) − η_ρϵ}: null-space curvature comes from second-order stationarity, range-space curvature from a large ρ (Lemma 2.1, pp. 5–8; remarks p. 9, linked to [5, Theorem 6]). (b) An induction carrying three invariants: iterate in C_R, gradient contraction, and the eigenvalue lower bound degraded by a Weyl-type perturbation [13, Thm 6.6] (pp. 10–12). (c) Feasibility of later iterates via [9, Corollary 2.7] (p. 12). (d) The reduction to gradient descent is shown by checking that the eigenstep test never fires and that the Armijo condition holds at the trial step, using an upper curvature bound along each segment (pp. 13–14). (e) Iteration count T = ⌈log_{4/(4−βt)}(√5 ω_ρ ϵ/ϵ′)⌉ with final curvature β′ ≥ β/2 (pp. 9–10). (f) Lemma 3.1 checks [9]'s Assumptions A1–A5 one by one for the sampled problems (pp. 17–18). (g) Strong Morse passes from the full to sampled problems ([1, Lemma 3.6], restated as Lemma 3.2, p. 18). (h) Warm-start lemma by a sign argument: |dᵀHd| ≥ β' from strong Morse together with dᵀHd ≥ −small forces dᵀHd ≥ β' (Lemma 3.3, pp. 19–22), with projection perturbation bounds from Stewart [14] (p. 21). (i) Lemma 3.4: monotonicity of the tolerance function in (b, κ, ρ), which yields one uniform parameter interval for all stages (pp. 23–24). (j) A geometric-sum count of per-stage costs (p. 27).
  - Transferable: device (h). Use a strong-Morse (|curvature| ≥ β) property plus approximate second-order stationarity to upgrade "curvature not very negative" to "curvature ≥ β" (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: device (d). To claim that a complex method reduces to a simple one locally, check every branch test and every line-search acceptance at the simple method's step (pp. 13–14).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none. The data statement says no datasets are analysed or generated because the work is theoretical (p. 27). Practical performance is delegated to the experiments of [1] (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: Lineage: Fletcher [6], Di Pillo and Grippo [2–4], Estrin–Friedlander–Orban–Saunders [5], Goyens–Eftekhari–Boumal [9] (p. 3). The paper extends the analysis of another group's algorithm ([9]) instead of proposing a rival, and it completes the authors' own [1] (pp. 3, 14). Morse programs are traced to Fujiwara [7] and Milnor [11] (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A "twofold" contribution stated in the abstract and repeated in §1 and §4 (pp. 2, 3, 27). Intuition paragraphs after Lemma 2.1 explain why ρ must be large (range-space curvature) while a small ϵ suffices in the null space (p. 9). Explicit hedge separating worst-case parameter rules from practice: the intervals are "natural for worst-case complexity guarantees", but they are not expected to be "absolutely necessary" (p. 22). The performance measure is justified before the theorem (counting individual ∇f_i and ∇c_i evaluations; other counts would agree under fixed steps, p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory only: a local rate plus a sample-complexity improvement (p. 27). Limits visible: the start must already be near a strong second-order point (p. 9); the ρ and t thresholds depend on unknown constants (pp. 9, 22–23); the strong Morse assumption is global over approximate stationary points (p. 16); no experiments (p. 27). The conclusion gives no future directions (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): the penalty parameter is fixed "sufficiently large" above a threshold ρ_β (p. 9) and, in progressive sampling, chosen from an interval (ρ_β, ρ_max] (p. 25). No update rule is designed, so the guarantee rests on an assumed-known threshold, the opposite emphasis to Method 2. Bears on Inner Tension 3: the 2026 work builds on a (smooth exact) augmented Lagrangian.
- Method 4 ⚠ (variant): he states and justifies the measure (individual gradient/Jacobian evaluations, p. 24) and says the worst-case parameter rules are not needed in practice (p. 22). He still produces a worst-case complexity improvement without measurements (Inner Tension 1).
- new-pattern candidate: "Local rate as complexity lever" (prove a local-linear rate for an existing global-complexity method, then use warm-started stages with geometric sample growth so that only the last stage pays N·log(1/ϵ); pp. 3, 24–27).
- new-pattern candidate: "Analyse someone else's algorithm" (extend [9] instead of proposing a rival; pp. 3, 13–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Decompose the penalty-function Hessian into N H N − R H R + 2ρ∇c∇cᵀ + O(ϵ) terms to read off separate null- and range-space curvature conditions (pp. 7–8).
- Prove that algorithm X reduces to gradient descent by verifying that X's branch and line-search tests are inactive or satisfied (pp. 13–14).
- For multi-stage sampling schemes, choose per-stage tolerances from one parameter µ ∈ (0, 1) so no stage is solved disproportionately tightly (p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "In this case, the algorithm reduces to a gradient descent algorithm applied to minimize Fletcher’s augmented Lagrangian." (p. 2)
- "We emphasize, however, that for good practical performance, we do not expect these parameter requirements to be absolutely necessary." (p. 22)

### S088 · NonOpt: Nonconvex, Nonsmooth Optimizer (Mathematical Programming Computation 2026, DOI 10.1007/s12532-026-00322-5; read as arXiv:2503.22826v1)
**Read level**: full (arXiv version). All 27 file pages of arXiv:2503.22826v1 / COR@L TR 25T-005 (dated 1 April 2025, p. 2) were read: abstract and §1 (pp. 2–3), §2 (pp. 3–4), §3 framework and modules (pp. 4–9), §4 IPM subproblem solver with Algorithms 2–3 (pp. 9–13), §5 implementation (pp. 11–14), §6 experiments (pp. 14–22), §7 conclusion (p. 22), references (pp. 22–25), Tables 2–4 (pp. 26–27). Figures 1–7 (CPU-time plots) and Figures 8–10 (images) are not in the text layer. The journal version was not read; differences from it are unknown.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Lara Zebiane (Lehigh ISE) (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: consolidation of the 2012–2022 nonsmooth line. The code grew from Matlab codes for [9, 10, 11], then SVANO for [12], then NonOpt for [7], now version 2.0 (p. 2). Listed in SKILL.md Trajectory ("Consolidation ... NonOpt").

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A software paper: present an open-source C++ package for minimizing locally Lipschitz, possibly nonconvex and nonsmooth functions, and introduce its new interior-point QP subproblem solver (abstract, p. 2). The stated gap is the absence of other actively developed open-source general-purpose codes for this class (p. 14).
  - Transferable: when your algorithmic line has matured over several papers, consolidate it into one extensible code whose paper documents the options that matter most (pp. 2–3, 7–9).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: One framework covers gradient sampling and proximal bundle: both build a cutting-plane model l_{k,m}(x) = max_j{f_{k,j} + g_{k,j}ᵀ(x − x_{k,j})} plus a quasi-Newton quadratic term inside an ∞-norm trust region, solved through its dual QP (3) (pp. 5–6). Only the point set differs (sampled points vs past iterates, p. 5). A line search imposes sufficient decrease (5); the differentiability check at x_{k+1} that GS theory needs is deliberately dropped (p. 6). BFGS/DFP with damping (7) from [12] keeps self-correction (p. 6). The new piece is a dual-QP interior-point method: Mehrotra predictor-corrector with a re-used LDL factorization, plus the Curtis–Nocedal [8] separate primal/dual step sizes, chosen by two 2-D convex QPs along a two-segment path to minimize a residual merit function (pp. 9–13). Engineering details: tuned initial points cut IPM iterations to about 25 % (p. 14); first solve without the trust-region variables (σ, ρ) and re-solve only if needed (p. 14); dual active set (DAS) when G_k has ≤ 25 columns, IPM otherwise (p. 17); objective scaling so ∥g₁∥∞ ≤ 10², and ϵ₁ = max{10⁻², 10⁻¹∥g₁∥∞} (p. 12).
  - Transferable: unify rival methods as one model with different data sets (here point sets) so a single code and a single subproblem solver serve both (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f locally Lipschitz on the interior of its domain (Assumption 2.1, p. 3). Guarantees need f real-valued plus either C¹ on an open full-measure set (gradient sampling) or weak upper semismoothness (bundle) (Assumption 2.2, p. 4). The code runs under the weaker Assumption 2.1, with safeguards against infinite values (p. 4). The "gradient" direction strategy has no general guarantee (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No new theorems; theory is cited ([3, 4, 21], [27, 34], [12]; p. 4). Devices that are explained: the primal–dual relation d_k = −W_k(G_kω_k + γ_k), unique although the dual solution need not be (pp. 5–6); splitting γ into positive and negative parts to obtain a standard-form QP (9) (pp. 9–10); G_kω_k as an approximation of the minimum-norm Clarke subgradient, with Carathéodory's n + 1 bound as the reason exact stationarity tests are too costly (p. 18).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Four sets (pp. 14–15), all NonOpt-only; external codes are omitted because NonOpt beat LMBM before, LMBM is no longer developed, and no other actively developed open-source code is known (p. 14). (i) DAS vs IPM on 900 randomly generated QPs built backwards from a prescribed primal solution d* (three d* types: TR inactive, half active, fully active), n from 100 to 1000, m ∈ {n + 1, 1.5n, 2n}, 10 seeds each; CPU time vs n, parsed by d* and by m (pp. 15–17). The n list prints "5000" among 100–1000, apparently a typo for 500 given the 900 total (p. 16). The paper states the uncovered case m ≪ n, which is what NonOpt usually meets (p. 17). (ii) Speed settings (Δf = 10⁻⁵, n_f = 10) on 20 scalable problems at n = 1000 (10 from Haarala et al. [18], 10 from UFO [25]; 12 convex, 8 nonconvex), three direction strategies CP, GC, G; iterations, function and gradient evaluations, final f and CPU per run; all runs about 85 s (pp. 19–20; Table 2, p. 26). (iii) Accuracy settings (Δf = 10⁻⁸, n_f = 20), about 580 s; gains modest on some problems, large for GC on Test29_6, 17, 19 (p. 20; Table 3, p. 27). (iv) Image denoising with 488,235 variables, four regularizers (one convex, three nonconvex), L-BFGS storage; (λ, β) tuned over a grid of powers of 2 by MSE against the clean image (pp. 20–22; Table 4, p. 27). The plain BFGS-type "G" strategy is often fastest (Table 2, p. 26), and this is said openly, with the caveat that it is inferior on some problems (p. 12). Single runs; known f* listed where available (Table 1, p. 20).
  - Transferable: generate QP test instances backwards from a prescribed optimal solution by constructing KKT-consistent data, so that correctness and difficulty (active/inactive trust region) are controlled (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: run the same test set twice, with speed and accuracy tolerances, and report total wall time for each, so the user sees the price of extra digits (pp. 15, 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Self-lineage through his own papers [7, 9–12] (p. 2). The DAS solver generalizes Kiwiel [20] to a W_k metric (p. 9); the step-size rule reuses his own Curtis–Nocedal 2007 [8] (pp. 9, 11). Default behaviour is presented as close to Lewis–Overton's unadulterated BFGS with weak Wolfe search [23] (pp. 11–12). Heuristic termination is placed alongside LMBM [19] and HANSO [30] (pp. 18–19).

### S089 · Robust Server Defense Against Unreliable Clients in One-Shot Fair Collaborative Machine Learning (FLICS 2026, DOI 10.1109/flics70075.2026.11621940; read as arXiv:2605.08616)
**Read level**: full (arXiv version). All 9 file pages of arXiv:2605.08616v1 were read: abstract and §I (pp. 1–2), §II background (pp. 2–3), §III method with Algorithms 1–3 (pp. 3–5), §IV experiments with Tables I–III (pp. 5–8), §V conclusion (p. 8), references (pp. 8–9). Figures 2–3 (heatmap and scatter) are not in the text layer. The FLICS proceedings version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Chia-Yuan Wu (Lehigh ISE, first author), Daniel P. Robinson (Lehigh ISE) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: applied fairness-in-ML line with the same group; it follows the authors' bilevel synthetic-data paper [3] (INFORMS J. Optim. 2026, p. 8). This is outside nonlinear-programming methodology proper.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In one-shot collaborative learning, clients send proxy data once, so the server cannot correct biased contributions over rounds (abstract, p. 1). Unreliable clients (malicious or careless) can send proxy data that harms group fairness (p. 1). Existing defenses assume many rounds and that unreliable clients are a minority (pp. 2–3). The entry is a server-side defense that still works when unreliable clients are the majority (p. 2).
  - Transferable: find the hidden majority assumption in rival methods (median-based "good regions") and design the experiment where it fails, at 60 % unreliable clients (pp. 3, 6–7).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A bilevel problem: the inner problem trains weighted, regularized logistic regression on all proxy data with client weights w (5)–(7); the outer problem picks w on the simplex to minimize loss on a small trusted root dataset subject to decision-boundary-covariance fairness constraints for SP or EO (4), (8) (pp. 4–5). The constraints are moved into the objective as penalties with parameter ρ_s and a mix ν (9)–(10) (p. 5). It is solved by hypergradient steps (implicit differentiation through the inner optimality condition, (12)–(13)) with Adam or gradient descent, and sort-based Euclidean projection onto the simplex (Algorithms 2–3, pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linear logistic classifier (6) (p. 5); binary sensitive attribute and labels (p. 4); a root dataset from the same distribution as each client's data, about 0.5 % of it (p. 4); the DBC fairness surrogate following Zafar et al. [52] (p. 4); a "fair proxy dataset" requested from every client (Definition 1, p. 5). The authors call the root-dataset assumption a limitation twice (pp. 4, 8). No convergence assumptions (no theory).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a for theorems. Computational devices: hypergradient via the inner Hessian linear system (13) (p. 5); simplex projection by sorting [50] (Algorithm 3, p. 4).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Law School (20,798 samples) and Dutch (60,420) datasets, K = 5 clients, random even split, 80/20 train/test (pp. 5–6). Unreliable fractions 20/40/60 %. "Realistic" scenarios: the most unfair clients (ranked by their own |SPD| or |EOD|) send raw data while the rest send fair proxy data from [3]; "ideal" scenarios: reliable clients send replicas of the fairest client's proxy (p. 6). Five seeds averaged (p. 6). Metrics: accuracy, |SPD|, |EOD|, evaluated only on reliable clients' test data, with a stated reason (p. 6). Dutch/EO skipped because the raw data already has |EOD| = 0.02 (p. 6). Settings: inner L-BFGS (tolerance 10⁻⁷, ≤ 1000 iterations), outer Adam (2000 iterations, lr 0.1), λ_θ = 10⁻⁴, ρ_s from 10 to 10⁴, multiplied by 10 every 400 iterations (p. 6). Baselines: unweighted aggregation; FedASL and FedNolowe adapted to one shot by training local models at the server and weighting once, with original-paper hyperparameters (p. 7). Results: learned weights in ideal scenarios zero out unreliable clients, while FedASL flips at 60 % (Table I, p. 7). Dutch/SP/60 %: |SPD| 0.0442 vs 0.1393 baseline, accuracy 77.84 → 76.76 % (p. 7). Law/SP/60 %: |SPD| 0.0403 vs 0.4948 (FedASL) and 0.2574 (FedNolowe) (p. 8). The rivals' higher accuracy is printed (Table III, p. 8). Under EO the accuracy loss is larger, e.g. Law School 88.57 → 81.54 % at 60 % (Table II, p. 8), more than the abstract's "little accuracy loss" suggests. The ρ_s = 0 rows serve as an ablation of the fairness penalty (Table II, p. 8).
  - Transferable: build two scenario families, an idealized one that isolates the mechanism (learned weights, Table I) and a realistic one for headline numbers (pp. 6–8).
  - Transferable: when rivals were designed for a different protocol, adapt them minimally to yours, say exactly how, and keep their published hyperparameters (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against multi-round robust aggregation (Byzantine-tolerant, median-based), anomaly filtering (FedCC, FLRAM), and weighting/trust methods (FLTrust with a root dataset, FedLAW) (p. 3). The root-dataset idea is credited to FLTrust-type work [25, 26, 46] (p. 3). Fairness-aware FL (FairFed, FAIR-FATE, GLOCALFAIR) is criticized as multi-round (p. 3). Builds on the authors' own [3] (pp. 3, 6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: IEEE-conference format; three bullet contributions (p. 2); the limitation sentence is repeated verbatim in §III and §V (pp. 4, 8); the abstract's claim "consistently outperforming other existing methods" (p. 1) is about fairness, while accuracy favours the rivals (Table III, p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Modelling plus an empirical demonstration (p. 2). Limits: the root dataset (pp. 4, 8); a linear model, two datasets, K = 5; no theory; ρ_s on a fixed schedule (p. 6). No future directions are given (p. 8).
