### S026 · Complexity analysis of a trust funnel algorithm for equality constrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2018, 10.1137/16M1108650; read as arXiv 1707.00337v1)
**Read level**: full, arXiv version (pp. 1–35: §§1–6, Algorithms 1–3, all lemma statements and proof prose, Tables 1–2, Appendix A on a TRACE-based phase 2, references). The extracted text breaks many displayed formulas into fragments, so the inequality chains inside the proofs on pp. 8–25 were followed through the lemma statements and proof prose, not symbol by symbol.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Daniel P. Robinson (JHU), Mohammadreza Samadi (Lehigh) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: written right after TRACE ([15], cited p. 2) and in the same DOE Early Career / NSF DMS-1319356 funding (p. 1). It carries the TRACE complexity machinery from unconstrained to equality-constrained problems.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a specific rival: the two-phase ARC method of Cartis–Gould–Toint [8], which gets an ε-feasible point in O(ε^{-3/2}) iterations (p. 1) but "the first phase of the algorithm entirely ignores the objective function" (p. 2). Curtis argues that [8] is a departure from SQP practice, where feasibility and optimality are sought simultaneously (p. 2). Motivation for caring about complexity at all is stated as a belief borrowed from convex optimization (p. 1, quote below). The problem is min f(x) s.t. c(x) = 0 with f, c ∈ C² (eq. 1, Assumption 1, p. 2).
  - Transferable: enter through a rival that has the right theoretical bound but a practical defect you can name in one sentence (here: phase 1 ignores f), and promise the same bound without the defect.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Keep the two-phase structure of [8] but make phase 1 a trust-funnel method (step decomposition into a normal step n_k on v(x) = ½‖c(x)‖² and a tangential step t_k in Null(J_k), §2.1, pp. 3–5) whose acceptance rules borrow TRACE's ρ = decrease/‖s‖³ tests (eqs. 16, 18, pp. 6–7) and its λ/‖n‖ ratio control (Compute Sigma, Algorithm 1 Steps 25–30, p. 8). An iteration is an F-iteration (objective focus) only if six conditions (15a)–(15f) hold, otherwise a V-iteration (p. 6). Changed relative to standard trust funnel [21]: stronger forms (15a), (15c), the new (15e)–(15f) "essential for achieving good complexity properties" (p. 6), a modified funnel update (17) (p. 6), and a tangential step that is computed and then discarded if (14a)–(14c) fail (p. 5). Unchanged: the two-phase architecture and the phase-1 stopping test ‖J_kᵀc_k‖ ≤ ε (Algorithm 1 Step 5, p. 8). For phase 2 the paper lists three options: a trust funnel without complexity claims (used in experiments), [8]'s target-following ARC, or a TRACE-based version of it (Appendix A), plus a reduced-space method when c is affine (pp. 25–26).
  - Transferable: graft a complexity-achieving acceptance rule (decrease measured against ‖s‖³ plus a multiplier/step-norm ratio) onto an existing practical framework instead of designing a new method from scratch.
  - Transferable: allow a computed sub-step to be thrown away after the fact when explicit compatibility tests fail (14a)–(14c), so that the analysis only ever sees steps that satisfy them (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, c twice continuously differentiable (Assumption 1, p. 2); iterates in a compact set and {‖H_k‖} bounded (Assumption 2, p. 7); Lipschitz Hessians of v and f along the path of iterates and trial steps for the complexity part (Assumption 22, p. 18); for phase 2, f bounded above and below on the ε_feas-feasible region and Lipschitz derivatives on the phase-2 path (Assumption 38, p. 30). The phase-1 bound is for ‖J_kᵀc_k‖ ≤ ε, not for ‖c_k‖; it turns into an infeasibility bound only under full row rank with singular values ≥ ζ_min (Corollary 37, p. 25). Proof-driven conditions are admitted as such: (15e)–(15f) are needed for complexity (p. 6). Inferred, not stated: the code sets κ_ht = κ_hs = 10^20 (Table 1, p. 27), so the conditions (14c) and (15f) that the analysis needs are, in practice, almost never binding.
  - Transferable: state openly which acceptance conditions exist only for the complexity proof (here (15e)–(15f), p. 6), so readers can see how far the analysed algorithm is from the one you would deploy.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Partition of the index set into F/V and then successful / contraction / expansion subsets (S^f, C^f, S^v, C^v, E^v; Lemma 3, p. 8). (ii) The funnel radius v^max is a monotone upper bound on v (Lemma 6, pp. 10–11) and decreases by at least κ_ρ(1 − κ_v2)‖s_k‖³ on successful iterations (Lemma 7, pp. 11–12), which turns successful steps into "cubic" decreases. (iii) Finite termination by contradiction on |I| = ∞ (Lemmas 16–20, Theorem 21, pp. 15–18). (iv) Complexity: lower bounds on ‖s_k‖ after successful steps from the λ/‖n‖ ≤ σ-bar ratio (Lemmas 25–26, pp. 20–21), counts of successful iterations (Lemmas 27–28, pp. 21–22), at most one V-expansion between successes (Lemma 13, p. 14), and a constant-factor growth of a critical ratio after each contraction (Lemmas 29–30, 33–35, pp. 22–25). (v) The final bound is a product: K(ε) = 1 + (K_σ(ε) + K^v_Δ)(K^v_C + 1)K^f_C = O(ε^{-3/2}) (Theorem 36, p. 25). (vi) Tolerance coupling for the full method: run phase 1 with ε = ε_feas·ε_inf so that it ends either ε_feas-feasible or at an approximate infeasible stationary point (eq. 49, pp. 25–26). (vii) Appendix A proves that TRACE can replace ARC inside [8]'s phase 2 by re-running [8]'s and [15]'s lemmas one by one (Lemmas 39–46, Theorem 47, pp. 30–35). Many proofs are delegated to the corresponding TRACE lemmas of [15] (e.g., pp. 13, 15–17, 19).
  - Transferable: count successful iterations through a monotone "funnel" quantity whose decrease per success is ≥ const·‖s‖³, then multiply by a bound on unsuccessful iterations between successes (Lemma 7 + Theorem 36, pp. 11–12, 25).
  - Transferable: couple the phase-1 tolerance as ε = ε_feas·ε_inf so one stopping test yields either feasibility or certified infeasible stationarity (pp. 25–26).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab, two modes of the same code: TF (Algorithm 1) and TF-V-only, which is identical except that t_k = 0 always, standing in for a feasibility-only phase 1 "such as in [8]" (pp. 26–27). Same phase 2 for both: a trust funnel based on [21] with the normal step never skipped; all subproblems solved to high accuracy with a Moré–Sorensen-type solver (p. 27). Stopping tests in (50) (p. 27); parameters in Table 1 (p. 27), with constants allowed to differ by place (κ_ρ = 10^{-12} vs 10^{-8}, γ_c = 0.5 vs 10^{-2}, p. 27). Test set: 190 equality-constrained CUTEst problems; removed with reasons and counts: 78 constant/null objective, 13 stopped at x₀, 3 stopped in one iteration by the infeasible-stationarity test, 2 with function-evaluation errors, 1 with tiny steps, plus "all problems on which neither algorithm terminated within one hour", leaving 33 (p. 27). The one-hour filter's count is not given; by subtraction it removed 60 of the remaining 93 problems (my arithmetic). Metrics: #V, #F in each phase, f and ‖g + Jᵀy‖ at the end of phase 1 (Table 2, p. 28). No CPU times, no profiles. The text says TF is "typically" better (p. 28); Table 2 also shows cases where it is not, e.g. BT5 (phase 2: 16 vs 4 iterations) and HS27 (phase-1 f = 87.7 vs 25.4), which go unremarked (p. 28).
  - Transferable: build the "rival" as your own code with the one contested component switched off (t_k ≡ 0), so the comparison isolates exactly the claimed contribution (pp. 26–27).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues Cartis–Gould–Toint [8] (complexity of two-phase ARC) and corrects its practical defect; borrows TRACE [15] and the Gould–Toint trust funnel [21] (pp. 2–3, 28). It calls the method "a next step" toward practical methods with good complexity (pp. 2, 28) and claims parity with [8], not dominance (Abstract; p. 28). Criticism of [8] is direct ("Unfortunately, however", Abstract, p. 2). Wider SQP literature is cited en bloc [3, 13, 14, 18, 19, 20, 26, 29] (p. 1), including his own inexact SQP [3] (p. 29).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is in the abstract: "the same worst-case iteration complexity is achieved, but with a first phase that also accounts for improvements in the objective function" (Abstract, p. 1). Each lemma is preceded by a one-sentence plain-language preview ("The next lemma reveals…", e.g., pp. 15, 16, 19). The paper is theorem-heavy (47 numbered results, pp. 7–35) with one experiment table. Hedging is moderate: "typically requires fewer iterations" (Abstract), "We do not claim worst-case iteration complexity guarantees for such a method" (p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: theoretical (O(ε^{-3/2}) for an objective-aware phase 1, Theorem 36, p. 25; TRACE-based phase 2, Appendix A) plus a small experiment. Limits stated: complexity holds for ‖Jᵀc‖, not ‖c‖, without full rank (Corollary 37, p. 25); the phase 2 actually used in experiments has no complexity guarantee (p. 26). Limits not stated: the tested configuration is therefore not the one analysed; there is no comparison with an SQP code or with [8] itself; the one-hour filter is outcome-based. No future work is given in the conclusion (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ⚠ variant: the paper keeps a two-phase architecture for the sake of complexity (pp. 1–2, 25) but moves part of Method 2 into phase 1. Each iteration decides between objective focus (F) and feasibility focus (V) through monitored conditions (15) (p. 6), and the funnel radius v^max is the steered parameter (eqs. 17, 19, pp. 6–7). This sits uneasily with Taste 3 ("one algorithm with monitored transitions beats a two-phase design"): here the phase switch remains and only the inside of phase 1 is steered.
- Method 4 (Audit the yardstick) ✗ contradiction (with his later stance): the opening premise is that complexity-minded design helped practice "at least for convex optimization" (p. 1), and complexity parity with [8] is the organizing goal (Abstract). His later warning that better complexity has yet to mean better performance for nonconvex problems (SKILL.md Taste, warning 3, ECOM 2021) is not applied here. The practical claim is checked only against an ablated copy of itself (pp. 26–28), and the complexity-only conditions are made almost inactive in code (Table 1, p. 27; inference).
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✅ evidence (Step 2–3): the feasibility-only rival is built inside the same code by switching off one component, with identical phase 2 and subproblem accuracy (pp. 26–27). ⚠ Missing: the incumbent's wins (BT5, HS27, p. 28) are not named in the text.
- Heuristic 7 (name removed problems with reasons) ✅/⚠: each removal class is named and counted (p. 27), but the largest cut, "neither algorithm terminated within one hour", is outcome-based and its count is left to the reader (p. 27).
- Heuristic 10 (keep the skeleton, replace the broken component) ✅ evidence: TRACE's acceptance and ratio machinery is carried over almost verbatim into a trust-funnel phase 1, and TRACE replaces ARC in [8]'s phase 2 (pp. 6–7, 30–35).
- new-pattern candidate: graft-a-complexity-rule — keep the practical framework (trust funnel) and add only the acceptance and ratio tests that carry the complexity proof, citing the source lemmas one by one (pp. 6, 13–19, 30–35).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn successful steps into countable progress through a monotone bound (the funnel radius) whose decrease per success is ≥ κ‖s‖³, then bound unsuccessful iterations between successes by a constant (Lemmas 7, 13, 30, 35; Theorem 36, pp. 11–12, 14, 22–25).
- Choose phase-1 tolerance ε = ε_feas·ε_inf so the same stopping test certifies either approximate feasibility or approximate infeasible stationarity (pp. 25–26).
- Test the contribution with a one-switch ablation of your own code (tangential step forced to zero), holding phase 2 and subproblem accuracy fixed (pp. 26–27).

**Quotes**:

- "However, we find this idea to be unsatisfactory since such an approach would entirely ignore the objective function." (p. 3)

### S027 · A sequential algorithm for solving nonlinear optimization problems with chance constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2018, 10.1137/16M109003X)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–29, published version: §§1–6, Algorithm 3.1, all lemma and theorem proofs in §4, Tables 1–4, Fig. 1 caption (figure itself not in the text layer), footnote, references).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Andreas Wächter (Northwestern IEMS), Victor M. Zavala (UW-Madison Chemical and Biological Engineering) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received August 2016, published March 2018 (p. 1); dedicated to Roger Fletcher (p. 1). It carries the Sℓ1QP penalty trust-region skeleton from smooth NLP into a nonconvex combinatorial feasible region.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Problem (P): min f(x) s.t. |{i ∈ I : c_i(x) ≤ 0}| ≥ M (p. 1), arising as the sample average approximation of a chance-constrained problem with M = ⌈(1 − α)N⌉ (p. 3), and in load shedding and soft constraints in MPC (p. 3). Entry points: existing CCP methods target global minimizers via convex approximations (conservative) or combinatorial methods that need problem structure (pp. 2–3); Luedtke et al.'s MILP methods are linear only (p. 3); MPCC reformulations "might be attracted to spurious solutions" (p. 4). The paper accepts local search on purpose: it can only prove that "a stationarity measure will vanish, not necessarily that a global minimizer will be found" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when the global problem is intractable, state explicitly that you trade global optimality for a local method with a stationarity guarantee, and then devote a subsection plus experiments to how bad the local minimizers can get (§4.2, §5.2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A new exact penalty φ(x; ρ) = ρf(x) + ⟨⟨c(x)⟩⟩_M, where ⟨⟨w⟩⟩_M is the smallest ℓ1 violation over all scenario selections S of size M (eq. 1, p. 4), equal to the minimum over S of standard ℓ1 penalties φ_S (eq. 6, p. 5). Each step solves a trust-region subproblem that minimizes, over a subset S_{M,k} of scenario selections, a piecewise-quadratic model; this is a QP with a linear cardinality constraint, solved as a big-M MIQP (eqs. 14, 18, pp. 8–9; eqs. 32–33, pp. 17–18). Changed relative to Sℓ1QP: the violation measure and hence a subproblem with binary variables. Unchanged: trust-region ratio test and radius update, with a reset δ_reset after each success (Algorithm 3.1, p. 9). Key design point: S_{M,k} needs to include only the ε-critical selections S_M(x_k; ε) (eq. 7, p. 6; p. 8), which keeps the subproblem small. With ε = 0 the method could ignore a constraint that becomes critical at the limit point (pp. 9–10). Setting M = N recovers standard Sℓ1QP (Remark 8, p. 11).
  - Transferable: build a nonsmooth combinatorial merit function as a pointwise minimum of classical exact penalties, so that classical theory applies selection by selection (eqs. 6, 8; Lemma 1, pp. 5–7).
  - Transferable: give the subproblem only the "ε-critical" part of the combinatorial structure that the convergence proof needs, and expose the rest as a cost-quality knob (ε in theory, γ in code; pp. 8–10, 19–20).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f and c_i Lipschitz with Lipschitz first derivatives on a bounded convex set containing the iterates, and {H_k} bounded (Assumption 4, p. 10); H_k positive semidefinite so each model q_{S,k} is convex (p. 8); fixed ρ and ε (p. 8); for Lemma 3, a constraint qualification for each critical selection, e.g. LICQ (pp. 7–8). Equal-probability scenarios are assumed for the SAA (p. 3). Admitted simplification: ρ is fixed, though "In practice, a suitable value is often not known a priori" (p. 26).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Lemma 1: x* is a local minimizer of φ iff it is a local minimizer of φ_S for all critical S (pp. 6–7), proved by a finiteness-of-selections and continuity argument. (ii) Lemmas 2–3: exact-penalty correspondence with (P) and with the violation measure, in the style of Han–Mangasarian (pp. 7–8). (iii) Criticality measure χ(x) = max over S ∈ S_M(x; 0) of χ_S(x), with χ_S the classical ℓ1 linear-model reduction in the unit ball (eqs. 19–20, Lemma 5, pp. 10–11). (iv) Global convergence Theorem 6 (p. 11), following the SLQP analysis of Byrd–Gould–Nocedal–Waltz [12]: Cauchy point per selection (Lemmas 10–11, pp. 12–13), a neighbourhood in which the radius stays above δ_min thanks to δ_reset (Lemma 13, pp. 14–15), and a summation contradiction (pp. 15–16). The paper stresses that the analysis "is not straightforward" because the models are nonconvex (p. 10). (v) §4.2: a local argument that a better minimizer at distance δ will be found when (ρL_f + L_c + H_max)δ² is small, and global optimality when everything is linear and the radius is large (pp. 16–17).
  - Transferable: when the merit function is a minimum over finitely many classical merit functions, define stationarity as the max of the classical stationarity measures over the active (critical) members (eq. 20, p. 11).
  - Transferable: add a radius reset δ_reset after each successful step; it is what makes the "radius bounded below near a non-stationary point" lemma go through (Lemma 13, pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab R2015b prototype, Cplex 12.6.2 for the MIQP with a fixed big-M (pp. 17–18), Hessian of the Lagrangian with inertia correction from Ipopt's Algorithm IC (pp. 18–19). The Cplex tuning is disclosed in detail (tolerances, numerical emphasis, interior-point root node because Cplex otherwise declared feasible problems infeasible, MipStart, 300 s limit, p. 19). Experiment 1: a 2-D nonconvex example with N = 5,000 scenarios, nine starting points including a deliberately bad local minimizer x_bad, grid over ρ ∈ {1, 0.1, 0.01} and γ ∈ [0.001, 1] (Tables 1–2, pp. 20–21; Fig. 1, p. 22). Experiment 2: a nonlinear cash-flow problem with 75 investment options and 20 periods, five random instances, N from 100 to 5,000, γ from 0.001 to 1, started from the robust solution computed by Ipopt (pp. 23–24). It reports OK counts, geometric means of iterations, |C_k|, scenario-set changes and time, and relative improvement over the robust solution averaged only over instances solved for all γ (Table 3, p. 25; Table 4, p. 26). Failures are reported and blamed on the subproblem solver (pp. 19, 24). A referee-requested run of BARON, SCIP and Couenne via NEOS is reported in one footnote without numbers (p. 26).
  - Transferable: plant a known bad starting point (a poor local minimizer) in a small example and tabulate, over a grid of the two parameters that should matter, whether the method escapes it (Tables 1–2, pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable: report an improvement relative to a natural conservative baseline (here the robust counterpart, Table 4), averaged only over instances solved under every setting, and state that restriction (p. 24).
- **D6 Positioning**: Presented as extending Luedtke et al.'s linear chance-constrained ideas to nonlinear problems (p. 2) and as an Sℓ1QP method in Fletcher's tradition (pp. 8, 11). Rivals are characterized, not run: convex approximations are "conservative" (p. 3), MINLP "may be intractable" (pp. 3–4), and MPCC regularization can stall at spurious points (p. 4). The global-solver comparison exists only in the footnote (p. 26). Scenario sampling for the CCP is set aside as future work with a pointer to [28] (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution is announced in the first sentence of §1 (p. 1). The core claims are hedged with "we claim" and "we stress" (pp. 3, 10, 26), and the scope limit (stationarity only) is in the introduction, not hidden in the conclusion (p. 3). Theory is followed by a non-proof subsection on why poor minimizers are unlikely (§4.2, pp. 16–17), which the experiments then test. Failures are framed as not the algorithm's fault (p. 24, quote below).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and theoretical (new penalty, Algorithm 3.1, Theorem 6) with a proof-of-concept implementation (pp. 17, 26). Limits stated concretely: ρ is fixed and has to be updated in practice; unlike standard NLP, a too-small ρ hurts solution quality, not only speed (p. 26); the big-M MIQP via Cplex is slow and should be replaced by Luedtke's branch-and-cut decomposition (pp. 18, 26–27); scenario sampling for CCP is left open (p. 4). Future directions: recourse via implicit formulations z_i ⇒ x ∈ P_i (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ⚠ variant: the penalty parameter that Method 2 would steer is fixed (p. 8), and the conclusion names a steering rule as the missing piece (p. 26). The paper adds a new reason why steering is harder here: ρ controls which local minimizer is reached, not only speed (Table 2, pp. 20–21; p. 26).
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ⚠ variant: the outer convergence proof dictates the minimum content of the inner subproblem (all ε-critical selections, pp. 8–10), and everything beyond that is a cost-quality trade-off (γ, pp. 19–20, 24–25). Inexactness enters only through Cplex time limits, with the incumbent used as the step (pp. 19, 26), which is not covered by Theorem 6.
- Method 3 (Break it on purpose) ⚠ variant: a hard case is built on purpose (starting from the bad local minimizer x_bad, p. 20) to test the claim of §4.2, but on a 2-D toy rather than by transforming a standard test set.
- Heuristic 10 (keep the skeleton, replace the broken component) ✅ evidence: the Sℓ1QP trust-region skeleton and the SLQP convergence proof [12] are kept, only the violation measure and the subproblem change, and M = N recovers the classical method (pp. 8, 10–11).
- Heuristic 3 (pick a failure users hit) ✅ evidence: the application pull (chance constraints in power, finance, water, chemical processes, load shedding) is set out before the algorithm (pp. 2–3).
- new-pattern candidate: nest the classical method — build the new method so that a parameter value (here M = N) gives back the classical algorithm and its known convergence result (Remark 8, p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define a combinatorial exact penalty as the minimum over scenario selections of ℓ1 penalties and prove local-minimizer equivalence selection by selection (eqs. 1, 6, 8; Lemma 1, pp. 4–7).
- Keep the subproblem tractable by including only ε-critical selections, with a scale-free fraction γ as the implementation knob, and tabulate quality against time over γ (pp. 19–20, Table 3 p. 25).
- Initialize a nonconvex combinatorial local search at the robust (all-constraints) solution and its multipliers, and report improvement relative to it (pp. 23–24).

**Quotes**:
- "We emphasize that these outcomes do not represent a failure of the proposed algorithm, but rather are a consequence of the inefficient manner in which we are solving the subproblems" (p. 24)
- "This is in contrast to standard nonlinear optimization, where a small value usually affects only the convergence speed, not the quality of the solution." (p. 26)

### S035 · Concise complexity analyses for trust region methods (Optimization Letters 2018, 10.1007/S11590-018-1286-2; read as arXiv 1802.07843v1)
**Read level**: full, arXiv version (pp. 1–10: §1, §§2.1–2.5 with all proofs, §3, references).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Zachary Lubberts, Daniel P. Robinson (both JHU Applied Mathematics and Statistics, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a short note after TRACE ([3], cited p. 8) and the inexact regularized Newton framework ([4], p. 10); it reduces the trust-region complexity story to its minimum.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Trust-region complexity is already covered by general frameworks [5, 6, 8] (Fan–Yuan, Grapiglia–Yuan–Yuan, Toint) (p. 2). The entry is pedagogical and structural: give the simplest analysis that shows "the essential aspects needed to obtain a first-order complexity bound" (p. 2), add a second-order variant, and contrast with a strategy that has better complexity but practical drawbacks (p. 2). Problem: min f(x), f ∈ C² with Lipschitz Hessian, bounded below (Assumption 1.1, p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a result already exists inside a general framework, write the minimal special case whose proof isolates the one mechanism that matters, and say so explicitly (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Tie the radius to the current stationarity measure: δ_k = γ_k‖g_k‖ (Update 2.7, p. 6), or δ_k = γ_k|(λ_k)₋| when negative curvature dominates in the sense ‖g_k‖² < |(λ_k)₋|³ (Update 2.10, p. 7). Only the coefficient γ_k is adapted: kept in [γ_low, γ_high] after success and multiplied by γ_c after failure (Algorithm 1, p. 3). Steps need only Cauchy decrease along −g_k or the leftmost eigenvector (eqs. 2.2–2.3, p. 3). The paper argues that Update 2.7 coincides with the traditional radius update except in two extreme scenarios, using typical values γ_e = 2, γ_low ≈ 10^{-8}, γ_high ≈ 10^8 (p. 7). §2.5 analyses a fixed radius √ε/β with exact subproblem solutions and every step accepted, giving O(ε^{-3/2}) (pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable: make the trust-region radius proportional to the stationarity measure you want to drive to zero (‖g‖, or |λ₋| for second order) and adapt only the proportionality constant (Updates 2.7, 2.10, pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1.1 (C², L-Lipschitz Hessian, bounded below, p. 2) and Assumption 2.1 (‖H_k‖ ≤ κ along the iterates, p. 4). Exact leftmost eigenvalue λ_k and eigenvector u_k are used in the radius rule and Cauchy point (pp. 2–3); their cost is not discussed. The fixed-radius method needs β = L/2 and exact subproblem solutions (p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Cauchy decrease is proportional to ‖g_k‖² or |(λ_k)₋|³ because the radius is (Lemma 2.2, p. 4). (ii) Model error is bounded by κγ_k²‖g_k‖² or (L/6)γ_k³|(λ_k)₋|³ (Lemma 2.3, p. 5). (iii) An induction gives a uniform lower bound γ_min on γ_k, because a small γ_k forces |ρ_k − 1| ≤ 1 − η (Lemma 2.4, p. 5). (iv) Consecutive unsuccessful iterations are at most ⌈log_{γ_c}(γ_min/γ_high)⌉, independently of the radius rule (Lemma 2.6, p. 6). (v) Telescoping f₀ − f_inf over successful iterations counts them as O(ε_g^{-2}) or O(max{ε_g^{-2}, ε_H^{-3}}); multiplying gives Theorems 2.9 and 2.12 (pp. 6–8). (vi) For the fixed radius, a two-case split on the trust-region multiplier ξ_k ≤ √ε or > √ε (pp. 8–9), following Yinyu Ye's lecture notes (p. 8). (vii) Byproduct: lim ‖g_k‖ = 0 directly, without the usual liminf-first argument, because decrease on every success is proportional to ‖g_k‖² (p. 10).
  - Transferable: the three-lemma complexity template (decrease ∝ measure^p on successes; a uniform lower bound on the radius coefficient by induction; a log bound on consecutive failures) then telescoping (Lemmas 2.2–2.8, pp. 4–6).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none (n/a); the paper is analysis only (pp. 1–10).
- **D6 Positioning**: Said openly to be a special case of [5, 6, 8] with a simpler analysis (pp. 2, 10). It cites ARC [1] and his own TRACE [3] and inexact regularized Newton framework [4] as the O(ε^{-3/2}) methods (pp. 8, 10), and credits Ye's lecture notes for the fixed-radius argument (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Opens with "let us immediately discuss the contributions of this paper" (p. 1). Each strategy is followed by a practical discussion: compatibility with the traditional update (p. 7), and four concrete costs of the better-complexity strategy (p. 9). The conclusion states an open question plainly (p. 10). Minor slips: the conclusion cites "Theorem 2.11" for what is Lemma 2.11 (p. 10), and "K ⊆ K_H" appears in the proof of Lemma 2.11 without K being defined (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theoretical and expository. Limits stated concretely: no analysis for the traditional radius update (p. 10); the O(ε^{-3/2}) fixed-radius strategy needs L, exact subproblems and ε chosen in advance, and takes tiny steps (p. 9). Not discussed: the cost of computing λ_k and u_k exactly in the second-order rule (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (Audit the yardstick) ✅ evidence: §2.5 asks what the better complexity class costs and lists four practical costs, concluding that the O(ε^{-3/2}) method "would likely lead to very poor behavior in practice" compared with the O(ε^{-2}) strategies (p. 9). This is an early (2018) written instance of Taste warning 3 (worst-case complexity sold as practical gain), and it contrasts with S026's premise (S026 p. 1).
- Signature work TRACE ✅ context: TRACE [3] is cited as the trust-region method with O(ε^{-3/2}) complexity (p. 8), and the conclusion's open question (complexity of the traditional radius update, p. 10) marks the gap TRACE closed by changing acceptance and radius rules.
- new-pattern candidate: minimal-instance analysis — strip a known framework down to the simplest instance that still yields the bound, to expose "the essential algorithm components" (pp. 2, 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use the three-lemma template (decrease ∝ measure^p on success; induction lower bound on the radius coefficient; log bound on consecutive failures) and telescope f₀ − f_inf to get an iteration bound in under two pages (pp. 4–8).

### S046 · R-Linear Convergence of Limited Memory Steepest Descent (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2018, 10.1093/imanum/drx016; read as COR@L TR 16T-010 / arXiv 1610.03831v1)
**Read level**: full, technical-report version (pp. 1–19: cover page, §§1–5 with all proofs, Table 1, figure captions for Figs. 1–10 (the plots themselves are not in the text layer), references).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Wei Guo (Lehigh ISE, p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: dedicated to Roger Fletcher and Jonathan Borwein (p. 2); same DOE Early Career and NSF funding as S026 (p. 2). An analysis paper on a first-order method (Fletcher's LMSD), outside his constrained-optimization mainline.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The BB method is known to converge R-linearly on strongly convex quadratics (Dai–Liao [2]), and Fletcher's LMSD [3] is known to converge, "However, to the best of our knowledge, the convergence rate of the method for m > 1 has not yet been analyzed" (p. 3). The gap is closed for strongly convex quadratics, argued to also describe behaviour near a strong local minimizer of a smooth function (p. 2). Problem: min ½xᵀAx − bᵀx with A ≻ 0 having r distinct eigenvalues (eq. 1, Assumption 2.1, p. 4).
  - Transferable: enter through a known result for the m = 1 case of a method family and generalize the proof to every value of the parameter (pp. 3, 7).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: No new algorithm: Algorithm LMSD is restated (p. 5), with stepsizes the reciprocals of eigenvalues of T_k = Q_kᵀAQ_k computed without products with A via a partially extended Cholesky factorization (eqs. 4–7, pp. 4–5). Design remarks: order the Ritz values decreasingly so stepsizes increase within a cycle (p. 5); when G_k loses rank, drop older columns (Remark 2.2, p. 5), and base that dropping on the bound ‖R_k^{-1}‖ ≤ ρ‖g_{k,1}‖^{-1}, noting that "These norms are easily computed in an implementation" (pp. 5–6, 8). A finite-termination result shows that the method stops once all reciprocal eigenvalues have been used as stepsizes (Theorem 2.4, Remark 2.5, pp. 6–7).
  - Transferable: turn an analysis assumption into a run-time test the code can check, and advocate that test as the practical safeguard (Assumption 3.4(ii) → column-dropping rule, pp. 5–6, 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Strongly convex quadratic (Assumption 2.1, p. 4); Assumption 3.4: no finite termination and G_k of full column rank with ‖R_k^{-1}‖ ≤ ρ‖g_{k,1}‖^{-1} (p. 8). The full-rank assumption is adopted because the variable-length-cycle analysis "would be extremely cumbersome" (pp. 5–6); the authors conjecture the R-linear rate also holds with column dropping (p. 5). The bound holds with ρ = 1 when m = 1 (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Write each gradient in A's eigenbasis, g_{k,j} = Σ d_{k,j,i}q_i, with the recursion d_{k,j+1,i} = (1 − α_{k,j}λ_i)d_{k,j,i} (Lemma 2.3, p. 6). (ii) Cauchy interlacing bounds the Ritz values θ_{k,j} ∈ [λ_{m+1−j}, λ_{n+1−j}] (Lemma 3.5, p. 8). (iii) θ_{k,j} = c_{k,j}ᵀΛc_{k,j} with ‖c_{k,j}‖ = 1, linking Ritz values to the weights (Lemma 3.6, p. 8). (iv) Per-component contraction constants δ_{j,i} and Δ_i (Lemma 3.7, p. 9); Δ₁ < 1 always, so the first component vanishes Q-linearly (Lemma 3.8, pp. 9–10). (v) Induction on p: if the first p weights are small, weight p + 1 becomes small within a k-independent number of cycles and stays small (Lemmas 3.9–3.10, pp. 10–13). (vi) A k-independent K with ‖g_{k+K,1}‖ ≤ ½‖g_{k,1}‖ (Lemma 3.11, p. 13), then R-linear constants c₁ = 2Δ^{K−1}, c₂ = 2^{−1/K} (Theorem 3.12, pp. 13–14), mirroring Dai–Liao's proof for m = 1 ("We provide it in full for completeness", p. 13).
  - Transferable: to extend an m = 1 proof to a block or cycle method, keep its skeleton (componentwise weights in the eigenbasis, induction over components, halving in K steps), and add one lemma that ties the new block quantities (Ritz values) to the same weights (Lemma 3.6, p. 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab, five 100-dimensional quadratics designed by spectrum: eigenvalues in [1, 1.9] (λ_n < 2λ₁, Q-linear case), even in [1, 100], five clusters, all but one near λ₁, all but one near λ_n (Table 1, pp. 14–15). Runs with m = 1 and m = 5, ε = 10^{-8}, random initial stepsizes, reporting outer and inner iterations (Table 1, p. 15). Figures plot the proof's own quantities: the weights |d_{k,j,i}| for i ∈ {1, 2, 50, 100} and the constants Δ_i, coloured by whether they are < 1 (pp. 15–19). The stated goal is illustration only, not tuning m (p. 14).
  - Transferable: design test problems by the property the theory depends on (here the spectrum), and plot the quantities the proof tracks (weights, contraction constants), not only the convergence curve (pp. 14–19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Fletcher [3] and Dai–Liao [2], with Raydan [5] (pp. 2–3, 15). Stated as an extension ("Our analysis builds upon the analyses in [3] and [2]", p. 3). Five references only (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Up front, the paper limits what the theory claims about practice (p. 3, quote below). The introduction promises a closing discussion of why practice beats theory, referencing a finite-termination result (p. 3), but §5 contains only a summary (p. 19); the finite-termination material sits in §2.3 (pp. 6–7). Each proof borrowed from the m = 1 case is flagged as such (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theoretical (R-linear rate for any history length m, Theorem 3.12, p. 13). Limits stated: quadratics only; full-rank G_k assumed although column dropping is advocated in practice (pp. 5–6, 8); the theory does not explain the often better observed Q-linear behaviour (p. 3); the choice of m is outside scope (p. 14). No future work is listed (p. 19).

**Method links**:
