# Batch c11 (core) · Frank E. Curtis

Batch intent: set two 2017 works side by side to see how Curtis's craft carries across two very different outputs: an expository tutorial for an OR audience on optimization for supervised learning (with Scheinberg), and a heavy convergence paper that moves an equality-constrained trust-funnel method into an interior-point, matrix-free setting (with Gould, Robinson and Toint). The question is which Methods (inner solve serving the outer solver; steering instead of switching; auditing the yardstick) appear in each, and what is missing when there are no experiments.

### S011 · Optimization methods for supervised machine learning: From linear models to deep learning (Leading developments from INFORMS communities 2017, 10.1287/educ.2017.0168; arXiv:1706.10207)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v1, pp. 1–22 all text including Tables 1–3 and Algorithms 1–3; figures exist only as captions and stray labels in the text layer; reference list pp. 22–27 skimmed).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Katya Scheinberg (both Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: written while the Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal review was still an arXiv report (cited as "Technical Report 1606.04838", p. 22); an expository bridge from deterministic NLP to ML for the INFORMS TutORials audience, in the period when Curtis's own line was turning to stochastic quasi-Newton and stochastic methods (self-citation [19], "A self-correcting variable-metric algorithm for stochastic optimization", p. 23). Which author wrote which part is not stated in the text.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a community gap rather than a technical gap: barriers "of differing terminology, goals, and avenues for collaboration" between statistics, CS and OR "have led to duplications of efforts" (p. 1). The stated aim is an overview of "key issues and research questions" for readers who know optimization but not ML (p. 1). The problem statement is built from the learning goal outward: maximize the probability of a correct prediction (1)–(2) (pp. 3–4), then make it tractable step by step.
  - Transferable: when writing for a neighbouring community, open with the concrete cost of the gap (duplicated effort) and promise a translation, not a survey.
- **D2 Core idea and design**: The organizing idea is the chain of approximations from the learning goal to a solvable problem: indicator objective (2) → binary-classification form (3)–(4) with the 01-loss → logistic surrogate (5)–(7) derived from the logit → empirical risk (8) because P is unknown → regularized problem (11) to control complexity (pp. 3–7). The algorithmic half then follows a fixed ladder for convex logistic regression: gradient descent with its O(nd) per-iteration cost (pp. 8–9), SGD with Robbins–Monro step sizes (pp. 9–10), SGD with momentum (Algorithm 1, p. 11), variance reduction (SVRG as Algorithm 2, SARAH, growing mini-batches; pp. 11–12), and second-order and quasi-Newton methods with four stochastic BFGS variants differing in how y_k is defined (pp. 13–15). The deep-learning section repeats the ladder for nonconvex training: formulation (24) and CNN filters (pp. 16–18), SGD (pp. 19–20), Hessian-free Newton-CG / Gauss–Newton / trust-region CG (pp. 20–21), subsampled Hessians and random-model trust-region analysis (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: organize a tutorial on methods by one fixed comparison axis (here: what changes in the step formula and what that costs per iteration) and walk every method along it, so the reader sees each method as one edit to the previous one.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Rates are stated under µ-strong convexity with L-Lipschitz gradient, or mere convexity (pp. 8–10); for (12) with λ > 0, µ ≥ λ and L ≤ max_i ‖x_i‖² (p. 9). The SGD-superiority argument assumes one can "sample indefinitely" from P (pp. 5, 10), and the authors flag that this assumption fails when the sample set is fixed (p. 11). The quasi-Newton SGD guarantee assumes two-sided bounds on E[B_k⁻¹∇_{S_k}F(w_k)] (p. 15), with the caveat that stochastic gradients "might be correlated with the Hessian approximations" (p. 16). DNN training is "highly nonconvex with numerous saddle points and local minimizers" (pp. 18–19).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No new theorems; the devices are expository, mostly cited. (i) Error decomposition (9) and the generic learning bound (10) (p. 6), reused in (17)–(18) to split ε between estimation error and optimization accuracy (ε̂ ≤ ε/2) and to conclude that any method touching all n samples per iteration costs at least O(1/ε²) per iteration once n ≥ O(ε⁻²), so SGD wins "at least in theory" (p. 10). (ii) An unbounded-ray argument showing the regularizer is needed for (12) to have a minimizer (p. 8). (iii) A VC-dimension illustration with 3 points in R² and 8 labelings (Fig. 2, pp. 6–7). (iv) The unbiasedness check E[v_t] = ∇F(w_t) for SVRG (19) and its failure for SARAH (20) (p. 12). (v) Complexity tables for GD, SGD, SVRG, SAG/SAGA, SARAH (Tables 2–3, p. 13). (vi) The Dennis–Moré condition and s_kᵀy_k > 0 as the crux of BFGS superlinear convergence (p. 14). (vii) Gauss–Newton matrix → true Hessian only under zero loss, otherwise convergence to a saddle point is possible (pp. 20–21). (viii) In the random-model framework the sample size is tied to the step or trust-region radius (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: to argue which optimizer is right for a statistical problem, write total error = estimation error + optimization error, give each half of the budget, and compare methods by the cost to reach the optimization half (eqs. (17)–(18), p. 10).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a — none run. The text cites others' evidence: a comparison of trust-region CG methods for DNNs ([88], 20–30 Hessian-vector products per iteration, different solutions found; p. 21) and the random-label overfitting result ([90], p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Leans heavily on Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal [11] for SG theory (pp. 10, 19) and Bousquet–Bottou [12] for the large-scale trade-off (p. 10); on the Scheinberg-side random-model line [3, 15, 16, 34] and SARAH [61] (pp. 12, 21–22); and on Curtis's own self-correcting stochastic BFGS [19] as one of four quasi-Newton variants (p. 15), presented neutrally beside oBFGS, multi-batch L-BFGS and sampled-Hessian y_k. Specialized QP solvers for hinge and least-squares losses are explicitly left out as "too specialized" (p. 5). Evaluation is mild and even-handed: each variant gets its weakness (instability with small Hessian batches, p. 15; noise-dominated slowdown of oBFGS, p. 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Audience devices first: a glossary table with an "a.k.a." column (Table 1, p. 2), a running business example (Product A and Factor X, Fig. 1, pp. 2–3), and algorithms in boxed pseudocode (Algorithms 1–3, pp. 11–14). Claims about theory are hedged and then undercut on purpose: "at least in theory" SGD is superior (p. 10), followed at once by "In practice, however, standard SGD is not necessarily the most effective approach" (p. 10) and by the remark that the SGD argument "should only be used as a theoretical guideline" (p. 11). Open questions are named in plain words ("remains somewhat of a mystery", p. 19; "It remains an open question", p. 20). There is no conclusion section: §3.4 runs straight into the references (p. 22). Minor typos survive in v1 ("one funds", p. 9; "definte", p. 15).
  - Transferable: after stating a worst-case or asymptotic advantage, put the practical caveat in the next sentence, and say which assumption in the theory fails in the application (here: sampling indefinitely vs a fixed sample set, pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: exposition and agenda-setting (no new algorithm, theorem or experiment). Open directions named: stochastic momentum lacks guarantees (p. 11); no practical second-order method yet provably achieves its observed practical gains (p. 15); designing methods that find good minimizers and avoid saddle points in DNNs (p. 20); "Additional experimentation and comparisons are needed" for negative-curvature trust-region methods (p. 21); subsampled trust-region ideas used "without theoretical justification" (p. 21). Limits (from the text): the logistic-regression section treats mainly the smooth ℓ2 case (p. 8); no numerical illustration of any claim.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (Audit the yardstick) ✅ evidence: the complexity argument for SGD is stated and then demoted because one of its assumptions (sampling indefinitely from P) fails for a fixed data set (pp. 10–11); learning bounds such as (10) "are not useful" for DNNs because the class complexity exceeds n (p. 20); a second-order method's practical gains are not yet reflected in any proved result (p. 15).
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ⚠ variant: in the random-model trust-region framework the accuracy of the sampled gradient and Hessian is dictated by what the globalization needs, with the size of |S_k| "connected to the trust region radius" (pp. 21–22). Here the inexact object is a sampled oracle, not an iterative linear solver, and the line is credited to Scheinberg-side papers [3, 34].
- Taste 5 ("The workhorse is a target, not an endpoint") ✅ evidence: SGD is "very sensitive to parameter choices" and the wrong choice "can severely slow progress or cause the algorithm to stall completely" (p. 11); the tutorial then spends its algorithmic half on alternatives to SGD (pp. 11–22).
- new-pattern candidate: error-budget split — divide the target accuracy between statistical and optimization error and judge algorithms by the cost of the optimization share (eqs. (17)–(18), p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: curvature-pair hygiene — in stochastic quasi-Newton, define y_k so that s_kᵀy_k stays bounded away from zero (same mini-batch, overlapping batches, damping, sampled-Hessian products; p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive the problem you will optimize from the application's goal through named approximations (indicator → surrogate loss → empirical risk → regularized risk), each with its reason (pp. 3–7).
- Compare stochastic and deterministic methods with the error-budget argument (17)–(18), then state which assumption the argument needs and when it fails (pp. 10–11).
- When surveying variants of one method (stochastic BFGS), describe each by the single quantity it redefines (y_k) and give the failure mode that motivated it (pp. 14–15).
- For a cross-community tutorial, put a glossary with an "a.k.a." column first (Table 1, p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "From this discussion, we can conclude that, at least in theory, SGD is a superior algorithm for large-scale (i.e., large n) ML applications." (p. 10)
- "Unfortunately, despite practical improvements that have been observed, there has yet to be a practical second-order method that provably achieves such improvements in theory." (p. 15)

### S039 · An interior-point trust-funnel algorithm for nonlinear optimization (Mathematical Programming 2017, 10.1007/S10107-016-1003-9)
**Read level**: full, TR version (Report NAXYS-02-2014, 6 February 2014, University of Namur repository "pre-print" copy; file pp. 3–44 text, i.e. §§1–5 with all lemmas and proofs; displayed inequality chains inside proofs followed for structure, not re-derived; references pp. 44–46 skimmed). The published 2017 version was not read; differences between the TR and the published version are unknown.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Nicholas I. M. Gould (STFC-RAL), Daniel P. Robinson (Johns Hopkins), Philippe L. Toint (Namur) (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: Curtis supported by DOE DE-SC0010615 and NSF DMS-1016291 (p. 3); the work joins his inexact / matrix-free SQP line (Byrd–Curtis–Nocedal [2]; Curtis–Schenk–Wächter inexact IPM [6], p. 44) with Gould–Toint's trust-funnel (no penalty, no filter) and targets the GALAHAD library (p. 44). Received-date information is not in this version.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap between two existing pieces. Gould–Toint's trust-funnel solves equality-constrained problems without a merit function or filter ([24], "then corrected in [23]" as stated on p. 4), and barrier-SQP methods reduce inequality-constrained problems to a sequence of equality-constrained barrier subproblems (Byrd et al. [3, 4], p. 4). But a naive implementation of the trust-funnel inside an interior-point method may leave convergence guarantees "elusive", because that method "does not innately possess the mechanisms necessary to avoid the boundary of the feasible region" (p. 4). A second driver is scale: second-derivative SQP methods are promising on small and medium problems, "but their effectiveness on large problems has not yet been confirmed" (p. 4). Problem form: min f(x) s.t. c(x) ≤ 0 (NP), solved via slacks as (NPs) (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable: enter by composing two published frameworks and naming the precise mechanism the composition lacks (here: keeping iterates strictly interior), so the contribution is the missing mechanism plus the proof that it suffices.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Solve each barrier subproblem (1.1) with a trust-funnel method: a monotonically shrinking bound v_k^max on the violation v = ‖c(x, s)‖ (the funnel, (2.2), pp. 5–6) replaces any merit function or filter. Each step is normal + tangential (p. 6). The normal step approximately minimizes a Gauss–Newton model of v in a scaled trust region ‖P_k⁻¹n‖ ≤ δ^v with a fraction-to-the-boundary constraint (2.5)–(2.6) (p. 6), requiring only Cauchy decrease along the scaled steepest-descent direction and a range-space condition that Krylov methods give automatically ((2.9)–(2.13), p. 7). Multipliers come from an approximate least-squares solve (2.23) stopped at the first iterate meeting one of three tests (2.26a–c) (p. 9). The tangential step reduces a primal-dual barrier model (2.17)–(2.18) with D_k ≈ Y_kS_k⁻¹ (pp. 7–8), either as a "relaxed SQP" step whose linearized infeasibility contracts (2.30d) (p. 10) or, when n_k = 0, a "very relaxed SQP" step bounded only by a fraction of v_k^max (2.35d), because exact null-space steps are "an unreasonable requirement in matrix-free settings" (p. 10). Criticality measures decide which computations run: a normal step only if π^v_k > ω_n(π^f_{k−1}) or v_k ≥ κ_vv v_k^max (2.8) (p. 6); multipliers and tangential step only if the normal step is well inside both radii (2.21) (p. 8). Each iteration is typed y (zero step, new multipliers only), f (barrier decrease within the funnel) or v (violation decrease, funnel shrinks by (2.54)) with separate trust regions δ^f, δ^v (pp. 11–13; Algorithm 1, p. 14). A slack reset (2.3) keeps c(x, s) ≥ 0 and can only reduce v and the barrier function (2.4) (p. 5). What changed relative to Gould–Toint: explicit interior constraints in the trust-region subproblems, scaled trust regions and scaled optimality measures (p. 4). An outer loop (Algorithm 2) drives µ → 0 with tolerances ε_π(µ), ε_v(µ) bounded by multiples of µ (4.3) and a capped D_k (4.4) (pp. 41–42).
  - Transferable: gate each expensive sub-computation by a cheap criticality test that compares the two goals (feasibility vs optimality) and skip it when it cannot pay off ((2.8), p. 6; (2.21), p. 8).
  - Transferable: when an exact subproblem condition (null-space step) cannot be met matrix-free, define a weaker alternative step and shrink its trust region to compensate ((2.34)–(2.35), pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, c twice continuously differentiable (Assumption 1.1, p. 4); iterates in a compact set (Assumption 3.1, p. 21); the iterative solver for (2.23) produces a bounded sequence whose model gradient vanishes (Assumption 2.1, p. 18); at limit points along which the scaled infeasibility measure vanishes, no active slacks and full-row-rank J (Assumption 3.2, p. 30), strengthened to a uniform lower bound κ_J on the smallest singular value of the scaled Jacobian at nearly feasible points (Assumption 3.3, p. 32). Proof-driven choices are admitted: the forcing-function condition (2.27) is imposed "For technical reasons (in the proof of Lemma 2.1(vii))" (p. 9); the projection in Definition 3.1 is "for theoretical purposes only" (p. 32); the specific D_k in (4.4) is assumed for the outer result, while other choices "only require a small modification in the proof" (p. 41). Equality constraints are suppressed "for simplicity" (p. 3) and handled only in a remark (pp. 43–44).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Global convergence only; no rates, no complexity. Skeleton: (i) Cauchy-decrease bounds for normal and tangential steps via feasibility of the scaled steepest-descent ray up to a computable α_B and a cited one-dimensional quadratic bound ([3, Lemma 1]) (Lemma 2.3, pp. 17–18; Lemma 2.6, pp. 19–20); (ii) an eleven-part bookkeeping lemma on the index sets Y, F, V, N, T, TD, T0, D (Lemma 2.1, pp. 15–16); (iii) funnel monotonicity v_k ≤ v_k^max by induction (Lemma 2.4, p. 18); (iv) finite termination of the iterative multiplier solve by a limit-point contradiction (Lemmas 2.5 and 2.8, pp. 18–19, 21); (v) uniform constants from compactness (Lemmas 3.1–3.3, pp. 21–22) and model-error bounds (Lemma 3.5, p. 23); (vi) "small radius ⇒ successful iteration" lemmas (Lemma 3.8, p. 25) and radius lower bounds by induction (Lemma 3.10, p. 26); (vii) the recurring contradiction device: show that an iteration labelled v would satisfy every condition of an f-iteration, contradicting V ∩ F = ∅ (Lemma 3.6, p. 24; Lemma 3.24, pp. 36–37; Theorem 3.27, pp. 38–40); (viii) case splits on the cardinalities |S| < ∞ (Theorem 3.11, pp. 26–27), |S_v| < ∞ (Lemma 3.24) and |S_v| = ∞ (Theorem 3.27); (ix) for the outer loop, tolerances proportional to µ plus a capped barrier Hessian to recover complementarity and y* ≥ 0 at limit points (Theorem 4.1, pp. 42–43). Result: under Assumptions 1.1, 2.1, 3.1–3.3, Algorithm 1 terminates finitely with an infeasible stationary point (v_k > κ_c) or an approximate first-order KKT point of the barrier problem (Theorem 3.28, p. 41); Algorithm 2 returns an infeasible stationary point or has a limit point that is a first-order KKT point of (NPs) (Theorem 4.1, p. 42).
  - Transferable: when an algorithm classifies iterations into types, prove convergence by showing that "too many failures of type V" would force an iteration to meet every condition of type F, which the classification forbids; the disjointness of the types does the work.
  - Transferable: prove well-posedness of an inexact inner solve by assuming only that the solver, run forever, converges to the exact quantity, and showing that a finite index meets one of several stopping tests (Lemma 2.5, pp. 18–19).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a — none. "We are currently implementing our new algorithm. Once complete, it will be part of the Galahad [19] thread-safe library" (p. 44).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Gould–Toint's trust-funnel [24, 23] to inequalities (pp. 3–4); adopts the barrier-SQP structure of Byrd et al. [3, 4] and the scalings used previously in [3, 6] (Curtis–Schenk–Wächter) (p. 4); borrows the y/f/v iteration distinction "in the spirit of" filter-SQP papers [9, 10, 11] (p. 11) while using "no merit function or filter" (abstract, p. 3). Prefers primal-dual over primal barrier Hessians citing "the widely accepted view" (p. 8). Criticism of rivals is gentle and specific: large-scale effectiveness of second-derivative SQP "has not yet been confirmed" (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution is announced as five enumerated features in the abstract (inexact subproblems; criticality measures choosing computations; no merit function or filter; inexact SQP steps; matrix-free) (p. 3). Long technical paper (44 report pages) with a fixed rhythm: definition → conditions → one sentence on each condition's role (e.g. after (2.30), p. 10; after (2.37), p. 11). A guide lemma collects "basic facts that may be deduced from the design of our algorithm" before the analysis (Lemma 2.1, p. 15). Parameter dependence on µ is tabulated (Table 4.1, p. 42). Algorithm 1 asks the user to choose more than twenty constants plus two forcing functions (Step 2, p. 14). Hedging is low and practical ("It is not necessarily prudent", p. 6; "may be wasteful", pp. 6, 8). The conclusion restates the theorems and calls the algorithm "extremely flexible" (p. 43) with no evidence beyond the analysis.
  - Transferable: after each algorithmic condition, add one sentence on its role ("Condition (2.30a) ensures ...", p. 10) so readers can map conditions to proof steps.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: algorithmic design plus global-convergence theory for a matrix-free interior-point trust-funnel method; no software, no experiments, no local rate. Limits visible in the text: implementation pending (p. 44); equality constraints only remarked (pp. 43–44); convergence relies on the uniform singular-value Assumption 3.3 near feasibility (p. 32) and on compactness of iterates (p. 21); many tuning constants with no guidance on values (p. 14); the outer theorem gives a KKT limit point, not convergence of the whole sequence (p. 42). Future direction stated: GALAHAD implementation (p. 44).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ✅ evidence: each subproblem only needs Cauchy decrease plus feasibility conditions derived from the trust-funnel's acceptance tests ((2.11)–(2.13), p. 7; (2.30), p. 10); the iterative multiplier solve stops at the first iterate meeting (2.26a–c), which are tied to termination, skipping, or computing a tangential step (p. 9; Lemma 2.5, pp. 18–19); "very relaxed" tangential steps exist because exact null-space steps are unreasonable matrix-free (p. 10). Here the globalization is a funnel with two trust regions, not an exact-penalty merit model, so the "test residual components separately" step of Method 1 does not apply.
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ⚠ variant: one algorithm moves between optimizing the barrier function (f-iterations) and reducing violation (v-iterations) without a restoration phase, decided each iteration by criticality measures and the funnel (pp. 6, 11–13); it returns an infeasible stationary point from inside the same loop (Algorithm 1 Step 8, p. 14; Algorithm 2 Step 7, p. 42). The steering object is the funnel bound v_k^max updated by (2.54) (p. 13), not a penalty or merit parameter, and no fast local convergence to infeasible points is claimed.
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✗ contradiction (absence): no comparison of any kind; the paper publishes a new solver design with implementation still pending (p. 44).
- Heuristic 2 (count a safeguard's cost) ⚠ variant: the design avoids work instead of counting it — a normal step "may be wasteful if the current iterate is nearly feasible" (p. 6), and new multipliers and a tangential step are skipped when (2.21) fails because their cost "may be wasteful" (p. 8); no cost is measured.
- new-pattern candidate: criticality-gated computation — compare feasibility and optimality criticality measures each iteration to decide which subproblems to solve at all ((2.8), p. 6; (2.21), p. 8; (2.26b), p. 9).
- new-pattern candidate: iteration typing as proof scaffold — classify every iteration into disjoint types with their own update rules and prove convergence by contradiction on the types (Lemma 2.1, pp. 15–16; Lemma 3.6, p. 24; Theorem 3.27, pp. 38–40).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To move an equality-constrained method into an interior-point setting, add (a) a slack-scaled trust region ‖P_k⁻¹d‖ with P_k = diag(I, S_k) and (b) fraction-to-the-boundary constraints to every subproblem, and scale the criticality measures the same way ((2.5)–(2.7), p. 6; (2.22)–(2.25), pp. 8–9).
- Use a slack reset s_i ← −c_i(x) whenever c_i(x) + s_i < 0, which can only lower both the violation and the barrier value, so c(x, s) ≥ 0 holds at the start of each iteration for free ((2.3)–(2.4), p. 5; Lemma 2.2, p. 17).
- Require only Cauchy decrease along a scaled steepest-descent direction plus a range-space condition that Krylov solvers satisfy automatically, so any matrix-free solver qualifies ((2.12)–(2.13), p. 7).
- Tie inner tolerances to the barrier parameter (ε ≤ ζµ) and cap the barrier Hessian diagonal to recover complementarity and dual sign in the outer limit ((4.3)–(4.4), p. 41; Theorem 4.1, pp. 42–43).
- Tabulate every constant that must depend on µ, with where it is used (Table 4.1, p. 42).

**Quotes**:
- "It is not necessarily prudent to compute a normal step in every iteration." (p. 6)
- "The algorithm is extremely flexible in that, during each iteration, it automatically determines the types of steps and updates that are expected to be most productive, where potential productivity is determined by available criticality measures." (p. 43)
