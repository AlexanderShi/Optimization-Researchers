### D002 · Single-Loop Deterministic and Stochastic Interior-Point Algorithms for Nonlinearly Constrained Optimization (CoRR 2024, arXiv:2408.16186)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–31 were read: abstract, §§1–7, Appendix A (Phase I algorithm, pp. 28–29) and the references. The proofs of §4 were read line by line. The version read is arXiv v1 dated 30 Aug 2024 (p. 2).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xin Jiang, Qi Wang (both Lehigh ISE, p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: an extension of Curtis–Kungurtsev–Robinson–Wang's stochastic-gradient interior-point method for bound constraints ([7], arXiv:2304.14907) to nonlinear inequality constraints (p. 3). It sits beside his stochastic SQP line, which it cites as the equality-constrained counterpart ([2, 3, 8, 9], p. 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is min f(x) s.t. Ax = b, c(x) ≤ 0, where constraint values and derivatives are tractable but objective values and derivatives can only be estimated. The method uses stochastic gradients and no objective values at all (abstract, p. 2; problem (1), p. 4). The entry is a gap: interior-point methods are "arguably the most effective" class in the deterministic nonconvex setting (Ipopt, Knitro, LOQO, p. 2), yet "little work has been done" on a stochastic version (p. 2). The paper names three obstacles (p. 3): (i) the nested loop needs a stationarity measure to end the inner loop, and this is intractable without exact gradients; (ii) barrier gradients are neither Lipschitz nor bounded; (iii) line-search or trust-region acceptance needs exact function values.
  - Transferable: before designing a stochastic version of a deterministic solver, list each component that silently needs an exact quantity (inner stopping test, Lipschitz gradients, acceptance test) and design one answer per obstacle (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Collapse the interior-point method into a single loop (Algorithm 1 "SLIP", p. 7). The barrier parameter µk and a neighbourhood parameter θk follow prescribed decreasing sequences with µk/θk−1 constant (pp. 5–6). Iterates stay in E ∩ N(θk−1), where N(θ) = {c(x) ≤ −θ1} (pp. 5–7). Each iteration: identify nearly active constraints Ak = {i : −η̄µk < ci(xk)} (p. 6); form qk, the (stochastic) gradient of the barrier-augmented objective (4) (p. 6); compute any dk satisfying (5a)–(5e): null-space, norm proportional to ‖Pqk‖, sufficient angle, and (5e) a sufficient move away from every nearly active constraint (p. 6). Then take the step γkαkdk, where αk is about 1/Lk, the local Lipschitz constant of the barrier gradient on N(θ) (Parameter Rule 1, p. 10), and γk is the positive root of a quadratic that keeps the segment inside N(θk) (pp. 10–11). Changed from a classical IPM: no inner loop, no line search, no objective values, a prescribed (not adaptive) barrier schedule, and a feasible method (pp. 3, 7). Kept: the log-barrier, multipliers z = −µ/c (18), and the KKT target (p. 14). For the stochastic case, dk comes from a null-space linear system with Hk (21) (p. 15), and γk is boxed into a prescribed interval [γk,min, γk,max], with γk,max = γk,min + γk,buff identical over all runs (Parameter Rule 2, p. 16). This makes the step-size multiplier almost deterministic, so the noise correlation term can be bounded (p. 16).
  - Transferable: when a step-acceptance safeguard (here the fraction-to-boundary-like γk) depends on the random direction, prescribe a run-independent bracket [γmin, γmin + buffer] for it. The correlation between the safeguard and the gradient error then costs at most buffer × αk × const (Parameter Rule 2, p. 16; Lemma 4.8, pp. 18–19).
  - Transferable: replace a nested barrier loop by coupled prescribed sequences (µk/θk−1 constant), so that no inner stopping test is ever needed (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.1: f, c continuously differentiable on an open set containing F, f bounded below, A full row rank, and a strictly feasible point exists, which rules out nonlinear equalities written as two-sided inequalities (pp. 4–5). Assumption 3.1, declared "not standard" (p. 3): a direction satisfying (5) exists at every iterate. The authors read it as nondegeneracy plus "µ1/θ0 being sufficiently large" (pp. 3, 6–7, 21) and discuss it in §5 (pp. 21–24). Assumption 4.1: Lipschitz f, ∇f, ci, ∇ci and bounded ci on a set containing the iterates (pp. 7–8). Stochastic: Assumption 4.2, unbiased estimates with an almost-sure bound ‖P(Gk − ∇f)‖ ≤ σ. The authors flag this as stronger than in the unconstrained case but say "we know of no analysis that can avoid a bounded-error assumption" for stochastic interior-point methods (p. 15). Assumption 4.3: Hk uniformly positive definite on Null(A) (p. 15). LICQ at the limit point is needed to recover KKT multipliers (Theorems 4.1–4.2, pp. 12–13, 20). The conservative step-size rules are admitted to be "for simplicity" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) A shifted barrier φ̃ = f − µ Σ log(−ci/κci): bounded below by f_inf, with the same gradient as φ and monotone in µ, so that decreasing µ also decreases φ̃ (eqs. (6)–(7), p. 8). (b) A local Lipschitz constant for the barrier gradient on line segments in N(θ), scaling like µ/(θθ̄) (Lemma 4.1, pp. 8–9). (c) Feasibility of the step is certified by the positive root of a strongly convex quadratic upper bound on ci along the ray (Lemma 4.2, p. 10). (d) A lower bound on γk by a case split, nearly active (uses (5e)) versus not nearly active (monotonicity of h(a) = −a + √(a² + b)) (Lemma 4.3, pp. 11–12). (e) A descent lemma on φ̃ with decrease proportional to γkαk‖P∇φ‖² (Lemma 4.4, p. 12). (f) Unsummability of γkαk ≥ min{αk, ρk, υk}, by matching exponents. The proof needs tµ = tθ in both directions, and tµ + tα ∈ [−1, 0) (Theorem 4.1, pp. 12–14). (g) KKT recovery: multiplier estimates z = −µ diag(c)⁻¹1 and least-squares y (18); complementarity forces zi → 0 off the active set; LICQ gives convergence of (y, z) (p. 14). (h) Stochastic: the noise term is split by the sign of the inner product with the law of total expectation. The bracket γmin ≤ Γk ≤ γmax leaves only γk,buff αk Bσ (Lemma 4.8, pp. 18–19). (i) Exponent conditions 2t + tα < −1 and t + 2tα < −1 make the noise sum finite, and Fatou's lemma then gives liminf ‖P∇φ‖ = 0 almost surely (Lemma 4.10, Theorem 4.2, pp. 19–21). (j) A pair of 2-D examples delimits Assumption 3.1. In Example 1 (polyhedral, constant gradients) it holds once µ/θ is large enough, via limiting directions as µ → ∞ (pp. 21–23). In Example 2 (c2 = x1 − x2², gradients that become opposed at the origin) the authors "are unable to show" it holds (pp. 23–24, Figure 1). Result type: asymptotic liminf only, with no rate or complexity bound (Theorems 4.1–4.2).
  - Transferable: device (a). To analyze a barrier function that is unbounded below, shift each log term by a known bound on |ci|. The shifted function has the same gradient, is bounded below and is monotone in µ, so barrier decreases and steps telescope together (p. 8).
  - Transferable: device (j). Accompany a nonstandard assumption with one example where you can prove it and one where you cannot, plus a picture of why (cone of nearly active gradients wide or collapsing) (pp. 21–24, Figure 1 p. 23).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Three experiments, all with Python SLIP on a 16-core Opteron node with 32 GB (p. 24). (1) Deterministic SLIP on CUTEst through PyCUTEst. Filtering by stated rules: 1315 problems → 396 after removing nonlinear equalities, problems without inequalities and problems too large for memory → 327 after a Phase I solve, with 10 evaluation errors and 59 Phase I failures removed (p. 25; Phase I is Algorithm 2, pp. 28–29). Settings: θ0 = −0.9 max c(x1), µ1 = max{0.1, 2θ0}, t = 0.7; Lipschitz constants estimated from n random points around x1; µ1 doubled whenever (5) fails, capped at 10⁴; γk allowed above 1 (p. 25). Budget 2×10⁴ iterations. Reported: f(xK) < f(x1) on every run, and a histogram of a relative stationarity measure (29) whose denominator takes the better of two initial measures (pp. 25–26, Figure 2). (2) A random SOCP, chosen convex to avoid different minimizers (p. 26). Deterministic SLIP is the reference (tα = −0.151, γ ≤ 10 without objective values), and 10 seeds of stochastic SLIP with N(0, I) gradient noise are run. All ten final values are listed (Table 1, p. 27). (3) A one-hidden-layer network (512 tanh units) on LIBSVM mushrooms with ‖w‖² ≤ 100, mini-batch 256, 10 runs, all final values listed (pp. 26–27; the text calls it "Table 6" but the table is unnumbered, p. 27). No external baseline: the authors say the deterministic version is not expected to compete with second-order IPMs, and that no stochastic IPM exists to compare against (p. 24). The high-stationarity outliers are put down to nonlinearity or degeneracy by conjecture, not by a test (p. 26).
  - Transferable: for a stochastic method with no fair rival, run the deterministic twin with identical parameters as the reference, report every seed's final value in a table rather than a mean, and choose a convex instance first so that seed-to-seed spread means noise rather than different minimizers (pp. 26–27).
  - Transferable: when f_inf is unknown, normalize the final stationarity by the better of the available initial stationarity measures (eq. (29), p. 25).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on [7] (bound-constrained stochastic IPM, same group) and says what is new: nonlinear inequalities and linear equalities (p. 3). Situates IPMs against augmented-Lagrangian and SQP methods via Nocedal–Wright [16] and the three IPM codes (p. 2). Claims priority: "to the best of our knowledge, there are no other interior-point methods in the literature that possess convergence guarantees in a stochastic setting that is comparable to ours" (p. 3). Cites the stochastic-SQP literature (own group and Na, Anitescu, Kolar, Mahoney, Fang, Qiu–Kungurtsev, Berahas–Bollapragada–Zhou) as the infeasible, equality-constrained alternative (p. 27). Prior work is not criticized; it is restated as a design obstacle.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A subsection titled "Contributions and Limitations" (§1.1, p. 3) leads with what the method will not do ("Let us emphasize upfront", p. 3) and repeats the disclaimer at §4.1 (p. 9) and §6 (p. 24). "We contend" is the main claim verb (pp. 3, 6). Parameter rules are stated first and then explained in prose ("We state the rule, then summarize the reasons", p. 10). A nonstandard assumption gets its own section with examples and a figure (§5, pp. 21–24). Proofs that repeat earlier ones are omitted with a pointer (Lemma 4.5, p. 17); Theorem 4.2 is proved in full "for the sake of completeness" (p. 20). Small slips: "Table 6" for an unnumbered table (p. 27), and "our algorithms performs" (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution levels: algorithm (single-loop feasible IPM, deterministic and stochastic), convergence theory (liminf stationarity, deterministic and almost sure), a Python implementation and three experiments (pp. 3, 27). The limits are stated concretely: a strictly feasible start is required (Phase I in Appendix A); no nonlinear equality constraints in either theory (p. 3); a nonstandard Assumption 3.1 whose parameters cannot be set in advance, "a computationally effective strategy ... is not straightforward to design" (p. 27); conservative step sizes (p. 7); the practical µ1 reset lies outside the theory (pp. 24–25). Future work: a single-loop infeasible IPM. They explain why slack variables do not simply work: the slack direction depends on the x-direction, so a uniform lower bound on γ is hard to keep (pp. 27–28).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 variant ⚠: Method 1 tunes the inner solve's stopping test to the outer globalization. Here the inner loop is removed altogether, because its stopping test (a stationarity measure) cannot be evaluated with stochastic gradients (p. 3). The outer schedule (µk, θk) is prescribed instead (pp. 5–6).
- Method 2 variant ⚠: one algorithm with no phase switch between barrier subproblems ("single-loop", p. 3). The barrier parameter is prescribed in the theory, not steered from predicted progress (p. 6). The implementation adds a steering-like reset that doubles µ1 when (5e) fails, up to 10⁴, and this lies outside the theory (pp. 24–25).
- Method 5 variant ⚠: no external rival is run, and the authors say why (no level playing field, p. 24). The deterministic twin with identical parameters is the reference for the stochastic runs (p. 26). The incumbent (Ipopt) is conceded without being run (pp. 3, 24).
- Heuristic 10 (keep the skeleton, build a deterministic twin) — evidence ✅: Algorithm 1(D) is analyzed because it "provides a useful precursor" for the stochastic setting (p. 9). The paper extends its own bound-constrained parent [7] (p. 3).
- Heuristic 7 (filters by rule with counts) — evidence ✅: CUTEst filter counts 1315 → 396 → 327, with the reasons given before any SLIP run (p. 25).
- Method 3 not applied: problems without a strictly feasible point are removed (p. 25), and degeneracy is only conjectured as the cause of poor runs (p. 26).
- New-pattern candidate: "limitations-first contribution statement" (§1.1, p. 3; repeated pp. 9, 24).
- New-pattern candidate: "assumption bracketed by a provable and an unprovable 2-D example" (§5, pp. 21–24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Shifted log-barrier with the same gradient and a lower bound, so that barrier-parameter decreases and steps telescope in one potential (p. 8).
- A prescribed, run-independent bracket [γmin, γmin + γbuff] on a direction-dependent safeguard, to bound noise–step correlation in expectation (pp. 16, 18–19).
- Step-to-boundary factor computed as the positive root of the quadratic upper bound of each constraint along the ray (p. 10).
- Deterministic twin as the reference curve for stochastic seeds, all seeds tabulated, convex instance first (pp. 26–27).
- Relative stationarity normalized by the better of the initial stationarity measures when f_inf is unknown (p. 25).

**Quotes**:
- "Let us emphasize upfront that we do not expect our method to be competitive with state-of-the-art interior-point methods in the deterministic regime, and especially not with those that employ second-order derivatives of the objective." (p. 3)
- "meaning that there is no such method in the literature against which our algorithm can be compared on a level playing field." (p. 24)

### S013 · Worst-case complexity of an SQP method for nonlinear equality constrained stochastic optimization (Mathematical Programming 2024, DOI 10.1007/S10107-023-01981-1; read as arXiv:2112.14799v2)
**Read level**: full. File pp. 1–31 were read in full: abstract, §§1–5 with all main-text proofs, and the references. Appendix §6 (proof of Theorem 1, pp. 31–32) was read. Appendix §7 (proofs of Lemmas 9 and 10, pp. 33–46) was skimmed: the tree construction, Lemma 15 (Chernoff), the definitions of good and bad leaf sets, Lemma 16's statement, Theorem 5, Lemmas 20–22, with the case analysis of Lemmas 17–19 (pp. 39–43) only glanced at. The version read is arXiv v2 dated 10 Jan 2022 (p. 2), not the journal version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michael J. O'Neill (CI Fellow), Daniel P. Robinson (both Lehigh ISE, p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: the complexity sequel to the stochastic SQP method of Berahas, Curtis, Robinson and Zhou (SIAM J. Optim. 2021, [2]). The algorithm analyzed is Algorithm 3.1 of [2] with three small changes (p. 8). It is cited as [8] in D002 (p. 30).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: min f(x) = E[F(x, ω)] s.t. c(x) = 0, with only unbiased stochastic gradients available (problem (1), p. 4; p. 2). The entry is a missing theory, not a new algorithm. Complexity results for stochastic constrained optimization existed only for simple sets (projection, Frank–Wolfe, [11, 12, 14]). The one SQP bound ([18], Na–Anitescu–Kolar) assumes a known merit-parameter threshold (p. 3). The paper takes the "highly stochastic regime" (only bounded variance) and confronts the adaptive merit parameter (p. 3). Motivation for SQP over penalty methods: "penalty methods are frequently outperformed by SQP methods" in both deterministic and stochastic contexts (p. 3).
  - Transferable: to get a theory paper out of a practical method, ask what the existing result assumes away (here a known merit threshold, [18]) and make that the object of analysis (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Same algorithm as [2]: an SQP step from (9), merit φ = τf + ‖c‖1 (4), τ decreased by (12)–(13), a ratio parameter ξ (14), and a step size projected into an interval set by βk and Lipschitz constants (pp. 7–9). Changes for the analysis: a fixed iteration limit kmax, a slightly stronger decrease requirement for τk in (13), and an output index k* sampled in proportion to βk (p. 8; Algorithm 1, p. 9). The analytical idea: condition on an event E (τ ≥ τmin, τ^trial,true ≥ τmin, ξ constant, at most smax decreases of τ) (p. 11). Isolate the one bad event Ek,3, a negative gradient-error inner product while τ decreases (p. 14). Introduce an auxiliary sequence T̂k = min{Tk, τ^trial,true_k} that the algorithm never computes but that ties the model reduction to stationarity (pp. 14, 16). Bound with high probability (i) the sum of probabilities of Ek,3 and (ii) the number of iterations with τ^trial,true < τk−1 (Lemmas 9–10, p. 20). Result: with constant β = γ/√(kmax+1) (Theorem 2, pp. 20–24; Corollary 1, p. 25), expected stationarity is O((τ−1(f0 − flow) + ‖c0‖1 + M)/√(kmax+1)) plus an adaptivity term O((τ−1 − τmin)(smax log kmax + log(1/δ))/√(kmax+1)), with probability 1 − δ. That is Õ(ε⁻⁴) when smax = O(log kmax) (pp. 6–7). With τmin known and the update disabled, only (7a) remains, and it holds surely (p. 7).
  - Transferable: when an adaptive parameter makes the potential function change over time, define a shadow parameter (here min of the actual and the "true-gradient" trial value) that the algorithm never computes but that ties the decrease to a stationarity measure. Then count separately the iterations where the shadow and the actual parameter differ (pp. 14, 20).
  - Transferable: present the bound as a "standard" part matching SG plus a separate additive "price of adaptivity" term, and say when the second term is harmless (pp. 6–7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1: f C¹, bounded below, ∇f bounded and Lipschitz; c and J bounded; each ∇ci Lipschitz; singular values of J bounded away from zero (LICQ everywhere on X) (p. 4). Assumption 2: ‖Hk‖ ≤ κH and Hk is ζ-positive definite on Null(Jk) (p. 7). Assumption 3: unbiased, variance ≤ M, plus a conditional first-moment bound Mτ on the bad event (20). The authors show (19) can imply (20), e.g. for mini-batches with p = b/N (p. 12). Assumption 4: with probability ≥ pτ the stochastic model term exceeds the true one. Gaussian noise gives pτ = 1/2 ([2, Example 3.17], p. 13). Lipschitz constants L and Γ are assumed known (p. 8). The authors call the conditions in E on the merit parameter "not necessarily minor" (p. 11). They justify them by zero-probability arguments in [1, 2] and by §4.5, where symmetric sub-Gaussian noise makes Assumption 3 and E hold with high probability, with smax = O(log(log(kmax/δ))) (p. 7; Lemma 13, p. 27). Constant ξ is "for the sake of simplicity" and relaxed in §4.6 (pp. 11, 27–29).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) A deterministic warm-up: an O(ε⁻²) bound for Algorithm 2.1 of [2] by contradiction on summed merit decreases (Theorem 1, p. 5; Lemma 14, Theorem 4, pp. 31–32). (b) Three-event decomposition of E[AkTk∇f^T(Dk − d^true)]: Ek,1 (inner product ≥ 0), Ek,2 (< 0, τ unchanged), Ek,3 (< 0, τ decreased). Three upper bounds come from adding and subtracting like terms, and they are averaged so that only the Ek,3 term and a θβ² term remain (Lemma 6, pp. 14–16). (c) A two-way split by the sign of Qk with the two bounds averaged (Lemma 7, pp. 17–18). (d) An auxiliary T̂k and difference bounds between model reductions at Tk and T̂k (Lemma 8, p. 19). (e) Conditional independence: whether τ^trial,true_k < τk−1 is fixed by G[k−1], so the bounds hold conditioned on either case (pp. 20, 22). (f) A high-probability bound on Σ P[Tk < Tk−1 | E, Fk]. Realizations are grouped into a tree by their discretized probability signature (p0..pk, w0..wk) with B bins. Good and bad leaf sets are defined, a multiplicative Chernoff bound is applied for independent Bernoullis (Lemma 15), and a union bound over binomial(kmax, j) paths gives δ̂ = δ / Σ binom(kmax, j) (eqs. (32)–(33), pp. 19, 33–36, 43). (g) The binomial sum gives the log factor, ℓ(smax, δ̂) = O(smax log kmax + log 1/δ) (Lemma 22, pp. 45–46). (h) Symmetry of the noise distribution gives pτ = 1/2 via the null-space representation of Dk (Lemma 11, p. 26). (i) A sub-Gaussian maximum bound over kmax + 1 iterations (Lemma 12, p. 26, following [16]). (j) An output index sampled ∝ βk, then Jensen to pass from the squared to the plain stationarity measure (pp. 6–8, 25). Complexity form: iterations = function evaluations = gradient evaluations (p. 2).
  - Transferable: device (b). When a product of correlated random quantities (step size × parameter × error) has to be bounded, condition on the sign of the error and on whether the parameter moved. Write one upper bound per way of pairing extremes, and average them so that the harmless cases cancel (pp. 14–16).
  - Transferable: device (f). To bound with high probability how often an adaptive parameter fires when its per-iteration firing probabilities are themselves random, discretize those probabilities into B bins, build a tree of realizations by signature, and apply Chernoff along each path (pp. 33–36).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none. The paper has no experiments. Practical relevance rests on [2] ("key to the practical performance of the algorithm", p. 29) and is not re-tested here.
- **D6 Positioning**: Presented as "the first worst-case complexity result for an SQP algorithm that operates in the highly stochastic regime" for deterministic nonlinear equality constraints (p. 3). The yardstick is the SG method for unconstrained nonconvex problems (Davis–Drusvyatskiy [11]). Matching it is called "arguably the best result that one can expect" (p. 3), and the match is restated after Corollary 1 (p. 25). [18] is acknowledged but limited to the idealized known-threshold case (p. 3). Penalty and augmented-Lagrangian approaches are described as sensitive to the penalty update and ill-conditioning, without naming failures of specific papers (p. 3). The conclusion places the technique as reusable for adaptive penalty ([5, 6, 17]), adaptive augmented Lagrangian ([9]), adaptive barrier ([20]) and penalty-interior-point ([8], Curtis's PIPAL) methods (p. 29).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A §2 "Outline of Main Results" previews the theorem and reads each term of the bound before any technical development (pp. 5–7). The deterministic result is framed modestly: "Theorem 1 is not surprising" (p. 5). The event E is justified in a three-bullet list that concedes its strength ("not necessarily minor", p. 11). Before the proof, the two "major changes" relative to [2]'s analysis are announced as a numbered list (p. 14). Theorem 3 (§4.6) is stated without proof as "a straightforward, but tedious extension" (p. 28). There is a slip in the conclusion: the stationarity measure is said to decay "at a rate of k −4 (ignoring log factors)" (p. 29), which contradicts the 1/√(kmax+1) rate and the Õ(ε⁻⁴) complexity of (7) (pp. 6–7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: theory (complexity of an existing algorithm, with new analysis tools for random adaptive parameters) (pp. 3, 29). The limits are stated: the analysis is conditioned on E; Lipschitz constants are assumed known, though "in practice one might only estimate" them (p. 8); equality constraints only, with full-rank J (p. 4); the adaptive-ξ result is unproved (p. 28). Not stated in the text: there are no experiments checking how often E holds or how large smax is in practice (unlike the event frequencies of [2] cited in SKILL.md Heuristic 1). Future work: apply the techniques to other algorithms whose auxiliary parameter sequence becomes a random process (p. 29).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 evidence ✅: the adaptive merit parameter is kept rather than fixed to a known threshold, and the whole analysis is organized around it as a random process (pp. 3, 6, 11, 29). The paper accepts a weaker, high-probability theorem in order to keep the steering rule.
- Method 4 variant ⚠: the bound is decomposed so that the reader sees what adaptivity costs (7b) separately from the SG-like terms (7a), and the idealized known-τmin setting is reported as the special case it is (p. 7). This audits the complexity statement itself, not an evaluation measure. Taste warning 3 is respected: no practical gain is claimed from the bound.
- Heuristic 1 variant ⚠: the analysis conditions on an event (E) as in [2], but here the event is not measured in runs. It is shown to hold with high probability under symmetric sub-Gaussian noise (§4.5, pp. 25–27).
- Heuristic 10 evidence ✅: the deterministic twin's complexity (Theorem 1, O(ε⁻²)) is proved first and used to explain what breaks in the stochastic case: (i) sufficient decrease, (ii) the tie to stationarity, (iii) a deterministic τmin (pp. 5–6).
- New-pattern candidate: "price-of-adaptivity term isolated in the bound" (pp. 6–7, 29).
- New-pattern candidate: "shadow parameter computed with the true gradient" (T̂k, τ^trial,true; pp. 10, 14, 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- An analysis-only shadow sequence T̂k = min{Tk, τ^trial,true_k}, plus a high-probability count of the iterations where it differs from the real one (pp. 14, 16, 20).
- Averaging several valid upper bounds to cancel terms of unknown sign in a three-event decomposition (Lemma 6, pp. 14–16).
- A signature tree with B-bin discretized probabilities plus Chernoff and a union bound, to control sums of random per-iteration probabilities (pp. 33–36, 43).
- Using the conditional independence of an event from the current sample (it is fixed by the past) to condition both bounds on it (pp. 20, 22).
- A preview section that reads each term of the final bound and says when it is harmless (e.g. smax = O(log log) under sub-Gaussian noise), before the proofs (pp. 6–7).

**Quotes**:
- "The high-probability aspect of this result arises purely due to the uncertainty of the behavior of the adaptive merit parameter sequence" (p. 3)
- "While key to the practical performance of the algorithm, the adaptivity of the merit parameter introduced a number of theoretical challenges to overcome." (p. 29)
