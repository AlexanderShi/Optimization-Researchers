### S073 · Equity-promoting integer programming approaches for medical resident rotation scheduling (Health Care Management Science 2025, DOI 10.1007/s10729-025-09736-4)
**Read level**: full. Read the main text pp. 1–22 in full (§§1–8), the E-Companion formulations A.1–A.7 (pp. 22–24), and Tables 7 and 12–14 (pp. 24, 36). Skimmed: proofs B.1–B.4 (pp. 23–31; statements and first steps read, the case analysis for each inequity measure in B.3 skimmed), the info template C (pp. 32–33), parameter tables D and G (pp. 34–38). Back matter p. 39 read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shutian Li (Lehigh ISE), Karmel S. Shehadeh (USC, corresponding author), Beth R. Hochman (NYU Surgery) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an integer-programming, healthcare-operations paper, outside his continuous-optimization line. It shares its fairness theme with S083 and the 2023 fair-ML work. Who did what is not stated (back matter p. 39 has no contribution statement).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a collaborator's practice problem: the chief resident at CUIMC spends "several weeks" building the yearly rotation schedule by hand, and the result often fails the constraints and is inequitable (p. 2). The resident-to-rotation assignment problem (RRAP) is posed as a feasibility problem, and the authors argue that a "blind, feasible assignment" leaves residents unequally served in vacation requests (p. 2). Gap in the literature: rotation scheduling gets less attention than shift scheduling, and few papers make equity a formal objective (pp. 3–4). Prior models also assume equal-length blocks (p. 5).
  - Transferable: show first that the plain feasibility model produces the inequity you want to remove (the red star in Fig. 4 is dominated by every Pareto schedule, pp. 17–18), then add the objective.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Three nested IP models: a feasibility model (1a)–(1m) that "mimics the manual method" (p. 8), an equity-neutral model (2) maximizing satisfied requests (p. 9), and a bi-objective equity-promoting model (4) that maximizes satisfied requests and minimizes an inequity measure ϕ chosen from seven (Table 3, p. 10). Minimizing inequity alone (3) gives the useless schedule with zero requests granted (p. 10). The weighting method is rejected because it can miss Pareto points of integer programs and depends on scaling (pp. 10–11). Instead of the plain ε-constraint method (Algorithm 1, p. 11), a Pareto Search Algorithm (Algorithm 2, pp. 12–13) brackets the front between the f2 = 0 solution (Prop. 1) and the max-f1 solution (Prop. 2), then moves up one granted request at a time. It fixes the requests already granted (10d) and grants one more to a least-served resident (10e). If that restricted IP is infeasible it falls back to the full problem (11) (p. 13). Prop. 3 says granting to a least-served resident is optimal for each measure (pp. 13–14). Prop. 4 says the restricted problem has the same optimal f2 as the full one (p. 14; proof p. 30). Symmetry-breaking constraints (12) fill blocks in order (p. 14).
  - Transferable: when an ε-constraint sweep on an integer objective gets slow, fix the previous point's granted decisions and add one unit to the worst-off agent; prove that this restriction loses nothing for your measures and keep the full ε-problem as fallback (pp. 13–14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: All rotation, ACGME and vacation rules are hard constraints because relaxing them risks accreditation (p. 9). Equity is measured only on the number of granted vacation requests, because same-class residents have identical rotation workloads (pp. 2, 9). The seven measures were chosen because they give linear IPs (p. 10). Test data: 18 instances built from six academic years (2018–2023) with synthetic vacation requests drawn by two rules A and B (pp. 15–16), plus a 2023–2024 case instance with 54 residents (p. 16). The real data are not public (p. 39).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Props 1–2 identify the two ends of the Pareto front by lexicographic solves (problems (6), (7), p. 12; proofs pp. 23–24). (ii) Integrality of f1 means consecutive non-dominated points differ by at least one granted request (Remark 1, p. 12), so ε = 1 is exact (p. 11). (iii) Prop. 3 is proved per measure through the "vacation request distribution vector" k (the count of residents with i granted requests) and differences ψ(k′) − ψ(k) (pp. 23–29, skimmed). (iv) Prop. 4 is proved by induction over Step 3 iterations (p. 30, skimmed). (v) The proofs give equivalences: MaxPair behaves like Range and MaxSumPair like MaxMeanDev (p. 20). The counts of extra variables and constraints per measure (Table 7, p. 24; Gini needs |R|² variables) explain the run-time ranking (p. 21).
  - Transferable: before timing several fairness measures, tabulate how many variables and constraints each linearization adds; it predicts which one will be slow (Table 7, p. 24; p. 21).
- **D5 Experiments**: Gurobi 9.5 from Python on a MacBook Pro M1, which the authors call the "computing capability available to a chief resident" (p. 17). On the case instance (pp. 17–18): Pareto fronts for five measures, with 3, 12, 13, 40 and 63 non-dominated points for Range, Gini, MeanDev, SumMaxPair and MaxMeanDev (p. 17). The price of equity (13) runs from 0 to 0.44 (pp. 17–18). The integrated model is compared with the practitioners' sequential approach (rotations first, then vacations), using three distinct rotation solutions "for a fair comparison"; the sequential fronts are dominated (p. 19). Resident-based and class-based equity are compared, and class-based schedules look bad when measured over all residents (pp. 19–20). Timing (Table 6, p. 21): 90 runs (18 instances × 5 replications, p. 20). The text says every front took "less than two hours" and also gives a range up to 2.2 hours for 185 residents (p. 20); Table 6's largest entry is 7972.1 s, about 2.2 hours (p. 21). The ε-constraint method solves none of the instances under Gini, MeanDev and SumMaxPair within a day and stops at the third iteration with MIP gaps of 63 %, 44 % and 79 % (p. 21). Per-step times are reported (Table 12, p. 36). Only one ε-constraint instance (Inst1-125-A) is described in detail (p. 21).
  - Transferable: run the timing study on the hardware the end user will have, and say so (p. 17).
  - Transferable: give the practitioners' sequential heuristic several distinct starting decisions rather than one, so that its loss is not an artefact of a bad draw (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on resident-scheduling surveys and models (Erhard et al., Guo et al., Bard et al., Smalley–Keskinocak, Proano–Agarwal; pp. 3–5). Claims four advances: block structures that vary by class, a choice of inequity measures instead of one, a Pareto search that finds the whole front, and a decision-support tool (p. 5). Prior work is described neutrally; criticism is limited to "did not develop decision-support tools" (p. 5) and the cost of ε-constraint methods (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: OR-application style: numbered contributions split into methodological and practical (pp. 2–3), "insights" lists in the results (pp. 17–18), and a conclusion that repeats them (pp. 21–22). Propositions sit in the main text; all proofs go to the E-Companion (p. 11). One small slip: §7 promises "comparison with the manual approach" in §7.3 (p. 15), and §7.3 is the sequential approach (p. 19). The measure choice is handed to the decision makers, who prefer measures that give more Pareto points (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions are modelling, an exact Pareto-front algorithm, a web tool developed with "multiple feedback sessions" and training sessions (p. 15), and computational insights (pp. 21–22). Limits are concrete but brief: "no clear winner among these inequity measures" (p. 18), no universal answer on individual- versus group-based equity (p. 20), data not public (p. 39). Future work: uncertainty such as resident absenteeism, and equity in shift scheduling (pp. 21–22). Not stated by the authors: vacation requests in the 18 timing instances are synthetic (pp. 15–16), and deployment results (whether CUIMC adopted the schedules) are not reported.

**Method links**: Method 4 ✅ (evidence: the paper audits its own yardstick. It shows that inequity alone rewards granting nothing, p. 10, compares seven measures and shows that each yields a different front, p. 17, and lets the decision maker choose, p. 18). Method 5 ✅ (evidence: the sequential baseline gets three distinct rotation solutions "for a fair comparison", p. 19; per-step timings are published, p. 36). Method 2 ⚠ (variant: the scalar trade-off parameter is not steered but replaced: the weighting method is rejected and the whole Pareto front is enumerated, pp. 10–11). New-pattern candidate: "constrain, don't weight" (pp. 10–11; see S082, S083).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Bracket a bi-objective integer front with two lexicographic solves, then step one integer unit at a time (pp. 12–13).
- Restrict each ε-step to "keep previous grants, add one to the worst-off", prove it optimal per measure, and keep the unrestricted problem as fallback (pp. 13–14, 30).
- Report the price of equity (13) beside the front so decision makers see what each equity level costs (p. 17).
- Count the extra variables and constraints of each fairness linearization before timing it (Table 7, p. 24).

### S082 · A Proximal-Gradient Method for Solving Regularized Optimization Problems with General Constraints (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2512.23166)
**Read level**: full. Read arXiv v2 (15 Jan 2026), pp. 1–31: §§1–4 and §§5.1–5.2.2 with proofs, the statements and proof structure of §5.2.3 (pp. 18–24; the case analyses of Lemmas 5.16, 5.18–5.22 and 5.24 skimmed), §§5.3–5.4 with proofs (pp. 24–26), §6 (pp. 26–31) and §7. References (pp. 31–33) checked for [7], [9], [13], [16]–[18], [41], [51].
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xiaoyi Qu, Daniel P. Robinson (both Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: extends the equality-constrained proximal-gradient method of Dai, Qu and Robinson [16] (SIAM J. Optim. 2025) to bound (hence inequality) constraints (p. 3), and links to the earlier ℓ1 structure-identification work with Chen and Robinson (refs. [10], [11], p. 31). It sits in the 2024–2026 "constraints as constraints, not penalties" phase (SKILL.md Inner Tension 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Problem (1.1): min f(x) + r(x) s.t. c(x) = 0, x ≥ 0, with f, c smooth and possibly nonconvex and r convex and possibly nonsmooth (p. 1). The entry is a property, not a rate: proximal-gradient methods identify the solution's structure (zero pattern under ℓ1), which saves computation, keeps physical meaning and allows switching to higher-order methods on the identified manifold (pp. 1–2). With nonlinear constraints, projections are intractable and "penalty-based methods ... may fail to preserve the structure of the solution" (p. 2). The related work is mostly augmented Lagrangian (O(ε⁻³) under CQs, O(ε⁻²) under transversality) or penalty-barrier (pp. 2–3). The SQP-type proximal method [51] assumes feasible subproblems and gives no identification result (p. 3).
  - Transferable: enter a crowded area (here, constrained composite optimization) through the property the incumbents lose (structure identification under penalties), not through a better rate (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm 3.1 (p. 5) uses a Byrd–Omojokun-type step decomposition. The normal step v_k approximately solves a bound-constrained trust-region subproblem on the linearized infeasibility with radius κ_v α_k δ_k, where δ_k is the norm of the projected gradient of ½‖c‖² (pp. 4–5). It only has to beat the Cauchy point found by projected backtracking ((3.4)–(3.8), pp. 4–5). The tangential step u_k solves a strongly convex proximal subproblem with Ju = 0 and the bounds, keeping r explicit; u = 0 is always feasible, so every subproblem is feasible (pp. 3, 5). Acceptance uses the ℓ2 merit function Φ_τ = τ(f + r) + ‖c‖₂ with the merit parameter decreased by rule (3.10) so that the step is a descent direction (p. 6). Rejection shrinks the proximal parameter α (p. 6). If δ_k = 0 and c_k ≠ 0 the method stops at an infeasible stationary point (Line 7, pp. 4–5). What changes relative to [16]: bounds Ω and the projected Cauchy machinery. The decomposition and merit function are kept.
  - Transferable: to add bounds to an equality-constrained decomposition method, put the bounds in both subproblems, keep u = 0 feasible for the tangential one, and require only Cauchy decrease (by projected backtracking) of the normal one (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 3.1: f and c bounded with Lipschitz, bounded gradients on an open convex set containing the iterates, and r convex with bounded subdifferential (p. 6). The results are layered: no CQ (§5.2.1, p. 11); a sequential CQ that [J_k; I_{A^v_k}] has full row rank with smallest singular value bounded below at every iterate (Assumption 5.1, p. 15), which the authors call "stronger than the LICQ" (p. 3); bounded X and LICQ at limit points (Assumptions 5.2–5.3, p. 18); strict complementarity for active-set identification (p. 24); partial smoothness, nondegeneracy (5.35) and α bounded away from zero for manifold identification (p. 25).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) A Cauchy-decrease lemma for the projected backtracking step, adapted from Moré [41], with three cases (Lemma 4.3, pp. 7–8). (ii) Lemma 4.5 links ‖v_k(1)‖ = 0 to δ_k = 0, introduced with "We suspect the first result is well-known in the literature but we could not find a suitable reference." (p. 9). (iii) A stationarity measure χ̄_k that uses ‖v_k(1)‖ instead of ‖c_k‖ when no CQ is assumed (5.1), "since ... it is possible that the iterates do not converge toward feasibility" (p. 11). (iv) Lemma 5.5 lower-bounds the merit parameter whenever it decreases by ε_τ‖v_k(1)‖² (p. 12), which gives the dichotomy of Theorem 5.8: either τ stays bounded below and O(ε⁻²) iterations reduce χ̄_k, or τ → 0 and a subsequence approaches infeasible stationarity (p. 14). (v) A shifted merit function Φ̄ that decreases monotonically, from [16, Lemma 3.14] (pp. 13–14), so the complexity bound follows by telescoping (pp. 14–15). (vi) Under Assumption 5.1, Lemma 5.9 gives ‖v_k(β_k)‖/β_k ≥ σ_min‖c_k‖ (p. 15), which converts χ̄ into the KKT measure χ (5.10) and yields Theorem 5.12, O(ε⁻²) (p. 17). (vii) For global convergence under LICQ at limit points: limit points are not infeasible stationary points (Lemma 5.14, p. 18), the limit set is compact (Lemma 5.17, p. 20), a finite cover by balls carries a uniform singular-value bound (Lemma 5.18, pp. 20–21), and then τ is eventually bounded below (Lemma 5.22, p. 22) and the multipliers are bounded (Lemma 5.24, pp. 23–24), which gives Theorem 5.25 (p. 24). (viii) Active-set identification follows in a few lines from the complementarity condition of subproblem (3.9) (Theorem 5.26, pp. 24–25). Manifold identification is reduced to a known partial-smoothness theorem [37, Thm 4.10] by showing dist(ȳ, ∂r(x_k + s_k)) → 0 (Theorem 5.27, pp. 25–26).
  - Transferable: when you assume no CQ, measure infeasibility stationarity by the projected-gradient step of ½‖c‖² (here ‖v_k(1)‖), and state the theorem as a dichotomy on whether the merit parameter stays bounded below (pp. 11, 14).
  - Transferable: to pass from a CQ at limit points to uniform constants along the iterates, cover the compact limit set by finitely many balls on which the singular-value bound holds (Lemmas 5.17–5.19, pp. 20–21).
- **D5 Experiments**: Python implementation; the normal subproblem uses the ∞-norm with Gurobi's barrier method and falls back to the Cauchy point if that is better, and the ℓ1 tangential subproblem is a QP solved by Gurobi's dual active-set solver (pp. 26–27). The implemented α update differs from the analysed one, with a statement of which analysis still holds (p. 27). Tolerances: ‖c‖ ≤ 10⁻⁶ and stationarity and complementarity ≤ 10⁻⁴, with a one-hour limit (p. 27). The comparator, Bazinga (an augmented Lagrangian code, Julia package), is called "the only open source code that can solve problem (1.1)" and stops at 10⁻⁶ on its own measures (p. 27). CUTEst test set (p. 28): problems with a nonconstant objective, 1 ≤ m ≤ n ≤ 100 and at least one inequality, reformulated as (6.4) with an artificial variable a whose ℓ1 weight λ = ‖y_eq‖∞ + 10 makes a = 0 a KKT point by construction. Problems IPOPT could not solve were removed, leaving 81; the pre-filter count is not given (p. 28). Table 6.2 reports six metrics (feasible, feasible with better objective, performs better, a zero, a small, KKT found): 71/13/14/76/76/70 against 67/5/13/55/56/58 for Bazinga (pp. 28–29). A wall-clock performance profile capped at t = 1000 favours Algorithm 3.1 (p. 29). On sparse CCA with planted structure (synthetic data following [13] Chu et al.), the method recovers the support (sl = 0) in all 9 settings, gives sparsity at least as high as Bazinga's, and is faster (pp. 30–31). There is one run per instance and no failure analysis.
  - Transferable: plant the answer. Choose the regularization weight from a reference solver's multipliers so that the sparse KKT point is known (λ = ‖y_eq‖∞ + 10), then count how often each method returns a = 0 exactly (p. 28).
  - Transferable: report several success columns (feasible, better objective, exact structure, KKT) instead of one flag (Table 6.2, p. 29).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds directly on [16] (equality constraints only) and positions against penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods [8, 17, 18, 26, 38, 46] and against the non-penalty methods [7, 51], with a specific weakness named for each (pp. 2–3). The criticism is technical: subproblems assumed feasible [51], equality constraints not handled [7], no complexity result [18] (pp. 2–3). Claims to be "the first proximal-gradient–type method" for this class (p. 31).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions are listed as three algorithmic properties and five results, each tied to a theorem number (p. 3). Every inequality chain is justified step by step with labelled reasons (i)–(vii) (p. 12). The paper is candid about a missing reference (p. 9) and about the implementation departing from the theory (p. 27). The conclusion is one paragraph of restatement with no future work (p. 31). Small slips: the figure caption says "More-Dolen performance profile" (p. 29), and Bazinga's time limit is described as "Algorithm 3.1 is terminated if the running time exceeds 1 hour" (p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithm plus theory (complexity without a CQ and under a sequential CQ, global convergence under LICQ, identification), plus preliminary numerics (abstract; p. 3). Limits are not written as such. Visible from the text: the complexity CQ is an assumption on the iterates (Assumption 5.1, p. 15), not checkable in advance; manifold identification assumes α bounded away from zero (p. 25); only first-order results, no local rate; experiments limited to n ≤ 100 (p. 28). Not stated by the authors: the comparison uses looser stationarity tolerances for Algorithm 3.1 (10⁻⁴) than for Bazinga (10⁻⁶) (p. 27), wall-clock times compare a Python code with a Julia code (pp. 27, 29), and the test set is filtered on a third solver's success (p. 28).

**Method links**: Method 2 ✅ (evidence: one algorithm without a feasibility phase. The merit parameter is updated by (3.10) from predicted decrease in linearized infeasibility (p. 6), the analysis rests on how small τ can get (Lemma 5.5, p. 12; Theorem 5.8, p. 14), and infeasible stationary points are a designed exit (pp. 4–5, 11)). This also bears on Inner Tension 3: penalties are avoided as a problem reformulation (p. 2) while the merit parameter stays inside the step acceptance (p. 6). Method 1 ⚠ (variant: the inner solve's accuracy comes from the outer analysis, but only as Cauchy decrease of the normal subproblem with a solver-plus-fallback implementation, pp. 4–5, 26). Method 3 ⚠ (variant: the CUTEst set is transformed by a mechanical rule, but to plant a known sparse KKT point, not to make the problems infeasible or degenerate, p. 28). Method 4 ✅ (evidence: six success columns instead of one flag, pp. 28–29). Method 5 ✗ (contradiction: asymmetric stopping tolerances, wall-clock comparison across languages, single runs, and filtering on IPOPT's success without the pre-filter count, pp. 27–29; cf. Heuristics 6–7 and Inner Tension 2). Heuristic 10 ✅ (same skeleton as [16], one rung up from equalities to bounds, p. 3). New-pattern candidates: "constrain, don't weight" (p. 2; see S073, S083); "plant the answer" (pp. 28, 30).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Keep the regularizer explicit in a tangential proximal subproblem whose zero step is always feasible (p. 5).
- State the no-CQ complexity theorem as a dichotomy on the merit parameter: bounded below gives O(ε⁻²), τ → 0 gives approach to infeasible stationarity (p. 14).
- Use a finite ball cover of the compact limit set to turn LICQ at limit points into uniform constants (pp. 20–21).

- Build test problems whose sparse KKT point is known by choosing λ above the reference multipliers (p. 28).

### S083 · Fair supervised learning through constraints on smooth nonconvex unfairness-measure surrogates (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2505.15788)
**Read level**: full. Read arXiv v3 (22 Oct 2025), pp. 1–31: main text pp. 1–13, Appendix A proof of Theorem 2.1 (pp. 17–19), Appendix B formal unbiasedness theorem (pp. 19–20), Appendix C dataset statistics (pp. 21–22), Appendix D SQP algorithm and learning-rate rule (pp. 22–25), Appendix E tables (Tables 2–4, pp. 24, 27, 31) and figure captions (pp. 23–30). Figures are captions only in the text layer, so plotted values were not read. References (pp. 13–17) checked for [10], [13], [43], [44], [47].
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Zahra Khatti, Daniel P. Robinson (both Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: follows the 2023 Optimization Letters paper with Liu and Robinson ("Fair machine learning through constrained stochastic optimization and an ϵ-constraint method", ref. [13], p. 14), whose stochastic SQP is the training algorithm here (pp. 7, 22). It is the application side of his stochastic-SQP line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In-processing fair ML (p. 2). Two weaknesses of current practice are named: convex surrogates (e.g. covariance) make a covariance small "but it does not guarantee that the original unfairness measure is kept within reasonable limits", and regularization terms are "expensive and time-consuming to tune" and may not enforce fairness strictly (p. 2). Conflicting multiple measures are a third difficulty (p. 2). Formal entry: Theorem 2.1, adapted from Yao et al. [43], bounds the true demographic-parity violation by the surrogate value plus γ when the surrogate is bounded in [0, 1] and saturates within γ of 0 or 1 on every data point (p. 6). The paper notes that [43] tested it only with regularization, "meaning that such constraints were not necessarily satisfied in practice" (p. 6).
  - Transferable: find a theorem in the application literature whose hypotheses were never enforced in that paper's experiments, and make enforcing them your contribution (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: (a) A bounded, smooth, nonconvex "smoothed-step" surrogate of the Heaviside function, built from ϕ(t) = min{max{0, t + 0.5}, 1} with Chen's smoothing max{t, 0} ≈ ½(t + √(t² + µ)) [10] applied to both maxes (pp. 7–8). (b) The surrogates are scaled, ϕ(αt) with α > 1, to push values toward 0 or 1 as Theorem 2.1 needs; the authors report that this did not make training harder (p. 8). (c) Hard constraints (5) on disparate impact (and equal impact) in the training problem (3) replace regularization (pp. 8–9). (d) Training uses the stochastic SQP of [13] (pp. 7, 24). Its QP has two two-sided constraints and is solved by enumerating active sets from small to large, factoring the KKT matrix once per iteration (Algorithm 1, pp. 23–24). The step size uses a nonmonotone moving-average rule after Zhang–Hager [47] (Algorithm 2, p. 25). (e) Mini-batches stratified by the sensitive attribute keep the surrogate gradient unbiased (Theorem 3.1, p. 9; formal p. 20).
  - Transferable: when a QP subproblem has only a handful of inequality constraints, enumerate candidate active sets from small to large and reuse one factorization, instead of calling a QP solver (pp. 23–24).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Binary sensitive feature and binary label, with extension to finitely many values via pairwise constraints asserted (p. 4). Focus on the independence criterion (p. 4). Prediction by thresholding a network output at τ (p. 5). Theorem 2.1 needs every training point's surrogate value within γ of 0 or 1 (p. 6), which scaling approximates but nothing enforces (p. 8). The unbiasedness theorem needs fixed counts N0, N1 per batch (pp. 19–20). The SQP's convergence assumptions are deferred to [13] (pp. 7, 24).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Proof of Theorem 2.1 (pp. 17–19): shift φ = 2ϕ − 1 to an odd function, split each group by predicted label, and apply the reverse triangle inequality |A| ≤ |A + B| + |B|. The appendix ends with "Note: the bound in theorem 1 of the main paper should be" 2γ + ϵ (p. 19), while the main text states ϵ + γ (p. 6). (ii) Theorem 2.2: the covariance surrogate equals N0N1/N² times the linear-ϕ demographic-parity surrogate (p. 6), which shows the covariance approach is a special, unbounded case outside Theorem 2.1 (pp. 6–7). (iii) Unbiasedness under stratified sampling by linearity of expectation over the conditional distributions P0, P1 (pp. 19–20).
  - Transferable: to criticise a popular surrogate, show it is a special case of your family with one hypothesis of your bound violated (the covariance surrogate as linear ϕ, unbounded, pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Experiments**: Dutch, Law and ACSIncome (a 50,000-point subset), 80:20 split, small networks (one hidden layer, or linear for Law), 500 epochs, full batch in the main text (pp. 9–10). Results are on training data, with test results said to be similar in Appendix E (p. 10). Experiment 4.1: the imposed δ is plotted against the achieved δ̂ for the surrogate (c_di) and for the true predictions (c̄_di). [44], covariance, p. 2), and alongside constrained approaches (Donini et al., Cotter et al., p. 3). [43] for the bound and on the authors' own [13] for the solver (pp. 6–7).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract is a lettered advantages list (a)–(c) (p. 1), and the contributions are bolder than in his optimization papers ("This is the first paper ...", p. 3). The main text contains one informal theorem, with the formal version in the appendix (p. 9). Internal inconsistencies remain in v3: the main-text bound (ϵ + γ, p. 6) against the appendix correction (2γ + ϵ, p. 19); the main text cites Figures 6–9 (pp. 10–11) while its own figures are 2–5 (pp. 11–13); "Adult dataset" in Appendix C (pp. 21–22) where the experiments use ACSIncome; Law listed with 20,798 points (p. 10) and N = 16,638 (Table 1, p. 22); d_lu listed twice in Algorithm 1 line 19 (p. 24).

The only limitation stated: "slightly higher computational costs ... outweighed by the savings that our approach offers in terms of tuning costs" (p. 13). Not stated by the authors: Table 3 runs both approaches over 8 hyperparameter values each (p. 27), so the tuning savings are argued rather than measured; α = 50 is tuned on the evaluation gap (p. 11); results are reported on training data (p. 10).

**Method links**: Method 4 ✅ (evidence, strong: the paper audits a yardstick, showing that a small surrogate does not imply a small unfairness measure. It proves when it does (Theorem 2.1, p. 6), shows that covariance falls outside the bound (pp. 6–7), and plots imposed against achieved levels for the true measure (pp. 10–12)). Method 2 ⚠ (variant, and evidence for Inner Tension 3: at the modelling level the fairness weight λ is removed in favour of hard constraints because tuning it is costly and nonmonotone (pp. 2, 11–12), while the training SQP keeps a merit parameter τ_k (p. 24)). Method 5 ⚠ (variant: equal 8-value grids for both approaches and 5 seeds (pp. 27–30), but the surrogate scale is tuned on the evaluation gap (p. 11) and the cost table contradicts "slightly higher" on ACSIncome (pp. 13, 27); cf. Inner Tension 2). New-pattern candidates: "constrain, don't weight" (pp. 2–3, 11–12; see S073, S082); "rote-learning baselines" (pp. 21–22).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Smooth a bounded piecewise-linear step via composed smoothed maxes (Chen [10]) and scale its argument so values saturate near 0 or 1 (pp. 7–8).
- Stratify mini-batches by the sensitive attribute so that group-difference constraint estimates are unbiased (pp. 9, 19–20).
- Enumerate active sets for a QP with very few inequality constraints, reusing one factorization (pp. 23–24).
- Plot imposed target against achieved level on the true measure, not the surrogate (pp. 10–12).
- Compute label-implied fairness levels and constant-predictor accuracies as reference lines (pp. 21–22).
