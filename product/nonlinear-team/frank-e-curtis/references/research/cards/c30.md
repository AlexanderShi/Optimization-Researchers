### S090 · Low-Order Explicit Hessian Imitation Method for Large-Scale Supervised Machine Learning (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2605.06945)
**Read level**: full. Read arXiv v1, pp. 1–35: §§1–5 (pp. 2–11), Appendix A (auxiliary-loss derivations, pp. 14–15), Appendix B (proof of Theorem 3.2, pp. 16–21; the steps that defer to Défossez et al. [2] skimmed, as the paper itself only cites them), Appendix C (setup, tuning tables, 3-seed summaries, stability table, pp. 21–35). References (pp. 11–13) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yunlang Zhu, Lingjun Guo, Zahra Khatti, Xiaoyi Qu, Chia-Yuan Wu, Lara Zebiane (all Lehigh ISE, p. 2); Curtis is last author · **Place in trajectory**: a stochastic diagonal-scaling (Adam-type) method that cites his SIAM Review survey with Bottou and Nocedal as ref. [1] (p. 11). It applies a classical deterministic idea, the generalized Gauss–Newton split of the Hessian of a composite function, to the second-moment term of Adam.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap between two literatures. Stochastic methods rely on averaging and momentum, while deterministic optimization "is dominated by (quasi-)Newton strategies" (p. 2). The paper asks whether one method can have both (p. 3). The technical hook is a structural criticism of Adam (p. 5). Adam's second-moment term averages the diagonal of ∇f∇fᵀ = ∇p H_{p,ℓ} ∇pᵀ, which approximates the Gauss–Newton term only if ∇ℓ∇ℓᵀ ≈ ∇²ℓ (eq. (4)), and "Assuming that (4) holds is not reasonable in general" (p. 5). A second point is that Adam "does not leverage knowledge of the composite structure" of the loss (p. 4).
  - Transferable: write the incumbent's heuristic quantity (here the squared gradient) as an approximation of a classical quantity (here the Gauss–Newton matrix), then name the exact condition under which the approximation holds (eq. (4), p. 5). The proposed method then fixes that condition.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Keep the GGN-type approximation ∇²f ≈ ∇p ∇²ℓ ∇pᵀ (eq. (5)). Justify it by assuming ∇²p_i ≈ 0 rather than ∇ℓ ≈ 0, because ∇ℓ ≈ 0 "might inherently contradict (4)" (p. 5). Replace the inner ∇ℓ∇ℓᵀ by v vᵀ with v = ∇_p z for an auxiliary loss z chosen so that v vᵀ ≈ ∇²_pp ℓ along the diagonal (eqs. (6)–(7), pp. 5–6). For binary cross-entropy, z = arcsin(tanh(p/2)) gives v² = σ(1−σ) = ℓ'' exactly (Theorem 3.1, p. 6; derivation pp. 14–15). MSE uses z = 1ᵀp (p. 14). Multi-class cross-entropy uses z = Σ arcsin(2σ_i − 1), where the derivative is only "≈" because σ_i depends on all of p (p. 15). Algorithm 2 (LEHI, p. 6) is the simplified Adam of [2] (Algorithm 1, p. 4) with one change: ṽ_k accumulates squared elements of the auxiliary gradient g̃_k instead of g_k² (line 7, p. 6). Momentum m_k, the bias correction α_k and ϵ are unchanged. Cost is "on par with" Adam because ∇f and ∇f̃ share ∇_w p (p. 6). In practice it is a second backward pass in PyTorch (p. 21). LEHIBRID swaps g̃_k for g_k "in every other iteration" (p. 7).
  - Transferable: when an adaptive method uses an outer product of a gradient as a curvature proxy, build an auxiliary function whose gradient squared equals the true second derivative of the outer loss. Then backpropagation delivers the curvature proxy at gradient cost (pp. 5–6, 14–15).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Model: f = ℓ∘p for one input–output pair, "easily generalized" to finite sums (p. 4). The design assumption ∇²_ww p_i ≈ 0 ("p is linear-like" near a solution, p. 5) is a motivation only and is not used in the theorem. Theorem 3.2 (p. 7): f is C¹ with an L-Lipschitz gradient and bounded below on an open convex set containing the iterates; g_k is unbiased (eq. (8)); and max{‖g_k‖_∞, ‖g̃_k‖_∞} ≤ √(M² − ϵ) (eq. (9)). No unbiasedness or accuracy condition is imposed on g̃_k. The theorem only needs g̃ bounded, so the Hessian-imitation property plays no role in the guarantee (pp. 7, 16–21).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) The proof is a transplant of Défossez et al. [2]. Lemma B.1 (p. 16) and Lemma B.3 (p. 18) are quoted with "Please refer to" [2], and the C and D terms defer to "pages 19–20", "page 22" and "page 23" of [2] (pp. 18, 20). (ii) Lemma B.2 (pp. 16–18) lower-bounds E[∇f(w_k)ᵀu_k]. It splits the momentum sum into a lagged-gradient term A and a drift term B (eqs. (11)–(12), pp. 16–17). B is bounded through Lipschitz continuity plus Jensen (eq. (14), p. 17) and a weighted Young inequality with λ = √(1−β1)/(2M√(j+1)) (p. 17). A is decoupled through the conditional surrogate v̂_{k,j,i}, which is measurable before g_{k−j} is drawn, so E[A1] = E[∇_i f² / √(ϵ + v̂)] (pp. 16, 18). (iii) A random output index R_K with P[R_K = k] ∝ 1 − β1^{K−k+1} gives the bound on E‖∇f(w_{R_K})‖² (Theorem 3.2, p. 7). Reader's note: the paper does not show that [2]'s bound on the C term, derived with the denominator built from the same g, carries over when the denominator is built from g̃; it only cites [2] (p. 18). The bound is "as small as desired" by choosing β2 → 1, then α → 0, then K large (p. 7).
  - Transferable: when your method changes only one ingredient of a method with a published proof, reuse that proof's lemmas by citation and re-prove only the step where the changed ingredient enters (here the expectation of A1 with the auxiliary denominator, pp. 16–18). State upfront that the guarantee is no stronger (p. 3).
- **D5 Experiments**: Six tasks (Table 2, p. 21): UCI Protein MLP regression, MNIST MLP, CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with ResNet-18, GPT-2 Small on Penn Treebank, and Llama-3.2-1B on a 2M-token FineWeb-Edu subset (pp. 8–10, 22–23). Baselines are Adam and AdamW, plus Sophia-G for the two language models (p. 7). Protocol: a log-scale learning-rate grid per optimizer (seven to ten values, pp. 8–11, 23–26) with all other hyperparameters fixed. ϵ = 10⁻² for LEHI and 10⁻⁷ for Adam/AdamW, and ϵ is not tuned for either (p. 8). A "confidence bound selection" score µ_K ± 2σ_K over the last ~10% of epochs is used (p. 7). The grid is screened on seed 0, the shortlisted rates are re-run on seeds 1 and 2, and 3-seed summaries are reported (pp. 23, 26–34). The rescreening matters: AdamW at lr 3×10⁻² has the best seed-0 FineWeb score (6.602 against LEHI's 6.674, Table 15, p. 27) but scores 7.2234 over three seeds (Table 22, p. 34). Hyperparameters are selected on the test split for UCI, MNIST, CIFAR and FineWeb. Only PTB uses a validation split, and the authors argue that "the relative improvements between methods remain valid" (p. 23). Margins: UCI 3-seed best scores are LEHIBRID 0.2496 and LEHI 0.2507 against Adam 0.2521 (Table 16, p. 28). PTB test scores are LEHI 3.7626, Sophia 3.8410 and Adam 3.9405 (Table 21, p. 34). FineWeb scores are LEHI 6.6680 and AdamW 6.8205 (Table 22, p. 34). Robustness: a PTB learning-rate sensitivity plot and a stability table with max ‖∇‖_∞, spike counts and NaN failures over 3 seeds (Figure 6, Table 1, pp. 11–12; Table 23, pp. 29–35). LEHI is "STABLE" at every rate from 3×10⁻⁶ to 10⁻¹, while Adam has NaN failures at large rates. Cost: one representative FineWeb run gives 2256.76 ms/step for LEHI against 1062.86 for Adam, with peak memory 58967 MB against 53103 MB (Table 3, p. 22). The overhead is called an artefact of a "non-optimized PyTorch implementation" (p. 21). All curves are per epoch or step, never time-matched. Inconsistencies seen: the appendix text names plotted rates that differ from the figure legends (UCI: "3×10−3 for LEHI" in C.4.1, p. 27, against lr0.1 in Figures 1 and 8, pp. 8, 28; MNIST: 10⁻¹ in C.4.2, p. 27, against lr0.003 in Figures 2 and 9, pp. 9, 29). The MNIST MLP is described with "1 output neuron" for 10 classes (p. 8).
  - Transferable: select hyperparameters with a risk-adjusted score (mean minus or plus 2× standard deviation over a final window) rather than the best final value. Then re-run the shortlisted settings on new seeds and report the re-run, even when it reverses a seed-0 winner (pp. 7, 27, 34).
  - Transferable: add a stability table to a learning-rate sweep, with max gradient norm, spike count and NaN runs per (optimizer, rate) over seeds. It turns "robust to the learning rate" into a count (Table 1, p. 12; Table 23, p. 35).
- **D6 Positioning**: Positioned as "another alternative of Adam designed with a unique motivation in mind" (p. 3), in a short literature review that lists Adagrad, RMSprop, Adam, NAdam, AdamW, AMSGrad and RAdam (p. 3). The analysis base is Défossez et al.'s simplified Adam [2] (p. 3). Sophia-G [9] is the only second-order rival, used for the language models only (p. 7). The paper discusses no Gauss–Newton or K-FAC-type methods beyond the high-level list on pp. 4–5. Criticism of Adam is structural and stated once ("not reasonable in general", p. 5), not rhetorical.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution paragraph hedges the theory before it is stated ("We highlight upfront that the convergence-rate guarantee ... is not stronger than that of Adam", p. 3). The results are framed as "situations when" the method wins (abstract; p. 3). The motivation (§3.1–3.2) is a derivation-driven bullet list of what each method class uses from eqs. (2)–(3) (pp. 4–5). The experiment narrative is figure-by-figure description (pp. 8–11) and stronger than the tables: "consistent gains" on UCI (p. 8) rest on a margin of ~1% in the 3-seed score (Table 16, p. 28). The conclusion names "the derivation of an appropriate auxiliary loss" as the main challenge (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic (an auxiliary-loss curvature proxy), theoretical (an Adam-level rate under bounded g̃) and empirical (six tasks up to 1.23B parameters, p. 10). Limits stated: each loss needs its own auxiliary loss (p. 11); runtime is about 2× Adam per step in the current implementation (pp. 21–22); and test-set tuning on most benchmarks (p. 23). Future work: a fused implementation and amortized, less frequent auxiliary-gradient updates "similar to ... Sophia-G" (p. 21); a conjecture that the choice of auxiliary loss gives flexibility "in a wide variety of settings" (p. 11). Not stated by the authors: the theorem does not use the Hessian-imitation property (D3), and wall-clock comparisons are absent.

**Method links**: Method 5 ⚠ (variant: the same grid and tuning rule for every optimizer, "an identical tuning setting" (p. 23), 3 seeds, and an incumbent winner reported when it shows up (seed-0 AdamW, pp. 27, 34). But tuning is on the test split for most tasks (p. 23), ϵ differs and is untuned (p. 8), and step-matched curves hide the ~2× per-step cost (Table 3, p. 22), so step 6 of Method 5, time-matching, is not done). Method 4 ⚠ (variant: the paper designs its own yardstick, a risk-adjusted selection score that rewards stability (p. 7), and states it; but the hidden trade-off, wall-clock time, is not shown). Also practises Heuristic 10 (keep the simplified-Adam skeleton and change only the second-moment ingredient, pp. 4, 6). New-pattern candidate: "match the incumbent's theorem, win in the experiments" (p. 3; also in card S015, c16). New-pattern candidate: "curvature by auxiliary function", which exploits composite structure ℓ∘p so that backpropagation delivers a second-derivative proxy at gradient cost (pp. 5–6, 14–15).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Rewrite the incumbent's heuristic statistic as an approximation of a classical object and isolate the condition under which it is valid (eq. (4), p. 5).
- Design an auxiliary function whose squared derivative equals the target second derivative (binary cross-entropy: arcsin(tanh(p/2)), pp. 6, 14–15).
- Prove the new method's rate by transplanting the incumbent's proof and re-deriving only the step the change touches; say upfront that the rate is no better (pp. 3, 16–18).
- Use a risk-adjusted (µ ± 2σ over a final window) selection score, rescreen shortlisted settings on fresh seeds, and add a NaN/spike stability table to every learning-rate sweep (pp. 7, 12, 23–35).
**Quotes**:
- "We highlight upfront that the convergence-rate guarantee for our proposed algorithm is not stronger than that of Adam; the point is that the algorithm has a comparable guarantee and can perform better in practice in various settings." (p. 3)
- "Assuming that (4) holds is not reasonable in general." (p. 5)

### S091 · A Gradient Sampling Algorithm for Noisy Nonsmooth Optimization (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2604.00278)
**Read level**: full. Read arXiv v2 / COR@L 26T-006-R1 (revised 13 Aug 2026), pp. 1–32: §§1–5 (pp. 2–23) with every lemma and proof in §3 (pp. 9–16), all experiment subsections (pp. 17–22), Algorithm 1 (p. 27) and the figure captions (pp. 26–32). References (pp. 23–26) skimmed. The figures themselves are not in the text layer, only their captions. Citations that need pages should use the version form [S091 arXiv v2 p. N] if the index file differs.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Albert S. Berahas (University of Michigan IOE), Lara Zebiane (Lehigh ISE) (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: the "noisy" rung of his gradient-sampling (GS) line. The paper cites SQP-GS [11], adaptive GS [12], the quasi-Newton GS method [13], inexact-subproblem GS [9], the 2025 MOS-SIAM book with Robinson [14] and the NonOpt software [16] as its own predecessors (pp. 2, 24). Berahas brings the noisy-smooth line ([1], Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal) (pp. 6, 23).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an assumption shared by the whole GS literature: "all previously proposed GS methods share the assumption that exact objective function and generalized gradient values are available" (p. 3). Noisy smooth optimization has guarantees, but "In the context of a GS method ... noisy optimization has not been explored" (p. 3). The paper explains why GS rather than bundle methods should be extended: GS is "arguably simpler to implement", and nonconvex proximal-bundle methods need "downshifting or tilting" parameters (p. 4). Noise is bounded and deterministic: the same x gives the same value, and errors "may be biased and uncontrollable" (pp. 3, 6).
  - Transferable: frame a new setting as the removal of one assumption shared by every paper in a line (exactness), and justify the line chosen for extension against its nearest rival by naming a concrete burden of the rival (tuning parameters for downshifting/tilting, p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (Noise-Tolerant GS, p. 27) keeps the GS skeleton: m ≥ n+1 points are sampled in a ball of radius ϵ_k, and the min-norm element of the convex hull of sampled approximate gradients is computed through the QP pair (4) (p. 8). Three things change (pp. 8–9). (a) The Armijo backtracking condition is relaxed by +ϵ_ls with ϵ_ls > 2ϵ_f (eq. (5), p. 8). (b) If backtracking falls below γϵ_k/(3(L_{f,X}+ϵ_g)), the step is set to zero and the method resamples (lines 14–16, p. 27). The authors justify this by "bad sample sets" rather than noise: "it is possible for the algorithm to yield insufficient decrease due to a bad sample set" (p. 9). (c) The differentiability check of standard GS is dropped because the algorithm cannot determine membership in D_∇f (p. 8). Sampling-radius reduction is triggered by ‖g̃_k‖ ≤ max{νϵ_k, ϕϵ_g} (line 6, p. 27). Termination fires when θϵ_k drops below max{ζ, ϵ_x}, with ζ = (6ϵ_ls(L+ϵ_g)/(ηγν²))^{1/3} (p. 27).
  - Transferable: when moving a sampling-based method to noisy oracles, separate the two causes of a failed line search, noise and a bad random sample. Handle noise by a relaxation term (ϵ_ls) and bad samples by a step-size floor that triggers resampling (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.1 (p. 5): f is locally Lipschitz and |f̃ − f| ≤ ϵ_f everywhere. A Lipschitz constant L_{f,X} of the true f on an enlargement of the f̃-sublevel set is "known by the algorithm". The authors call this "The strongest part of Assumption 2.1" (p. 6) and say it is not needed by noisy-smooth methods [1, 38] or by exact GS (p. 6). f̃ may be discontinuous (p. 6). Assumption 2.2 (p. 6) is "a unique contribution to the literature". (a) g̃(x) ∈ ∂_{ϵx} f(x) + B(ϵ_g), a Goldstein ϵ_x-subdifferential plus an ϵ_g ball. (b) ∂_{ϵ/3} f(x) ⊆ G̃_ϵ(x) + B(ϵ_g) for ϵ ≥ ϵ_x. This "decouple[s]" errors from misidentifying the point of nondifferentiability (ϵ_x) from ordinary derivative errors (ϵ_g) (pp. 6–8). It reduces to the smooth noisy assumption when ϵ_x = 0 (p. 8). Three worked |x| examples (Examples 2.1–2.3, pp. 7–8; Figure 1, p. 26) compute admissible (ϵ_x, ϵ_g), e.g. ϵ_x > 3δ/2 and ϵ_g = 0 for a δ-shifted kink. Assumption 3.1 (p. 14) posits a positive probability of a "good" sample set, which replaces the noiseless argument based on continuous differentiability almost everywhere. That argument fails because g̃ may be discontinuous (p. 13). The factor 1/3 in 2.2(b) is "a natural choice" for the step-size bound (p. 6).
  - Transferable: when an error has two physically different sources, give each its own constant in the assumption (a domain shift ϵ_x and a value error ϵ_g), so that the bound degrades through the right channel. Then check each constant on a worked one-dimensional example (pp. 6–8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Lemma 3.1 (pp. 10–11) is a noisy version of Kiwiel's GS lemma [31, Lemma 3.1]. If dist_{G̃}(0) > 2ϵ_g and G lies within ϵ_g of G̃, then a near-min-norm ũ satisfies ũᵀv > η‖ũ‖² for all v ∈ G. The proof is by contradiction, through compactness and the projection theorem. (ii) Lemma 3.2 (p. 11) collects deterministic invariants: radius bounds, sublevel containment, ‖g̃_k‖ ≤ L+ϵ_g, and decrease > ϵ_ls whenever α_k > 0 (eq. (8)). (iii) Lemma 3.3 (pp. 12–13) gives the step-size lower bound under a good sample set. It uses a contradiction with Lebourg's mean value theorem: the failed trial point lies within ϵ_k/3, so its generalized gradient must be in ∂_{ϵ_k/3} f, which conflicts with (12). The condition ϵ_ls > 2ϵ_f absorbs the two noise terms (p. 13). (iv) Lemma 3.4 (pp. 14–15) shows that a non-terminating run with f̃ bounded below has probability zero. Each eventually-constant-radius event has probability zero by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma, and the countable union is then taken (p. 15). (v) Lemma 3.5 (p. 15) gives dist(0, ∂_{2ϵ_k} f) ≤ ‖g̃_k‖ + ϵ_g. (vi) Theorem 3.1 (pp. 15–16): almost surely either f(x_k) → −∞, or termination with ϵ_k = O(max{ϵ_ls^{1/3}, ϵ_x}) and stationarity O(ϵ_g + max{ϵ_ls^{1/3}, ϵ_x}). The unexpected exponent 1/3 instead of 1/2 is announced before the analysis (p. 10) and explained after the proof: the step-size floor is proportional to ϵ_k, so (8) has ϵ_k³ (p. 16). The paper also warns that iterates may leave and return to approximate stationarity in practice (p. 16).
  - Transferable: if your rate has a surprising exponent, flag it before the analysis and trace it after the proof to the single lemma that causes it (pp. 10, 16).
  - Transferable: for "terminates almost surely", define the event that the radius is eventually constant at index k̂, show that it has probability zero with the second Borel–Cantelli lemma under a per-iteration success probability, and take the countable union (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Experiments**: All runs use NonOpt's Python interface with GradientCombination and Backtracking (p. 17). The implementation is not Algorithm 1: it uses quasi-Newton matrices and adaptive sampling, m = 10, no Lipschitz constant (step floor 10⁻²⁰), ϵ_1 = 10, ϵ_x = 0 and NonOpt's own termination. The authors "expect" the theory to extend [12, 13] (p. 17). Three setups. (1) Nonsmooth Rosenbrock f = (1−x)² + 100|y − 2x² + 1| with artificial uniform noise, ϵ_f ∈ {10⁻¹,…,10⁻⁴}, ϵ_g = √ϵ_f, ϵ_ls = 2.1ϵ_f, one run per level from (−1.2, 1) (p. 17; Figures 2–4, pp. 28–29). (2) A purpose-built nonsmooth, nonconvex semilinear-elliptic PDE control problem (n = 64 actuators, 32×32 grid). Its nonsmooth features "are introduced to obtain nonsmoothness" (p. 18). It has a computable L ≈ 4.26 (p. 19) and a provable error bound from the Picard residual tolerance τ (eq. (22), p. 19). The final gap f(x_500) − f_ref is ≈ 1.13, 0.93, 3.72 and 5.05 × ϵ_f (p. 20; Table 1, p. 21). Measured max |f̃ − f| is "between one and two orders of magnitude below" ϵ_f (p. 20). The share of iterates with misidentified active indices falls from 29% to 2%, while their gradient errors stay ≈ 0.14–0.15 regardless of ϵ_g (pp. 20–21; Figure 6, p. 30). (3) A heart-disease binary classifier (1,026 points, one hidden ReLU layer) (p. 21). With adaptive sampling to enforce known bounds ("for demonstration purposes only", p. 21), samples trade off against accuracy (Figure 7). With fixed mini-batches of 128 and an ϵ_ls sweep (p. 22), ϵ_ls ∈ {0.1, 1, 10} gives 99.7–100% accuracy, small ϵ_ls stalls (48–83%) and ϵ_ls = 100 drops to ~93% (p. 22). Accuracy is "over the training dataset" (Figure 8 caption, p. 32). No baseline solver is run in any setup: no exact-oracle GS, no proximal-bundle method with inexact information such as [26], and no stochastic subgradient method. Results are single runs per setting, as far as the text says (pp. 17–22).
  - Transferable: build a test problem whose noise is controllable and certifiable (here through a PDE residual tolerance with an M-matrix bound), so that the claim "accuracy ∝ ϵ_f" can be checked against measured gaps and measured errors over several orders of magnitude (pp. 18–21).
  - Transferable: instrument the assumption itself. Split iterates by whether the error mechanism the theory separates actually occurred (misidentified active index or not), and plot the two error populations against the tolerance (Figure 6, p. 30; p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends the authors' own GS line [5, 6, 9–16] and the noisy-smooth line [1, 38] (pp. 2–4, 6). Davis et al.'s GS-with-complexity method [19] is "quite distinct in spirit" (p. 4), and subgradient-type guarantees "require somewhat restrictive assumptions, such as weak convexity" (p. 4). Hare–Sagastizábal–Solodov [26] is the closest relative (p. 4). The paper claims to be "the first gradient-sampling method that explicitly incorporates bounded evaluation errors" (p. 3; repeated p. 22). The criticism of rivals is about scope or tuning burden, not about performance, and none of the rivals is tested.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Heavy use of explicit concessions: requiring knowledge of the Lipschitz constant is called "not ideal" (p. 9); the 1/3 exponent "may be surprising" (p. 10); "Such a conclusion is all that one can expect in our noisy setting" (p. 10). Every requirement in (6) gets a bullet saying why it is there and that it is "always satisfiable" (pp. 8–9). An informal preview of the theorem precedes the analysis (p. 10). The parameter-insensitivity claim, that only ϵ_ls matters (p. 16), is stated as experience: no sweep over γ, η, θ, ν, ϵ_x or ϵ_g is shown. The PDE section argues that the decoupled assumption is "not merely a technical convenience" from measured data (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic and theoretical (the first noise-tolerant GS method with an a.s. approximate-stationarity guarantee, a new two-constant noise assumption) plus an implementation in NonOpt. Limits stated: the Lipschitz constant must be known in theory (pp. 6, 9); the exponent is 1/3 (p. 16); iterates can drift from approximate stationarity after the theoretical tolerance is reached (p. 16); and the analysed algorithm differs from the implementation (p. 17). Future work: estimate L from max |f̃(x_k) − f̃(x_{k+1})|/‖x_k − x_{k+1}‖ over long steps (p. 22) and extend the theory to quasi-Newton or adaptive-sampling variants (p. 17). Not stated: the absence of any comparison method, and the fact that no complexity bound is given.

**Method links**: Method 4 ⚠ (variant: the audit is applied to the theory's own noise model rather than to a benchmark measure. Bounding all gradient error by ϵ_g alone would force ϵ_g ≈ 0.15 and make the theorem "vacuous", so measured error populations justify the decoupling (pp. 20–21)). Method 3 ⚠ (variant: the hard instance is built to have a property, nonsmoothness "introduced to obtain nonsmoothness" plus certifiable noise (p. 18); it is not a mechanical corruption of a standard set; artificial noise on the Rosenbrock function over four orders of magnitude, p. 17).
