### S023 · Recent developments in security-constrained AC optimal power flow: Overview of challenge 1 in the ARPA-E grid optimization competition (Operations Research 2023, DOI 10.1287/OPRE.2022.0315; read as arXiv:2206.07843 v1)
**Read level**: full: main text pp. 1–27 read in full (§§1–6, endnotes); references pp. 28–37 skimmed; e-companion EC.1 (phasor example, pp. 38–41) skimmed, EC.2 (platform details, pp. 41–43) read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: I. Aravena, D. K. Molzahn, S. Zhang, C. G. Petra (team gollnlp), F. E. Curtis, S. Tu, A. Wächter, E. Wei, E. Wong (team GO-SNIP), A. Gholami, K. Sun, X. A. Sun (team GMI-GO), S. T. Elbert, J. T. Holzer, A. Veeramany (organizers, PNNL) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Curtis's only applied power-systems overview; companion to the GO-SNIP algorithm paper (Curtis et al. 2021, arXiv:2110.01737, cited p. 31). It is a joint voice of 16 authors; no passage is attributable to Curtis alone.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry point is an external event, not a gap in a method: ARPA-E's Grid Optimization Competition Challenge 1 (2018) and a special issue holding the top three teams' papers (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). The problem is the preventive SC-AC-OPF model (24): minimize base-case generation cost plus penalized slacks, with base-case, post-contingency and complementarity-type transition constraints for voltage control and droop control (eqs. (7)–(12), pp. 7–8; (24), p. 11). It is called "large-scale, nonsmooth, nonconvex, and nonlinear", with multiple local minima and a strongly NP-hard feasibility problem (p. 2). The applied stake is quantified as $6B to $19B of US electricity costs yearly (p. 2). The paper is written for operations researchers without power-engineering training and answers questions the organizers heard during the competition, "why are there angles?" and "why are there two types of power?" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when writing for a neighbouring community, build the problem section around the questions outsiders actually asked, starting from DC circuits and ending at the full model (§2, pp. 3–11; phasor worked example in EC.1, pp. 38–41).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: No new algorithm. §5 summarizes the three winners (pp. 22–24). GO-SNIP (Curtis's team) used "an interior-point method that iteratively enforces constraints associated with additional contingencies", contingency screening with parallel processing, "tailored heuristics for complementarity constraints", and avoided "certain degeneracies by modifying the problem formulation"; it found best-known solutions in 20% of cases and second-best in over half (p. 23). gollnlp used nonconvex relaxations of complementarity, two-stage decomposition and asynchronous parallelism, best-known in 58% (pp. 22–23). GMI-GO used a two-level ADMM with smoothing of complementarity (p. 23). Shared features: decomposition or iterative contingency incorporation, contingency ranking and screening, approximation or relaxation of the complementarity constraints, parallelism, fallback mechanisms for failures, C++, and Ipopt with HSL (pp. 23–24).
  - Transferable: when comparing several winning designs, list the shared elements first and only then the distinguishing ones (p. 23–24); the overlap (all three used Ipopt+HSL, fallbacks, screening) is itself a finding.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Preventive model: no decisions between base case and post-contingency; the transition is governed by automatic controllers (p. 6; endnote 1, p. 27). Voltage control and droop control are complementarity constraints (pp. 7–8). Soft constraints: power-balance and line-overload violations are slack variables with convex penalties in the objective (eqs. (20)–(24), pp. 10–11), and the contingency part of the objective is averaged over |K| (p. 11). Polar formulation chosen among alternatives (p. 10). The competition split solving into Code 1 (base case, 10 or 45 minutes) and Code 2 (post-contingency, two seconds per contingency) (pp. 19–20).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a (no theorems). One analytical remark only: "analytical derivations showed that the two scoring methods were approximately equal to each other under a monotone transformation" (p. 21), derivation not given.
- **D5 Numerical experiments (evaluation design of the competition)**: 27 teams (p. 16). Final event: 17 synthetic networks × 20 scenarios plus 3 confidential industrial networks × 4 scenarios = 352 problems, 500 to 30,000 buses, thousands of contingencies (p. 17). Fixed hardware: six 24-core nodes, 64 GB each, 144 cores, meant to mimic an ISO's resources (p. 18). Hardware failures were requeued, timeouts rerun once, bad results not rerun unless resource contention was suspected (p. 19). Scoring: hard constraints checked, soft violations penalized by piecewise-linear functions, so "any “infeasible” solution is not really infeasible, but only worse than a feasible one to some degree" (p. 20). Two scoring methods: geometric mean over scenarios then networks, and area under performance profiles (p. 21). A constant term in any objective distorts relative and absolute optimality measures, and network size scales objective values; the geometric mean was meant to keep every instance meaningful (p. 21). Failed, unreadable, infeasible or very poor outputs received a precomputed "worst-case" score from a projected reference operating point (pp. 21–22; footnote 8, p. 28). Scale: over 200,000 computing tasks from 70,000 problems, 16 million CPU-hours (p. 18).
  - Transferable: assign every failed or malformed run a fixed, precomputed fallback score instead of dropping it, so robustness enters the ranking (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: when two aggregate scores are proposed, check whether they are monotone transforms of each other before claiming they reward different things (p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: History runs from economic dispatch (1900s) through Carpentier's 1962 OPF and Dy Liacco's security constraints after the 1965 blackout, to Benders and generalized Benders decomposition, LP-based industrial codes and later NLP (pp. 11–13). Parallel decomposition of IPM factorizations (Qiu et al.; Petra 2019) and formulation-level decomposition with Ipopt subproblems are cited (pp. 13–14). Approximations (DC power flow), convex relaxations and convex restrictions are presented with their trade-offs: relaxations bound the optimum but may give infeasible points (pp. 14–15). Positioned against other competitions (metaheuristics, L2RPN, Kaggle), with the claim that existing platforms lack timed runs on fixed hardware (pp. 15–16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Tutorial tone in §2 (pp. 3–11). Results are stated for each team in percentages of best-known solutions (pp. 22–23). The conclusion is unusually self-critical for an overview: three listed advantages, then three listed limitations, each in bolded bullets (pp. 25–26). Hedging is light; claims are framed as "support for efforts to move nonlinear optimization techniques into industrial applications" (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: problem formulation for OR readers, history, competition design and a summary of winning algorithms (abstract, p. 1; p. 24). Stated limits of competition research: scoring rewards implementation over theory and neglects suboptimality bounds, infeasibility proofs and worst-case rates (p. 25); benchmark results "may overlook alternative algorithms" that lacked robust implementations (p. 26); unseen datasets preclude offline, data-driven methods, partly answered by Challenge 2's Monarch-of-the-Mountain event, where only 2 of 62 improved scenarios gained more than 1% (p. 26); secrecy during competition disincentivized dissemination (p. 26). Future work: Challenges 2 and 3 (multi-period, corrective controls, switching), uncertainty, stability, distributed algorithms (p. 27). Not reported: per-team numerical tables or the industrial-network results (teams received only overall scores for confidential data, p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (evidence ✅, joint voice): the paper audits its own yardstick: why geometric-mean scoring, what a constant objective term does to optimality measures, the near-equivalence of the two scoring rules (p. 21), and the admission that implementation-weighted scoring may hide better algorithms (p. 26).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅, joint voice): common hardware, formulation and scoring are named as the gap the competition fills: earlier OPF work used "incomparable problem formulations", "inconsistent datasets, varying computing hardware" (p. 25).
- Method 2 (variant ⚠): the exact-penalty view of infeasibility appears here in the problem statement and scoring (soft constraints with penalty slacks, pp. 10–11, 20), not as a steering rule inside an algorithm.
- Method 3 (variant ⚠): GO-SNIP removed degeneracies "by modifying the problem formulation" (p. 23), the opposite move to manufacturing degenerate variants; detail is in the GO-SNIP paper, not read here.
- new-pattern candidate: "Competition as benchmark protocol": unseen instances, fixed hardware, timed runs, worst-case fallback score (pp. 17–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Precompute a feasible "worst-case" reference solution per instance and give it to any failed run; robustness then costs score instead of disappearing from the comparison (pp. 21–22).
- Split a two-stage solver into a timed first-stage code and a separately timed recourse code, matching what practice needs (base-case setpoints) and what evaluation needs (recourse solutions) (pp. 19–20).
- End an overview with explicit limitations of the evaluation itself, not only of the methods (pp. 25–26).

**Quotes**:
- "From this perspective, any “infeasible” solution is not really infeasible, but only worse than a feasible one to some degree." (p. 20)
- "Finally, since implementation quality was of utmost importance to avoid “worst-case” scores from code failures, the benchmark results from the competition may overlook alternative algorithms that could have performed better if they had more robust implementations or had better exploited the parallel computing resources." (p. 26)

### S043 · Worst-case complexity of TRACE with inexact subproblem solutions for nonconvex smooth optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2023, DOI 10.1137/22M1492428; read as arXiv:2204.11322 v1 / COR@L TR 22T-006)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–25 read in full (cover, §§1–5, all algorithms, lemmas, theorems and proofs, numerical section); references pp. 25–27 skimmed. Figures 1–4 are performance profiles whose curves are not in the text layer; only captions and the authors' descriptions were read (pp. 23–25).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Qi Wang (Lehigh, student) (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: follows TRACE (Curtis, Robinson, Samadi 2017, ref. [12]) and closes the inexact-subproblem conjecture stated there (p. 2); sits beside the inexact regularized Newton framework [13] and trust-region Newton-CG with Royer and Wright [14] (p. 26).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: TRACE was the first trust-region method with O(ε^{-3/2}) iteration complexity but "requires exact solutions of the arising trust-region subproblems, which is impractical in large-scale settings" (p. 2). The paper answers the conjecture from [12], quoted verbatim, that variants with inexact solves could keep the guarantees (p. 2). Motivation is also practical: cubic-regularization methods "can disappoint in practice compared to traditional trust-region algorithms" (p. 4), so a near-traditional trust-region method with optimal complexity is worth having (p. 4). The cost measure is chosen deliberately: Hessian-vector products, "the most expensive operations required when solving many large-scale problems" (p. 3). Second-order complexity is put out of scope as impractical at large scale (p. 4).
  - Transferable: open by quoting your own earlier paper's conjecture and state how far the new result goes beyond it (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: i-TRACE keeps TRACE's acceptance ratio ρ_k(s) = (f_k − f(x_k+s))/‖s‖³ (eq. (7), p. 8) and its expansion/contraction logic, and replaces the exact subproblem solve by a truncated Lanczos trust-region method (tltr, Algorithm 1, p. 7) over Krylov subspaces K_{k,j} (p. 5). The subproblems are solved exactly in the tridiagonal reduced space, S_{k,j}(δ) by Moré–Sorensen and R_{k,j}(λ) by a tridiagonal solve (p. 6). Inexactness is controlled by the full-space residual tests (5a) ‖r‖ ≤ ξ1‖s‖² or (5b)–(5c) (p. 6), checked cheaply in reduced form (6) via μ_{k,j} = γ_{k,j+1}|e_{j+1}^T t_{k,j}| (p. 7; Lemma 3.1, p. 10). Find Decrease Step (fds, Algorithm 2, pp. 8–9) adjusts the radius with the Krylov subspace fixed; if the accepted step fails (6), the subspace is enlarged by one Lanczos step and fds is called again (Algorithm 3, p. 9). What did not change: the ‖s‖³ decrease test, the σ-interval on λ/‖t‖, and the contraction rules from TRACE (p. 8). Dropped for brevity: TRACE's auxiliary sequence {Δ_k}, made unnecessary by assuming a Lipschitz Hessian upfront (pp. 3, 9–10).
  - Transferable: to make an exact-subproblem complexity method inexact, keep the outer acceptance test unchanged and nest a "grow the Krylov subspace" loop around the radius-update loop, so the radius logic always works on a fixed reduced problem (Algorithm 3, p. 9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1.1: f ∈ C², bounded below, with Lipschitz gradient and Hessian on an open convex set containing the iterates and all trial points (p. 4). The authors defend stating it on algorithmic quantities ("should not be seen as a deficiency of our analysis") by monotone decrease and the ‖s‖³ decrease (pp. 3–4). Assumption 3.1: g_k ≠ 0 (p. 10). The Hessian-vector bound O(ε^{-3/2} min{n, log ε^{-1}}) requires a uniform (κ̄, λ̄) bounding the condition number of the regularized tridiagonal matrix (Lemma 3.17, pp. 18–19; Theorem 3.2, p. 20); otherwise the bound is O(ε^{-3/2} n) (p. 20). Local rate: Assumption 3.2, a limit point with H(x*) ≻ 0 and locally Lipschitz Hessian (p. 21). Admitted convenience: global convergence under weaker assumptions (no Lipschitz Hessian) is claimed possible but not proved, "for the sake of brevity" (pp. 3, 9).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Well-posedness chain: Lanczos terminates by the invariant-subspace index (8) (Lemma 3.2, pp. 10–11); the inexact step satisfies (4a)–(4d) because r ⊥ Q (Lemma 3.1/3.2, pp. 10–11); the line-15–16 interval search is well posed by monotonicity of λ/‖t(λ)‖ (Lemma 3.3, p. 11). (b) Decrease when λ is large: λ ≥ (H_Lip + 2η)‖t‖ ⇒ ρ_k ≥ η, from a Taylor bound and the model decrease ≥ ½λ‖t‖² that uses r^T s ≤ 0 (Lemma 3.6, p. 12). (c) Counting inside fds: index sets A, E, C; at most one expansion because an expansion or contraction cannot be followed by an expansion (Lemmas 3.7–3.8, p. 13); finitely many contractions by contradiction λ → ∞ (Lemma 3.10, p. 14); uniform bound K_C on contractions by a geometric growth argument on λ/‖t‖ between σ_lower and σ_max (Lemma 3.18, p. 20). (d) The key step-to-next-gradient inequality ‖s_k‖ ≥ c‖g_{k+1}‖^{1/2} (Lemma 3.14, p. 16), where the inexact residual enters only through max{ξ1, ξ2 g_Lip}; summing f_{k−1} − f_k ≥ η‖s_{k−1}‖³ gives the O(ε^{-3/2}) count (Theorem 3.1, pp. 16–17). (e) Hessian-vector count from the Gould–Simoncini Lanczos residual bound ‖r‖ ≤ (2‖g‖H_max κ/(ω+λ))((√κ−1)/(√κ+1))^j (Lemma 3.16, p. 18), solved for the j at which (6a) or (6b)–(6c) must hold (Lemma 3.17, pp. 18–20); the hard case is avoided because only small residuals, not near-global subproblem solutions, are needed (p. 17). (f) Function evaluations = K_C × Hv bound (Theorem 3.3, p. 21). (g) Local Q-superlinear/quadratic rate by forcing μ_{k,j} = o(‖g_k‖) or O(‖g_k‖²) and citing Dembo–Eisenstat–Steihaug (Theorem 3.4, p. 22), after showing all late steps are interior Newton-type steps (Lemma 3.20, pp. 21–22). Several proofs are omitted as "the same reasoning as in" TRACE (Lemma 3.12, p. 15).
  - Transferable: when converting an exact-solve complexity proof to inexact solves, find the single inequality that links step norm to the next gradient (here Lemma 3.14) and design the inner termination test so the residual enters that inequality as just another O(‖s‖²) term (pp. 6, 16).
  - Transferable: get an inner-iteration count by inverting a Krylov residual bound of the form C·ρ^j against your termination test, which yields log(1/ε) under bounded conditioning and n otherwise (pp. 18–20).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: i-TRACE, TRACE and ARC "were implemented in a single software package in Matlab"; 90-minute wall-clock and 8 GB limits on Lehigh's COR@L cluster (p. 22). For fairness the i-TRACE and TRACE codes both include the auxiliary sequence {Δ_k} (p. 22). Parameters listed; three inexactness settings (ξ1, ξ2) ∈ {(0.1, 0.01), (1, 0.1), (9, 0.9)}, ξ3 = 10⁶ (p. 22). ARC uses Lanczos with termination TC.s, which equals (6b) with κ_θ ≡ ξ2, run with matching values {0.01, 0.1, 0.9} (p. 23). Common termination ‖g_k‖ ≤ 10⁻⁵ max{1, ‖g_0‖} (eq. (14), p. 23). Test set: all 238 unconstrained CUTEst problems (p. 23). Solved: i-TRACE 214/218/219 by setting, TRACE 188 "due to hitting the time or memory limit much more often" (p. 23); ARC 211/214/216 (p. 24). Profiles (Dolan–Moré, τ axis cut at 20) for function evaluations, gradient evaluations and Hessian-vector products, on problems solved by all compared codes: 188, 207, 211, 214 (pp. 23–25). Findings: i-TRACE ≈ TRACE in function and gradient evaluations; setting 1 needs more Hv products but fewer gradient evaluations (pp. 23–24); i-TRACE "performs at least as well as" ARC, best edge in Hv products at setting 3 (pp. 24–25). Not reported: CPU time, failure lists, run-to-run variation (deterministic codes).
  - Transferable: when your rival has an inexactness parameter too, map it onto yours (TC.s ≡ (6b) with κ_θ ≡ ξ2) and sweep both at matched values, producing one profile per matched setting (pp. 23–25).
  - Transferable: report the reliability count per setting first, then profile only the commonly solved set, so the profile is not read as a robustness claim (pp. 23–24).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on classical trust-region O(ε⁻²) results (tight, Cartis–Gould–Toint) and the optimal O(ε^{-3/2}) of Nesterov–Polyak and ARC (p. 4). Concedes that cubic methods can be competitive with more complicated parameter updates (Gould–Porcelli–Toint, p. 4). Names TRACE and the Newton-CG trust-region method [14] and the Royer–Wright line search [32] as alternatives (p. 4). Credits Lanczos trust-region literature and says "The results in [22] play an important role in this paper" (p. 5). Self-citation is heavy but explicit (refs [11]–[14], p. 26).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contribution sentence in §1.3: "the first inexact variant of trace ... that offers iteration and gradient evaluation complexity bounds that match those of trace" (p. 5). Every assumption choice is followed by a defence paragraph (pp. 3–4, 9–10). Repeated "for the sake of brevity" to skip the Δ_k analysis and some proofs (pp. 3, 9, 10, 15). Small slips: Section 3.2 refers to "(1.2)" for bound (2) (p. 15); fds returns "δ_{k,j,t}" (p. 8); Theorem 3.3 reuses the name K_H(ε) for function evaluations (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: same iteration and gradient complexity as TRACE, a Hessian-vector bound O(ε^{-3/2} min{n, log ε^{-1}}) under a conditioning assumption, same local rates (pp. 5, 20–22, 25). Algorithmic: i-TRACE. Empirical: a user-tunable trade-off between derivative evaluations and Hv products (pp. 3, 24). Limits visible but not all stated: the tested code includes Δ_k while the analysis drops it, and the paper only claims that guarantees are kept (pp. 9, 22); the log(1/ε) bound depends on an unverifiable uniform conditioning condition (p. 20); first-order complexity only (p. 4); no CPU time; Matlab prototype. No future directions in the conclusion (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (evidence ✅): the Lanczos termination tests (5)/(6) are derived from what the outer complexity proof needs; "finding subproblem solutions with residuals that are sufficiently small is all that is needed for our purposes" (p. 17; Lemma 3.14, p. 16). Variant detail: here the outer requirement is a complexity inequality, not a merit-function model decrease.
- Method 5 (evidence ✅): the ARC rival is built in the same Matlab package with a matched inexactness rule and identical termination test (pp. 22–23); TRACE and i-TRACE share Δ_k "for a fair comparison" (p. 22). Tuning effort for the shared parameters is not reported.
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): reports function evaluations, gradient evaluations and Hv products as separate profiles and presents the trade-off between them as the result, instead of one aggregate cost (pp. 3, 23–24).
- new-pattern candidate: "Inexactness as a user dial": present the inner-accuracy parameter as a knob that trades one expensive operation against another, and show both sides (pp. 3, 24).
- new-pattern candidate: "Tested algorithm ≠ analyzed algorithm, disclosed": the code keeps Δ_k that the analysis dropped, with a claim that guarantees survive (pp. 9, 22); same pattern in S070 (ζ, p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Quote the conjecture from your own earlier paper at the start and resolve it (p. 2).
- Check inner termination in the reduced (tridiagonal) space with the Lanczos identity ‖r‖ = γ_{j+1}|e_{j+1}^T t| instead of forming the full-space residual (Lemma 3.1, p. 10; Algorithm 1 line 14, p. 7).
- Offer two alternative inner termination tests, a step-norm one that is always satisfiable and a gradient-relative one that may stop earlier, and prove the same complexity for either (pp. 6–7, 18).
- Bound the number of contractions per outer iteration uniformly by sandwiching a geometrically growing ratio between fixed constants (Lemma 3.18, p. 20).

**Quotes**:

- "In particular, with more exact subproblem solutions, our algorithm often requires fewer derivative evaluations at the expense of more Hessian-vector products, whereas with more inexact subproblem solutions, it often requires fewer Hessian-vector products at the expense of more derivative evaluations." (p. 3)

### S070 · A variance-reduced and stabilized proximal stochastic gradient method with support identification guarantees for structured optimization (AISTATS 2023, arXiv:2302.06790)
**Read level**: full: main text pp. 1–14 read in full (§§1–5); references pp. 14–16 skimmed; Appendix A proofs (pp. 17–30) skimmed: lemma statements, the martingale/Azuma–Hoeffding step of Theorem 3.4 (pp. 18–22), the union bound in Corollary 3.5 (p. 22), the opening of the Theorem 3.9 proof (p. 28) and the RDA comparison Lemma A.5 (pp. 29–30); Appendix B read (B.1 pp. 30–31, B.2 text p. 31, Tables 3–6 pp. 33–36). Figure 3 curves (p. 32) not in the text layer.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yutong Dai (Lehigh, first author, student), Guanyi Wang (NUS), Daniel P. Robinson (Lehigh) (p. 1); Curtis is third author · **Place in trajectory**: part of the Curtis–Robinson structured-sparsity line (reduced-space ℓ1 method, Chen–Curtis–Robinson 2017; FaRSA-Group, Curtis–Dai–Robinson 2022; both cited p. 14); here moved to the stochastic setting, with support identification as the bridge to subspace-accelerated second-order methods (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Problem (1): min f(x) + r(x), f an expectation of smooth convex losses, r a non-overlapping group-sparsity regularizer (pp. 1–2). Why support identification: it enables subspace acceleration with truncated Newton on the identified support (p. 2). The gap: plain proximal SG does not identify supports because the gradient error does not vanish (p. 2); variance-reduced methods that do (ProxSVRG, SAGA) need full gradients or an N×n table and finite sums, "not practical for applications involving streaming data or large N" (p. 3); RDA avoids both but proves identification only per iteration with high probability (p. 3). Table 1 lays out rate, identification complexity, exact-gradient frequency and storage for all four (p. 3).
  - Transferable: tabulate the competitors on the resource axes your method improves (exact gradients, storage) next to the theoretical axes, so the niche is visible in one table (Table 1, p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Start from PStorm (Storm momentum estimator d_k = v_k + (1 − β_k)(d_{k−1} − u_k), Xu and Xu 2020) and add one line: the stabilization x_{k+1} = x_k + ζβ_k(y_k − x_k) after the prox step (Algorithm 1, line 12, p. 5). The stabilization permits a constant step size α_k ≡ α, which the support-identification proof needs (pp. 4–5). Unchanged: Storm estimator, two stochastic gradients per sample at x_k and x_{k−1}, no full gradients, O(n) storage (pp. 3, 5). Parameters: β_k = min{1/2, c/(k+1)}, constant α (Assumption 3.3, p. 7).
  - Transferable: when a known estimator lacks the property your proof needs (here a constant step), look for a one-line averaging/relaxation of the iterate that buys it, and run the unmodified method as the ablation (pp. 5, 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f μ_f-strongly convex, ∇ℓ(·,ξ) L_g-Lipschitz for every ξ (Assumption 3.1, p. 6). Almost-sure bounds: subgradients of r ≤ G_r, gradient noise ≤ σ, ‖d_k‖ ≤ G_d (Assumption 3.2, p. 6); the last is argued "slightly weaker than a bounded iterates assumption" (p. 7, proof in A.4). Non-degeneracy δ* > 0: strict dual feasibility for zero groups (Assumption 3.7, p. 9). Admitted conveniences: strong convexity is used to get an explicit bound on K and can be dropped under an assumed rate sequence (p. 6; Remark 7, p. 10); the a.s. noise bound might be relaxed to sub-exponential tails, left as future work (pp. 6–7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Variance reduction with high probability: the estimator error ε_k is written as the endpoint of a martingale (Lemma A.2, pp. 17–18), the squared increments are bounded almost surely using the product bound on Π(1 − β_j) (Lemma A.1, p. 17; Lemma A.3, p. 20), and a Pinelis Azuma–Hoeffding inequality in Banach spaces gives P[‖ε_k‖ ≥ ρ_k] ≤ 2exp(−ρ_k²/2h(k)) (Theorem 3.4, p. 7; proof pp. 21–22). (b) Summable failure probabilities: choosing η_k = η_0/k² and a union bound makes all events hold for all k ≥ k̄ with probability ≥ 1 − η_0π²/6 (Remark 2, Corollary 3.5, p. 7; p. 22). (c) Iterate rate O(√(log k / k)) w.h.p. (Theorem 3.6, pp. 7–8). (d) Deterministic identification lemma: if ‖(z − x*)_g/α + ∇_g f(x*)‖ < δ* on zero groups then S(y) ⊆ S(x*), and ‖y − x*‖ < Δ* gives equality (Theorem 3.8, p. 9), extending Sun et al. to group ℓ1 and to both inclusions (Remark 5, p. 9). (e) Consistent identification: combine (d) with the high-probability events, giving K = max{(C41/δ*)⁴, (C42/Δ*)⁴, k̄} (Theorem 3.9, p. 9; proof pp. 28–29). (f) Contrast: for RDA the per-iteration failure probabilities are O(k^{-1/4}), not summable, so no union bound over k ≥ K is possible (Remark 6, p. 10; Lemma A.5, pp. 29–30). Constants grow with the squared condition number (Remarks 3 and 6, pp. 8, 10).
  - Transferable: to upgrade "with high probability at each large k" to "with high probability for all large k", make per-iteration failure probabilities summable (η_k = η_0/k²) and apply the union bound; then show the competitor's failure probabilities are not summable (pp. 7, 10).

Baselines: ProxSVRG, SAGA, RDA (the methods with identification theory) plus PStorm as the ablation of the stabilization (p. 11). Ground truth x* from FaRSA-Group solved to 10⁻⁸ (p. 11). Step sizes: 0.1/L_g for ProxSVRG, SAGA, S-PStorm following Xiao–Zhang; RDA's γ tuned over {10^j} on the 32 instances from a9a, covtype, phishing and w8a, which are part of the 80 test instances (p. 11); PStorm's step from its paper (p. 12) and later "fine-tuned ... to be fair" (p. 31). ζ is increased by 1 per iteration, a choice "not covered by the convergence theory", with a cap suggested to restore coverage (p. 12). Batch 256, 1000 epochs or 12 hours; SAGA failed immediately on 5 datasets (48 instances) because of memory (pp. 12, 30). Four identification metrics on per-epoch iterates (total identifications, first identification, first consistent identification, last-iterate support recovery), 3 independent runs averaged (p. 12). Aggregation: per-instance rank scores 5…1, 0 for failure to identify, summed and normalized (p. 12; Figure 1, p. 13). Objective gaps shown as a colour map per instance (Figure 2, p. 13). - **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory: high-probability variance reduction of Storm with a constant step (Theorem 3.4), O(√(log k/k)) iterate rate (Theorem 3.6), consistent support identification with explicit K (Theorem 3.9). Algorithm: S-PStorm. Empirical: better rank scores (pp. 13–14). Stated limits/open: sublinear rate under strong convexity, and whether a linear-rate method without storage or exact gradients exists is open (Remark 4, p. 8); nonconvex extension; inexact prox for overlapping groups (p. 14). **Transferable techniques**:
