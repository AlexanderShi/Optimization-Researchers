### S010 · An interior-point algorithm for large-scale nonlinear optimization with inexact step computations (SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing 2010, DOI 10.1137/090747634)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–27 read in full; reference list pp. 27–29 skimmed). First page checked: title and "FRANK E. CURTIS" as first author (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Olaf Schenk, Andreas Wächter · **Place in trajectory**: the inequality-constraint and production-code rung of the inexact Newton line: SQP 2008 → nonconvex (S017) → rank-deficient [16] → this paper (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Problems with n, p, q "in the millions" (p. 1) where storing or factoring derivative matrices is "inefficient or intractable" (p. 1), for example because of fill-in (p. 2). The entry is a critique of the two usual workarounds. Nonlinear elimination needs many highly accurate constraint solves and turns simple bounds on dependent variables into nonlinear inequalities (p. 2). Reduced-space PDE methods pay for repeated null-space projections and do not allow general inequality or state bounds (p. 3). The stated goal is a full-space method that "emulates an efficient nonlinear programming approach" using only matrix-vector products and preconditioners (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open by listing the concrete failure modes of each competing workaround (elimination, reduced space) in one paragraph each, then state the desiderata your method must meet (p. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Take the equality-constrained matrix-free method of [16] and add inequalities by a slack-scaled interior-point formulation. The scaled derivatives γ, A, W (pp. 4–5) make the primal-dual system look exactly like the one in [16], so the SMART termination tests carry over (p. 5: "The scaling is critical in the design of the algorithm"). The step is split into a normal step and a tangential step. The normal step solves a trust-region least-squares problem with radius ω‖A_kᵀc_k‖, which needs no heuristic radius update (p. 5). The tangential step comes from a perturbed Newton system that stays consistent even when A_k is rank deficient (p. 6). There are three termination tests: model reduction with the old π; a multiplier-only step when ‖A_kᵀc_k‖ is small relative to dual infeasibility; and a constraint-model reduction that triggers a π increase (pp. 9–10). An inertia-free Hessian modification fires when the tangential component fails the curvature test (p. 10). Two further pieces: a slack reset s ← max{s, c_I(x)} (p. 7), and an Armijo-type condition on the computable model reduction Δm in place of the directional derivative (p. 10). Unchanged: the line-search exact-penalty globalization and the barrier-subproblem outer loop (Algorithm 2.2, pp. 10–11).
  - Transferable: when extending a method to a new constraint class, choose a variable scaling that makes the new linear system formally identical to the predecessor's, so its termination tests and lemmas transfer unchanged (pp. 5, 11–12).
  - Transferable: give the normal-step trust region a radius proportional to the infeasibility-stationarity measure (ω‖Aᵀc‖). It vanishes at infeasible stationary points and needs no radius-update heuristic (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, c_E and c_I are C¹ (p. 1). Assumption 3.1: after finitely many modifications W_k ⪰ 2θI, and the iterative solver can reach arbitrary accuracy (p. 12). Assumption 3.3: iterates lie in a convex set where the functions and first derivatives are bounded and Lipschitz, and {W_k} is bounded (pp. 12–13). Full row rank appears only as a case in Theorem 3.4 (p. 13), not as a global assumption. LICQ at limit points is needed only to recover KKT multipliers (Theorem 3.13, p. 19). Admitted gaps in the implementation: the flexible penalty of [15] is swapped in, with only "we claim that the use of this tool does not affect our convergence theory" (p. 20). The Σ_k perturbation is added specifically so that Assumption 3.3 can hold (p. 20).
  - Transferable: when an implementation choice could break an analysis assumption, add a cheap projection or perturbation that restores it (here, bounding S⁻¹Y between multiples of μS⁻², p. 20) and say so.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Most of the proof works by reference. The method was "intentionally constructed to resemble the method presented in [16]" (p. 11), so only the line-search results are re-proved (p. 12). Lemma 3.5 bounds the log-barrier change through a one-variable estimate and gives a model-accuracy inequality with a ξ₁πα²‖d‖² term (p. 13). Lemma 3.6 uses a scaled and shifted penalty φ̂ = (ϕ−χ)/π + ‖c‖ that is monotone even while π increases. It bounds the slacks by the growth argument (ln s)/s → 0 (p. 14). Lemma 3.9 is a contradiction on the set Ξ = {z : ‖A(z)ᵀc(z)‖ ≥ ξ}. It shows that test-2-only iterations cannot persist, then gives a uniform step-length lower bound and a constant decrease on Ξ (pp. 15–16). Theorem 3.4 has three outcomes (full-rank limits → KKT; otherwise ‖Aᵀc‖ → 0; bounded π → also dual stationarity, p. 13). Theorem 3.13 is an outer-loop trichotomy (infeasible stationary / π → ∞ / feasible with KKT under LICQ, pp. 18–19). Two named "critical" components close the proof: the slack reset and the scaled system (p. 18).
  - Transferable: when the penalty parameter can increase, prove monotonicity for φ̂ = (ϕ−χ)/π + ‖c‖ with χ the infimum of ϕ. It is monotone even though φ itself is not comparable across π values (pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable: after the proof, add a paragraph naming which algorithmic components the proof needed and what would fail without them (stall at a non-stationary point without the slack reset, p. 18).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Implemented inside Ipopt 3.7.0 with PARDISO's SQMR and an incomplete multilevel preconditioner (pp. 19, 22). Ipopt's filter line search was replaced by the paper's penalty line search (p. 20). The rival is default Ipopt with PARDISO's direct solver, the same software stack (p. 23). Test set: 684 CUTEr/COPS AMPL models, after removing problems with two-sided constraints (a software limitation) and problems with n ≤ p (pp. 22–23). Exit statuses are reported in full (Table 4.2, p. 23). Success: Algorithm 2.2 85 %, Ipopt 94 %. Objective values agree within 1 % except in 8 cases better for the new code and 23 better for Ipopt (p. 23). Scale tests use two PDE models. In 3-D boundary control (Example 4.1), N goes up to 80 (n = 512,000). Ipopt solves only up to N = 50, where it takes 33,704 s CPU against 3,402 s with identical iterations and objective (Tables 4.3–4.4, pp. 24–25). In hyperthermia (Example 4.2), n reaches 125,020 and Ipopt results are shown only up to N = 40 (Tables 4.5–4.6, p. 26). Several local solutions explain differing objective values (p. 26). The Table 4.6 caption reads "Example 4.1" for Example 4.2 data (p. 26). The parameters in Table 4.1 are given without tuning details (p. 22).
  - Transferable: to test only the linear-algebra idea, put both arms in the same production code and linear-algebra package (iterative vs direct PARDISO in Ipopt). Report full exit-status tables, not just success rates (pp. 19, 23).
  - Transferable: split the evaluation into robustness on a broad set (where you expect to lose a little) and scaling on a family of instances of growing N (where the method is meant to win) (pp. 22–26).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Continues [11, 12, 16] (Byrd–Curtis–Nocedal 2008, 2010; Curtis–Nocedal–Wächter 2009), described as a ladder (p. 2). Against full-space PDE methods [5, 6, 7, 26, 38], the paper claims inequality handling and "definitive and practical global convergence guarantees" (p. 3). Against reduced-space methods [27, 28, 30, 39, 45], it gives specific drawbacks rather than dismissal (p. 3). Wächter–Biegler's failure example [46] motivates the normal-step trust region (pp. 5, 27). Criticism is stated as limits of other approaches, not attacks on authors.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Goal sentence first ("Our goal is thus to design...", p. 2), then the contribution in one sentence: "with an appropriate scaling of the search space, the method developed in [16] can be enhanced" (p. 2). Results are hedged: "preliminary implementation" (p. 3); "very encouraging" (p. 24). The failure count is owned directly: "Our approach fails more often" (p. 24). The algorithm is introduced with an exact-step framework first (Algorithm 2.1, p. 7) and the inexact version second (Algorithm 2.2, pp. 10–11). The roadmap promises "a discussion of future work" in §5 (p. 3), but §5 contains almost none (pp. 26–27).
  - Transferable: present the exact-step framework as a separate algorithm before the inexact one, so the reader sees exactly which lines inexactness changes (pp. 6–7, 10–11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic and software contributions: termination tests for iterative solvers inside an IPM, matrix-free, globally convergent including to infeasible stationary points (pp. 26–27). Stated limits: only an algebraic preconditioner that exploits sparsity, with problem-specific preconditioning deferred (p. 3); a code "not as mature as IPOPT" (p. 24); iterative solves can raise CPU time on small problems (p. 24); two-sided constraints unsupported (p. 23). Future direction: PDE-specific preconditioners and problems where no preconditioner exists, such as Helmholtz (p. 3). No local convergence rate analysis (not claimed anywhere in the paper).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the three SMART tests are derived from the penalty-model reduction Δm. The dual residual is controlled while "the primal residual vector r_k does not need to be controlled explicitly" (pp. 8–10). Hessian modification is triggered by the tests, not by inertia (p. 10). The tests "must be enforced with this scaling" (p. 18). Production-code timing at scale (pp. 24–26).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the rival is the same code with only the linear solver changed (iterative vs direct PARDISO in Ipopt, pp. 19, 23). Incumbent wins are printed (94 % vs 85 %; 23 vs 8 better objectives, p. 23).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (weak): one algorithm whose outcomes include infeasible stationary points (Theorems 3.4, 3.13, pp. 13, 18–19). The flexible penalty is adopted because a high early π "hampered progress later on" (p. 20).
- Method 3: not exercised (no constructed infeasible or degenerate variants; standard sets only, p. 22).
- Heuristic 10 evidence (not a Method; recorded as a note): the ladder is stated explicitly ("first in the case of a sequential quadratic programming (SQP) framework, then when handling nonconvexity...", p. 2).
- Note for synthesis: the SKILL's Method 1 step 6 attributes "under 9 minutes" vs "approximately 40 minutes" per iteration to this paper via the research page. This paper's tables report total CPU seconds (e.g., 3,402 s vs 33,704 s at N = 50, p. 25) and contain no such per-iteration figures. The figure may come from a later paper (not verified here).
- new-pattern candidate: **reduce-to-predecessor design**: build the new method so that it is formally the predecessor in a rescaled space, and prove by citation (p. 11: "intentionally constructed to resemble the method presented in [16]").

**Transferable techniques**:
- Rescale variables (here slacks via S_k) so the extended Newton system matches a predecessor's. Termination tests and lemmas then transfer, and only the line-search lemmas need re-proving (pp. 5, 11–12).
- Use the computable model reduction Δm in the Armijo condition instead of the directional derivative, which is awkward to compute when c_k = 0 (p. 10).
- A slack reset s ← max{s, c_I(x)} after each step guarantees that ‖Aᵀc‖ → 0 means stationarity for the infeasibility measure (pp. 7, 18).
- In the solver loop, aim first for a tighter residual bound (4.3) to keep fast local behaviour, and fall back to the bare termination tests after a fixed number of iterations (p. 22).
- State which test-set exclusions come from software limits (two-sided constraints) or problem shape (n ≤ p). These are filters decided before the runs (pp. 22–23).

**Quotes**:
- "Our approach fails more often, but this is to be expected, as our code is not as mature as IPOPT and an increase in iteration count often follows from the use of inexact Newton steps." (p. 24)
- "the benefits of these techniques are only realized if inexact step computations are allowed and controlled appropriately in order to guarantee global convergence of the algorithm." (p. 2)

### S017 · An inexact Newton method for nonconvex equality constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming 2010, DOI 10.1007/s10107-008-0248-3)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–26 read in full; reference list pp. 26–27 skimmed). First page checked: title and "Frank E. Curtis" among Byrd, Curtis, Nocedal (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd, Jorge Nocedal · **Place in trajectory**: the nonconvexity rung, directly after the strictly convex inexact SQP of 2008 [2]; PhD period at Northwestern (affiliation, p. 1); received August 2007 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Matrix-free, globally convergent line-search methods for large equality-constrained problems where Hessian, Jacobian and primal–dual matrix cannot be factored (p. 2). The specific weak spot of the predecessor [2] is negative or zero curvature. Line-search methods usually convexify when the reduced Hessian is "known not to be positive definite", which needs inertia that is unavailable without factorization (p. 2). Existing nonconvex methods with guarantees all factor something (p. 4). Inexact reduced-space SQP exists, but "full-space primal–dual SQP methods ... have not received much attention in the nonconvex inexact case" (p. 4).
  - Transferable: locate the gap by crossing two axes (full-space vs reduced-space, exact vs inexact, convex vs nonconvex) and show which cell is empty (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Stop interpreting the step as a QP solution and judge it by its effect on the exact-penalty model m_k(d; π) = l_k(d) + π‖r_k(d)‖ (pp. 4–5). The acceptance condition is built in stages. First comes the unconstrained exact case: Δl ≥ max{½dᵀWd, θ‖d‖²} (p. 7). Then the constrained convex case (3.7), then the tangential-curvature failure (p. 8), and finally the Model Reduction Condition (3.12) with Υ_k as a computable upper bound for ‖u_k‖² (pp. 8–9). Two SMART tests follow: Test I is model reduction with the old π plus a relative residual bound; Test II is a constraint-model reduction plus a curvature-or-sufficiently-normal condition that licenses a π increase (pp. 10–11). The Hessian Modification Strategy fires only when neither test can pass, by (4.7a–c) (p. 11). "The resulting algorithm fully integrates the procedures of step computation and step selection" (p. 2). In convex regions it reduces to the inexact SQP of [2] (abstract, p. 2). Unchanged from [2]: the merit function, the line search and the primal–dual system (2.2). Changed: the acceptance condition and the modification rule.
  - Transferable: derive a new acceptance condition step by step: the exact unconstrained case, then the constrained convex case, then the failure case it must exclude, then the inexact version. Each step in the paper is one displayed inequality (pp. 6–9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f and c are smooth and possibly nonconvex, with t ≤ n (p. 2). Assumption 1 (p. 12): iterates lie in a convex set with bounded functions and first and second derivatives; {λ_k} is bounded; Jacobians have full row rank with singular values bounded below; {W_k} is bounded; the primal–dual system is consistent for each W_k. The predecessor's assumption of a positive definite reduced Hessian is dropped (p. 13). Full row rank is the strong assumption; the authors do not flag it as a convenience, and it is relaxed in the later rank-deficient work cited in S010 ([16] there). For the modification rule, they simply "assume that the chosen method for modifying W_k is such that after a finite number of modifications we have W_k sufficiently positive definite" (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Lemma 1 bounds the normal component through the pseudo-inverse Aᵀ(AAᵀ)⁻¹ and residual bounds (pp. 13–14). Lemma 3 bounds the directional derivative of φ by −γ₄(Υ_k + max{‖c_k‖, ‖r_k‖}) (p. 14). Lemma 4 shows π_k stays bounded through a three-case split: low curvature with a sufficiently normal step; ‖u‖² < γ₆‖v‖²; and a large tangential component where ½uᵀWu ≥ (θ/2)‖u‖² (pp. 15–16). In each case −gᵀd − (curvature term) is bounded by a constant times (‖c‖ − ‖r‖) (p. 16). Lemma 5 gives a step-length lower bound from a Taylor bound with a finite π̂ (p. 17). Lemma 6 telescopes the merit decrease to get ‖c_k‖ → 0 and d_k → 0 (pp. 17–18). Theorem 5.1 then follows by citing Theorem 4.10 of [2], because the limits match its Lemma 4.9 (p. 18).
  - Transferable: to prove that the penalty parameter stays bounded, show that every π increase happens only on steps where the objective-model loss is O(‖c‖ − ‖r‖). Split on the ratio of tangential to normal step length so that each case yields such a bound (pp. 15–16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab prototype; Kelley's GMRES with no preconditioner, "Since we are only interested in demonstrating the robustness of our approach" (p. 19). MINRES and SQMR gave "very similar" results (p. 19). At most n+t GMRES iterations per W_k (p. 19). Test set: 44 CUTEr AMPL problems after two filters: memory (n+t > 1,000 removed) and Assumption 1, where a problem was removed "if the algorithm ever encountered a Jacobian A_k with a singular value ... below 10⁻⁴" (p. 20). The second filter is decided by what the runs showed. Table 3 reports a mechanism log: average positive eigenvalues of the modified primal–dual matrix (showing that the strategy "does not fully convexify the subproblem during every iteration") and the % of steps passing Test I vs Test II (pp. 20–22). There are two rivals built from [2]. ISQP-naive ignores negative curvature and fails on 8 of the 44 problems, marked "–" in Table 4. ISQP-ideal computes inertia and is "an idealized approach" with factorizations (pp. 22–24). Failure modes are defined explicitly: iteration limit, ascent direction for every π, α_k ≤ 10⁻⁶ (p. 24). Performance profiles cover outer and inner iterations (pp. 24–25). INS never fails, but on some problems it uses far more inner iterations than both rivals (eigenc2: 57,714 vs 8,687 naive and 3,625 ideal, p. 23). The text does not discuss this; its only comment explains naive's apparent robustness by many problems being convex along the iterates (p. 25).
  - Transferable: log the mechanism, not just the outcome: per-problem % of steps accepted by each termination test and the eigenvalue count after modification (Table 3, pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: build the rivals as one-switch variants of the same code (naive: drop the new safeguard; ideal: add the information you assume unavailable). This brackets your method between a floor and a ceiling (pp. 22–24).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Builds directly on [2] (same authors). Nonconvex globally convergent methods [5, 6, 8, 9, 12, 16, 22] are acknowledged but placed as needing factorizations (p. 4). Inexact reduced-space SQP (Heinkenschloss–Vicente, Ulbrich, Heinkenschloss–Ridzal; Ridzal's review) is credited as yielding "good results" (p. 4). The method is distinguished from penalty methods: "should not be considered a penalty method per se" (p. 5). The paper claims no direct competitor exists ("we are unaware of another globally convergent line search algorithm that does not require inertia information", p. 22), and the comparison is therefore internal.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: §3 builds the central condition from intuition before any algorithm (pp. 6–9); the condition is called "the centerpiece of the algorithm" (p. 8). Each test is followed by a sentence on what kind of step it admits (pp. 10–11). Mild affect words: "comforting" twice (pp. 20, 22), "surprisingly robust" (p. 25). Final remarks give two practical extensions (κ ≥ 1 with an extra bound for left preconditioning, and a flexible modification rule) rather than a list of open problems (pp. 25–26).
  - Transferable: after each acceptance test, add one sentence on which steps it is meant to admit and why it is needed (pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic and theoretical contribution: global convergence to first-order points without inertia or convexity assumptions (p. 18). Limits: equality constraints only; full-rank Jacobians (Assumption 1(c), p. 12); no preconditioner and small problems (n+t ≤ 1,000), with large-scale study "outside the scope of this paper" (p. 20). The inexactness parameters "should preferably be tuned for each application" (p. 18), pointing for tuning to a numerical study by Curtis and Haber that the reference list marks as in preparation (pp. 18, 26). Future work: apply the method to the equality-constrained barrier subproblems of an interior-point method (p. 25), which S010 carries out.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (central): "places primary importance on attaining a sufficient reduction in a local approximation of a merit function during each iteration", which decides acceptance, Hessian perturbation and penalty change (p. 2). The inertia-free Hessian Modification Strategy (p. 11) and the idealized factorizing ceiling ISQP-ideal (p. 24) are already cited in the SKILL; confirmed.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: rivals built inside the same framework with one component varied (naive / ideal, pp. 22–24).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: no rival outside the family. The paper claims none exists (p. 22), and the comparison commentary is selective: it is silent on inner-iteration losses to both rivals (Table 4, p. 23; text p. 25).
- Heuristic 7 contradiction (not a Method; note): the Assumption 1 filter removes problems on which "the algorithm ever encountered" a near-singular Jacobian (p. 20). This filters on what the runs showed, the same pattern as the LICQ anti-example the SKILL cites for 2020.
- Heuristic 1 evidence (note): Υ_k and ν_k are computable bounds replacing unobservable ‖u‖², ‖v‖² (pp. 19–20). Test I/II percentages log how often each branch fires (pp. 21–22).
- new-pattern candidate: **reduce-to-predecessor design**: "automatically reduces to an inexact SQP method in convex regions of the search space" (p. 2). The final theorem is obtained by matching the predecessor's limits (p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Acceptance rule max{½dᵀWd, θ‖d‖²}: one inequality that keeps the convex-case step and still guarantees sufficient descent under negative curvature. 2θ is read as the smallest acceptable Rayleigh quotient (p. 7).
- Computable bounds for orthogonal components: ν = ‖A d‖²/‖A‖² ≤ ‖v‖² and Υ = ‖d‖² − ‖A d‖²/‖A‖² ≥ ‖u‖². ‖A‖² is replaced by min{n‖A‖₁², t‖A‖∞²} (pp. 19–20).
- Set θ ← 10⁻⁸ max{‖W_k‖₁, 1} so the acceptance tests are independent of the scaling of f and c (p. 18).
- On a Hessian modification, restart the Krylov solver from the last trial step rather than from zero (pp. 12, 19).
- Define failure explicitly (iteration cap, no descent for any π, α ≤ 10⁻⁶) before showing profiles (p. 24).

**Quotes**:
- "we intend to forgo the interpretation of our step as an (approximate) solution to a local optimization subproblem and instead jump straight to its effect on our globalization mechanism." (p. 4)
- "The resulting algorithm fully integrates the procedures of step computation and step selection and automatically reduces to an inexact SQP method in convex regions of the search space." (p. 2)

### S003 · A sequential quadratic programming algorithm for nonconvex, nonsmooth constrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2012, DOI 10.1137/090780201)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–25 read in full; reference list pp. 25–27 skimmed). First page checked: title and "FRANK E. CURTIS" with Michael L. Overton (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michael L. Overton · **Place in trajectory**: the nonsmooth rung of the SQP-penalty machinery (postdoc with Overton). Received December 2009, accepted February 2012 (p. 1). Later demoted to comparator for BFGS-SQP (SKILL Signature Work Anatomy).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: f and c are locally Lipschitz and C¹ on open dense sets, with solutions often at nondifferentiable points (abstract, p. 1). The entry joins two mature lines, SQP for smooth constrained problems and gradient sampling for nonsmooth unconstrained problems (pp. 1–3): "The successes of these and other methods ... have laid the groundwork" (p. 3). The paper also argues why a plain SQP will not do: steepest-descent-type failures on nonsmooth problems, and no known perturbation strategy that avoids suboptimal kinks (p. 4).
  - Transferable: name the two parent methods and the one sentence that shows each alone fails on the new class. Here, SQP with H = I and no active constraints is steepest descent, which is known to fail on nonsmooth problems (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Put sampled gradients into the linearized objective and constraints of an ℓ1-penalty SQP subproblem. All linearizations use function values at x_k, not at the sample points (unlike cutting planes), so nonconvexity does no harm (p. 3). The sample sets are independent per function (p. 7). Penalty slack variables keep the QP always feasible (pp. 5–6). Parameters: ρ multiplies f and is decreased (more weight on feasibility) only when the model reduction Δq is small and v(x_k) > θ. Otherwise θ is tightened, and ε is reduced in either case (step 4, pp. 7–8). Theorem 3.3 has two outcomes: stationary for φ_ρ* with feasibility along K, or ρ → 0 with stationarity for the violation measure v (p. 9). Nesting: with p = 0 the method is S1QP; with m = 0 it is a gradient-sampling method (p. 9). Rejected alternatives with reasons: aggregating constraints into max_j c_j is not scale invariant and discards gradient information (p. 7); using the same sample set for all functions breaks Lemma 3.8 (pp. 7, 13).
  - Transferable: when a tempting simplification (one aggregated constraint, shared samples) is rejected, write the reason: invariance lost, information discarded, or the proof step that breaks (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 3.1: locally Lipschitz, C¹ on open dense subsets (p. 9). Assumption 3.2: iterates and samples lie in a convex set with bounded functions and first derivatives, and H_k has eigenvalues bounded above and below (p. 9). Calmness, a weak CQ implied by MFCQ and Slater, gives exact penalization and Clarke-KKT multipliers (Theorems 2.1–2.2, cited, p. 5). Sample size p ≥ n+1 for Carathéodory (p. 12). Iterates must stay in D, "an unlikely event" to violate "in exact arithmetic", kept "necessary for our analysis" (p. 8). Admitted mismatch: L-BFGS safeguards are imposed to satisfy Assumption 3.2, although "in [31] the authors argue that BFGS updates can be effective ... when the Hessian approximations are allowed to become ill-conditioned" (p. 18). Changes in ρ corrupt the L-BFGS pairs in a way "difficult to quantify" and are ignored (p. 18). The stationarity concept is defined through the penalty subproblem rather than Clarke stationarity (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Lemma 3.6 shows Δq = ½dᵀHd, so a small model reduction certifies ε-stationarity (p. 10). Lemma 3.7 shows the direction is a descent direction for φ_ρ through the directional-derivative formula over active and violated sets (p. 11). Lemma 3.8 is the core probabilistic device. Carathéodory's theorem gives p points whose gradients' convex combination realizes the subproblem's optimal model. Small balls around them form a positive-measure set T of sample sets that the uniform sampling hits with positive probability. Hence persistent "bad" iterations are a probability-zero event (pp. 12–13, 15). The proof also explains why each function must be sampled independently (p. 13). Lemma 3.9 bounds the QP solution by ξ₁ρ + ξ₂‖d̂_k‖, with d̂ the minimum-norm minimizer of the violation model (pp. 13–14). Theorem 3.3 follows Kiwiel's GS proof [30], extended to ρ → 0 (pp. 14–16). Kiwiel is thanked for improving the analysis (p. 25).
  - Transferable: for probability-one convergence of a sampling method, construct an explicit open set of "good" samples (Carathéodory points plus small balls) whose probability is positive near any non-stationary limit. Then show that the algorithm missing it forever has probability zero (pp. 12–13, 15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab with MOSEK as QP solver, chosen after testing "a few QP solvers" (pp. 16–17); code public (p. 16 footnote). The implementation adds two things beyond the theory: L-BFGS H_k with safeguards (pp. 17–18), and no sampling for known-smooth functions, stated as a conjecture ("we conjecture that it is not necessary", p. 18). Parameter choice: p = 2n beats p = n+1 in iterations, and "The remaining values were decided upon during the course of our experiments" (p. 18). Protocol: 10 random starting points per problem instead of repeated runs from one point, because "the algorithm behaved consistently" (p. 19). There are 500 iterations with the optimality-based termination switched off (p. 19). Problems: a constructed nonsmooth Rosenbrock with a max-type constraint, whose solution sits at a kink of both functions (pp. 19–20); entropy-matrix eigenvalue products (Table 5.1, p. 21); ℓ_q compressed sensing with q = 1 and 0.5 (non-Lipschitz), exact and noisy (pp. 21–23); robust quadratic constraint with n = 100 (pp. 23–24). Ablation: p = 0 (no sampling) on Example 5.1 stalls at error 8e-01 against 2e-12. "similarly poor results were obtained for all our remaining test problems" (pp. 19–20). No external solver is compared; Example 5.2 values are "comparable to those obtained in [8]" (p. 21). Example 5.2 and 5.3 report the best run (best optimality estimate or lowest objective, p. 21); Examples 5.1 and 5.4 plot all 10 runs and give medians (pp. 19–20, 24). The optimality measure E_k is tracked as the smallest value per ε, because single values depend on the sample set (p. 17).
  - Transferable: an ablation that switches off exactly the one new component (p = 0) on every test problem, shown as side-by-side trajectory plots for the same starting points (Fig. 5.2, pp. 19–20).
  - Transferable: for a sampling-based method, track the best optimality estimate per sampling radius rather than the last one, and say why (p. 17).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Inherits SQP (Han, Powell; surveys, p. 1) and GS (Burke–Lewis–Overton, Kiwiel, pp. 2, 14). Bundle methods are distinguished on two precise points: linearization at x_k vs at sample points, and fresh samples each iteration vs past iterates (p. 3). The cost of fresh samples is conceded ("may be prohibitive for some applications") and parallel evaluation is proposed (pp. 3–4). Closest prior work: bundle(-filter) methods [24, 28, 29], "though these methods and ours differ significantly in detail" (p. 4). Unconstrained reformulations and penalty approaches are listed without critique (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution is stated as a generalization: "Algorithm 2.1 generalizes each of these methods to nonsmooth constrained problems" (p. 9). The necessity of GS is argued in the introduction and demonstrated in §5 (pp. 4, 19–20). The authors defend the method against expected objections: "This should not be viewed as a deficiency of our algorithm" (p. 7). Results are framed modestly: "preliminary implementation", special-purpose solvers "will most likely provide better results" (p. 19). The conclusion gives concrete tailoring ideas (null-space sampling for Example 5.3, convex variant (6.1), SLP-GS) (pp. 24–25).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic and theoretical contribution: the first general SQP-type method for nonconvex nonsmooth constrained problems with probability-one convergence, plus public code (pp. 9, 16). Stated limits: fresh gradient samples every iteration can be prohibitive (p. 3); QP size grows as p(m+1) and the QP is the main cost (pp. 18, 25); special-purpose solvers likely better (p. 19); no rate analysis. Future work: tailored sampling, a smaller QP for convex problems, and SLP-GS. SLP-GS is cheaper per iteration but "the rate of convergence is typically much slower", and its convergence is unproven (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the p = 0 ablation on all problems, "providing strong evidence that the GS procedure is critical for the effectiveness of our approach" (pp. 19–20). Already cited in the SKILL; confirmed with page.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: no tuned external rival (only "comparable to those obtained in [8]", p. 21). Parameters "decided upon during the course of our experiments" (p. 18). Best-of-10 reporting for Examples 5.2–5.3 (p. 21). This matches the SKILL's say–do flag for 2012 (03 PE13).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: one algorithm whose outcomes include convergence to stationary points of the violation measure (Theorem 3.3(b), p. 9). However, the penalty update is a classical decrease by a fixed factor, triggered at ε-stationarity events (step 4, pp. 7–8). It is not a progress-based steering rule inside the step.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak): optimality is judged by an error estimate E_k built from the Clarke-KKT conditions and the best value per ε. The solver's own termination check is turned off (pp. 17, 19).
- Heuristic 5 evidence (note): the L-BFGS safeguards are kept for the theory while the Lewis–Overton remark on ill-conditioned BFGS is recorded (p. 18). This foreshadows the guarantee-free BFGS-SQP. SLP-GS is the abandoned path (p. 25).
- new-pattern candidate: **reduce-to-predecessor design**: p = 0 gives S1QP and m = 0 gives GS (p. 9).
- new-pattern candidate: **proof-required detail, cost argued away**: independent sample sets per function are required by Lemma 3.8, and their cost is argued "insignificant" next to gradient evaluations and the QP (pp. 7, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Linearize all sampled gradients at the current function value (f(x_k) + ∇f(x)ᵀd), not at the sample point, so the model stays useful without convexity (p. 3).
- Use the model reduction Δq both as the line-search sufficient-decrease measure and as the trigger to shrink the sampling radius and update ρ/θ. One computable quantity drives all parameter updates (pp. 7–8).
- Keep a separate violation tolerance θ: when the model reduction is small, tighten θ if feasible enough, otherwise lower ρ (p. 7).
- Report runs from several random starting points to the iteration cap with the termination test off. Plot f(x_k) and v(x_k) for all runs (Figs. 5.2, 5.6, pp. 20, 24).
- Exploit known structure that the theory does not cover (no sampling for smooth functions, 2n̄ samples for functions of n̄ variables) and label it a conjecture (p. 18).
