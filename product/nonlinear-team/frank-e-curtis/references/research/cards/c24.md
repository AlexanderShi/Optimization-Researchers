### S075 · Derivative-free bound-constrained optimization for solving structured problems with surrogate models (Optimization Methods and Software 2024, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2024.2329588; arXiv:2202.12961)
**Read level**: full. Main text read in full, arXiv v3 file pp. 1–28 (abstract, §§1–6, acknowledgements, references). Supplementary Appendix A (proofs of Lemmas 2.4–2.6 and Theorem 3.7, pp. 29–33) and Appendix B (proof of Theorem 2.8, pp. 33–37) skimmed for structure (lemma sequence and the devices used), not checked line by line. First page checked: title matches and Frank E. Curtis is the first author (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shima Dezfulian (corresponding author, Northwestern IEMS) and Andreas Wächter (Northwestern IEMS) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a derivative-free, application-driven side line (DOE ARPA-E grant DE-AR0001073 and NSF CCF-2008484, p. 25), with Wächter as co-author; his usual trust-region and inexactness themes carried into model-based DFO.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: entry from cost structure in applications: black-box function evaluations are the bottleneck, and a cheap surrogate (lower-accuracy PDE solves) or a history of evaluations from previously solved related problems could stand in for exact values at interpolation points (pp. 1–2, 4–5). The motivating instance is a sequence of related least-squares data-fitting problems indexed by exogenous hyperparameters θt (§1.5, pp. 5–6). The gap named: existing model-based DFO methods "do not consider how to exploit approximate function values at interpolation points" from surrogates or nearby prior values (p. 7). Problem stated as min over a box of h(F(x)) with h glass-box and F a vector of black boxes, bounds unrelaxable (§1.3, p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: state a gap as "everything in the literature evaluates exactly at point type X; we allow approximations there, at a controlled accuracy", which turns an application cost observation into an algorithmic degree of freedom.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: keep a standard model-based trust-region DFO framework (fully linear models, criticality step, Cauchy decrease, ratio test; Algorithm 1, pp. 11–12; "similar to the general trust-region DFO framework considered in [26]", p. 11) and change one thing: interpolation values may be approximations F̃i(x, δk) with |Fi − F̃i| ≤ κapp δk and δk = capp Δk² (line 2 of Algorithm 1, pp. 4, 11), while exact evaluations are still required at iterates and trial points (p. 4). Two supporting pieces: (i) a new interpolation-set routine for unrelaxable bounds that searches along ± null-space directions projected onto the box (Algorithms 3–4, pp. 14–15), an approximation of max ||Zᵀd||² over the trust region and box (problem (30), p. 15); (ii) a regularized local linear regression over history points within an approximation radius δ, with an unregularized intercept (§4.1, pp. 18–19), plus a candidate-set ordering that scores each history point by how many element functions can be approximated there (§4.3, p. 22). Composite structure is exploited by building separate models per black-box component and combining them through a Taylor model of h (eq. (22), p. 10), following DFO-GN, POUNDERS and DFLS (pp. 2, 7).
  - Transferable technique: tie the allowed error of every cheap approximation to the square of the trust-region radius, so that the fully linear model conditions survive unchanged and the convergence proof is the classical one.
  - Transferable technique: replace an expensive global subproblem (max of a convex function over a box ∩ ball) by a coordinate-direction search whose outcome is still provably good enough for the threshold the theory needs (pp. 14–17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Fi continuously differentiable with Lipschitz values and gradients on an open convex set containing the enlarged sublevel set ∩ Ω; h twice continuously differentiable, Lipschitz, with bounded gradient and partial derivatives (Assumption 2.1, p. 8); model smoothness and bounded gradients (Assumption 2.2, p. 9); f bounded below (Assumption 2.7, p. 13); Lipschitz continuity of Fi jointly in (x, θ) for the regression scheme (Assumption 4.1, p. 19; Assumption 4.5 for least squares, p. 21). No noise: accuracy requirements "must hold surely" (p. 7). Simplification admitted: requiring fully linear models in every iteration "has been simplified for our discussion and analysis", with a sketch of how to relax it (Remark 1, p. 12). Unrelaxable box only; extension to closed convex Ω is conjectured, not done (p. 4). κapp need not be known by the algorithm (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (1) fully linear models from approximate interpolation values: Lemma 2.4 (p. 10), proved by adapting Wild's thesis Theorem 4.1 with integral-remainder identities (A1)–(A2) and an extra κ̄app Δk² term (A5) (pp. 29–30); the composite model inherits full linearity (Lemmas 2.5–2.6, p. 10). (2) Convergence lim πfk = 0 (Theorem 2.8, p. 13) via the standard lemma chain B.1–B.7 (criticality step, radius lower bound, finitely many successes, Δk → 0, liminf, then lim; pp. 33–37); the authors say the proof "follows that of other such convergence analyses" (p. 13). (3) Well-poisedness under bounds: Lemma 3.1 (step size lower bound at the trust-region boundary, p. 15), Lemmas 3.2–3.4 (pp. 16–17) and Theorem 3.5 giving an explicit ξ threshold depending on min_j(xU,j − xL,j), Δmax and the norm constant (p. 17); Lemma 3.6 identifies the projection test with the last diagonal entry of an R factor in a QR factorization (p. 18), from which Theorem 3.7 gives Λ for condition (18) (p. 18; proof pp. 32–33). (4) Regression error bound: translation invariance of the weight vector β (Lemma 4.2, p. 20), affine weights βᵀ1 = 1 (Lemma 4.3, p. 20), then |F̃i − Fi| ≤ LFi ||β||₁ δ with ||β||₁ bounded through the smallest eigenvalue of the centred design matrix (Theorem 4.4, pp. 20–21). The constant κapp in (41) depends on the number of history points N and σmin (p. 20). No complexity bound.
  - Transferable technique: when a regression or interpolation estimate must meet an absolute accuracy bound, show the weights are affine (sum to one) and bound their ℓ1 norm; Lipschitz continuity plus the radius of the data neighbourhood then gives the bound directly (pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable technique: reduce a geometric well-poisedness test to a pivot of a QR factorization so that the implementation check and the proof quantity are the same number (Lemma 3.6, p. 18).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: in-framework ablation: AlgH (Algorithm 1 using history) against Alg∅, "an algorithm that has all of the same features of Algorithm 1 except that does not utilize prior function evaluations to approximate function values at interpolation points" (p. 22). Python implementation (SciPy, NumPy) on a stated workstation (p. 23). One problem shown: the COPS methanol-to-hydrocarbons ODE least-squares problem (5 parameters, 21 residuals); a second COPS problem (catalytic cracking of gas oil) and others were tested, "The results were similar in all cases" (p. 23), but not shown. Sequence of T = 100 related instances with randomized initial conditions and up to 10 % relative measurement noise (pp. 23–24); budget fixed at two simplex gradients, 2p(nx+1) = 252 evaluations per instance, justified by online settings where "a good approximate solution suffices" (pp. 22–24). N = 100 macro replications; plotted: accumulated improvement of mean final objective of AlgH over Alg∅ with a ±1.96σ/√N band, and the mean number of approximated (not evaluated) function values per instance, over half of the budget by t ≈ 10 (p. 24; Figure 1, p. 25). Parameter guidance stated: ξ = 10⁻³, not too small nor near the Theorem 3.5 bound; λ = 10⁻⁶ only to make (40) unique (p. 23). No external DFO code (DFO-LS, POUNDERS, BOBYQA, all discussed on p. 7) is run. No failure cases reported.
  - Transferable technique: when a method's gain comes from reusing information, plot the mechanism count (how many evaluations were replaced) beside the outcome curve, so the reader sees the gain grow with the size of the history (p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: for a sequence of related instances, report cumulative improvement over the sequence with a confidence band across macro replications rather than per-instance wins (p. 24).
- **D6 Positioning**: surveys DFO by family (direct search, finite differences, model-based; p. 6), then model-based trust-region methods, constrained variants (BOBYQA, COBYLA, CONORBIT, convex-constrained methods) and structure-exploiting least-squares methods (DFO-LS, DFO-GN, POUNDERS, DFLS; p. 7). Credits the closest idea (reuse of prior values in bilevel DFO, Conn and Vicente [17]) while noting it gives "no specific mechanism for approximating function values" (p. 7). Positions itself as a framework extension of [26] (Hough and Roberts) and [10] (p. 11; convergence "in a similar manner as for Algorithm 10.1 in [16] and Algorithm 1 in [10]", p. 12). Restatement, not criticism, of prior work.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: contribution stated as one "distinguishing feature" in the abstract and repeated nearly verbatim in the conclusion (pp. 1, 24). §1.1 names both arms of the comparison and what separates them (p. 2). Proofs moved to a supplement with "for completeness" phrasing (pp. 13, 18). Practical-parameter advice follows the theory threshold (p. 23). The claim wording outruns the experiment: abstract and conclusion say the algorithm "outperforms a state-of-the-art DFO algorithm" (pp. 1, 25), while the experiment compares against Alg∅, the same framework minus the history (pp. 2, 22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic framework plus analysis plus a regression approximation scheme and a bound-handling interpolation routine; prototype-level experiments (pp. 2, 24–25). Limits visible but not collected in a limitations paragraph: exact evaluations still needed at iterates and trial points (p. 4), noise-free sure accuracy (p. 7), fully linear models assumed every iteration for the analysis (Remark 1, p. 12), only box constraints with the convex-set extension conjectured (p. 4), one problem shown (p. 23), no external baseline. Future direction stated only as the closed convex set conjecture (p. 4); the conclusion gives none (pp. 24–25).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 variant ⚠: the accuracy of the cheap "inner" quantity (surrogate or regression value at interpolation points) is derived from what the outer trust-region globalization needs, δk = capp Δk², so that fully linear models and the classical convergence proof survive (pp. 4, 10–11). The inner object is a function-value surrogate in DFO, not a linear system or QP solve, hence variant.
- Method 5 evidence ✅: the rival is built inside the same framework with one component switched off (Alg∅, pp. 2, 22) and results are averaged over 100 macro replications with a confidence band (p. 24).
- Method 5 variant ⚠: no external code is run although DFO-LS and POUNDERS are discussed (p. 7), only one of several tested problems is shown (p. 23), and the abstract and conclusion call the in-framework ablation "a state-of-the-art DFO algorithm" (pp. 1, 25), which Workflow E's claim calibration would narrow.
- new-pattern candidate: amortize expensive evaluations across a sequence of related instances (history-based surrogate values with a provable accuracy radius) (pp. 5–6, 18–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Couple surrogate accuracy to the trust-region radius (δ ∝ Δ²) so the standard fully linear theory applies without change (pp. 4, 11).
- Bound a regression estimate's error through affine weights and the ℓ1 norm of the weight vector (Lemmas 4.2–4.3, Theorem 4.4, pp. 20–21).
- Replace a nonconvex geometry subproblem by ± coordinate-direction projections onto the box and prove an explicit well-poisedness threshold (Theorem 3.5, p. 17).
- Plot the mechanism count (approximated evaluations per instance) beside the outcome curve (p. 24).

**Quotes**:
- "The main difference between our algorithm and other modern model-based DFO frameworks is that function value approximations may be used at interpolation points in place of exact function values." (p. 12)
- "Importantly, the actual value of κapp need not be known by our algorithmic framework." (p. 4)

### S076 · Incremental quasi-Newton algorithms for solving a nonconvex, nonsmooth, finite-sum optimization problem (Optimization Methods and Software 2024, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2023.2296432; arXiv:2207.09788)
**Read level**: full. Whole arXiv v1 read, file pp. 1–22 (abstract, §§1–5, Tables 1–2, disclosure, funding, references). Figure 3 (performance profile, p. 19) is an image and its curves are not in the text layer; only the caption and the authors' description (p. 16) were read. Table 2 rows are not labelled with data-set numbers in the extraction (pp. 16–19); the block order is assumed to follow Table 1. First page checked: title matches (arXiv title says "Problems", plural) and Frank E. Curtis is the second author (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Gulcin Dinc Yalcin (Eskisehir Technical University, postdoctoral fellow at Lehigh on a TÜBİTAK fellowship; pp. 1, 20) · **Place in trajectory**: continues his nonsmooth quasi-Newton line (cites his BFGS works with Que and with Robinson and Zhou, refs [22], [23], p. 21) into the incremental, finite-sum machine-learning setting; experiments run on the Lehigh COR@L polyps cluster (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: entry from an application objective: the transductive SVM (semi-supervised classification) objective (2), whose three terms are strongly convex, convex nonsmooth and nonconvex nonsmooth (p. 2). The incumbent IQN/IBFGS method of Mokhtari, Eisen and Ribeiro has guarantees only for convex smooth objectives (pp. 2–3). The paper is framed as an empirical question: whether IQN strategies can be effective on (2), which "would provide evidence that they should be considered viable options more generally" for (1) (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when guarantees are out of reach, frame the paper as a yes/no viability question on one hard, structured application problem and answer it with a family of variants (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: keep the IBFGS skeleton (per-component Hessian approximations, one component updated per iteration, O(n²) inverse update via Sherman–Morrison; Algorithm 1, (10)–(12), pp. 9–11) and change only how the gradient displacement is formed, so that curvature pairs stay positive and updates are not skipped. Five variants (pp. 10–13): I-BFGS applied directly with generalized gradients; I-BFGS-DC, which writes the nonconvex term as g − h and freezes the subgradient of h at the old point so that sᵀy ≥ 0 (pp. 4, 10–12, Algorithm 2); I-BFGS-S on a smoothing function with a per-component smoothing-parameter decrease rule from Chen (Algorithm 3, p. 12); I-BFGS-C, which adds 2ρ s to y when the smoothed component is weakly convex (Algorithm 4, p. 13; weak convexity via Proposition 2.4, p. 6); I-BFGS-SC, which also distributes the ½||w||² term and a β-term over all components so every term is strongly convex (§2.4, p. 7; §3.5, p. 13). Unit step sizes in the stated algorithm, with shorter steps used in the implementation (p. 10); the standard skip test with constant c is kept (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: when a quasi-Newton update would be skipped on a nonconvex component, use the problem's DC structure (freeze the concave part's subgradient) or a known weak-convexity modulus (add 2ρ s to y) to build a displacement with sᵀy ≥ 0 (pp. 10–13).
  - Transferable technique: redistribute a shared strongly convex regularizer across the finite-sum components so that each component becomes strongly convex and the incremental method's assumptions hold component-wise (§2.4, p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: no assumptions for the methods actually tested; "Convergence guarantees for IBFGS in our nonconvex and nonsmooth setting are elusive" (p. 2). Locally Lipschitz objective with Clarke generalized gradients (p. 9). A guarantee is available only in a limiting regime: after some iteration the smoothing parameters and convexification base point are fixed and the terms are strongly convex with Lipschitz Hessians (Assumption 3.1, p. 14), cyclic index choice (Lemma 3.2, p. 14); then the guarantee is for the approximate, not the original, objective (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: no new theorem. Supporting devices: DC decomposition of max{0, 1 − |t|} as max{0, |t| − 1} − (|t| − 1) (p. 4, Figure 1, p. 5); smoothing functions for (t)+ and |t| with a comparison of exact-outside-µ versus twice-differentiable choices (pp. 5–6); weak convexity of the smoothed objective from a convex Lipschitz outer function composed with a smooth inner function, giving explicit ρi ≥ 2(||xi||² + 1)/µ, which tends to infinity as µ → 0, so the unsmoothed F is not weakly convex (pp. 6–7); Lemma 2.5 (strong convexity plus Lipschitz gradient gives two-sided curvature bounds, p. 8). The convergence statements are cited verbatim-in-substance from Mokhtari et al.: Lemmas 3.2–3.3 and Theorem 3.4 (local superlinear convergence, pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable technique: before smoothing a nonsmooth nonconvex term, compute the weak-convexity modulus of the smoothed version as a function of µ; its blow-up as µ → 0 tells you why the original cannot be convexified locally and how aggressive the convexification must be (pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 12 data sets (UCI, LIBSVM and two generated sets; Table 1, p. 15), ten-fold cross-validation on sets 1–8 and fixed splits on 9–12 (p. 15), with 10 % to 100 % labelled data per set (p. 16). Baseline: TSVM-Bundle, a bundle method designed for this formulation, with "method-specific parameters as described in [3, 28]", the same iteration limits and starting points, subproblems solved by Gurobi (p. 16). Iteration limits 10⁴ (sets 1–8) and 2m (sets 9–12); random starting points in [−5, 5] (p. 15). Hyperparameters C1, C2 chosen from a small grid "as those yielding the best results for the testing data set" (p. 15). Reported: average testing error for every variant, data set and labelled fraction (Table 2, pp. 16–19), and a performance profile on a relative objective-value measure t = 1 + (f − f_best)/(f_worst − f_best) (p. 16; Figure 3, p. 19). Time only "in passing" because the codes are prototypes: about 1.8 s and 50 s for the I-BFGS variants versus orders 10³ s and 10⁵ s for TSVM-Bundle (p. 16). The number of random starts per data set is not stated. The table itself shows TSVM-Bundle with the lowest testing error in many rows (e.g., most rows of the fourth block and several rows of the ninth block, pp. 17–18) and nearly identical values (about 9.5) across all methods on the eleventh block (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: report the full per-instance outcome table even when the headline rests on a summary profile, so that readers can see where the incumbent wins (pp. 16–19).
- **D6 Positioning**: places the work at the meeting point of quasi-Newton theory (BFGS guarantees for smooth convex and nonconvex; nonsmooth BFGS mostly empirical, Lewis and Overton cited for limited guarantees) and incremental methods (incremental gradient, subgradient and Newton; IQN of Mokhtari et al. as the base, p. 3). Claims novelty narrowly: "there has not been prior work on the variants of the IBFGS strategy that are proposed and investigated in this paper" (p. 3). Cites his own nonsmooth quasi-Newton papers as the basis for using BFGS on nonsmooth functions (p. 3; refs [22], [23], p. 21). The TSVM formulation and bundle baseline come from Astorino and Fuduli (refs [2], [3], p. 20).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: question-and-answer contribution paragraph ("Our main goal is to answer the question ... Our answer is in the affirmative", p. 2). Open about missing theory, with a wider justification: guarantees are elusive "for numerous other algorithms ... that are applied regularly in practice to great effect" (p. 2). Each variant gets a short motivation sentence before its pseudocode (pp. 10–13). A caveat is recorded where the design could misbehave: gradient displacements may mix two smoothing parameters, "We did not find this to be an issue in our experiments, but it is something of which one should be aware" (p. 12). The headline claim is strong: all variants "outperform a state-of-the-art bundle method" (abstract, p. 1; pp. 2, 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic (five IBFGS variants) and computational; no new theory (pp. 2, 13–15). Limits stated: no guarantees for the nonsmooth nonconvex problem (pp. 2, 13); prototype implementations, so no detailed timing (p. 16); the smoothing-parameter mixing caveat (p. 12). Limits not stated: one application formulation; hyperparameters selected on test data (p. 15); profile measure (objective value) differs from the table measure (testing error) without discussion (p. 16). No future directions given; the conclusion restates the result (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 evidence ✅: the external baseline gets the same iteration limits and starting points and its published parameters (p. 16), and the full per-instance table is printed, including rows where it wins (pp. 16–19).
- Method 5 contradiction ✗: C1 and C2 are selected by best testing-set results (p. 15); timing compares prototypes with a Gurobi-backed bundle code only "in passing" (p. 16); the number of random runs is not stated; and the "all outperform" claim is not narrowed although Table 2 shows the bundle method best in many rows (pp. 1, 17–18). This falls below the Method 5 steps 5–7 and the Workflow C checkpoint.
- Method 4 variant ⚠: the conclusion rests on a performance profile over a best-to-worst normalized objective-value measure (p. 16), while the application's own yardstick (testing error, Table 2) is mixed; the paper does not ask whether the profile measure favours the I-BFGS variants.
- new-pattern candidate: keep the quasi-Newton skeleton and repair only the curvature pair through problem structure (DC split, weak-convexity shift, strong-convexity redistribution) when the information model changes (pp. 7, 10–13). This is close to Heuristic 10 (deterministic twin, replace only the broken component) but at the level of the secant pair.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Freeze the concave part's subgradient in a DC split to guarantee sᵀy ≥ 0 for a BFGS update (Algorithm 2, pp. 11–12).
- Shift y by 2ρ s using an explicit weak-convexity modulus of a smoothed component (Algorithm 4, p. 13; ρ bound, p. 7).
- Distribute a common regularizer across finite-sum components to make each strongly convex (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "Our main goal is to answer the question: Can IQN (such as IBFGS) strategies be effective and efficient when solving the nonconvex and nonsmooth problem (2)" (p. 2)
- "Since ours are only prototype implementations of the methods, we do not provide detailed time comparisons of the runs." (p. 16)
