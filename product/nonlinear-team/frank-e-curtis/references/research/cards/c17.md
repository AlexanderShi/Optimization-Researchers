### S049 · A self-correcting variable-metric algorithm framework for nonsmooth optimization (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2020, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drz008; arXiv 1708.02552)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v5 text). Main text pp. 1–21 read in full (§§1–6, Tables 1–4, acknowledgements). Appendix C (proof of Theorem 3.1, pp. 24–25) read. Appendix A (dual subproblem, pp. 21–23), Appendix B (geometric view of the BFGS update, pp. 23–24) and Appendix D (bundle lemmas, pp. 25–27) skimmed. Reference list (pp. 27–30) only spot-checked.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Daniel P. Robinson (Johns Hopkins AMS) and Baoyu Zhou (Lehigh ISE, the student who wrote the QP solver while visiting Andreas Wächter at Northwestern in summer 2017, p. 21) · **Position in trajectory**: this is the "restore guarantees later" step after BFGS for nonsmooth problems and Curtis–Que's restricted quasi-Newton and adaptive gradient-sampling methods (refs [15], [16], pp. 3, 16). It takes the damping-toward-a-fixed-matrix idea from Curtis's 2016 stochastic self-correcting paper (ref. [14], p. 8). Funded by DOE Early Career and NSF DMS (p. 21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper sorts earlier variable-metric methods for nonsmooth problems into three groups (p. 3): (1) methods without quasi-Newton updating, such as Shor's R-algorithm; (2) quasi-Newton methods that have guarantees only "when the updates are restricted to ensure that the resulting Hessian approximations remain sufficiently positive definite and bounded in all iterations" (refs [15, 16]); (3) "unadulterated" BFGS, which has guarantees only in a few specific cases (ref. [38]). The gap it names is that group 2 over-restricts the updates and group 3 "might break down (e.g., due to a failed line search) before any stationarity guarantee has been offered" (p. 3). A side point: bundle-method rates count only serious steps, not the null steps that produce them (p. 3). The motivation comes from smooth optimization, where quasi-Newton methods sit between steepest descent and Newton in cost per iteration (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: sort the prior methods by how they treat the one object you change (here the matrix update: none / restricted / unrestricted), and place the new method in one group with one named difference.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: SVANO (p. 6) takes the step s_k = −W_k(G_kω_k + γ_k). Here G_kω_k is a convex combination of subgradients at nearby points, γ_k is a perturbation from a line search or trust region, and the step must give the sufficient decrease (2.7). The matrix W_k is updated by the standard inverse BFGS formula (2.10), but with v_k = β_k H s_k + (1−β_k) y_k, where β_k is the smallest value in [0,1] that gives η ≤ s_kᵀv_k/‖s_k‖² and ‖v_k‖²/s_kᵀv_k ≤ θ ((2.8)–(2.9), p. 6). **What is kept**: the BFGS formula and a free choice of y_k ("in theory, any element of R^n will suffice", p. 8). **What changes**: the damping pulls toward a fixed matrix H, not toward H_k as in standard Powell damping, because only a fixed H gives constants η, θ that hold for all k (p. 8). The step-computation template is generic. It covers cutting-plane, bundle and gradient-sampling steps, with either a line search or a trust region (p. 6). The trust-region subproblem (2.13) is solved through its dual (2.15), whose constraints are just one affine equality and some bounds (p. 7). When a new cut or sample is added, the old dual solution plus a new variable set to zero stays feasible, which gives a warm start (p. 8). The paper gives three instances: BFGS with a weak Wolfe line search (SVANO-BFGS-Step, p. 12); a bundle trust-region step for convex f (SVANO-Bundle-Step, p. 13); and two nonconvex variants, a Schramm–Zowe downshifted bundle method and adaptive gradient sampling (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: if the theory needs a safeguard on the update pair, damp toward a fixed reference matrix, not the current iterate's matrix, so that the bounds hold with constants that do not depend on k (p. 8).
  - Transferable: pose cutting-plane or sampling subproblems in dual form, so that each added piece only appends a variable and the previous optimum stays a feasible starting point (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.1: f is bounded below, locally Lipschitz, and C¹ on an open set of full measure (p. 4). Stationarity means 0 in the Clarke subdifferential; the ε-subdifferential is also used (pp. 4–5). The complete convergence theory covers only the convex bundle instance (Theorem 4.1, p. 14). The nonconvex instances need weak (lower) semismoothness so that line searches terminate (p. 16). The classical BFGS instance fits the theory only if α̃_k ≥ α̃_min holds in every iteration (p. 12). Remark 2.1 and §6 claim that the framework still works for f unbounded below or extended-real-valued "with slight modifications" (p. 20). This is stated, not proved.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) The potential ψ(H) = tr(H) − ln det(H), with trace and determinant recurrences (3.2) and the decomposition (3.4), following Byrd–Nocedal's tool [11] (p. 9). (ii) Theorem 3.1 (p. 10): for any p ∈ (0,1), at least ⌈pK⌉ of any K iterations have bounded cosines and Rayleigh quotients. The proof (App. C, pp. 24–25) sums the recurrence to bound the average of the nonnegative loss terms ζ_k (C.3). It then bounds each of the ⌈pK⌉ smallest ζ_k by c₀ = (ψ(H₁) + θ − 1 − ln η)/(1 − p) (C.4). (iii) Corollary 3.1 carries this over to W_k (p. 10). (iv) Theorem 3.2: on the "good" subsequence, sufficient decrease forces ‖G_kω_k + γ_k‖ → 0 and ‖s_k‖ → 0 (p. 11). (v) Theorem 3.3, "a more practical version" of Kiwiel's Lemma 2.1: vanishing convex combinations of ε_k-subgradients with ε_k → 0 give a stationary limit (p. 11). (vi) For the bundle instance, Moreau–Yosida regularization lemmas (4.2), Lemmas 4.1–4.3, adapted from Ruszczyński §7.4 [52] (pp. 14, 25). Theorem 4.1 couples them with the radius update (4.4) and proves δ_k → 0 by contradiction through the angle between t_k and γ_k (4.7) (pp. 14–15). No rate or complexity bound is given.
  - Transferable: when uniform bounds on quasi-Newton matrices cannot hold in every iteration, prove that they hold on a fixed fraction of iterations with the tr − ln det potential, and run the descent argument only on that subsequence (pp. 10–11, 24–25).
  - Transferable: shrink the trust-region or sampling radius only when a computable stationarity test passes (max{υ₁‖Gω+γ‖, υ₂‖s‖, υ₃‖Gω‖} ≤ δ_k, (4.4)). Then δ_k → 0 picks out the subsequence on which stationarity is proved (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: C++ code with its own active-set QP solver, which borrows from Kiwiel [31] and supports the variable metric and an ∞-norm trust region (p. 17). All three instances use W₁ = I and y_k = g_{k+1,1} − g_{k,1} "so that our comparison of the algorithms mentioned above would be based on common choices of these values" (p. 17). Parameters: α = 1e-15, η = 1e-12, θ = 20, τ = 0.5. θ "had a large impact on performance" (p. 17). The test set is the first ten problems of Haarala et al. [24] with n = 50, five convex and five nonconvex (Table 1, p. 17). Success is the theory-based test (5.1), ‖G_kω_k‖ ≤ 10δ_k and δ_k ≤ 1e-4. Failure is 1e4 iterations or a stepsize below 1e-15 (p. 17). Four counters are reported: iterations, function evaluations, gradient evaluations and subproblems (p. 18). Results (Table 2, p. 18): Bundle and GS end "Stationary" on all ten problems; BFGS does so only on maxq, with "Stepsize" exits elsewhere, though its final f values are often near optimal. Ablation (Table 3, p. 19): with θ = ∞, Bundle fails on 5 of 10 problems and GS on 4 of 10 (iteration or stepsize limits). BFGS still fails on 9, now hitting the 1e4 iteration limit twice with up to 478,038 function evaluations (mxhilb). The paper explains this through ν/ξ → 0 in (5.2), i.e. the constants of Theorem 3.1 degrade (pp. 18–19). A small η did not have the same effect, explained by η entering as ln η in (C.4) (p. 19). The external codes LMBM (Fortran) and HANSO (Matlab) were run at their defaults (Table 4, pp. 19–20) and called "difficult to compare" because their termination tests differ (p. 20). SVANO-GS is randomized but was run once per problem, with the run said to be "representative" of its average (p. 17). No CPU times and no performance profiles. A weak Wolfe line search after successful bundle steps was added because it "improved the performance" (p. 16). This tweak lies outside the analysed instance.
  - Transferable: ablate the one constant the theory needs (here the upper curvature bound θ, set to ∞), and trace the degradation back to the constant in the proof that blows up (pp. 18–19, (5.2), (C.4)).
  - Transferable: report several cost counters (#func, #grad, #subs), because which instance wins "depends on the performance measure of interest" (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in group 2 but "unique in that it exploits the self-correcting properties of BFGS-type updating" (p. 3). It builds on Byrd–Nocedal's trace–determinant tool [11] (p. 9), Kiwiel's lemma [35] (p. 5), Schramm–Zowe's nonconvex bundle method [53] (p. 16), Ruszczyński's textbook proofs [52] (p. 14), and Curtis's own [14]–[16] (pp. 3, 8, 16). Toward the Lewis–Overton BFGS line [38], the stance is careful, not dismissive: §4.1 is said to illustrate "gaps in the analysis" only (p. 12). Overton is thanked for comments, and Appendix B is based on notes by Burke, Lewis and Overton (pp. 21, 23).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is in the abstract: the framework "does not overly restrict the manner in which the step computation matrices are updated, yet the scheme is controlled well enough that global convergence guarantees can be established" (p. 2). Hedging is open: "The hope is that the framework proposed in this paper can offer..." (p. 3), "We conjecture that this behavior can be explained as follows" (p. 18). The paper admits Theorem 3.2's conclusions "are not entirely consequential in their own right" (p. 11). The classical BFGS instance, with its failure mode, comes before the positive instances "to motivate the more involved strategies that we present" (p. 12). Theory and experiment are tied together by explaining each table through a named inequality ((5.2), (C.4), pp. 18–19). The conclusion is two paragraphs: one on extensions, one on why L-BFGS is omitted (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a framework with generic convergence theory, plus a C++ code with three instances. Limits, stated: a complete proof only for the convex bundle instance (Theorem 4.1, p. 14); for the nonconvex instances the paper does "not present complete details and analyses" (p. 15); L-BFGS is omitted because "limited memory BFGS techniques do not typically perform as well as a full memory approach" in nonsmooth optimization (p. 20), an observation not supported by data in the paper. Limits, unstated (inferred from the text): ten problems at n = 50; one run of a randomized method; θ = 20 chosen without a tuning account despite its "large impact" (p. 17); no rates. Future directions: only the unbounded or extended-real-valued case (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic 5 (keep the guarantee-free fast variant as comparator, restore guarantees later) ✅ evidence: SKILL.md already cites this paper as the guarantee-restoring step. Inside the paper, the guarantee-free classical BFGS is kept as an instance and comparator (§4.1, p. 12; Table 2, p. 18).
- Heuristic 10 (keep the skeleton, replace only the broken component) ✅ evidence: the BFGS formula and the descent framework stay, and only the curvature pair is replaced by the damped v_k (pp. 6, 8). The device comes from his stochastic paper [14] (p. 8).
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ⚠ variant: rivals in one framework with common W₁ and y_k (p. 17) and a one-constant ablation (θ = ∞, Table 3, p. 19) match Steps 1–3. But the external codes run at defaults with incomparable termination (p. 20), the randomized SVANO-GS gets single runs (p. 17), and θ = 20 has no tuning account (p. 17). These are the lapses the method's say–do note already records.
- Method 4 (Audit the yardstick) ⚠ variant: success is the paper's own theory-based test (5.1) (p. 17), which Step 4 warns against. Still, the tables print f(x_end), the text admits SVANO-BFGS "did not always perform poorly in terms of the final objective value" (p. 18), and the paper declines to rank itself against LMBM and HANSO because their termination tests differ (p. 20).
- new-pattern candidate: guarantee on a fraction of iterations. Prove that good matrix properties hold on at least ⌈pK⌉ of K iterations instead of all of them, and run the convergence proof on that subsequence (Theorem 3.1, p. 10; Theorem 3.2, p. 11; App. C, pp. 24–25).
- new-pattern candidate: predict the rival's failure, then show it (already a candidate from S047). The line-search breakdown of classical BFGS is argued in §4.1 (p. 12) and seen as "Stepsize" exits on 9 of 10 problems (Table 2, p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Damp quasi-Newton update pairs toward a fixed matrix H, so that the curvature bounds (2.9) hold for all k with fixed η, θ, and self-correction (Theorem 3.1) gives bounded matrices on a fraction p of iterations (pp. 6, 8, 10).
- Use ψ(H) = tr(H) − ln det(H) as a potential, and select the ⌈pK⌉ iterations with the smallest per-step losses to get uniform constants on them (pp. 9–10, 24–25).
- Write one algorithm template whose step-computation block is generic (convex weights ω_k plus perturbation γ_k), and prove convergence once for the template, then per instance (pp. 5–6, 11, 14).
- Ablate the theory-required constant (θ = ∞) and explain the observed degradation through the proof's constants (pp. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "One cannot maintain such assurances if Hk is used in place of H in (2.8)." (p. 8)
- "Overall, this influence of θ on our numerical results shows that a practical benefit of our self-correcting framework is that it allows our code to enforce theoretically sound termination criteria." (p. 19)

### S057 · Inexact sequential quadratic optimization with penalty parameter updates within the QP solver (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2020, DOI 10.1137/18M1176488; arXiv 1803.09224)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v3, "Extended version", whose title ends "within the QP solve"). pp. 1–30 read in full (§§1–7: framework, DUST, finite-update theory, complete algorithm and global theory, implementation, experiments, conclusion; Tables 1–4). Tables 5–8 (per-problem results for the feasible, β_φ = 0.5, β_φ = 0.99 and infeasible runs, pp. 30–37) skimmed. References (pp. 37–38) read. Fig. 1 (ρ trajectories, p. 27) exists only as axis text in the text layer.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: James V. Burke (U. Washington), Hao Wang (ShanghaiTech), Jiashan Wang (U. Washington) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the third rung of the extra-solves ladder in Heuristic 2. Two exact QP solves per iteration (SQuID, ref. [3], Burke–Curtis–Wang 2014) and two approximate QP solves (ref. [8], Curtis–Johnson–Robinson–Wächter 2014) become one inexact QP solve (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: SQP methods "have traditionally faltered in large-scale settings due to the expense of (accurately) solving large-scale quadratic subproblems" (p. 1). Inexact-SQP guarantees exist [8], but "the practical efficiency of such an approach remains an open question" (p. 1), and any globalization can misguide when it does not "immediately guide the algorithm toward promising regions" (p. 1). The entry is the timing of the penalty update: earlier methods update "in hindsight" or "at the expense of numerous subproblem solves" (p. 2). The paper sets itself against [3] and [8] by counting QPs per iteration (p. 2). The problem is an NLP with equality and inequality constraints, the exact penalty φ(x, ρ) = ρf(x) + v(x), and an infeasibility certificate defined as a stationary point of v (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable: place a new method by counting subproblem solves per iteration against your own earlier methods, and make "one" the headline claim (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The penalty parameter changes during the iterative QP solve. The solver keeps primal–dual feasible estimates (d^(j), u^(j)) for the penalty QP and a dual estimate w^(j) for the feasibility QP (ρ = 0) (p. 7). Three ratios compare model reduction to a weak-duality bound: r_v, r_φ (3.6) and the complementarity ratio r_c (Rc) (pp. 7–8). A relaxation ω, fixed within a solve and driven to zero across iterations, keeps the ratios well defined near stationary points (pp. 5, 7). DUST (p. 9): terminate if (Rφ), (Rc) and (Rv) all hold; if (Rφ) and (Rc) hold but (Rv) fails, set ρ ← θ_ρρ (3.10); otherwise continue. After the solve, PSST (p. 14) applies a posterior reduction so that (4.1), an inexact model-reduction condition with margin ω_k, holds. Design choices: the term ρf(x_k) is dropped from the model so the QP's optimal value does not shift with ρ (p. 5), and ρ appears only in the dual objective, so dual iterates stay feasible when ρ changes (p. 7). **What is kept**: a backtracking line search on the exact penalty (Algorithm 1, p. 5; Algorithm 2, p. 15). Implementation devices: an L-BFGS compact-form inverse updated in closed form when ρ changes (pp. 23–24), and dual coordinate descent at O(n + l) per coordinate by maintaining two running sums (pp. 24–26).
  - Transferable: build inexact-solve tests from a primal value and a dual bound. Passing a ratio then certifies a fixed fraction of the exact model reduction by weak and strong duality ((3.7)–(3.8), p. 8).
  - Transferable: formulate the subproblem so that the adaptive parameter enters only the dual objective. Dual feasibility then survives parameter changes in mid-solve (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f and c are C¹ (p. 3). Assumption 1: H_ρ positive definite for every ρ ∈ [0, ρ_{k−1}] and all constraint gradients nonzero (p. 6), defended by large-scale practice (positive-definite limited-memory approximations) and preprocessing (p. 6). Assumption 2 concerns the solver iterates (p. 11). Assumption 7: a compact convex X with 0 in its interior, bounded functions and derivatives along the iterates, uniform eigenvalue bounds, bounded κ₀ and bounded multipliers (pp. 15–16). The analysis "focuses on primal convergence" (p. 15). No local rate.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Lemmas 3–5 bound dual and primal iterates and show that primal and dual subproblem values are Lipschitz in ρ (pp. 11–12). Theorem 6 (p. 13): below an explicit threshold ρ̃ (3.15), (Rφ) and (Rc) imply (Rv), so DUST triggers only finitely often in a subproblem (proof by contradiction, pp. 13–14). Before the formal proof, an informal case analysis shows what DUST should do at stationary and non-stationary x_k (pp. 9–10). Global theory: a stepsize lower bound α_k ≥ CΔl (Lemma 10, p. 17); a shifted penalty ϕ = ρ(f − f̲) + v that is monotone even as ρ falls (Lemma 11, p. 17); vanishing model reductions and duality gaps (Lemma 12, pp. 17–18). Theorem 14 (p. 18): limit points are stationary for v; they are KKT points if ρ → ρ* > 0; and if ρ → 0, either all limit points are feasible or all are infeasible (contradiction with monotone ϕ, pp. 18–19). A three-case argument (Lemma 15, pp. 19–22) leads to Theorem 17: if ρ → 0, every limit point is infeasible stationary or a feasible point where MFCQ fails. The proof uses the dual form of MFCQ (pp. 22–23). Corollary 18 states the trichotomy (p. 23).
  - Transferable: prove that an adaptive parameter freezes after finitely many updates, so that the inner solver's own convergence theory applies from then on (pp. 5, 10, 13).
  - Transferable: when a penalty parameter may go to zero, show what its limit points are through the dual (multiplier) form of a constraint qualification (Theorem 17, pp. 22–23).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Python on a 2014 MacBook Air (p. 26). Feasible set: 126 CUTEr Hock–Schittkowski problems with the exact Hessian, an eigenvalue floor of 1e-4 and a condition-number cap of 1e6 (p. 26). All parameters are listed (p. 26). Stopping: v∞ ≤ 1e-5 and opt ≤ 1e-4, at most 200 iterations (p. 26). Table 1 (p. 27): 115/126 solved against SQuID's 110/122. The SQuID numbers are "obtained from [3]", with 122 (later 123) problems "due to compiling errors" and a different termination test (relative KKT residual scaled by ρ) (footnotes, pp. 27–28). Sensitivity (p. 27): ω₀ = 1e-1 solves the same 115 problems; β_φ = 0.5 gives 113 and β_φ = 0.99 gives 111, all extra failures being subproblem iteration limits. Coordinate descent is poor on ill-conditioned subproblems (> 5e5 steps, p. 27). The Hessian-modification constant was changed by hand for hs72, hs75 and hs93 (p. 28) and for five hs_inf problems (p. 28). Infeasible set: x₁ ≤ 0 and x₁ ≥ 1 added to every problem, 116/126 against SQuID's 111/123 (Table 2, p. 28). Large scale: 13 CUTEr problems with up to 20,000 constraints, L-BFGS, a 2,000-step subproblem limit (Tables 3–4, pp. 28–29). "For the remaining problems not shown" the QP solver failed (p. 28), and the number of problems attempted is not given. CPU: SQuID finished none of them within 10 minutes; the slowest new run (LUKVLE10) took 64 s; Ipopt solves it "in only a couple of seconds" (p. 29). The final ρ is reported per problem (Tables 4–8), with ρ trajectories for hs11, hs43 and hs61 (Fig. 1, p. 27).
  - Transferable: report the final value of each adaptive parameter for every problem and plot its trajectory on a few problems, to show that the update rule does not drive it to zero (Fig. 1, p. 27; Tables 4–5).
  - Transferable: when a production solver beats your prototype on your own showcase problem, say so in the text and name the reason (p. 29).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper continues the authors' SQuID [3] and the inexact SQO method of [8], restating each one's QP count and cost (p. 2). It groups Burke [1], Han [9] and Han–Mangasarian [10] as hindsight updates, and [3], Byrd–Lopez-Calva–Nocedal [5] and Byrd–Nocedal–Waltz [7] as multi-solve steering (p. 2). Nesterov [12] supports the coordinate-descent cost argument (p. 26). Ipopt [17] is credited with "two decades of software development" (p. 29).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Three bullet-point contributions (p. 2). The central claim is "We prove that our algorithm does not reduce the penalty parameter unnecessarily" (p. 2). Informal reasoning comes before formal proof ("intuitive arguments", pp. 9–10). "Outperforms" is claimed on success counts over different problem sets and termination rules (pp. 27–28). Small slips: Table 1 prints 91.20 % for 115/126 (= 91.27 %) (p. 27); the organization paragraph skips §5 (p. 2); Lemma 15 is cited as "Theorem 15(iv)" (p. 22). The conclusion restates the one-subproblem novelty and names two concrete improvements (pp. 29–30).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: an algorithm (the update rule within the solve), convergence theory and a Python prototype. Limits, stated concretely: "the performance could be further enhanced with the development of a more efficient QP subproblem solver and a more robust approach to addressing ill-conditioning of the Hessian approximation" (p. 30); coordinate descent is weak on ill-conditioned QPs (p. 27); large-scale failures are deferred (p. 28). Limits, unstated (inferred from the text): the SQuID comparison is not run in one framework; no local convergence analysis; per-problem hand changes of a Hessian constant (p. 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ✅ evidence: the paper SKILL.md cites for "updates inside the QP solve". The penalty update is a test on predicted progress inside the subproblem solver (DUST, p. 9; PSST, p. 14), with convergence to infeasible stationary points or MFCQ-failure points when ρ → 0 (Corollary 18, p. 23).
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ✅ evidence: the inner solver stops by ratio tests built from what the penalty line search needs (r_v, r_φ, r_c, pp. 7–9), with ω_k margins so that near-stationary points do not force exact solves (p. 5).
- Heuristic 2 (count a safeguard's extra solves) ✅ evidence: the third rung, "only requires solving a single QP in each iteration" (p. 2), restated in the conclusion (p. 30).
- Method 3 (Break it on purpose) ✅ evidence: the hs_inf set made by adding x₁ ≤ 0 and x₁ ≥ 1 (p. 28).
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✗ contradiction (partial): the main rival's numbers are copied from [3], with a different termination test and different problem counts (pp. 27–28). The rival is neither re-run nor placed in the same framework. On the other side, the incumbent's win (Ipopt on LUKVLE10) is printed (p. 29), as in Step 7.
- Heuristic 7 (name removed problems) ⚠ variant: the large-scale table shows only successful runs, and the attempted set is not specified (p. 28).
- new-pattern candidate: prove that the adaptive parameter freezes. Show that the in-solve update triggers only finitely often below an explicit threshold, so the solver's own guarantees resume (Theorem 6, p. 13; pp. 5, 10).
- new-pattern candidate: duality-gap certificates for inexact subproblem solves. Primal reduction over (initial value − dual bound) ≥ β certifies a β-fraction of the exact reduction ((3.6)–(3.8), pp. 7–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Monitor three ratios inside an iterative QP solver: a penalty-model ratio, a feasibility-model ratio and a complementarity ratio. Reduce the penalty parameter only when the first and third pass and the second fails (DUST, pp. 8–9).
- Add a vanishing slack ω_k to every ratio and model-reduction test, so that inexact tests stay satisfiable near stationary points (pp. 5, 7, 14).
- Make the infeasible test set mechanical, adding x₁ ≤ 0 and x₁ ≥ 1 to every problem (p. 28).
- Maintain running sums (v = Σλ_j a_j, p = Σλ_j q̃_j) so that each dual coordinate step costs O(n + l) instead of O(mn + ml) (p. 26).

**Quotes**:
- "This strategy represents a stark contrast to previously proposed techniques that only update the penalty parameter after a sequence of iterations, in hindsight at the end of an iteration [1, 9, 10], or at the expense of numerous subproblem solves within a single iteration [3, 5, 7]." (p. 2)
- "(On a contemporary laptop to computer, the state-of-the-art code Ipopt [17] solves problem LUKVLE10 in only a couple of seconds, but that code benefits from two decades of software development.)" (p. 29)

### S007 · Sequential quadratic optimization for nonlinear equality constrained stochastic optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2021, DOI 10.1137/20M1354556; arXiv 2007.10525)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v1, 20 Jul 2020, not the SIAM version; journal-only changes such as the later numbering are not visible here). pp. 1–28 read in full (§§1–5 with all lemmas and proofs) and references pp. 28–30 read. Figures 1–3 (pp. 7, 27, 28) exist only as captions in the text layer, so the performance profiles and box plots themselves were not read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Albert S. Berahas (U. Michigan IOE), Daniel P. Robinson and Baoyu Zhou (Lehigh ISE) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: opens the stochastic-SQP line. It joins the stochastic-gradient analysis style of the SIAM Review survey (ref. [2], p. 3) to the line-search SQP of the 2008–2010 inexact-SQP papers (refs [4, 5], p. 8). Signature Work in SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The setting is deterministic equality constraints and a stochastic objective f(x) = E[F(x, ω)], where only unbiased stochastic gradients can be computed (p. 3; problem (15), p. 13). The entry has two parts. First, the stochastic methods that exist are penalty-based (refs [7, 18, 21, 27]) "so do not benefit from advantages of SQP techniques", and stochastic Frank–Wolfe needs convex constraints (p. 3). Second, the deterministic state of the art, line-search SQP on an exact penalty, cannot be used because "it is assumed that line searches would be intractable" (abstract, p. 2). Penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods are said to suffer from ill-conditioning, nonsmoothness and "sensitivity to the scheme for updating the penalty parameter" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when an application removes one ingredient of the state-of-the-art method (here the line search), state exactly that ingredient as the obstacle and keep everything else.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Step 1 is a deterministic twin, Algorithm 1 (p. 8). It is line-search SQP with the line search replaced by a stepsize that minimizes an upper bound on the change in the ℓ₁ merit function. Because the merit function has a kink at α = 1, the minimizer falls before, at or beyond 1 (three cases, pp. 7–8; Fig. 1). Lipschitz estimates are multiplied by ρ > 1 until the bounds (11) hold (p. 8). The merit parameter rule (7)–(8) is based on model reduction, not on the multipliers. Remark 7 says this choice is "often better in practice" (p. 6). Step 2 is the stochastic method, Algorithm 3 (p. 15). It solves the same SQP system with a stochastic gradient (16) and keeps the same merit rule ((17)–(18), pp. 13–14). It adds an adaptive lower estimate ξ̄_k of Δq/(τ‖d‖²) ((20)–(21), p. 14) and projects the adaptive stepsize into [β_kξ̄_kτ̄_k/(τ̄_kL+Γ), the same + θβ_k²] (Line 7, p. 15). The interval has width θβ_k² (Lemma 23, p. 16). **What is kept**: the SQP system, the ℓ₁ merit function and the merit-parameter rule. **What changes**: the line search becomes a Lipschitz-based stepsize, then a projected one.
  - Transferable: design the stochastic method by first building and analysing a deterministic twin without the ingredient the stochastic setting forbids (pp. 2–3, 6).
  - Transferable: keep an adaptive stepsize but project it into an interval of width O(β²), so that adaptivity adds only a second-order term to the expectation analysis (Lemma 23, p. 16; Remark 32, p. 24).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1 (p. 4): f is C¹ with Lipschitz, bounded gradient; c is bounded with Lipschitz gradients; and the singular values of ∇cᵀ are bounded away from zero. This is LICQ, described as "a relatively strong assumption … but it is a reasonable one in our context due to the significant challenges that arise in the stochastic setting" (p. 4). Assumption 4: H_k is bounded and positive definite on the null space of J_k (p. 5). The Hessian of the Lagrangian is not required, because only global convergence is studied (p. 6). Stochastic part: gradient estimates are unbiased with variance at most M (Assumption 19, p. 13); Lipschitz constants are known in the analysis (p. 14); iterates stay in a set with bounded derivatives, "admittedly not ideal in a stochastic setting" (Remark 20, p. 14). Assumption 27 conditions on the event E_τ,small (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Deterministic: d_k = u_k + v_k, split into tangential and normal parts (p. 8). A critical quantity Ψ_k is defined with index sets K_u and K_v (p. 9). Lemmas 9–12 bound ‖v‖, the curvature, ‖d‖² and Δq by Ψ_k (pp. 9–10). Lemma 13 gives the merit upper bound with the kink term |1 − α| (p. 10), and Lemma 14 makes a three-case sufficient-decrease argument (pp. 10–11). The merit parameter is eventually constant (Lemma 16, p. 12), the stepsize is bounded below (Lemma 17, p. 12), and Theorem 18 follows (pp. 12–13). The standard backtracking SQP (Algorithm 2) is analysed in the same lemmas "for ease of comparison" (pp. 4, 8). Stochastic: Lemma 21 carries the deterministic constants over unchanged (pp. 15–16). ξ̄_k is bounded below deterministically (Lemma 22, p. 16). The key merit bound is Lemma 24 (pp. 16–18). Lemma 25 gives E[d̄_k] = d_k, and Lemma 26 an expectation inequality from the reduced Hessian (pp. 18–19). Lemma 28 bounds E[ᾱ_kτ̄_kgᵀ(d̄ − d)] by splitting on the sign of gᵀ(d̄ − d) and using only the interval width (pp. 20–21). Theorem 30 covers a fixed β (limit proportional to β) and diminishing β_k (Σβ = ∞, Σβ² < ∞) (pp. 22–23); Corollary 31 follows (p. 23). Event analysis: the runs are split into E_τ,small, E_τ,big and E_τ,0 (p. 20). E_τ,big has probability zero if the stochastic trial value under-shoots with probability at least p (Proposition 33, Gaussian Example 34, pp. 24–25). E_τ,0 cannot occur if gradient errors are bounded, e.g. finite-sum sampling (Proposition 35, pp. 25–26). The result is expected-stationarity bounds of stochastic-gradient type; no high-probability or complexity statement beyond (26).
  - Transferable: split the analysis of an adaptive parameter under noise into events. Prove convergence conditioned on the good event, then argue the bad events away separately, with probability zero or under a stated extra condition (pp. 20, 24–26).
  - Transferable: bound the expectation of (adaptive stepsize × zero-mean error) by conditioning on the sign of the error term, so that only the width of the stepsize interval enters (Lemma 28, pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable: prove the new method and the standard one in the same lemma sequence, stating where they differ, so that "matches the state of the art" is checkable line by line (pp. 4, 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab. Of the 123 equality-constrained CUTE problems, those were kept for which f is not constant, n + m ≤ 1000, and "the LICQ held at all iterates in all runs of all algorithms that we ran", giving 49 problems (p. 26). Deterministic test: H_k is the Lagrangian Hessian with inertia correction by adding multiples of the identity; all parameters are listed; termination is relative at 1e-6 within 1e4 iterations (p. 26). Dolan–Moré profiles of iterations and function evaluations, capped at t = 20 (p. 26; Fig. 2, p. 27). The goal is stated as reliability, not efficiency. Stepsizes: < 1 in 40.9 %, = 1 in 41.8 %, > 1 in 17.3 % of iterations (p. 26). Stochastic test: g̅_k ~ N(g_k, ε_N I) with ε_N ∈ {1e-8, 1e-4, 1e-2, 1e-1}, 10 runs per problem, 490 instances per noise level (pp. 26–27). The budget is 1,000 iterations for Stochastic SQP and 10,000 for the stochastic subgradient method, whose τ was tuned per instance over 11 values, so the baseline got 110 times the iterations (p. 27). L and Γ are estimated from gradient differences near x₀ and shared by both methods. H_k = I "for fairness of comparison with the (first-order) subgradient method" (p. 27). Errors are measured with the true gradient and least-squares multipliers, at the last sufficiently feasible iterate or else the least infeasible one (p. 27). Results are box plots (Fig. 3, p. 28, not readable in the text layer). Event check: τ̄_k ≤ τ_k^trial held in 99.92 %, 99.10 %, 99.22 % and 99.65 % of iterations for the four noise levels, and in 100 % of the last 100 iterations (p. 27).
  - Transferable: measure the event your theory conditions on, in every run, and report its frequency per noise level (p. 27).
  - Transferable: give the baseline the advantages (per-instance tuning over a wide grid, 10× iterations) and state the total multiplier (110×) (p. 27).
  - Transferable: evaluate stochastic runs with deterministic quantities (true gradient, least-squares multipliers) at a defined iterate, so that noise does not flatter the error measure (p. 27).
- **D6 Positioning**: Line-search SQP on an exact penalty is named "one of the state-of-the-art algorithms" (refs [14, 15, 26], p. 3). Penalty and augmented-Lagrangian methods [8, 9, 12, 17, 25] are criticized for conditioning and penalty sensitivity (p. 2). Stochastic penalty-based work from ML and vision venues [7, 18, 21, 27] and stochastic Frank–Wolfe [13, 16, 19, 20, 22, 28, 33] are set aside as not SQP or not for general nonlinear constraints (p. 3). The stochastic results are "of the type offered by stochastic gradient schemes for unconstrained optimization [2]" (p. 3), and Remark 32 draws the parallel with SG's constant-step trade-off (p. 24). The deterministic analysis "follows standard analysis for line-search SQP methods; see, e.g., [4, 5]", which are Curtis's own inexact-SQP papers (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Expectations are managed early: the deterministic method is "as reliable" but, "as should be expected, it is sometimes less efficient" (p. 3), repeated with "We do not claim..." (p. 26). Admissions are explicit: Remark 20 calls the boundedness assumption "admittedly not ideal" (p. 14), and θ = 0 is "detrimental in practice" despite its theoretical benefits (p. 24). §3.2.2 is titled "Poor Merit Parameter Behavior" and lists the bad events by name (p. 24). The strongest claim, "consistently and significantly outperforms" (pp. 3, 28), rests on box plots that a reader of the text alone cannot check.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: two algorithms (a deterministic twin and the stochastic SQP), deterministic convergence, and expectation results under an event, with experiments. Limits, stated: LICQ (p. 4); bounded iterates (pp. 14, 28); Lipschitz constants known in the analysis but estimated near x₀ in practice (pp. 14, 27); results conditional on E_τ,small, with E_τ,0 argued away only under bounded noise (pp. 20, 26). Limits, unstated (inferred from the text): the test set was filtered on run outcomes (LICQ "in all runs of all algorithms", p. 26); inequality constraints are not covered (title, p. 4). Future direction: only the θ = 0 variant that removes the bounded-gradient need (p. 28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace a line search by minimizing a Lipschitz-based upper bound on the nonsmooth merit function, handling the kink at α = 1 with three explicit cases (pp. 7–8, 10–11).
- Increase Lipschitz estimates geometrically until a verifiable upper-bound inequality holds, and reset them downward at the start of each iteration (Algorithm 1, p. 8; "1/2 times the estimates from the previous iteration", p. 26).
- Add an adaptive lower estimate ξ̄ of model reduction/(τ‖d‖²) that is bounded below deterministically, and use it to fix the stepsize interval (pp. 14, 16).
- Report the event frequency from the theory per noise level next to the performance results (p. 27).
