### S058 · Central groupoids, central digraphs, and zero-one matrices A satisfying A^2 = J (Journal of Combinatorial Theory, Series A 2004, DOI 10.1016/j.jcta.2003.10.001)
**Read level**: full. The whole preprint was read, file pp. 2–16 (abstract, §§1–4, acknowledgment, references). File p. 1 is the ResearchGate cover. The version read is the authors' preprint of 25 Sept 2003 (p. 2), not the typeset journal version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: John Drew, Chi-Kwong Li (William and Mary), Daniel Pragel (SUNY Albany, then Caltech) (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: an undergraduate NSF REU paper (grant DMS 9987803, p. 16), based on the 2002 REU report [2] by Curtis and Pragel (pp. 3, 16). Curtis's address is already Northwestern (p. 2). It predates all of his optimization work.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The objects are central digraphs (exactly one length-two walk between every ordered pair of vertices), equivalently 0-1 matrices with A² = J_n, and central groupoids ((x∗y)∗(y∗z) = y) (p. 3). The entry is a long-open classification problem. Hoffman asked for all such matrices, "which seems to be a very difficult problem" (p. 3), and the problem remains open for k ≥ 4 (p. 5). The paper does not attack the classification head-on. It does three things around it: re-proves the basic theory with short proofs, settles a specific conjecture (Fletcher's row/column multiplicity conjecture, pp. 5–6), and introduces a local move (the "switch") as a tool (§3, pp. 7–14).
  - Transferable: when the headline problem is out of reach, enter through a sharper sub-question that someone has conjectured on (here Fletcher's multiplicity conjecture, p. 5) and through a new elementary operation that relates objects to one another (switches, p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The key tool is the switch. Replace a 2×2 identity submatrix A[p,q;r,s] by its complement. Theorem 3.1 shows the result stays in A_n iff {p,q} ∩ {r,s} = ∅, rows r and s are equal, and columns p and q are equal. The rank changes by at most one because Ã − A = ±(e_p − e_q)(e_r − e_s)^t (pp. 7–8). This turns static structure into a walk through A_n. Theorem 3.3 gives an explicit schedule of three switch types that takes the standard matrix A0 to a matrix of maximal rank ⌊(n+1)/2⌋, one rank unit per switch (pp. 8–12). That yields a short proof of Shader's rank theorem, whose original proof was a set of delicate constructions (p. 8). Other new results: the decomposition of A into k permutation matrices with P1² = I (Lemma 2.4, Theorem 2.5, pp. 6–7); spectra of words in A and A^t (Theorem 2.6, p. 7); embedding every central groupoid of size k² into one of size (k+1)² with rank + 1 (Theorem 4.1, p. 15); at most k sub-central groupoids of size (k−1)² (Theorem 4.3, pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: define a local move on your objects, characterize exactly when it preserves membership, and bound how much it changes the quantity of interest (rank ≤ 1). You then have a constructive path to extremal objects (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Pure combinatorial matrix theory. The model is A_n, the 0-1 n×n matrices with A² = J_n, nonempty iff n = k² (Theorem 2.1, p. 4). Classification is up to permutation similarity (pp. 4, 13). Smoothness, noise or oracle notions are n/a. The switch programme depends on an unproved premise. Conjecture 3.2, that every A in A_n is reachable from the standard matrix by switches, is left open: the authors say they can neither prove nor disprove it (p. 8). Problem 3.6, whether identical rows and columns always exist so that switches are possible, is also open (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Row and column sums are forced equal by computing A³ two ways (AJ = JA). Perron root and trace give k ones on the diagonal (Theorem 2.1, p. 4). (b) The Jordan structure follows because A² is diagonalizable, so the nilpotent blocks have size ≤ 2. The rank lower bound comes from AA^t being PSD with trace k³ and eigenvalues ≤ k², with an equality case that forces the standard matrix (Theorem 2.2, p. 5). (c) A parametric block counterexample for all k ≥ 4, shown concretely for k = 4 (row multiplicities {4,4,3,3,1,1} vs column {4,4,2,2,2,2}) (Theorem 2.3, p. 6). (d) A graph-theoretic count of loops and two-cycles (Lemma 2.4, p. 6), then the Brualdi–Ryser corollary to split A into permutation matrices (Theorem 2.5, p. 7). (e) Cyclic invariance of the spectrum (XY and YX have equal eigenvalues) to reduce any word to one containing A² (Theorem 2.6, p. 7). (f) A rank-one-update bound on rank change (p. 8). (g) An upper-bound-plus-attainment argument. The number of switches bounds the rank increase, so exhibiting final rank ⌊(n+1)/2⌋ proves that every switch raised rank by exactly one (p. 11). (h) Legality of the whole switch schedule, in any order, is certified by showing that the set of touched row indices and the set of touched column indices are disjoint (p. 10). (i) Linear independence is shown by vectors whose leading ones sit in distinct positions, built partly by differences of rows (pp. 11–12). (j) Block-matrix multiplication for the (k+1)² embedding (p. 15). (k) A uniqueness-of-walks argument for Theorem 4.3, credited to the referees (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: device (g). When each move can raise a monotone quantity by at most one, count the moves and show that the end state reaches start + count; every move is then exactly +1, with no step-by-step tracking (p. 11).
  - Transferable: device (h). To certify that a long sequence of local moves stays feasible regardless of order, show that the coordinates each move reads (its preconditions) and the coordinates any move writes are disjoint (p. 10).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There are no timing experiments. The computation is enumeration and verification. Computer programs from the REU report [2] are public (p. 3). They establish the six permutation-similarity classes of A9 (p. 4, proof deferred to [2]). Conjecture 4.2 was checked on the 101 matrices of A16 listed in [2], and by computer for k = 2, 3 (p. 15). Maximal-rank matrices in A16 and A25 produced by the switch scheme are printed in full (pp. 12–13), so the reader can follow the proof's basis construction (p. 12). The authors list scientific computation as one of their approaches (p. 3).
  - Transferable: print the worked instance the proof constructs (the full A16 and A25 matrices with the switched entries marked, p. 12) so a reader can trace the abstract index bookkeeping on a concrete case.
- **D6 Positioning**: Self-described as partly a survey: "A survey of known results is given, including short proofs for some of them" (p. 2). It builds on Hoffman's 1967 problem, Knuth's 1970 notes and Shader's 1974 rank theorem (pp. 3, 16), and on Fletcher's 1991 dissertation and 2001 paper for the conjectures (pp. 5, 7–8). Prior work is assessed by simplicity of proof, not attacked. Shader's result is called "interesting", and the critique is only that its proof needs a long list of checks (p. 8). The authors flag their novelty item by item ("The following proposition is new.", p. 7; "We have settled his conjecture", p. 5). The elegant proof of Theorem 4.3 is credited to the referees (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract has four short sentences (survey, new results, settled conjectures, open questions, p. 2). The organization paragraph sits at the end of §1 (p. 3). Every construction is followed by its smallest concrete instance (k = 3 matrices pp. 3–5; k = 4 counterexample p. 6; A16 and A25 pp. 12–13). After a terse legality proof the authors add an intuition remark about how the forbidden index lists grow (p. 11). Open problems are numbered and placed where they arise: Problems 2.7, 3.5 and 3.6 and Conjectures 3.2 and 4.2 (pp. 7, 8, 14, 15). Hedging is low and specific, e.g. the plain admission that Conjecture 3.2 could be neither proved nor disproved (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical, in combinatorial matrix theory. It gives short proofs of known facts (§2), a negative resolution of Fletcher's conjecture for k ≥ 4 (p. 6), the switch tool and a short proof of Shader's rank theorem (§3), and embedding and counting results for sub-central groupoids (§4). Limits are stated concretely. The classification for k ≥ 4 remains open (p. 5). Conjecture 3.2 is unresolved (p. 8). Conjecture 4.2 is verified only for k = 2, 3, for the 101 matrices of A16, and for certain subsets in [2] (p. 15). Problems 2.7, 3.5 and 3.6 are left as future directions (pp. 7, 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (variant ⚠, analogue only): Theorem 2.3 builds a structured instance family on purpose, for all k ≥ 4, to break a conjecture, and exhibits the smallest member (p. 6). This is a pure-mathematics ancestor of Method 3's habit of manufacturing hard instances instead of waiting for them. It concerns counterexamples to a conjecture, not solver test sets.
- No link to Methods 1, 2, 4 or 5. There is no solver, globalization or benchmark in this work (§§1–4).
- new-pattern candidate: **Re-prove a known theorem with a new elementary tool.** Switches turn Shader's delicate constructions into a short schedule-plus-counting proof (pp. 8–12). The surveyed basics also get short proofs (pp. 2, 4).
- new-pattern candidate: **Computer verification of small cases, then a stated conjecture.** The six classes of A9, the 101 matrices of A16, and k = 2, 3 are checked before Conjecture 4.2 is stated (pp. 4, 15).
- new-pattern candidate: **Numbered open problems placed where they arise** (pp. 7, 8, 14, 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Upper-bound-plus-attainment: if each move changes a quantity by at most 1, prove the final value equals start + number of moves, and every move is then exactly +1 (pp. 8, 11).
- Order-independent feasibility of a move schedule: show that the precondition coordinates (rows and columns that must stay equal) and the modified coordinates of all moves are disjoint (p. 10).
- Rank lower bound by exhibiting row-space vectors with leading ones in distinct positions, using differences of rows to move the leading one (pp. 11–12).
- After each general construction, print its smallest nontrivial instance (k = 4 counterexample, p. 6; A16 and A25, pp. 12–13).

**Quotes**:

- "In particular, we shall use approaches from algebra, combinatorial theory, matrix theory, and scientific computations." (p. 3)

### S081 · Determinant optimization on binary matrices (American Journal of Mathematical and Management Sciences 2006, DOI 10.1080/01966324.2006.10737660)
**Read level**: full. File pp. 1–20 (synoptic abstract, §§1–6, acknowledgements) were read in full. Appendix B (tabu parameter grid, pp. 24–25) was read in full. Appendix A (optimal matrices, pp. 21–23) was skimmed: its summary table on p. 21 was read, but the ±1 matrix entries are lost in the text layer. References were read (p. 26). Formula symbols (=, −, ±, ≤) are often missing from the extraction, so the case labels n = 4k, 4k−1, 4k−2, 4k+1 below are reconstructed from section order and from the n values used (e.g. 17, 21, 25, 41 in Table 2, p. 16).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Rex K. Kincaid (William and Mary) (p. 1); Chi-Kwong Li acknowledged as co-supervisor of the honors thesis (p. 20) · **Place in trajectory**: written from Northwestern IE/MS (p. 1). It extends the 2003 honors thesis (S086, cited p. 26). This is Curtis's first optimization paper: a discrete, nonlinear-objective problem attacked with spectral theory, exhaustive enumeration and then local search and tabu search.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Problem 1 asks for the m×n ±1 matrix with the most 2×2 submatrices of nonzero determinant. This is the {0,1} question of most "odd" 2×2 submatrices, rewritten in ±1 form because the conversion gives an objective in a more convenient analytic form (p. 2). Problem 2 is the D-optimal design problem max det(A^tA) (p. 2). The entry point is a gap in recent work. Problem 1 "is thoroughly covered in Marks et al. (2003)", but one case remains unsolved: square n×n with n = 4k+1. The authors offer different approaches for that case, including computational ones (p. 2). The motivation is the links to Hadamard matrices, D-optimal designs and Turán-number bounds (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a recent paper has thoroughly covered a problem, take its single unsolved case as your target and bring a method family the prior paper did not use (here, computation and heuristics; p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: (1) A unifying lens. Through compound matrices, M(A) = ¼·E₂(eig(AA^t)) (Theorem 1, pp. 3–4). Problems 1 and 2 are then two instances of one objective function, tr C₂(AA^t) and tr C_n(AA^t) (p. 4). Majorization bounds tr C_k(AA^t) with equality iff AA^t = nI_m, so Hadamard submatrices solve both problems (Theorem 2, p. 4). (2) Case analysis through deletions from Hadamard matrices. The authors give alternative compound-matrix proofs for n = 4k−1 and 4k−2 by computing eig(B^tB) after deleting one or two rows and columns (Theorems 5–6, pp. 7–8), and a bound for 4k+1 by deleting three (Theorem 10, p. 10). (3) Constructive enumeration for n = 4k+1. Fix as many rows as possible up to the equivalences of Theorem 4 and pre-set a goal sequence v of counts after each added row. A program (CreateCE.m) then enumerates row additions. This proves M(A9) = M_9 (pp. 11–13) and builds A13 (p. 14). (4) Once enumeration becomes too large for a computer at n ≥ 17 (p. 14), the authors switch to local search on single-entry sign flips. Algorithm 1 is best-improvement descent with multistart (p. 15). Algorithm 2 is tabu search with two tabu lists, one forbidding re-flipping an entry for L1 iterations and one forbidding two flips in the same row within L2 iterations (pp. 16–17).
  - Transferable: express two different combinatorial objectives as the same symmetric spectral function at two orders (E_k of eig(AA^t)). Majorization then gives both bounds and the equality characterization at once (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Entries are restricted to ±1 (p. 3). Optimal solutions in §4 assume the Hadamard conjecture is true (pp. 2–3, 6). Solutions are counted up to the equivalence operations of transposition, row and column permutation and sign changes (Theorem 4, p. 5), and the paper restricts to square matrices on the strength of Marks et al. (p. 5). The yardstick U in Tables 2–4 is the theoretical upper bound under the assumption that an Ehlich-type matrix exists (p. 16). The 9×9 proof assumes a matrix beating A9 would have property (P1) or (P2) (p. 11). Smoothness and noise models are n/a (discrete problem).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Counting nonzero entries of the second compound as tr(C₂C₂^t)/4 and the identity tr C₂(AA^t) = E₂(λ) (Theorem 1, p. 4). (b) Majorization with an equality case (Theorem 2, p. 4). The proof attributes section 19.F and section 4.A to "Marks et al. (2003)", although p. 4 earlier credits section 19.F to Marshall and Olkin, an apparent citation slip. (c) Normalizing a Hadamard matrix by the equivalences so that one row and column are all ones, then deleting it so that B^tB = (n+1)I − J has an explicit spectrum (Theorem 5, p. 7). Block-structured analogues are used for two and three deletions (Theorem 6, p. 8; Theorem 10, p. 10). (d) Per-pair counting M(A) = Σ k_{ij}·k̄_{ij}, with perfect and near-perfect pairs (Theorem 3, eq. (3.1), definitions pp. 5–6). (e) A Diophantine parity argument that fixes the structure of the first three rows in the 9×9 case (pp. 11–12). (f) A Table-1 catalogue of the possible inner-product multisets for any matrix that beats a known value, used to bound the enumeration (pp. 12–13). (g) Computer enumeration as proof: every enumeration attempt yields no final solution, hence A9 is optimal (p. 13).
  - Transferable: device (f). Before enumerating, list every structure a better solution would have to have (here, the possible multisets of off-target inner products and the objective each gives), and enumerate only within those (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Test sizes are n = 17, 21, 25 and 41, all 4k+1 (Tables 2–4, pp. 16–18). Algorithm 1 is run from the structured start 2I − J, which is a global optimum for n = 3, 4, 5 (p. 15), and from 100 random ±1 starts. Reported are S (result from 2I − J), the average, the best, the bound U and the % gap to U (p. 16). Tabu search uses the same protocol, with the best parameter set chosen from a 16-point grid of (L1, L2) ∈ {50, 30, 10, 5} × {8, 5, 3, 1} at t_max = 200/300/400/900 and seed 123456789, printed in full in Appendix B (pp. 17, 24–25). There is a longer 1000-restart run (Table 4, p. 18). Results: tabu beats multistart in best and average for all n (p. 17). The gap to U is a fraction of a percent (pp. 16–18, 19). The 1000 restarts give only small improvements, which the authors read as either optimality or a sign that another approach is needed (p. 18). The second tabu list mattered only at n = 41 (p. 18). The authors report two things against tabu: an aspiration criterion found nothing better (p. 16), and a single run of Algorithm 1 can beat a single run of Algorithm 2 (p. 17). Comparison is by objective value under fixed iteration budgets; no CPU times are reported (grep of the text for time units finds none). Tuning was done on the same four instances the results are reported on. The "best set" selection rule is not stated. Matching Table 3 to Appendix B, the chosen row is the best-average row for n = 17, 21 and 25, but for n = 41 it is (10,5), which has the best maximum (343636) while (10,1) has the higher average (343574 vs 343567) (pp. 17, 24–25). The new best-known values are listed in Appendix A (p. 21).
  - Transferable: report heuristic results as a percentage gap to a theoretical bound, state the bound's assumption next to it, and include one structured start alongside the random restarts (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: print the whole parameter grid with the seed and budgets in an appendix rather than only the winning setting (pp. 24–25).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper connects three literatures: Hadamard maximum determinant (Hadamard 1893; Brenner–Cummings), D-optimal designs (Hedayat–Zhu; Hedayat–Sloane–Stufken) and Turán numbers (de Caen et al.) (p. 2). Marks et al. (2003) is the immediate predecessor. It is credited for the optimal values in three cases (pp. 7, 8, 9) and for the Theorem 9 bounds (p. 9). The paper's own role is different approaches and alternative compound-matrix proofs (pp. 2, 7, 8). The heuristics are borrowed whole from Glover's tabu search (pp. 16–17). Problem 2 results are taken from Hedayat–Zhu (Theorems 8 and 11, pp. 9–10), whose name is misspelled "Heyadat" in several places (pp. 9, 10, 13, 18).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The synoptic abstract states the scope and the empirical claim: near-optimal solutions for 13 ≤ n ≤ 41 and the best known solutions to date for the 4k+1 cases up to 41 (p. 1, symbols partly lost). The roadmap is at the end of §1 (pp. 2–3). Each case of §4 opens by saying whether the optimum is known and who proved it (pp. 7–9). The paper says plainly where proof stops and computation takes over: optimality cannot be proved for the best values found, so two heuristic algorithms are offered (p. 14). Hedging is used on the empirical side (the 1000-restart plateau "may imply" two readings, and the link between the two problems "remains unclear", p. 18). §6 "Future research" gives an eigenvalue table of the optimal matrices as a structural lead (Table 5, pp. 19–20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution has three layers. Theory: a unifying compound-matrix view and alternative proofs (pp. 3–8). Computation: a proof by enumeration for n = 9 and a construction of A13 (pp. 11–14). Heuristics: the best known values for n = 17, 21, 25 and 41 (pp. 16–18, 21). Stated limits: no optimality proof for n ≥ 17 (p. 14). Whether the optimal sets of Problems 1 and 2 coincide for large 4k+1 remains unclear; at n = 17 they are disjoint (p. 18). The deletion construction may not always yield a solution (p. 10). Future directions are concrete: study the repeated eigenvalue structure (p. 19). Treat max tr C₂(AA^t) over a_ij ∈ {±1} as an integer program, and maximize it over the linear relaxation −1 ≤ a_ij ≤ 1 for bounds and starting points (p. 20). That last suggestion points from discrete to continuous optimization.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): Partial match. The two heuristics share one move set, the same structured start and the same 100 random restarts (pp. 15–17). The paper prints the point where the simpler method can win (one run of Algorithm 1 can beat one run of Algorithm 2, p. 17) and a feature that did not help (aspiration, p. 16). Unlike Method 5 Step 6, tabu parameters were tuned on the evaluation instances and the selection rule is unstated (pp. 17, 24–25). This is the same ⚠ the SKILL notes for 2012–2019 ("Parameters tuned on the evaluation set"). Here, unlike those, the whole tuning grid is reported.
- Method 4 (variant ⚠): The yardstick is the gap to a bound U whose validity depends on an unproved existence assumption (Ehlich-type matrices), and the paper says so beside every table (p. 16). It also reads its 1000-restart plateau two ways: optimality, or the method's own limit (p. 18). This means stating the yardstick's assumption rather than auditing it.
- Heuristic 3 (variant, in the card only): the problem picked is the single case prior theory left open (p. 2), not a user-facing failure.
- new-pattern candidate: **One spectral lens for two problems.** Problems 1 and 2 become tr C_k(AA^t) at k = 2 and k = n, and known results are re-proved through that lens (pp. 3–8).
- new-pattern candidate: **Exact until burdensome, then heuristics with a gap-to-bound.** Enumeration proves the small cases (n = 9, 13), and local search with gap to U is used once enumeration breaks down at n ≥ 17 (pp. 14, 19).
- new-pattern candidate: **Computer enumeration inside a proof**, pruned by a pre-derived structural catalogue (Table 1) and goal sequences v (pp. 12–14). This parallels S058's computer checks.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Rewrite a counting objective on ±1 matrices as a symmetric function of eig(AA^t) through compound matrices, and use majorization for bounds with equality cases (pp. 3–4).
- Normalize by symmetries (sign changes, permutations) so that a row and column are all ones, then delete it; the remaining Gram matrix has the form cI − J with a closed-form spectrum (p. 7).
- Prune an exhaustive search by (i) fixing rows up to symmetry, (ii) a pre-derived list of structures any improving solution must have, and (iii) a goal value the partial objective must hit after each added row (pp. 11–14, 19).
- For a combinatorial local search, run one structured start plus many random restarts and report start value, average, best, bound and % gap. Print the full parameter grid with seed (pp. 15–18, 24–25).

**Quotes**:
- "Thus, heuristic search techniques proved useful in uncovering near-optimal solutions when constructive techniques became computational burdensome" (p. 19)
- "In our experiments, however, no better solutions were found by including an aspiration criterion." (p. 16)
