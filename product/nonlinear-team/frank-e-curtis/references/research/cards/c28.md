### S092 · Progressively Sampled Equality-Constrained Optimization (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2510.00417)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–24: main text, numerics, conclusion, references); Appendix A (pp. 24–38) skimmed — Lemma A.1 statement and proof start (pp. 24–26), Lemma A.2 (p. 28), Lemma A.4 statement (pp. 33–34), Theorem A.1 and the start of its proof (pp. 37–38) read; the algebra of the remaining appendix proofs not checked. Version read: arXiv v2 (13 May 2026).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Lingjun Guo, Daniel P. Robinson · **Position in trajectory**: a new rung in the stochastic-constrained line (2020–): sample-average approximation (SAA) with sampled constraints, using the 2021 stochastic SQP [1] as one of the subproblem solvers (pp. 20, 22).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: min f(x) s.t. c(x) = 0 where both f and c are averages (or expectations) over large numbers of terms; motivating settings are least squares with a known target residual and physics-informed learning where the expected residual of a differential equation must be zero (p. 2). Entry point: the progressive (adaptive) sample-size method of Mokhtari–Ozdaglar–Jadbabaie [10] for unconstrained ERM, which gains in worst-case gradient-sample complexity because a minimizer of a sampled objective lies close to that of an enlarged sample (p. 2); the paper samples the constraint terms as well and says why this is "quite distinct": constrained second-order complexity is needed, termination must use approximate constrained stationarity (least-squares multipliers), and perturbing a constraint changes the problem fundamentally, so acute-perturbation theory is required (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: when lifting an unconstrained sampling idea to constraints, list explicitly the three things that break (subproblem complexity, termination measure, geometry of the perturbed feasible set) and give each one a named tool before writing the algorithm (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm 1 (p. 6) solves a sequence of sampled problems (3) with nested sample sets S_{f,k} ⊇ S_{f,k−1}, S_{c,k} ⊇ S_{c,k−1}, sizes multiplied by θ_f, θ_c each outer iteration, each subproblem warm-started at x_{k−1} and stopped when (x_k, y_{S_k}(x_k)) is (ϵ_k, ζ_k)-stationary in the second-order sense (6) (pp. 5–6). The subproblem solver is left generic: any method with (a) an O(max{ϵ^−2, ζ^−3}) bound from a cold start and (b) an O(log(ϵ_{k−1}/ϵ_k)) bound from a warm start inside the strongly Morse region (Theorem 3.1, pp. 17–18). Least-squares multipliers need only be computed when the solver thinks it is done (p. 5). What is not changed: the per-subproblem method is a standard deterministic one (Fletcher-augmented-Lagrangian second-order method of Goyens–Eftekhari–Boumal [6], or the SQP of [1]) (pp. 18, 20).
  - Transferable: state the outer scheme against a generic inner-solver contract (cold-start complexity + warm-start local rate) and prove the total sample bound once; then instantiate the contract with one concrete solver in a corollary (Theorem 3.1 → Corollary 3.1, pp. 17–19).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Assumption 3.1 (pp. 6–7): (a) uniform lower bound σ_min on the smallest singular value of every sampled Jacobian with |S| ≥ p1 (rules out infeasible stationary points), (b) the solver converges to second-order points, (c) the full-sample problem is (α, β)-strongly Morse with respect to the Lagrangian at least-squares multipliers. Assumption 3.2: bounded first and second derivatives (p. 7). Assumption 3.3: deterministic finite-population "variance" bounds of single terms around the full-sample functions, with the constraint-Jacobian bound relative to ‖∇c(x)‖² (p. 8). The authors admit (a) and 3.3 could be weakened to high-probability statements but keep them deterministic "for ease" (pp. 7, 8); Remark 3.1 concedes that in practice the method may be run with first-order stationarity only, and the second-order requirement is there to get the complexity result (p. 7); Remark 3.2 justifies (c) via Morse programs (Fujiwara) (p. 7). Infeasible-stationary-point scenarios excluded because they "would distract from the essential properties of our algorithm" (p. 6). Appendix A analyses constraint sampling only, with the full objective sample (p. 24).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (a) finite-population sampling bound: write the sampled average as N/|S| times the full average minus the omitted terms, then Cauchy–Schwarz, giving errors proportional to ξ_S = sqrt(N(N−|S|))/|S| (Lemma 3.2, p. 9); (b) acute perturbations: if |S| exceeds a threshold proportional to N, sampled and full Jacobians are acute perturbations, proved by a singular-value perturbation bound on I + ∇c(x)†(∇c_S(x) − ∇c(x)) (Lemma 3.3, p. 10); (c) Stewart's 1977 perturbation theory of pseudo-inverses and projections to bound the difference of least-squares multipliers, with a typo in Stewart's Theorem 5.2 corrected (Lemma 3.4, pp. 11–13); (d) a projection lemma bounding the full-range component of a sampled null-space vector (Lemma 3.5, p. 13); (e) inheritance of strong Morse-ness: the sampled problem is (α_S, β_S)-strongly Morse with α_S ≥ α/2 by decomposing d_S into full null-space and range parts (Lemma 3.6, pp. 14–17); (f) the warm-start device: with tolerances ϵ_k = τ1 ξ_{S_k}, the previous subproblem's output already lies in the next subproblem's strongly Morse region (Lemma A.1, pp. 24–26), where Fletcher's augmented Lagrangian is locally strongly convex so plain gradient descent needs only ⌈log2(5ωϵ_k/ϵ_{k+1})⌉ iterations (Lemma A.4, pp. 33–34); (g) a geometric sum of |S_k| log terms (Theorem A.1 proof, p. 38). Complexity form: |S1| max{ϵ^−2, ζ^−3} + Σ_k |S_k| log(ϵ_{k−1}/ϵ_k) gradient evaluations versus O(N max{ϵ^−2, ζ^−3}) one-shot (p. 18).
  - Transferable: to show a warm-started sequence is cheap, set each subproblem tolerance to the sampling-error scale ξ_{S_k}, prove that the previous output then sits inside the next problem's nondegenerate (strongly Morse) region, and pay only a log factor per stage (Lemma A.1 with (46), pp. 24–26; Lemma A.4, pp. 33–34).
- **D5 Experiments**: Matlab R2024b (p. 19). Measure: individual constraint-gradient (or data) accesses, not iterations (pp. 19, 22). (i) Artificial 2-D problem, f(x) = x1, constraint terms x1 − x2² + a sin(φx1 + ω1) + a cos(φx2 + ω2), a = 10⁻⁴, φ = 100, N = 2048 (p. 19); Figure 1 checks numerically that both the full problem and a 64-sample problem are (0.6, 0.8)-strongly Morse on [−1, 1]² (pp. 19–20). p1 = 64 against one-shot p1 = N, one random x0 shared by both, tolerances (43) tied to ξ_S (p. 20); with the Fletcher-AL solver (ρ = 10) progressive sampling needs a small fraction of the constraint-gradient evaluations for final tolerances 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁶ (Figure 2, pp. 20–21); the same holds with the first-order SQP of [1], which has no second-order guarantee (Figure 3, pp. 20, 22). (ii) PINN for a damped harmonic oscillator: MLP with two 128-node tanh layers, Nf = Nc = 512, constraints = two initial conditions + averaged ODE residual = 0 (pp. 21–22); a 10 × 10 grid of (p_{f,1}, p_{c,1}) values; every row and column minimum has a sampled start below 512; best (16, 32) uses about 35 % of the data accesses of (512, 512) (Table 1, p. 22). (iii) Rival: a "geometric increase" scheme mimicking adaptive-sampling methods (fixed kmax inner iterations, then grow the sample and the merit parameter) on a 3 × 3 × 3 grid; all 27 settings need more accesses than the (16, 32) progressive run (Table 2, p. 23). Repeats per grid cell and the sample-selection rule are not reported (pp. 22–23), and the rival was run at the (16, 32) start chosen from Table 1 (p. 23). Table 1 is noisy (e.g. row p_{f,1} = 1 ranges from 1.88e7 to 1.95e8, p. 22).
  - Transferable: when the theorem needs an unknown initial sample size, report the full grid of initial sample sizes against the one-shot corner of the same table and mark the row and column minima, so the reader sees how wide the good region is (Table 1, p. 22; motivation p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: presented as the constrained counterpart of [10]; leans on recent second-order complexity work for equality constraints ([6] Goyens–Eftekhari–Boumal, [3] Dimou–O'Neill 2026) and on classical perturbation theory (Stewart 1973, 1977, 1979) (pp. 3, 9–13); concentration results ([2], [8], [15]) are invoked only to justify focusing on the SAA (p. 4). The adaptive-sampling literature is engaged only through an in-house imitation, with no specific paper cited at that point (p. 23). Points out and corrects a typo in [13, Theorem 5.2] (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing**: opens "We propose, analyze, and test" (p. 2) and closes with the same triad (p. 23). Assumptions are each followed by a paragraph saying how mild they are; two "formal remarks" are flagged as important (p. 7); Remark 3.3 argues pre-emptively that the |S| ∝ N requirement "should not be seen as a limitation" (p. 10). The theorem text says plainly that a valid p1 "is not necessarily known in practice" and points to the experiments (p. 17). The conclusion is one paragraph with no future directions (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic framework + worst-case sample-complexity theory + small experiments. Limits, stated: the sample size needed for the acute-perturbation and Morse inheritance results is a fixed fraction of N (pp. 10–11); a valid p1 is unknown in practice (p. 17); deterministic rather than high-probability assumptions (pp. 7–8); infeasible stationary points ruled out by assumption (p. 6); appendix covers constraint sampling only (p. 24). No future work section (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): subproblem tolerances are derived from what the outer scheme needs, namely ϵ_k = τ1 ξ_{S_k} so the warm start lands in the next subproblem's strongly Morse region (Lemma A.1, pp. 24–26; practical rule (43), p. 20); the outer object is a sample-size schedule, not a merit function or trust region.
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): the one-shot baseline is the same Algorithm 1 with p1 = N and the same solver, and the adaptive-sampling rival is built inside the same code with one component changed (pp. 19–23); but the rival is run only at the progressive method's best start (16, 32) chosen from the evaluation grid, and run counts per cell are not reported (pp. 22–23).
- Heuristic 1 (evidence ✅, not a numbered Method): a stronger distributional assumption is rejected partly because it "would generally not be verifiable in practice" (p. 11).
- new-pattern candidate: "inner-solver contract" — prove the outer bound against a stated complexity contract for any subproblem solver, then instantiate it (pp. 6, 17–19).
- new-pattern candidate: "check the assumption numerically on a 2-D instance" — Figure 1 measures the strong-Morse constants on the test problem before running the comparison (pp. 19–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Count cost in individual sample-gradient accesses so that progressive and one-shot runs are compared on the quantity the theorem bounds (pp. 18–22).
- Tie the inner stopping tolerance to the sampling error scale, adding a constant so the final tolerance does not vanish: ϵ_k = 10⁻⁶ sqrt(N(N−|S_k|)/|S_k|² + 1) (eq. (43), p. 20).
- Test the outer scheme with a second subproblem solver that does not satisfy the theory's contract (first-order SQP) to show the gain does not depend on the contract (pp. 20, 22).
- Bound sampled-versus-full multipliers through classical pseudo-inverse perturbation theory rather than probabilistic concentration (Lemma 3.4, pp. 11–13).

**Quotes**:
- "it was better to run the subproblem solver to a prescribed accuracy before increasing the sample size, rather than employ an adaptive-like approach that increases the sample sizes more gradually." (p. 23)
- "such an assumption would generally not be verifiable in practice." (p. 11)

### S093 · Active-Set Identification in Noisy and Stochastic Optimization (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv:2509.00888)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–27, including all proofs, numerics, conclusion and references). Version read: arXiv v1 (31 Aug 2025).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel P. Robinson, Lara Zebiane · **Position in trajectory**: tooling for the stochastic/noisy constrained line; the conclusion points to active-set SQP and multiplier estimation in noisy settings as the next step (p. 25), consistent with the "noise moving into constraints, multipliers and active sets" direction in SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: identifying the active inequalities at a local minimizer from a nearby point matters for active-set algorithms (Byrd–Gould–Nocedal–Waltz SLQP [9, 10]) and for multiplier estimation (p. 2). Classical guarantees (Burke–Moré, Wright, Facchinei–Fischer–Kanzow, Oberlin–Wright, Hare–Lewis, Lewis) assume exact values plus a constraint qualification (p. 2). The entry point is a gap: "little has been done" to extend identification to errors; the one exception, Na–Anitescu–Kolar [27], ties estimation to one algorithm and allows no constraint noise (p. 3). The paper aims at algorithm-independent identification with noise in objective and constraint values and derivatives, deterministic (simulation) or stochastic (sampling) (p. 3).
  - Transferable: frame a paper as "classical guarantee X under exact data → same guarantee under bounded errors", name the single prior exception and the two ways it is narrower (algorithm-specific, no constraint noise) (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: two identification procedures evaluated at a point x with noisy data. (1) LP-LPEC from Oberlin–Wright [29]: estimate multipliers by an LP surrogate (11)/(31) of the KKT-residual minimization, then threshold c̃_i(x) ≥ −(βρ̃̄)^σ (pp. 6–7, 14). (2) A new QP variant replacing Oberlin–Wright's LP with trust region by an ℓ1-penalty QP with proximal term (1/2)θ‖d‖² (13)/(14), always feasible, with active-set estimate {i : c_i + ∇c_i^T d ≥ 0} (pp. 7–8). Reason for the QP: it is the kind of subproblem that recent noisy-setting algorithms already solve for search directions (pp. 5–6). The noisy extensions keep the subproblems and thresholds and change only the data (pp. 10, 14–15).
  - Transferable: when extending an identification or certification test, prefer a variant built on a subproblem the host algorithm already solves, so identification adds no extra solve (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Assumptions**: noise model is a uniform deterministic bound on the errors of f, e, c and their gradients (23), so one analysis covers deterministic and stochastic noise (p. 10). LP-LPEC route: MFCQ + second-order condition (4) at x* (Theorems 3.2, 4.1, pp. 7, 14); the admissible noise level ϵ̃_x depends on the point x and must shrink with ρ(x, y, z) as x → x*, which in practice may require noise reduction (pp. 14, 18). QP route: LICQ + strict complementarity at a strict local minimizer, but the noise tolerance is uniform in a neighbourhood, so no noise reduction is needed (Theorem 4.2, p. 17; p. 18). Stochastic-algorithm results assume X_k → x* almost surely and a per-iteration conditional probability ≥ p that the noise is small (Theorems 4.3–4.4, pp. 18–19).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (a) QP route, exact case: contradiction over the finitely many candidate index sets, closedness of the finitely generated cone range[∇e] + pos[∇c_i] (Rockafellar Thm 19.1), and a lower bound on the dual QP objective at (y*, z*) against an upper bound from the separation distance τ, with θ ≤ ‖x − x*‖^−ζ (Theorem 3.3, pp. 8–10); (b) sign-pattern bookkeeping for noisy constraint values (sets PP, PN, NP, NN) plus a mean-value bound on the square root to show |ρ̄ − ρ̃̄| ≤ ϵ_ρ̄ → 0 (Lemma 4.2, pp. 11–14); (c) for the QP route, the noisy QP solution is the solution of the KKT linear system (36) on A(x*), and the classical linear-system perturbation bound (Lemma 2.1) shows d stays near 0 and multipliers stay positive (Lemma 4.4, pp. 16–17); (d) Han–Mangasarian exact-penalty theory makes the slacks zero for ν large (Theorem 4.2, p. 17); (e) stochastic corollaries in two lines each: a.s. convergence puts X_k in the identification ball, after which the per-iteration probability carries over (Theorems 4.3–4.4, pp. 18–19).
  - Transferable: to extend a local identification theorem to noisy data, write the identification test as the solution of a nonsingular linear system on the optimal active set and apply the standard perturbation bound for Kv = b (Lemma 2.1 → Lemma 4.4, pp. 5, 16–17).
- **D5 Experiments**: (i) two strongly convex 2-D problems between two parabolas, one with one active constraint, one with both (p. 19); a grid of points around x*; parameters M = 10⁸, β = 0.7071 (a scaling rule in a footnote, offered as a conjecture), σ = 0.7, ν = 100, θ = 5 (p. 19); noise uniform on [−ϵ, ϵ] for ϵ ∈ {0, 10⁻², 10⁻¹}, 8 noisy instances per grid point; heatmaps of the fraction of exact identifications (pp. 19–22). Findings: robust at 10⁻², less reliable at 10⁻¹; LP-LPEC is worse with one active constraint and better with two, with the caveat that this depends on parameter choices (p. 20). (ii) Constrained NN on the UCI heart-disease data (1025 points, 13 features): one hidden layer of 6 tanh nodes (91 variables), log loss, monotonicity-in-cholesterol constraints as a max over each of 10 groups, test set = top 20 % by cholesterol (pp. 20, 23). Unconstrained L-BFGS: infeasible (max violation 1.1e3), train 92.4 %, test 78.5 %; quadratic penalty (ρ = 10²) with L-BFGS: feasible, train 89.0 %, test 83.9 % (p. 23). Identification is tracked along the penalty iterates: without noise LP-LPEC is right early only because every iterate violates all constraints, which the authors call luck; QP identifies later; both are right in later iterations (Figure 3, p. 23). With minibatch (size 32) noise and 10 batchings per iterate, LP-LPEC degrades early and QP over-predicts activity, which happens to help (Figure 4, pp. 23–24). The host algorithm in (ii) is deterministic; noise enters only the identification subproblems (p. 23), so the stochastic-algorithm theorems of §4.3 are not exercised directly.
  - Transferable: test an identification procedure by recording, at every iterate of an ordinary solver run, how many elements of the final active set it gets right, once with exact data and once with several minibatch resamplings per iterate (pp. 23–24).
- **D6 Positioning**: "Following primarily [29]" (Oberlin–Wright) for both the LP-LPEC route and the template of the QP proof (pp. 3, 8); credits the deterministic-noise literature (Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal, Oztoprak–Byrd–Nocedal, Sun–Nocedal, Xie–Byrd–Nocedal, Dezfulian–Wächter, Curtis–Dezfulian–Wächter) and the stochastic-constrained literature, much of it his own (pp. 3, 25–27); the closest competitor [27] is described factually by its scope (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**: contribution stated as "showing that such guarantees can be extended" (p. 3); the organization paragraph separates the "one new contribution" in the exact setting from the "main contributions" in §4 (p. 4); after Theorem 3.3 a paragraph explains what each parameter threshold (θ, ν) does intuitively (p. 10). Numerical comparisons carry explicit hedges (pp. 20, 24), and the NN constraints are flagged as "for demonstration purposes only" (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory (local identification under bounded noise, two procedures, a stochastic-algorithm corollary) + illustrations. Limits stated: purely local results that need small noise, x-dependent for LP-LPEC (pp. 14, 18); comparisons parameter-dependent (pp. 20, 24). Future: noisy active-set algorithms that estimate the set then compute a direction on it, and extending the equality-only multiplier guarantees of [12] to inequalities (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the identification test is designed to reuse the QP that the outer noisy-setting algorithm already solves for its search direction (pp. 5–6); here the inner subproblem serves a second purpose rather than having its accuracy set by the globalization.
- Method 4 (evidence ✅, weak): the authors check why a count looks good, attributing LP-LPEC's early success to all iterates being infeasible rather than to the method (p. 23), and explain QP's better noisy count by over-prediction (p. 24).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅): reports where each procedure wins and narrows the claim to "these comparisons do not necessarily hold in general" (pp. 20, 24).
- Heuristic 10 (evidence ✅, not a numbered Method): one skeleton climbs exact (§3) → bounded deterministic/stochastic noise (§4.1–4.2) → inside a stochastic algorithm (§4.3) (pp. 5–19).
- new-pattern candidate: "perturbation bridge" — carry an exact-data theorem to noisy or sampled data by a classical perturbation bound (linear systems here; pseudo-inverses in S092) (pp. 5, 16–17).
- new-pattern candidate: "check the assumption or guarantee numerically on a 2-D instance" — heatmaps over a grid around x* (pp. 19–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define one noise model as uniform error bounds on every function and derivative value (23) so that one proof serves deterministic and stochastic noise (p. 10).
- Contrast two procedures by the shape of their noise requirement (point-dependent with noise reduction vs uniform in a neighbourhood) rather than only by empirical accuracy (pp. 14, 17–18).
- Turn a domain prior into group-wise constraints (one max-constraint per data group rather than per point) to keep a constrained-learning test problem tractable, and report what the constraints did to test accuracy (pp. 20, 23).

**Quotes**:
- "this demonstrates a situation in which the LP-LPEC approach is lucky always to identify violated constraints as being active at the optimal solution." (p. 23)
- "Naturally, the behavior of the methods can vary with these parameter choices, so these comparisons do not necessarily hold in general." (p. 20)

### S063 · A single-loop stochastic feasible interior-point algorithm for nonlinear inequality-constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming 2026, DOI 10.1007/s10107-025-02320-2)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–35: introduction, algorithm, deterministic and stochastic analyses with proofs, §5 discussion of assumptions, numerics, conclusion); Appendix A (Phase I algorithm, pp. 35–37) skimmed; references (pp. 37–38) checked for [8]. Version read: published open-access journal version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Xin Jiang, Qi Wang · **Position in trajectory**: extends the bound-constrained stochastic-gradient IPM of Curtis–Kungurtsev–Robinson–Wang [8] (SIAM J. Optim. 2025) to nonlinear inequality and linear equality constraints (p. 3); the "noisy IPM from 2022" entry of the SKILL.md trajectory, now with stochastic gradients and no function values.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: min f(x) s.t. Ax = b, c(x) ≤ 0, where constraint values and derivatives are exact but the objective is accessible only through stochastic-gradient estimates, with no objective values (pp. 1–2). Entry point: IPMs dominate deterministic nonconvex practice (Ipopt, Knitro, LOQO), yet "little work" extends them to the stochastic regime (p. 2). Three named obstacles: the nested loop needs an inner stationarity test that cannot be evaluated; barrier gradients are neither Lipschitz nor bounded; line-search or trust-region globalization needs function values (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open by listing the exact features of the deterministic method that the new information model makes impossible, one per paragraph-sentence, and design the method to remove each (p. 2 → §3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: a single-loop feasible IPM (SLIP, Algorithm 1, p. 9): prescribed sequences μ_k ↓ 0 and θ_k ↓ 0 with μ_k/θ_{k−1} constant (pp. 6–7); iterates kept in shrinking neighbourhoods N(θ) = {c(x) ≤ −θ1} (p. 6); one (stochastic) barrier-gradient step per iteration; the direction must satisfy (6a)–(6e), where (6e) forces descent away from nearly active constraints A_k = {i : −η̄μ_k < c_i} (p. 7); prescribed step sizes α_k from local Lipschitz estimates, and a factor γ_k chosen as the positive root of a quadratic so the segment stays in N(θ_k) (pp. 8, 12). The nested loop, the inner stationarity test and the line search are removed; barrier function, feasibility of iterates and projected-gradient descent are kept. In the stochastic case the direction is D_k = −Z(Z^T H_k Z)^{−1} Z^T Q_k (pp. 18–19).
  - Transferable: replace a nested barrier loop by prescribed, coupled decay of the barrier and neighbourhood parameters (μ_k/θ_{k−1} constant) plus a step-length cap that keeps the iterate in the current neighbourhood (pp. 6–9).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Assumption 1: f, c C¹, f bounded below, A full row rank, a strictly feasible point exists, so nonlinear equalities are excluded (p. 5). Assumption 3: Lipschitz and boundedness constants on a set containing the iterate segments (p. 9). Assumption 2 (nonstandard, p. 8): a direction satisfying (6) exists at every iterate, explained as nondegeneracy plus μ1/θ0 large enough (pp. 3, 8), and examined in §5: holds for a polyhedral 2-D example when μ/θ is large (Example 1, pp. 26–28), cannot be shown when constraint gradients become opposite (Example 2 with c2 = x1 − x2², p. 29; Fig. 1). Assumption 4: unbiased gradients with an almost-sure bound σ on the projected error (p. 18); the authors know of no stochastic IPM analysis that avoids a bounded-error assumption (p. 18). Assumption 5: uniformly bounded H_k on Null(A) (p. 18). The deterministic version is not expected to be competitive and is analysed as a precursor (pp. 3, 11).
- **D4 Proof devices**: (a) a shifted barrier φ̃ = f − μ Σ log(−c_i/κ_{ci}) that has the same gradient as the barrier, is bounded below by f_inf and is increasing in μ, so decreasing μ never increases it (pp. 9–10); (b) a local Lipschitz constant for the barrier gradient on N(θ), L = L_∇f + μ/(θθ̄)(...) (Lemma 2, p. 10); (c) γ_k as the positive root of the quadratic upper model of c_i along the step, with a uniform lower bound from a two-case argument, not nearly active vs nearly active using (6e) (Lemmas 3–4, pp. 12–14); (d) sufficient decrease ∝ γ_kα_k‖P∇φ‖² (Lemma 5, p. 15) and non-summability of γ_kα_k under polynomial schedules with t_μ = t_θ, giving liminf ‖P∇φ‖ = 0 and KKT limits under LICQ via z_k = −μ_k/c(x_k) (Theorem 1, pp. 15–17); (e) stochastic: γ_k is confined to a prescribed, run-independent band [γ_{k,min}, γ_{k,max}] with a buffer of order k^t, so the correlation between the random step factor and the noise is handled by splitting the expectation on the sign of the noise inner product (Law of Total Expectation, Lemma 9, pp. 22–23); exponent conditions t + t_α ∈ [−1, 0), 2t + t_α < −1, t + 2t_α < −1 make the noise terms summable (Parameter Rule 2, p. 19; Lemma 11, pp. 23–24); Fatou's lemma gives liminf → 0 almost surely (Theorem 2, pp. 24–25).
  - Transferable: when a random step-scaling factor is correlated with gradient noise, confine it to a deterministic band and bound the cross term by splitting on the sign of the noise inner product; the band width then appears as a summable error (Parameter Rule 2 and Lemma 9, pp. 19–23).
- **D5 Experiments**: no level-playing-field competitor exists, so there is no external baseline (p. 30). (i) Deterministic SLIP in Python on CUTEst via PyCUTEst: 1315 problems → 396 after removing problems with nonlinear equalities, without inequalities, or too large to load; a separate Phase I (Appendix A) then fails on 59 and hits evaluation errors on 10, leaving 327 (pp. 30–31).   - Transferable: when no competitor exists, use the method's own deterministic twin as the reference, report every seed of the stochastic version in a table, and keep the test problems' feasible regions convex so differences across seeds reflect noise, not different local minimizers (pp. 32–34).
- **D6 Positioning**: direct extension of his group's bound-constrained method [8] (p. 3); distinguishes itself from non-Lipschitz stochastic-gradient work ([25], [7], [24]) by noting that the barrier function changes every iteration (pp. 2–3); claims no other IPM has comparable stochastic guarantees (p. 3); points to stochastic infeasible Newton/SQP methods for equality constraints (many his own) as a possible route to an infeasible version (pp. 34–35).
- **D7 Writing**: limitations stated before results: "Let us emphasize upfront" that the method will not compete with state-of-the-art deterministic IPMs (p. 3); the nonstandard assumption is announced in the contributions paragraph and given a whole section (pp. 3, 25–30). Parameter rules are followed by a paragraph explaining each design choice (pp. 12, 20) and a concrete admissible setting (t = −0.99, t_α = −0.01, p. 19). The conclusion explains in detail why the slack-variable infeasible variant is hard (pp. 34–35).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory (deterministic and almost-sure stochastic convergence of a single-loop IPM) + Python implementation + demonstrations. Limits, stated: feasible method only, a strictly feasible start needed (pp. 3, 5); no nonlinear equalities (p. 3); a nonstandard Assumption 2 whose parameters are not computable in advance, so tuning or resets are needed (pp. 26, 28, 34); conservative prescribed step sizes (p. 8); bounded-error noise (p. 18); not competitive deterministically (p. 30). Future: practical directions meeting Assumptions 2/5, and a single-loop infeasible IPM, with the slack-variable obstacle spelled out (pp. 34–35).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (variant ⚠): one loop with no inner barrier-subproblem test, but the barrier parameter follows a prescribed schedule rather than being steered by measured progress (pp. 6–9); in the implementation μ1 is reset (doubled) when the direction test (6) fails, an adaptive correction outside the theory (pp. 30–31); feasibility is obtained by a separate Phase I algorithm, a two-phase structure forced by the requirement of a strictly feasible start (pp. 3, 5, 30–31, 35).
- Method 4 (evidence ✅, weak): without f_inf, success is judged by a relative stationarity measure whose denominator takes the more favourable initial value, i.e., the measure that is harder for the method (p. 32).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠): no external baseline because none is comparable (p. 30); the deterministic twin serves as the reference for the stochastic runs and all 10 seeds are printed (pp. 32–34), but parameters such as t = 0.7 and the μ1 reset factor are set without a reported tuning procedure (p. 31).
- Heuristic 7 (evidence ✅, not a numbered Method): test-set filters are set before the solver runs and each removal is counted with its reason (1315 → 396 → 327; 59 Phase I failures, 10 evaluation errors) (pp. 30–31).
- Heuristic 10 (evidence ✅, not a numbered Method): deterministic Algorithm 1(D) is analysed first "as a useful precursor", then the stochastic version reuses Lemmas 1–4 with one bound changed (pp. 11, 20).
