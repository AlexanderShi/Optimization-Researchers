### S038 · A reduced-space algorithm for minimizing ℓ1-regularized convex functions (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2016, 10.1137/16M1062259; arXiv:1602.07018)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v1, pp. 1–23: all text, Appendix A proof of Lemma A.1, Tables 4.1–4.3 and reference list; Figs. 4.1–4.2 (pp. 22–23) exist only as captions in the text layer, so the plots were not read).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Tianyi Chen (JHU), Daniel P. Robinson (JHU) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Curtis's DOE Early Career and NSF DMS-1016291 period (p. 1); the method is named FaRSA (p. 2) and moves his "inner solve serves the outer solver" and monitored-transition ideas into sparse convex optimization for model prediction.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a comparison of two incumbents that each win on a different problem class: LIBLINEAR (newGLMNET, coordinate descent on a piecewise-quadratic proximal-Newton subproblem) and the orthant-based active-set method OBA; OBA "was shown to be slower than LIBLINEAR when the Hessian matrices were diagonally dominant, but faster otherwise" (p. 2). The paper treats them as complementary and sets out to "capitalize on the advantages of both while avoiding their disadvantages" (p. 2). Problem: min F(x) = f(x) + λ‖x‖1 with f convex and C², motivated by l1-regularized logistic regression, LASSO and elastic net (p. 1). A further entry point is OBA's need for an ISTA step every iteration to ensure convergence (contribution (ii), p. 2).
  - Transferable: when two state-of-the-art codes each win on a different, identifiable problem class (here diagonal dominance of the Hessian), make the design goal a framework that can host either code's subproblem solver.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Split first-order optimality into two measures, β(x) for the zero variables and φ(x) for the nonzero ones (p. 3; Lemma 2.1, pp. 3–4), and choose the iteration type by comparing them: if ‖β(x_k)‖ ≤ γ‖φ(x_k)‖, minimize a quadratic model over a chosen subset of the current nonzero variables (reduced-space subproblem); otherwise free some zero variables with a reduced ISTA-like step along −β (Algorithm 1, p. 5; pp. 4–7). The subproblem need not be solved exactly: any d̄_k with g_kᵀd̄_k ≤ g_kᵀd_k^R (d_k^R the Cauchy-like steepest-descent minimizer of the model) and m_k(d̄_k) ≤ m_k(0) is accepted, which holds for a Newton step, any Newton-CG iterate and asymptotically for coordinate descent (line 10, p. 5; p. 6). A projected backtracking line search (Algorithm 2, p. 5) accepts mere decrease when a variable hits zero (support shrinks) and first tests the largest step that stays in the orthant before a standard Armijo backtrack (p. 6). What changed relative to OBA: no ISTA step every iteration; support updates driven by the β/φ test; inexact subproblem conditions (p. 2). The unit β-step equals a reduced ISTA step (Lemma A.1: the full ISTA step is −(β + φ), pp. 19–21). The paper says the work started from a bound-constrained QP reformulation solved by Dostál-type proportioning methods and then deviated from it (p. 3).
  - Transferable: define separate optimality measures for the two parts of the variable space (here zero and nonzero variables) and let their ratio decide which kind of step to take; the sum of the measures can then be tied to a known step (here ISTA) by a lemma.
  - Transferable: state subproblem accuracy as two inequalities (descent at least that of a Cauchy-like reference direction, model value no worse than zero) so that Newton, truncated CG and coordinate descent all qualify without separate analysis.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 3.1 (p. 7): f convex, C², bounded below on the level set, Lipschitz gradient, and Hessian uniformly positive definite and bounded (θ_min, θ_max). The uniform positive definiteness is used in Lemma 3.7 (p. 10) and in Theorem 3.11 (unique solution, pp. 12–13); the paper does not discuss when it fails for logistic regression; the implementation adds a 10⁻⁸ diagonal to H_k, "an approach also adopted by OBA and LIBLINEAR" (p. 14). Termination tolerance ε = 10⁻⁶, 1000 iterations, 10 minutes (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Lemma 2.1: limit points are solutions iff β and φ vanish along the subsequence, by a component-wise case analysis (pp. 3–4). (ii) Partition of iterations into S_β, S_φ^ADD (a nonzero variable becomes zero) and S_φ^SD (sufficient decrease within the orthant) (p. 7), and finite termination by showing each set finite (Theorem 3.10, p. 12). (iii) S_β finite by a sufficient-decrease bound F(x_{k+1}) ≤ F(x_k) − κ_β max{‖β‖², γ²‖φ‖²} summed against boundedness below (Lemmas 3.3–3.5, pp. 8–9). (iv) S_φ^ADD finite by a counting argument: after S_β ends, a variable that becomes zero stays zero, and "at most n variables may become zero" (Lemma 3.6, p. 9). (v) The size bound ‖d̄_k‖ ≤ (2/θ_min)‖g_k‖ for any d̄_k with m_k(d̄_k) ≤ m_k(0), proved by maximizing the norm over the model's sublevel set (Lemma 3.7, p. 10). (vi) Two-case step-size lower bound for the projected line search (full step in orthant vs. α_B test), giving decrease proportional to ‖φ‖² (Lemma 3.8, pp. 10–12). (vii) Convergence of the whole sequence without the termination test via level-set argument and monotonic F (Theorem 3.11, pp. 12–13). No complexity bound or local rate.
  - Transferable: to prove finite termination of an active-set method, split the iterations by what happened to the support and kill each set separately (decrease summation for two, a counting argument for the one that only removes variables).
  - Transferable: bound an inexact subproblem step by the largest-norm point of the model's sublevel set {d : m(d) ≤ m(0)} (Lemma 3.7, p. 10); any solver that does not increase the model then inherits the bound.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Preliminary Matlab FaRSA "that we are happy to provide upon request" (p. 14), CG as the only subproblem solver, with three termination rules: relative residual 10⁻¹, too many CG components in the "wrong" orthant, and a step-norm cap that works as "an implicit trust-region constraint" (pp. 15–16). The β-direction is rescaled by a safeguarded length δ_{k,β}, "fully covered by the theory" (p. 16). Test set: 31 binary-classification datasets (mostly LIBSVM, plus OBA's synthetic set and two Alzheimer's datasets), with exclusions named and reasoned (regression and multiclass removed except mnist made binary; HIGGS, kdd2010, epsilon, url, webspam dropped for memory; only a9a and w8a from their families) (pp. 13–14; Table 4.1, p. 15). For 12 datasets both unscaled and scaled versions are run (p. 13), giving a harder non-diagonally-dominant test in Table 4.3 (p. 18). Rival: OBA only, the authors' own Matlab code, same tolerance, "made no modifications to their code" (p. 16); times averaged over 10 runs (p. 16); final objective values not reported because identical (p. 16). Results: FaRSA faster on 26 of 31 scaled problems, OBA faster on cod-rna, SUSY, synthetic, gene-ad, colon-cancer (p. 16; Table 4.2, p. 17); unscaled: FaRSA solves 9 of 12, OBA fails by "ascent" on three because of its fixed Lipschitz estimate, which the authors chose not to fix (p. 17; Table 4.3, p. 18). Instrumentation: average 34.12 iterations of which 29.93 in S_φ (p. 16). Run time vs. tolerance plots (Figs. 4.1–4.2, pp. 18, 22–23). LIBLINEAR is not run; its comparison is inferred through OBA's published results (p. 18).
  - Transferable: report a run-time-versus-requested-accuracy plot per problem (Figs. 4.1–4.2, p. 18) so that a reader who wants a low-accuracy solution can see whether the ranking holds there too.
  - Transferable: keep a scaled and an unscaled version of each dataset that has one (p. 13), and report them in separate tables; scaling is a hidden property that changes which method wins.
- **D6 Positioning**: Places FaRSA between first-order methods (ISTA, FISTA, SpaRSA), proximal-Newton (LIBLINEAR/newGLMNET and others) and orthant-based methods (OBA and others) (p. 2); treats LIBLINEAR and OBA as "valuable state-of-the-art algorithms that complement each other" (p. 2) rather than as targets. Credits a bound-constrained-QP reformulation route (SpaRSA-style recasting plus Dostál) as the origin of the work (p. 3). Acknowledges the OBA authors (Keskar, Nocedal, Oztoprak, Wächter) for their code and discussions (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Contributions as a numbered list (i)–(iv) in §1.1, ending in "the practical performance of our method is state-of-the-art" (p. 3). Every algorithmic line is explained in prose immediately after the algorithm box (pp. 4–7). Results text names the problems where the rival wins (p. 16) and the one unscaled problem where OBA is better (p. 17). The conclusion states a forward expectation without data: FaRSA "will be competitive with LIBLINEAR on all classes of problems" once coordinate descent and subproblem-size control are added (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: algorithm + global convergence theory + preliminary implementation. Limits stated by the authors: the implementation is preliminary; OBA and FaRSA "will often be outperformed by LIBLINEAR when the second derivative matrices are diagonally dominant" (p. 18); |I_k| control and adaptive subset choices not implemented (pp. 14, 16). Limits visible but not stated: strong convexity (uniformly PD Hessian) assumed (p. 7); only one rival actually run; no local rate or complexity. Future: CD subproblem solver, subproblem-size control, a released solver (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ✅ evidence: the conditions "that determine how accurately each subproblem must be solved" are an explicit design feature (abstract, p. 1; line 10, pp. 5–6), derived from what the line-search analysis needs (Lemmas 3.7–3.8, pp. 10–12); the CG stopping rules add tests tied to the outer line search (wrong-orthant count, implicit trust region, pp. 15–16).
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ⚠ variant: one algorithm moves between shrinking and growing the support by a monitored test ‖β‖ ≤ γ‖φ‖ (line 6, p. 5), not by a separate phase; the trade-off here is between optimality measures on two variable sets, not between objective and feasibility, and γ is fixed (γ = 1, p. 14).
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ⚠ variant: the rival is run unmodified with the same tolerance, averaged over 10 runs, and its wins are named (pp. 16–17); but the strongest incumbent (LIBLINEAR) is not run, and the rival's fixable "ascent" failures are counted against it rather than adopting a fix (p. 17), the opposite of Step 4.
- Method 3 (Break it on purpose) ⚠ variant: harder instances are produced by withholding the standard preprocessing (unscaled datasets, p. 13; Table 4.3, p. 18) rather than by a constructed infeasible or degenerate variant.
- new-pattern candidate: split-measure iteration choice — separate optimality measures per variable block decide the step type, and their sum is tied to a classical step (Lemma 2.1, p. 3; Lemma A.1, pp. 19–21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Two-inequality inexactness test (Cauchy-like descent plus model no worse than zero) that admits Newton, truncated CG and coordinate descent (pp. 5–6).
- Finite-termination proof by partitioning iterations according to what happens to the support, with a counting argument for the support-shrinking set (Lemmas 3.5–3.9, pp. 9–12).
- CG termination on "too many components in the wrong orthant" and on a step-norm cap acting as an implicit trust region (pp. 15–16).
- Scaled and unscaled versions of the same datasets reported in separate tables (pp. 13, 17–18).

**Quotes**:
- "This indicates that FaRSA quickly identifies the orthant that contains the optimal solution." (p. 16)
- "Although simple adaptive strategies could be used to avoid such issues, we made no such attempts because we did not want to make any edits to their code." (p. 17)

### S040 · Adaptive augmented Lagrangian methods: Algorithms and practical numerical experience (Optimization Methods and Software 2016, 10.1080/10556788.2015.1071813; arXiv:1408.4500)
**Read level**: full for the main text (arXiv v1 "Detailed Version", pp. 1–21 including references); Appendix A (well-posedness, pp. 22–28) read in part (Lemmas A.1–A.3, A.5, A.6 and the proof of Theorem 2.2); Appendix B (global convergence, pp. 28–34) skimmed for structure (Lemmas B.1–B.6, case split); Appendix C (per-problem tables C1–C7, pp. 34–46) skimmed (headers and first rows only).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Nicholas I. M. Gould (STFC-RAL), Hao Jiang (JHU), Daniel P. Robinson (JHU) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: companion to the adaptive AL trust-region method of Curtis, Jiang and Robinson (Math. Program., 10.1007/s10107-014-0784-y), cited as [15] (p. 1); it carries the exact-penalty steering rules of Byrd, Nocedal and Waltz [8] into AL methods and into the production code Lancelot (Galahad) (pp. 1–2, 17–18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a failure mode of traditional AL methods in early iterations: poor penalty parameter or multiplier choices give "little or no improvement in the primal space" and the iterates "may diverge from even a well-chosen initial iterate" (pp. 1–2). Motivation for AL itself: matrix-free implementation and guarantees under weak assumptions for extreme-scale problems (p. 1). Problem form: min f(x) s.t. c(x) = 0, l ≤ x ≤ u, with a built-in transition to the feasibility problem min ½‖c(x)‖² over the bounds when the instance is (locally) infeasible (eqs. (1)–(2), p. 2). The paper has two stated contributions: a line-search version of [15] and extensive numerical experience including Lancelot (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a method class has a known early-iteration pathology caused by a parameter guess, enter by making the parameter update part of the step computation rather than of the outer loop.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Each iteration computes a steering step r_k that approximately minimizes the Gauss-Newton model of constraint violation in a trust region of radius θ_k = δ‖F_FEAS(x_k)‖ (eqs. (8)–(9), p. 5), then a trial step s_k on a model of the AL; the penalty parameter μ is reduced inside the iteration until s_k predicts a reduction in linearized violation at least a fraction of the steering step's, or a bounded increase tied to the target t_j (conditions (16a)–(16c), p. 7; Algorithm 3 lines 19–23, p. 8). What changed relative to [15]: line search instead of trust region, and a convexified piecewise-quadratic AL model q̃ (with max{·, 0} on the curvature term) so that every direction is a strict descent direction for the line search (pp. 4–5; Lemma A.6, p. 27). What did not change: the multiplier-update logic with targets t_j, T_j and a multiplier bound Y_j (Algorithm 3, p. 8; eqs. (18)–(20), pp. 7–9); the penalty is "not adjusted on the basis of a test like that on line 26, but instead relies on our steering procedure" (p. 9). Cauchy steps with an adaptive tolerance ε_k (Algorithms 1–2, pp. 6–7) make the steering test computable.
  - Transferable: when a trust-region framework is ported to a line search, convexify the model's curvature term so that the model reduction certifies a descent direction (q̃, p. 5; Lemma A.6, p. 27).
  - Transferable: size the steering subproblem's trust region proportional to the current stationarity measure of the feasibility problem (θ_k = δ‖F_FEAS‖, p. 5), so that the steering step shrinks near stationary points of the violation.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 2.1: f and c twice continuously differentiable at each x_k (well-posedness, p. 9). Assumption 2.3: iterates and trial points lie in a convex compact set on which f and c are C² (global convergence, p. 9). No constraint qualification; the authors state that the theory cannot show the penalty parameter stays bounded "under a suitable constraint qualification" (pp. 20–21). Theorem 2.4 cites "Assumptions 2.2 and 2.3" (p. 9) although only 2.1 and 2.3 are defined in the text read (likely a typo).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Well-posedness via limits as μ → 0: the AL model tends to the violation model uniformly on a ball, the AL gradient tends to Jᵀc, and the AL Cauchy step tends to the steering Cauchy step, so the steering loop terminates (Lemma A.3, (A1a)–(A1d), pp. 23–24; Lemma A.5, p. 27). (ii) Strict descent from the convexified model: ∇L ᵀs_k ≤ −Δq̃(s_k) (Lemma A.6, p. 27), "the primary motivation for our use of the convexified model" (p. 27). (iii) Global convergence by a four-way case split on whether the multiplier is updated finitely or infinitely often and whether μ_k → 0 (Appendix B, Lemmas B.3–B.6, pp. 30–34), giving three outcomes: infeasible stationary limit points; stationary limit points with μ bounded away from zero; or μ → 0 with feasible limits and stationarity under an extra condition (Theorem 2.4, pp. 9–10). (iv) Reuse: many lemmas are cited from [15] with the changes argued ("we claim that this difference does not affect the veracity of the result", p. 23); Lemma B.1 relies on Dirichlet's test (p. 28). The conclusions are "exactly the same as in [15, Theorem 3.14]" (p. 10). No complexity or local rate.
  - Transferable: prove that an inner parameter-reduction loop terminates by showing the parameter-dependent quantities converge, as the parameter goes to its limit, to the quantities the test compares against (Lemma A.3, pp. 23–24).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Two codes. (a) Matlab, six variants in one package differing in step acceptance and in multiplier/penalty updates: BAL-LS, BAL-TR (basic AL), AAL-LS, AAL-TR (steering), and AAL-LS-safe, AAL-TR-safe (steering switched off once μ ≤ 10⁻⁴) (pp. 10, 13). The subproblem is solved by a projected CG active-set routine, and the implementation's departures from the stated algorithm are listed with a Cauchy-decrease "sanity check" that restores the theoretical requirement (p. 11) and a list of parameter and update changes ("we claim that none of these differences represents a significant departure", p. 12). Infeasibility is declared only after μ ≤ μ_min "to avoid premature termination" (p. 12). Test sets: CUTEst with ≤ 1000 variables and constraints, 383 problems, 323 kept where at least one solver succeeded (p. 13); COPS, 17 problems after excluding by name 5 that all solvers failed (p. 15); 28 OPF problems, seven IEEE systems in four formulations (pp. 16–17). Measures chosen per method type: iterations and function evaluations for line search, iterations and gradient evaluations for trust region, with reasons (pp. 13–14). Dolan–Moré profiles truncated at 16 plus failure counts (25/3/16 and 30/12/20, pp. 13–14); Morales outperforming factors on the small sets (p. 15). Final penalty values tabulated by decade to check that steering does not drive μ needlessly small (Table 2, p. 14; Table 3, p. 19). (b) Lancelot: only steering added, other mechanisms unchanged, "In this manner, we were also able to isolate the effect that steering had on numerical performance" (p. 10; pp. 17–18); three settings, CUTEst ≤ 10,000 variables and constraints, 457 problems, 364 solved by at least one (p. 19). Mixed outcomes are reported: on COPS the line-search advantage "is not overwhelming" (p. 15); OPF results suggest the opposite LS/TR ranking from COPS (p. 17); the safeguarded Lancelot gave smaller final μ than the unsafeguarded one, which the authors found "counterintuitive" and explain by the decrease factors 0.1 vs. 0.7 (p. 20).
  - Transferable: when a new rule may push a parameter to an extreme, tabulate the final parameter value by decade over the whole test set for every arm (Tables 2–3, pp. 14, 19) and compare the counts in the extreme bins (171 vs. 168 below 10⁻⁴, p. 20).
  - Transferable: test a proposed safeguard as its own arm (here steering turned off below a threshold) instead of adding it by default; here it lost (pp. 13–14, 19–20).
  - Transferable: in an experiment section, list each departure of the implementation from the analysed algorithm, and add a cheap runtime check (Cauchy-decrease comparison) that restores the condition the theory needs (pp. 11–12).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues the steering line for exact penalty methods (Byrd–Nocedal–Waltz [8]; Byrd–Lopez-Calva–Nocedal [7]; Mongeau–Sartenaer [29]) and its own predecessor [15] (pp. 1, 20). Traditional AL (Conn–Gould–Toint [11], Andreani et al. [2], Birgin–Martínez [3]) is the baseline, reimplemented as BAL and run as unmodified Lancelot (pp. 9–10). The conclusion suggests steering would also help other AL methods such as Birgin–Martínez's and PENNON (p. 21). Gould, a Lancelot author, is a co-author, and the modified code is to be released as Lancelot in Galahad 2.6 (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: "Detailed Version" with proofs and full tables moved to appendices, so the main text is algorithm, theorem statements and experiments (p. 2). The paper repeatedly says "we claim" for implementation choices (pp. 11, 12, 23). Theory-versus-practice is argued from data: modifications that would guarantee convergence in case (iii) are not considered to "have practical benefits" (p. 10), and the weaker guarantee "should not be a concern in practice" (pp. 20–21). Hedges are attached to specific results ("though the advantage is not overwhelming", p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: algorithm variant with theory on par with [15] plus an empirical study in research and production code. Limits stated: penalty boundedness not provable under a CQ (pp. 20–21); PCG subproblem solver not guaranteed to converge exactly (p. 11); target values decreased linearly "for simplicity" (p. 9). Limits visible: Matlab problems ≤ 1000 variables; problem filtering on success by at least one solver (pp. 13, 19); no CPU time comparison. Future: apply steering to other AL methods (p. 21); a decrease factor that grows as μ shrinks in Lancelot (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ✅ evidence: penalty updates driven by predicted progress in linearized feasibility inside the step computation (pp. 2, 4–5, 7–8), with the multiplier update decoupled from the penalty decision (p. 9), a built-in transition to infeasible stationary points (p. 2; termination (22), p. 12), and final-parameter tables (Tables 2–3, pp. 14, 19) as Step 5 prescribes; extends Method 2 from exact penalty to AL.
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✅ evidence: six arms in one Matlab package where "most of the algorithmic components were exactly the same" (p. 10), and the production incumbent modified only in steering to "isolate the effect" (pp. 10, 18); mixed results printed (pp. 15, 17, 20).
- Method 1 (The inner solve serves the outer solver) ✅ evidence: steering tests use only Cauchy steps, so μ can be fixed before the expensive direction solve (p. 11), and the PCG direction is replaced by the Cauchy step when it fails the Cauchy-decrease requirement of the analysis (p. 11).
- Method 4 (Audit the yardstick) ⚠ variant: the paper discounts its own weaker theoretical guarantee on empirical grounds (pp. 10, 20–21), weighing theory by practical effect, but the yardstick (profiles on success-filtered sets) is not questioned (p. 13).
- new-pattern candidate: safeguard-as-arm — the safeguard a cautious reader would demand is implemented as a separate variant and shown to cost performance (pp. 13–14, 19–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Final-parameter-by-decade tables for every arm, read at the extreme bins (Tables 2–3, pp. 14, 19–20).
- A safeguard ablation as a separate arm (AAL-*-safe, lancelot-steering-safe; pp. 13, 18).
- Insert the new rule alone into an existing production code to isolate its effect (Lancelot, pp. 17–18).
- Convexified model for line-search descent (p. 5; Lemma A.6, p. 27).
- Declare infeasibility only after the penalty parameter is below a threshold (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "In this manner, we were also able to isolate the effect that steering had on numerical performance" (p. 10)
- "Across the board, our results indicate that safeguarding was not necessary and would typically degrade performance when compared to the unrestricted steering approach." (p. 20)

### S042 · Handling nonpositive curvature in a limited memory steepest descent method (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2016, 10.1093/imanum/drv034)
**Read level**: full (published version, pp. 1–26: all text, Appendix A proofs of Theorems 3.1–3.3, Appendix B Tables B1–B3; Figs. 1–10 survive only as captions or axis residue in the text layer, so the bar plots and contour plots were not read beyond their captions and the prose describing them).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Wei Guo (Lehigh ISE, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: received 7 November 2014, revised 10 March 2015 (p. 1); funded by the DOE Early Career award and NSF DMS-1319356 (p. 21). Unconstrained first-order method design, outside his SQP line, but built around the same question: what should a method do when the information it relies on (here curvature) is unusable.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry is a gap in the Barzilai–Borwein (BB) and Fletcher limited-memory steepest descent (LMSD) literature: when the objective is nonconvex, "the sophisticated mechanisms designed to compute step sizes are abandoned, and instead the step sizes are chosen arbitrarily (e.g., as prescribed constants)" (p. 2). The literature review documents this case by case (Raydan 1997, Dai et al. 2002, Xiao et al. 2010, Biglari–Solimanpur 2013 revert to a large constant; other works treat only convex quadratics) and notes Fletcher "admits that his approach leaves unanswered the question of how to handle nonconvexity" and throws away the sweep (pp. 5, 7). A contour plot of the step size as a function of the current gradient (Fig. 1, pp. 5–6) shows the two BB rules carry no information when s_kᵀy_k < 0.
  - Transferable: before proposing a fix, map the incumbent rule's output over a two-dimensional slice of its inputs (here step size vs. g_k for fixed s_k, Fig. 1, p. 5) so the region where it discards information is visible.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Step sizes come from minimizing a one-dimensional cubic model f_k + g_kᵀs + ½q_k‖s‖² + (1/6)c_k‖s‖³ along −g_k (eqs. (3.1)–(3.3), p. 8). The quadratic coefficient is always the "harmonic" BB value q̂_k (p. 9), chosen because it gives conservative steps when s_kᵀy_k ≈ 0 of either sign (p. 9); q̂ − q̄ measures gradient change orthogonal to s_k (decomposition y_k = u_k + v_k, pp. 8–9). The cubic coefficient is zero when q̂_k > 0 and otherwise c_k = c(q̄_k − q_k)/‖s_k‖, a form derived from two independent criteria (least-squares fit of the previous gradient, and matching curvature at x_{k−1}) that agree up to a constant (eqs. (3.6)–(3.11), pp. 9–10), read as a finite-difference third derivative (p. 10). Special cases (y_k = 0, s_kᵀy_k = 0, parallel s_k, y_k) are each assigned Ω or ω with a stated reason (pp. 10–11), and all steps are projected onto [ω, Ω] (Algorithm 1, p. 11). For m > 1, Fletcher's Ritz and harmonic-Ritz values give q̄ and q̂ (eq. (3.14), p. 13), using symmetrized T̃_k and P̃_k (Theorem 3.2, p. 14); interlacing guarantees c ≥ 0 (Theorem 3.3, p. 14); if the history is degenerate the oldest gradients are dropped instead of discarding the sweep (Algorithm 2, p. 15). What did not change: with positive curvature the method "reduces to a variant of Fletcher's LMSD method" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a parameter could be fixed by two plausible criteria, derive both; if they agree up to a constant, adopt the one-parameter family (here c(q̄ − q)/‖s‖, p. 10).
  - Transferable: design the fix so it is inactive when the incumbent's assumption holds, so the method reduces to the incumbent there and inherits its behaviour (p. 2; p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f continuously differentiable (p. 3). The global convergence result needs f bounded below and ∇f Lipschitz on an enlarged level set (Theorem 3.4, p. 16), and applies only to the variant with the Zhang–Hager nonmonotone line search; the basic algorithm "does not enforce sufficient decrease" (p. 2). Fletcher's matrices are motivated by the strictly convex quadratic case (pp. 6–7) and symmetrized heuristically for general f (pp. 13–14). Nonpositive eigenvalues may arise "merely due to nonquadratic features" and are still called nonpositive curvature "for ease of exposition" (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Theorem 3.1: T_k is well defined iff G_k has independent columns, and invertible iff additionally the entries of R_k⁻¹Q_kᵀg_k do not sum to 1, via a determinant–trace identity (pp. 14, 22–23). (ii) Theorem 3.2: Hessenberg times upper triangular stays Hessenberg, so symmetrizing gives tridiagonal T̃ and pentadiagonal P̃ (pp. 14, 23). (iii) Theorem 3.3: |q̄_{k+j}| ≤ |q̂_{k+j}| by writing (P⁻¹T)⁻¹ as a diagonal plus rank-one structure in the eigenbasis of T and counting positive/negative eigenvalues of shifted matrices (interlacing) (pp. 14, 23–24). (iv) Global convergence is "a special case of Zhang & Hager (2004, Theorem 2.2)" (Theorem 3.4, p. 16), not proved anew. No rate analysis beyond the remark that BB rates are inherited near a strictly convex minimizer (p. 2).
  - Transferable: to guarantee a sign condition on a derived coefficient across a whole eigenvalue set, prove an interlacing inequality between the two spectra it compares (Theorem 3.3, pp. 23–24).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab; three algorithms that "differed only in the manner in which step sizes were computed" (p. 16): cubic, quad-ritz, quad-hritz (the last two set the step to Ω on negative eigenvalues), all with the same Zhang–Hager nonmonotone line search and parameters (pp. 16–17); safeguards at 10⁻¹² and 10¹² (p. 16). m ∈ {1, 3, 5} (p. 17). Test set: CUTEst unconstrained problems at their largest preset size, kept if (a) at least 50 variables, (b) at least one run of some algorithm took ≥ 5 s, (c) at least one run met a nonpositive quadratic coefficient, (d) at least one run succeeded, leaving 30 problems (p. 17). Measures: function and gradient evaluations (iterations = gradient evaluations), Morales logarithmic outperforming factors clipped to [−1, 1] (p. 17). Results: harmonic Ritz better than Ritz at m = 1; cubic and quad-hritz similar at m = 1 "with better performance provided by quad-hritz on some problems" (p. 17); cubic better on a majority at m = 3, 5 (p. 20); best with cubic and m = 5, larger m not better, attributed to more frequent nonpositive eigenvalues (p. 20). The per-problem tables show where cubic loses at m = 1 (e.g. EIGENBLS 10,075 vs. 1232 function evaluations, GENHUMPS 19,378 vs. 2135; Table B1, pp. 24–25). No CPU times, no L-BFGS comparison although Fletcher's LMSD is introduced as competitive with L-BFGS (pp. 1–2).
  - Transferable: when the paper is about one phenomenon, filter the test set to problems where the phenomenon occurs (criterion (c), p. 17), and say so; but note that criteria (b) to (d) also select on run outcomes.
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Fletcher (2012) and the BB family; defines its contribution against the "large prescribed constant" practice of Raydan, Dai–Yuan–Yuan, Xiao et al., Biglari–Solimanpur (p. 5), distinguishes itself from Kafaki–Fatemi's modified BB (p. 5), and rejects Hermite-interpolation higher-order models because they "may not shed any light on what may be a reasonable step size" and do not generalize to m > 1 (p. 2). Fletcher is treated respectfully and reinterpreted as a cubic-model method "in which the cubic term is zero" (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The thesis appears in the abstract and paragraph 3 of the introduction ("The main contribution ... is that it provides a novel strategy for computing step sizes when solving nonconvex optimization problems", p. 2). The algorithm is built up from m = 1 to general m (pp. 8–15), with figures of the step-size map before and after (Figs. 1–2, pp. 6, 12). The notation is explained (footnote on "bar" and "hat", p. 9). The convergence claim is phrased "we claim" (p. 16). The acknowledgement says referees improved "the aims and scope of our numerical results" (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution level: algorithmic (a step-size rule for nonpositive curvature) plus structural theorems; convergence borrowed. Limits stated: large m does not help, "an excessive amount of nonpositive curvature is not easily overcome" (p. 20). Limits visible: small outcome-filtered test set (30 problems, p. 17); no wall-clock or L-BFGS comparison; global convergence only with an added nonmonotone line search (p. 16). No future directions stated (pp. 20–21).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (Fair-fight benchmarking) ✅ evidence: rivals are built inside the same code with only the step-size rule varied and a shared line search (p. 16), and the incumbent's wins at m = 1 are acknowledged (p. 17) and visible in the tables (pp. 24–25).
- Method 3 (Break it on purpose) ⚠ variant: instead of constructing hard instances, the test set is filtered to problems where nonpositive curvature actually occurs (p. 17); the filter uses run outcomes, which Heuristic 7 warns against.
- Method 2 (Steer, don't switch) ⚠ variant: the method keeps one step-size rule across convex and nonconvex regions (the cubic term switches on only when q̂ ≤ 0, p. 11) instead of abandoning the model and setting a constant, as Fletcher and BB variants do (pp. 5, 7); no penalty or merit parameter is involved.
- new-pattern candidate: reduce-to-incumbent design — the new mechanism is active only in the failure case, so the method coincides with the incumbent elsewhere (p. 2; m = 1 reduction, p. 13).
- new-pattern candidate: map the incumbent's rule — a contour plot of the rule's output over its inputs exposes where it discards information (Figs. 1–2, pp. 5–6, 11–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Contour map of a step-size rule as a function of the current gradient for fixed previous step, before and after the fix (Figs. 1–2, pp. 5–6, 11–12).
- Derive a coefficient from two independent fitting criteria and keep the common form with one constant (eqs. (3.6)–(3.11), pp. 9–10).
- Give each degenerate case a named step size with a one-sentence rationale (Ω for "appears affine", ω for no parallel information; pp. 10–11).
- Drop the oldest history vectors until the factorization is valid, instead of discarding the whole memory (Algorithm 2, p. 15).
- Interlacing proof to certify the sign of a derived coefficient (Theorem 3.3, pp. 23–24).
