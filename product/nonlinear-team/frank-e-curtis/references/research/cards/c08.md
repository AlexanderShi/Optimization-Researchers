### S024 · A globally convergent primal-dual active-set framework for large-scale convex quadratic optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10589-014-9681-9)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–31, including the Appendix on semismooth Newton equivalence (pp. 27–29); I read the tables in full and skimmed the reference list.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Zheng Han (Lehigh PhD student), Daniel P. Robinson (Johns Hopkins) · **Position in trajectory**: the inner-solver strand (QP solvers for AL and SQO methods), 2013–2015. It leads into Curtis–Han's inexact-subproblem PDAS report (cited as TR 14T-010 in S067, p. 13) and into S067.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The method of Hintermüller, Ito and Kunisch (HIK) [25] changes many active-set indices per iteration and is very fast. But it is proved convergent only for bound-constrained QPs with (perturbed) M-matrix Hessians, and it can cycle on other strictly convex QPs (p. 3; Theorem 2, p. 11). Classical active-set methods change one index per iteration, and that is "the main contributing factor" in their cost at scale (p. 2). The motivation is the inner solve of AL and SQO methods: active-set methods can exploit good starting points and stay accurate under ill-conditioning (p. 2). Problem (1): a strictly convex QP with equalities and bounds (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The iterates are index partitions (A_l, A_u, I, U), not primal-dual points. The primal-dual values come from a reduced subproblem (Algorithm 2, p. 6). The novelty is an auxiliary "uncertain" set U whose bounds are enforced explicitly. When U = ∅ an iteration costs one reduced linear solve, exactly as in HIK (eq. (7), p. 7). Indices go into U only when needed to stop cycling (p. 3). There are two update rules: Algorithm 5 counts how often each index changes set and moves an index into U once its counter reaches q_max (p. 13); Algorithm 6 is a nonmonotone watchdog on the KKT residual over the last p iterates, which can also remove indices from U (pp. 15–16). Algorithm 1 restores feasibility of the partition when there are equality constraints (p. 6). What stays the same: the HIK update (16) remains the default move (p. 10). Globalization uses no merit function or filter; it rests on monotonicity in |U| or in the residual (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: H is symmetric positive definite, A has full row rank, ℓ < u. "All of which can be guaranteed by preprocessing" (p. 4). For the analysis the problem is assumed feasible; infeasibility is handled by a Phase-1 LP in the implementation (p. 5). The HIK convergence result needs H = M + K with M an M-matrix and ‖K‖ small (Theorem 2, p. 11). The new strategies need only strict convexity (Theorems 3–4, pp. 14, 17).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Theorem 1 (p. 8): if the current sets are subsets of the optimal ones, the subspace minimization returns the exact solution. This turns convergence into "reach a subset partition in finitely many steps". (ii) A finite-state monotonicity argument for Algorithm 5: every iteration either enlarges U or raises some counter (Lemma 2, by contradiction via the residual r, pp. 13–14), so in the worst case U = N and the method terminates (Theorem 3, p. 14). (iii) For Algorithm 6, the max of the last p+1 residuals decreases strictly, and there are finitely many partitions (Lemma 3 and Theorem 4, pp. 15–17). (iv) A single 3-variable counterexample (Example 1, pp. 11–12) shows HIK cycling; the same example is then run through Algorithms 5 and 6 (Tables 2–3, pp. 15, 17). It was built with n = 3 because HIK provably converges for n ≤ 2 (p. 12). (v) The Appendix (Theorem 5, pp. 27–29) shows that the method with U ≡ ∅ is a semismooth Newton method. Along the way it corrects HIK: their proof shows equivalence only after the first iterate, and their superlinear claim is "immediate" since convergence is finite (p. 11). Every result is finite termination; there are no rates.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab, with CPLEX primal simplex for the partition-feasibility LP (25) and qpOASES for subproblems with U ≠ ∅ (pp. 18–19). The test problems are random, with roughly equal numbers of lower-active, upper-active and inactive variables (pp. 19–20). Bound-constrained QPs: n ∈ {10²,10³,10⁴} × cond(H) ∈ {10²,10⁴,10⁶}, 50 instances each. General QPs: n = 10⁴, m ∈ {10, 20}, 10 instances each (pp. 20, 24). Measures include iterations, SM calls, linear solves, qpOASES iterations and |U| statistics (last and average). The |U| statistics measure how much the safeguard is used (pp. 20–21). HIK (Algorithm 4) fails on 5 of 50 and 4 of 50 of the largest instances; these failures are reported in a table footnote (Table 4, p. 20). The new methods solve everything, and U is mostly empty (Tables 5–7, pp. 21–23). The comparison with qpOASES run directly comes with a warning that the counts "should not be compared directly" (p. 24). q_max = 5 and p = 5 "were chosen as they resulted in good performance in our experiments" (p. 20), that is, tuned on the evaluation set. The success test is KKT ≤ 10⁻⁶ with an iteration limit of 1.1n (p. 20).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper presents itself as a "straightforward enhancement" of HIK [25] that is still novel (p. 3). It names related fast-active-set methods that lack general guarantees [2,3,5,26,28,32] (p. 3) and contrasts in detail with Hager's dual active-set algorithm DASA (subproblem structure, how index sets are updated, infeasibility handling; pp. 9–10). Projected-gradient methods with subspace phases are judged effective "provided they are not too ill-conditioned" (p. 4). It corrects a technical point in HIK directly (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is in the introduction (p. 3), followed at once by the practical point that U is usually empty ("perhaps more important than these guarantees", p. 3). The paper keeps repeating that the worst case is never seen (p. 14). Hedges such as "typically" and "in our experience" are frequent. A side-by-side summary puts primal methods (monotone objective), dual methods (monotone dual objective) and this framework (monotone |U| or residual) in parallel (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic and theoretical (finite global convergence for all strictly convex QPs) plus a Matlab implementation. The limits are stated concretely: the method is less efficient with equality constraints, U is nonempty more often there, and keeping partitions feasible costs extra. No results are given for more equality constraints for that reason (pp. 25, 27). The paper recommends classical active-set methods when there are few degrees of freedom (p. 27). Future work: iterative (inexact) subproblem solves with monitored inexactness (p. 27).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: the work is motivated as the QP engine for AL and SQO, and inexact inner solves are the stated next step (pp. 2, 27). Method 3 evidence ✅: a minimal hand-built counterexample (n = 3, chosen because n ≤ 2 provably converges) is reused across all strategies (pp. 11–17). Method 5 evidence ✅, with a caveat ⚠: the incumbent's failures and the paper's own weaker equality-constrained results are both printed, and the qpOASES comparison carries a warning (pp. 20, 24–25, 27); but q_max and p were chosen on the test set (p. 20). Method 2 variant ⚠: a single algorithm whose safeguard set U grows only on observed trouble (counters or residual watchdog) and otherwise stays HIK, rather than switching to a classical active-set phase (pp. 3, 13, 16). Heuristic 1 evidence: the safeguard's activity (|U|) is tabulated per run (Tables 5–7). New-pattern candidate: **minimal-intervention safeguard** (the fast unguarded method is the default; the guarantee comes from a monotone finite-state device that is dormant in practice).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Globalize a fast, unguaranteed combinatorial iteration by adding an auxiliary set that only ever grows (or a nonmonotone watchdog on a residual over a finite state space). Convergence then follows from pigeonhole or monotonicity, with no merit function or line search (pp. 13–17).
- Prove a "subset ⇒ exact solution" lemma first (Theorem 1, p. 8). Each globalization strategy then only has to show that it reaches such a state.
- Build the smallest counterexample to the incumbent (check what dimension is provably safe, then go one higher). Run the same instance through every new variant and show the iterate tables (Tables 1–3).
- Report how much the safeguard is used (average and final |U|, QP-solver iterations versus plain linear solves), so readers see the cost of the guarantee (Tables 5–7, 9–11).
**Quotes**:
- "We stress, however, that while the proof relies on such worst-case behavior, we have never witnessed such an occurrence in practice." (p. 14)
- "Variables that change index sets numerous times may be considered sensitive and are prime candidates for membership" (p. 12)

### S050 · Iterative reweighted linear least squares for exact penalty subproblems on product sets (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2015, DOI 10.1137/130950239; read as arXiv 1402.1917 v1)
**Read level**: full, on the arXiv v1 preprint "Matrix-free solvers for exact penalty subproblems" (same four authors; per the work-list note it is the preprint of this paper under an earlier title). All pages below are arXiv pages. I read pp. 1–33 in full. The standard ADAL convergence proofs (§3.2, pp. 18–21) and the formula layers of §§4–5 (pp. 24–27) were skimmed, because the equation extraction is fragmentary; their prose was read. Differences between this preprint and the journal version are not checked.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: James V. Burke (U. Washington), Hao Wang (Lehigh), Jiashan Wang (U. Washington) · **Position in trajectory**: the matrix-free inner solver for exact-penalty (Gauss–Newton / SQP-type) subproblems, the inner-solve line of Method 1, 2014–2015.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Exact-penalty convex-composite methods solve subproblems min gᵀx + ½xᵀHx + dist(Ax+b | C) with a line search or a trust region (p. 1). At scale these subproblems need matrix-free solvers (pp. 1–2). The paper assumes H ⪰ 0, although in practice H approximates the Lagrangian Hessian "and so may be indefinite" (p. 2). The entry point is structural: C is a product of convex sets with the norm Σ‖·‖₂, so the distance splits into blocks (eqs. (1.3)–(1.6), p. 2). Intersections of convex sets fit as a special case (Example 1.1, p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: IRWA, the iterative re-weighting algorithm, minimizes a weighted linear least-squares model built from projections onto the C_i. The weights are w_i = (dist² + ε_i²)^(−1/2) (eq. (2.1), p. 4), so each iteration is a convex quadratic solvable by CG (p. 4). The relaxation vector ε is reduced only when the step is small relative to the residual (test (2.4), p. 5). For C_i = {0} this is classical IRLS for ℓ1 fitting (Remark 2.2, p. 5). The comparison method is an ADAL (alternating direction augmented Lagrangian) "for comparison with IRWA". Its p-subproblem separates with closed-form projections and its x-subproblem is a convex QP (pp. 16–17). Nesterov-accelerated IRWA keeps convergence through a fallback when the extrapolated point is worse (§4, pp. 24–25). A variant for systems of equations and inequalities avoids weights blowing up on strictly satisfied inequalities (§5, p. 26).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: H is positive semidefinite (p. 2). Cluster points exist under a horizon-cone coercivity condition (2.13) or simpler sufficient conditions: X compact, H ≻ 0, or C compact with a kernel condition (Theorem 2.8 and Corollary 2.9, pp. 7–9). The complexity theorem needs X = Rⁿ, H ≻ 0 and a fixed relaxation vector, ε^k ≡ ε with ‖ε‖₁ ≤ ε/2 (Theorem 2.14, p. 13), so it does not cover the adaptive-ε algorithm that is run. ADAL needs a saddle point to exist (Assumption 3.3, p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) A smoothing J(x, ε) = φ + Σ√(dist² + ε_i²), jointly convex in (x, ε) (eq. (2.5), p. 5). IRWA is a descent method on it, through a majorization lemma from concavity of √t (Lemma 2.5, eqs. (2.7)–(2.8), pp. 6–7). (ii) Horizon-cone (recession) analysis characterizes compact level sets (Theorem 2.8, pp. 7–8). (iii) A subsequence argument: either ε → 0, or the step test eventually holds and forces ε down (Lemma 2.11, pp. 9–11). Stationarity is then 0 ∈ ∂J₀ + N_X (Theorem 2.12, p. 11). (iv) An O(1/ε²) rate: 1/ε Lipschitz continuity of the smoothed gradient (Lemma 2.15, p. 13), sufficient decrease (Lemma 2.16, p. 14), and the standard 1/δ_{k+1} − 1/δ_k ≥ α/τ² telescoping (Theorem 2.17, pp. 14–15). Adding the smoothing error ‖ε‖₁ ≤ ε/2 gives the result (p. 16). (v) The ADAL rate is imported from Wang–Banerjee [26] with a notation-translation table (Remark 3.13, p. 23). The paper argues that O(1/ε) results for other ADAL splittings do not apply, because their subproblems are as hard as the original (p. 22).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Python/NumPy/SciPy. Both methods use CG stopped at 10% of the initial residual, warm-started from the previous point (p. 28). Experiment 1: 500 random instances (A is 600×1000; 300 equations and 300 inequalities), shown as efficiency curves of percent of problems solved against total CG steps, at 50/75/90/95% duality-gap reduction. ADAL "stumbled" at 95% on 8 problems (pp. 28–29). Experiment 2: 20 problems of growing size, CG steps and CPU time, with similar results (pp. 29–30). Experiment 3: 40 random ℓ1-SVM problems solved to the algorithms' own stopping tests, comparing final objective, CG steps and false-positive "zeros" in the recovered support. IRWA is better here (fewer than 14 false positives against a median of about 1000 for ADAL at 10⁻⁵; Figs. 6.4–6.5, pp. 31–32). Accelerated against plain IRWA: the accelerated version always reaches a lower objective, with one exception in CG steps (Fig. 6.6, p. 32). The paper admits sensitivity to ε⁰ (IRWA) and µ (ADAL), with "no theoretical justification" for the choices; dimension-dependent automatic rules are used (p. 28).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper places itself in the convex-composite and exact-penalty tradition (Burke, Fletcher, Powell; p. 1) and the IRLS lineage (Beaton–Tukey, Schlossmacher, O'Leary, Osborne, Wolke–Schwetlick; pp. 2, 5). ADAL is presented as standard technology "adapted to this setting" (p. 2). Its convergence proofs are "standard for algorithms of this type", included "for the sake of completeness" (p. 18). Earlier complexity results are reframed rather than criticized (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract's claims are calibrated ("in certain cases, IRWA is shown to be more reliable than ADAL", p. 1), and the conclusion keeps them ("perform similarly on many test problems", p. 33). Open questions are flagged as conjectures or open issues: an O(1/ε) rate for the accelerated versions (pp. 24, 33). Notation warnings are written into the text (‖y‖ ≠ ‖y‖₂, p. 2; L(x,p,u) versus L(x,p,u,µ), p. 16). The paper is theorem-heavy; the intuition is short and comes mostly before the algorithms.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic (IRWA for arbitrary convex product sets, p. 33) and theoretical (global convergence plus O(1/ε²) for the unaccelerated variants). Stated limits: complexity only for the unaccelerated methods, while acceleration is "the preferred implementation" (p. 33); parameter initialization is left to "future research" (p. 28); indefinite H is excluded (p. 2). Unstated: the rate is proved for fixed ε, not for the adaptive rule (p. 13); the tests use random data only and do not embed the solver in an outer penalty-SQP loop.

**Method links**: Method 1 variant ⚠: this is an inner solver designed for the subproblems of an outer exact-penalty method, matrix-free (pp. 1–2). But the inner CG stops at a fixed 10% relative residual, not a test derived from the outer method's needs (p. 28), and no outer loop is run. Method 2 variant ⚠: the relaxation vector ε is updated by a test on progress (step size against residual, (2.4), p. 5), a parameter-update rule as the design object outside the SQP penalty family. Method 5 evidence ✅: the rival (ADAL) is built in the same Python code with the same CG solver and stopping rule, and the similar results are printed (pp. 16, 28–30, 33). Method 4 evidence ✅: the complexity claims of other ADAL splittings are checked against what one of their iterations costs (p. 22). Heuristic 6 evidence: support-recovery false positives are used as the metric that exposes the difference (pp. 31–32).
**Transferable techniques**:
- For a nonsmooth distance-penalty subproblem, introduce the jointly convex ε-smoothing J(x, ε) and show your reweighted quadratic step is a majorize-minimize descent step on it. The √t concavity bound gives the descent inequality in two lines (Lemma 2.5, pp. 6–7).
- Drive the smoothing parameter down with a step-versus-residual test (‖q_i‖ ≤ M(‖r_i‖² + ε_i²)^(1/2+γ)) instead of a fixed schedule. The convergence proof then argues "either ε → 0 or the test eventually fires" (pp. 5, 10).
- Use horizon-cone (recession) conditions to get compact level sets and cluster points, and give a short list of easy sufficient conditions for readers (Theorem 2.8 and Corollary 2.9, pp. 7–9).
- When comparing two inner solvers, give both the same inner linear solver, tolerance and warm start, and report percent-solved against total inner (CG) steps at several duality-gap reductions (Fig. 6.1, p. 28).
**Quotes**:
- "The primary novelty of our work is a newly proposed iterative re-weighting algorithm (IRWA) for solving such problems involving arbitrary convex sets of the form (1.3)." (p. 33)
- "More principled methods for initializing and updating these parameters is the subject of future research." (p. 28)

### S067 · Primal-dual active-set methods for isotonic regression and trend filtering (arXiv preprint 2015, arXiv 1508.02452)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–13 (arXiv v2, 3 Apr 2016), all of it including the references. The figures (pp. 5–12) are images; only their captions and the surrounding text are available.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Zheng Han (Lehigh PhD student; first author) · **Position in trajectory**: carries the PDAS line (S024; Curtis–Han inexact PDAS report, ref. [19], p. 13) over to ML and statistics problem classes, 2015–2016.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Isotonic regression is used to calibrate classifiers (p. 1). The incumbent PAV (pool-adjacent-violators) algorithm is O(n) but "inherently sequential" and cannot be warm-started. That matters for sequences of related IR problems and in online settings (pp. 1, 3). Trend filtering (TF) generalizes IR, and plain PDAS can cycle on it (p. 5). Problems (IR) and (TF) are stated on p. 2.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The dual of IR is a bound-constrained QP whose Hessian DΩ⁻¹Dᵀ is an M-matrix, which is exactly the class where the HIK PDAS method is guaranteed (pp. 3–4). Algorithm 2 runs that PDAS in block form: it starts from any partition, merges several blocks at once, and keeps running sums α, β so that each merge costs O(1) (p. 4). For TF, a generic three-step PDAS (subspace minimization, termination check, partition update; pp. 6–7) gets a new safeguard SF2. SF2 changes only a fraction p of the violating indices, adapts p with factors δ_s and δ_e, and judges progress against the maximum of a FIFO queue of recent |V| values (Algorithm 5, pp. 7–8). This replaces the prior safeguard SF1 [20,21], which after t_max failures switches to moving one index (Algorithm 4, p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: IR has positive weights ω (p. 2). TF has smooth convex f and convex nonsmooth g = ‖Dθ‖₁ or ‖(Dθ)₊‖₁ with difference operators of order 1–2 (pp. 2, 5). The IR guarantee inherits the M-matrix condition of [17] (Theorem 3.1, p. 4). For SF2 the paper says it "preserves the convergence guarantees established in [20]", but gives no proof (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) Equivalence transfer: Algorithm 2 generates the same iterates as the HIK PDAS applied to the dual BQP, so finite convergence comes from Theorem 3.1 of [17] (Theorem 3.2 proof, p. 4). (ii) Work counting: initialization is O(n), each merge is O(1) thanks to the accumulators, and there are at most n merges, so the total is O(n) (p. 4). (iii) A cycling counterexample for TF taken from the authors' earlier QP example (Example 4.1, Table 1, p. 7). (iv) SF2's convergence is argued informally: the reference value (the queue maximum) decreases monotonically and p is reduced when there is no progress (p. 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Python 2.7 with NumPy/SciPy, ω = 1 (p. 8). IR: PDAS against the Python PAV that later went into scikit-learn, on y_i = i + N(0,4), 10 instances for each n from 10⁴ to 3.3×10⁵, both from singleton partitions. Boxplots of time and merges show PDAS faster, with "nearly identical" merge counts (pp. 8–9). Warm start: instances perturbed by N(0,10⁻²); PDAS needs far fewer merges while PAV cannot benefit (Figs. 4–5, pp. 9–10). TF: PDAS, SF1 and SF2 on uniform [0,10] data (described as "minimum pattern"), n ∈ {10⁴, 1.7×10⁵, 3.3×10⁵}, λ = 10, a cap of 800 iterations, and success rates by problem type. Unguarded PDAS fails 100% on second-order D, SF1 fails at the larger sizes, SF2 solves all (Table 2, p. 10). An IPM [11] is included with a stated handicap: it is "implemented in Matlab (as opposed to Python)" and only set up for ℓ1 (p. 10). Warm-started SF2 is compared with IPM on perturbed instances (Fig. 8, pp. 10–12). Safeguard parameters are given (t_max = 5; m = 5, δ_s = 0.9, δ_e = 1.1, p. 10); how they were chosen is not reported.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper builds on HIK [17] and on the authors' own globalized PDAS [18] and inexact PDAS [19] (pp. 3, 13). It takes SF1 from Kim–Park [20] and Byrd–Chin–Nocedal–Oztoprak [21] and says plainly that SF2 "performs better in our experiments" (p. 7). Against PAV it states what PAV does well (O(n), simplicity) before its drawbacks (p. 3). Alternatives for TF (IPM, specialized ADMM, proximal methods) are listed, and the advantage claimed for active-set methods is finite termination and accurate solutions (p. 1). The difficulty of warm-starting IPMs is cited from the literature (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Short, workshop-length (12 pages plus references). The drawbacks of the incumbent are argued in the first person ("we argue", p. 3). One small worked example (y = {6,4,2,9,11,4}, Figure 1, p. 5) explains parallel merges. The conclusion is promotional ("innovative", "powerful", p. 12). Small errors remain in the text: update (4) writes N ← (P\V_N) ∪ V_AN, where N\V_N is presumably meant (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic (PDAS for IR with O(n) work, warm-starting and parallelizable merges; safeguard SF2) with experimental evidence. Limits: parallelism is only claimed, since merges were run sequentially (p. 8); SF2's convergence is asserted, not proved (p. 8); the data are synthetic only; and the comparison with IPM crosses languages (p. 10). No future work is stated beyond the general claim (p. 12).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅: a constructed cycling instance for TF is shown before the safeguard is introduced (Example 4.1, p. 7). Method 5 variant ⚠: the language handicap of the IPM baseline is stated (p. 10), and equal merge counts are conceded (p. 9). But timing comparisons cross implementations and languages, there are 10 instances per size, and the safeguard parameters are untuned or unreported (pp. 8–10). Method 2 variant ⚠: a single algorithm adapts its aggressiveness (the fraction p) from monitored progress instead of switching to a conservative one-index fallback (pp. 7–8). This is "steer, don't switch" applied outside the penalty family. New-pattern candidate: **minimal-intervention safeguard** (second instance in this batch); **warm-start as an evaluation axis** (pp. 9–12; also the motivation in S024, p. 2).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Before designing an algorithm for a structured problem, dualize it and check whether the dual lands in a class with a known guarantee (here an M-matrix BQP). Then write the algorithm in the primal's structure so it generates the same iterates, and inherit the theorem (pp. 3–4).
- Prove linear work by keeping running sufficient statistics (sums α, β) so that each merge costs O(1), and bound the number of merges by n (p. 4).
- Safeguard design: instead of a hard switch after t_max failures, act on a fraction p of the violators. Shrink or grow p according to whether the current measure beats the maximum of a FIFO queue of recent values (Algorithm 5, p. 8).
- Show the advantage the incumbent cannot have with a perturbation protocol: solve a base instance, perturb the data slightly, warm-start from the old solution, and count the work (pp. 9–10).
**Quotes**:
- "However, we argue that the PAV algorithm does have critical drawbacks when it comes to solving large-scale problems of interest today." (p. 3)
- "This shows that our proposed safeguard, which allows more aggressive updates, can be more effective than a conservative safeguard." (p. 10)

### S028 · A self-correcting variable-metric algorithm for stochastic optimization (Proceedings of The 33rd International Conference on Machine Learning 2016, PMLR vol. 48; no DOI or arXiv id listed)
**Read level**: full: pp. 1–12, including the appendix of proofs (pp. 10–12); the reference list (pp. 9–10) was skimmed. The figures are images; only their captions and the surrounding text are available.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (single author); the acknowledgments thank Jorge Nocedal and Daniel P. Robinson for "insightful conversations" (p. 9) · **Position in trajectory**: Curtis's first stochastic quasi-Newton paper, written at the same time as the Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal SIAM Review survey (cited as a working paper, pp. 5, 9). It is a rung on the deterministic-to-stochastic ladder (Heuristic 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Existing stochastic quasi-Newton methods (oBFGS/oLBFGS, SGD-QN, RES, SQN, SDBFGS) each have a specific problem: two gradients per iteration plus a convexity requirement, over-regularization, or a need for subsampled Hessians. These problems matter most on nonconvex problems such as DNN training (pp. 1–2). The reframing: the field has focused on which secant equation to use, whereas BFGS succeeds in deterministic optimization because of its self-correcting properties, which DFP lacks (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Keep the BFGS update but choose v_k = β_k s_k + (1−β_k) α_k y_k. Here β_k ∈ [0,1] is the smallest value for which the two curvature bounds (9) hold (eq. (6), p. 3; Algorithm SC-BFGS, p. 4). Two choices differ from Powell damping: s_k is used instead of H_k s_k, and α_k scales y_k, which "normalizes" the stepsize and was found empirically to stabilize (p. 3). An optional subroutine, SC-BFGS-sub, re-samples or averages gradient estimates within a budget k̂_max until surrogate versions of the analysis conditions (13)–(14) hold. If the budget runs out, the update is skipped (p. 5). An L-BFGS variant costs about 1 + 2m/b times an SG iteration (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f = E[F(w, ξ)], continuously differentiable, bounded below, with an L-Lipschitz gradient on an open set containing the iterates (Assumption 3, p. 4). The key additional conditions are (13) ρ‖∇f‖² ≤ ∇fᵀE[M_k g_k] and (14), a bound on E‖M_k g_k‖². The author flags that they "should not be taken lightly", because g_k and M_k are dependent (p. 4), and that they "cannot be verified" without exact gradients (p. 5). With exact gradients, Corollary 2 makes them hold (p. 5). Strong convexity is added for the rates (Theorem 6, p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (i) The Byrd–Nocedal (1989) potential γ(H) = trace(H) − ln det(H) and its one-step recursion (8) (p. 3). If (9) holds every iteration, the bounds on cos δ_k and ‖H_k s_k‖/‖s_k‖ hold for at least ⌈pK⌉ of the first K iterations, for any p ∈ (0,1) (Theorem 1, pp. 3–4). The proof (pp. 10–11) is an averaging/order-statistic argument on the nonnegative terms ζ_k. (ii) Corollary 2 turns these into bounds on g_kᵀM_k g_k and ‖M_k g_k‖ (p. 4; proof pp. 10–11). (iii) The standard SG analysis: an expected-decrease lemma (Lemma 4, p. 4), Robbins–Monro step sizes giving Σα_k E‖∇f‖² < ∞ (Theorem 5, p. 5, mimicking Bottou 1998), and a sequence-convergence lemma (Lemma 8, p. 11). (iv) Strongly convex rates: linear convergence to an αLσ/(2cρ) neighbourhood with a fixed step, and O(1/k) with α/k by induction (Theorem 6, p. 5; proofs p. 12). The induction step writes "αcµ" where ρ is meant (p. 12), a notation slip.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Matlab. The variants are SC, SC-s (with the subroutine, using averages of already computed estimates rather than fresh samples, p. 6), SC-L and SC-L-s, against SG and oBFGS/oLBFGS. There are three problems: a1a logistic (d = 123), rcv1 logistic (d = 47,236) and a 3-layer sigmoid network on 20,000 MNIST samples (d = 27,540) (pp. 6–8). The budget is max{n, 6400} sample accesses, with batch size 64 and m = 5 (p. 6). All methods sweep the same diminishing and fixed step-size grids plus their own parameters (η, θ, ρ, τ, ω₃), and "the results provided are for the inputs that yielded the lowest final testing loss" (p. 6). That is, selection on the test set, from one random starting point and one run per setting (p. 6). Training and testing losses and CPU time are tabulated (Tables 1–5, pp. 7–8). SC costs more CPU time than SG on a1a (Table 1, p. 7). oLBFGS on the nonconvex problem is stopped when sᵀv ≤ 0, and that is why its best settings looked worse (p. 8). In Tables 3–5 the limited-memory runs are labelled "SC"/"OBFGS" (pp. 7–8), a labelling slip. The code is publicly available (p. 6).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper criticizes each predecessor by name and mechanism (pp. 1–2) and positions itself on the deterministic theory of Powell (1976), Byrd–Nocedal (1989) and Byrd–Nocedal–Yuan (1987) (pp. 2–3). Variance-reduction methods (SVRG, SAGA) are named as the other route beyond SG (p. 1). The SG analysis is borrowed openly from Bottou (1998) and Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal (pp. 5, 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: There are five bullet-point contributions, opening with a provocative reframing ("too much emphasis", p. 2). The theory is called "motivating", not decisive (pp. 2, 8), and its limits are admitted in a dedicated "Practical Considerations" subsection (p. 5). The ML-venue register is concise, with all proofs in the appendix. The claims in the conclusion stay with the three problems ("two convex and one nonconvex", p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic (a damping choice that preserves self-correction; a noise-reduction subroutine whose conditions come from the theory) and theoretical (convergence and rates under Assumption 3). Limits stated: (13)–(14) cannot be verified, and the subroutine costs at least two gradients (p. 5). Limits visible but unstated: three test problems, best-of-grid selection on test loss, single runs. Future direction: "general conditions for adaptive gradient noise reduction" (pp. 2, 6).

**Method links**: Method 5 variant ⚠: the same tuning grids are given to all methods, including the SG baseline (p. 6). But results are best-of-grid on test loss, from one starting point and one run, which matches the lapse recorded in Method 5's "Say–do" note (single runs for a randomized method). Method 2 variant ⚠: the damping weight β_k is set each iteration by the smallest value satisfying a computable test (9) (pp. 3–4), rather than by fixed regularization as in RES/SDBFGS (p. 2). The parameter rule is the contribution, outside the penalty family. Heuristic 1 evidence: unverifiable analysis conditions (13)–(14) are replaced by surrogate tests with an independent gradient estimate (SC-BFGS-sub, p. 5). Heuristic 10 evidence: the deterministic BFGS skeleton is kept and only the curvature pair is changed (pp. 2–3). New-pattern candidates: **port the property, not the formula** (identify which property of the deterministic method carries its success, here self-correction, and design the stochastic version to preserve that property; p. 2); **minimal-intervention safeguard** (third instance in this batch: the smallest β_k, p. 4).
**Transferable techniques**:
- When carrying a deterministic method to the stochastic setting, first ask which proved property makes it work (here the γ = tr − ln det bounds from Byrd–Nocedal 1989). Then enforce exactly the inequalities that the property needs ((9), p. 3), choosing the smallest correction that satisfies them.
- Damp the curvature pair toward s_k (not H_k s_k) with a weight found by a one-dimensional search on [0, 1]. This makes the trace and determinant bounds hold by construction (eq. (6), p. 3).
- Turn the analysis assumptions into a runtime test: check them with an independent mini-batch gradient as surrogate, under a budget k̂_max, and skip the update if the budget runs out (SC-BFGS-sub, p. 5).
- To bound how bad an update sequence can get, use the order-statistic step: since the average of the ζ_k is bounded, at least ⌈pK⌉ of them are at most (γ(H₁) + θ − 1 − ln η)/(1−p) (pp. 10–11).
