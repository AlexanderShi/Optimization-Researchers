### S082 · A trust region method for noisy unconstrained optimization (Mathematical Programming 202(1), 445–472, 2023, DOI 10.1007/s10107-023-01941-9; arXiv:2201.00973)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole arXiv v1 main text, pp. 1–24 (abstract, §§1–5, acknowledgments, references), and the supplementary material, pp. 25–34. The supplement has section headers and figure captions only, and its figures (like Figs. 1–4 of the main text) are images that are not in the text layer. The text read is the v1 preprint, under its v1 title (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shigeng Sun (Northwestern, Engineering Sciences and Applied Mathematics) (p. 1); Richard Byrd is thanked "for many valuable suggestions and insights", and Figen Oztoprak, Andreas Wächter and Melody Xuan for proofreading (p. 23) · **Place in trajectory**: the noise programme (2017/18–2025). It extends his group's relaxed-line-search work for unconstrained problems [2, 27] and for equality-constrained problems ([21] = S084) to trust regions, "as trust region methods require different proof techniques" (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Classical trust-region methods assume exact function and gradient values (abstract, p. 1). Their design and analysis "are well established in the absence of noise", but not under noise (p. 2). The entry is the authors' own experiments: the classical method "often tolerates significant levels of noise" but "can fail in certain situations" (p. 2). The broken component is located precisely. In the ratio of actual to predicted reduction (6), the numerator can be of order ε_f while the denominator is proportional to Δ_k. When Δ_k << ε_f the ratio "may exhibit wild oscillations" (p. 4). Problem statement: min f with f̃ = f + δ_f and g̃ = g + δ_g, where |δ_f| ≤ ε_f and ‖δ_g‖ ≤ ε_g (p. 4). The motivating applications are mixed-precision arithmetic, finite-difference derivatives and computational noise (p. 2).
  - Transferable: before redesigning a method for noise, find the exact place in a test where one side scales with the noise and the other with the step (here O(ε_f) against O(Δ_k) in the ratio test, p. 4). That names the single component to change.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Only the acceptance ratio changes, to ρ_k = [f̃(x_k) − f̃(x_k + p_k) + rε_f] / [m_k(0) − m_k(p_k) + rε_f] with r > 2 (p. 4). "All other aspects of the classical trust region method remain unchanged" (p. 2). Algorithm 1 is otherwise textbook: shrink by 1/ν if ρ < c1, expand by ν if ρ > c2, accept if ρ > c0 (p. 5). Typical values are c0 = 0.1, c1 = 1/4, c2 = 1/2 and ν = 2, and the theory needs r = 2/(1 − c2) (p. 5). Both numerator and denominator are relaxed "to be consistent with the classical narrative of trust region methods where a ratio close to 1 is an indication that the model is adequate". Relaxing only the numerator would give a relaxed Armijo test as in [2, 21]. The authors find the two-sided form "easier to motivate" and it gives "tighter bounds" (pp. 4–5). The method needs only the size of the function errors and costs nothing extra (abstract, p. 1). Any step with Cauchy decrease is allowed (dogleg, Newton-CG, exact; p. 5). A practical option expands the radius only when ρ > c2 and ‖p_k‖ = Δ_k (p. 5).
  - Transferable: when you relax an acceptance test for noise, relax it so that the test keeps its classical meaning (shift both sides of the ratio so that ρ ≈ 1 still means a good model), and take the relaxation constant from the proof (r = 2/(1 − c2)) instead of tuning it (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1: Lipschitz gradient with constant L (p. 6). Assumption 2: bounded errors in f and g; "We impose no other conditions on the errors, other than boundedness" (p. 6). Assumption 3: ‖B̃_k‖ < L_B (p. 6). Assumption 4: full Cauchy decrease. A fraction of Cauchy decrease would do, but the authors avoid it "to avoid the introduction of more constants" (p. 7). Assumption 5: {f̃_k} bounded below (p. 7). No convexity is assumed (p. 2). The noise is bounded and non-diminishing (p. 2). The algorithm needs ε_f, and ε_g enters only the analysis (pp. 1, 4). The framework is deterministic and worst-case, and the authors set it against probabilistic analyses [13, 6, 16] (p. 3). The extension to the practical radius-expansion rule is said to need "certain technical conditions on the step computation" (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Lemma 1 bounds |ρ_k − 1| using Taylor's theorem, the noise bounds and Cauchy decrease, with M = (L_B + L)/2. It shows that ρ_k can be pushed toward 1 by shrinking Δ_k "up until the noise term ε_f dominates" (p. 7). (b) Lemma 2: if ‖g̃_k‖ > rε_g + γ and Δ_k ≤ Δ̄ = γ/(rM), then ρ_k > c2 and the radius grows (p. 8). Corollary 1 gives a lower bound Δ_k > γ/(νrM) (p. 9). The authors' Remark marks this as the new element. Classical analysis only shows that the radius does not shrink below a threshold; here "We need to show that the trust region eventually becomes large enough with respect to the noise level so that progress can be made" (p. 9). (c) Lemma 3 gives a fixed decrease per accepted step. η is chosen as the smallest positive root of a quadratic, and a slack μ > 0 keeps the lemma meaningful when there is no noise. The slack is later absorbed by setting μ = ε_g/2 (pp. 9–11). (d) Theorem 6 is proved by contradiction: if the iterates stayed outside C1 = {‖g‖ ≤ (r + 1)ε_g + β/2}, there would be infinitely many accepted steps with a fixed decrease, so f̃ → −∞ against Assumption 5 (pp. 11–12). The achievable accuracy depends on ε_g and √ε_f (p. 12). (e) Proposition 1: an accepted step can raise f̃ by at most r(1 − c0)ε_f (p. 12). Corollary 2 keeps the radius bound by induction (p. 13). (f) Theorem 7 is an excursion analysis. After the first visit to C1 the iterates never leave the level set C2 = {f ≤ sup_C1 f + 2ε_f + max[G, r(1 − c0)ε_f]} (p. 13). Case 1 starts with a large radius and decreases at once. Case 2 counts the l = ⌈log_ν(Δ̄/Δ_{K+1})⌉ iterations the radius needs to regrow, and bounds the total displacement by a geometric series, Σ Δ < ν²Δ̄/(ν − 1) (pp. 14–17). G scales with ε_g², ε_g√ε_f and ε_f (p. 17). (g) "This analysis is more complex than for line search methods due to the effects of memory encapsulated in the trust region update" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: in a noisy trust-region analysis, prove that the radius must grow past a noise-determined threshold when the gradient is large compared with the noise (Lemma 2), not only that it cannot collapse (Remark, p. 9).
  - Transferable: to bound how far iterates stray after leaving a good region, count the iterations the radius needs to regrow (a ceiling of a logarithm) and bound the displacement during them by a geometric series (pp. 14–16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Coded in MATLAB. Noise is uniform in f and uniform in a ball for g (79), which satisfies Assumption 2 (p. 18). Parameters are c0 = 0.1, c1 = 1/4, c2 = 1/2 and ν = 2. The subproblem is solved by Newton-CG to 10⁻⁸. There is "no stop test": every run is 200 iterations, to show asymptotic behaviour (p. 18). §4.1, "Failure of the Classical Trust Region Algorithm", uses (i) a quadratic in R⁸ with D = diag(1e−5, …, 1e−3.25) (condition number about 56), ε_f = 10⁻¹, ε_g = 10⁻⁵, the exact Hessian, x0 = (1000, 0, …, 0) and Δ0 = 1. Here the classical method's ρ_k oscillates and the radius collapses (pp. 18–19). (ii) A tridiagonal function with N = 200 and a very small Δ0, where Algorithm 1 regrows the radius and the classical method does not (pp. 19–20). Each figure has four panels: the true gradient norm with the ε_g line and a 25-iteration running minimum, the radius, the distance to the solution, and ρ_k clipped to ±5 (p. 18). §4.2 uses a noisy, possibly indefinite Hessian (83)–(84), ε_f = 10, ε_g = 100, ε_B = 1000 and a random start. The fairness device: "at each iterate we inject exactly the same noise into both algorithms" (p. 21). The classical method's loss is printed. Its shrinking radius "can be beneficial", giving milder gradient oscillations, while Algorithm 1 reaches a lower objective value (p. 21). §4.3 tests the bound's scaling. R(ε_f, ε_g) = log10 of the bound C over the summed best gradient norms of 10 seeds is computed for ε_f, ε_g ∈ [10⁻², 10²]. Its small variation supports the predicted dependence on ε_g and √ε_f, valid only "when averaging multiple runs" (pp. 21–22). The supplement adds Rademacher noise (p. 25), more uniform-noise levels (p. 26), and Schittkowski problems 271, 289 and 293, each from Δ0 = 10⁻⁶ and Δ0 = 1 (pp. 27–34). There is no aggregate over a test set. The text calls it "a small selection of unconstrained optimization problems" (p. 18).
  - Transferable: when a bound is pessimistic in level, test its functional form. Over a grid of noise levels, plot log(bound / observed best accuracy averaged over seeds); a flat surface supports the predicted scaling (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: feed the same noise realization to both arms at every iterate, so that differences come from the algorithms and not from the draws (p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: The literature review places the norm test (Carter [10]; Byrd et al. [9]; Bollapragada et al. [7, 8]), relaxed Armijo line searches (Berahas, Byrd and Nocedal [2]; Berahas, Cao and Scheinberg [3]; Jin, Scheinberg and Xie [18]), noisy BFGS [27, 24], stochastic SQP (Berahas, Curtis, Robinson and coauthors [4, 5, 14]), probabilistic trust-region analyses [13, 6, 16] and complexity with adaptive accuracy [11, 1, 17] (pp. 2–3). The tone is neutral. The deterministic bounded-error style "stands in contrast" with convergence-in-probability analyses, and "It remains to be seen which approach is more useful for the design of noise tolerant optimization methods—or whether the two approaches complement each other" (p. 3). The design is credited to his group's line-search papers [2, 27, 21] (p. 2). Ten of the 27 references have Nocedal as an author (pp. 23–24).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract states the single change, what it needs ("only requires information about the size of the errors"), its cost (none), the result and the failure demonstration (p. 1). The intro gives the one-change claim in its second paragraph (p. 2). Interpretive paragraphs follow the lemmas and theorems: what Lemma 1 suggests (p. 7), the Remark on how the analysis differs from the classical one (p. 9), the summary of Lemmas 2–3 (p. 11), and where the √ε_f dependence comes from (pp. 12, 17). Hedging is explicit and placed with the evidence. The bound is "pessimistic … as is to be expected of convergence results that assume that the largest possible error occurs at every iteration" (pp. 19–20). The classical method's occasional advantage is conceded (p. 21). Single runs vary a lot (p. 22). The final remarks take one page (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic (a one-term change) plus theoretical: an infinite subsequence with ‖g_k‖ = O(√ε_f, ε_g), and the iterates stay in C2 (pp. 11–13, 23). With no noise, C1 and C2 coincide and ‖g_k‖ → 0 (p. 23). The limits are concrete. The noise is bounded. The bound is worst-case and pessimistic (p. 21). Claims hold only on average over seeds (p. 22). The classical method sometimes behaves better (p. 21). The experiments use no stop test (p. 18) and few problems (p. 18). Future direction: when noise can be reduced, run Algorithm 1 until it stalls, lower ε_f and ε_g, and restart with the new ε_f in (7). This is "a disciplined approach for achieving high accuracy in the solution" (p. 23). Not addressed: how to obtain ε_f, what to do if it is wrong, and a stop test.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (evidence ✅): the trusted engine is kept and only the ratio test is changed, driven by an explicit noise level (pp. 2, 4–5). The failure is shown first on an easy, deliberately chosen problem with internal diagnostics (ρ_k, radius) in §4.1 (pp. 18–19; also Heuristic 1). Convergence is proved to a noise-determined neighbourhood (Theorem 6, p. 11).
- Method 3 (variant ⚠, step 4): there is no recovery tree, no noise re-estimation and no exit flag. The runs have no stop test (p. 18), and the only adaptive element is an outer restart with reduced noise, sketched for the case where noise can be lowered (p. 23).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅): the bound is plotted against runs and called pessimistic (Fig. 2, pp. 19–20). This goes beyond step 4, because the bound's scaling in (ε_f, ε_g) is also tested over a grid and 10 seeds (Fig. 4, pp. 21–22).
- Method 1 (evidence ✅, partial): identical noise is injected into both arms (p. 21), and the unmodified method's advantage is printed (p. 21).
- Method 1 (contradiction ✗, step 7): illustrative problems without an aggregate. There are two failure demos, one "representative" run and three Schittkowski problems in the supplement, with no profile over a test set (pp. 18, 20, 27–34). This is the same lapse that SKILL.md records for his 2024 paper.
- new-pattern candidate: **Collapse check.** The new rule and bound reduce to the classical result when the new feature is switched off. Here μ keeps Lemma 3 meaningful when ε_f = ε_g = 0 (p. 11), and with no noise C1 = C2 and ‖g_k‖ → 0 (p. 23). It recurs in S084 (p. 15) and S102 (pp. 5, 8, 11).
- new-pattern candidate: **Two-set neighbourhood template.** C1 is visited infinitely often, and C2 is never left after the first visit (Theorems 6–7, pp. 11–17). The same template appears in S084 (Theorem 3.11, pp. 13–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Two-sided relaxation of the acceptance ratio by rε_f with r = 2/(1 − c2), which keeps "ρ ≈ 1 means good model" (pp. 4–5).
- A "radius must grow" lemma: if ‖g̃‖ > rε_g + γ and Δ ≤ γ/(rM), the step is very successful (Lemma 2, p. 8).
- An excursion bound: count regrowth iterations l = ⌈log_ν(Δ̄/Δ_{K+1})⌉ and sum the radii as a geometric series (pp. 14–16).
- A slack μ that keeps a lemma meaningful at zero noise, absorbed later as μ = ε_g/2 (p. 11).
- A scaling test of a pessimistic bound: log10(bound / observed best accuracy over seeds) on a noise grid (pp. 21–22).
- Identical noise realizations in both arms of a comparison (p. 21).
- A four-panel diagnostic figure: true gradient norm with the noise line, radius, distance to solution, clipped ρ_k (p. 18).

**Quotes**:

- "We cannot, however, recommend this type of trust region reduction as a general procedure for handling noise since failures can happen unexpectedly." (p. 21)

### S084 · Constrained optimization in the presence of noise (SIAM Journal on Optimization 33(3), 2118–2136, 2023, DOI 10.1137/21M1450999; arXiv:2110.04355)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole arXiv v1 text, pp. 1–22 (abstract, §§1–5, acknowledgement, references), Tables 4.1–4.6, and the captions of Figure 4.1 (the figure itself is an image).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Figen Oztoprak (Artelys Corporation), Richard Byrd (University of Colorado, Boulder) (p. 1); Shigeng Sun thanked for a careful reading (p. 20) · **Place in trajectory**: the constrained branch of the noise programme, described as "a first step in this investigation" (p. 3). It was motivated by KNITRO runs (pp. 2–3), and S082 cites it as its constrained precursor (S082 p. 2). The paper promises a forthcoming local-behaviour paper that will sharpen the bounds (p. 20). This text does not say which later paper that is.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: min f(x) s.t. c(x) = 0, solved by SQP with an ℓ1 merit function while seeing only noisy f̃, c̃ and their noisy derivatives (pp. 1–2). The stated goal is to find "the aspects of the algorithm that are most susceptible to errors (or noise)—and redesign them" (p. 2). The applied motive is finite-difference derivatives [15] (p. 2). The empirical entry: the authors injected noise into challenging equality- and inequality-constrained problems, used noise-aware finite differences, and ran KNITRO. For low noise the answers were acceptable, "even though one might suspect the default algorithm to be brittle in this setting". At higher noise the solution quality fell (pp. 2–3). Theory came next: "To guide this investigation, it is essential to develop a convergence theory" (p. 3). The scope is narrowed on purpose to equality constraints and a classical SQP method, "a simple method in this setting", with noise that cannot be reduced (p. 3).
  - Transferable: enter a new regime by running the production solver on a few hard problems with injected noise, record where quality breaks, then narrow to the simplest problem class and method that still shows the challenge (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm 1, "Noise Tolerant SQP Algorithm" (p. 5). It uses H_k = β_k I, a QP step (1.4)–(1.5), least-squares multipliers (2.2) and the classical penalty update (2.3) from *Numerical Optimization*. When π is increased it is at least doubled ("increased substantially"), which makes it eventually constant (p. 4). Backtracking by halving (p. 11) enforces the relaxed Armijo condition φ̃(x_k + α_k d_k) ≤ φ̃(x_k) + να_k ℓ̃(x_k; d_k) + R with R = 2(ε_f + π_k ε_c) (p. 5). Because of R, "the line search cannot fail" (p. 5). The paper calls this "The only unconventional part of Algorithm 1" (p. 18). The contribution is stated as "introducing a relaxation in the line search procedure while keeping all other components of the SQP method unchanged" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: set the relaxation margin to the largest change noise alone can cause in the merit function (R = 2(ε_f + πε_c)). The test then cannot fail through noise, and the proof has its constant (pp. 5, 11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumptions 3.1: Lipschitz gradients of f and of each c_i (p. 5). Assumptions 3.2: bounded noise in f, c, g and J, in stated norms (p. 6). Assumptions 3.3: b_l ≤ β_k ≤ b_u, σ_min(J_k) ≥ γ > ε_J, and bounded sequences {f_k}, {‖c_k‖}, {‖g_k‖}, {‖J_k‖} (p. 6). The noise is bounded, which is "not always the case in practice (e.g. when noise is Gaussian)". Its level is assumed known or estimable (p. 2). The authors admit several conveniences. H_k = β_k I is used "because allowing more general choices introduces more constants in the analysis without contributing to the main goals" (p. 2). With a general H_k, the least-squares multiplier rule (2.3) "will not lead to a convergent method" (p. 4). A unique QP solution is "admittedly a strong assumption" that leaves out regularization and trust regions (p. 5). Safeguards against an unbounded merit function are left out "for simplicity" (p. 6). Without strong convexity the supremum w in C2 could be infinite, "an unlikely scenario" (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) The descent lemma is applied to the true (noise-free) merit function φ, and the analysis works with ℓ(x; s) (pp. 6–7). (b) The step is split into a range-space part v_k and a null-space part u_k (3.12). The bounds use Stewart's perturbation bounds for projections, ‖P̃_k − P_k‖ ≤ ε_J/γ (Lemma 3.4, pp. 7–8). (c) Lemma 3.5 shows that a large enough π makes ℓ(x_k; d_k) negative up to noise terms (p. 8). Corollaries 3.6–3.7 restate this with the stationarity measure ψ_π(x) = ‖P(x)g(x)‖²/b_u + πτ‖c(x)‖₁ (pp. 9–10). (d) Since π increases by at least doubling and the multipliers are bounded, π_k = π̄ for k ≥ k0. The rest of the analysis is run at fixed π̄ (p. 10). (e) |ℓ̃ − ℓ| is bounded by a constant ε_ℓ (p. 11). Theorem 3.8 gives a step-length threshold α̂_k, and halving then gives α_k ≥ ᾱ (pp. 11–12). (f) The critical region C1 is the set where at least one of the three sufficient-progress conditions (3.43), (3.39), (3.41) fails (Definition 3.9, p. 13). C2 is a level set (Definition 3.10, p. 14). Theorem 3.11: the iterates enter C1, stay in C2 and return to C1 infinitely often (p. 14). (g) §3.5 unpacks the constants. E and ε_ℓ scale like δ = 1/(γ − ε_J), 1/b_l and η/b_l, so near-rank-deficient Jacobians enlarge C1 (p. 15). With m = 0 the conditions reduce to those of Berahas et al. [1] (p. 15).
  - Transferable: define the "noise-dominated" region as the union of the places where each sufficient-progress inequality of your proof fails. The main theorem then has a single region to state, and every term of it can be traced back (Definition 3.9, p. 13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Implemented in Python with ν = 0.1, τ = 0.9 and β_k = 50 (p. 15). The experiments are meant to "supplement the theoretical results" by reporting ‖x_k − x*‖, "it suffices to test only a few examples" (pp. 15–16). Three small CUTEst problems are used: HS7, BT11 and HS40 (p. 16). Uniform noise ξ ~ U(−ε1, ε1) is added to f and c, and ψ ~ U(−ε2, ε2) to each gradient and Jacobian entry. x* comes from a run with exact gradients (p. 16). Figure 4.1 plots log2‖x_k − x*‖ over 1000 iterations at ε1 = ε2 = 10⁻³. The penalty parameter became fixed "within the first 15 iterations" (pp. 16–17). Ablation: the relaxation is switched off and on at ε = 10⁻⁵, 10⁻³ and 10⁻¹. Disabled, the line search fails near x* (for example HS7 at iteration 77 with best distance 7.8e−3). Enabled, it never fails (HS7 reaches 4.9e−8 by k = 500 at ε = 10⁻⁵) (Tables 4.1–4.3, p. 18). Mis-estimation stress test: the noise estimates are set to 10, 100 and 1000 times too large or too small. Runs use a "naive modification of termination tests in standard packages" (4.1), and each end is marked (opt) or (ls) (pp. 18–19, Tables 4.4–4.6). Underestimation causes line-search failures, and overestimation stops the run early. At ε = 10⁻¹, underestimation by a factor of 10 ran longer and ended closer to x* before failing (for example HS7 2.6e−4 against 2.7e−2), and the authors note that (4.1) may then never be satisfied (p. 19). Summary: "over-and underestimation of the noise levels can be harmful in ways that are dependent on the implementation" (p. 19). The text refers to the tables as "Figures 4.6–4.4" (p. 19).
  - Transferable: an ablation table with the repair disabled (iteration of failure, best accuracy) against enabled at several iteration budgets, at three noise levels (Tables 4.1–4.3, p. 18).
  - Transferable: stress the estimate your method depends on by running with it 10×, 100× and 1000× too large and too small, and mark how each run ended (optimality test or failure) (Tables 4.4–4.6, p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Early work: Polyak's 1978 review of penalty and Lagrange methods under noise (p. 3), Hintermüller's bounding-surrogate SQP, and Schittkowski's non-monotone NLPQLP, which is "reported to be successful in practice, but no convergence theory were presented" (p. 3). The closest work is the stochastic SQP line of Berahas, Curtis, Robinson, Zhou and O'Neill [2, 3, 6] (p. 4), which includes his former student Curtis. It is treated respectfully: those papers "give careful attention to the behavior of the penalty parameter", and [2] has "compelling numerical results" (p. 4). The difference is stated plainly. They assume a well-behaved penalty parameter, and they resolve the boundedness issue "by making the same assumption as in this paper, namely that noise is always bounded" (p. 4). The unconstrained relaxed line search of Berahas, Byrd and Nocedal [1] is compared directly with this paper's C1 and C2 (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A "Contributions of this work" subsection has the claim and its hedge side by side: the analysis is deterministic, "yielding somewhat pessimistic bounds", but "only slight modifications are needed" (p. 3). A dedicated discussion section opens "since the critical region C1 defined in (3.44) is complex" and turns the region into orders of magnitude and conditioning effects (p. 15). Strong assumptions are labelled as such where they are made ("admittedly a strong assumption", p. 5). The final remarks open with "Two questions guided this research" and close with a list of open problems (p. 20). A label slip: the tables are called "Figures 4.6–4.4" (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is mainly theory (p. 3): convergence to and retention in noise-determined neighbourhoods for a minimally modified SQP, plus small experiments showing that the relaxation helps (p. 18). The limits are explicit and specific. The analysis needs a Jacobian that is "never close to singular" (p. 20). The constants C_g and C_c and the penalty value π̄ can be large and depend on x0 (p. 15). The analysed algorithm has no regularization and no trust region (p. 5). Future work is named. A practical globalized algorithm is "the subject of future work" (p. 5). Sharper accuracy bounds will come from a local analysis (p. 20). The open problems are "the thorny issue of how to design a proper stop test", singularity, a nondiagonal Hessian, and noisy inequality constraints (p. 20). Robust implementations "should include" a recovery procedure like that of Berahas et al. [1] (pp. 19–20).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (evidence ✅, strong): the guiding question is the minimal modification of a classical algorithm "when the noise level can be estimated" (p. 20). One component is changed and the rest kept (p. 3). The noise level is explicit (p. 2). The repair is ablated: with the relaxation off, the line search fails near x* (p. 18, step 5). There is also a stress test on the estimate (pp. 18–19), which goes beyond step 5.
- Method 3 (variant ⚠, step 4): the recovery tree is named as necessary ("Robust implementations … should include such features", p. 20) but is not built. The stop test (4.1) is called naive (p. 18).
- Method 4 (evidence ✅): failure modes and open choices are written into the paper as next work: globalization (p. 5), local bounds, stop test, singularity, nondiagonal Hessian, inequality constraints (p. 20). The paper itself began from a KNITRO failure observation (pp. 2–3).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠, step 1): the version analysed is a stripped SQP (H = βI, unique QP, no trust region or regularization), not the code run in KNITRO, and the authors note that the penalty rule would not converge with a general H_k (pp. 2, 4–5). No bound is plotted against runs. The experiments measure ‖x − x*‖ while the theory is stated in φ (p. 15). The pessimism of the bounds is admitted (p. 3).
- Method 1 (contradiction ✗, step 7): three small CUTEst problems with no aggregate and no rival code, declared as illustrations, "it suffices to test only a few examples" (pp. 15–16).
- new-pattern candidate: **Two-set neighbourhood template** (C1 entered and revisited infinitely often, C2 never left; Theorem 3.11, p. 14). It is shared with S082 (pp. 11–17), and the paper notes the analogous level set in Berahas et al. [1] (p. 15).
- new-pattern candidate: **Collapse check.** With m = 0, C1 reduces to the conditions of the unconstrained analysis [1] (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A relaxation margin equal to the largest change noise can cause in the merit function, so the backtracking line search "cannot fail" (p. 5).
- A penalty update that at least doubles when it increases, so π is eventually constant and the analysis can run at a fixed π̄ (pp. 4, 10).
- Define the critical region as the union of the failure sets of the proof's sufficient-progress conditions (Definition 3.9, p. 13).
- After a complex theorem, add a section that reduces its region to orders of magnitude and conditioning dependences (δ, 1/b_l, σ_min(J)) and checks it against the unconstrained special case (p. 15).
- Ablation tables (repair on and off, several budgets, three noise levels) and an estimate-misspecification grid with ending-type markers (opt)/(ls) (pp. 18–19).
- Simplify the analysed method (H_k = β_k I), say why, and state what would break in the general case (pp. 2, 4).

**Quotes**:

### S102 · Constrained and composite optimization via adaptive sampling methods (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 44(2), 680–709, 2024, DOI 10.1093/imanum/drad020; arXiv:2012.15411)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole arXiv v1 text, pp. 1–16 (abstract, §§1–6, references), and Appendix A, pp. 17–26. The appendix consists of figure captions only, since the plots are images.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yuchen Xie (Northwestern IEMS), Raghu Bollapragada (University of Texas at Austin, Mechanical Engineering), Richard Byrd (University of Colorado, Boulder) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: carries the adaptive-sampling line of his machine-learning period (the norm test of Byrd, Chin, Nocedal and Wu [7]; the inner-product test of Bollapragada, Byrd and Nocedal [5, 6]; p. 3) into constrained and composite problems. Posted at the end of 2020, inside the noise period. S082's final remarks connect the two lines: when noise can be reduced, the bounded-noise method can be restarted with smaller errors, as in the sampling setting [13, 6, 7] (S082 p. 23).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is min f(x) s.t. x ∈ Ω, with f stochastic and Ω a deterministic convex set (p. 1). It is generalized to the composite problem min f + h with h convex and possibly nonsmooth (pp. 1, 3). The entry is a named failure of the existing mechanism. The norm test (1.3) "lies behind most algorithms and theory of adaptive sampling methods" (p. 1). Under constraints it fails "because it is based on pointwise decisions that cannot correctly predict the quality of the proximal gradient step" (abstract, p. 1). Figure 1.1 is a one-picture counterexample: a strongly convex quadratic with one linear constraint, iterate on the boundary near x*, where ‖∇f(x_k)‖ stays large. The test therefore does not force the gradient error to zero, and an estimate that passes it gives a poor step (p. 2). A second critique is aimed at a priori sample growth: with a geometric rate a^k, behaviour "depends very strongly on the value of a, and there are no clear guidelines on how to choose it" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: open with the smallest geometric example in which the field's standard test passes and the step is still bad (here one linear constraint, Fig. 1.1, p. 2). It justifies the new test in one picture.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Judge the gradient estimate by the full step it produces, not at a point. The test is Var_k[g_k] ≤ ξ‖(E_k[x_{k+1}] − x_k)/α_k‖² (1.4), which compares the error with the projected or proximal step instead of with ‖∇f‖ (pp. 2–3). Because this needs a look-ahead, the natural framework is proximal gradient (p. 3). The algorithm runs in two stages. It computes a trial prox step with the current sample, sets a new sample size, and if the size grew, draws only the extra samples and recomputes the step (Algorithm 1, p. 5; Algorithm 2, p. 12). An explicit design requirement: "when h is not present, Algorithm 1 should reduce to the iteration (1.2)-(1.3)", the norm-test method (p. 5). In the practical rule, the sample variance (3.14) replaces the population variance, and the trial-step norm replaces ‖E_k[x_{k+1}] − x_k‖ (pp. 11–12). S_k = max{a, S}, at the cost of one prox evaluation and S stochastic gradients (pp. 12–13). An inner-product variant (4.3) asks only for descent with high probability (p. 13). Kept unchanged: the prox-gradient iteration, unbiased mini-batch gradients and a fixed step (pp. 4–5, 7).
  - Transferable: when a pointwise accuracy test breaks under a projection or prox, move the test to the output. Compare the estimator's variance with the squared length of the step it generates, computed with a trial step from the current sample (pp. 3, 11–12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumptions 3.1: f is [μ, L]-smooth (strongly convex with Lipschitz gradient), and h is closed, convex and proper (p. 6). Assumptions 3.4, for the sublinear result: f convex with L-Lipschitz gradient (p. 8). g_k is unbiased and adapted to the filtration (3.3) (p. 6). The step is fixed at α_k = (1 − η)/L (p. 7). The authors admit several conveniences. Condition (3.6) is "the most general condition we have identified", but "it does not seem to be possible to enforce this condition in practice, even approximately" (p. 8). The computable (3.10) is "more restrictive" (p. 11). The practical rule approximates (3.10) (pp. 11–12) and holds only approximately "for large S" (pp. 12–13). The inner-product test treats ḡ_k and d̄_k "as independent while they are not" (p. 13). Nonconvex f is outside the assumptions (pp. 6, 8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Lemma 3.2 is a one-step inequality from the L-smooth upper model and the prox optimality, evaluated at the comparison point x̃_k = βx* + (1 − β)x_k with β = μα_k. With that choice the strong-convexity "Term 1" cancels exactly (pp. 6–7). (b) Theorem 3.3: under (3.6), Q-linear convergence in expectation with factor 1 − (1 − η)μ/L (p. 7). For h = 0, (3.6) reduces to the norm test with ξ = η/(1 − η) (p. 8). (c) Lemma 3.5 and Theorem 3.6 give, in the convex case, E[φ_k − φ*] ≤ L‖x0 − x*‖²/(2(1 − η)k) by telescoping ‖x_k − x*‖², with monotone expected values from z = x_k (pp. 8–9). (d) Theorem 3.7 turns (3.6) into a computable condition. The bias-variance split E‖x_{k+1} − x_k‖² ≥ ‖E x_{k+1} − x_k‖² bounds one side. For the other, the deterministic point x̂_{k+1} = prox(x_k − α_k∇f_k) is inserted and the nonexpansiveness of prox is used, so the cross term is at most α_k Var_k[g_k] (p. 10). (e) The sample-size rule (3.13) and Corollary 3.8 follow from Var = population variance / S_k (p. 11).
  - Transferable: to control a stochastic cross term (∇f − g)ᵀ(x_{k+1} − x_k), subtract the step taken with the true gradient. That step is measurable, so its cross term has zero mean, and prox nonexpansiveness bounds the rest by α·Var (p. 10).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The test problem is binary classification with ℓ1-regularized logistic loss, λ = 1/N (p. 13), on six datasets: covertype, gisette, ijcnn, MNIST, mushrooms and sido (Table 5.1, p. 14). Three batch-size strategies are compared: GEOMETRIC, S_k = ⌈S0(1 + γ)^k⌉ with γ varied; NORM, rule (3.15); and IP, rule (4.3). Each strategy's step is chosen as the best value from the set {2⁻¹⁰, 2⁻⁷, …, 2¹⁵}. S0 = 2. The stop is ‖x_{k+1} − x_k‖/α_k ≤ 10⁻⁸ or 100 epochs. φ* comes from 50,000 iterations of deterministic proximal gradient (p. 14). Plots show the optimality gap against effective gradient evaluations Σ_j S_j/N, and the batch fraction against iterations. Each strategy has a parameter-sweep panel plus a panel comparing the best run of each (p. 14; Fig. 5.1 caption, p. 15). Mushrooms is in the main text and the other five datasets are in Appendix A (pp. 14, 17–26). Findings: IP is the most efficient in effective gradient evaluations. NORM is comparable to the best geometric runs. "whereas the geometric strategy can be quite sensitive to the choice of γ, the norm and inner product tests are fairly insensitive to the choice of η" (p. 14). Not reported: CPU times, and the number of random repetitions per configuration (p. 14).
  - Transferable: for an adaptive rule against an a priori schedule, show each method's parameter sweep next to the best-run comparison. Insensitivity to the rule's own parameter, against the schedule's sensitivity, is the practical argument (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the norm-test lineage: Carter's deterministic version [8], Friedlander and Schmidt's geometric growth [11], the stochastic norm test of Byrd et al. [7] (his own), the inner-product test of Bollapragada et al. [5] (his own), and Cartis and Scheinberg [9], Pasupathy et al. [16], Roosta-Khorasani and Mahoney [17, 18] and De et al. [10] (p. 3). Inexact proximal-gradient studies [19] "do not propose an automatic procedure for improving the quality of the gradient" (pp. 3–4). The rival a priori schedule [13] is criticized for having no guidance on its rate (p. 3). There is an explicit priority note: "N.B." says that [1] (Beiser et al.), on a similar subject, had just been posted, and that the two papers differ (p. 3, footnote p. 4).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract runs motivation → why the existing mechanism fails → the look-ahead fix → results for strongly convex and convex f → experiments (p. 1). The failure picture comes early (p. 2). After the theorems the text twice checks that the result collapses to the known case ("it is reassuring that when h = 0, condition (3.10) reduces to the norm test", p. 11; also p. 8). Caveats are candid and placed where they arise (pp. 8, 13). The final remarks name the cornerstone condition (3.6) and one open question (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are an algorithm (look-ahead adaptive sampling for proximal gradient, plus a practical inner-product variant) and theory (linear convergence in expectation for strongly convex f, O(1/k) for convex f, under (3.6) or (3.10); pp. 7–11). Stated limits: (3.10) is more restrictive than (3.6), and it is open "whether there is a condition with similar properties as (3.6), that is amenable to computation and less restrictive than (3.10)" (p. 14). The inner-product test has no theory (p. 13). Further limits visible in the text: convex f only (pp. 6, 8), one problem class (p. 13), and a step-size grid search in the experiments while the theory fixes α = (1 − η)/L (pp. 7, 14). The paper does not comment on this last gap.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (evidence ✅, stochastic analogue): the engine (proximal gradient with unbiased sampled gradients) is kept. The component that breaks under constraints, the sample-size control, is replaced (pp. 1–3). The repair is driven by explicit estimates, the sample variance and the trial step (pp. 11–12). It has a built-in recovery path: if the trial step shows the sample is too small, resample and recompute the step, reusing the old samples (Algorithm 2, step 3, p. 12; p. 5). The failure is shown first on one easy, deliberately chosen picture (Fig. 1.1, p. 2; also Heuristic 1).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠): the rival a priori schedule is the baseline and is given a γ sweep. Every method gets the same step-size grid. Cost is counted in effective gradient evaluations, and all six datasets are reported (pp. 14, 17–26). But nothing is handicapped on his side, and the summary panel compares best runs (p. 15).
- Method 5 (variant ⚠, step 1): the theorems cover the idealized condition (3.10), while the code runs its approximation (3.15) and the theory-free inner-product test (pp. 11–13). No bound is plotted against runs, and the standard rates do not separate NORM from GEOMETRIC theoretically (pp. 7–11, 14).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅, step 5): what cannot be proved is kept as a stated open question (p. 14) and an admitted heuristic (the independence caveat, p. 13).
- new-pattern candidate: **Collapse check.** It is stated as a design requirement here: the new test must reduce to the norm test when h = 0 (p. 5), and this is checked after Theorems 3.3 and 3.7 (pp. 8, 11). It recurs in S082 (pp. 11, 23) and S084 (p. 15).
- new-pattern candidate: **Look-ahead accuracy test.** Accept an inexact quantity only if the step it produces is long compared with its error, computed from a cheap trial step (pp. 2–3, 11–12). Only this paper in the batch shows it.

**Transferable techniques**:
- A one-picture counterexample showing that the standard test passes while the step is bad (Fig. 1.1, p. 2).
- A test on the step instead of the point: Var(g) ≤ ξ‖(x_{k+1} − x_k)/α‖², evaluated with a trial prox step from the current sample, with extra samples drawn only when needed (pp. 3, 5, 12).
- Find the most general sufficient condition first (3.6), then a computable, stronger one (3.10), and say which is which (pp. 8, 10–11).
- A comparison point x̃ = μα x* + (1 − μα)x_k that cancels the strong-convexity term (pp. 6–7).
- Bound the stochastic cross term with a measurable true-gradient prox step and nonexpansiveness (p. 10).
- Report against effective gradient evaluations Σ S_j/N, with batch-fraction plots and per-method parameter sweeps (p. 14).
