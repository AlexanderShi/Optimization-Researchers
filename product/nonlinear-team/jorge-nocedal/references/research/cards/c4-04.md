### S078 · Assessing the potential of interior methods for nonlinear optimization (Large-Scale PDE-Constrained Optimization 2003, pp. 167–183, doi:10.1007/978-3-642-55508-4_10)
**Read level**: full; this is the second read (round-1 card in c04). I read all 17 file pages of the OCR text:
- abstract and §1 (pp. 1–3);
- §2, the overview of the codes (pp. 3–6);
- §3, the testing environment (pp. 6–7);
- §§4–5, results by problem class (pp. 7–15);
- §6, final remarks with Table 1 (pp. 15–16), and the acknowledgments;
- the references (pp. 16–17), skimmed.

On the PDF page images I checked pp. 3, 6, 7, 9, 12, 14 and 15: the exponent of the tolerance, the LOQO barrier rule (11), the signs in "n ≥ 100" and "n ≥ 50", and the two Table 1 rows that the OCR garbles. The six performance-profile plots were not digitised; only their captions (problem counts) are used.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales (ITAM), Richard A. Waltz, Guanghui Liu, Jean-Pierre Goux (Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: as in c04. KNITRO's authors test KNITRO 1.00 against LOQO 4.05, SNOPT 5.3-4 and FilterSQP 1.0 (pp. 3–5). The runs date from fall 2000 (p. 7, footnote 1), so the 2003 chapter reports data that were two to three years old at publication.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: unchanged from c04: are the interior methods in LOQO and KNITRO competitive with established large-scale SQP codes, and where are they weak (pp. 1–2)? The size range is now readable: problems from CUTE "that have up to 10,000 variables or constraints" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and study design**: Codes are chosen to differ along named axes (p. 2), and each class is reduced to its algorithmic core (pp. 7, 10, 12–13), as in c04. The numbers and formulas the prose card lacked:
  - **LOQO 4.05** (pp. 3–4) is a line-search primal-dual method that factors the primal-dual matrix (3).
    - Merit function: the ℓ2 penalty ψ = f − μ Σ ln sᵢ + ν‖g(x) + s‖₂² (4). The norm is squared, which I read on the page image of p. 3. The penalty ν is set by a heuristic that starts from ν = 0 (p. 4).
    - Barrier parameter: μ is redefined at every iteration by rule (11), μ = 0.1·min(…)³·sᵀy/q with ξ = minᵢ sᵢyᵢ/(yᵀs/q) (p. 12, read on the page image). The rule lets μ increase between iterations (p. 12).
    - Nonconvexity is handled by adding a multiple of the identity, which may take several refactorizations (p. 4).
  - **KNITRO 1.00** (pp. 4–5, 12–13) solves the barrier subproblems with a trust-region SQP step: a Byrd–Omojokun normal step plus a projected-CG tangential step.
    - Barrier parameter: μ falls "by a fixed factor of five" after each barrier subproblem (p. 4), and the initial μ is always 0.1 (p. 15).
    - Merit function: f − μ Σ ln sᵢ + ν‖g(x) + s‖₂ (6), which is non-squared and non-differentiable (p. 4).
    - Cost per iteration: one factorization of the projection matrix (5), ncg + 2 backsolves and ncg Hessian–vector products (p. 13).
  - **SNOPT 5.3-4** (p. 5) is a line-search SQP code with an augmented-Lagrangian merit function (7). It keeps a dense BFGS approximation of the reduced Hessian and switches to limited-memory BFGS when n is large.
  - **FilterSQP 1.0** (pp. 5–6) is a trust-region SQP code with an ℓ∞ trust region (page image p. 7), a filter and a restoration phase.
  - **Theory status**: the reference numbers are now legible. KNITRO's global theory is "[4]", which is Byrd, Gilbert and Nocedal, Math. Program. 89(1) 2000, i.e. S009. Its rate theory is "[6]", Byrd, Liu and Nocedal 1997 (pp. 5, 16). The round-1 S009 card had to infer the KNITRO→S009 link; the text states it, as theory for "an algorithm similar to that implemented in KNITRO" (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model (test environment)**:
  - **Test set** (p. 6): 509 problems, a subset of the 740 problems in the Benson–Vanderbei AMPL translation of CUTE as of December 2000. LP, QP, feasibility and bound-only problems are excluded, and default dimensions are used. The class sizes come from Table 1 and the figure captions: 187 unconstrained (95 with n ≥ 100), 64 equality-constrained (27) and 258 general (67) (pp. 8–15). By my sum, 187 + 64 + 258 = 509.
  - **Platform** (pp. 6–7): a Sun Ultra 5 with 385Mb of memory, running SunOS 5.7, in double precision, with all codes at their default settings.
  - **Limits** (p. 7): 30 minutes of CPU time and 1000 major iterations; reaching either counts as a failure. The stopping tolerance is 10⁻⁶ on all solvers (the OCR prints "10-®"; read on the page image).
  - **Stopping tests** (p. 6): KNITRO's test was modified to be almost identical to SNOPT's. "Some differences in final precision were noticed, but these were not significant."
  - **CPU reporting**: CPU times are reported only "for problems with more than 100 variables" (p. 6). However, p. 9 and Table 1 print n ≥ 100 (page images), a small inconsistency inside the paper.
  - **Derivative disparity**: SNOPT uses first derivatives only. The tests ran in fall 2000, when no second-derivative SNOPT existed (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis techniques (no proofs)**:
  - **Performance profile** (p. 8): defined as r_{p,s} = t_{p,s}/t*_p, with a large M for failures, and π_s(τ) = the share of problems with log₂ r_{p,s} ≤ τ (8)–(9). It is plotted for τ ∈ [0, 12] (pp. 8–14). The source cited is the Dolan–Moré Argonne report ANL/MCS-P861-1200, dated 2001 (ref. [10], p. 16).
  - **Size-artefact test** (p. 14): 16 CUTE problems, each in three versions "with increasing dimensions ranging generally in the thousands", run with KNITRO and SNOPT. There was no clear trend.
  - **Iteration counts** (p. 14): among problems with n ≥ 50 (page image), KNITRO needed fewer iterations than LOQO in about 2/3 of them. Iterations are "not reported in our tables".
  - **Accounting corrections**, as in c04: LOQO's extra evaluation is discounted (p. 10, footnote 2), and SNOPT's possible undercount is noted (p. 14).

  - **The paper's caveats**: LOQO's unconstrained stopping test is "often less demanding" (p. 10), and SNOPT's evaluation counts are not comparable when the objective is linear (p. 14).
  - **Figures** (Figs. 1–6, pp. 8–14): CPU profiles over 95, 27 and 67 problems, and function-evaluation profiles over 187, 64 and 258.

  - **KNITRO's own weaknesses, now with their parameters**: the fixed initial μ = 0.1 "can be highly detrimental in some cases" (p. 15); its time per iteration is higher than LOQO's on "a significant percentage" of the problems (p. 15); the CG cost grows on ill-conditioned problems (p. 9).

### S061 · On the convergence of Newton iterations to non-stationary points (Mathematical Programming 99(1), 127–148, 2004, doi:10.1007/s10107-003-0376-8)
**Read level**: full; this is the second read (round-1 card in c04). I read all 25 file pages of the OCR text of Report OTC 2001/01, dated April 23, 2001 (p. 1):
- the introduction (pp. 2–3);
- nonlinear equations: Theorems 2.2–2.3, Examples 1–2 and §2.1 on regularization (pp. 3–13);
- unconstrained minimization: Lemma 3.2 (pp. 13–16);
- constrained optimization, §§4.1–4.3 (pp. 16–24);
- the references (pp. 24–25), skimmed.

Where the OCR garbles formulas or table cells, I checked the PDF page images: pp. 3–5, 7, 10–14, 17–18 and 20–23. The journal version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Marcelo Marazzi (Northwestern IEMS) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the report is dated April 23, 2001 (p. 1). That is about a year after Wächter and Biegler's failure example appeared in Math. Program. 88(3), 2000 (ref. [14], p. 25). The journal version followed in 2004.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: as in c04. The data are now readable:
  - **Powell's example** is F(x, y) = (x, 10x/(x + 0.1) + 2y²)ᵀ with unique solution (0, 0). Started at (3, 1) with an exact line search, the iterates converge to z ≈ (1.8016, 0.0000), and F′ is singular along the whole x-axis (pp. 3–4; the formula and the start were read on the page images).
  - **Fletcher's question** is quoted from *Practical Methods of Optimization*, 2nd ed., p. 121 (p. 14; ref. [5], p. 25).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: the theorem statements and algorithm the prose card could not give.
  - **Definition 2.1** (p. 3): z is a singular non-stationary point when F(z) ≠ 0, F′(z) is singular and ∇φ(z) ≠ 0.
  - **Theorem 2.2** (p. 5): α_k is the first local minimiser of φ = ½‖F‖². The point z satisfies (2.6a), rank F′(z) = n − 1, and (2.6b), F(z) ∉ R(F′(z)) (∉ read on the page image).
    - (i) If D²φ(z; v_n) = Σᵢ Fᵢ(z) v_nᵀ∇²Fᵢ(z) v_n > 0 (2.8), then for every ε > 0 there is a δ > 0 such that ‖x_{k+1} − x_k‖ ≤ ε whenever ‖x_k − z‖ ≤ δ and F′(x_k) is nonsingular.
    - (ii) If D²φ(z; v_n) < 0, then for all sufficiently small δ there is a T > 0 with ‖x_{k+1} − x_k‖ ≥ T.
  - **Extensions** (pp. 8, 10):
    - The line-search condition extends to the first interval satisfying the Wolfe conditions (2.25), with 0 < η < β < 1 (p. 8).
    - **Theorem 2.3** (p. 10) keeps only (2.6b). It then asks whether Σᵢ Fᵢ(z)∇²Fᵢ(z) (2.26) is positive definite on the null space of F′(z), which gives small displacements, or negative definite, which keeps them bounded below.
  - **The range hypothesis is needed** (pp. 10–11). Two systems make the point:
    - **Conclusion fails**: F(x, y) = (x² + y², y²)ᵀ at z = (2, 0). Here (2.6a) and (2.8) hold and (2.6b) fails; the Newton step points straight at (0, 0), so the displacement stays bounded below.
    - **Example 2, conclusion holds anyway**: F(x, y) = (x + y², 2(x − 1)y)ᵀ. The iterates stay on T = {(1, y)}, where ‖∇φ(1, y)‖ = (1 + y²)√(1 + 4y²) ≥ 1 (2.30).
  - **Lemma 3.2** (p. 14, page image): f is C², ∇²f(x_k) is positive definite for all k, and the steps satisfy the Wolfe conditions (3.3). If z is a singular non-stationary point (Definition 3.1: ∇f(z) ≠ 0, ∇²f(z) positive semidefinite and singular) with rank ∇²f(z) = n − 1 and ∇f(z) ∉ R(∇²f(z)), then the iterates stay bounded away from z.
  - **Algorithm 1** (p. 20, page image) is a feasible primal-dual method for fixed μ:
    - B_k = ∇²L(x_k, λ_k) + A_kG_k⁻¹Λ_kA_kᵀ, or a modification of it;
    - the step solves B_k d_k = −∇ψ_μ(x_k);
    - α_k satisfies the Wolfe conditions on ψ_μ;
    - the multiplier step is the largest α̂_k ∈ [0, 1] with λ_{k+1} ≥ (1 − τ)λ_k, τ ∈ (0, 1), "say" 0.995.
  - **Theorem 4.1** (p. 20): under A1–A3, every limit point of Algorithm 1 is a stationary point of the barrier function ψ_μ (4.2).
  - **Safeguard** (p. 22): multipliers capped at (λ̂_k)ᵢ = min{(λ_k)ᵢ, cμ/gᵢ(x_k)} with c > 1, plus a modified Cholesky factorization. Both switch off near a regular stationary point of the barrier problem.
  - **Example 3** (p. 17): min ⅓(x − 1)³ + x subject to x ≥ 0, with F_μ(x, λ) = ((x − 1)² + 1 − λ, xλ − μ)ᵀ and μ = 0.01. Its unique solution, about (5 × 10⁻³, 1.99), is the only stationary point of φ_μ = ½‖F_μ‖².
  - **Example 4** (p. 23) has the constraints ½[x + y + √2 z + (y − x)²] = 0 and (√2/2)(x + y + √2 z − 2)(y − x) = 0, plus the bound z ≥ −1. An affine change of variables maps it onto Example 2. The iterates stay in the plane P = {(1, y, z)}, where ‖∇φ‖ ≥ 1 (p. 24).
  - **The Wächter–Biegler instance** is restated in its full form (p. 23): x₁² − x₂ − 1 = 0, x₁ − x₃ − 2 = 0, x₂ ≥ 0, x₃ ≥ 0.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: as in c04, now with the exact hypotheses:
  - (2.6a)/(2.6b) and F ∈ C² (pp. 3, 5);
  - first local minimiser or first Wolfe interval; backtracking is excluded (p. 8);
  - positive definite ∇²f(x_k) and rank n − 1 (p. 14);
  - for the constrained case: μ held fixed until the barrier problem is solved (p. 17); strictly feasible iterates; A1, f bounded below on the feasible region; A2, ∇²f, ∇²gᵢ and ∇gᵢ bounded on its convex hull; A3, the eigenvalues of B_k bounded above and away from zero (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques** (constants read on the page images of pp. 7, 21–22):
  - **Long, aligned directions**: ‖d_k‖ → ∞ and d_k/‖d_k‖ → v_n (2.10) (p. 6).
  - **One-dimensional reduction**: h_k(τ) = φ(x_k + τd_k/‖d_k‖), with h′_k(0) = −‖F(x_k)‖²/‖d_k‖ < 0 (2.14) (p. 6).
  - **Case (i)** (p. 7): h″_k(τ) ≥ ½ρ for τ ≤ T (2.19), and |h′_k(0)| < ¼ρT (2.21). The Taylor bound h_k(τ) ≥ h_k(0) + τ[h′_k(0) + ¼ρτ] (2.22) then puts the first local minimiser at τ_k < −4h′_k(0)/ρ, which tends to 0.
  - **Case (ii)** (pp. 7–8): h″_k ≤ ½ρ < 0, so every minimiser lies in [T, ∞).
  - **Lemma 3.2** (pp. 14–16), by contradiction along a subsequence converging to z. The first Wolfe condition forces the displacements τ_k to zero. The curvature condition and the mean-value theorem keep τ_k bounded below, because h′_k(0) stays bounded away from zero.
  - **Theorem 4.1** (pp. 21–22): the iterates stay in S_σ = {x : g(x) ≥ σe}, and ∇ψ_μ is Lipschitz with constant L on S_{σ/2}. Two cases:
    - (a) α_k ≥ (β − 1)∇ψᵀd/(L‖d‖²), so the decrease is at least η(1 − β)/L·(∇ψᵀd/‖d‖)² (4.13)–(4.14).
    - (b) the step is at least σ/(2C) (4.15), so the decrease is at least −(ησ/2C)·∇ψᵀd/‖d‖ (4.16).

    Hence −∇ψᵀd/‖d‖ → 0 (4.17), and the eigenvalue bounds give that this quantity is at least (β₂/β₁)‖∇ψ_μ‖, so ‖∇ψ_μ‖ → 0.
  - **Prediction plot**: the theorem's prediction is plotted as −h′_k(0)/h″_k(0) on the grid [1, 3] × [−1, 1] (Figure 2, p. 9).
  - **Checking the theorem on runs**: at the numerical limit of Example 3 the paper checks (2.6) and (2.8) (p. 18).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: the three tables, unreadable in round 1, now with every value.
  - **Table 1** (p. 12): regularized Newton (2.31), (F′ᵀF′ + ρI)d = −F′ᵀF, used only when the eigenvalues of F′ᵀF′ fall below ρ. It runs on Example 1 from (1.7, 0.1) with backtracking on φ. The run stops when ‖F_k‖ < 10⁻⁶ (exponent read on the page image) or when the line search fails after 100 trials; the iteration limit is 1000. Results:

    | ρ | Iterations | Regularized steps | Final ‖F‖ | Final ‖∇φ‖ |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | 0 | 25 (line search failed) | 0 | 9.585 | 4.662 |
    | √macheps | 1000 (limit) | 495 | 9.585 | 4.662 |
    | 10⁻⁴ | 1000 (limit) | 452 | 9.563 | 4.774 |
    | 10⁻² | 1000 (limit) | 940 | 1.629e−04 | 9.034e−07 |
    | 10⁻¹ | 1000 (limit) | 994 | 1.392e−03 | 1.492e−05 |

    Here macheps = 2.22 × 10⁻¹⁶. The largest eigenvalue of F′ᵀF′ was "of order 100", so ρ = 0.1 is not negligible (p. 12).
