### S004 · Optimization methods for large-scale machine learning (SIAM Review 2018, DOI 10.1137/16M1080173; arXiv:1606.04838)
**Read level**: full. Pp. 1–84 were read in full: abstract, contents, §§1–9, Appendix A (convexity-based SG analyses) and Appendix B (proofs). The reference list (pp. 85–95, 164 entries) was skimmed only to count self-citations and to check what is and is not cited. The version read is arXiv v3 (p. 1), not the typeset journal article.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Léon Bottou (Facebook AI Research, New York) and Frank E. Curtis (Lehigh ISE) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a survey from his machine-learning period. It places the group's earlier ML papers inside one map: dynamic sample sizes [28] (§5.2.1, p. 45), subsampled Hessians [29] (§6.1.1, p. 53) and second-order ℓ1 methods [30, 31] (§8.2, pp. 80–82). These sit alongside Bottou's SG and learning-tradeoff work [18]–[25], [89] (pp. 35, 65). Nocedal's support was ONR and DOE grants (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters through the application field, not through a gap in a theorem. Three questions organize it: how optimization problems arise in ML and what makes them hard; which methods have been most successful and why; and what the recent advances and open questions are (p. 3). The first question is answered with two case studies. One is text classification as convex regularized logistic regression on RCV1 (d = 47,152; pp. 4–6, (2.3)). The other is DNNs for speech and vision, which are nonconvex with tens of millions of parameters (pp. 6–9, (2.5)). Learning theory then turns this into the object to optimize: expected risk R(w) = E[f(w; ξ)] against empirical risk Rn (pp. 9–15, (3.1)–(3.6)). The thesis comes in the introduction: "large-scale machine learning represents a distinctive setting in which traditional nonlinear optimization techniques typically falter" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: open a methods paper for a new application field with that field's own case studies. Then restate them as an optimization object (here expected vs empirical risk) under which the advantage of the field's dominant method, and its limits, can be proved.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Two moves. (i) An organizing map. Fig. 3.3 (p. 20) puts SG at the origin. Noise-reduction methods lie on the horizontal axis (dynamic sampling, gradient aggregation, iterate averaging; Fig. 5.1, p. 40). Second-order methods lie on the vertical axis (diagonal scaling, quasi-Newton, Gauss–Newton, Hessian-free Newton, natural gradient; Fig. 6.1, p. 51). Batch Newton sits in the far corner, and combinations off the axes are invited (p. 21). (ii) One generalized SG algorithm (Algorithm 4.1, p. 22). Its direction g(wk, ξk) may be a stochastic gradient, a mini-batch gradient, or Hk times a mini-batch gradient ((4.2), p. 22). The theory therefore covers stochastic Newton and quasi-Newton steps, provided the expected direction gives sufficient descent (p. 22). When a classical engine is adapted, the engine is kept and only the noise-sensitive part is changed. Newton-CG keeps CG and an Armijo search and samples the Hessian more lightly than the gradient (Algorithm 6.1, p. 53), because "the iteration is more tolerant to noise in the Hessian estimate than it is to noise in the gradient estimate" (p. 53). L-BFGS keeps the two-loop recursion. Its curvature pairs come either from the same sample at both points ((6.13), p. 57) or from a subsampled Hessian-vector product vk ((6.14)), computed separately from the step and refreshed every 10 or 20 iterations ("SQN", p. 58; Algorithm 6.2, p. 59).
  - Transferable: to move a quasi-Newton method to noisy gradients, keep the step formula and repair only the curvature pairs. Form them on a separate, larger sample or from Hessian-vector products, and update them only every L iterations so the cost is spread out (pp. 57–58).
  - Transferable: give different components different sample sizes according to how much noise each tolerates. Here the Hessian sample is smaller than the gradient sample (p. 53), and the method is "only recommended when Sk is large" (p. 54).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The standing assumptions are the following.
  - Assumption 4.1: F has an L-Lipschitz gradient (p. 23).
  - Assumption 4.3 limits the first and second moments: F is bounded below over the iterates; ∇F(wk)ᵀE[g] ≥ µ‖∇F(wk)‖² and ‖E[g]‖ ≤ µG‖∇F(wk)‖; and V[g] ≤ M + MV‖∇F(wk)‖² (p. 24). This allows nonzero variance at stationary points and quadratic growth (p. 24).
  - Assumption 4.5: strong convexity, required for every rate (p. 26).

  Several choices are admitted to be for convenience:
  - The ξk are independent in order "to avoid requiring certain machinery" (footnote 4, p. 22).
  - Strong convexity is assumed because practical convex models are regularized, many objectives are locally strongly convex, and extensions exist (Inset 4.2, p. 25).
  - Results are stated in expectation rather than almost surely because, "in our view, they do not provide significant additional insights into the forces driving convergence of the method" (p. 25).
  - Smoothness rather than convexity is the foundation. This covers nonconvex and scaled steps but pushes nonsmooth regularizers into §8 (App. A, pp. 82–83).
  - The work-complexity model takes E(n, ε) ∼ 1/n + ε (4.34), "the fastest rate that remains compatible with elementary statistical results" (p. 36). The comparison is thus run under "the most favorable rate that one may reasonably assume" (p. 36), and SG still wins.
  - Sampling with or without replacement changes which risk is being minimized (footnote 3, p. 21).
  - Transferable: in a stochastic analysis, put all the noise into one bound of the form M + MV‖∇F‖². Then M = 0 recovers the deterministic result, M > 0 gives a noise floor, and M ζ^{k−1} gives the noise-reduction regime, all with one proof template (pp. 24, 27, 41).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The devices, (a) to (k):
  - (a) Two fundamental lemmas, "from which all of our results will be derived" (p. 23): the expected descent inequality (Lemma 4.2, p. 23) and its moment-bounded form (Lemma 4.4, p. 24).
  - (b) Fixed step, strongly convex case. Subtracting the asymptotic constant ᾱLM/(2cµ) turns the recursion into a contraction inequality, giving linear convergence to a neighbourhood fixed by the noise (Theorem 4.6, (4.15)–(4.16), pp. 26–27).
  - (c) A heuristic halving schedule: halve the step when the gap reaches twice its floor. Each halving needs twice as many iterations, which gives O(1/k) (pp. 27–28, (4.17)–(4.18), Fig. 4.1).
  - (d) Diminishing steps β/(γ + k). An induction on ν/(γ + k) gives the rate but needs β > 1/(cµ) (Theorem 4.7, pp. 28–29). How much that condition matters is shown by an example from [105]: with c overestimated, the distance to the solution is still above 10⁻² after 10⁹ iterations (p. 29). Choosing β = 2/(cµ) exposes the constant (2/µ²)(L/c)(M/c) in (4.24), which ties the bound to conditioning (p. 30).
  - (e) Mini-batching, compared through the constants. M becomes M/nmb, but the step cannot be multiplied by nmb, so the benefit has to come from hardware and parallelism (pp. 30–31).
  - (f) Nonconvex results, each with its own device:
    - telescoping sums (Theorem 4.8, p. 32);
    - a weighted sum (Theorem 4.10, p. 33) and its lim inf corollary (Theorem 4.9, p. 33);
    - convergence in probability of a randomly chosen iterate, via Markov's inequality (Corollary 4.11, p. 34);
    - a full limit, via the convergent positive and negative variation sums S⁺ and S⁻ (Corollary 4.12, proof p. 84).
  - (g) Work complexity:
    - the error split Eapp + Eest + Eopt (4.31) and the time-budget problem (4.32);
    - Table 4.1: SG reaches error ∼ 1/Tmax, batch gradient ∼ log(Tmax)/Tmax + 1/Tmax (pp. 35–37);
    - the Ω(1/k) lower bound for noisy oracles, which SG matches up to constants (pp. 36–37).
  - (h) Noise reduction. A geometrically decaying variance gives a linear rate by induction (Theorem 5.1, pp. 41–42). Geometric batch growth nk = ⌈τ^{k−1}⌉ then gives total work O(1/ε), by counting with a geometric sum (Theorem 5.3, pp. 43–44).
  - (i) The descent test ‖g − ∇F‖ ≤ χ‖g‖ (5.11) is checked in practice through a sample-variance test (5.12), with a geometric backup (p. 45).
  - (j) The same descent-lemma template is reused for randomized coordinate descent (Theorem 7.1, p. 72) and for proximal gradient (Theorem 8.1, pp. 75–76).
  - (k) Choosing a measure by testing it on a one-line example. On qk(w) = |w| the minimum-norm subgradient is discontinuous (footnote 9, p. 80), so "this measure is not continuous, making it inadequate for these purposes" (p. 80). The norm of an ISTA step is used instead as the inexactness test for proximal-Newton subproblems (p. 80).
  - Transferable: compare methods by total work to reach ε, not by rate per iteration. Theorem 5.3 shows that a linearly convergent method whose per-iteration cost grows geometrically still matches SG's O(1/ε) (pp. 43–44). §5.3.3 compares SG's κ²/ε with aggregation's (n + κ) log(1/ε) (p. 49).
  - Transferable: when an adaptive test cannot be guaranteed to meet a theorem's hypothesis, pair it with a preset backup schedule that does. If test (5.12) grows the sample more slowly than a geometric sequence, geometric growth is imposed (p. 45).
  - Transferable: to choose an inexactness measure for a nonsmooth subproblem, require that it vanish exactly at the minimizer and vary continuously, and test candidates on |w| (p. 80).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The paper declines benchmarking on purpose. It does not compare methods "based on the results of numerical experiments". Instead it focuses on "fundamental computational trade-offs and theoretical properties", to avoid a bias "toward a limited test set and implementation details" (p. 4).
  - Its only experiment is Fig. 3.1 (pp. 17–18): batch L-BFGS against SG with a fixed step α = 4 on RCV1 logistic regression. Cost is counted in accessed data points (sample gradient evaluations) over the first 10 epochs.
  - The text discloses that SG's step had to be tuned: "it was necessary to run SG repeatedly using different choices for the stepsize α until a good choice was identified for this particular problem" (p. 17). It adds that with more epochs the batch method would overtake SG (p. 19).
  - The single-problem plot is nonetheless called "representative of typical behavior in practice" (p. 17), with no aggregate behind it.
  - Figs. 2.2 and 2.4 report other groups' results (pp. 8–9). Other practical claims are cited rather than run: the factor-of-three speed-up of diagonal Gauss–Newton scaling (p. 65), the SGD-QN erratum (p. 65), and SVRG's advantage once iterates near the solution (p. 47).
  - There are no test sets, no performance profiles and no failure counts.
  - Transferable: if a theory-first survey includes one illustrative plot, count cost in a unit fair to both sides (accessed data points), say which side was tuned (p. 17), and say where the picture reverses (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper sides with neither community.
  - Against ML practice: the view of SG as the ideal method "is far from settled" (p. 40), and "it would be premature to conclude that SG is a perfect solution for large-scale machine learning problems" (p. 37).
  - Against the optimizers' habit: the batch view is the natural one for "many in the optimization research community" (p. 15), and the next pages argue for SG on intuitive, practical and theoretical grounds (pp. 16–19). Still, "We do not claim, however, that batch methods have no place in practice" (p. 19).
  - Credit is generous and historical:
    - Robbins–Monro as a landmark, first proposed "not as a gradient method, but as a Markov chain" (Inset 3.1, p. 16);
    - Bertsekas's example (p. 17);
    - the Bottou–Bousquet tradeoffs that §4.4 follows (p. 35);
    - Nemirovski–Yudin and the noisy-oracle lower bounds (pp. 36–37);
    - Polyak–Juditsky averaging (p. 49).
  - Rival families are restated rather than attacked, and the criticism is graded:
    - gradient aggregation "should not be regarded as clearly superior to SG" (p. 49);
    - the variance of the noise added by batch normalization "is poorly controlled" (p. 68);
    - for orthant-based codes, "some popular implementations lack convergence guarantees [58, 30]", where [30] is the group's own paper (p. 82);
    - general bound-constrained techniques, including his own L-BFGS-B [163] and Lin–Moré [95], "may be less effective by not exploiting the structure" of the ℓ1 reformulation (p. 78).
  - Reference list: 164 entries (pp. 85–95). Nocedal is an author of 8 ([28]–[31], [97], [113], [114], [163]) and Bottou of 12 ([18]–[25], [65], [86], [88], [89]). A search found no entry with Curtis as an author.
  - The decoupled stochastic quasi-Newton scheme is introduced as an approach "which we refer to for convenience as SQN" (p. 58), with no citation at that point. A search of the reference list found no SQN paper: no entry has Hansen as an author, and arXiv:1401.7020, which SKILL.md Workflow B cites as SQN v1, is not listed.
  - Transferable: when writing for two communities, state each one's default in its own terms and argue against both before presenting the synthesis (pp. 15–19, 40).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The main writing moves:
  - Structure driven by questions: three questions (p. 3) are answered in order, and case studies come before methods (§2).
  - One schematic reused three times as a road map (Figs. 3.3, 5.1, 6.1; pp. 20, 40, 51).
  - History and caveats moved into insets (Inset 3.1 on Robbins, p. 16; Inset 4.2 on analysis choices, p. 25; Inset 4.3 on regret bounds, p. 39).
  - A "Commentary" closing each method family, separating theory from practice (§4.5, pp. 37–38; §5.3.3, p. 49; end of §8.2.2, pp. 81–82). For example: "There is, in fact, a large gap between asymptotical behavior and practical realities" (p. 37).
  - Theorems followed by "what can be learned" paragraphs (Role of Strong Convexity, Role of the Initial Point, Trade-Offs of (Mini-)Batching; pp. 29–31).
  - Costs given as numbers:
    - an L-BFGS product costs 4md, so with m = 5 a step costs 20 times an SG step;
    - mini-batches of 256 are common, and should not fall below "say, 20 or 50" (p. 57);
    - a Newton-CG step costs maxcg × factor × gcost + gcost, aiming at maxcg × factor ≈ 1 (p. 54).
  - Small explicit examples carry the intuition:
    - Bertsekas's region of confusion (Example 3.1, p. 17);
    - a hand-derived Hessian-vector product (Example 6.1, p. 52);
    - F(w1, w2) = ½w1² + log(e^{w2} + e^{−w2}), where at (3, 3) the Newton direction ≈ [−3, −101] is worse than the gradient direction (p. 66).
  - Hedging is calibrated and local: "we suspect" (p. 45), "it is not known" (pp. 49, 55), "remains to be seen" (p. 68), "remains an open question" (p. 71).
  - Transferable: after each theorem, add a short paragraph naming the constant and the practical setting it fixes, e.g. β = 2/(cµ) and initial step α1 = µ/(LMG) (p. 30).
  - Transferable: price every enhancement in units of the base iteration before claiming a benefit: 4md against d (p. 57); 2m + n gradient evaluations per SVRG outer step (p. 47); "roughly twice as expensive" for diagonal Gauss–Newton (p. 65).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is synthesis plus a unified theory: "A concise, yet broadly applicable convergence and complexity theory for SG" (p. 82). The paper proposes no new algorithm and ships no software.
  - Limitations are stated specifically:
    - parallel and distributed computing are not covered at length, and SG "may not be the best suited method for emerging computer architectures" (p. 82);
    - ADMM and EM are left out (p. 82);
    - rates are proved only for strongly convex functions (Inset 4.2, p. 25);
    - for subsampled-Hessian Newton, no rate faster than linear has been shown (p. 54);
    - of the strategies for nonconvex Newton-CG, "none have proved to be totally satisfactory or universally accepted" (p. 55);
    - for stochastic quasi-Newton methods, "the full potential of the quasi-Newton schemes discussed above (and potentially others) is not yet known" (p. 58);
    - guarantees for momentum in stochastic settings "are elusive" (p. 69);
    - whether randomized coordinate descent is more effective in practice is open (p. 71).
  - Future directions named:
    - algorithms that adapt to local convexity instead of needing a fixed β (p. 37);
    - distributed settings, where the higher per-iteration cost of mini-batch and second-order methods can pay off (p. 38);
    - "an algorithm that performs like SG at the start and transitions to a regime of reduced variance in an efficient manner" (p. 45);
    - combinations of noise reduction and second-order steps (pp. 21, 57).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence. The survey puts SG, not his own L-BFGS, at the centre, because the problem's structure favours it: redundant data, a statistical target and a time budget (pp. 3, 16–19, 34–37). Its only experiment shows SG beating batch L-BFGS in the early epochs (Fig. 3.1, pp. 17–18). It also prefers devices that exploit structure:
  - storing scalars instead of gradients in SAGA for linear models (p. 48);
  - coordinate descent for objectives of the form (7.9) (pp. 72–73);
  - ℓ1-specific gradient projection over general bound-constrained techniques, his own L-BFGS-B [163] included (p. 78);
  - not interior-point methods, because they do not promote sparse iterates (p. 79).

  This is also case evidence for Heuristic 9 (publish the map of a field before your own method). The group's own methods [28]–[31] appear only as cells of the map (pp. 45, 53, 80–82).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant. Received wisdom is put on trial on both sides: ML's view of SG as the ideal method (p. 40) and the optimizers' preference for batch methods (pp. 15–19). But the trial is by theory and cost accounting (pp. 4, 34–37, 49), not by building and testing the dismissed simple option.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant. The goal is Method 1's (methods, not implementations), but the paper reaches it by declining benchmarks (p. 4) rather than by running handicapped ones. The one plot does disclose that SG, the rival to his L-BFGS, was the side that got tuned (p. 17). Against the stop rule in step 7, though, the plot is a single problem called "representative of typical behavior in practice", with no aggregate (p. 17).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence. In the ML setting the classical engine is kept and the failing component is named. BFGS updates are corrupted by noisy gradient differences, and because BFGS is an overwriting process, "the effects of even a single bad update may linger for numerous iterations" (p. 57). The repair touches only that component: same-sample differences, or curvature pairs from Hessian-vector products computed separately from the step (pp. 57–58). Dynamic sampling has an estimate-driven test (5.12) with a geometric backup as its recovery path (p. 45). Newton-CG keeps its line search and subsamples the Hessian (p. 53).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence. The work-complexity analysis is built to separate SG from batch gradient in the big-data regime (Table 4.1, p. 37), and SG from gradient aggregation (p. 49). It is checked against practice: "These observations are supported by practical experience" (p. 36, pointing to Fig. 3.1). The bound is tied to conditioning through ν ∝ (L/c)(M/c) (p. 30). The benefit of stochastic quasi-Newton is placed in the constant, not the rate (p. 56).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant, a tension with Taste warning sign 3 and with Inner Tension IT5. The summary attributes SG's success to complexity: SG is the method "whose success derives from its superior work complexity guarantees" (p. 82). Complexity is used here as the reason to prefer a method, although it is paired with an explicit gap between asymptotics and practice (p. 37).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, weak. Each method family closes with explicit open questions (pp. 45, 49, 55, 58, 69, 71). Whether the call on p. 45 for a method that moves from SG-like behaviour to reduced variance led to the group's later progressive-batching L-BFGS work (SKILL.md cites arXiv:1802.05374) is inferred from timing only. It was not checked in this batch.
- New-pattern candidate: prove once for an abstract direction (Algorithm 4.1 and (4.2), p. 22; two lemmas, pp. 23–24; template reused on pp. 41, 72, 75).
- New-pattern candidate: price devices in base-iteration units (pp. 47, 54, 57, 65).
- New-pattern candidate: allocate sample accuracy by how much noise each component tolerates (Hessian sample smaller than gradient sample, p. 53; curvature pairs on a larger sample, computed less often, p. 58).
- New-pattern candidate: survey by trade-offs, not benchmarks (p. 4), a Method 1 variant for review papers.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write one generalized algorithm whose search direction is a parameter (stochastic gradient, mini-batch, or Hk times mini-batch). Derive every theorem from two lemmas that need only sufficient descent in expectation plus a second-moment bound. One proof then covers SG, mini-batch SG and stochastic quasi-Newton (pp. 22–24).
- State the variance bound as M + MV‖∇F‖². Fixed-step theorems then show a noise floor ᾱLM/(2cµ); M = 0 recovers deterministic linear convergence; and M ζ^{k−1} gives linear rates for noise-reducing schemes (pp. 26–27, 41–42).
- Compare methods by total work to ε under a time budget, using the split Eapp + Eest + Eopt. Assume the statistical rate most favourable to the rival (batch) and show your method still wins (pp. 35–37).
- To prove that a growing-sample method is efficient, count total sample-gradient evaluations with a geometric sum and show O(1/ε). Pair the adaptive variance test with a preset geometric backup so the theorem stays valid (pp. 43–45).
- For a stochastic quasi-Newton method, compute curvature pairs from a separate, larger sample (same seed at both points, or a subsampled Hessian-vector product) and update them every 10–20 iterations, not every step (pp. 57–58).
- In Newton-CG for finite sums, subsample the Hessian more lightly than the gradient, and choose the CG cap so that maxcg × factor ≈ 1. A step then costs about as much as an SG step (pp. 53–54).
- Price each enhancement in base-iteration units before arguing for it: 4md for an L-BFGS product (20× SG at m = 5), 2m + n evaluations per SVRG cycle (pp. 47, 57).
- To pick an inexactness measure for a nonsmooth subproblem, require that it vanish exactly at the minimizer and vary continuously. Check candidates on |w|; the ISTA-step norm passes and the minimum-norm subgradient fails (p. 80).
- In an illustrative plot, count cost in a unit fair to both sides (accessed data points), say which side was tuned, and say where the ranking reverses (pp. 17–19).
