### S119 · iNEOS: An interactive environment for nonlinear optimization (Applied Numerical Mathematics 40(1–2), 49–57, 2002, doi:10.1016/S0168-9274(01)00063-0)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–14: abstract, all five sections, figures, references). First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1). Version read: preprint dated "May"; journal pages not mapped. All numbers (perturbation size, accuracy target, evaluation time, problem sizes) are lost in the text layer.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Marcel Good and Jean-Pierre Goux (Northwestern), Victor Pereyra (Weidlinger Associates) (p. 1); Todd Plantenga thanked (p. 12) · **Position in trajectory**: a software-infrastructure side line of the OTC/NEOS years, built on the reverse-communication design of L-BFGS-B (pp. 3, 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: NEOS forces the user to submit the whole model as AMPL, Fortran or C files. This shuts out users whose model is proprietary, whose function evaluations are too slow to delegate, or whose model is written in several languages (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: exploit the structure of the algorithm, not of the problem. A quasi-Newton iteration needs only x_k, f_k, g_k and the line-search trial values, and these numbers do not reveal the model (p. 3).
  - The client keeps the simulation and the server returns trial points; CORBA keeps both processes alive and synchronized (pp. 3–4).
  - CORBA was chosen over a Nexus prototype (no object orientation, no built-in multi-client support, deployment problems) and over DCOM (it would have to be bought and installed on Unix clients) (pp. 4–5).
  - A SolverFactory object makes adding a solver a new class (pp. 5–6). The L-BFGS-B interface has two methods, Init and NextIterate, wrapped in a procedural C/C++ library that Fortran codes can call (p. 7).
  - Only bound constraints are handled (L-BFGS-B); general constraints would need second derivatives sent to the server (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: n/a (software). The CORBA overhead is argued to be acceptable because simulations take minutes or hours and each exchange sends O(n) numbers (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: one controlled validation on a proprietary finite-element model of a piezoelectric crystal (ten parameters, bounds, finite-difference Jacobian, pp. 8–9). Impedances are generated from target parameters, the start is a perturbation of the target, and success means recovering each parameter to a set accuracy. Client in California, server in Illinois, through a firewall; the fitted impedance is shown after 20 L-BFGS iterations (pp. 9–11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: software contribution. Limits stated: bound constraints only (p. 4), no user-level security, and an API that still exposes solver-specific details. A common XML solver interface is proposed, but its overhead "must be measured relative to the total interaction time" (p. 12). The claim that iNEOS "is capable of solving challenging problems" rests on one ten-parameter run (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 3): when the model is proprietary and hidden from the solver, the general method that needs only function and gradient values is the right tool. That property here becomes the privacy argument (pp. 3, 9).
- New-pattern candidate: *algorithm information structure as a deployment interface*: the reverse-communication design already in TRON and L-BFGS (a driver computes f and g, the solver returns the next iterate) is turned into a remote, model-hiding service (p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Validate an optimization pipeline on a real simulator with a planted solution: generate the data from known parameters, start from a perturbation, and check whether the parameters are recovered to a set accuracy (p. 9).
- Before building a service around a solver, list what each iteration actually needs (here x, f, g and trial values) and design the interface around that minimum (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "In nonlinear optimization an improved solution can be generated just by knowing the current iterate and the numerical values of the function and gradient at the current point" (p. 3)
- "These arrays of real numbers do not reveal the nonlinear model to an observer of the data submissions" (p. 3)

### S035 · An algorithm for nonlinear optimization using linear programming and equality constrained subproblems (Mathematical Programming 100(1), 27–48, 2003, doi:10.1007/s10107-003-0485-4)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–33, all text; figures seen; all table entries lost). First page checked: the file's title is "An Active-Set Algorithm for Nonlinear Programming Using Linear Programming and Equality Constrained Subproblems". It is an Optimization Technology Center report dated September (year lost), with "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1). The abstract is the published paper's, so this is the report version of the same work under an earlier title.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Nicholas I. M. Gould (RAL), Richard A. Waltz (Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: first paper of the SLQP strand (code SLIQUE). The theory follows in S058, the penalty rule is generalized in S126/S042, and the method became Knitro-Active (S126 p. 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: active-set SQP and interior methods handle thousands of variables but "are likely to become very expensive" as problems grow. The target is the cost of solving a general inequality-constrained QP at every iteration (p. 2). The LP-plus-EQP idea of Fletcher and Sainz de la Maza "has received little attention beyond this" (p. 2). The study is framed by two questions: how well the LP predicts the optimal active set, and what an iteration costs against interior and active-set competitors (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: kept from Fletcher–Sainz de la Maza: an LP to guess the active set, then an EQP for optimality. Changed (p. 2):
  - The LP linearizes the ℓ1 penalty function inside an ℓ∞ trust region, so it is always feasible (pp. 5–6).
  - A Cauchy point is found along d_LP by backtracking on the model only, with no function evaluations (p. 8).
  - The EQP has its own ℓ2 trust region, and its Hessian adds the curvature of violated constraints (pp. 9–10). It is solved inexactly by projected CG–Lanczos (GLTR) (p. 11).
  - The trial step comes from a dogleg-like line search between the Cauchy and EQP points (pp. 11–12).
  - The LP trust region may shrink after accepted steps, so that only locally active constraints enter the working set (p. 7).
  - The penalty parameter is set by the LP (pp. 14–16). If linear feasibility is attainable, ν is chosen to reach it. Otherwise ν must give a fraction of the best attainable infeasibility reduction, computed by an LP with ∇f = 0; ν is raised by a factor of ten and the LP re-solved. ν is not raised when no significant feasibility progress is possible. A safeguard lowers ν after five consecutive successful feasible iterations, at most twice (p. 16).
  - The SOC step starts from the trial point and uses the already factored projection matrix (pp. 12–13). Least-squares multipliers cost one extra backsolve (p. 14).

  Differences from Chin–Fletcher: an ℓ1 merit function instead of a filter, and a dogleg path instead of the full EQP step or the Cauchy step (pp. 30–31).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f, h and g are twice continuously differentiable (p. 3). Simple bounds are kept out of the merit function and satisfied by all iterates, but omitted from the exposition for simplicity (p. 6). No preconditioner is used (p. 11).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no proofs. The LP trust-region rule and the Cauchy decrease are justified by a separate global convergence theory, which "also provides a range of admissible values for the constants" (p. 7; see S058) and builds on Yuan (p. 30).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Test set*: CUTEr as of May (year lost), excluding unconstrained, equality-only, LP and feasibility problems and eight problems too large for memory. Problems are split into QP, bound-constrained (BC) and general (GC) classes and sized by n + m (pp. 19–20).
  - *Codes and protocol*: SLIQUE (MINOS for the LPs) against KNITRO and SNOPT, all at default settings, with SNOPT's superbasics limit raised. Limits on CPU time and major iterations; reaching one counts as failure. One tolerance for all, and the stopping tests made very similar to SNOPT's (pp. 18–19). SNOPT's "requested accuracy could not be achieved" exits are counted as failures, for consistency with the other two codes (pp. 20–21).
  - *Measures*: performance profiles in evaluations and CPU time on medium and large problems, with timings on a dedicated machine (pp. 21–28). A per-task time breakdown (LP, EQP, factorization, evaluations, other), each problem weighted equally, over solved problems taking at least one second (p. 28).
  - *Results*: SLIQUE is less robust than KNITRO and nearly as robust as SNOPT, with a sharp loss of reliability on large problems. Ten large QPs failed on memory, and some of the remaining large-problem failures came from the CPU limit (other counts lost) (p. 21). LP solves dominate the time, for two reasons: very many simplex steps in the first iterations, and warm starts that are not completely successful near the solution (pp. 28–29). KNITRO outperforms both active-set codes on GC problems (pp. 26–28).
- **D6 Positioning** (one line): the LP-based penalty rule is presented as new against rules that track multiplier size or optimality conditions (p. 14); filter versus penalty is left to testing (p. 30).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithm and software. Four novel features are listed (p. 30). The code is called the first stage of algorithmic development, with five planned improvements: faster LP solves and better initial active sets, the LP trust-region update, SOC or a nonmonotone strategy, EQP preconditioning, degeneracy handling (p. 31). Of the penalty rule the paper says the choice "does not appear to be a problematic issue in our approach" (p. 29). The S179 report of the same period still lists penalty management as an outstanding issue (S179 p. 1).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: their own two codes' stopping tests were made to match SNOPT's, all codes ran at defaults, limits counted as failures, and SNOPT's near-successes were counted as failures for consistency (pp. 19–21). The loss to their own KNITRO is printed (p. 21).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 5): active-set and interior codes are compared like with like, and each loss is explained. SNOPT suffers from large reduced spaces, SLIQUE from LP time (pp. 29–30).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: open choices are written down as the agenda. The choice of the LP radius is left as an open subject of investigation (p. 7), warm starts are "a challenging research question" (p. 29), and five improvements are listed (p. 31). The code reappears as Knitro-Active (S126 p. 12).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (worked-case lineage): ν is set from an explicit estimate, the best attainable reduction in linearized infeasibility, computed by the ∇f = 0 LP (pp. 14–16). This is the earliest form of steering, without S126's third condition (5.14) (S126 pp. 9–10).
- New-pattern candidate: *price the repair in reused factorizations and warm starts*: the SOC reuses the factored projection matrix and least-squares multipliers cost one backsolve (pp. 13–14); the extra LPs are cheap thanks to warm starts (p. 16). See also S075 p. 15 and S126 pp. 12–13.
- New-pattern candidate: *specify by admissible conditions so the theory covers the code*: the LP trust-region update is backed by a theory that gives a range of admissible constants rather than fixed values (p. 7; see S058 pp. 10, 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before tuning, locate the bottleneck with a per-task time breakdown weighted equally per problem and restricted to solved runs of at least one second; here it was the LP solves, through long first solves and weak warm starts (pp. 28–29).
- Count a rival's almost-converged exit as a failure when your own codes count the same case as a failure, and say so in the paper (pp. 20–21).
- Get the best attainable feasibility reduction cheaply by re-solving the same LP with the objective gradient set to zero (pp. 15–16).
- Tie the radius of an active-set predictor to the length of the step just taken and let it shrink after accepted steps, so that inactive constraints drop out of the working set (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "Although it is nearly impossible to enforce a uniform stopping condition the stopping conditions for Slique and Knitro were constructed to be very similar to that used in Snopt" (p. 19)
- "because it allows us to work simultaneously on optimality and feasibility but testing would be needed to establish which approach is preferable" (p. 30)

### S075 · Feasible interior methods using slacks for nonlinear optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 26(1), 35–61, 2003, doi:10.1023/A:1025136421370)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–20, all text; the appendix table on pp. 20–27 was glanced at, and its entries are lost). First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1). Version read: preprint dated February (year lost). Theorem and equation numbers, table values and test-set sizes are unreadable.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd, Richard A. Waltz (p. 1); Guanghui Liu, Marcelo Marazzi and José Luis Morales thanked (p. 20) · **Position in trajectory**: KNITRO's feasible mode, built on the trust-region interior method with the Byrd–Omojokun normal step.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: some applications need iterates that satisfy the inequality constraints, either because the objective is undefined outside them or because an early stop must return a feasible point (p. 2). Most NLP interior codes are infeasible. The entry is to make feasible and infeasible interior algorithms "variants of the same basic method" (p. 2). The paper notes that no study had compared the two (p. 17).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *Slack reset*: after each trial step, set s_T = g(x_T) (Algorithm FeasReset). It runs after an infeasible phase that continues until all inequalities exceed a threshold. The linear system and data structures stay the same; only two instructions change (p. 5).
  - *Equivalence*: for Newton primal-dual steps, the reset makes feasible mode identical to a classical Fiacco–McCormick barrier method. For trust-region or inexact steps this holds only asymptotically (pp. 6–7).
  - *Failure*: on GAUSSELM with KNITRO steps, the merit function increases even as the radius goes to zero. The reset perturbation Δs stays comparable to the step, and the run fails (pp. 8–9).
  - *Repair*: require r_g = 0 for inequality constraints at feasible iterates (Feasible Step Conditions, p. 12). In KNITRO only the Cauchy direction of the dogleg normal step changes, computed from an augmented system with three stated properties (pp. 13–14), at the cost of one backsolve with factors already in hand (p. 15).
  - A simpler variant that rejects infeasible trial points can also fail when there are equality constraints; it was not explored further (pp. 16–17).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f, h and g sufficiently smooth (p. 3), and a step generator compatible with the merit function for small steps (p. 4). The Theorem needs a Lipschitz ψ and Jacobian and a step that satisfies the linearized inequality constraints (p. 10). The Corollary needs a sufficiently large merit parameter, Lipschitz A and ∇f, an iterate that is not stationary for ‖h‖, and a small radius (p. 14). The large-parameter assumption is called not restrictive, since practice already does it (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Δs = O(‖d_x‖²) when the step satisfies the linearized constraints, against a merit decrease of order ‖d‖, so for small steps the reset cannot block acceptance (pp. 10–11). Line-search steps satisfy the condition. Standard trust-region normal steps do not, because their least-squares relaxation makes both r_h and r_g nonzero; this explains the failure (pp. 11–12). For a small radius the dogleg step is a multiple of the new Cauchy direction (p. 15).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - GAUSSELM tables before and after the fix (pp. 9, 16), and the modified versus unmodified normal step on problems with equality constraints (p. 16).
  - Feasible versus infeasible KNITRO on a CUTE set: a log2 ratio of function evaluations per problem, sorted by absolute value. Only problems where both versions found the same solution and the feasible version entered feasible mode are kept, with the default KNITRO stopping test (pp. 17–18).
  - KNITRO's slack adjustment is kept in both versions, which damps the difference (pp. 18–19).
  - Finding: no clear winner. Three problems were much better for the feasible version, but not because of feasibility (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithm design plus guidance for implementers:
  - keep the distinction between inequalities and slacks in the problem formulation (pp. 19–20);
  - reset the slacks before evaluating the merit function (p. 20);
  - for partial feasibility, reset only the slacks of the inequalities that must be honoured (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the infeasible slack-based engine is kept. Only the slack reset changes, and in trust-region codes only the Cauchy component of the normal step (pp. 5, 13–14), at the cost of one backsolve (p. 15). The failure is first shown on one chosen problem with an internal diagnostic, ‖Δs‖ (pp. 8–9; Heuristic 1).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the theorem separates two practical variants. It shows why line-search versions survive the reset and why the KNITRO trust-region version fails (pp. 10–12).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (step 5): feasible and infeasible methods are compared inside one code, each in its best KNITRO implementation, even though this damps the disparity (p. 19).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (step 5): the expectation that the freedom of infeasible methods helps (or hurts) is tested, and the surprise is recorded: no clear superiority (p. 17).
- New-pattern candidate: *price the repair in reused factorizations*: the new Cauchy direction reuses the KNITRO factorization at the cost of one backsolve (p. 15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the feasible variant as a switch inside the infeasible code (same linear algebra, two changed instructions), so the comparison isolates feasibility (pp. 5, 17).
- Diagnose a stall by tabulating, per iteration, the quantity your change introduces: here ‖Δs‖ against the radius and the merit reduction (pp. 8–9).
- Compare two variants with a per-problem log2-ratio bar chart, restricted to problems where both found the same solution (pp. 17–18).
- When a reset or projection follows the step, prove its perturbation is second order provided the step satisfies the linearized constraints, then check which step generators meet that condition (pp. 10–12).

**Quotes**:
- "To our surprise the tests we report below do not indicate a clear superiority of either method" (p. 17)
- "taking the view that all methods should be tested using their best implementation within the KNITRO framework" (p. 19)

### S085 · A starting point strategy for nonlinear interior methods (Applied Mathematics Letters 17(8), 945–952, 2004, doi:10.1016/j.aml.2003.09.005)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–9). First page checked: the repository cover (p. 1) and the manuscript title page with "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 2). Version read: peer-reviewed author version from the University of Namur repository; journal pages not mapped. The OCR garbles the formulas, the formula of Rule 2 is missing (p. 4), and the figure curves are not extractable.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: E. Michael Gertz (UW-Madison CS), Annick Sartenaer (Namur) (p. 2); José Luis Morales (the MATLAB IPM code) and Richard Waltz (the KNITRO 3.0 implementation) thanked (p. 9) · **Position in trajectory**: a KNITRO interior component, shipped in KNITRO 3.0 (p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: interior methods for LP and QP do poorly from bad starting points, and LP heuristics for the start are in commercial codes. For NLP, however, the topic "has not received much attention" (p. 2). The LP heuristics do not carry over. In LP the user gives no initial estimate and the functions are defined everywhere, so large starting values are common. In NLP users give estimates near a minimizer of interest, and large multipliers can introduce non-convexity (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: from preliminary values, compute an affine-scaling step (μ = 0) of the primal-dual system. Measure how far the full step violates s ≥ 0 and λ ≥ 0, then shift s and λ to be sufficiently positive by Rule 1 or Rule 2. Keep x, y and z at their preliminary values, which include the user's starting point, and set μ1 = s1ᵀλ1/m (pp. 4–5).
  - In codes that do not keep z and λ separately, apply the rule to z and evaluate the initial Hessian at z0, not z1. Three reasons are given: all codes then compute identical steps, a large z1 combined with an indefinite constraint Hessian cannot distort the model, and one evaluation is saved (p. 4).
  - Algorithm 3.1 gives the procedure (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f, h and g twice continuously differentiable (p. 3). The justification is an analogy with path following for convex QP and its central-path neighbourhood (p. 5). The test code (Algorithm 5.1) handles only convex problems and stops on negative curvature (pp. 6–7).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no theorem. The argument: in QP the slack and multiplier steps and the step lengths do not depend on (x, y, z), so only (s1, λ1) matter, and the user's x can be kept (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - A damped short-step MATLAB path-following code (MA27) on the Maros–Mészáros QPs: Rules 1 and 2 against no strategy, in iteration profiles (p. 7).
  - 58 CUTEr inequality problems, after removing problems that every strategy solved in under 10 iterations, problems with n + m < 50 and problems with negative curvature: a "slight improvement" (p. 7).
  - KNITRO 3.0 (Direct) on 66 hard problems selected for this study (Brunel problems, known difficult ones, some CUTEr). Only Rule 1, "which gives the best results for KNITRO", is shown against no strategy, with a "dramatic improvement" (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a heuristic, recommended as an option, with no theory. The paper does not discuss its limits.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the result becomes an option rather than a claimed default (quote 2, p. 8). It is tested in two frameworks, KNITRO and a bare code written "To demonstrate the general applicability of our approach" (p. 6) (step 3).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the classical LP start heuristic is kept, and only the part that breaks in NLP changes. The variables that define the model keep their preliminary values, and only the sign-constrained s and λ are reset (pp. 5–6). There is no estimate-driven recovery.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the KNITRO set was "selected specifically for this study", and of the two rules only the better one is shown (p. 8). Easy problems were filtered out of the CUTEr set (p. 7). Here the scales tilt toward the strategy, not against it.

**Transferable techniques**:
- When porting an LP/QP heuristic to NLP, split the variables into those that define the model (keep the user's values) and the sign-constrained ones (reset them), and say why (pp. 5–6).
- Use one cheap affine-scaling step to estimate the scale of slacks and multipliers before choosing their starting values (p. 4).
- Evaluate the first Hessian at the unshifted multipliers, so that a large shifted multiplier cannot make it indefinite (p. 4).
- Test a solver heuristic both in the production code and in a stripped-down code written for the study, to show that it is not an artefact of one implementation (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "This topic has not received much attention in spite of the fact that nonlinear interior methods can be as sensitive as their linear counterparts to a poor initial guess." (p. 2)
- "As a result of this testing, we recommend this as an option in nonlinear interior methods." (p. 8)

### S179 · 4.7 Sequential linear-quadratic programming for huge optimization problems (R. H. Byrd, N. I. M. Gould, J. Nocedal and R. Waltz) (RAL Numerical Analysis Group Progress Report January 2002–December 2003, 39–40, 2004, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (both pages). First page checked: section title with "J. Nocedal" among the named authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement. This is a section of a group progress report in the collective "we" of the four authors. The drafter is not stated; since the report is the RAL group's, the wording may be Gould's (inferred). Not peer-reviewed · **Co-authors**: Byrd, Gould, Waltz (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the project statement between S035 and S058.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: SQP and interior methods work up to about 10⁵ variables, but a nonconvex QP is too complicated a subproblem for huge problems of 10⁷ or more (the superscripts are flattened in the text). LPs of that size are solved routinely, but plain SLP is "notoriously slow" (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Fletcher and Sainz de la Maza's LP–EQP acceleration, whose open questions are named: what to do if the LP or the EQP is infeasible or unbounded. Their version minimizes an ℓ1-penalty LP in a polyhedral trust region and the EQP in a second, elliptical trust region, so both subproblems are feasible and bounded. The "art" is in adjusting the two radii (p. 1).
- **D3 Assumptions**: n/a.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: global convergence of a two-trust-region framework has been established (Byrd, Gould, Nocedal and Waltz 2003, the S058 line). Local convergence is the next goal, encouraged by numerical experience (p. 1).
- **D5 Experiments**: none here; the section points to SLIQUE's CUTEr comparisons (Byrd et al. 2002) (p. 1).
- **D6 Positioning** (one line): the listed open issue, how to manage the penalty parameter (p. 1), sits beside S035's conclusion that the penalty choice is not a problem (S035 p. 29); S126 later treats it as the central difficulty (S126 pp. 5–7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: outstanding issues are listed: managing the ℓ1 penalty parameter, solving and truncating the EQP, and how accurately to solve the LP. The claim that the approach will solve huge problems is stated as a belief. The project is presented as a bridge between the competing GALAHAD and KNITRO teams (p. 1).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the report keeps an explicit list of unresolved practical choices (penalty, EQP truncation, LP accuracy) as the agenda (p. 1).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (stated): collaboration across competing solver teams to combine LP and EQP technology (p. 1). This is the group's stated intent; the RAL drafter may be Gould.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Frame a method by two scales: where the incumbent's subproblem stops being solvable (a nonconvex QP at 10⁷ unknowns) and where a simpler subproblem is routine (an LP of the same size). Then design around the simpler one (p. 1).

**Quotes**:
- "The difficulty is simply that a (non-convex) quadratic program is too complicated a subproblem for huge problems." (p. 1)
- "Since we believe that collaboration is the best way to tackle huge problems, the project also acts as a friendly and useful bridge between the competing GALAHAD and KNITRO software development teams." (p. 1)

### S058 · On the convergence of successive linear-quadratic programming algorithms (SIAM Journal on Optimization 16(2), 471–489, 2005, doi:10.1137/S1052623403426532)
**Read level**: full (pp. 3–26: abstract, §§1–5 with every proof followed step by step, references). First page checked: RAL report cover with "J Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1); title page p. 3. Version read: RAL-TR-2004-031, November 18, 2004 (report page = file page − 3); SIOPT pages not mapped.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Byrd, Gould, Waltz; two referees thanked (p. 24) · **Position in trajectory**: the theory for S035's SLIQUE; S126 cites it as the analysis of the penalty SLQP method (S126 p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the SLQP algorithm of S035 uses two trust regions that influence each other, so Yuan's nonsmooth theory does not apply (p. 7). Chin and Fletcher's SLP-filter method has "no precaution to guard against large or unbounded QP steps" (p. 7). The aim is global convergence for a fixed σ, then a penalty update that converges to KKT points or to critical points of the infeasibility (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**:
  - *Algorithm 2.1* (p. 9): an LP in a polyhedral trust region. A Cauchy step backtracks along d_LP with a sufficient-decrease test of the quadratic model against the linear model (2.2). The step taken may be any step in the second trust region that does no worse than the Cauchy step on the model. A standard ratio test follows.
  - *LP radius*: given as admissible properties, not a formula (pp. 9–10). After a success it is not below the Cauchy step and not increased unless the full LP step was taken. After a failure it lies in [min(θ‖d‖, Δ_LP), Δ_LP].
  - The algorithm and analysis are also written for composite nonsmooth problems ω(F(x)) (p. 6).
  - *Algorithm 4.1* (p. 20): keep σ while Ψσ(x, δ) ≥ ξσθ(x, δ), i.e. while the model decrease keeps pace with the attainable reduction in linearized infeasibility. Otherwise raise σ by at least a fixed amount. The test radius is δ_k = mid(δmin, Δ_LP, δmax), so the LP already solved can be reused (pp. 19–20).
  - The simple rule (4.12) "is not to be recommended", because it grows without bound near feasibility (p. 21).
- **D3 Assumptions**: P1: f, g, h and their derivatives Lipschitz on a bounded convex set containing the iterates. P2: bounded B_k, "made to simplify the analysis" (pp. 10–11). MFCQ is needed in the feasible case of Theorem 4.4 (p. 23).
- **D4 Analysis**:
  - Yuan's criticality measure Ψ(x, Δ) (p. 11).
  - Lemma 3.1: Ψ is concave and increasing in Δ (pp. 11–12).
  - Lemma 3.2: at non-critical points the LP trust region is active for small radii (p. 12).
  - Lemma 3.3: Cauchy decrease (p. 13). Lemma 3.5: a lower bound on the Cauchy step (p. 14).
  - Lemma 3.6, called "crucial": a joint recursion bounds min(Δ_k/γ, Δ_LP_k) from below (pp. 14–16).
  - Theorem 3.8: liminf Ψ_k(1) = 0, proved by two cases, Δ_LP bounded or unbounded (pp. 17–18).
  - Theorem 4.1: a feasible critical point of φσ is a KKT point (p. 19).
  - Theorems 4.3–4.4: if σ stays bounded, a cluster point is a KKT point or an infeasible critical point. If σ → ∞, a cluster point is an infeasible critical point, a KKT point, or a feasible point where MFCQ fails. The last case is proved by contradiction: near a feasible limit point that satisfies MFCQ but is not a KKT point, a direction that is both linearly feasible and a descent direction exists. Every σ then passes the test, so σ would stop growing there (pp. 22–24).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. "strong numerical evidence" for active-set identification and a superlinear rate is mentioned but not shown (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a theory paper with global convergence only. Limits stated:
  - no rate and no finite active-set identification (p. 24);
  - Theorem 4.3 "essentially holds for Slique", whereas a Theorem 4.4 analogue is unclear because of extra implementation conditions (p. 24);
  - updating σ only after approximately minimizing φσ is set aside, since it "may prove to be inefficient" (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the version analysed is the code. The radius framework is deliberately general so that it covers SLIQUE's current and future implementations, and the paper says which theorem applies to SLIQUE (p. 24).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: global convergence only, with the rate deferred (p. 24), whereas Method 5 step 3 asks for a rate.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the penalty rule measures the current σ against an explicit estimate, θ(x, δ), the best attainable reduction in linearized infeasibility (pp. 19–20). This is the theoretical root of steering (S126 p. 13).
- New-pattern candidate: *specify by admissible conditions so the theory covers the code*: implementation rules are stated as the inequalities the proof needs (pp. 10, 24). Compare S035 p. 7 (a range of admissible constants) and S126 p. 13 (a less restrictive condition in the analysis).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Specify implementation-dependent rules (here the LP radius update) by the inequalities the proof needs, not by a formula, so that one theorem covers several implementations (pp. 10, 24).
- When two trust regions interact, combine their update recursions into one lower bound on the smaller of the two radii, instead of bounding each separately (pp. 15–16).
- To reuse a quantity computed at the current radius in a criticality test stated at radius 1, clamp the radius into [δmin, δmax]. The concavity and monotonicity bounds (3.6) and (4.13) then transfer the inequality (pp. 11, 20–21).

**Quotes**:
- "It should be noted that the theory of non-smooth optimization developed by Yuan [19, 21] cannot be applied to the algorithm considered here and in [1, 16] because in these algorithms the two trust regions influence each other, whereas Yuan assumes that a single trust region is used." (p. 7)
- "Our framework for trust-region radius updates is deliberately general. This is because we wished it to apply in the case of the current implementation of our evolving nonlinear programming code Slique [1] as well as to cover its future evolution." (p. 24)

### S027 · Interior methods for mathematical programs with complementarity constraints (SIAM Journal on Optimization 17(1), 52–77, 2006, doi:10.1137/040621065)
**Read level**: partial. Read:
- abstract and §1 (pp. 1–3); §2 with Examples 1–2 (pp. 3–7);
- §3: definitions and the statements of Theorems 3.2, 3.4, Corollary 3.5 and Theorem 3.6, with the first lines of its proof (pp. 7–13);
- §4: Assumptions 4.2, Theorem 4.5 and the start of its proof (pp. 14–17);
- §§5–6 (pp. 19–25) and the references (pp. 26–27).

Not followed: the proofs of Theorems 3.4 and 4.5 and of Lemma 4.3. First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1). Version read: preprint dated July 14, 2005 (Report OTC 2004-10 / ANL/MCS-P1211-1204, p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Sven Leyffer (Argonne), Gabriel López-Calva (Northwestern IEMS; his PhD thesis is listed as in progress, ref. [20], p. 27) · **Position in trajectory**: the degenerate-NLP strand (penalty regularization). S126 cites it as a dynamic penalty rule (S126 p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Fletcher and Leyffer found LOQO and KNITRO slower and less reliable than SQP codes on MacMPEC, the opposite of ordinary NLP experience (p. 2). The NLP form of an MPCC violates MFCQ at every feasible point (p. 2). Penalization is chosen over relaxation "because we view it as a general tool for handling degenerate nonlinear programs" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: put π x1ᵀx2 in the objective, keep x1, x2 ≥ 0, and apply an interior method to the barrier problem (pp. 3–4).
  - Algorithm I is an outer loop that accepts a barrier solution only if ‖min{x1, x2}‖ ≤ ε_comp (pp. 4–5).
  - Two examples show the flaw of holding π fixed for a whole barrier problem (pp. 5–7). In ralph2 the penalty problem is unbounded for π < 2 and the iterates diverge. In scale1 the iterates approach a saddle point while π < π* = 200, and complementarity stagnates.
  - *Dynamic rule*: within the inner iterations, multiply π by 10 when complementarity exceeds ε_comp = μ^0.4 and has not dropped below 0.9 times its maximum over the last 3 iterates (pp. 19–20).
  - *Classic rule*: update π only after a barrier problem is solved (p. 19).
- **D3 Assumptions**: the global theory assumes the inner algorithm always succeeds (p. 9).
  - Theorem 3.4 (p. 9): C¹ near the limit and MPCC-LICQ give C-stationarity; adding π^k x^k → 0 at corner indices gives strong stationarity.
  - Corollary 3.5: a bounded π suffices (p. 12).
  - Local theory: C² Lipschitz functions, MPCC-LICQ, primal-dual strict complementarity and MPCC-SOSC (Assumptions 4.2, p. 14), plus rate conditions (4.10)–(4.11) on μ and the tolerances (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 3.4 builds on Hu and Ralph's proof for exact subproblem solves (p. 11). The penalty sequence need not be monotone (p. 12). That is what allows "an indirect proof" of Theorem 3.6 for interior-relaxation methods, which reads the relaxation multipliers ξ^k as penalty parameters π^k (p. 13). Theorem 4.5: near a regular solution with π > π*, the stopping tests hold after a single step, with no backtracking and no update of π, and convergence is superlinear (pp. 16–17).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *Setup*: ipm-d, a MATLAB interior code, chosen because it lacks production regularizations and scalings (p. 20). 74 MacMPEC problems under 1,000 variables, plus added badly scaled ones (pp. 20–21).
  - *Comparison*: NLP, Fixed (π = 10⁴), Classic and Dynamic, in total-iteration performance profiles, with two initial π (1 and ‖∇f(x0)‖) (p. 22). NLP is worst; Fixed is better but beaten by Classic and Dynamic. Case plots follow for ralph2, scale1, bard3 and bilin (pp. 23–24).
  - *Failures*, each named and attributed (pp. 24–25):
    - scale4 converges to a C-stationary point; ralph2 fails under Classic;
    - ex9.2.2 and ralphmod have no multipliers at the solution;
    - the incid-set, pack-rig and pack-comp groups have other degeneracies; tap-09 has a rank-deficient Jacobian.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory plus algorithm. Future work: a KNITRO implementation for large MPCCs, and regularization for other forms of degeneracy (p. 25). Limit: MacMPEC is almost all well scaled, so Dynamic and Classic barely differ there, and the case for Dynamic rests on examples (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the interior engine is kept and only the handling of complementarity is added, the penalty term and its update. "The main point of this section is to demonstrate how to adapt an existing interior-point method" (p. 20).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the paper asks "whether the penalty parameter needs to be updated at all" and tests it. A fixed large π (Fixed, 10⁴) loses to the adaptive rules (pp. 7, 22).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (step 5): all four methods run in one bare code, chosen so that production heuristics do not blur the effect (p. 20); the failures are listed (pp. 24–25).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: practice is checked against theory. Convergence to C-stationary points, which the theory allows (p. 8), is observed on scale4 (p. 24).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: failures traced to degeneracy beyond complementarity become the next topic (general regularization, refs. [15, 16]) and a planned KNITRO implementation (p. 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- State the outer loop's parameter rule as non-monotone in the theory even if the code is monotone. This lets you reuse the proof for a neighbouring method by reading its multipliers as your parameter (pp. 12–13).
- Trigger a penalty increase from a windowed test (the current value against a fraction of the maximum over the last m iterates), gated by a tolerance tied to μ, so that an oscillating measure does not inflate the parameter (pp. 19–20, 24).
- Test a regularization in a bare research solver without production heuristics, so that the measured effect is the approach's own (p. 20).
- Set the complementarity tolerance exponent from the distance-to-solution rate without strict complementarity (√μ); here ε_comp = μ^0.4 (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "We chose to work with ipm-d because it is a simple interior solver that does not employ the regularizations, scalings, and other heuristics used in production packages that alter the MPCC, making it harder to assess the impact of the approach proposed in this paper." (p. 20)
- "We find it interesting that convergence to C-stationary points is possible in practice and is not simply allowed by the theory." (p. 24)

### S126 · Steering exact penalty methods for optimization (Optimization Technology Center, Northwestern University, technical report, February 25, 2006, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–23). First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors, dated February 25, 2006 (p. 1). This is the earlier report version of S042 (OMS 2008), which card c05 read from the April 10, 2007 report.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd, Richard A. Waltz; Fletcher and Gould thanked (p. 20) · **Position in trajectory**: generalizes the penalty rule of S035 and S058 to penalty SQP and to merit functions.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: exact penalty methods are back in favour because they handle degenerate problems and MPCCs (p. 2). The paper tells a three-stage history: unconstrained penalty minimization, linearly constrained subproblems, and per-iteration steering (p. 2). Sℓ1QP-type methods never reached production software, which the authors conjecture is due to the choice of ν (p. 5).
  - Two one-dimensional examples: with ν < 1 the iterates move away from the solution (p. 6); for min x³ no fixed ν works from every start (p. 7).
  - Multiplier-based rules and a huge fixed ν are rejected (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea**: an auxiliary LP, the subproblem with ν = ∞, gives the best attainable reduction in linear feasibility. Three guidelines follow, made precise as (5.13) with ε1 and (5.14) with ε2 (pp. 8–10), and implemented in Algorithm 5.1 (p. 10).
  - In SLQP (Knitro 4.0 Active): ν0 = 10, ε1 = 0.1, ε2 = 0.5; ν is multiplied by 10 and the LP re-solved until the conditions hold (p. 12).
  - Trust-region SQP relaxations (Byrd–Omojokun, Powell–Yuan, Celis–Dennis–Tapia) are recast as rules of the same kind (pp. 15–17).
  - For line-search merit functions, the rule is ν⁺ = ν_TRIAL + 1 (pp. 18–19).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f, h and g twice continuously differentiable (p. 3). §7 treats equality constraints only, with W positive definite on the null space (p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no theorems. The examples are revisited under Algorithm 5.1 (pp. 13–14), and a descent derivation is given ((7.30)–(7.31), p. 19). Global convergence is delegated to [7] (S058), whose condition is less restrictive than (5.14) (p. 13).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - *ADLITTLE*: the fixed-ν failure was "confirmed ... experimentally". Knitro-Active solves the problem in 1 iteration with Δ0 = 10¹⁰ and in 6 iterations with Δ0 = 10 (pp. 14–15).
  - *Huge-fixed-ν ablation* on a CUTEr subset: problems solved fell from 485 of 616 to 449 at ν = 10⁵ and to 321 at ν = 10¹⁰ (p. 15).
  - *Cost*: the extra LPs take under three percent of the simplex iterations (p. 13).
  - Two efficiency claims come without a table (pp. 12, 19).
- **D6 Positioning** (one line): the rule differs from Chen–Goldfarb and from Leyffer et al. [29] (= S027) in judging ν by its effect on the step, based on the current model (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - penalty SQP was not implemented (p. 11);
  - extending the guidelines to penalty line-search methods is left open (p. 19);
  - choosing ν "requires, in some cases, the solution of an auxiliary subproblem" (p. 20).
- **Version note**: compared with the 2007 report read for S042 (card c05), the argument, examples and numbers are the same, and most pages agree or shift by about one page. For example, the open question is on p. 19 here and p. 20 there. The S042 card reports a comparison with Yuan's condition [42] at p. 10 of the 2007 report. That comparison is absent from pp. 9–10 here, and Yuan is not in this report's reference list (pp. 20–23).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: this is the SKILL.md worked case in its earliest report form (pp. 5, 7–12): the engine is kept, only ν is replaced, and the rule is driven by an explicit estimate (the ν = ∞ LP).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the heuristic of a huge fixed ν is ablated (p. 15).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: Fletcher's ADLITTLE is used as a specification and acknowledged (pp. 14–15, 20).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the support is examples and one ablation. Theory is delegated to S058 under a weaker condition, with no rate (p. 13).
- New-pattern candidates: *specify by admissible conditions so the theory covers the code* (p. 13); *price the repair in warm starts* (pp. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Recast rival relaxation rules (the Byrd–Omojokun β, Powell–Yuan, Celis–Dennis–Tapia) as instances of one rule: ask for a fraction of the attainable feasibility gain. This shows that a new rule belongs to an established family (pp. 15–17).
- Report the extra cost of a parameter-selection loop as a share of the main solver's work (extra simplex iterations under three percent), not in isolation (pp. 12–13).
- Test a folk heuristic (fix one huge parameter value for all problems) directly: rerun the full test set at two large fixed values and report the number of problems solved (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "The choice of the penalty parameter then ceases to be a heuristic and becomes an integral part of the step computation." (p. 2)
- "The methods proposed in Section 5 differ from these strategies in that they assess the effect of the penalty parameter on the step to be taken, based on the current model of the problem." (p. 15)
