### S011 · Knitro: An Integrated Package for Nonlinear Optimization (Large-Scale Nonlinear Optimization 2006, DOI 10.1007/0-387-30065-1_4)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–22 read in full; reference list pp. 22–25 skimmed). First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1). Version read: author preprint dated July 6, 2005, which describes Knitro 5.0 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd, Richard A. Waltz · **Place in trajectory**: the package paper for Knitro 5.0. It gathers the 1999 Interior/CG algorithm ([6], p. 6), the 2004–2006 Interior/Direct hybrid (S013; [38], p. 5) and the SLQP active-set method ([5], p. 12) into one code; S042 and S105 build on it.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper starts from the user's dilemma, not from a theorem. Even "the most efficient algorithms available at present provide no guarantees of success or of fast performance over a range of applications" (p. 1). Each algorithm sits in its own package with its own formats, options and conventions, so users face "a steep learning curve", and NEOS "addresses only some of these issues" (pp. 1–2). The model for the remedy is the integrated LP/MIP package: "The impressive success of an integrated approach of this sort for linear and integer programming ... argues for a similar approach to be taken in nonlinear optimization" (p. 2). Benchmarks cannot yet say which problems suit interior and which suit active-set methods ("the rapid pace of software development makes it difficult to arrive at concrete conclusions", p. 3).
  - Transferable: open a software paper with the user's choice problem (many codes, no uniform winner) and an existing integrated package from a neighbouring field as the model (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Three algorithms are integrated around shared modules (Fig. 1, p. 3).
  - **Interior/Direct**: a primal-dual line-search step from factoring (3.4). The step is rejected if the inertia is not (n+m, l+m, 0) or if the step lengths fall below α_min; Interior/CG steps then replace it (pp. 5–7, Algorithm 3.1).
  - **Interior/CG**: an SQP trust-region method "specifically tailored to the barrier problem". The S⁻¹ scaling of the slacks in the trust region is "crucial" (p. 8). The normal step is computed by dogleg inside 0.8Δ (pp. 8–9). The tangential step uses projected CG with Steihaug termination (p. 9; Algorithm 3.2, p. 10).
  - **Active**: SLQP (pp. 12–14). An ℓ1-penalty LP with ‖d‖∞ ≤ Δ_LP, solved by simplex, picks the working set. Then come a Cauchy step and an EQP step solved by projected CG (Algorithm 4.1, p. 14).

  Changed relative to the classical designs:
  - SLQP instead of SQP, because general QP subproblems limit problem size and second derivatives in SQP have "proved to be difficult" (p. 12).
  - A trust-region fallback instead of Hessian modification (p. 6).
  - A penalty parameter chosen at every iteration by extra LP solves, instead of being held fixed (pp. 14–15).

  Unchanged: log-barrier subproblems (3.1), the nondifferentiable ℓ2 merit function (3.3) and standard ratio tests (pp. 4–5, 10).

  Further features:
  - Four adaptive barrier rules besides the monotone rule (pp. 4–5).
  - Hessian options: exact, Hessian-vector products, finite-difference products, and BFGS/SR1/L-BFGS with Powell damping (p. 18).
  - A feasible mode through the slack reset s⁺ = c_I(x⁺) (p. 18).
  - An affine-scaling initial-point strategy (p. 19).
  - Automatic reduction on special classes (pp. 19–20) and crossover (pp. 20–22).
  - Transferable: put a cheap explicit diagnostic (inertia count, step length below a threshold) in front of a line-search Newton step, and fall back to a trust-region step instead of modifying the Hessian (pp. 5–7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, c_E and c_I are twice continuously differentiable (p. 2). Only local minimizers are sought, and the user's x0 is to be respected, because user-supplied estimates "often lie in the vicinity of a minimizer of interest" (p. 19). Projected CG assumes G positive definite on the null space of A; Steihaug termination covers the case where it is not (pp. 16–17). LP or QP structure must be declared by the user (p. 20). The infeasibility heuristics cannot separate an infeasible problem from an infeasible stationary point (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a for theorems; the paper proves nothing. Convergence is cited from other papers: the penalty update "guarantees that ν is chosen large enough to ensure convergence to a stationary point [4]" (p. 15); Interior/CG rests on [3, 6] (p. 6); Interior/Direct on [38] (p. 5). Short arguments given in the text:
  - Inertia (3.6) makes d a descent direction for φν (p. 5).
  - The σ switch (3.18) makes the directional derivative negative (p. 11).
  - Projected CG iterates are reduced CG on ZᵀGZ preconditioned by ZᵀDZ, so no null-space basis is needed and ill-conditioning in Z is avoided (p. 17).
  - The infeasibility test uses the theory of [3]: A_Eᵀc_E → 0 and A_Iᵀc_I⁻ → 0 when no feasible point is found (p. 20).
  - The crossover radius (7.22) excludes all inactive constraints if the active-set estimate is correct (p. 22).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none; the paper has no numerical results. The two performance statements rest on experience:
  - The merit function is "as tolerant as a filter mechanism", citing Wächter–Biegler's Table 2 (p. 5).
  - The penalty update gives "an overall savings in iterations (and total LP solves)", and the extra LPs are cheap thanks to warm starts (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper surveys Snopt, FilterSQP, Minos, Lancelot, Conopt, Loqo, Barnlp, Ipopt, Mosek and Pennon by approach (p. 2), and the new SLQP work of Chin–Fletcher and Byrd et al. (p. 3). It sets itself apart in three places:
  - from line-search interior codes (Barnlp, Ipopt, Loqo) that modify the Hessian (p. 6);
  - from penalty rules that hold ν fixed: "Unlike most strategies proposed in the literature [11]" (p. 14);
  - from every nonlinear interior code on crossover (p. 20).

  It answers the reported interference of merit functions [22, 34] with its own experience and the filter authors' own table (p. 5). Collaborators are credited by what each contributed (p. 22).
  - Transferable: when you dispute a rival school's claim, cite the rival's own table that supports you (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A software description: the figure comes first (p. 3), each algorithm gets a box (pp. 7, 10, 14, 15, 16, 21), and each design choice gets a one-sentence reason ("We prefer to revert to a trust region iteration because ...", p. 6). The hedges are explicit: "The descriptions are inevitably incomplete" (pp. 3–4); "Since it is not known at present which one is the most effective in practice" (p. 5). "As is well known" carries several central claims (pp. 1, 19, 20). There is no conclusion; the paper ends with crossover (p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a software package and an integration of algorithms. The limits are stated concretely:
  - no infeasibility certificate (p. 20);
  - initial-point strategies only in Interior/Direct (p. 19);
  - a D = I preconditioner, with banded and incomplete Cholesky preconditioners promised for "future releases" (p. 17);
  - a DIRECT active-set option foreseen (p. 3);
  - LP/QP must be flagged by the user (p. 20);
  - the barrier rule left to user experiment (p. 5).

  The paper offers no numerical evidence for the integrated design.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: integration with crossover, on the LP/MIP analogy (pp. 2–3, 20–22): "We take the view that interior-point and active-set methods will both be needed in the years to come" (p. 3).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the undecided barrier rule is exposed as an option (pp. 4–5), and failure modes and missing pieces are listed (infeasibility, p. 20; preconditioner, p. 17). The preconditioner promise is taken up in S105 (pp. 12–17).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: Interior/Direct keeps the primal-dual line-search engine. On explicit diagnostics (the inertia count; step lengths below α_min) it replaces only the failing iteration by a trust-region step (p. 6). The repair is a fallback to a second engine, not a modified component. The SLQP penalty update (pp. 14–15) is the early form of the steering in S042.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the reported interference of nonsmooth merit functions is challenged with "our experience" and the rival's table, not with a dedicated test (p. 5).
- Also Heuristic 5: parameter values are given as numbers (τ = 0.995, p. 5; ρ = 0.1, p. 11; 0.8Δ, p. 8; ν0 = 10‖(y,z)‖∞, p. 21).
- New-pattern candidate: special-case reduction as a design requirement (pp. 2, 19–20). New-pattern candidate: cite the rival's own data (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Require a general solver to reduce automatically to the accepted special-purpose method on each simple class, and say so in the paper: unconstrained → trust-region Newton-CG; nonlinear equations → Levenberg–Marquardt; LP → simplex (pp. 19–20).
- Build one shared kernel (projected CG with a constraint preconditioner) for several algorithms. Reuse its factorization for the normal step, the multipliers and an initial feasible point (pp. 15, 17).
- Guard a line-search Newton step with a trust-region step that fires on cheap explicit diagnostics, not on Hessian modification (pp. 5–7).
- Crossover from interior to active set: a tolerance-based active-set estimate and one EQP step first. Only if that fails, run an LP with radius min c_i/‖∇c_i‖ over the inactive constraints and ν0 = 10‖(y,z)‖∞ (pp. 21–22).
- Initial point: evaluate the first Hessian with the original multipliers z0, not the shifted z1, so that large shifted multipliers do not add artificial nonconvexity (p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "We prefer to revert to a trust region iteration because this permits us to compute a step using a null-space approach, without modifying the Hessian" (p. 6)
- "Although crossover techniques have received much attention in the context of linear programming [27], to the best of our knowledge, none of the nonlinear interior codes provide an option for it." (p. 20)

### S013 · An interior algorithm for nonlinear optimization that combines line search and trust region steps (Mathematical Programming 2006, DOI 10.1007/s10107-004-0560-5)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–17 read in full; reference list pp. 18–20 skimmed). First page checked: title and "J. Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1). Version read: preprint dated September 8, 2004.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard A. Waltz, José Luis Morales, Dominique Orban · **Place in trajectory**: Knitro-Direct, the second interior algorithm in Knitro, added to the first release's trust-region CG method ([6], pp. 1–2). S011 describes it as Interior/Direct, and S042 (pp. 18–20) later analyses its merit-parameter rule.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Every line-search interior method must handle two issues: a nonconvex quadratic model, and rank deficiency of the Hessian or the Jacobian (p. 2). Each code handles them differently: Loqo's identity shift, perturbed factorizations, ℓ1/ℓ2 penalization, or a restoration phase (p. 2). The internal motive is that the first-release projected CG "can be expensive when the Hessian of the Lagrangian ... is ill-conditioned" (p. 2). The goal is a method "more robust and efficient than either a pure trust region or a pure line search interior approach" (p. 1), implemented "easily" inside existing Knitro (p. 2). The known failure behind the safeguard is convergence of line-search interior iterations to non-stationary points [7, 26] (p. 4).
  - Transferable: state the two issues every rival method must solve and list how each rival solves them, then offer yours as one more option on that list (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Direct-factorization steps are always tried first. A trust-region step that guarantees progress is invoked when one of three triggers fires (Algorithm 2.1, p. 5):
  1. the inertia count neig exceeds l + m;
  2. the step to the boundary is too short, min{α_z^max, α_λ^max} ≤ δ;
  3. backtracking fails after imax = 3 trials or with α_T < δ.

  The values are η = 10⁻⁸, δ = 10⁻⁵, imax = 3 (p. 6). Hessian modification is rejected because it "may introduce undesirable distortions in the model" and can require several factorizations (p. 4). A dog-leg combination is rejected because the Cauchy step needs a different factorization and is ill-defined under negative curvature (p. 6).

  The hand-off between the two kinds of step is designed explicitly (pp. 8, 12):
  - after a successful line-search step, Δ_{k+1} = 2α‖d‖ (3.22);
  - after a trust-region step, the first backtrack is α_T = min(1/2, Δ/‖d‖);
  - after a rejected trust-region step, the method stays with trust-region steps until one succeeds.

  Other components:
  - The penalty parameter comes from a model decrease, pred ≥ ρν‖c‖, with σ = 1 only if dᵀWd > 0, ρ = 0.1, and ν⁺ = ν_TRIAL + 1 (pp. 6–7).
  - A second-order correction is tried only when the rejection is due solely to constraint growth (p. 8).
  - Slack reset (3.13) (p. 9).
  - Scaled NLP stopping tests (3.14) (p. 10).
  - μ is divided by 100 if the last barrier problem took fewer than three iterations, else by 5 (p. 10).
  - Barrier tolerances are tied to the NLP tolerances (pp. 10–12).
  - MA27 pivot-tolerance escalation (p. 12).
  - Compact L-BFGS with Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury (p. 13).

  Unchanged from the standard method: the primal-dual Newton system (2.4), the fraction-to-boundary rule with τ = 0.995, and backtracking (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, h and g are twice continuously differentiable (p. 2). The method takes exact second derivatives or quasi-Newton approximations (p. 1). The L-BFGS inversion assumes A has full rank; otherwise the method falls back to the trust-region step (p. 13). The paper states no assumptions under which convergence would hold (see D4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: There is no convergence theorem. The paper asserts that the method "possesses global convergence properties similar to those of the algorithms implemented in FilterSQP [15] and Knitro [5]" (p. 4), and leaves the safeguarding steps to [6] (p. 6). Derivations given in the text:
  - The ν rule gives Dφν ≤ −ρν‖c‖ (3.5)–(3.8). The paper remarks that σ = 1 under negative curvature could destroy descent (p. 7).
  - Barrier-test satisfaction implies NLP-test satisfaction when ε_μ^opt ≤ ε^opt − μ and ε_μ^feas ≤ ε^feas (3.16)–(3.18). This leads to the barrier tolerances (3.19)–(3.20) and μ_min = min{ε^opt, ε^feas}/100 (3.21) (pp. 11–12).
  - The inertia criterion cites [21, Lemma 16.3] (p. 4).
  - The low-rank inverse (3.24) (p. 13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Knitro-Direct is compared with Knitro-CG (Knitro 3.0) on 968 CUTEr problems (as of June 5, 2003), with limits of 15 CPU minutes and 3000 outer iterations, a reached limit counted as a failure, and ε_opt = ε_feas = 10⁻⁶ (p. 14). Results are shown as Dolan–Moré log₂ profiles (p. 14).
  - **Function evaluations, all 968 problems**: "remarkably similar" robustness and efficiency; the two codes share stop tests, merit function, second-order correction and barrier rule (p. 14, Fig. 1).
  - **CPU time**: only on the 420 problems with n + m̄ ≥ 1000, since timings on small problems are "uninteresting and can be unreliable" (pp. 14, 16, Fig. 2).
  - **Mechanism behind the timings**:
    - on the 29 problems where Direct was ≥10× faster, about 90% of Knitro-CG's time was spent in CG, and only one of them reported negative curvature;
    - where Direct was much slower, factorization took ≥85% of the time or negative curvature was frequent (p. 15).
  - **Table 1** (788 solved problems): the trust-region step was invoked in 22.9% of iterations: 15.9% for negative curvature, 6.0% for failed backtracking, 1.0% for a cut-back (p. 16).
  - **Quasi-Newton options** on 548 problems with n + m̄ < 1000: SR1 is slightly better than BFGS, and L-BFGS (p = 20) is close to dense BFGS, so it is suggested as the default (pp. 16–17).
  - **Exact Hessian vs L-BFGS vs finite differences** on all 968 problems (Fig. 4): the exact Hessian solved 788 and L-BFGS 693; the finite-difference Hessian-vector option is "only slightly less efficient" in evaluations (p. 17).
  - No external code (Ipopt, Loqo) is run.
  - Transferable: report why a safeguard fires, as a table of iteration shares per trigger (p. 16), and explain CPU wins by the internal share of time per kernel (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper credits the rival: identity-shift convexification "was first shown to be effective in the context of nonlinear interior methods by the Loqo software package [25]" (p. 2). It sets the fallback against the Hessian modification of other line-search codes (p. 4), and cites the Wächter–Biegler failure [26] and Byrd–Marazzi–Nocedal [7] as the failure to prevent (p. 4). It dismisses superlinear barrier rules [11, 17, 22, 29] because they "employ various parameters that can be difficult to select in practice" (p. 10). The ν rule is attributed to El-Hallabi [13] (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Design choices are argued by numbered reasons (p. 4: "for two reasons"; p. 6: "for two reasons"). Parameter values are given inline as "In our tests we choose ..." (pp. 4, 6, 7, 10, 12). The introduction promises that "We will argue that the algorithm ... has favorable convergence properties" (p. 2); the paper keeps that promise only with the analogy on p. 4. One notational slip: the Armijo test of Algorithm 2.1 uses α_z^max (p. 5), while (3.9) uses α_λ^max (p. 8). There is no conclusion; the paper ends with the discussion of Fig. 4 (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm plus its software (Knitro-Direct). Its limits are visible only in the results: Direct loses when factorization is expensive or negative curvature is frequent (p. 15); the quasi-Newton versions solve fewer problems (693 vs 788, p. 17); the finite-difference option may be inefficient in CPU time if gradients are expensive (p. 17). There is no comparison with external codes and no future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the classical primal-dual engine is kept and is replaced, iteration by iteration, only when explicit diagnostics fire (the inertia count, step length thresholds, backtrack count), with a trust-region recovery path (pp. 4–5). The repair is a fallback to a second engine, with the radius handed over between them (pp. 8, 12).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: two complementary step computations in "a unified algorithmic and software framework" (p. 2).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the rival's idea is credited (Loqo, p. 2), and a known external failure (Wächter–Biegler; Byrd–Marazzi–Nocedal) is answered with a design (p. 4).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the baseline is the group's own previous release, not a rival code. The shared components control implementation effects (p. 14), and CPU time is reported only where set-up cost does not dominate (p. 14), as in Method 1 step 7.
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (in this paper): global convergence of the implemented hybrid is asserted by analogy to FilterSQP and Knitro (p. 4), not analysed for the version that is shipped.
- Also Heuristic 2 (the trigger frequencies in Table 1, p. 16; the time shares, p. 15), Heuristic 5 (the simple μ rule over superlinear rules, p. 10; L-BFGS as the default, p. 17) and Heuristic 8 (the new algorithm inside Knitro, compared with Knitro's default, p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: tolerance coupling, i.e. inner stopping tests set so that inner success implies outer success (pp. 10–12). New-pattern candidate: practice first, rationale later (the ν rule "is inspired by trust region methods", p. 6; S042 analyses it afterwards, pp. 18–20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Hand state between line-search and trust-region steps: Δ = 2α‖d‖ after a line-search step; first backtrack min(1/2, Δ/‖d‖) after a trust-region step; stay with trust-region steps after a rejected one until it succeeds (pp. 8, 12).
- Couple the inner and outer tolerances: ε_μ^opt = max{θμ, ε^opt − μ}, ε_μ^feas = max{θμ, ε^feas}, μ_min = min{ε^opt, ε^feas}/100, so the barrier subproblem is never oversolved and its success implies NLP success (pp. 10–12).
- Scale the stopping tests by max{1, ‖∇f‖∞} and by the initial infeasibility, and state which invariances each factor buys (pp. 9–10).
- Try a second-order correction only when the rejection comes solely from constraint growth, reusing the existing factorization: one forward and one back solve plus one evaluation (pp. 8–9).
- On a singularity message, raise the pivot tolerance by a factor of 10 from 10⁻⁸ up to 0.5; apply at most five steps of iterative refinement when the relative residual is large (p. 12).
- Report the trigger shares of a safeguard, and the internal time shares behind CPU differences (pp. 15–16).

**Quotes**:
- "In this paper we describe a mechanism for stabilizing the line search iteration that is different from those proposed in the literature." (p. 2)
- "We use instead the following simple strategy for updating µ that has performed as well in our tests as have more complicated superlinear rules." (p. 10)

### S042 · Steering exact penalty methods for nonlinear programming (Optimization Methods and Software 2008, DOI 10.1080/10556780701394169)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–20 read in full; reference list pp. 21–24 skimmed). First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1). Version read: Optimization Technology Center technical report dated April 10, 2007.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd, Richard A. Waltz · **Place in trajectory**: it generalizes the penalty update of the SLQP paper ([6]; Knitro-Active, S011 pp. 14–15) into guidelines for penalty SQP, and then analyses the line-search merit rule of S013 (pp. 18–20). This is the Method 3 worked case of SKILL.md.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Exact penalty methods are back in favour because they handle degeneracy, inconsistent linearizations and MPCCs (p. 2). The paper tells the history in three stages: penalty functions as an unconstrained route (ineffective), linearly constrained subproblems that "leave open the question of how to choose the penalty parameter", and per-iteration steering (p. 2). The diagnosis: Sℓ1QP-type methods "were never incorporated into production-quality software", which the authors conjecture is due to the choice of the penalty parameter (p. 5).
  - Example 1 (min x s.t. x ≥ 1): for ν < 1 the penalty function is monotone, so iterates move away from the solution (p. 6).
  - Example 2 (min x³ s.t. x ≥ −1): for every ν there is a starting point with no decreasing path to x* (p. 7).
  - The multiplier-based rule fails, and so does a huge fixed ν (p. 7).
  - These difficulties drove the field to filter methods (p. 7).
  - Transferable: motivate a parameter rule with two one-dimensional counterexamples, one for "too small" and one for "no fixed value works from every start", each with a figure (pp. 6–8).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: ν is set against an auxiliary LP, the ν = ∞ version of the subproblem, which gives the best attainable reduction in linearized infeasibility inside the trust region (5.10) (pp. 8–9). There are three guidelines (p. 9):
  1. reach linear feasibility when it is attainable;
  2. otherwise achieve a fraction ε1 of the best reduction (5.13);
  3. make the model decrease at least ε2ν times the feasibility gain (5.14) (p. 10).

  Algorithm 5.1 implements them (p. 11). Two variants are given: one treats both cases with (5.13) but then needs ε1 → 1 for a fast rate; the other skips recomputing d(ν_k) (p. 11).

  In SLQP (Knitro 4.0 Active; pp. 12–13): ν0 = 10, ε1 = 0.1, ε2 = 0.5, and ν is multiplied by 10 and the LP re-solved until the conditions hold.

  Two links to other methods:
  - Trust-region SQP: the Byrd–Omojokun β, the Celis–Dennis–Tapia Cauchy step and ε1 "all play similar roles" (pp. 16–17).
  - Line-search merit functions: rule (7.28)–(7.29), ν⁺ = ν_TRIAL + 1 with the σ switch, follows from the guidelines (pp. 18–20).

  Unchanged: the ℓ1 penalty function and the penalty-SQP/SLQP step; only the ν update is new.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, h_i and g_i are twice continuously differentiable (p. 3). For suitable ν, stationary points of φν are KKT points or infeasible stationary points (p. 3). Near a regular solution, any ν > ‖λ*‖∞ suffices [26] (p. 7). In §7, W is assumed positive definite on the null space of the Jacobian (p. 19), and only equality constraints are treated, "For simplicity" (p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: There are no theorems; the paper relies on constructions and short derivations.
  - Two counterexamples with plots (pp. 6–8).
  - The examples revisited under Algorithm 5.1. In Example 1 the rule forces ν ≥ 1 or ν > 1 depending on Δ (p. 14). In Example 2, (5.14) requires ν > 3x_k²/(1 − ε2), which "enlarged the basin of attraction of the solution to include the current iterate" (p. 14, Fig. 3).
  - The descent derivation (7.28) ⇒ (7.31), Dφν ≤ −ε2ν‖h‖, with the remark that it fails if σ = 1 and dᵀWd < 0 (pp. 19–20).
  - Global convergence is cited, not proved: the SLQP analysis in [7] uses a weaker condition than (5.14), "Therefore the analysis in [7] is of broad applicability" (p. 13).
  - Transferable: when the analysed condition is weaker than the one you implement, show the inclusion so that the theory covers the code (p. 13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **ADLITTLE (Netlib LP)**: the penalty function needs ν ≳ 3.31 × 10³. The fixed-ν failure was reproduced: "We confirmed this behavior experimentally." (p. 15).
  - **Knitro-Active on ADLITTLE**: with ν0 = 10, Δ0 = 10¹⁰ and scaling off, it raised ν to 10⁴ and solved the problem in one iteration; with Δ0 = 10 it took 6 iterations, reaching the final ν = 10⁴ at iteration 3 (p. 15).
  - **Ablation of the huge-fixed-ν heuristic** on a CUTEr subset: solved problems fell from 485 of 616 to 449 at ν = 10⁵ and to 321 at ν = 10¹⁰. The failures were traced to small increases in infeasibility near the feasible region that dominate the objective (pp. 15–16).
  - **Cost**: the extra LPs used "less than three percent" of the simplex iterations (p. 13).
  - **Claims without data**: two claims come with no table or test set: Algorithm 5.1 is "substantially more efficient and robust than classical strategies" (p. 13), and the merit rule is "significantly more effective" in Knitro-Interior/Direct (p. 20). Penalty SQP was not implemented (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper "reviews, extends and analyzes" the strategy (abstract, p. 1), whose earlier form is in [6] (p. 2). It places itself against:
  - Fletcher's Sℓ1QP (p. 5);
  - multiplier-based rules (p. 7);
  - Chen–Goldfarb [10] and Leyffer et al. [30], who unlike this paper do not "assess the effect of the penalty parameter on the step to be taken" (p. 16);
  - Yuan's condition [42]: similar, but it uses actual decrease and acts on the next step (p. 10).

  It recasts Byrd–Omojokun, Powell–Yuan and Celis–Dennis–Tapia as relaxations of the same kind (pp. 16–17). It cites Wächter's critique of nonsmooth merit functions in line searches [38] (p. 18) and Wächter's own controlled comparison [39] as support (p. 20). Fletcher and Gould are thanked (p. 20).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract's headline is "the choice of the penalty parameter ceases to be a heuristic" (p. 1), repeated in the introduction (p. 2). Conjectures are flagged as conjectures (p. 5). The guidelines are stated in plain words first, then as inequalities, then as an algorithm (pp. 9–11). The motivating examples are revisited after the method (pp. 13–15). Limits are written candidly: "We have not yet developed a software implementation of the penalty SQP method just outlined" (p. 11). The paper ends with an open question (p. 20), and the Final Remarks are three sentences long (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: an algorithmic principle (steering by the best attainable feasibility), its implementation in Knitro-Active, its unification with normal-step trust-region SQP, and a justification of the line-search merit rule. Limits:
  - penalty SQP untested ("cannot evaluate the computational tradeoffs", p. 11);
  - extension to penalty line-search methods "remains an open question" (p. 20);
  - the variant needs ε1 → 1 for a fast rate (p. 11);
  - the main efficiency claims carry no table (pp. 13, 20).

### S105 · A numerical study of active-set and interior-point methods for bound constrained optimization (Modeling, Simulation and Optimization of Complex Processes 2008, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-79409-7_18)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–20 read in full, including Tables 1–3; reference list pp. 20–21 skimmed). First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Long Hei, Richard A. Waltz · **Place in trajectory**: the follow-up numerical study of the Knitro 5.0 algorithms (S011, S013) against specialist bound-constrained codes. It takes up the incomplete Cholesky preconditioning promised in S011 (p. 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is timing: interior-point and active-set methods have both matured, so "It is therefore an appropriate time to evaluate the contributions of these new algorithms in order to identify promising directions of future research" (p. 1). Two obstacles to a fair evaluation are named: implementation details and heuristics, and test sets whose features (nonconvexity, degeneracy, ill-conditioning) affect methods differently (pp. 1–2). The remedy is to restrict attention to bound-constrained problems. Their simple geometry "eliminates the difficulties caused by degenerate constraints" and isolates ill-conditioning, and fast specialized gradient-projection codes put "great demands on the general-purpose methods" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: pick the problem class that removes one difficulty (here degeneracy) and where a specialist code sets a hard bar, so that the general methods' remaining weaknesses become visible (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: The study has three parts, plus a constructive piece.
  - **Methods**: four active-set codes (SNOPT, the SLQP Knitro/Active, the gradient-projection codes TRON and L-BFGS-B) and two interior codes (Knitro/Direct, Knitro/CG) (p. 2). Both interior codes come from Knitro "to minimize the effect of implementation details" (p. 3). SNOPT and L-BFGS-B are held back to the quasi-Newton section (p. 3), so methods are compared like with like on Hessian information.
  - **Part 1**: exact-Hessian tests (pp. 3–8).
  - **Part 2**: preconditioning (pp. 9–17).
    - For SLQP: incomplete Cholesky of the reduced Hessian ZᵀGZ. With bounds, Z holds unit vectors, so this is "essentially the same as that used in tron" (pp. 10–11).
    - For Knitro/CG: block elimination gives the condensed system (11), then incomplete Cholesky of ∇²L + A_IᵀD_sA_I (12). Adding the positive diagonal makes the δI modification less likely (p. 13). The barrier preconditioners are D_s = Σ or μS⁻² (p. 14).
  - **Part 3**: limited-memory quasi-Newton tests with m = 20 in all codes (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f smooth, with bounds l ≤ u (p. 2). The preconditioning analysis assumes G positive definite on the null space and ignores the trust-region and negative-curvature features (pp. 9–10). μS⁻² is justified by being near the central path, Λ ≈ μS⁻¹ (p. 14). The test set is large CUTEr bound-constrained AMPL models, excluding repeated models (pp. 3–4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a for theorems. The explanatory devices:
  - TRON's reduced CG and preconditioned projected CG are equivalent ("It is easy to see that the two approaches are equivalent", p. 11).
  - The block elimination (9) → (10) → (11) (p. 13).
  - A dimension count for the CG puzzle: SLQP works in n − t_k dimensions against n for the interior method, but this "accounts only partly" for the difference (pp. 7–8).
  - Trust-region shapes (14) vs (15). With D_s = μS⁻², small μ "does not penalize a step approaching the bounds s ≥ 0 as severely as (14)", which leads to premature approach to the boundary. D_s = Σ fails when multipliers are inaccurate. Preconditioning also distorts the barrier stop test (pp. 16–17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **Table 1**: 29 problems, n from 2000 to 100000 (p. 5). TRON 1.2 and Knitro 5.0 (Direct, CG, Active), a 10000-iteration limit, and default settings and stopping tests that "appeared to provide comparable solution accuracy in practice" (p. 4).
    - Reported per run: iterations, evaluations, CPU, active bounds (TRON), average CG per iteration, and Knitro/CG's endCG/n. Failures are coded X1–X5 (pp. 4–5). By my count of the table: TRON 3 failures, Direct 1, CG 3, Active 3.
    - Profiles over evaluations and CPU (p. 6).
    - Findings: no significant difference in robustness, with Direct "slightly more reliable"; TRON "almost uniformly superior" to Active in iterations; interior methods take 10–30 iterations; Knitro/CG is slowest in CPU because its CG is unpreconditioned; TRON's average CG count per step is "rarely greater than 2", thanks to incomplete Cholesky (p. 7).
  - **Table 2**: a MATLAB version of Knitro/CG on 7 small problems (n = 100–324) × 9 preconditioner options (pp. 14–15). For example, biggsb1's total CG fell from 3962 to 44, and obstclbm's from 7900 to 49, with incomplete Cholesky (p. 15). Barrier preconditioning helps alone but adds nothing on top of incomplete Cholesky; diagonal Hessian preconditioning "rarely provides much benefit"; cvxbqp1's iteration counts change with the preconditioner (p. 16).
  - **Table 3**: SNOPT 7.2-1, L-BFGS-B and the three Knitro codes, all L-BFGS with m = 20, all at defaults (pp. 17–18). The Knitro codes show "A sharp drop in robustness and speed". L-BFGS-B is "by far the best" in CPU time with Active second; SNOPT and the interior codes need comparable time, "unacceptably high" on some problems (p. 20).
  - Transferable: tabulate internal counters (average CG per iteration, CG at the last iteration divided by n, active bounds at the solution) beside the usual iterations, evaluations and CPU. They are what let you explain the profiles (pp. 4–5, 7–8).
- **D6 Positioning**: The study takes the Gould et al. survey as its map (p. 1). Its remedies point to the group's own next work: the Byrd–Waltz parametric LP and bound-adding SLQP [6] (p. 8). TRON's incomplete Cholesky is credited to Lin–Moré [14] (p. 7); preconditioning in interior methods cites [1, 8, 13] (p. 3). No other interior-point code (Ipopt, Loqo) is run; the choice is defended as controlling implementation effects (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract leads with the authors' own method losing: SLQP "is robust, but is not as effective as gradient projection at identifying the optimal active set" (p. 1). The observations are grouped under run-in heads (Function Evaluations, CPU Time, Active-set identification), each followed by a mechanism (pp. 7–8). The hedges are visible: "we speculate" (p. 16); "more research is needed" (p. 8). There is no conclusion heading; the paper ends with an "In summation" paragraph (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: a numerical study, plus the design of incomplete Cholesky preconditioners for SLQP and for the bound-constrained interior method. Limits:
  - preconditioning tested only in a MATLAB prototype on n ≤ 324 (p. 14);
  - the Knitro/Active incomplete Cholesky results deferred to "a forthcoming publication" (p. 11);
  - stopping tests left at defaults, not aligned (p. 4);
  - only bound constraints.

  Future directions: SLQP active-set identification via parametric LP and bound adding (p. 8); "improved updating procedures" for quasi-Newton in general-purpose methods (p. 20).

**Method links**:

**Transferable techniques**:
