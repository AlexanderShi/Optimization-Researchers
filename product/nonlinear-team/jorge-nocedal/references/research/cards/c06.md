### S029 · Adaptive barrier update strategies for nonlinear interior methods (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2009, DOI 10.1137/060649513)
**Read level**: full. Whole text read, file pp. 1–25: abstract, §§1–8, acknowledgment. The reference list (pp. 23–25) was skimmed. The version read is the authors' preprint of July 21, 2008 (p. 1), not the typeset journal version. Figures 1–5, 7 and 8 are performance profiles whose curves are not in the text layer. Their content is taken from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Andreas Wächter (IBM T. J. Watson), Richard A. Waltz (Northwestern) (p. 1); Richard Byrd thanked "for many valuable suggestions" (p. 22) · **Place in trajectory**: KNITRO / nonlinear-interior period. It is the barrier-rule study that SKILL.md Method 4 links, by timing, to the 2006 Knitro option for barrier updates.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The subject is how to choose the barrier parameter µ in primal-dual nonlinear interior methods for (2.1), min f s.t. c(x) = 0, x ≥ 0 (p. 3). The monotone Fiacco–McCormick rule supports global convergence but "can be very sensitive to the choice of the initial point, the initial value of the barrier parameter and the scaling of the problem", and recovers slowly when iterates hit the boundary early (p. 4). Adaptive rules (Mehrotra probing, LOQO) are fast but lack guarantees. The direct NLP extension of Mehrotra's predictor–corrector "does not result in a robust method", and the corrector is named as the main source of instability (p. 2). The motivation is stated as a belief about robustness: methods must react to scale changes and correct overly aggressive µ decreases (p. 1). The entry is empirical. §3 compares the existing rules on CUTEr with no globalization ("In this initial investigation, we do not impose a rigorous globalization scheme on the methods", p. 2). In IPOPT the line search is switched off, so pure fraction-to-the-boundary steps are measured (p. 6).
  - Transferable: before designing a safeguard, run the unsafeguarded candidates (here with the line search disabled, p. 6) to measure the quality of the raw steps. The result tells you how little the safeguard should interfere (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: There are three pieces. (a) *Quality function*: with µ = σxᵀz/n, σ minimizes a linear quality function q_L(σ). q_L is the KKT error predicted at the probing point after the fraction-to-the-boundary rule, and it is exact for LP (p. 8). Because ∆(σ) = ∆(0) + σ(∆(1) − ∆(0)), two solves of (2.5) suffice, after which each q_L evaluation costs a few vector operations (p. 8). A golden trisection runs on [σmin, 1] or on [1, σmax = 1000], with at most 12 evaluations. Values σ > 1 are allowed so that the algorithm "can recover from overly aggressive reductions of the barrier parameter" (p. 9). (b) *Globalization*: a free mode with any µ rule runs under a progress test, and falls back to a monotone (Fiacco–McCormick) mode that ends as soon as progress is restored (pp. 9–10). Algorithm A requires a nonmonotone decrease of the KKT error Φ by κ within l_max iterations (κ = 0.9999, l_max = 5, re-entry µ̄ = 0.8xᵀz/n; pp. 10–11). Algorithm B uses an (f, θ) filter with margin δ_k = κ1 min{κ2, Φ} (κ1 = 10⁻⁵, κ2 = 1; pp. 11–12). (c) *Conditional MPC*: the corrector is kept only if complementarity does not grow by more than a factor of 2 (LP, p. 20). In NLP it is rejected if complementarity grows at all or if the merit or filter test fails, and it is never used in monotone mode (pp. 20–21). Unchanged: the primal-dual system (2.5), the fraction-to-the-boundary rule (2.8), and each code's merit function or filter (pp. 3–4, 10). The stated reason for (a) is a rule "not based on heuristics but follows a clear-cut objective" (p. 2). The light-touch design of (b) follows from the §3 finding that unglobalized steps already converge on most problems (p. 7).
  - Transferable: when the step is affine in a scalar parameter, solve at two parameter values and search the parameter over a cheap predicted merit (p. 8).
  - Transferable: wrap any heuristic in a free/monotone two-mode scheme. The supervising test (nonmonotone KKT decrease or a filter) leaves the heuristic alone while it makes progress (pp. 9–12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f and c are twice continuously differentiable (p. 3). Only first-order stationarity is targeted. Second order would need the smallest reduced-Hessian eigenvalue, "too expensive in the large-scale case" (p. 4). Theorem 5.1 assumes that "the monotone mode always terminates successfully". For Algorithm B it also assumes {f(x_k)} bounded below and {‖c(x_k)‖} bounded above (p. 12). The first assumption hands the hard case to each code's existing monotone theory (p. 13). q_L is exact only for linear f and c (p. 8). It is nonconvex in σ, and the trisection ignores that "qL may not necessarily be unimodal", relying on the observation that it usually is (pp. 8–9).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Theorem 5.1 (pp. 12–13). For Algorithm A, Φ falls by κ at least every l_max iterations, so Φ_k → 0 directly. For Algorithm B, a contradiction argument follows Lemma 3.3 of Fletcher et al. [13]. Every acceptance with margin ≥ ε adds a square of area ≥ ε² to the filter inside a bounded box, and the filter only grows (pp. 12–13). The infeasible case is handled by inheritance. If the iteration stays in monotone mode, KNITRO's limit point is stationary for the ℓ2 infeasibility [4], and IPOPT stays in a restoration phase that minimizes ℓ1 infeasibility, so both report local infeasibility (p. 13). The MPC failure is explained geometrically, not by a theorem. In LP the corrector is a Newton step from the full affine-scaling point, but it uses the matrix at the current point and is added at the current point. When α_aff is tiny and the corrector is large, complementarity grows (pp. 18–19, Figure 6). Fast local convergence is argued informally, not proved (p. 22).
  - Transferable: the filter-area argument (bounded box, margin ε, each acceptance adds area ε², monotone filter) is a reusable template for the global convergence of any filter-supervised scheme (pp. 12–13).
  - Transferable: to explain a predictor–corrector failure, write the right-hand side at the predicted point, then ask at which point the correction is actually applied (p. 18).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Two codes are used, IPOPT and KNITRO-Direct (a line search occasionally safeguarded by a trust-region step, p. 6). In KNITRO's trust-region iterations the LOQO rule replaces Mehrotra probing, because probing is expensive there (p. 6). Test set: all CUTEr problems (January 2005) with at least one inequality or bound, minus infeasible, unbounded or non-evaluable ones, giving 599 problems at default sizes (pp. 6–7). Problems on which successful runs end at significantly different objective values are excluded (37 for IPOPT and 54 for KNITRO in Figure 1, p. 7). Dolan–Moré logarithmic profiles are drawn in function evaluations and CPU time (pp. 7, 14–16). Limits are 3000 iterations and 1800 CPU seconds, on the October 2005 development versions. The two codes ran on different machines (p. 13), and the norms in q_L and Φ mirror each code's own termination test (p. 13). The NETLIB LPs are run with no preprocessing and x0 = 0 (p. 13). Table 1 gives the share of iterations in monotone mode (3.70–8.39%) and the distribution of σ (p. 16). Failures are classified one by one (pp. 16–17). KNITRO has 38: 18 solved with larger limits, 5 near-optimal, 2 NaN, HS110 hitting the −10²⁰ unboundedness cutoff (solved in 4 iterations when the cutoff is changed), KTMODEL found to have incorrect gradients, and 11 unresolved, named. IPOPT has 39. For MPC, PCx diverges on Forplan from x = e, z = e (Table 2, p. 19). On the 94 NETLIB problems PCx solves 90 by default but only 28 with its starting-point strategy disabled, and no-corrector and conditional MPC restore robustness (Figure 7, p. 20). The safeguarded corrector in NLP appears in Figure 8 (p. 21). Both globalization frameworks were tested, but only the filter results are reported (p. 12).
  - Transferable: when profiling algorithm variants, drop the problems on which the variants reach different local solutions, and say how many were dropped per code (p. 7).
  - Transferable: end a benchmark with a failure taxonomy: limits, near-optimal, evaluation errors, cutoff values, bad test-problem derivatives, and unresolved cases by name (pp. 16–17).
  - Transferable: to test received wisdom that may rest on a hidden ingredient, switch that ingredient off (PCx's starting-point strategy) and rerun the whole test set (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The rivals' heuristics are credited. LOQO's rule is "particularly noteworthy because of its success in practice" (p. 5), and Mehrotra's method "has proved to be very effective for linear and convex quadratic programming" (p. 2). Most global-convergence work on NLP interior methods ignores adaptive µ. Three exceptions are named [11, 23, 24] (p. 2), and the analyses in [11, 24] are said to enforce only a stationarity measure, not objective decrease (p. 4). Cartis [7] is credited for an independent analysis of MPC failure (p. 20), and Armand et al. [1, 2] for superlinear µ control (p. 22). Co-author Wächter is IPOPT's author ([27], p. 25), so the "two frameworks" are the authors' own two codes (pp. 1–2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The introduction ends with a four-bullet contribution list (p. 2). Each design decision is tied back to a §3 observation ("Motivated by the initial observations of these experiments", p. 2; p. 7). Hedging is moderate, mostly in the abstract ("Numerical experiments suggest", p. 1). The conclusion opens with a two-sided verdict (quote 1, p. 21) and records surprise: "These observations were unexpected" (p. 21). The theory is a half-page theorem placed after the algorithms; most of the paper is empirical.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic (the quality-function rule, conditional MPC), theoretical (a globalization framework for any µ rule), empirical (the corrector is harmful without a starting-point strategy, even in LP) and software (both codes) (pp. 2, 21). Limits are written concretely: 38 and 39 failures listed (pp. 16–17); CPU gains "less pronounced" than evaluation gains (p. 14); fast local convergence not addressed: "We have not done so because the asymptotic behavior of the method proposed in this paper has proved to be acceptable in practice" (p. 22). The future direction offered is to combine the rule with Armand et al.'s µ strategies (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the open choice of barrier rule is studied in two frameworks, including how the incumbent heuristic fails (two codes, p. 2; MPC failures, §7, pp. 17–21). This confirms Method 4 step 3 from the body. The paper does not mention the Knitro option for barrier rules, so the arrow from the 2006 option to this study is still inferred from timing. The frameworks are the authors' own codes (Wächter co-author, p. 1), not a rival's used from outside.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the engine is kept (system (2.5), fraction-to-the-boundary, each code's merit or filter) and only the µ component is replaced (pp. 3–4, 8–10). The replacement is driven by an explicit predicted quantity, "a measurable and achievable decrease in the (linearized) KKT error" (p. 9). It has a recovery path: the monotone mode (pp. 10–12), and σ > 1 to undo over-aggressive decreases (p. 9). Variant detail: the estimate is a model prediction, not a noise level.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the LP view that the corrector "is known to accelerate the interior-point iteration without degrading its robustness" (p. 17) is put on trial by removing its hidden ingredient, the starting point, which takes PCx from 90 to 28 of 94 problems (p. 20). Surprise is recorded and the verdict is scoped: reliability "depends crucially on heuristics, such as the choice of the initial point" (p. 21).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (partial): the comparison is method-level within each code, with rules swapped inside IPOPT and inside KNITRO and the two codes never ranked against each other (pp. 6, 13). Failures are printed (pp. 14–17). No handicapping of the authors' own rule is reported.
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction: Theorem 5.1 is a safety net that holds for "any rule" (Algorithms A and B, pp. 10–12), so by design it cannot separate the quality function from the monotone rule or from MPC. The preference rests on profiles, and the rate is deferred on practical grounds (p. 22). This fits Heuristic 6 (robustness first), not Method 5 steps 2–3.
- new-pattern candidate: **extreme-value probe to set a trade-off parameter**. Mehrotra probing solves at µ = 0 (the affine-scaling step, p. 5), and the quality function solves at σ = 0 and σ = 1 and interpolates (p. 8). Compare S052's ρ = 0 reference step (p. 15) and the ν = ∞ LP in Method 3's steering case.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Measure raw, unglobalized steps first (line search off) and design the globalization to intervene only on stalls (pp. 6–7).
- Two-mode supervision: any heuristic runs in a free mode, a nonmonotone KKT-error test or a filter decides when to fall back to a provably convergent monotone mode, and the fallback ends as soon as progress returns (pp. 10–12).
- Parameter search over a linearized merit at affine-in-σ steps: two linear solves, then a bracketed golden-section search that allows values beyond 1 for recovery (pp. 8–9).
- Filter-area contradiction proof for global convergence (pp. 12–13).
- Failure taxonomy at the end of a benchmark, with names and causes (pp. 16–17).
- Remove one hidden ingredient of an accepted method (here the starting-point heuristic) and rerun the full test set (p. 20).

**Quotes**:
- "We have seen in this paper that, both for linear and nonlinear programming, classical barrier update strategies with global convergence guarantees are overly conservative, while strategies that are often fastest in practice are based on heuristic formulas and are not globally convergent." (p. 21)
- "This observation suggests that the globalization scheme that we propose in Section 5 should interfere minimally with the iteration; it should be active only when the algorithms appears to be making no progress." (p. 7)

### S047 · Data assimilation in weather forecasting: a case study in PDE-constrained optimization (Optimization and Engineering 2009, DOI 10.1007/s11081-008-9051-5)
**Read level**: full. Whole text read, file pp. 1–22: abstract, §§1–7. The reference list (pp. 21–22) was skimmed. The version read is the authors' preprint of January 18, 2008 (p. 1). Figures 1–2 are plots; their content is taken from captions and prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: M. Fisher and Y. Trémolet (ECMWF), S. J. Wright (Wisconsin) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the ECMWF strand (SKILL.md trajectory, c. 1992–2009). This is the paper SKILL.md lists as not read and which he calls "my most important contribution" (2026 interview). The paper itself describes the operational ECMWF system and does not say which parts are the authors' own.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an operational application. 4D-Var produces the initial conditions for 7- to 10-day forecasts (abstract, p. 1), and the paper's aim is to show how "practical demands of the application dictate the various algorithmic choices" (abstract, p. 1). The problem is the weighted least-squares objective (2) over the initial state x̄, with a background term and observation terms, subject to model integration x_i = M_i(x_{i−1}) (1) (p. 4). It is abstracted to equality-constrained data fitting (3) (p. 6). Six features are listed (pp. 5–6): about 7 × 10⁹ variables (3 × 10⁸ per state, N = 24 time points); less than two hours of a 12-hour cycle for the solve; a nonconvex objective with nonlinear constraints; forward integrations affordable only a few times; adjoint-coded derivatives that are not exact ("Some terms are omitted due to the difficulty of writing their adjoints, and certain discontinuities are ignored"); and impractical second derivatives. In its favour, the previous forecast supplies a good starting point (p. 6).
  - Transferable: open an application paper with a numbered list of the features that constrain the algorithm (size, time budget, evaluation cost, derivative accuracy), then refer back to it at every choice (pp. 5–6).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The paper argues for, and describes, the operational method; it does not propose a new one. (a) Limited-memory quasi-Newton Hessians for SQP are rejected for two reasons: there are too few iterations to accumulate curvature, and they ignore the least-squares structure. Gauss–Newton is chosen instead (pp. 7–8). (b) SAND (full-space SQP with the GN Hessian, (8)) is weighed against NAND (eliminate the states, (12)). The GN-NAND step is shown to equal a feasible variant of GN-SAND in which the d component is discarded, written with a null-space matrix Z (pp. 9–10; "This observation is not new", p. 10). The choice is made by three criteria, "storage, stability, and computational cost" (p. 10). Storage favours NAND (7 × 10⁹ components are too many for SAND, p. 10). Stability favours SAND: its basis is flexible, and it never integrates the model at a poor x̄, where "increasing modes" can blow up (pp. 10–11). Cost is equal in 4D-Var, though SAND could apply the M_i in parallel (p. 11). The verdict is NAND for the current system (p. 11), with a hybrid that keeps some time-point states as variables as the remedy if instability appears (p. 11). (c) Multilevel inexact GN: the subproblem (15) is solved at reduced resolution (the restriction S deletes high-frequency spectral coefficients and S⁺ pads zeros), with simplified physics and a longer time step. This is credited as "A breakthrough in 4D-Var" to Courtier et al. [5] (p. 13). (d) Two-level preconditioning. First, a change of variables with the Cholesky factor L of B makes the Hessian identity plus low rank, with eigenvalues ≥ 1 and a condition number still 10³–10⁴ (p. 14). Second, a Lanczos spectral preconditioner (19) is built from the first subproblem's eigenpairs, with µ_j = min(λ̂_j, 10), and carried to the later, higher-resolution subproblems (pp. 15–16). Operational settings are K = 3 outer iterations, 70 Lanczos iterations in the first, m = 25 vectors saved, about 30 CG iterations in the later ones, and no line search (p. 17).
  - Transferable: when weighing full-space against reduced-space formulations, first show when the two steps coincide (the null-space equivalence). Then decide by storage, stability and cost, and keep a hybrid in reserve (pp. 9–11).
  - Transferable: a limited-memory spectral preconditioner. Harvest Lanczos eigenpairs from the first, cheap low-resolution subproblem and reuse them in later ones (pp. 15–17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: This is strong-constraint 4D-Var: the model is assumed perfect over the window. The authors call this "a questionable assumption", to be lifted by weak-constraint 4D-Var (p. 20). Smoothness of x(x̄) needs D_x c uniformly nonsingular (p. 8). The linearized operators are approximations with simplified physics, not exact derivatives (pp. 6, 13). Fast local GN convergence in three steps is argued from the good initial guess, which makes the large-scale problem "only weakly nonlinear" (p. 16). There is no line search, so no guarantee that J decreases (p. 17). The general stance is that "theoretical guarantees of performance must be sacrificed for the sake of practical efficiency" (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No theorems. The derivation devices are four. (i) The NAND/SAND equivalence: eliminate d from (8) and rewrite with the null-space matrix Z (pp. 9–10). (ii) A spectral argument for (17): the identity plus a positive semidefinite sum of rank far below the dimension has eigenvalues ≥ 1 and many unit eigenvalues (p. 14). (iii) Once the m leading eigenpairs have converged, the preconditioner (19) reduces the condition number from λ1 to λ_{m+1} (p. 15). (iv) Lanczos orthogonalization also speeds up minimization, by Notay's result on duplicate eigenvectors (p. 16).
  - Transferable: device (ii). Pick a change of variables (here the prior-covariance Cholesky factor) that makes the Hessian identity plus low rank. The condition number is then the largest eigenvalue, and a few eigenpairs fix it (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There is no comparative benchmark; the evidence is operational. Figure 1 shows the 45 leading Ritz values (p. 15). Figure 2 shows gradient-norm reduction with and without the Lanczos preconditioner on the second subproblem "for a typical case", about 27% fewer iterations (p. 17). Table 1 gives the runtime per evaluation of the nonlinear and linearized operators by resolution on 1024 processors, for example T799/25 km at 350 s and T95/200 km at 1.7 s (p. 18). A cycle is allowed 1h15min, and one full nonlinear evaluation takes almost 6 minutes (p. 18). The value m = 25 "was made empirically" (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: 4D-Var is placed inside PDE-constrained optimization: optimal control, design and parameter identification share its challenges (p. 2). Related work: Young et al.'s NAND approach in aerodynamic design (iterative recovery of the states, line searches), the Biros–Ghattas LNKS method as a SAND approach, and Nabben–Vuik and Giraud–Gratton on spectral preconditioners (pp. 18–19). The method's ingredients are credited to ECMWF work (Courtier et al. [5] p. 13; Trémolet [15, 16] pp. 17, 20; Fisher et al. [6] p. 20). Nocedal's own work appears only as the textbook [12] (pp. 7, 9) and as Morales–Nocedal automatic preconditioning [10], cited for "a related discussion" (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A case study written "from the perspective of numerical optimization" (p. 2). The formulation section ends with an explicit verdict sentence (p. 11). Concrete numbers appear throughout (pp. 5, 17–18). Where ECMWF practice is not backed by theory the text says so plainly ("no guarantee that J decreases", p. 17). Hedging is low.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is expository and analytical: why Gauss–Newton, why NAND, why multilevel solves and spectral preconditioning. It also documents an operational algorithm (pp. 2, 16–17). Limits and future work are concrete. Growing nonlinearity (moist processes, cloud and precipitation observations) will need more outer iterations (pp. 19–20). Weak-constraint 4D-Var brings the states back as variables, i.e. SAND or the hybrid (p. 20). Descent failures are studied in [15] (p. 17). The paper does not say which parts are Nocedal's own.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: structure first. The least-squares structure leads to Gauss–Newton, and quasi-Newton updating, his own family, is rejected for this problem (pp. 7–8). This replaces SKILL.md's "not read" for this paper. The paper confirms the choice and its reasons, but it does not tell the 2026 interview's story, and it credits the multilevel GN method to Courtier et al. [5] (p. 13), not to the authors.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: in operations the authors accept no convergence guarantee (pp. 2, 17) and give only local-convergence reasoning (p. 16).
- Also (not Methods): Heuristic 9 ✅ (entering a field through a practitioner's problem, with practitioner coauthors, p. 1); Heuristic 5 ✅ (preconditioning the first outer iteration from a previous forecast is declined because "the savings would not be significant", p. 18).
- new-pattern candidate: **formulation choice by explicit criteria**: SAND vs NAND vs hybrid, decided by storage, stability and cost (pp. 9–11), and strong vs weak constraint (p. 20). This bears on the Roundtable Card's listed blind spot "reformulation": here formulation is the paper's main subject.

**Transferable techniques**:
- A numbered feature list of the application, from which each algorithmic choice is derived (pp. 5–6).
- The full-space vs reduced-space decision by storage, stability and cost, after proving when the two GN steps coincide (pp. 9–11).
- A multilevel subproblem: solve the GN model at lower resolution with simplified physics and prolong the step (p. 13).
- Prior-covariance change of variables, then a Lanczos spectral preconditioner reused across outer iterations, with eigenvalue estimates capped (µ_j = min(λ̂_j, 10)) (pp. 14–17).

**Quotes**:
- "This paper shows how practical demands of the application dictate the various algorithmic choices that are made in the nonlinear optimization solver, with particular reference to the system in operation at the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts." (p. 1)
- "Moreover, a quasi-Newton approximation to (6) ignores the “least-squares” structure of this Hessian, namely, that the first term in (6) involves only first-derivative information." (p. 8)

### S062 · On the geometry phase in model-based algorithms for derivative-free optimization (Optimization Methods & Software 2009, DOI 10.1080/10556780802409296)
**Read level**: full. Whole text read, file pp. 1–10: abstract, §§1–4, references. The version read is the authors' revised preprint of August 12, 2008 (p. 1). Figures 1–7 are plots; their content is taken from captions and prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Giovanni Fasano (Ca' Foscari Venice and INSEAN), José Luis Morales (ITAM) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a derivative-free excursion between the Wedge method (Marazzi and Nocedal 2002, ref [9]) and the 2017–2023 noise programme. It is the paper SKILL.md Method 2 already cites from this preprint (pp. 2, 4, 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The received practice is that model-based DFO methods include a geometry phase, which replaces interpolation points "with the exclusive goal of improving the position of the interpolation set" (p. 2). The question is what happens if it is dropped altogether. The reason for asking is cost in code: "the geometry phase adds substantial complexity to these algorithms" (p. 2). The paper is framed as "A numerical study" (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable: pick a component whose only role is a theoretical safeguard and which adds much code, and ask what is lost without it (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Algorithm I (pp. 3–4) uses full quadratic interpolation with q = (n+1)(n+2)/2 points and a monomial basis, and rebuilds the model at every iteration (O(n⁶) work). The trust-region step comes from Moré–Sorensen, and the radius is multiplied by 1.5 or 0.75 with α = 0. On success, the point farthest from x_c is replaced by the trial point. On failure the trial point replaces it only if it is not farther away. Poisedness is not controlled. The method is "a simplification of the method described in [9]" (Wedge) (p. 3). It is the simplest version on purpose, not the cheapest: Lagrange-polynomial updating (O(n⁴)) and Powell–Frobenius updates are named and set aside (p. 4). The interpolation matrix is scaled, M̂ = MD, following Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente (p. 6).
  - Transferable: when testing the removal of one component, strip the rest of the algorithm to its simplest form as well, and name the economies you gave up (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Smooth unconstrained problems with n between 2 and 15, and full quadratic models only (pp. 1–2). No noise and no theory. Algorithm I has "a slight numerical advantage" because it moves the reference point at every iteration; the authors do not believe this changes the observations (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No proofs. The error bounds of [5] are not used, because "they depend on unknown Lipschitz constants (and may not always be tight)" (p. 8). The quantities are measured instead. log10 cond(M̂) is plotted per iteration with success and failure markers (pp. 6–8). Further measurements: the cosine between the model gradient and the exact gradient (obtained from AMPL) (p. 8); the relative gradient error, above 1 in more than 50% of the iterations of a typical run (p. 8); and the share of unsuccessful iterations, 25–50% (p. 8). The mechanism is offered as a conjecture. Unproductive steps shrink the radius and cluster the points, and so "seem to play a role similar to the geometry phase" (p. 7). A self-correction sketch for point sets near a low-dimensional subspace is left as "an open question" (p. 9).
  - Transferable: when theory bounds a quantity through constants nobody knows, measure that quantity on the test problems, using exact derivatives from a modelling language (AMPL) (p. 8).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 60 unconstrained CUTEr problems with n = 2–15. KNITRO with second derivatives gives the reference optimal values to about 14 digits and reference evaluation counts (Table 1, pp. 4–5). The competitors are NEWUOA and DFO, both with geometry phases. NEWUOA is run with (n+1)(n+2)/2 points "so that newuoa uses the same amount of information" (p. 4). Runs stop at 3 or 6 correct significant figures in f, or at 15,000 evaluations. ### S052 · Infeasibility detection and SQP methods for nonlinear optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2010, DOI 10.1137/080738222)
**Read level**: full. Whole text read, file pp. 1–23: abstract, §§1–5 including all proofs. The reference list (pp. 21–23) was skimmed. The version read is the authors' preprint of October 16, 2008 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Frank E. Curtis (Courant Institute, NYU; a team member) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the active-set SQP side of the KNITRO period. It follows the steering rules (Byrd, Nocedal and Waltz 2008, ref [7]) and the line-search penalty method [6]. SKILL.md links it in Method 4 and in Method 3's worked case, the latter marked "body not read".

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Methods that are fast on feasible problems can be very slow on infeasible ones: "a great number of function evaluations is often required before a declaration of infeasibility can be made" (p. 1). The pull comes from applications: branch and bound for MINLP and parametric studies (pp. 1–2). The rival design, a switch between optimizing and minimizing infeasibility (Fletcher–Leyffer), is criticized for the difficulty of designing the switch criterion (p. 2). The goal is stated as a rate in both outcomes: "fast local convergence guarantees no matter if a problem is feasible or infeasible" (abstract, p. 1). Problem (1.1) has inequality constraints only, for simplicity, with the ℓ1 infeasibility v(x) of (1.2) as the fallback target, and infeasible stationary points are defined (p. 2). Global infeasibility in nonconvex problems is set aside as a global-optimization question (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: state the target as a rate in both outcomes (feasible and infeasible), not only as convergence to something (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Fletcher's Sℓ1QP penalty-SQP engine (2.1)–(2.7), with φ(x; ρ) = ρf + v, is kept (p. 3). Two things change. (a) The model Hessian: ρ multiplies only ∇²f, not the constraint-curvature terms, W = ρ∇²f − Σλ∇²g (2.4) (p. 3). At ρ = 0 the model therefore stays quadratic and keeps constraint curvature, whereas the standard model Q_k (4.5) becomes linear (p. 16). (b) The penalty update, Algorithm II (p. 15). The current ρ is kept if the step is linearly feasible. Otherwise the ρ = 0 subproblem is solved for a reference step d̄. ρ is then decreased until linearized feasibility is reached where attainable, or else until the step gets a fraction ε1 of d̄'s reduction (4.3). It is decreased further until the model decrease is a fraction ε2 of the ρ = 0 model's (4.4). Finally step f sets ρ ≤ [E_k(0)/v_k]², forcing ρ to shrink like the square of the feasibility-problem error (pp. 15–16). The update "follows the spirit of the steering rules" of [7] (p. 14). The reasons: a standard SQP method with exact linearizations has inconsistent or ill-conditioned subproblems near infeasible stationary points, its steps blow up and convergence is linear at best (p. 4). SNOPT's ℓ1 fallback is a switch that does not aim at a fast infeasible rate (p. 5).
  - Transferable: when one parameter trades two goals, solve the subproblem at the parameter's extreme value (here ρ = 0) to learn what is attainable, then move the parameter until a fixed fraction of that is achieved (p. 15).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumptions 3.2 (p. 7) are: f and g twice continuously differentiable near x̂; full row rank of the active Jacobian; strict complementarity, λ̂ ∈ (0, 1) on the active constraints; second-order sufficiency for the auxiliary problem (3.6). The local analysis assumes unit steps, delegated to a watchdog or a second-order correction (p. 5). The exact Hessian is assumed. If W is indefinite, the QP solver must pick the minimizer of Lemma 3.4, for example the one closest to x_k (p. 13), and positive-definite modifications must satisfy (3.33) (p. 14). Equality constraints are said to extend "without difficulty" but are not treated (p. 2), yet Example 2 has equalities (p. 18). The prototype adds a multiple of the identity to W (pp. 16–17), which departs from the exact Hessian of the analysis; the size of the shift is not reported.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The proof skeleton is announced first, as the chain (3.9): a triangle inequality through the penalty solution z^ρ, a quadratic Newton contraction towards z^ρ, and an O(ρ) distance from z^ρ to ẑ (p. 8). Devices: Lemma 3.1 characterizes penalty KKT points by λ = 1 (violated), λ = 0 (strictly satisfied) and λ ∈ (0, 1) (active) (p. 6). Infeasible stationary points are recast as KKT points of the equality-constrained auxiliary problem (3.6) (pp. 6–7). The implicit function theorem on F(x, λ_A, ρ) gives a smooth path with ‖z^ρ − ẑ‖ ≤ Cρ (Lemma 3.3, pp. 8–9). Near x̂, the SQP step solves a linear system (Lemma 3.4, pp. 10–11). The Newton contraction is uniform in ρ, via Dennis–Schnabel Theorem 5.2.1 (Lemma 3.5, pp. 11–12). Theorem 3.6: ρ_k = O(‖z_k − ẑ‖²) gives quadratic convergence and ρ_k = o(‖z_k − ẑ‖) superlinear (p. 12). In the special case of n linearly independent active constraints, x^ρ = x̂ for all ρ < ρ_min, so ρ need not go to zero (§3.1, p. 13). The quasi-Newton extension uses the Boggs–Tolle–Wang / Powell characterization (3.33) (p. 14). The claim that exact-linearization SQP is slow near infeasible stationary points is asserted ("One can show", p. 4) without proof.
  - Transferable: for a parameterized method, split the error as ‖z_{k+1} − ẑ‖ ≤ ‖z_{k+1} − z^ρ‖ + ‖z^ρ − ẑ‖. Prove a Newton contraction uniform in ρ and an O(ρ) path bound by the implicit function theorem, then read off how fast the parameter must decrease (pp. 8–12).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: A MATLAB prototype, with QPs solved by quadprog, τ = 0.5, η = 10⁻⁸, ρ0 = 1, ε1 = ε2 = 0.1, and separate optimality and infeasibility stopping tests at 10⁻⁶ (pp. 16–17). Five examples are shown, each as a per-iteration table of f_k, v_k, E_k(ρ_{k−1}), E_k(0), ρ_k, ‖d_k‖ and α_k. unique is hand-built and infeasible, with a weight of 0.3 chosen so that only the first constraint is active (p. 17, Table 1). robot (CUTEr, 14 variables) is made infeasible by one added constraint (p. 18, Table 2). isolated has no active constraints at x̂ (pp. 18–19, Table 3). batch (46 variables) is feasible and shows that the update does not impede a feasible solve, which converges quadratically. batch1 adds one bound, tl[1] ≥ 5, to simulate a branch-and-bound node (p. 19, Tables 4–5). nactive tests the §3.1 special case, and Table 7 ablates step f (pp. 20–21). The one comparison: KNITRO's line-search interior method needs 42 iterations and 151 function evaluations to declare unique infeasible (p. 18). There is no aggregate test set, and the QP-vs-LP cost trade-off against the steering rules is declared out of scope (p. 16).
  - Transferable: make infeasible test instances by adding one inconsistent constraint or bound to a standard feasible problem, and run the feasible original alongside (pp. 18–19).
  - Transferable: build one example per case of the theorem (one active constraint at a strict minimizer of infeasibility; none active; n active), and ablate the rule that the special case says is unnecessary (pp. 17–21).
- **D6 Positioning**: Two rival designs are contrasted directly. One is the Fletcher–Leyffer switch (p. 2). The other is SNOPT's fallback to ℓ1 mode: "it starts as a regular SQP method and only invokes a penalty approach if difficulties are encountered", and it "makes no attempt to achieve a fast rate of convergence to stationary points in the infeasible case" (p. 5). Fletcher's Sℓ1QP is credited as the origin (pp. 2, 4), together with the long literature on penalty regularization (p. 5). The authors' own steering rules [7] are repositioned: global convergence yes, a fast infeasible rate no (p. 16). Other penalty updates [19, 2, 27, 11] use behaviour over several iterations, whereas steering uses the problem at the current iterate (p. 16). The reference list has no homogeneous self-dual infeasibility work (pp. 21–23).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract has four sentences, and the problem is stated in the first paragraph (p. 1). The proof roadmap (3.9) comes before the lemmas (p. 8). After the theorem comes a plain summary sentence (quote 2, p. 13) and two caveat subsections (§§3.1–3.2, pp. 13–14). Limits are written as scope statements, not apologies (p. 16). Hedging is low.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are theoretical (a local quadratic or superlinear rate to infeasible stationary points under a rate condition on ρ), algorithmic (the modified Hessian and the penalty update) and illustrative (pp. 1, 13, 15). Limits are concrete: the analysis is local, and the nonconvex global question is excluded (pp. 2–3). The QP reference step against the steering rules' cheaper LP is untested, because the test "requires a sophisticated software implementation" (p. 16). The exact Hessian, or a carefully modified one, is required (pp. 13–14). Future work: detect the n-active case and skip the quadratic decrease of ρ (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the classical engine is kept, and the penalty component (update rule and Hessian scaling) is rebuilt from an explicit estimate of the best attainable feasibility progress, the ρ = 0 subproblem (pp. 14–16). Reading the body confirms SKILL.md's "Inferred link" for doi:10.1137/080738222: the update is built on the steering rules (p. 14).

The 2006 paper is cited as [8], but its limitation sentence is not, so that arrow is still partly inferred.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: illustrative examples only, with no aggregate, but with the scope stated (p. 16). This is a second instance, after the 2024 paper, of the exception that Method 1's 🔴 names.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Scale only the objective's curvature by the penalty parameter, so the model keeps constraint curvature at ρ = 0 (pp. 3, 16).

### S023 · On the use of stochastic hessian information in optimization methods for machine learning (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2011, DOI 10.1137/10079923x)
**Read level**: full. Whole text read, file pp. 1–23: abstract, §§1–5, acknowledgments. The reference list (pp. 22–23) was skimmed. The version read is the preprint of January 13, 2011 (p. 1). Figures 1–7 are images with no data in the text layer; their content is taken from captions and the prose of §4.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Gillian M. Chin (Northwestern IEMS, supported by an NSERC fellowship and a Google grant), Will Neveitt (Google Research) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an early paper of the machine-learning period (2009/10–2019 in SKILL.md), on Google data (p. 14). Dynamic sampling is announced as "the subject of a future study" (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The starting point is that Newton-CG and L-BFGS are the standard large-scale deterministic tools (p. 2). For loss or expectation objectives the paper asks "whether one can design Newton-CG and limited memory BFGS methods that exploit the properties of such objective functions" (p. 2). Two weaknesses are named. Newton-CG's savings in outer iterations "normally do not compensate for the higher cost of the iteration", because each Hessian-vector product costs at least one gradient (p. 4). L-BFGS's scaled-identity initial matrix "contains little curvature information" (p. 9). The setting is fixed as batch (SAA), not online (pp. 2–3), justified by distributed computing, where very small batches let communication latency dominate (p. 3). The problems are (1.1) and (1.2), with smooth convex losses (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The one insight is that the Hessian can be much less accurate than the gradient, so Hessian-vector products use a subsample S much smaller than the gradient sample X (pp. 3, 5). Sub-sampled Newton-CG (Algorithm S-Newton, p. 5) runs CG on ∇²J_S p = −∇J_X with a residual test (2.3) or a maxcg limit, then an Armijo backtracking step. The cost model is maxcg × factor × gcost. Choosing |S| and maxcg so that factor × maxcg ≈ 1 makes a step cost about one gradient (p. 6). In the stochastically initialized L-BFGS (Algorithm SLM, p. 10), the two-loop recursion is unchanged except for the initial matrix, which is replaced by a few CG iterations on ∇²J_S r = q (Procedure I, p. 10), with a Wolfe line search. S is redrawn at every iteration "to avoid bias" (pp. 6, 11). Kept: the CG inner solver, the two-loop recursion and standard line searches.
  - Transferable: write the cost of a step as (inner iterations) × (relative cost of one inner operation) × (gradient cost), and tune the sample size so that the product is about one gradient (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Smooth convex losses, as in log-linear models (p. 2). The theory assumes J uniformly convex, made so by adding δ‖w‖² (p. 7), with subsampled Hessians uniformly positive definite (2.6) and bounded (2.7) (p. 7). Only the "semi-stochastic" case X_k = D (full gradient, sampled Hessian) is analysed and tested (pp. 6, 10, 13). Dynamic sampling is deferred (pp. 3, 6).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Theorem 2.1 (pp. 7–8): the CG iterates minimize the quadratic model over a Krylov space that contains the gradient. With an orthonormal basis Q of that space this gives the angle bound −pᵀ∇J/(‖∇J‖‖p‖) ≥ γ1/γ2 and ‖p‖ ≥ ‖∇J‖/γ2, and then Zoutendijk's analysis via Griva–Nash–Sofer Theorem 11.7. Theorem 3.1 (p. 11): the CG output r is written as H0 q with H0 = Q[QᵀHQ]⁻¹Qᵀ + Q̄Q̄ᵀ, an explicit symmetric positive definite matrix with eigenvalues in [min{1, 1/γ2}, max{1, 1/γ1}], so Liu–Nocedal's L-BFGS convergence theorem (Theorem 7.1 of [6]) applies. Both results are global convergence in the convex case. There are no rates, and no analysis of the stochastic case.
  - Transferable: to show that an inexact inner solve acts as a bounded preconditioner, complete the Krylov projector with the identity on the orthogonal complement and bound the eigenvalues of the resulting matrix (p. 11).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There is a deterministic sanity check first (S = X = D), on a convex quadratic with condition number 100 and on a quadratic-plus-exponential, both with n = 100. SLM's outer iterations fall as maxcg grows, but total CG work makes it uncompetitive, and this is reported as a failure (Tables 1–2, pp. 12–13). The main test is one Google speech problem: multinomial logistic regression with m = 168,776 frames, 129 classes, 79 features and n = 10,191 (p. 14). The cost axis is the "number of accessed data points" (function, gradient and Hessian-vector blocks), used in lieu of CPU time; CPU plots are said to look similar (p. 16). The vertical axis is exp(−J) (p. 16). The baseline is standard L-BFGS, "widely used in the machine learning community for tasks of this type" (p. 13), with memory t = 20 against S-Newton and t = 5 against SLM (pp. 15–16). Classical Newton-CG with maxcg = 10 is the other comparator (p. 15). Results: at a 13% correct-classification probability, S-Newton is about three times faster than classical Newton-CG and twice as fast as L-BFGS (p. 16). maxcg (2, 5, 10, 50) and p (1% to 100%) are swept for both methods, with the losing settings shown: maxcg = 2, and SLM with p = 100% or with large maxcg (pp. 16–17). S-Newton is "efficient for the range p ∈ [1%, 25%]" (p. 16), and the two new methods perform similarly (p. 18). Hessian samples are taken as consecutive blocks of a shuffled index array, with wrap-around (p. 13). There is a single data set; repeated runs are not reported, and the baseline's parameters are not swept.
  - Transferable: count work in data-point accesses (one unit per loss term evaluated in f, the gradient or a Hessian-vector product), so methods with different inner loops share one axis (p. 16).
  - Transferable: test a stochastic idea first in its deterministic limit (full sample) to check that it is "not unsound", and report it if it loses there (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper sets itself apart from Newton-like ML methods (Lin–Weng–Keerthi's trust-region Newton for logistic regression, Yu et al.'s quasi-Newton method for nonsmooth losses, Bottou): "All of these approaches are significantly different from the techniques proposed here" (p. 4), with no comparison against them. It borrows SAA language from stochastic programming (p. 2) and avoids sampling second derivatives directly, citing Shapiro and Homem-de-Mello on noisy estimators (p. 19). The baseline is justified by community use (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The thesis is hedged ("can be useful in some machine learning applications", p. 3), and the conclusion is stronger ("an effective way of accelerating", p. 19). The method's motivation comes as three lettered considerations, a)–c) (pp. 4–5). Results are reported as speed-up factors at a fixed accuracy level (p. 16). The comparison of the two methods ends with "the best choice among them may be problem dependent" (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic (two subsampled-curvature methods), a light theory (global convergence in the convex case) and one application (pp. 3, 19). Limits: the semi-stochastic case only (p. 6); preconditioning of the inner CG unexplored (p. 19); one problem. Future work: dynamic sample sizes (p. 3).
