### S124 · A trust-region algorithm for noisy equality constrained optimization (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv:2411.02665)
**Read level**: full. Read all of pp. 1–34: abstract, §§1–5, every proof in §3 line by line, acknowledgements and references. The text refers to an appendix (p. 23), but arXiv v1 contains none.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Shigeng Sun (Northwestern ESAM; corresponding author Nocedal) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: this paper extends Sun & Nocedal's noisy unconstrained trust-region method (ref. [26], *Math. Program.* 202, 2023; p. 2) to the Byrd–Omojokun SQP step used in KNITRO-CG. It is the trust-region companion of the line-search SQP of Oztoprak, Byrd & Nocedal (ref. [24], p. 3) and the last arXiv preprint before his declared pause.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is equality-constrained NLP where f, c, ∇f and ∇c are observed only with noise or computational error δf, δc, δg, δA (eqs. 1–4, p. 2). There are three entry points. (a) Trust-region methods for noisy constrained problems "have received comparatively less attention", because the radius carries noisy information from earlier iterates and this makes the analysis harder than for line searches (abstract; p. 2). (b) Classical trust-region methods "can struggle or even fail" under noise (p. 3, citing [26]). (c) Most noisy constrained methods assume full-rank Jacobians (p. 3). Other methods remove the trust region's memory "by either using a predetermined trust region schedule or defining the trust radius as a multiple of the current gradient norm" (p. 3). He chooses Byrd–Omojokun (BO) because it is the backbone of KNITRO and is robust to rank-deficient Jacobians (abstract; p. 2). The problem statement is a design goal: "Our goal is to propose a variant of the Byrd-Omojokun algorithm" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: choose the production algorithm whose distinctive strength (here, rank-deficient Jacobians) the noisy-method literature has given up, and make keeping that strength part of the problem statement (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The BO machinery stays: a normal step with contraction ζ (eqs. 9–10, p. 3), the relaxed full step (eq. 11, p. 4), least-squares multipliers (eq. 12, p. 4), the nonsmooth merit f̃ + ν‖c̃‖ and the standard penalty increase to pred > π1 ν vpred (eqs. 13–17, p. 4; Alg. 1 lines 10–14, p. 5). Only the ratio test changes. ξ(ϵf + νkϵc) is added to both ared and pred (eq. 19, p. 4), with ξ = 2/(1−π0) (Alg. 1 line 4, p. 5). The radius update is the plain one: ×τ on acceptance, ÷τ on rejection (Alg. 1 lines 17–20, p. 5). The noise levels ϵf and ϵc are inputs (Alg. 1 line 2, p. 5). The final remarks give the design claim: "Only the ration test (20) must be safeguarded" (sic), and the penalty rule is left unchanged (p. 33). Subproblems may be solved inexactly. A fraction of Cauchy decrease is enough for the theory (p. 4), and this lets BO "scale into the tens of thousands of variables" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: relax a ratio test by adding the same noise term to numerator and denominator. The constant ξ = 2/(1−π0) is chosen so that every accepted step satisfies ared > π0·pred − 2(noise) (eq. 54, p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Assumption 1: f and c are Lf- and Lc-smooth (p. 5).
  - Assumption 2: the *generated sequences* Ãk, W̃k and c̃k are bounded and f̃k is bounded below (pp. 5–6). This is an assumption on the iterates, not on the problem.
  - Assumption 3: deterministic bounds ϵf, ϵc, ϵg, ϵA on all four errors. This is defended as "the case in many practical applications", citing S128 (p. 6).
  - Assumption 4: singular values of Ãk bounded below by σmin. It is used only for the feasibility-norm and optimality results (pp. 16, 19).
  - Assumption 5: the null-space basis error ‖δZ‖ ≤ ϵZ. It is called "realistic" if QR pivoting is kept consistent (p. 23), and its justification is sent to an appendix that is missing.
  - The Hessian approximation need only be bounded and symmetric (p. 5). There is no stochastic model. The experiments inject uniform noise (p. 29).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The skeleton is used twice, once for feasibility and once for optimality.
  1. A Cauchy decrease bound for the normal step (Lemma 1, p. 6).
  2. Model accuracy: |ared − pred| ≤ ML(ν)Δ² + (ϵg + νϵA)Δ + 2(ϵf + νϵc) (Lemma 2, pp. 6–7).
  3. Outside a noise-scaled threshold ‖ÃᵀC̃‖ > Ev(k) + γ, a small radius forces acceptance and growth (Lemma 3, pp. 7–8). The radius is then bounded below (Corollary 1, pp. 8–9).
  4. Each accepted step lowers the rescaled merit Φ = f/ν + ‖c‖ by χμ² + μ√(…). Here γk′ is chosen as the positive root of a quadratic so that the noise term 2ek′ cancels exactly (eq. 44, p. 9; Lemma 4 and eq. 57, pp. 9–11).
  5. A contradiction with boundedness below then shows that the iterates visit region C^I_Ac infinitely often (Prop. 1, pp. 11–12).
  6. A displacement bound, the geometric series of radii τ/(τ−1)·Δ̄ (Lemma 5, pp. 12–13), shows that once inside, the iterates never leave the larger merit-level region C^II_Ac (Prop. 2, pp. 13–16).

  Under full rank, ‖v‖ ≤ Γ1·vpred (Lemma 7, pp. 18–19), the penalty parameter is bounded and pred ≥ Γ2·hpred (Lemma 8, pp. 19–20). The same four steps then run on the reduced gradient ‖g̃ᵀZ̃‖ (Lemmas 9–12, Props. 3–4, pp. 20–28).

  Named devices: the "anchor iterate" k′, which states the regions in terms of the monotonically increasing ν (Remark 1, p. 9); the "settling iterate" k″ (Remark 3, p. 20); and Observation 1, which shifts f > 0 so that Φ is monotone in ν (p. 11). Remark 2 notes that region sizes are inversely proportional to νk′, "This seemingly surprising fact" (p. 16). The result is purely qualitative: noise-determined neighbourhoods (Theorem 2 and Table 1, pp. 28–29), with no rate or complexity. The constants are explicit but never evaluated.
  - Transferable: the visit-infinitely-often / never-leave pair, with the displacement bound from the geometric series of radii, is a reusable template for any trust-region method whose radius carries noisy memory (pp. 11–16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Setup: a prototype inside KNITRO-CG (`algorithm = 2`), modified by Figen Oztoprak (Artelys) to offer the new ratio as an option with the noise level as input. KNITRO's default stopping tests are used throughout (p. 29).
  - Test problems and noise: CUTEst problems through the Python interface. Uniform noise on [−ϵ, ϵ] is injected into f, c, g, the Hessian and the Jacobian. Gaussian noise was also tried, "with similar results", which are not shown (p. 29).
  - Selection: "over 50" equality-constrained problems were run, and three are reported as typical (p. 29).
  - Three arms: noise-free unmodified KNITRO, noisy unmodified, and noisy modified (p. 30).
  - The cases:
    - HS7 with a deliberately tiny Δ0 = 10⁻⁷ and ϵ = 0.1 (about 0.033 relative error, p. 29). The unmodified code fails because "the trust region radius is driven to zero prematurely" (pp. 30–31).
    - ROBOT with Δ0 = 1. The unmodified code shrinks the radius from about iteration 39 (p. 31), and restart heuristics still make wrong decisions (p. 32).
    - BYRDSPHR, where the unmodified code also gets below the noise level, with more iterations and oscillations. The authors call this success "brittle" (p. 32).
  - Plots: objective, ‖c‖, optimality error and step length against iterations, with a dashed line at f* + noise level (p. 30).
  - Missing: an aggregate table or profile, a count of how many of the 50+ problems improved, and the number of runs or seeds (pp. 29–32).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on [26] (p. 2) and on the authors' earlier noisy trust-region work.
  - Classical equality-constrained trust-region methods (Celis–Dennis–Tapia, Powell–Yuan, Vardi): BO "strikes the right balance between robustness and scalability", citing his own implementation paper [20] (p. 2).
  - Noisy unconstrained trust-region work: ratio relaxations ([26], [4], [17]) and numerator-only relaxation with high-probability rates ([8], Cao–Berahas–Scheinberg) (p. 3).
  - Noisy constrained work: relaxed line-search SQP [24], step-search SQP [3], stochastic TR-SQP [14]. Only [2] (Berahas–Curtis–O'Neill–Robinson) handles rank deficiency (p. 3).
  - The differentiator is that the trust region keeps its history (p. 3). There is no criticism of rivals beyond scope.
  - Reference hygiene: [5] and [6] are the same Byrd–Gilbert–Nocedal 2000 entry, and [5] is cited as the source of the BO algorithm (pp. 2, 33).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The order is problem statement → algorithm (pp. 3–5) → 24 pages of theory (pp. 5–29) → 4 pages of experiments (pp. 29–32) → a one-paragraph close (p. 33). Contribution sentences are unhedged: "Our results are of significant generality" (p. 2), "This paper presents a comprehensive convergence theory" (p. 33). The strongest design claim, that only the ratio test needs a safeguard, first appears in the final remarks (p. 33). Intuition comes from Remarks 1–3 and Table 1 (pp. 9, 16, 20, 29).

  - "Omojukun" (p. 5); "ration test" (p. 33).
  - In Corollary 1's proof, "∆¯(γ)/ν (by Step 6 of Algorithm 1)" where τ and line 20 seem meant (p. 9).
  - Prop. 4 is titled "...of Feasibility" but treats optimality (p. 26).
  - A (1 − τ) denominator in P^II_gZ is negative for τ > 1 (pp. 26–27).
  - "Theorem 2" has no Theorem 1 (p. 28).
  - Two remarks are numbered 3 (pp. 20, 29).
  - A missing appendix is cited (p. 23).
  - Regions C1/C2 are attributed to [27] (Sun & Xie) where [26] seems meant (p. 29; inferred).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm (a one-component change), theory (noise-floor regions that also cover rank-deficient Jacobians), and software (an option in KNITRO) (pp. 29, 33). There is no limitations section, and the final remarks state no limitation or future direction (p. 33). Implicit limits: the noise levels must be supplied (Alg. 1 line 2, p. 5); equality constraints only (p. 2); the optimality part needs full rank and Assumption 5 (pp. 16, 23, 28).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence, constrained exemplar: the BO engine and penalty rule are kept and only the ratio test is made noise-aware from explicit noise levels (eq. 19, p. 4; p. 33). This matches the step-3 exemplar from Oztoprak–Byrd–Nocedal, here in a trust-region version. There is no recovery tree or noise re-estimation (Alg. 1, p. 5), so step 4 is absent.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the repair ships as a KNITRO option, with the noise level as a user input (p. 29).
- Method 1 ✗ contradiction: 3 of "over 50" problems are reported, with no aggregate (p. 29). This is exactly the 🔴 case in Method 1 step 7. The competitor is the unmodified version of the same code, so stopping tests are aligned (p. 29). That part of Method 1 holds.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: it analyses the version that ships (BO as in KNITRO-CG, inexact subproblems allowed, pp. 4–5) but gives no rate. The region constants are never compared with runs; only a noise-level line is plotted (p. 30).
- Heuristics 1, 3 and 8 ✅: the failure demo is forced with Δ0 = 10⁻⁷ (p. 29), the three arms are run (p. 30), and the prototype lives inside the production solver (p. 29).
- New-pattern candidate: two-tier noise-floor convergence statement. The iterates visit a stationarity region infinitely often and never leave a larger merit-level region, stated relative to an anchor iterate (pp. 9–16, 28–29). Remark 3 says the optimality regions reduce to the unconstrained regions C1 and C2 when there are no constraints (cited as [27], p. 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ratio relaxation: ρ = (ared + ξ·noise)/(pred + ξ·noise) with ξ = 2/(1−π0). Acceptance then implies ared > π0·pred − 2·noise, which feeds directly into the merit-decrease lemma (eqs. 19, 54–57, pp. 4–5, 10–11).
- A proof template for a trust region with memory: radius lower bound outside the critical region (Lemma 3 / Corollary 1) → fixed decrease per accepted step (Lemma 4) → visit infinitely often (Prop. 1) → displacement ≤ τ/(τ−1)·Δ̄ (Lemma 5) → never leave the merit-level region (Prop. 2) (pp. 7–16).
- Anchor-iterate trick for an increasing penalty parameter: fix k′, state every threshold through νk′, and shift f > 0 so the rescaled merit f/ν + ‖c‖ is monotone in ν (Remark 1, Observation 1, pp. 9, 11).
- Stage the assumptions. First prove a feasibility-stationarity result (‖AᵀC‖) that needs no rank condition. Add full rank only to get ‖c‖ small, a bounded penalty and optimality (Prop. 1 → Corollary 2 → Lemma 8, pp. 11–20).
- Experiment design: force the known weakness (Δ0 = 10⁻⁷) on an easy problem. Run three arms. Mark f* + noise level on every plot. Also show a case where the unmodified method succeeds, and say why that success is brittle (pp. 29–32).

**Quotes**:
- "When adapting the Byrd-Omojokun method to problems where the noise level can be estimated, it is not necessary to change the penalty parameter update rule or other components of the algorithm." (p. 33)
- "Our work distinguishes itself from previous studies by considering a standard trust region method for equality-constrained optimization, as opposed to modifications that eliminate history by either using a predetermined trust region schedule or defining the trust radius as a multiple of the current gradient norm." (p. 3)

### S128 · Design guidelines for noise-tolerant optimization with applications in robust design (SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing 2025, doi:10.1137/24m1632279; read as arXiv:2401.15007 v2)
**Read level**: full. Read all of arXiv v2, pp. 1–28: §§1–7, Theorem 6.7 and its proof, and Appendices A–D, all read in full.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yuchen Lou (Northwestern IEMS), Shigeng Sun (Northwestern ESAM) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the noise programme's methodological statement. It packages the group's relaxed line search, noise-aware difference intervals and quasi-Newton spacing into "design guidelines" (pp. 1–2), tested on a PDE-based robust-design application instead of synthetic noise (p. 2). S124 cites it as evidence that bounded noise is realistic (S124 p. 6).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The question is "whether existing optimization methods require substantial redesign to ensure robustness in the presence of noise, or if certain modifications are sufficient" (p. 1). The paper's answer: only a few operations are fragile under noise. These are comparisons of noisy function values (line search, trust region), differences of noisy function values (finite differences) and differences of noisy gradients (quasi-Newton) (p. 1). The applied entry is a critique of evidence. The literature tests "either synthetic noise or simple machine learning models", which leaves realistic effectiveness open (p. 2). The test bed is the Ng–Willcox acoustic horn: six flare half-widths, bounds, and the robust objective E[s] + 3√V[s] estimated by sampling (eqs. 3.1–3.4, pp. 5–6). Its noise level can be tuned through the sample size N (p. 2).
  - Transferable: before designing a noise-tolerant method, list the algorithm's operations that compare or difference noisy quantities. Those are the only places that need a safeguard (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The paper has three guidelines (pp. 1–2).
  1. Soft comparisons: relax Armijo by 2ϵA (eq. 4.1, p. 8), with ϵA ← λϵf and λ ∈ [1, 2] (eq. 4.2, p. 9).
  2. Robust difference intervals: h ≈ 8^{1/4}√(ϵf/L) (eq. 4.3, p. 9).
  3. Controlled gradient differences for quasi-Newton methods (p. 2).

  The case-study algorithm, GP-LS, is a plain projected gradient with relaxed backtracking (Alg. 1, p. 8). A more sophisticated projected backtracking was judged unnecessary (p. 8). α0, the initial trial step, is treated as the key noise knob: it should shrink as noise rises, in line with SGD step-size theory (p. 12). The new component is a self-calibrated line search. Every T iterations it computes the average number of backtracks over the last T iterations:
  - if it is ≥ 3: ϵA ← min{1.5ϵA, 2ϵf} and α0 ← 0.5α0;
  - if it is ≤ 0.1: ϵA ← 0.5ϵA and α0 ← min{1.5α0, 10⁻¹}.
  Backtracking is capped at βk ≥ ρ^{3T}, after which the step is discarded (eqs. 5.3–5.5, pp. 13–14). The rule decreases faster than it increases (1.5 × 0.5 < 1), "because it is less harmful to perform more backtracks than accepting a poor step" (p. 14). The rationale for staying with classical methods: they embody "important algorithmic ideas" such as active-set estimates and the efficiency of primal-dual interior methods (p. 2).
  - Transferable: tune two line-search parameters from the algorithm's own telemetry (average backtracks) with asymmetric multiplicative rules and a hard cap. This is an implicit noise estimate that needs no extra sampling (pp. 13–14; App. B calls it "an implicit way of estimating the local noise level", p. 25).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Noise taxonomy: Polyak's stochastic noise versus deterministic error. Computational noise is modelled as random (p. 3).
  - Noise level: the standard deviation, following Moré–Wild (p. 3). Bias is ignored because it "will cancel out" in comparisons and differences (pp. 3, 9).
  - Estimation: sample standard deviation (eq. 2.5), ECNoise for computational noise, and a global average over sampled points (eq. 2.7) (p. 4).
  - Theory (pp. 15–16): Ω closed and convex, f bounded below, L-Lipschitz gradient, and bounded noise ϵb in f and ϵg in g (Assumptions 6.1–6.4, p. 15). Bounded noise is defended as physical in engineering, while the probabilistic-bound line of work is called "more sophisticated but also more involved" (p. 16).
  - Theorem 6.7 needs α0 + 2c < 1 and ϵA > ϵb (p. 19). Yet §4.1 says that choosing ϵA > ϵb "tends to be excessively cautious and can degrade performance" (p. 9), and §5.1 recommends ϵA "in the lower range of the estimated values" (p. 11). The theorem covers the conservative setting, not the recommended one.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Theorem 6.7 gives lim inf |pₖᵀgₖ| ≤ ε̄, with ε̄ = ϵg²/γ² + [2α0L/(cρ(…))](ϵA + ϵb) (eqs. 6.15–6.16, p. 19). This is stated as an O(ϵb + ϵg²) target (p. 16). The proof devices:
  - The stationarity measure −pᵀg of the projected step (Lemmas 6.3 and 6.5, pp. 16–17; Lemma D.1, pp. 27–28).
  - Lemma 6.6 bounds the noisy measure −p̃ᵀg̃ below by a multiple of the true measure, through the noise-to-signal ratio γk = ‖δg‖/√(−pᵀg). It uses the projection inequality (Lemma 6.1) three times plus AM–GM (pp. 17–18).
  - Finite termination of the relaxed backtracking, which follows from ϵA > ϵb (p. 19).
  - A case split on γk against a threshold γ: either the step length is bounded below by ρ/(α0L) (Case 1), or noise dominates and the decrease is charged to η (Case 2) (pp. 19–21).
  - A uniform per-iteration inequality (6.31), then a contradiction with f bounded below (p. 21).

  No rate is given. Extension to the self-calibrated version is "readily" claimed, without proof (p. 21). The novelty claim reads: "no prior results exists for noisy gradient projection methods with a line search, to our knowledge" (p. 16).
  - Transferable: when a noisy gradient enters a projection, compare noisy and true stationarity measures through the ratio γk. Split iterations into "signal dominates" and "noise dominates" cases, each with its own decrease bound, then merge them into one inequality (pp. 17–21).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Application and noise: a single application with n = 6. Each sample needs a finite-element solve of a linear system of order 30,000 (p. 6). Noise is controlled by N = 100, 50, 10 (pp. 10, 12, 13) and visualised on a 2-D slice (Figs. 2–3, pp. 6–7). A computational-noise variant (GMRES at 10⁻⁶) is shown but not used further (p. 7).
  - Sampling: every evaluation uses a new batch ("sample inconsistent"), with the sample-consistent case in App. C (pp. 10, 26–27).
  - Baseline: GP-F (fixed step) at α ∈ {10⁻¹, 10⁻², 10⁻³}. Its best α was found "after extensive experimentation" (p. 10).
  - One-factor sweeps:
    - ϵA ∈ {10⁻², 10⁻³, 10⁻⁴}, derived from λ = 2 and from a minimum over 100 sampled points (pp. 10–11);
    - h ∈ {10⁻¹, 10⁻², 10⁻³} against analytic gradients, with CPU time on the x-axis because analytic-gradient cost is not countable in evaluations (pp. 11–12);
    - α0 ∈ {1, 0.25, 10⁻³} at N = 50 (pp. 12–13);
    - at N = 10, parameters "chosen after experimentation" (p. 13); GP-LS-cal with T = 5 (p. 15).
  - Metrics: the objective is re-estimated with N = 100. It is plotted against iterations and against effort = N × function calls (p. 10), with a moving average at N = 10 (p. 15).
  - Losses printed:
    - analytic gradients are best, but the margin over h = 10⁻² "is not significant" (pp. 11–12);
    - ϵA = 10⁻² oscillates (p. 11);
    - at N = 10, "GP-LS no longer has an advantage over GP-F" (p. 13).
  - Not reported: repeated runs or seeds, a second problem, and GP-F in Fig. 10, so the claim that the gap to fixed-step SGD is narrowed (p. 2) rests on comparing objective levels across figures (pp. 13, 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper synthesises the group's own line: relaxed Armijo [2, 40], difference intervals [39], gradient-difference spacing [38, 44], plus Moré–Wild [26, 27] and Berahas–Cao–Scheinberg [3] (pp. 1–2). It concedes the rival's ground: with highly noisy gradients, diminishing steplengths "can be beneficial" (p. 2). It contrasts its self-calibration with Sun & Xie's variance-based rescaling [41] (p. 14). Two received views are framed as targets (pp. 10, 11). The wider claim is against solver practice. Packages (LANCELOT, SNOPT, MINOS, IPOPT thesis, L-BFGS-B) and textbooks treat errors with heuristics "seldom documented or justified" and only for roundoff (p. 22). A footnote names Gill–Murray–Wright Ch. 2 as the exception, though "far from exhaustive" (p. 22). The authors' own L-BFGS-B is on the criticised list (ref. [45], pp. 22, 24).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper uses an advocacy register: "This paper advocates for strategies" (abstract), "This paper argues" (p. 1). The three fragile operations are a numbered list on p. 1. There is an explicit hedge: "We do not argue that the only way to design nonlinear optimization methods for noisy problems is to adapt existing deterministic methods" (p. 2). Received wisdom is quoted, then tested (pp. 10, 11). Parameter advice is given as numbers: λ ∈ [1, 2] (p. 9), "choose ϵA to be in the lower range" (p. 11), and the constants of (5.3)–(5.4) (p. 14). Experiments (§5) come before theory (§6) (p. 3). The close is modest: "This paper attempts to be a step toward that goal" (p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a methodology (guidelines and noise-level estimation), an algorithmic component (the self-calibrated line search), a first convergence result for noisy GP with a line search, and an application study (pp. 2, 16). Limits are stated in places, not in a section:
  - one application and bound constraints only, with future studies "for more general constrained optimization problems" (p. 22);
  - if the noise distribution varies strongly, ϵA must be recomputed or ϵb used (p. 9; App. B);
  - self-calibration "may fail to provide sufficient safeguards in some extreme cases" (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence, programme statement: "adapting classical deterministic methods" through guidelines that "make use of estimates of the noise level" (abstract). Repairs are limited to the three fragile operations (p. 1) and driven by ϵf (pp. 4, 9). There is also a recovery element: a backtrack cap with the step discarded (eq. 5.5, p. 14).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: "It is common practice to avoid line searches" is tested (p. 10), and so is "A common view in optimization is that finite difference gradient approximations should be avoided" (p. 11). Parameters are varied both ways (h and ϵA at 10⁻¹…10⁻⁴, pp. 10–12), and the rival's wins are printed (p. 13).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the competitor GP-F gets a tuning grid and "extensive experimentation" (p. 10), and effort is counted as N × function calls (p. 10). But the authors' own method is tuned as well (λ, α0 and the N = 10 parameters "chosen after experimentation", pp. 10, 13). There is no handicap and only one problem.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the theorem covers ϵA > ϵb, the setting the experiments call "excessively cautious" (pp. 9, 19). The analysed version is not the recommended one, the bound is not compared with runs, and the self-calibrated extension is asserted without proof (p. 21).
- Heuristic 5 ✅: parameter advice is given as numbers (pp. 9, 11, 14).
- New-pattern candidate: fragile-operations audit. Enumerate the operations that compare or difference noisy quantities and safeguard only those (p. 1). It sharpens Method 3 step 1 and might merge into it.
- New-pattern candidate: telemetry-driven self-calibration. Parameters adapt from the algorithm's own behaviour statistics, as an implicit noise estimate (pp. 13–14, 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before writing a noise-tolerant variant, list the algorithm's comparisons, function differences and gradient differences. Safeguard only those, each from one noise-level estimate (pp. 1–2).
- Set the Armijo relaxation to ϵA = λϵf with λ ∈ [1, 2], from the lower range of pointwise estimates. Overestimating it lets noisy steps through, while underestimating it only costs extra backtracks, which a cap contains (pp. 9–11).
- Self-calibrated line search: every T iterations, read the average backtrack count and adjust ϵA and α0 multiplicatively, shrinking faster than growing, with a hard backtrack cap (eqs. 5.3–5.5, pp. 13–14).
- Use a real application whose noise level has a knob (sample size N), and report effort as N × function evaluations. Switch to CPU time when one arm's cost (analytic gradients) cannot be counted in evaluations (pp. 6, 10–11).
- Treat sample-consistent versus sample-inconsistent evaluation as an explicit experimental factor (§5 vs App. C, pp. 10, 26–27).
- Cheap noise-bound estimators: empirical Chebyshev, the maximum deviation from an accurate mean, and the sample range, with the conditions under which each is consistent (App. B, pp. 25–26).

**Quotes**:
- "These guidelines stem from the observation that, in the presence of noise, only few operations can lead to numerical difficulties in optimization methods." (p. 1)
- "A common view in optimization is that finite difference gradient approximations should be avoided in the noisy setting." (p. 11)

### S150 · A feasible method for constrained derivative-free optimization (Operations Research Letters 2026, doi:10.1016/j.orl.2025.107398; read as arXiv:2402.11920 v1)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole of arXiv v1 text, pp. 1–12 (abstract, §§1–4, references), and Appendix A Tables A.1–A.5 (pp. 12–17) in full. The Appendix B figures and Table B.1 (pp. 18–21) were read; Tables B.2–B.5 (pp. 22–25) were skimmed. The ORL journal version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Melody Qiming Xuan (Northwestern IEMS) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the last paper on his list (published 2026). It turns from finite-difference quasi-Newton (the study of Shi, Xuan, Oztoprak & Nocedal, ref. [25], OMS 2022) to an interpolation-model method for constrained DFO. It is the endpoint that SKILL.md Method 2 records as his model-based turn.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Interpolation-based trust-region (IBO) methods are "among the most efficient and reliable" for unconstrained DFO, but extending them to general constraints "is not straightforward and has received limited attention" (p. 1). The entry is a dormant published idea, the feasible method of Conn et al. [6] (p. 1). Its earlier numerical results "are, however, inconclusive" and the approach "is rarely (if ever) used in practice" (p. 2). The setting is narrowed on purpose. Derivatives of f are unavailable, and the constraints and their derivatives are "quite inexpensive compared to the cost of evaluating f". The paper flags this assumption as "important" (p. 2). Nonconvex objective and constraints are allowed (abstract; p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: FIBO minimises the interpolation model of f subject to the *original* nonlinear constraints and a trust region (eq. 2.4, p. 3). This is a general NLP, solved by KNITRO (p. 3). Everything else is the unconstrained IBO method unchanged: ratio test on f, radius update and interpolation-set update (pp. 3–4; Alg. 1, p. 4). "one can start with any IBO method for unconstrained optimization and replace the step computation (2.2) by (2.4)" (p. 3). The iterates stay feasible if the subproblem solver succeeds, so the model need only represent f (p. 4). That matters when f is undefined outside the feasible set (p. 4). The base code dfotr is used "without altering its logic" (p. 6). Why this design: it is easy to wrap around any IBO code and any NLP solver, unlike penalty/augmented-Lagrangian heuristics or "complex IBO methodology" (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Smooth f and cᵢ; no derivatives of f; cheap c and ∇c (p. 2). There are no convergence assumptions because there is no theorem. Feasibility of every iterate assumes the NLP solver succeeds at each iteration (p. 4). A feasible x0 and, when f is undefined outside, a feasible initial interpolation set are needed; the cost of the latter "We do not discuss" (pp. 4–5). The noise claim, that FIBO "can be applied to noisy DFO problems without modification", rests on IBO's robustness "for reasons that are not well understood" and needs no noise level (p. 5). It is not tested (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a. The paper has no convergence analysis. It cites convergence results only for convex constraints ([5], fully linear models; [10], generalised fully linear models) (p. 6) and claims to go "beyond [10]" by allowing nonconvex constraints (p. 2), with no proof. The only analytical content is a per-iteration cost account: O(n⁶) for vanilla IBO against O(n²) for NEWUOA-style model updates, plus the cost of many constraint and model evaluations inside the subproblem (pp. 2, 5).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - FIBO: Python, built around dfotr (vanilla IBO, no geometry phase, said to trail NEWUOA "closely" in evaluations), with KNITRO 12.4 SQP and L-BFGS on the subproblem using exact constraint gradients (pp. 6–7).
  - Baseline FD: KNITRO 12.4 applied directly, with SQP, L-BFGS at memory 10, forward-difference ∇f and exact constraint derivatives. `xtol = 1e-8`, `xtol_iters = 1` and `findiff_terminate = 0` are set "to obtain a similar convergence criterion as in FIBO" (p. 7).
  - COBYLA and COBYQA are excluded because they interpolate the constraints too, "putting them at a disadvantage" (p. 6).
  - Budget and stopping: 500 × max(m, n) f-evaluations for both methods; FIBO stops when Δ < 10⁻⁸; the ratio-based early stop is disabled (p. 7).
  - Test set: 38 noiseless CUTEst general-constrained problems (p. 7; Tables A.1–A.5, pp. 13–17).
  - Starting point: feasible, found by KNITRO with a zero objective. Its cost is excluded in §3 and included in App. B, with "the conclusions remain the same" (pp. 7, 18).
  - Metrics:
    - log₂ ratio of final accuracy with a 10⁻⁸ floor (following Morales [13]) (p. 7);
    - log₂ ratios of objective and of constraint evaluations needed to reach f ≤ f* + τ·max(1, |f*|), for τ = 10⁻¹, 10⁻³, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁷ (pp. 7–8).
  - Results:
    - FD "slightly outperforms FIBO in terms of accuracy" (p. 7);
    - FIBO needs fewer objective evaluations at every τ (p. 8);
    - FD is "far more efficient in terms of constraint evaluations, as expected" (p. 8).
  - What only the tables show:
    - FIBO hits the evaluation limit with feasibility error 1.798 × 10^308 and zero subproblem iterations on LOADBAL, EXPFITA and SYNTHES1;
    - it stops on a singular interpolation system on HS44, HS66, HS67, HS98 and HIMMELBI (HS44: f = −1.000 against KNITRO's −15.00);
    - KNITRO carries an undefined (†) on HS89, with feasibility error 0.1332 (Table A.1, p. 13).
    The main text discusses none of these, and the final remarks say "we never encountered failures" of the subproblem solves (p. 9).
  - CPU time is much higher for FIBO on CUTEst (e.g. HS100: 29.46 s against 0.21 s, Table A.1, p. 13). This is consistent with the stated cost model (f must dominate, p. 8) but is not commented on.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Credit: the originators, Conn–Scheinberg–Toint [6], are credited (pp. 2, 9), and Powell's NEWUOA is praised as "a major contribution to the field" (p. 5).
  - Critique of the earlier evidence (p. 6):
    - [6]'s results "are not correct", because LANCELOT also finite-differenced the analytically available constraints and those evaluations were counted;
    - Conejo et al. [4] compared against "an inefficient method" (HOPSPACK's augmented-Lagrangian pattern search);
    - CONDOR "provides no numerical comparisons".
  - Own work: the group's finite-difference study [25] justifies the baseline (p. 6) and the noise expectation (pp. 5, 10). Sun–Nocedal [26] supplies the counterexample of IBO failing under noise, called "rare" (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: This is a short letter, with about ten pages of text before the appendices (pp. 1–10). The abstract is hedged ("the method's promising potential"). The body is stronger: FIBO is "one of the most efficient methods for important classes of constrained DFO applications" (p. 2), and the neglect is "a situation this article attempts to rectify" (p. 9). Claims are tied to the cost model in each place they appear (pp. 5, 8, 9). Criticism of prior studies is direct and specific (p. 6). Editing lapses in v1: "gleen" (p. 6); "wheras" (p. 7); "the performance of IBO and FD" meaning FIBO (p. 8); and p. 7 sends the reader to "Appendix B" for the feasible-start details, which are in Appendix A (pp. 12–17).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a numerical rehabilitation of a published algorithm, plus an implementation recipe (pp. 2–3, 9). The final remarks state limits concretely:
  - the subproblem must be solved to optimality, and "it cannot be assured that an optimal solution of this subproblem is always found" (p. 9);
  - noise was "not explored", although the authors expect FIBO to work there (pp. 9–10);
  - the cost of building a feasible interpolation set is left open (p. 5);
  - there is no convergence theory, and none is claimed.
  The only future direction is the noisy case (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the received verdict ("inconclusive", "rarely (if ever) used", p. 2) is put on trial. **Transferable techniques**:
- To get a constrained DFO method cheaply when the constraints are cheap: keep an existing unconstrained interpolation trust-region code and replace only its subproblem with "minimise the model subject to the original constraints and the trust region", solved by an off-the-shelf NLP solver (pp. 3, 6).
- Before declaring a published method ineffective, check its benchmark accounting: which evaluations were counted, and whether derivatives that were available got finite-differenced anyway. Also check the strength of its baseline (p. 6).
- Report per-problem log₂ ratios sorted in ascending order, for final accuracy (with a floor) and for evaluations-to-tolerance at several τ. Count objective and constraint evaluations separately, so that the trade-off is visible (pp. 7–9).
- Report results with and without the cost of the feasibility phase (Appendix B, p. 18).
- Align a commercial baseline's termination with yours through its documented options (`xtol`, `xtol_iters`, `findiff_terminate`) and give both the same evaluation budget (p. 7).
