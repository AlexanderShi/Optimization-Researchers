- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the preface starts from the reader, not from a gap in the literature. It is "a book for people interested in solving optimization problems", and students and practitioners need to understand "the capabilities and limitations" of the algorithms (p. 6). Five application examples follow: a trajectory, seismic fitting, a portfolio, process control and shape design (pp. 6–7). The scope is continuous optimization only, so discrete and stochastic optimization are omitted (p. 6).
  - Transferable: open a field text by saying whom it serves and what it leaves out, before any content.
- **D2 Core idea and architecture**:
  - *Stated emphasis*: large-scale techniques, "interior-point methods, inexact Newton methods, limited-memory methods, and the role of partially separable functions and automatic differentiation" (p. 7). The preface also claims to treat trust-region methods and SQP "more thoroughly than existing texts" (p. 7).
  - *Contents*: 18 chapters plus Appendix A (pp. 10–19). The following reading of the architecture is title-level:
    - Unconstrained optimization is Chapters 2–11 (book pp. 10–313). Constrained optimization is Chapters 12–18 (book pp. 314–573).
    - Linear programming gets two chapters, simplex (13) and interior-point (14). They sit between constrained theory (12) and the NLP algorithms (15–18) (pp. 15–16).
    - Newton's method has a chapter of its own, 6 "Practical Newton Methods" (p. 12). Quasi-Newton methods (8) come after "Calculating Derivatives" (7) (p. 13).
    - Chapter 9 "Large-Scale Quasi-Newton and Partially Separable Optimization" puts L-BFGS (9.1), the compact representation (9.2) and three sections on partial separability side by side (9.4–9.6, with a subsection "Sparsity vs. Partial Separability") (pp. 13–14).
  - *Largest chapters* (card's count, from the contents' page spans): QP, Chapter 16 (book pp. 438–487); SQP, Chapter 18 (526–573); and constrained theory, Chapter 12 (314–359).
  - *Interior methods for NLP* have no chapter. In the contents they appear only as 16.7 "Interior-Point Methods" for QP and 17.2's "Relationship to Primal–Dual Methods" under the log-barrier method (p. 17).
  - *Absent topics*: no section title mentions derivative-free methods, noise or filters (pp. 10–19).
  - Transferable: lay out a field text from fundamentals to large-scale methods, then structured problem classes (least squares, equations), then constrained theory, LP and NLP algorithms. Give special structure its own sections next to the generic large-scale method.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Prerequisites are linear algebra (including numerical linear algebra) and calculus, with background in the Appendix (p. 7). "Formal mathematical requirements are kept to a minimum" (p. 6).
  - The modelling treatment is "light and serves mainly to set the stage for algorithmic developments" (p. 8).
  - The treatment of optimality conditions is "thorough but not exhaustive" (p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof policy**:
  - The major theoretical results "are stated, and in many cases proved, in a rigorous fashion", and readers may skip the proofs (p. 8).
  - The contents set proofs apart as subsections, "Proof of Theorem 4.3" and "Proof of Theorem 12.1" (pp. 11, 15). They give convergence sections to line searches (3.2), trust regions (4.3) and quasi-Newton methods (8.4, with "Global Convergence of the BFGS Method" and "Superlinear Convergence of BFGS", p. 13), and "Analysis of Algorithm 14.2" for LP (p. 16).
  - Nonlinear CG has a subsection "Behavior of the Fletcher–Reeves Method" next to "Global Convergence" (p. 12). This is title-level only; its content was not read.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a as experiments. The preface promises "simple computer exercises that require only minimal programming proficiency" in most chapters (p. 7). It reports the teaching experience that the material is best learned with programming projects, where the student learns the algorithms' "complexity,memory demands, and elegance" (p. 7). The only performance title is "Numerical Performance" in 5.2 (p. 12). Specific software packages are mostly omitted: readers are sent to Moré and Wright's *Optimization Software Guide* and to the NEOS Software Guide (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Other texts are named for what the book leaves out or treats lightly (p. 8):

  - The acknowledgments name 24 colleagues (card's count), among them the later team members Gill, Gould, Toint and "Andreas Waechter" (p. 9). They thank the DOE and NSF sponsors (p. 9).
  - The Final Remark quotes Fletcher's 1987 description of the field: "fascinating blend of theory and computation, heuristics and rigor" (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The style is stated as "conversational". The aim is to make the discussion "flow in a natural way" rather than be "as concise as possible", so the book "is comparatively long" (p. 7).
  - The chapter template: a nonrigorous opening with figures, then motivated algorithms stated explicitly, then rigorous results (p. 8).
  - The preface has labelled sections: THE AUDIENCE, EMPHASIS AND WRITING STYLE, TOPICS NOT COVERED, ADDITIONAL RESOURCE, ACKNOWLEDGMENTS and FINAL REMARK (pp. 7–9).
  - *Comparison*: the two sentences quoted in the a2-02 abstract card of S010 (the Springer landing-page description) are verbatim sentences of this preface's first paragraph (p. 6). This settles a2-02's open point ("whether it reproduces the preface was not checked").

  - *Stated limits*: the "TOPICS NOT COVERED" section (p. 8) omits network, integer, stochastic, nonsmooth and global optimization. It also omits "detailed treatment of some important" topics that are "the focus of intense current research, including interior-point methods for nonlinear programming and algorithms for complementarity problems" (p. 8; a stray accent from "Lemarechal" sits between the two fragments in the text layer). ### B006 · Numerical Optimization, First Edition: back matter with Appendix A (Background Material), references and index (Springer free preview) (book material 1999, https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/bbm:978-0-387-22742-9/1)
**Read level**: full, at three depths:
- *Appendix A* (pp. 1–35): all the prose was read. The displayed formulas are garbled in the text layer, which drops "=" and other symbols (e.g. "xk  x", p. 2), so no formula is taken from it.
- *References* (pp. 36–48): all 259 entries were listed and tallied by author and year (card's count). A "—" ditto entry is assigned to the author of the entry before it.
- *Index* (pp. 49–60): every entry line was scanned, and terms were searched.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Stephen J. Wright · **Voice**: joint · **Place in trajectory**: 1999; the latest dated reference is from 1999 (M. H. Wright, *SIOPT* 9, entry [252], p. 48).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: background material meant to make the book self-contained (the preface, B005 p. 7). The appendix sends the reader to Chapter 2 for rates of convergence (p. 2).
- **D2 Architecture**:
  - A.1 "Elements of Analysis, Geometry, Topology" (pp. 2–18) covers the topology of IRn, continuity and limits, derivatives, directional derivatives, the mean value theorem, the implicit function theorem, the geometry of feasible sets (tangent and normal cones, after Clarke), order notation and scalar root-finding.
  - A.2 "Elements of Linear Algebra" (pp. 18–35) covers norms, subspaces, eigenvalues and the SVD, the determinant and trace, LU/Cholesky/QR (Algorithm A.1, Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting, p. 26), Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury, the interlacing theorem, floating-point arithmetic, and conditioning and stability.
  - *Comparison* with the second edition's contents (B001 p. 16), title-level:
    - The order is reversed: linear algebra comes first in 2006.
    - The 2006 appendix adds "Symmetric Indefinite Factorization", "Sequences", "Rates of Convergence" and "Convex Sets in IRn".
    - It drops "Geometry of Feasible Sets".
    - It adds an Appendix B, "A Regularization Procedure".
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Everything is set in IRn, and Hilbert-space extensions are omitted (p. 21).
  - Feasible sets are mostly described algebraically. The purely geometric view is presented with its advantages and disadvantages, and the authors note that "most of the development of theory, algorithms, and software for constrained optimization" assumes an algebraic description (p. 11).
  - Computation is in double precision, with a unit roundoff "typically about 10−15" (p. 32).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - Small numerical examples check the definitions (Example A.1 for the chain rule, Example A.2 for the mean value theorem, pp. 9–10).
  - The cone property of the tangent cone is proved by rescaling the scalar sequence (p. 12).
  - The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality is proved through a nonnegative quadratic in α (p. 20).
  - The implicit function theorem is stated after Lang (p. 10), and the interlacing theorem is cited from Golub and Van Loan (p. 30).
  - Stability judged against practice: in the worst case the growth factor of Gaussian elimination is around 2^(n−1), but large growth factors "are rarely observed". The verdict is therefore "stable for all practical purposes", while Gaussian elimination without pivoting is "definitely unstable" (pp. 34–35).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a. The only numerics are the well- and ill-conditioned 2×2 systems with perturbed right-hand sides (pp. 33–34).
- **D6 Positioning: references and index**:
  - *Reference counts* (pp. 36–48; card's count): 259 entries. The latest dated entry is from 1999 ([252]) and 7 are from 1998. Several are still "to appear", e.g. [121] and [258] (pp. 42, 48).
  - *Most-listed authors* (card's count): Powell 19 (pp. 38–39, 41, 45–46); Moré 16; Toint 14; Schnabel 12; Nocedal 11; Byrd 11; Griewank 11; Fletcher 10; Gill 10; Gould 10; M. H. Wright 10; S. J. Wright 8.
  - *Nocedal's 11*: the 1980 limited-storage paper [183] (S007); L-BFGS 1989 [151] (S002); Acta Numerica 1992 [184] (S019); Byrd, Nocedal and Yuan 1987 [38] (S020); the compact representations of 1994 [37] (S014); the reduced-Hessian analysis of Byrd and Nocedal [36] (S039, given as Math. Program. 49 (1991)); Gilbert and Nocedal 1992 [103] (S012); Lalee, Nocedal and Plantenga 1998 [146] (S034); Byrd, Hribar and Nocedal, OTC report 97/05 of July 1997 [34] (S008, report version); Algorithm 778 [259] (S005); and Duff, Nocedal and Reid 1987 [73] (S070) (pp. 38, 40–41, 43, 45, 48).
  - *S. J. Wright's 8*: his 1997 book *Primal-Dual Interior-Point Methods* [255], three interior-method preprints dated 1997, 1998 and 1994 (revised 1998) [254, 256, 258] and the Moré–Wright *Optimization Software Guide* [173] (pp. 44, 46, 48).
  - *Derivative-free work*: by title, only Brent's 1973 book [27] (p. 37). Fletcher and Leyffer's filter report, "Nonlinear programming without a penalty function" (NA/171, 1997, [86]), is cited (p. 40), but the index has no "filter" entry.
  - *Test sets*: the CUTE report [25] (p. 37) and MINPACK-2 [5] (p. 36).
  - *Index* (pp. 49–60), at index level:
    - Present: "self-correction, 200" under the BFGS method (p. 50); "Complexity of algorithms, 392, 395, 396, 410, 415–417", all of them in the LP chapters (p. 50); "Robustness, 7" (p. 57); and code entries for LANCELOT, MINOS and SNOPT (pp. 49, 55, 58).
    - Interior methods for nonlinear programs are indexed only at pp. 411, 510–512 and 526 (p. 56).
    - Absent: no entries for filter, noise, derivative-free or Nelder–Mead, and none for KNITRO or NITRO (searched, pp. 49–60).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: each definition is followed by a small example, marked ❏ Example … ❐ (pp. 9–10). The floating-point section ends with "the well-known adage of numerical computing—that one should avoid taking the difference of two similar numbers if at all possible" (p. 33). Conditioning is defined as a property of the problem and stability as a property of the algorithm, "and we adhere to them in this book" (pp. 33–34).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - This is background, not method.
  - *Comparison* with B007 (p. 7): the reference list here already carries the three legible reference corrections of the authors' 2/25/2000 addendum. Entry [26] reads "L. El Ghaoui" (p. 37), [71] reads "P. Deuflhard" (p. 39), and [234] reads "Quasi-Newton" and "Nonlinear Programming 3" (p. 47).
  - The printed pages here are two lower than the addendum's: the addendum cites 588, 612, 614 and 622, and here the items are on 586, 610, 612 and 620 (pp. 13, 37, 39, 47). Whether the "= 0" of (A.24) is present cannot be checked, because the text layer drops "=" (p. 13).
  - Inference: Springer's preview reproduces a later, corrected printing, not the first printing (see B007, D8).

**Method links**:
- Method 5, evidence ✅ (framing, index-level, joint): complexity is indexed only on the LP pages (p. 50). The appendix gives a practical stability verdict against a pessimistic worst-case growth bound and keeps the bound in view (pp. 34–35). Both are in line with taste warning 3 (complexity as the reason to prefer a method). This is not exclusive: Wright's taste mark 1 ("Theory that tracks what wins in practice") and his Method 2 cover it. Exclude from say–do counts.
- Taste mark 4 (self-correction), framing: the joint textbook indexes "self-correction" under BFGS (p. 50). The body page was not read.

- Research Trajectory (dating): in 1999 there are no DFO, noise or filter topics in the index (pp. 49–60). The 2006 edition adds them (B001 pp. 10–11, 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Follow every definition with a small numerical example that the reader can check (pp. 9–10).
- When a worst-case bound is pessimistic, state the bound, state what is observed in practice, and draw the practical verdict explicitly (pp. 34–35).
- Separate conditioning (a property of the problem) from stability (a property of the algorithm) before discussing error (pp. 33–34).

**Quotes**:
