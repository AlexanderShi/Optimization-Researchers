### S028 · A reduced Hessian method for large-scale constrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 5(2), 314–347, 1995, doi:10.1137/0805017)
**Read level**: partial. Read the abstract and introduction (pp. 2–7), the basis choice (pp. 7–8), the correction terms, update criterion, damping and penalty rules (pp. 8–15), Algorithm II (pp. 15–17), the assumption blocks and statements of the semi-local, R-linear and superlinear results (pp. 18–25, 29–33, proofs skimmed) and the final remarks (p. 34). Most equations are unreadable in this text layer (see page mapping). First page checked: title and "Lorenz T Biegler Jorge Nocedal and Claudia Schmid" (p. 1); the file is a preprint with a separate title page.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Lorenz T. Biegler, Claudia Schmid (Chemical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the equality-constrained reduced-Hessian SQP line (Nocedal–Overton, Byrd–Nocedal analyses of Coleman–Conn updating, cited pp. 4, 17), aimed at process-engineering problems with few degrees of freedom; a companion computational paper is announced (pp. 17, 34).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Equality-constrained problems with large n but small n − m, where only first derivatives are available (pp. 2, 4). Earlier reduced-Hessian methods approximate only ZᵀWZ and drop the cross term ZᵀWYp_Y. That is "quite adequate" with orthonormal bases (p. 4), but large problems need cheap bases from variable elimination, and then dropping the cross term can make the algorithm inefficient (p. 4). A companion-paper two-dimensional example shows the error ratio ‖x_k + d_k − x*‖/‖x_k − x*‖ "can be arbitrarily large even close to the solution" (p. 7).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Kept: the SQP range-space/null-space split, BFGS on the reduced Hessian, the ℓ1 merit function (pp. 2–6). Changed: a correction vector w_k ≈ ZᵀWYp_Y enters the null-space step with a damping factor (p. 4). Two ways to get w_k: (a) a Broyden approximation S_k of the rectangular matrix ZᵀW, fitted to a secant equation in reduced Lagrangian gradients, costing no extra evaluations; (b) finite differences of reduced gradients along Yp_Y, costing up to three gradient evaluations per iteration (pp. 4–5, 8–10). Algorithm II uses Broyden by default and switches to finite differences only in the middle of three regions of the (‖p_Y‖, ‖p_Z‖) plane, where "Broydens method is not good enough" (p. 16; region labels are lost in extraction). The BFGS update is skipped when p_Y is large relative to p_Z (Update Criterion I, p. 12), because s_kᵀy_k can then be negative even near the solution (p. 11). The finite-difference variant is shown to be close to the Coleman–Conn method (pp. 8–9; abstract: "in some sense a practical implementation").
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: For the semi-local result (p. 18): iterates in a convex set D with f, c and their first and second derivatives bounded; A of full column rank with bounded basis matrices; sufficiently positive curvature at updates; ‖w_k‖ bounded by a multiple of ‖c_k‖. The authors call the first condition "rather strong" (p. 18). For the rate results (p. 21): second-order sufficiency, Lipschitz Hessians, smooth bases. They also assume the line search keeps iterates in the level-set component of x_k, admitting "There is no practical line search algorithm that can guarantee this condition" (p. 22), and call the local lemma's assumptions "restrictive" (p. 23). Unit steplengths are assumed for the superlinear result; the Maratos effect is left to the companion paper's watchdog (p. 30).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: No boundedness of B_k or S_k is assumed (p. 17). The proofs reuse the Byrd–Nocedal result that at least half of the updated iterates are "good iterates" with cos θ bounded away from zero (pp. 12–13). The rate is tied to the updating frequency |U_k|/k (p. 24), and the condition number is controlled through ψ(B) = tr(B) − ln det(B) (p. 24). R-linear convergence holds "regardless of how often updating takes place" (p. 29). Bounded deterioration of Broyden's method then shows that S_k stays bounded and the safeguards become inactive (pp. 29–30). One-step Q-superlinear convergence is proved by a case analysis over the three regions (pp. 32–33), against the two-step rate of methods that ignore the cross term (p. 4).
- **D5 Experiments**: none in this paper; the computational experience is deferred to the companion paper (p. 34).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic and theoretical: a cross-term-corrected reduced-Hessian SQP with local and one-step superlinear convergence (abstract, p. 2). Stated motivation: the group's earlier reduced-Hessian code for large problems was often subject to instabilities, and the aim was a more robust algorithm that resembles full-space SQP at lower cost (p. 34). Deferred to the companion paper: constants and tolerances, basis changes, the damping computation, the update criterion away from the solution, inequality constraints (p. 34), and a possible fourth region where the curves of the region figure intersect (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (a pre-noise instance): the reduced-Hessian SQP engine is kept and only the dropped cross term is repaired, driven by an explicit estimate (Broyden S_k) with a costlier fallback (finite differences) in the region where the estimate is not good enough (pp. 4, 16). The safeguards on w_k are designed to switch off asymptotically (pp. 10, 30).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the result separates methods (one-step Q-superlinear against two-step superlinear when the cross term is ignored, p. 4), and the analysis covers the version that skips updates in arbitrary patterns (p. 34). Stated: "We have focused only on convergence results that helped us in the design of the algorithm" (p. 34).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak): instabilities in the group's own earlier code are the stated reason for the paper (p. 34).
- new-pattern candidate: **design-driving analysis**. Drop the convenient boundedness assumptions so that the proof shows which safeguards the code needs. The final remarks say that analysing B_k and S_k without boundedness assumptions suggested safeguards that are useful in practice and keep the superlinear rate (p. 34, paraphrased; the sentence is damaged in extraction).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a correction term is costly to compute exactly, carry a cheap secant (Broyden) estimate. Switch to finite differences only in the region of step-component sizes where the analysis shows the cheap estimate is not accurate enough, and say so in the algorithm statement (pp. 16–17).
- Safeguard a possibly unbounded quasi-Newton estimate with a bound that scales with ‖p_Y‖ and a summable sequence. Then prove that the safeguard is inactive near the solution, so it costs no rate (pp. 10, 29–30).
- If a needed quantity depends on the unknown solution (the error ‖e_k‖ in the update criterion), state the theory with it and give the computable surrogate of the same order (‖Zᵀg‖ + ‖c‖) for the code (pp. 12, 17).

**Quotes**:
- "Skipping the BFGS update may appear to be a crude heuristic, but we argue that it gives rise to a sound algorithm" (p. 11)
- "We have focused only on convergence results that helped us in the design of the algorithm and that revealed its main properties" (p. 34)

### S088 · Towards a discrete Newton method with memory for large-scale optimization (Nonlinear Optimization and Applications, Plenum, 1–12, 1996, doi:10.1007/978-1-4899-0289-4_1)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–12), but every number in the text and Tables 1–3 (pp. 10–12) is lost in this text layer, so table entries, problem dimension, memory size and CG limits were not readable. First page checked: title and "Richard H Byrd Jorge Nocedal and Ciyou Zhu", "Technical Report OTC" (p. 1). The file is the OTC report version.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd, Ciyou Zhu (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an L-BFGS-era unconstrained paper; its reference list includes the Nash–Nocedal numerical study of L-BFGS and truncated Newton (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For large problems with gradients but no Hessian, both standard methods have significant drawbacks that can lead to unacceptable computing times (p. 2). Discrete truncated Newton needs many gradient evaluations per iteration; it is hard to choose the inner accuracy; and the curvature information gathered in each inner CG cycle is thrown away (p. 3). L-BFGS can be very slow on ill-conditioned problems (p. 2; also p. 5).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: View the inner CG cycle "from the point of view of variable metric methods" (p. 3). The inner difference pairs (v_i, g(x_k + v_i) − g(x_k)) satisfy the curvature condition because CG stops on negative curvature (p. 6), so they can be stored as limited-memory BFGS pairs. Truncated-Newton steps are then interleaved with L-BFGS steps that use this memory (pp. 5–7). Storage is split into inner pairs and outer pairs (pp. 6–7). Open choices are listed as such: the trigger for a new Newton step, which inner pairs to keep, using the pairs as a CG preconditioner, the update order, and the scaling (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Strong Wolfe line search (p. 3). The tests use strongly convex functions, so no negative-curvature test is needed (p. 8). No convergence theory.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a (no proofs).
- **D5 Experiments**: L-BFGS (benchmark), DINEMO (keeps inner pairs) and ALTERNATE (same schedule, keeps only the outer pair) share the Moré–Thuente line search (p. 8). They are tested on a quartic family whose departure from quadratic and whose diagonal D (condition number and eigenvalue distribution) are varied, with a very stringent stopping test, counting function/gradient evaluations (pp. 8–9, 12). The first test (evenly spread eigenvalues) favours L-BFGS as conditioning worsens (p. 10). Clustered spectra at the same condition number favour DINEMO (pp. 10–12). DINEMO beats ALTERNATE in the great majority of the tests (p. 12).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: An outlined algorithmic idea tested "in a controlled setting" (p. 2). Stated limits: the many implementation questions were not explored (pp. 7–8), and "all the implementation questions listed" must be investigated before DINEMO could become a powerful general-purpose algorithm (p. 12). Not read: any later development of DINEMO.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (variant at the algorithm level): two complementary methods are combined, not one crowned: "combine in a novel way the best properties of each method" (p. 2).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: implementation effects are controlled by an ablation arm that differs only in the idea (ALTERNATE) and by a line search shared by all codes (p. 8).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the undecided choices are listed in the paper and deferred as the next work (pp. 7, 12).
- Workflow D evidence: a tempting conclusion ("We may be tempted to draw the following conclusion", p. 10) is tested with a further experiment before it is drawn.
- new-pattern candidate: **controlled spectral test family**. Build a synthetic family in which condition number, eigenvalue distribution and nonlinearity can be changed one at a time. Here it showed that the eigenvalue distribution, not the condition number, decides (pp. 9–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To test whether saving information helps, add an arm identical except that the information is discarded (DINEMO against ALTERNATE), and fix the line search and step schedule across arms (p. 8).
- When a result seems to depend on conditioning, rerun with the same condition number and a different eigenvalue distribution: two clusters, then a widened middle cluster that CG cannot resolve (pp. 10–11).
- When a design has many interacting choices, say so, test only the core hypothesis in a controlled experiment, and list the rest as open questions (pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "The key idea is to preserve the information generated during the inner CG iteration to improve the quality of the limited memory matrix used in subsequent steps" (p. 7)
- "the distribution of eigenvalues plays a crucial role, as we will show below" (p. 10)

### S046 · On the local behavior of an interior point method for nonlinear programming (Numerical Analysis 1997, 37–56, 1997)
**Read level**: partial. Read the abstract and introduction (pp. 1–4), Assumptions I (pp. 4–5), §2 statements of the conditions for superlinear convergence (pp. 6–10), the §3 feasibility results with their key argument (pp. 10–14), §4 (p. 14), §5 practical strategies (pp. 14–18) and the final remarks (p. 18). Proof algebra is largely unreadable (formula layer lost). First page checked: title and "Richard H Byrd Guanghui Liu y Jorge Nocedalz", "Report OTC" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd, Guanghui Liu (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the rate paper for the Byrd–Hribar–Nocedal interior trust-region SQP algorithm (built on the Byrd–Gilbert–Nocedal framework), the method family that became NITRO/KNITRO (pp. 2, 19; the NITRO/KNITRO link is inferred, not stated in this text).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The algorithm of [2] was shown to be robust, to use second derivatives and sparsity well, and to be competitive with LANCELOT in function evaluations, but "No attempt was made" there to make it rapidly convergent (p. 2). The goal is "the theoretical foundation for an implementation that gives a superlinear rate of convergence" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Near the solution the step is an inexact primal-dual Newton step: projected CG leaves a residual r that must be driven down faster than the current implementation does (p. 5). The Newton Jacobian does not depend on μ, so the region of fast convergence does not shrink as μ decreases (p. 6). The free parameters are the barrier stopping tolerance, the residual tolerance, the fraction-to-the-boundary τ and the μ update. The paper derives conditions on them under which one step at once (i) keeps the slacks positive with no backtracking, (ii) satisfies the next barrier stopping test and (iii) converges superlinearly (pp. 14–15). Three strategies are checked against these conditions. Linear μ decrease gives only a linear rate (pp. 15–16). μ⁺ as a function of μ gives a superlinear rate (pp. 16–17). μ⁺ tied to the optimality residual ‖F(z)‖ "seems to be more attractive" (pp. 17–18). Complementarity-based variants resemble LP rules (p. 18).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumptions I at the limit: KKT point, locally Lipschitz Hessians, linear independence of active gradients, second-order sufficiency and strict complementarity (pp. 4–5). The analysis assumes an iterate already near the minimizer, relying on the global theory of [1] (p. 4). The analysed iteration "is an idealization" of the coded one; resetting multipliers by least squares partly bridges the gap (pp. 5–6, 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Inexact-Newton perturbation bounds on the barrier path give superlinear convergence when the new barrier parameter is o(‖F(z)‖) and the CG residual is bounded by a multiple of a power of ‖F(z)‖, and quadratic convergence under an O(·)-type version of these conditions (Theorem, p. 8; corollary with least-squares multipliers, p. 10; exponents and some symbols are lost in extraction). A heuristic, admittedly "not rigorous" argument shows why the slacks could become negative (p. 11). It is followed by a theorem tying τ and the μ decrease to positivity of the new slacks (p. 12). The one-step barrier test fails "if the barrier parameter decreases quadratically" (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: none.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Theoretical, aimed at implementation: sufficient conditions and concrete parameter strategies (pp. 14–18). It differs from earlier local IPM analyses in keeping the slacks positive without a backtracking line search and in handling an inner barrier stopping test (p. 18). Limits: regularity (Assumptions I) throughout. The constants M, Q, L, C that measure KKT conditioning are kept explicit "in a future study" of degeneracy and ill-conditioning (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: a rate result for the method as implemented (trust-region SQP with CG, fraction-to-boundary, inner stopping test), with the gap between the analysed and the coded iteration stated (pp. 5–6). The results discriminate between strategies: linear μ decrease is proved linear only (p. 16).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (link to later options inferred): the undecided barrier-update and fraction-to-boundary choices are made the subject of the study and given as alternative strategies, not one rule (pp. 3, 15–18).
- Heuristic 6 evidence (not a Method; recorded as a note): robustness first in [2], the rate in a follow-up paper (p. 2).
- new-pattern candidate: **design-driving analysis** (as in S028): the theorems are converted into checkable parameter rules for the code (pp. 14–18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write down every property the step must have near the solution (slack positivity without backtracking, meeting the next inner stopping test, a fast rate). Derive each as an inequality on the same few parameters, then check each candidate parameter strategy against all of them (pp. 14–18).
- Keep the problem constants (KKT-conditioning bounds) explicit in the local analysis, so that a later study of degeneracy can reuse the proofs (p. 18).
- Analyse an idealized iteration, then add the one implementation feature (least-squares multiplier resets) that closes most of the gap, as a corollary (pp. 5–6, 10).

**Quotes**:
- "the goal of this paper is to develop the theoretical foundation for an implementation that gives a superlinear rate of convergence" (p. 2)
- "the results obtained here will be directly applicable to that algorithm" (p. 5)

### S034 · On the implementation of an algorithm for large-scale equality constrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 8(3), 682–706, 1998, doi:10.1137/S1052623493262993)
**Read level**: full for the parts a supplement card needs: abstract and §1–§2 (pp. 1–4), §3 statements (pp. 4–7), §4 implementation details (pp. 7–16), §5 test setup, tables and discussion (pp. 16–23) and §6 (pp. 22–24); proofs are by reference only. First page checked: title and "MARUCHA LALEE, JORGE NOCEDAL, AND TODD PLANTENGA" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Marucha Lalee, Todd Plantenga (Sandia) (p. 1); Richard Byrd thanked "for his insights" (p. 24) · **Place in the trajectory**: the equality-constrained core code for the interior and inequality algorithms "currently under investigation" ([34], [3]; [3] is Byrd–Gilbert–Nocedal, pp. 1, 24), the line that leads to NITRO/KNITRO (inferred, not stated).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Many SQP trust-region methods for equality constraints had been proposed in ten years, but to the authors' knowledge none had received a careful implementation (p. 22). The paper implements the Byrd–Omojokun method for problems with "a thousand or more unknowns" (p. 1). Inequalities are left out deliberately, to test robustness on the simpler case and because this code is the core of the inequality algorithms then in progress (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Byrd–Omojokun splits the SQP subproblem into a vertical step in the range of A_k inside ζΔ and a horizontal step in the null space (pp. 2–3); for n = m it reduces to Levenberg–Marquardt (pp. 3–4). Both are solved by dogleg or by Steihaug CG, transformed for ellipsoidal trust regions (pp. 4–7). Implementation choices that matter: an "unscaled Steihaug iteration" that drops (ZᵀZ)⁻¹ to save cost, with a fall-back to the regular iteration (p. 10); Z_k by direct elimination with MA28 and Heuristic I for basis changes (pp. 10–11); compact L-BFGS with Powell damping when second derivatives are missing (pp. 11–12); a non-squared ℓ2 merit function with a penalty rule plus Heuristic II (pp. 12–14); second-order correction (p. 14); an aggressive radius update (pp. 14–15). Algorithm ETR offers Cholesky or CG solves for A_kᵀA_k (pp. 15–16). All test parameters are printed (p. 16).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Twice continuously differentiable f, c; n ≥ m (p. 1); A(x_k) of full column rank (p. 2), which the authors call not intrinsic to the method (p. 24). An approximate Cauchy step is adequate for global convergence when its error tends to zero as fast as ‖Z_kᵀḡ_k‖ (analysis "similar to" [6], p. 8).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: none new; properties are cited (dogleg convergence carries over [33], p. 5; the Algorithm 4.1 tolerance gives a Q-quadratic rate by an analysis similar to [11], p. 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: CUTE equality problems, some modified to be harder (p. 16). Each heuristic is first tested only on the problems where it changes anything (Tables 5.2–5.4, pp. 17–19). Robustness: 102 hard starting points, 7 failures from rank-deficient Jacobians (p. 19). Large problems with exact Hessians against L-BFGS and three vertical-step options (Tables 5.6–5.7, pp. 20–21). A comparison with LANCELOT A at the same tolerance 10⁻⁵, LANCELOT given its best of 11 preconditioners (pp. 22–23). Losses printed: more CPU time on several problems (p. 22).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Software and empirical evidence that the method "is quite effective and merits further development" (p. 23). Named limits: rank deficiency, probed with the constructed problem ORTHRGDS (no convergence to the stop tolerance once more than 5 columns of A* vanish, p. 22); no preconditioners (p. 8); the horizontal subproblem takes up to 60% of the time with L-BFGS (p. 23). Six numbered refinements, and "the most pressing need" of handling inequality constraints (pp. 23–24).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the rival is run at the same stop tolerance and credited with its best preconditioner, and the losses are printed (p. 22). Evaluation counts and CPU time are both reported, because users weigh them differently (p. 22).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the choice of linear algebra cannot be decided once for all, so it is exposed to the user: "We therefore make both options available to the user in our software" (p. 20). Failure modes (rank deficiency) are probed with a constructed problem and listed with numbered refinements as the next work (pp. 22–24).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: structure decides the solver option: an ill-conditioned large Jacobian ruins CG, and fill-in ruins Cholesky (p. 20).
- Heuristic 6 and Heuristic 5 evidence (not Methods; recorded as a note): robustness is tested first with hard starting points (p. 19); all defaults are printed as numbers (p. 16).

### S101 · The modified absolute-value factorization norm for trust-region minimization (High Performance Algorithms and Software in Nonlinear Optimization, 225–241, 1998, doi:10.1007/978-1-4613-3279-4_15)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–15), but the results table (pp. 13–14) and all numbers and formulas are lost in this text layer. First page checked: title and "Nicholas I M Gould and Jorge Nocedal", "RALTR" (p. 1); the file is the Rutherford Appleton Laboratory report version.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Nicholas I. M. Gould (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an unconstrained trust-region paper with Gould, released as an HSL code (abstract, p. 1); inspired by Nick Higham's comments at a Dundee Numerical Analysis Conference (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Trust-region theory works for any norm uniformly related to ℓ2, yet "Little attention has been given to the appropriate choice of norm" even though the choice affects every iteration (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: The ideal trust region would use the |H| norm, which reflects the model's curvature and needs a single spectral factorization, but that factorization is too costly for large n (pp. 3–4). Instead, factor H = PLBLᵀPᵀ and take M = PL|B|LᵀPᵀ, where |B| flips the signs of negative pivot eigenvalues (p. 4). The subproblem then becomes block-diagonal and trivially solvable from the one factorization (pp. 4–6). In the convex case, shrinking the radius amounts to a backtracking line search along the (quasi-)Newton direction (abstract, p. 6). In the indefinite case the solution is a root of a quartic found by Newton, with the Moré–Sorensen hard case covered (pp. 6–7). Near-singular H is handled by a Cheng–Higham-type modified absolute-value factorization and a Newton secular-equation solver (pp. 7–9). Goldfarb's earlier PLLᵀPᵀ norm is rejected because it does not reflect the scaling of the problem: for diagonal H it stays the ℓ2 norm however ill-conditioned H is (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Uniform equivalence of M to ℓ2 requires bounded H and bounded L. H is bounded for a Newton model with iterates in a bounded set (p. 10). Bounded L holds for Bunch–Parlett, Fletcher and the Duff–Reid sparse code, but not necessarily for Bunch–Kaufman or LAPACK, which "must be viewed as untrustworthy for our application" (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Eigenvalue bounds on M through Higham's theorem and entry bounds on L (pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Experiments**: A standard trust-region algorithm (p. 12) with three norms (ℓ2, absolute-value, Schnabel–Eskow modified Cholesky from LANCELOT) on the larger CUTE problems with frequent negative curvature, under a 30 CPU-minute limit (p. 11). Three categories of outcome: ℓ2 best on easy problems; dense factors make factorization norms lose; the new norm wins on most hard, highly nonlinear problems (pp. 12–14). The cost is many wasted function evaluations, traced to an initial radius ‖M‖ that is too large and to pivot sign changes (p. 14).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A norm, a subproblem solver and an HSL code (p. 1). The claim is scoped: "We do not pretend" the norm "is uniformly appropriate", but it should be considered for ill-conditioned problems (p. 14). Fletcher's objection to Goldfarb's proposal at a NATO ARI (distortion, pivot-order changes upsetting radius management) is accepted as applying here too; pivot-order changes are recognized as a "possible defect" under investigation (pp. 14–15).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the recommendation is tied to problem structure, and where each norm loses is recorded: ℓ2 on easy problems, cost of factors on dense Hessians, the new norm on hard problems (pp. 12–14).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (weak): costs are printed alongside wins (wasted evaluations, their two causes, p. 14).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: one component of the classical trust-region method (the norm) is replaced and the rest kept (Algorithm, p. 12), but the change is not regime-driven and has no estimate or recovery path.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak): a rival's objection to a related method (Fletcher on Goldfarb) is applied to their own method and turned into ongoing work on radius adjustment (pp. 14–15).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Pick the trust-region norm from the factorization you already compute for the step, so the subproblem becomes diagonal or block-diagonal and is solved without extra factorizations (pp. 4–6).
- When a numerical kernel underlies a convergence requirement (bounded L), check which library implementations meet it and name the ones that do not (p. 10).
- Report results in categories of problem behaviour rather than one aggregate, so the reader sees where the method should and should not be used (pp. 12–14).

**Quotes**:
- "For problems involving a large number of variables, this decomposition is likely out of the question" (p. 4)
- "are currently investigating more sophisticated schemes for trust-region management" (p. 14)

### S118 · Active set and interior methods for nonlinear optimization (Proceedings of the International Congress of Mathematicians 1998, Documenta Mathematica Extra Volume III, 667–676, 1998, doi:10.4171/dms/1-3/64)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–10). First page checked: title (printed "Ative Set and Interior Methods" in the text layer) and "Richard H. Byrd and Jorge Nocedal" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an invited ICM survey written while the Byrd–Hribar–Nocedal interior code was being developed (reference [1], OTC 97/05, p. 8); it lays out the active-set against interior choice that KNITRO later resolved by offering both (per SKILL.md Method 6; not stated here).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: "The most important open problem in nonlinear optimization is the solution of large constrained problems" (p. 1). The KKT system is not suitable for direct computation: the number of active sets grows exponentially and KKT points can be maximizers (p. 2). Methods are organized around "two fundamental ideas: transformation and approximation" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea (survey structure)**: Exact ℓ1 penalty functions: simple, but nondifferentiable and sensitive to the penalty parameter (pp. 2–3). SQP as the most powerful active-set method, whose QP gives both an active-set guess and the right level of approximation (pp. 3–4). Interior methods: the barrier problem with slacks (pp. 4–5), then three advances. (1) Primal-dual steps, derived without duality by multiplying the rational complementarity equation by S (pp. 5–6). (2) Coping with ill-conditioning: direct solves tolerate it; CG needs the change of variables that makes Σ tend to the identity (pp. 6–7). (3) Mehrotra-type predictor-corrector probing, so far only for convex problems (p. 7). Final comparison: active-set methods give an active-set guess and warm starts but solve costly inequality QPs. Interior subproblems are easier, but interior methods do not identify the active set until high accuracy, cannot reuse information, and are not yet known to be as robust on nonconvex problems (p. 8).
- **D3–D5**: n/a (a survey; no theorems or experiments of its own; conclusions "based on the limited numerical experience" accumulated so far, p. 8).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A critical assessment, not a result. Open questions named: whether the difficulty of choosing the penalty parameter is serious enough to sink the exact-penalty approach (p. 3); whether merit functions should allow controlled increases, citing Fletcher–Leyffer's filter report [5] (p. 3); whether the primal-dual transformation "is the best possible" (p. 6); whether interior methods are as robust as active-set methods (p. 8). Predicted direction: interior codes "may need to switch to an active set iteration" (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: neither approach is crowned. Each side's advantages and drawbacks are listed, and a hybrid (an interior code switching to active set) is anticipated (p. 8), which is the integration-with-crossover step of Method 6.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: undecided choices are written down as open questions: the penalty parameter (p. 3), merit-function acceptance (p. 3), the barrier-parameter rule left to predictor-corrector probing (p. 7). The penalty-parameter question is the one later answered by penalty steering (Method 3 worked case; that link is inferred from the topic, not stated here).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (weak): the survey asks what the theory of ill-conditioning means for codes (direct against CG solves, pp. 6–7) and draws a design consequence: quasi-Newton variants should approximate only the Hessian of the original Lagrangian, not the barrier Hessian (p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate: **explain a method by its key transformation**. Present the primal-dual method as a nonlinear transformation of the optimality conditions that suits Newton's method, not by its received name; "primal-dual" is called "not very descriptive of the key idea" (p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a survey, close with a side-by-side ledger of what each method family does well and badly at the subproblem level (active-set guess, warm start, subproblem cost, robustness), and state that the verdict rests on limited experience (p. 8).
- Derive the primal-dual system by multiplying the rational complementarity equation by S, so that Newton's method sees a quadratic, and use this to explain why it beats the primal barrier step (pp. 5–6).
- When a matrix becomes unbounded by construction (Σ = ΛS⁻¹ as μ → 0), find the change of variables that sends it to the identity before applying CG, and state the price in CG cost (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "it has not yet been firmly established whether the difficulty in choosing the penalty parameter is serious enough to prevent it from becoming a powerful technique for large-scale optimization" (p. 3)
- "future interior point codes may need to switch to an active set iteration, if necessary" (p. 8)
