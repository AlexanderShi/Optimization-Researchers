### S007 · Updating quasi-Newton matrices with limited storage (Mathematics of Computation 1980, DOI 10.1090/s0025-5718-1980-0572855-7)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–10 was read: abstract, §§1–6, acknowledgements and references. Several displayed formulas ((1), (4), (5), (13), (19)) and the §4 recursion are garbled in the OCR. Their content is taken from the surrounding prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author; IIMAS, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, p. 9) · **Place in trajectory**: the origin of L-BFGS, here called SQN (S002 p. 4 confirms "it is called the SQN method"). Received 29 May 1979 (p. 1). It thanks J. Dennis "for guiding comments" and L. Nazareth "for his continuing advice" (p. 9), and it builds on the Nazareth–Nocedal Stanford SOL report 78-29 and their manuscript on variable-storage methods (refs. 8–9, p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Quasi-Newton matrices need n(n+1)/2 storage, which is impossible for large n (p. 1). There were two existing ways out. Sparsity-preserving updates (Toint, Shanno) need the sparsity structure. Variable-storage CG methods (Buckley; Nazareth; Nazareth–Nocedal) need no structure, but after a number of iterations they discard the quasi-Newton matrix and restart from a diagonal (p. 1). The entry is that one wasteful step, the wholesale discard. The aim is a matrix that is "updated continuously", so as to have "a more up to date model of our function" (p. 1).
  - Transferable: find the single step in the incumbent family that throws information away (here the periodic restart), and design the variant that never discards everything at once.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Store each BFGS correction as the pair (s, y). Once m pairs are stored, drop the oldest pair and insert the newest one at every iteration (abstract, p. 1). The obstacle is in the usual sum form H̄ = H + U(s, y, H): later corrections depend on earlier ones, so dropping U(s0, y0, H0) "would have to change all the other ones" (p. 2). Storing extra vectors avoids this, but it loses positive definiteness or quadratic termination (p. 2). The fix is to use the product form H̄ = vᵀHv + ρssᵀ (eq. (3), p. 2), in which dropping a correction just means setting v = I and ρssᵀ = 0 (p. 2). This gives the "special BFGS matrices" (4)–(5) (p. 3), and m = 1 gives Shanno's memoryless BFGS (p. 4). The matrices are used in two ways: in a quasi-Newton iteration (SQN, (17), p. 6) and in a preconditioned CG iteration (SCG, (13), p. 5), which uses the matrix before the most recent one as preconditioner (p. 5). The product H·g is computed by a recursion "essentially the same as the formula for the usual BFGS" (Matthies–Strang), at most 4nM + 2M + n multiplications (pp. 7–8). What is unchanged: the BFGS formula and the line search. BFGS is chosen "since it is considered to be the most efficient", and the paper believes that similar algorithms cannot be developed for the other members of the Broyden θ-class (p. 1).
  - Transferable: when a representation blocks the operation you need (deleting an old update), switch to a mathematically equivalent representation (product form instead of sum form) in which the operation is trivial (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Unconstrained smooth f. yᵀs > 0 at every step, justified because "In gradient-related methods this can always be done, provided the line search is sufficiently accurate" (p. 2). H0 is positive definite and diagonal (p. 2). The quasi-Newton equation in past directions needs a strictly convex quadratic and conjugate steps (p. 3). Quadratic termination needs exact line searches (pp. 5–6). Local convergence on nonlinear functions needs "asymptotically exact line searches" and is only sketched, by reference to the Nazareth–Nocedal manuscript [9] (p. 7). The experiments use an inexact Wolfe-type search (p. 9), so the analysed and the tested versions differ.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) Property (a): positive definiteness is inherited from H0 when yᵢᵀsᵢ > 0 (p. 3). (b) Property (b): Hₖyⱼ = sⱼ for the past m directions on a quadratic with conjugate steps, from the identity vᵢyᵢ = 0 (eqs. (6)–(7), pp. 3–4). (c) Quadratic termination: SCG and SQN directions coincide with PCG using the fixed preconditioner H0, shown up to m+2 and then by induction (eq. (14), pp. 5–6). (d) Bounded condition number. Fletcher's eigenvalue theorem (18), min{λ,1} ≤ λ⁺ ≤ max{λ,1}, applies because "any Hk obtained by means of the special update can also be obtained using the usual BFGS formula", which yields the chain (19) (pp. 6–7). (e) Local convergence for nonlinear f is claimed "in a similar way as it was done in [9]" and is not proved here (p. 7).
  - Transferable: prove a property of a new variant by showing that each matrix it produces is a matrix the classical method would produce from some start, so the classical theorem transfers unchanged (p. 7).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Table I (p. 8) counts function evaluations for CG, SCG (MSTORE 2, 4, 8), SQN (MSTORE 3, 4, 8), BFGS and Shanno's method. The problems are Fletcher's helix (n = 3), Biggs (6), Powell singular (4), Wood (4), Extended Powell (8, 16, 20) and Trigonometric (10, 15, 20) (pp. 8–9). BFGS and Shanno were run with the Shanno–Phua code, and all methods used the same safeguarded cubic-interpolation line search with constants .0001 and .9 (p. 9). The stopping test was ‖g‖ < 10⁻⁸ (10⁻⁶ for Powell singular), on a Burroughs B6700 (p. 9). Larger MSTORE values that use more storage than BFGS were run on purpose, "as we are concerned with the effect of storing more vectors" (p. 9). Two results are omitted but declared. SQN with MSTORE = 2 "does not perform well … and we do not report its results here" (p. 9). On the Trigonometric problems the methods converged to different solutions, so only SQN is reported (p. 9). Where the OCR is legible, the rows show the trend, e.g. Biggs: CG 235, SCG 60/49/46, SQN 95/77/68, BFGS 50, Shanno 82. They also show exceptions, e.g. Wood, SCG 146/181/155 (p. 8), which matches "few instances where the performance does not improve" (p. 9). All problems have n ≤ 20, despite the large-scale motivation.
  - Transferable: when the claim is that more memory helps, push the memory parameter beyond the range anyone would use in practice and say why you did it (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: The lineage is Perry, Shanno, Buckley and Nazareth (pp. 1, 10). The critique is specific: the restart discard (p. 1). Shanno's method is credited as "considerably more efficient than the standard conjugate gradient", and SQN with MSTORE = 3 is only "somewhat faster than Shanno's method" (p. 9). The H·g recursion is credited to Matthies–Strang [6] (p. 7). The theory rests on Fletcher [5] and Stoer [14] (pp. 6–7).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Ten pages. The abstract states the contribution in five plain sentences, the strongest being that the matrices "have some desirable properties" (p. 1). An unproved belief is labelled as belief ("We believe that similar algorithms cannot be developed…", p. 1). The conclusion lists three proved properties and then "Numerical experiments indicate that the resulting algorithms are very efficient and that their performance improves consistently as the storage used increases" (p. 9). That is slightly stronger than the "few instances" caveat in §5 (p. 9). Roughly six pages of theory to two of numbers. The recursion is given as Fortran-like pseudo-code (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm (the limited-memory update and its H·g recursion) with small-scale theory: positive definiteness, quadratic termination, the past-m secant property and a bounded condition number on quadratics (p. 9). There is no global convergence proof for nonlinear f, and local convergence is only sketched (p. 7). The tests are small (n ≤ 20), and the MSTORE = 2 SQN results are withheld (p. 9). No future directions are stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 3, variant ⚠: the engine is kept (BFGS update and line search) and only the storage/updating component is replaced (pp. 1–2). The driver is a storage limit, though, not an explicit estimate with a recovery path.
- Method 6, evidence ✅ (weak): the motivation contrasts structure-needing sparse updates with a method that needs no structural knowledge (p. 1). This is the side of step 3 that keeps a general method for when the structure is unknown.
- Method 1, variant ⚠: all methods share one line search, and the competitors run in their authors' code (p. 9). But one weak configuration is withheld (only declared in a sentence), not printed (p. 9).
- Method 5, contradiction ✗ (early-career): the analysis covers quadratics with exact line searches, while the tested version uses an inexact search (pp. 5–6, 9). So the analysed version is not the version people run. S020 and S002 close the gap.
- New-pattern candidate: transfer the classical theorem through an equivalence (p. 7; also S002 p. 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use the product (multiplicative) form of a rank-two update when you need to delete or reorder corrections (pp. 2–3).
- Show quadratic termination of a modified method by proving it generates the same directions as a known method with a fixed preconditioner (pp. 5–6).
- In a memory-parameter study, include settings beyond practical use, and flag them explicitly as diagnostic runs (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "A disadvantage of these methods is that after a certain number of iterations the quasi-Newton matrix is discarded" (p. 1)
- "For some values of MSTORE the limited storage methods are actually using more storage than the BFGS. In practice, one would of course never do this." (p. 9)

### S020 · Global convergence of a class of quasi-Newton methods on convex problems (SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis 1987, DOI 10.1137/0724077)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–20 was read: abstract, §§1–7, acknowledgements and references. The scan's equations are largely garbled. The argument is reconstructed from the prose, which is fully readable. The §6 table is partly garbled. (The work list spells the title "cass"; the paper's title page has "class", p. 1.)
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd (University of Colorado, Boulder) and Ya-Xiang Yuan (DAMTP, Cambridge) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Nocedal is now at Northwestern EECS (p. 1). Received 8 July 1986 (p. 1). His first joint work with Byrd in this batch. It thanks Jorge Moré and Michael Powell (p. 19). It is the paper behind the Acta 1992 / 2026 summary that the Broyden class converges except DFP. SKILL.md Method 5 previously listed this body as not read.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters from a gap between theory and practice: "there are some important gaps in what is understood about the behavior of these methods as implemented in practice" (p. 1). Powell (1976) proved global convergence of BFGS on convex functions with the line search used in codes, "This result however has not been extended to the DFP method, nor to any other algorithm in Broyden's class" (p. 1). Each earlier result has a named weakness. Stoer's result is weak because its ε depends on the initial Hessian approximation (p. 2). Ritter's assumptions are "not known" to be satisfied "by any practical line search" (p. 3). Dixon showed that all members coincide with exact line searches, but "their performance varies markedly when inexact lines searches are performed" (p. 2). The question is why DFP behaves worse.
  - Transferable: take the one existing proof that matches the implemented algorithm (Powell 1976), try to extend it to the whole family, and treat the member where it breaks as the finding.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: No new algorithm. The paper is an analysis of Broyden's class (1.4) with φ ∈ [0, 1) under the Wolfe conditions (1.5)–(1.6) (pp. 1–2). The key insight comes from two scalar monitors of Bₖ. The trace equation (3.2) has a fourth term that shifts the eigenvalues to the left in proportion to λₖ/cos²θₖ (pp. 7–8). The determinant inequality (3.9) has small curvature corrected by an increase in some eigenvalues (p. 9). When cos θ is small, "little progress may be achieved, but a self correcting mechanism takes place" (p. 8). The left-shift term is absent in the DFP method, φ = 1 (p. 10). Results:
  - Theorem 3.1: global convergence for uniformly convex f (pp. 10–11).
  - Lemma 4.2: R-linear rate (p. 12).
  - Theorem 4.1: Q-superlinear when the unit step is tried first and the Hessian is Hölder at x* (p. 14).
  - Theorem 5.1: lim inf ‖gₖ‖ = 0 for convex f that is bounded below with bounded Hessian (p. 15).
  - The results extend to φₖ varying in [0, 1) and bounded away from 1 (pp. 3, 19).
  - Transferable: control the two failure directions of a matrix sequence with two monitors. The trace catches eigenvalues that grow too large, and the determinant catches eigenvalues that shrink too small (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is twice continuously differentiable. For §§3, 4 and 6 the level set is convex and f is uniformly convex on it (Assumption 2.1, p. 3). §5 relaxes this to convex, bounded below, with bounded Hessian (Assumption 5.1, pp. 14–15). Superlinearity also needs Hölder continuity of G at x* and unit steps tried (p. 14). B1 is any positive definite matrix (p. 1). The authors flag what they cannot remove: "It is not known if the assumption that f is convex can be weakened" (p. 3). The nonconvex corollary is only local, with the caveat that "there is no guarantee that the line search will prevent the sequence from leaving this connected component of the level set" (p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - Lemma 2.1 bounds ‖sₖ‖ between c1‖g‖cos θ and c2‖g‖cos θ, and gives the per-step decrease fₖ₊₁ − f* ≤ [1 − αmc1cos²θₖ](fₖ − f*). It holds for any quasi-Newton method with a positive definite Hessian approximation, however Bₖ is updated (pp. 4–6).
  - Lemma 2.2 reads the steplength λₖ as the ratio of model curvature to true curvature along sₖ (p. 6).
  - Lemma 3.1 bounds each trace term, using Goldfarb's ‖y‖²/yᵀs ≤ M (pp. 7–8).
  - Lemma 3.2 uses Pearson's determinant identity and the AM–GM inequality to show the product of the steplengths is at least c4^k, so the steplengths are bounded below in mean (pp. 9–10).
  - Theorem 3.1 argues by contradiction: if cos θₖ → 0, the left shift drives the trace negative (pp. 10–11).
  - Lemma 4.2 applies AM–GM twice to the products of cos² (pp. 12–13).
  - Theorem 4.1 changes variables so that G(x*) = I, using invariance, then goes from summability Σ‖xₖ − x*‖^p < ∞ through Griewank–Toint Prop. 4 to Dennis–Moré Theorem 6.4 (pp. 11, 14).
  - §5 shows that the excess term ηₖ → 0, so that "the trace equation (5.4) is therefore essentially the same as for BFGS" (pp. 15–16).
  - The paper says openly that it "borrows heavily from the techniques developed by Powell (1976)" (p. 6).
  - Transferable: get global convergence from a subsequence with cos θ bounded away from zero plus the angle-based decrease lemma. Get that subsequence by contradiction from a positive quantity (the trace) that would otherwise go negative (pp. 6, 10–11).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: §6 (p. 18) has one deliberately chosen 2-D non-quadratic function (6.2) satisfying Assumption 2.1. It follows Powell (1986): x1 = (cos 70°, sin 70°) and B1 = diag(1, 10⁴), "one very large eigenvalue". It was run on a Vax 11/780 in double precision with Moré's line search (α = 10⁻⁴, β = 0.9), counting iterations to ‖g‖ ≤ 10⁻⁴‖g1‖ for several φ (p. 18). The legible counts, 15, 21, 26, 32, 66, 115, 630, 2233 and 4041, grow as φ goes toward 1. The OCR does not keep their alignment with the φ values (p. 18). The run also reports an internal diagnostic. Under BFGS the trace fell from 10⁴ to 3 in 10 iterations, while after 3000 DFP iterations it was still 1100 (p. 18; the OCR prints the exponent as "104"). It also checks the opposite case: "when small eigenvalues are given, neither method has difficulties" (p. 18). Other functions are mentioned ("not uncommon to observe this type of result") with no data (p. 18). The experiment confirms a mechanism; it is not a benchmark.
  - Transferable: design one tiny experiment that isolates the mechanism the proof identified (a start matrix with one huge eigenvalue), sweep the family parameter, print the internal quantity the proof tracks (the trace), and also run the opposite case (small eigenvalues) as a control (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: "This generalizes Powell's well-known result for the BFGS method" (abstract, p. 1). The paper reviews Dennis–Moré, Stachurski, Ritter, Griewank–Toint, Stoer and Werner, naming each one's limitation rather than dismissing it (pp. 2–3). It credits independent concurrent work: Xie Yuan-Fu (1986) "has independently proven a result similar to the one of §3, but with additional restrictions" (p. 3). It ties the DFP analysis to Powell's (1986) 2-D quadratic study (pp. 17–18).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract covers the assumptions, result, exception, generalization and insight in five sentences (p. 1). The insight is claimed as a contribution in its own right: the uniformly convex analysis "gives us much insight into the properties of the Broyden class of algorithms, and is one of the main contributions of this paper" (p. 3). Each theorem is preceded by an intuition passage: the eigenvalue-shift discussion and two cases (pp. 8–9), and "The basic idea of the proof is as follows" (p. 10). The DFP question is stated as an open problem: the authors could neither prove convergence nor build a counterexample (p. 19). Hedging is moderate ("seems", "it is not clear", pp. 17–18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory with an explanation: family-wide global and superlinear convergence, and a mechanism (self-correction) for the empirical BFGS/DFP gap (pp. 1, 19). The limits are specific: convexity is needed (p. 3); the §5 rates are not extended, because they need G(x*) to be nonsingular (p. 14); the nonconvex result is local only (p. 19); DFP is left open, though convergence for convex f is known with exact line searches (Powell 1971, 1972) (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 5, evidence ✅ (strong): the analysis takes the practical line search "as implemented in practice" (p. 1). The theory separates BFGS from DFP (abstract; pp. 10, 17, 19). A targeted run confirms the predicted deterioration (p. 18). What could not be proved is kept as an open question (p. 19, step 5). This is the body that SKILL.md Method 5 cited as "not read".
- Method 1: n/a (no benchmark).
- Also Heuristic 1 (failure demo on a small chosen problem with an internal diagnostic, the trace, p. 18) and Heuristic 10 (Byrd as theory partner, p. 1).
- New-pattern candidates: trace–determinant bookkeeping (pp. 7–10; also S002 p. 23); family-level theorem that locates the boundary member (abstract, pp. 10, 19; also S012 pp. 11–13, 20–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the trace of the updated matrix as a sum of signed terms and identify the term that shifts the eigenvalues to the left. The member that lacks it is your suspect (pp. 7–8, 17).
- Bound the geometric mean of the steplengths through the determinant and AM–GM when individual steps cannot be bounded (pp. 9–10).
- Assume G(x*) = I without loss of generality, using affine invariance, before a local-rate argument (p. 11).
- Confirm a convergence-theory prediction with a one-function experiment that reports the quantity the proof tracks (p. 18).

**Quotes**:
- "However there are some important gaps in what is understood about the behavior of these methods as implemented in practice." (p. 1)
- "Nevertheless, we have seen in this paper that the DFP method can be much less efficient than the BFGS method, and that the reason for this is that it lacks the self-correcting property given by the fourth term on the right-hand side of equation (3.2)." (p. 19)

### S002 · On the limited memory BFGS method for large scale optimization (Mathematical Programming 1989, DOI 10.1007/bf01589116)
**Read level**: partial. The whole text of file pp. 1–27 was read: abstract, all sections including the convergence analysis and final remarks, acknowledgements and references. The text layer, however, drops every digit and most math symbols. Every table entry, every equation and section number, and the parameter values (m, the line-search constants, problem numbers, n) are blank, and so are the digits of the scaling labels ("M1"–"M4" print as "M"). The formulas cannot be read. The prose is intact. The version read is the Northwestern EECS preprint (p. 1), not the journal pagination.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Dong C. Liu (Northwestern EECS) (pp. 1–2) · **Place in trajectory**: the study that made L-BFGS (the 1980 SQN, p. 4) a standard. The code was released through the Harwell library (p. 4). The acknowledgements thank Griewank and Lemaréchal, "Richard Byrd for suggesting the scaling used in method M", and Jorge Moré, who encouraged the investigation (p. 25). The convergence proof cites Byrd–Nocedal's analysis tool (pp. 24, 26).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Limited-memory methods were known to beat CG on medium problems, but little was known about how they compare with Newton's method or the partitioned quasi-Newton algorithm on large problems (p. 3). No one had compared the limited-memory methods with each other since Gill–Murray, so their most effective implementation was not known (p. 3). The entry is an empirical gap. The method had only been tested against the weak baseline (CG), never against its strongest neighbours. A convergence analysis is added (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable: if a method class has only been compared with the easy baseline, the next paper is the head-to-head with the strongest neighbours: the rival in the same class and the structure-exploiting method (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: L-BFGS is described as almost identical in its implementation to BFGS, the only difference being the matrix update (p. 4). Algorithm 1 (p. 5) uses a Wolfe line search with the unit step always tried first, then applies the stored pairs to H0, computing the product with Strang's recursion (p. 5). It changes two things from 1980. The line search is no longer forced to make a cubic interpolation, and quadratic termination is given up on purpose, to make the method resemble BFGS "as much as possible" (p. 6). The new ingredient is a dynamic scaling of H0 (scaling section, pp. 13–15). The scalings tested are none, initial only, the scalar γₖ = yᵀs/‖y‖² every iteration, and a diagonal Dₖ minimizing the Frobenius-norm secant residual over the last m steps, with safeguards (pp. 13–14). The labels' digits are lost; this mapping is inferred from the descriptions on p. 14. The two line-search modes become user options (p. 13). For the analysis, an equivalent Algorithm 2 updates a bounded direct matrix B⁽⁰⁾ₖ "m̃" times (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: when you make a cheap version of a classical method, keep it as close to the classical method as possible and drop theoretical properties that do not matter at scale, saying so (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Smooth f, with analytic f and g available and n large (p. 2). The convergence theorem assumes f twice continuously differentiable, a convex level set, uniform convexity bounds on G (Assumptions, p. 22), and bounded {B⁽⁰⁾ₖ} and inverses (pp. 22–23). The tests require low accuracy "because this is common in practical applications" (p. 7). Because most test functions are cheap, both evaluation counts and timings are reported (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (a) The key observation: the results "are easy to establish after noting that all Hessian approximations Hk are obtained by updating a bounded matrix m times using the BFGS formula" (p. 21). (b) A trace bound from ‖y‖²/yᵀs ≤ M2 summed over at most m̃ updates (p. 23). (c) A determinant lower bound via the Pearson/Powell formula (p. 23). (d) Together these give cos θₖ ≥ δ, and with the Powell-type decrease inequality an R-linear rate for both f and x (pp. 23–24). (e) Scope of the result. It extends to other line searches, including backtracking, by adapting Byrd–Nocedal (p. 24). The γₖ scaling satisfies the theorem's conditions (p. 24). Shanno–Phua and Buckley–LeNir are special cases when m varies, "Therefore Theorem … applies also to these two methods" (p. 24). The authors say plainly that R-linear convergence is the best one can expect for L-BFGS, while Buckley–LeNir can be made n-step quadratically convergent (p. 24).
  - Transferable: for a memory-limited quasi-Newton method, bound the trace and the determinant uniformly. Every matrix is at most m updates away from a bounded seed, so no self-correction argument is needed (pp. 21–24).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The test set (table on p. 7) draws on Gill–Murray, Moré et al., Buckley–LeNir, Toint's partially separable set, Gilbert–Lemaréchal, and the authors' own matrix square-root problems (p. 7). n and the stopping tolerance are blank in the text (p. 7). The protocol:
  - **Competitors as their authors recommend**: the Buckley–LeNir code ran at its defaults, "precisely as they recommend" (p. 8); PQN used Toint's Harwell routine (pp. 6, 19); CONMIN and Polak–Ribière CG with restarts were also run (p. 16).
  - **Same answer checked**: every run was verified to converge to the same solution (p. 8).
  - **Two cost columns**: results are given as iterations/evaluations and iteration time/function time/total time (pp. 8, 16).
  - **Summaries**: tallies of "wins" (pp. 10, 13), histograms of the speedup NFUN(small m)/NFUN(large m) (pp. 10–11), rank tallies for the scalings by function calls and CPU time (p. 15), and very large problems on the Alliant FX/8 at Argonne (p. 15).
  - **Fair-accounting moves**: (i) Buckley–LeNir needed fewer iterations because of its more accurate line search, so L-BFGS was rerun with a forced interpolation before comparing CPU time (p. 12). (ii) PQN was run with and without variable reduction, because users "may not wish or may not be able to supply" the rule, and with two start matrices (p. 19). (iii) A dense-Hessian problem was kept to show PQN misapplied (p. 20).
  - **Full data elsewhere**: the complete results are in the companion report NAM 04 (p. 7).
  - **Losses printed**: PQN is "vastly superior" in function evaluations on two problems (p. 20), and "for large problems with inexpensive functions the simple CG method can still be considered among the best methods available to date" (p. 18).
  - **Unexplained result admitted**: storage gives only marginal gains on very large problems, "The reason for this is not clear to us" (p. 16).
  - Transferable: report iteration time and function time in separate columns. Readers with cheap and with expensive functions then each get the answer that fits them (pp. 6, 8).
  - Transferable: when the rival wins on a count, find the ingredient responsible (here the line-search accuracy), give it to your own method, and compare again (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: Limited-memory methods are placed between CG and quasi-Newton (p. 3). The lineage is Perry, Shanno, Buckley, Nazareth, Nocedal, Gill–Murray and Buckley–LeNir (p. 3). The critique of Buckley–LeNir comes with a mechanism ("In our view … for the following reason": restarts discard the m corrections, and the first BFGS-cycle iterations use little information, p. 11). The paper concedes the comparison's dependence on implementation. A newer Buckley code fixes the line search (p. 11). Gilbert–Lemaréchal, using it, found smaller differences than this paper reports (p. 4). The Griewank–Toint partitioned quasi-Newton method gets strong praise (p. 24).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract states the loss: PQN is "clearly superior" on some problems (p. 2). The conclusions are written as instructions to the user of the Harwell code: low storage and accurate line searches when the function is cheap, an inaccurate search when it is expensive (p. 18). Hedging is calibrated ("Our numerical experience appears to indicate", p. 15; "We feel that an explanation of this requires further research", p. 16). The paper bounds its own evidence: "all the comments and conclusions made in this paper are based on data presented here and in the accompanying report" (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is mainly empirical and algorithmic: a method-level benchmark, the dynamic scaling, user advice and a released code (pp. 4, 18). The theory is modest: R-linear on uniformly convex problems (p. 24). Stated limits: R-linear is the best possible (p. 24); the effect of m on very large problems is unexplained (p. 16); the Gill–Murray scaling was erratic and not reported (p. 15). The future direction is dynamic scaling, "perhaps this is one of the most important topics of future research in large scale optimization" (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1, evidence ✅: competitors run "precisely as they recommend" (p. 8), the same solution verified (p. 8), losses in the abstract (p. 2), full data in a companion report (p. 7). One variant ⚠: the alignment move is an upgrade of their own method (the line search, p. 12), not a handicap.
- Method 6, evidence ✅: the partitioned quasi-Newton method is recommended over the authors' own method when structure information is available (p. 24). L-BFGS is kept for when it is not, since it needs only function and gradient values (p. 24).
- Method 4, evidence ✅: the undecided line-search accuracy becomes two options in the code (p. 13, step 2).
- Method 5, variant ⚠: the convergence theorem does not discriminate. It covers Shanno–Phua and Buckley–LeNir equally (p. 24) and says the rival can have a better rate (p. 24). The discrimination is left to the experiments.
- Also Heuristic 5 (the cheaper of two equally effective scalings "should be preferred since it is less expensive to implement", p. 15), Heuristic 4 (Gill–Murray scaling tried and dropped, p. 15) and Heuristic 7 (Harwell code, p. 4).
- New-pattern candidates: transfer the classical theorem through an equivalence (p. 21; also S007 p. 7); trace–determinant bookkeeping (p. 23; also S020 pp. 7–10); cost-split reporting with a companion full-data report (pp. 6–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Run each rival at its authors' recommended defaults, check that all methods reach the same solution, and keep the full tables in a companion report (pp. 7–8).
- Test a structure-exploiting rival both with and without the extra user input it needs (here variable reduction), since real users may not supply it (p. 19).
- Include one problem that looks structured but is not (a dense Hessian), to show the cost of misapplying the structured method (p. 20).

**Quotes**:
- "Our aim is that the limited memory method resemble BFGS as much as possible, and we disregard quadratic termination properties, which are not very meaningful, in general, for large dimensional problems" (p. 6)

### S012 · Global convergence properties of conjugate gradient methods for optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 1992, DOI 10.1137/0802003)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–29 was read: HAL and INRIA covers, abstract (English and French), §§1–6 and references. The version read is INRIA Rapport de Recherche 1268, June 1990 (p. 3), not the SIOPT pagination. Formulas are partly garbled in the OCR, and Tables 2–3 (pp. 25–26) are largely unreadable. No numbers from them are used.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jean Charles Gilbert (INRIA Rocquencourt) (p. 3) · **Place in trajectory**: written while L-BFGS was being established. It reuses the Liu–Nocedal matrix square-root problems and the Gilbert–Lemaréchal test functions (p. 23). It complements the quasi-Newton theory of S020 with nonlinear CG theory. Nocedal is at Northwestern EECS, with DOE and NSF support (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a named paradox. Fletcher–Reeves is sometimes as efficient as PR and HS but often much slower, yet it has a clean global convergence theory (Zoutendijk with exact searches, Al-Baali with inexact ones) (p. 5). Polak–Ribière and Hestenes–Stiefel are preferred in practice, but Powell (1984) showed PR can cycle. Since Powell's steplength "would probably be accepted by any practical line search, it appears unlikely that a satisfactory global convergence result can be found" for PR and HS (p. 6). "This disconcerting state of affairs motivated the present study" (p. 6). Scope: methods without regular restarts, with practical line searches (abstract, p. 4; p. 5), and no rates (p. 6).
  - Transferable: when theory blesses the method practice rejects and cannot cover the method practice prefers, ask for the smallest modification of the preferred method that inherits the theory of the blessed one (pp. 6, 11).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: There are two families of globally convergent methods, both changing only βₖ and the line search (p. 24).
  - **Family 1 (§3)**: any βₖ with |βₖ| ≤ βₖ^FR converges under strong Wolfe with σ2 < 1/2 (Lemma 3.1, Theorem 3.2, pp. 10–11). This suggests the hybrid PR-FR (3.7). It clips PR to [−FR, FR] (p. 11), unlike Touati-Ahmed–Storey, and allows negative β (p. 11). Under the conditions that make FR stall, it sets β to PR, so that it "avoids the inefficiencies" of FR (p. 12).
  - **Family 2 (§4)**: β ≥ 0 together with "Property (*)" (β is bounded, and small when the previous step is small) converges, given the Zoutendijk condition and sufficient descent (Theorem 4.3, pp. 16, 18). PR and HS have Property (*) (p. 16), which gives Corollary 4.4 for PR+ = max{β^PR, 0}, Powell's 1985 suggestion (p. 20). Sufficient descent is enforced by the line search: if the directional derivative is positive at the strong-Wolfe point, a minimizer has been bracketed, and the search continues until (4.1) holds (pp. 14, 24).
  - PR+ is read as "an automatic restarting procedure", less restrictive than Powell's restart test (p. 20).
  - Transferable: name the property that makes the good method behave well (Property (*)), prove the theorem for every method with that property, then check which named methods have it (pp. 16–17).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Bounded level set, and f continuously differentiable with Lipschitz gradient in a neighbourhood of it (Assumptions 2.1, pp. 6–7). No convexity. Line searches: Wolfe (2.4)–(2.5), strong Wolfe (2.16)–(2.17) with σ2 < 1/2 for the FR family, or an "ideal" first-stationary-point search (2.6) (pp. 7–9). §4 assumes sufficient descent (4.1) and iterates staying in the level set, without fixing a line search (p. 14). All results are lim inf ‖gₖ‖ = 0 (pp. 11, 18).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - The Zoutendijk condition Σcos²θₖ‖gₖ‖² < ∞ (Theorem 2.1, p. 7) is the hub. §2 shows that the earlier analyses share one template: if the gradients stay away from zero, show ‖dₖ‖² grows at most linearly, which contradicts (2.14) (p. 8).
  - Lemma 3.1 bounds ⟨g, d⟩/‖g‖² by induction, with geometric sums of σ2 (p. 10).
  - Proposition 3.3 (a sharpness result) shows that |β| ≤ c·β^FR with c > 1 is not enough. It recycles Powell's (1984) fourth example and checks that PR's β there satisfies the relaxed bound (pp. 12–13).
  - Lemma 4.1 shows Σ‖uₖ − uₖ₋₁‖² < ∞, so the directions change slowly (pp. 15–16).
  - Lemma 4.2 is a counting argument over windows of Δ consecutive iterations: a fraction of the steps cannot be small (pp. 17–18).
  - Theorem 4.3 contradicts the bounded level set (pp. 18–20).
  - §5 gives two constructions showing that the admissible intervals cannot be combined. One is a modified Powell example (pp. 20–21); the other uses [−1, 1] with exact searches against [0, ∞) (p. 21).
  - Proposition 5.1 builds a C∞ strongly convex 2-D function on which PR with exact searches gives β^PR < −β^FR < 0. It takes an explicit quadratic run and adds a small bump inside a ball that the segment between the first two iterates misses (pp. 21–22). This shows that the 1969 Polak–Ribière result is not a consequence of either theorem (p. 22).
  - Transferable: show a sufficient condition is sharp by reusing a published counterexample and checking that it satisfies your relaxed condition (pp. 12–13).
  - Transferable: build a counterexample by running the method on an explicit quadratic, then perturbing the function only inside a ball that the computed path avoids, so the early iterates are unchanged (p. 22).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: §6 (pp. 23–27):
  - **Test set**: the Table 1 problems (p. 23), all with at least 100 variables, "Since conjugate gradient methods are mainly useful for large problems" (p. 24). Sizes go up to 10000 (p. 23).
  - **Methods**: four, FR, PR-FR, PR and PR+, differing only in the choice of βₖ and possibly in the line search (p. 24).
  - **Line search**: Moré–Thuente with σ2 = 0.1, chosen so that Theorem 3.2 covers FR and PR-FR (p. 24). It was extended for PR and PR+ to enforce (4.1), with at least two function evaluations per search and the Shanno–Phua initial trial step (p. 24). The σ1, σ3 and stopping-test exponents are garbled in the OCR (p. 24).
  - **Hygiene**: a problem was dropped unless all methods reached the same solution (p. 24). A "mod" column counts the iterations in which β was modified (p. 25). Runs stop at 9999 evaluations, and line-search failures are marked (p. 25).
  - **Findings**: β was constrained in most iterations of PR-FR but rarely modified in PR+ (p. 25). "The performance of methods PR-FR, PR and PR* is comparable. Overall, PR* appears to be better than PR. The FR method is clearly the least efficient" (p. 25). With restarts every n, FR improves but stays last, and the others do not change, so those results are not shown (pp. 25, 27).
  - **Mechanism check**: on problem 45 (n = 100), cos θ stays small and roughly constant for hundreds of iterations while the steps are tiny. A steepest-descent restart ends the cycle, as §3 predicted (p. 27).
  - Transferable: report how often the safeguard actually fires (the "mod" column), so readers see whether the convergent modification changes the method in practice (p. 25).
  - Transferable: set the line-search constants to the values the theorem needs (σ2 = 0.1 < 1/2), so the code you test is the algorithm you analysed (p. 24).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper recasts Polak–Ribière (1969), Zoutendijk (1970), Powell (1984) and Al-Baali (1985) as one shared approach (p. 6). It is explicit about its debts and its margin: "very based upon the work of Al-Baali (1985) … and are slightly stronger than those given by Touati-Ahmed and Storey (1990)" (p. 9). PR+ is credited to Powell (1985) (pp. 9, 20). Powell's 1984 paper is called remarkably laborious, as a compliment (p. 5). Powell's restart criterion is reinterpreted twice (pp. 13, 20).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The motivation is a paradox, stated in one sentence (p. 6). Scope exclusions come up front ("this paper does not study the rate of convergence", p. 6). The proof idea comes in plain words before the lemmas: "The idea of our analysis is simple, but is somewhat concealed in the proofs", followed by a three-step roadmap (p. 14). Negative results sit beside positive ones (Prop. 3.3; §5), and the paper admits it cannot generalize the results of §§3 and 4 (p. 21). The bilingual abstract follows INRIA style (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory (two convergent families and their sharpness) plus algorithms (PR-FR, and PR+ with a sufficient-descent line search) and a numerical check (pp. 11, 20, 25). Limits: lim inf only, with no rates (p. 6); the |β| ≤ c·β^FR case is open, and only descent is shown for σ2 < 1/(2c) (p. 12); the families cannot be merged (pp. 20–21); the strongly convex PR result of 1969 is not recovered (p. 22). No future directions are stated beyond these open points.

**Method links**:
- Method 5, evidence ✅ (strong): practical line searches and no restarts, i.e. the method as run (abstract, p. 4; p. 5). The theory explains why FR stalls and PR recovers, and the run confirms it (pp. 11–12, 27). The parameters in the experiments are set to match the theorem (p. 24). The unprovable cases are kept as stated limits (pp. 12, 21).
- Method 3, variant ⚠: the practitioners' engine (PR) is kept, and only β plus the line search's descent test are changed (pp. 11, 20, 24). PR-FR "falls back on the FR method" as a safeguard (p. 12). The repair comes from a theorem, not from an explicit estimate.
- Method 1, evidence ✅ (step 5): the methods are compared inside one code and line search, differing only in β (p. 24), with the same-solution check (p. 24).
- New-pattern candidates: family-level theorem that locates the boundary member (pp. 11–13, 16, 20–22; also S020); sharpness by recycled counterexample (pp. 12–13, 20–21); safeguard-firing counts in result tables (p. 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Organize a convergence paper around one hub condition (Zoutendijk) and one template (‖dₖ‖² grows at most linearly → contradiction), then vary only what each method contributes (pp. 7–8).
- Enforce sufficient descent for nonnegative-β CG methods with a line search: when the strong-Wolfe point has a positive directional derivative, keep bracketing until (4.1) holds (pp. 14, 24).
- Read a restart rule as a β-truncation (or the reverse) to compare safeguards on one scale (pp. 13, 20).
