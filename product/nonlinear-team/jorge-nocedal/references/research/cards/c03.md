### S049 · Large scale unconstrained optimization (The State of the Art in Numerical Analysis, IMA Conference Series 63, OUP 1997, pp. 311–338; doi:10.1093/oso/9780198500148.003.0012)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1–20 (abstract, §1 structure, §2 Newton and Hessian-free Newton, §3 quasi-Newton and limited memory, §4 nonlinear CG, acknowledgments) in the June 23, 1996 Northwestern EECS report; bibliography pp. 20–24 skimmed to identify refs [30], [53], [65]. Table 3 (p. 16) is scrambled in both text layers; its per-problem numbers were not read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: none. The text names Jorge Nocedal as sole author (p. 1); the second author given in the work list (S. J. Wright) is not borne out. Thanks go to Marcelo Marazzi and Guanghui Liu (p. 20) · **Place in the trajectory**: a mid-1990s survey between the Acta Numerica theory survey of 1992 (cited as ref. [53] for small and medium problems, p. 2) and the constrained interior-point work of S008. Its L-BFGS and L-BFGS-B tables come from his own group's L-BFGS-B report (ref. [65], Zhu, Byrd, Lu & Nocedal, cited on pp. 14, 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The survey is restricted to large n (n > 500 is the working assumption, p. 2) and organised by the three algorithm classes Newton, quasi-Newton and CG (p. 2). It starts with problem structure "because understanding the characteristics of the objective function is crucial in large scale optimization" (p. 2; the text layer splits "ob jective"). The trigger is new technology: automatic differentiation and automatic detection of partially separable structure, which "may also popularize partially separable quasiNewton methods" (p. 2; abstract p. 1). Small-scale methods are excluded explicitly, with a pointer to existing reviews (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open a survey with a section on problem structure, before the algorithms. Name the enabling technology (here AD) that is changing the trade-offs, and say which part of the field you leave out and where it is reviewed.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The thesis is that the right large-scale method depends on exploitable structure. Partial separability gives a compact representation through internal variables U_i and internal functions (minimum-surface example, pp. 3–4). It also gives cheap AD, either through a compressed sparse Jacobian of the element functions (p. 6) or through small element Hessians from Hessian-vector products (pp. 6–7). And it gives element-by-element quasi-Newton updating (pp. 13–14). Partial separability is strictly more general than sparsity: an element function with a dense Hessian is a function of one variable, and protein folding is partially separable with a dense Hessian (p. 5). Group partial separability extends this to least squares and penalty functions, and LANCELOT exploits it (pp. 5–6). Newton's method, as a trust region with truncated CG (Algorithm I, three stopping tests, pp. 7–9), is "the most powerful algorithm" and "the most reliable method for solving large problems" (p. 7). Limited memory is for problems without structure (p. 13). The survey also audits two implementations. LANCELOT stops its preconditioned CG when ‖p_j‖ leaves the trust region, but with preconditioning the quantity that grows monotonically is ‖Dp_j‖, so LANCELOT may stop too early. Minpack-2's TRCG treats preconditioning as a scaling of the trust region, so an iterate that leaves never returns (pp. 9–10).
  - Transferable: when a stopping rule is justified by a monotonicity property (here the growth of the CG iterates' norm), check that the property still holds after preconditioning or a change of variables, i.e. in the norm the code actually tests (pp. 9–10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is smooth with the gradient available; the Hessian may be analytic, sparse finite differences or AD (p. 7). The CG convergence results assume a bounded level set, a Lipschitz gradient on a neighbourhood and no restarts (p. 19). Partially separable quasi-Newton updating cannot rely on BFGS, because the curvature condition may fail element by element even when the total step satisfies it, and an element Hessian may be concave. LANCELOT therefore starts with BFGS and switches to SR1 when an element's curvature condition fails (pp. 13–14). Sparse quasi-Newton matrices are not guaranteed to be positive definite (p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: This is a survey, so nothing is proved here; the devices are reported with their role. (a) With p₀ = 0, the CG iterates increase monotonically in norm, so it is safe to stop at the trust-region boundary (p. 9). (b) The first CG iterate is the Cauchy step, which gives global convergence (p. 9). (c) A residual test relative to ‖g_k‖ tightens automatically and controls the rate of convergence (pp. 8–9, 11). (d) The compact representation H_k = H₀ + [S_k H₀Y_k]·(2m×2m inner matrix)·[S_kᵀ; Y_kᵀH₀] costs the same as the two-loop form in the unconstrained case, but pays off for products H_kA with sparse A and for bounds (pp. 15–16). (e) The sparse quasi-Newton update solves min ‖BS_k − Y_k‖_F subject to symmetry and the sparsity pattern; it is unique under a linear-independence assumption, the analysis gives the minimum number of pairs, and 2 pairs suffice for an arrowhead matrix (pp. 17–18). (f) For nonlinear CG, Polak–Ribière (PR) may fail under an exact first-stationary-point line search. β_k = max{β_k^PR, 0} with a modified strong Wolfe search converges, and so does any |β_k| ≤ β_k^FR. That bound is tight: some c > 1 with |β_k| ≤ cβ_k^FR admits a failure example (pp. 19–20). Item (f) includes his own Gilbert & Nocedal results (ref. [30]).
  - Transferable: when you survey convergence results, report for each one whether it changed the practical method (p. 20), not only what it proves.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There are three small tables, each a handful of chosen problems; there is no aggregate. Table 1 (p. 10) gives Bouaricha and Moré's preconditioned Newton on Minpack-2 GL2 and MSA at n = 2500, 10,000 and 40,000. Iterations stay at 6–10, which the author calls "very good"; he adds that the limited-memory code has considerable difficulty on these problems as n grows, and he hedges on typicality (p. 10). Table 2 (p. 15) compares L-BFGS (m = 5) with LANCELOT's Newton (defaults) on four CUTE problems, with nfg and time on a Sparcstation-2. The problems are chosen because they "typify situations we have observed in practice" (p. 15). CRAGGLVY: 95 vs 15 nfg, similar time (13 vs 10). FMINSURF is labelled "highly atypical", since L-BFGS needs fewer evaluations (186 vs 316). DIXMAANI is ill-conditioned: 1237 vs 8 nfg. EIGENCLS: LANCELOT needs fewer evaluations (543 vs 2900) but more time (2300 vs 563) (p. 15). Three conclusions follow (p. 15): the limited-memory matrix is of poor quality; its iteration is cheap enough that it may still win on time; and it is less reliable, with m = 20 or 30 not always a cure. Table 3 (p. 16) compares L-BFGS-B with LANCELOT on four bound-constrained CUTE problems, again with nfg and time and with the number of active bounds. The text says LANCELOT needs far fewer evaluations, L-BFGS-B does well on time, and L-BFGS-B fails on a few CUTE bound problems (p. 17). Other results are reported only qualitatively: Iterated Subspace Minimization shows "some promise" but no clear improvement over the standard Newton method (p. 11); the Discrete Newton Method with Memory was tested only in a controlled setting (p. 17); preliminary tests of sparse quasi-Newton give fewer iterations than L-BFGS but at too high a cost per iteration (p. 18); and the globally convergent PR variants give only marginal improvement (pp. 19–20).
  - Transferable: for a comparison table, pick a few problems that each typify one regime you have observed: the common case, the atypical win (labelled atypical), the ill-conditioned loss, and few evaluations at a high cost. Report both evaluations and time (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning in the literature**: Rivals are credited generously. LANCELOT's built-in suite of preconditioners is an "excellent feature" (p. 9). Its partially separable quasi-Newton method is judged nearly as effective as Newton's method on CUTE (p. 14). His own limited-memory methods "are not as robust and as rapidly convergent as partially separable quasiNewton methods but are probably much more widely used" (p. 13). Criticism is technical and specific: the LANCELOT termination inconsistency (p. 9); and the observation that sparse updates were explored and abandoned in the late 1970s, then resurrected (p. 17). He includes his own CG theory among the results that "have not lead to signicant practical advances" (p. 20; ref. [30] is Gilbert & Nocedal). His group's Discrete Newton Method with Memory is presented as tested only in a controlled setting, with extensive tests not yet performed (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The survey speaks in the first person singular: "I will not discuss" (p. 2), "I do not know however if this kind of performance is typical" (p. 10), "I do not know if signicant improvements in performance can be realized" (p. 12), "to the best of my knowledge" (p. 12). Hedged forecasts: "It is dicult to predict what the relative importance of these concepts will be ten years from now" (p. 7). Each algorithm class ends with a "Current Research and Open Questions" subsection (pp. 11–12, 17–18), and "deserves some investigation" / "deserves careful investigation" mark untested comparisons (pp. 10, 12). Concepts are explained through one worked example, the minimum-surface element function, with its sparsity, invariant subspace and internal variables (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable: end each section of a survey with an open-questions list that names the specific comparison nobody has run, e.g. residual, model-reduction and angle stopping tests for inner CG: "no systematic comparison of these alternatives has been undertaken" (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limitations**: The contribution is a map of the field, not theory or software. Its open-question register: the inner-CG stopping test, including the effect of a recurred rather than computed residual (p. 11); Lanczos continuation past negative curvature (pp. 11–12); dynamic preconditioning, not studied "to the best of my knowledge" (p. 12); general-purpose preconditioners for Hessian-free Newton (p. 12); the choice of subspace in Iterated Subspace Minimization (p. 11); storage-halving limited-memory methods (p. 17); whether sparse quasi-Newton can be competitive, perhaps inside KKT systems (p. 18); and scaling of nonlinear CG directions (p. 19). The limits are not stated as such. The numbers come from others' or his group's earlier reports, with four problems per table and no aggregate (pp. 10, 15–16).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the survey is organised around structure (pp. 2–7). Limited memory is placed where no structure exists (p. 13). His own L-BFGS is shown losing to Newton and to partially separable quasi-Newton on reliability and evaluations (pp. 13–15).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: convergence theory is judged by whether it changed practice. Globally convergent PR variants give "only a marginal improvement" (p. 19), and the CG theory, his own included, "have not lead to signicant practical advances" (p. 20).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (step 1: write the undecided choices down): the "Current Research and Open Questions" subsections list untested design choices of the Newton–CG and limited-memory codes (pp. 11–12, 17–18). That later papers answer them is not shown here.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the comparisons use the rival's code at "all its default settings" with L-BFGS at m = 5 (p. 15), but there is no stopping-test alignment or handicap. The tables are illustrative problems without an aggregate (pp. 15–16), which is what Method 1 step 7 flags.
- New-pattern candidate: **monotone CG-iterate norm as a design invariant**. It is used to justify stopping at the trust-region boundary, and to find an implementation inconsistency under preconditioning (pp. 9–10); see also S008 p. 15.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Choose four problems that each typify one observed regime, label the atypical one, and report evaluations and time together (p. 15).
- Check that the norm in which a CG trust-region iteration grows monotonically is the norm the code tests; with preconditioner D it is ‖Dp‖, not ‖p‖ (pp. 9–10).
- Use the compact (2m×2m) limited-memory representation when the method needs products H_kA with sparse A, or bounds, where the two-loop recursion gives no access (p. 16).
- End each survey section with the concrete comparisons nobody has run (p. 11).

**Quotes**:

### S024 · Combining trust region and line search techniques (Advances in Nonlinear Programming, Kluwer 1998, pp. 153–175; doi:10.1007/978-1-4613-3335-7_7)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1–23 (abstract, §1 introduction, §2 subproblem lemmas and Algorithm 2.6, §3 Algorithm 3.1 and convergence theory, §4 numerical results, §5 final remarks) in Report OTC 98/04 (March 14, 1998); references pp. 23–25 skimmed. Table II (p. 22) is partly scrambled in the layout (the BFGS entries of problems 7 and 11 are displaced).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Ya-xiang Yuan (Computing Center, Academia Sinica, p. 1). Thanks go to Jorge Moré for GQTPAR and to Richard Byrd and Nick Gould (p. 23) · **Place in the trajectory**: the third Nocedal–Yuan work (after quasi-Newton convergence 1987, cited on p. 19, and self-scaling quasi-Newton 1993, per 01-publications). It is an early line-search / trust-region hybrid for unconstrained problems; the text does not claim any link to the later Knitro-Direct hybrid.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is cost. A trust-region method rejects an unsuccessful step and re-solves the subproblem, which for large n means one or more factorizations of (B_k + λI). That is "quite adequate for small problems", while a line search needs very little computation per trial point (p. 2). The question: how to incorporate backtracking in a trust-region method "so as to avoid resolving the subproblem" when a step fails, without weakening convergence (p. 2). Two problematic cases are analysed before any design (p. 3). (i) A direction nearly orthogonal to −g, where a line search may fail through rounding but a shrinking trust region tends to steepest descent. That robustness to "noise and rounding errors" (citing Carter) "should be preserved". (ii) An excessively long step from a very small B_k, where shrinking the trust region only mimics backtracking at a much higher cost. The conclusion: backtrack only when the direction is sufficiently downhill (p. 3).
  - Transferable: before hybridising two globalization mechanisms, write down the failure case each one handles well and require the hybrid to keep both (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The trust-region step is computed so that it is always a direction of sufficient descent. Its angle with −g_k stays bounded away from π/2 while ‖g_k‖ is bounded away from zero and ‖d_k‖, ‖B_k‖ are bounded (p. 3; (3.1)–(3.2), p. 11). If f increases, the method backtracks from the failed point, along the straight line or along any curved path of trial steps whose lengths decrease and whose angle with −g_k is no worse than d_k's ((3.4)–(3.5), p. 11). The radius is then reduced ((3.8), p. 12). Algorithm 2.6 (p. 10) solves the subproblem inexactly. It picks λ between 0 and ‖B‖ + (1 + ε)‖g‖/Δ (ε > 0 small, (2.33)) with B + λI positive definite, factorizes B + λI = RᵀR, and updates λ by a Newton step on equation (2.40) instead of Moré–Sorensen's (2.41). Any ‖d(λ)‖ in [Δ/τ, Δ] is accepted (τ > 1; (2.30), pp. 8–9, where the symbols are lost in both text layers), so the solver "always solves positive denite systems" and avoids the so-called hard case (pp. 9–10, 23). What is unchanged: the ratio test and radius update of Step 4 ((3.10)–(3.11), p. 12), and B_k, which may be exact Hessians or quasi-Newton approximations (p. 12). Toint had combined line searches with trust regions at every iteration; here backtracking happens only after a failed trial point (p. 3).
  - Transferable: make the inexact subproblem solver certify the property that the cheap fallback needs (here sufficient descent, which makes backtracking safe), rather than redesigning the globalization (pp. 3, 9–11).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumptions 3.2 (p. 13): the iterates stay in a closed, bounded, convex set S, and f is twice differentiable on S with ‖∇²f‖ ≤ M. B_k needs only Σ 1/M_k = ∞ with M_k = max_{i≤k} ‖B_i‖ ((3.19), p. 13; (3.43), p. 17), so ‖B_k‖ may grow linearly (p. 19). The Newton corollary assumes B_k = ∇²f(x_k), with the Newton step taken whenever B_k is positive definite and the step lies inside the trust region (p. 18). In the hard case a boundary solution is chosen, following Moré and Sorensen (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The lemmas build up to Theorem 3.7. Lemma 2.1 is the classical characterization of trust-region solutions, plus a generalized-inverse closed form (pp. 4–5). Lemma 2.2 is Powell's Cauchy-decrease bound (p. 5). Lemmas 2.3–2.4 bound the descent of the exact step, d*ᵀg ≤ −½‖g‖ min{Δ, ‖g‖/(2‖B‖)} ((2.20), pp. 5–7). Lemma 2.5 shows by three cases, the hard case among them, that the cosine of the angle between d*(Δ) and −g is monotone in Δ, so exact solutions at smaller radii are admissible curved backtracking points (pp. 7–8, 11). The inexact d(λ) is the exact solution for radius ‖d(λ)‖ and inherits decrease and descent ((2.31)–(2.32), p. 9). An alternative condition (2.33) on λ removes the lower bound on the step size, since "a Newton step could be shorter" (pp. 9–10). Global convergence is proved by contradiction from ‖g_k‖ ≥ ε. The model decrease is summed over successful iterations (p. 13), giving lim min{Δ_k, 1/M_k} = 0 (pp. 13–14). Lemmas 3.3–3.5 then bound Δ_k from below by a constant times 1/M_k, for ratio failures and for backtracking separately (pp. 14–17). Powell's lemma on sequences (Lemma 3.6, p. 17) closes the argument: liminf ‖g_k‖ = 0 under Σ 1/M_k = ∞ (Theorem 3.7, pp. 17–18). Corollary 3.8 gives, for Newton's method, lim ‖g_k‖ = 0 for convex f, a positive semidefinite Hessian at a limit point and a quadratic rate (pp. 18–19; "similar to" Fletcher's theorems, p. 19). The authors contrast this with line-search quasi-Newton theory, which needs control of the condition number or a trace–determinant analysis (Powell 1976; Byrd, Nocedal & Yuan 1987), "which is technically dicult" (p. 19).
  - Transferable: to prove a hybrid convergent, reduce every new kind of step to the two inequalities the old framework needs (Cauchy-type model decrease plus sufficient descent, (3.1)–(3.2), p. 11), then reuse Powell's sequence lemma with M_k = max_{i≤k} ‖B_i‖ (pp. 14–17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The test set is the 18 Moré–Garbow–Hillstrom problems, n = 2 to 20 (p. 20), with four arms in one Fortran code base (pp. 19–20): BFGS with a Wolfe line search (constants 0.01 and 0.9); a pure trust region with BFGS, skipping the update when sᵀy ≤ 0; TR+BT with BFGS; and TR+BT with SR1, applied only when |sᵀy| ≥ 10⁻⁶‖s‖‖y‖. Backtracking uses truncated quadratic interpolation, α = max{0.1, 0.5/(1 + (f(x_k) − f(x_k + d_k))/d_kᵀg_k)} (p. 20). All arms share Δ₁ = 1, B₁ = I and the stopping test ‖g_k‖ ≤ 10⁻⁸ (Sun Sparcstation 1, p. 20). Iterations and function and gradient evaluations are counted per arm, because the arms differ in evaluations per iteration (p. 20). Results (Table I, p. 21; text p. 21): the pure trust region beats the line search in function evaluations, and BFGS beats SR1 inside TR+BT. TR+BT and the pure trust region need a similar number of evaluations (the pure trust region needs slightly fewer, but the difference "is not signicant"), but TR+BT needs fewer iterations "in most of the problems". Card writer's sums from Table I: 986 function evaluations for the pure trust region, 999 for TR+BT BFGS and 1125 for line-search BFGS. TR+BT BFGS needs fewer iterations than the pure trust region on 13 of 18 problems, as many on 3 and more on 2 (problems 5 and 8). SR1 exceeds 300 iterations on problems 4 and 12. A second test on medium-size problems from Liu & Nocedal 1989 (n = 50–121, Table II, p. 22) reverses the update ranking: "Surprisingly SR now clearly outperforms BFGS" (p. 22). No large-scale test is run, although large n is the motivation (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: when the first table favours one variant, test a second problem family before concluding, and print the reversal together with "we are unable to conclude" (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning in the literature**: The analysis builds on Fletcher, Gay, Moré, Moré–Sorensen, Powell, Sorensen and Eisenstat–Walker (p. 3). Toint's every-iteration line search is the nearest prior work (p. 3). The final remarks set two alternatives side by side: dogleg, which needs B_k positive definite and is slow when B_k is ill-conditioned, and the Schultz–Schnabel–Byrd restricted-subspace method. The latter is conceded to have "the same theoretical properties", and the claimed edge is practical only: backtracking may be more effective when the trial step is very poor (pp. 22–23). The novelty claims are Algorithm 2.6, which avoids the hard case, and a step "suciently steep to allow for a safe backtracking line search", which "has not received much attention in the literature" (p. 23).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper opens with cost, follows with two concrete problematic cases and only then the design (pp. 2–3). The convergence claim is comparative, "as strong as the results obtained previously for pure trust region methods" (p. 19). Large-scale promise is hedged and deferred: it may be effective "for solving large problems but we will not discuss this here" (p. 21). The reversal is marked "Surprisingly" (p. 22). The paper is lemma-heavy, with short motivating paragraphs between lemmas (pp. 4–19).
- **D8 Contribution and limitations**: The contribution is an algorithm plus global and local convergence theory (Theorem 3.7, Corollary 3.8). The evidence is numerical on small and medium problems only. The stated limits are specific: an efficient large-scale implementation is left as the subject of future research (pp. 21–22); the update formula is undecided (p. 22); and a competing method has the same theory (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the same move of keeping the engine and changing one component. The ratio test, radius update and choice of B_k are untouched, and only the response to a failed trial step changes, from re-solving to backtracking (pp. 2, 11–12). The trigger is cost, not a component that breaks in a new regime, and there is no estimate or recovery tree. The robustness of the trust region to noise and rounding is kept on purpose (p. 3).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the analysed algorithm is the inexact one actually coded (Algorithm 2.6 inside Algorithm 3.1, pp. 10–12). The theorem is placed against what line-search quasi-Newton theory needs: unbounded ‖B_k‖ with at most linear growth, against condition-number or trace–determinant control (p. 19).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (step 5, controlling implementation effects): all four arms run in one code with the same B₁, Δ₁, stopping test and curvature safeguards. Evaluations are counted per arm according to what each arm actually evaluates (pp. 19–20).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (step 5, recording surprise): the BFGS vs SR1 reversal is printed as a surprise, with an explicit non-conclusion (p. 22), but no received wisdom is put on trial.
- New-pattern candidate: **line-search / trust-region hybrid with a cheap fallback**. The trust-region step is computed as the primary step, and cheap backtracking is used only after it fails, under a condition that keeps the trust-region theory (pp. 3, 11–12).
- New-pattern candidate: **property-certifying subproblem solver**. The inexact solver is designed to deliver exactly the inequalities the global analysis and the fallback need (pp. 9–11); compare S008's vertical step kept inside a fraction of the radius and its penalty rule making pred proportional to the vertical decrease (S008 pp. 10, 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove that the angle between the exact trust-region step and −g is monotone in Δ (Lemma 2.5, pp. 7–8). Exact solutions at smaller radii then become admissible curved backtracking points (p. 11).
- Use Powell's sequence lemma (Lemma 3.6, p. 17) with M_k = max_{i≤k} ‖B_i‖ to get liminf ‖g_k‖ = 0 for quasi-Newton matrices whose norms grow at most linearly (pp. 17–19).
- Update λ by a Newton step that accepts any ‖d(λ)‖ in [Δ/τ, Δ]. Only positive definite factorizations are needed, and the hard case never arises (pp. 10, 23).
- In a comparison of globalization variants, report iterations and function and gradient evaluations separately for each arm, since evaluations per iteration differ (p. 20).

**Quotes**:
- "Note that we did not need to assume that the matrices … are bounded, but only that their norms increase at most linearly" (p. 19)
- "Surprisingly SR1 now clearly outperforms BFGS, and we are unable to conclude that one updating formula is preferable to the other one" (p. 22; the text layer drops the "1" of SR1)

### S008 · An interior point algorithm for large-scale nonlinear programming (SIAM Journal on Optimization 9(4), 877–900, 1999; doi:10.1137/S1052623497325107)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1–24 (abstract, §1, §2 barrier/SQP framework, §3 subproblem formulation, multipliers, trust region, vertical and horizontal steps, merit function and SOC, linear algebra, complete NITRO algorithm, §4 numerical tests with Tables 1–5, §5 final remarks) in the preprint of July 27, 1997; references pp. 25–28 skimmed. In Table 5 (p. 24) some NITRO cells are blank in both text layers (HS28, HS51, HS52, HS53); those entries were not read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Mary E. Hribar (Rice CAAM; her Northwestern PhD thesis is ref. [27], p. 27), Jorge Nocedal (Northwestern ECE) (p. 1). Thanks go to Guanghui Liu (p. 23) · **Place in the trajectory**: the NITRO paper, the interior / trust-region line that 01-publications SW3 traces to KNITRO (the link is not in this text). It implements the Byrd–Gilbert–Nocedal framework, ref. [8] (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The target is min f(x) subject to h(x) = 0 and g(x) ≤ 0 (p. 2, as typeset (1.1)), possibly nonconvex, with n large and first and second derivatives available; quasi-Newton is mentioned as a possible extension (p. 2). The gap (pp. 2–3): most interior methods for NLP are line-search methods. The convex ones extend LP methods. The local analyses "do not make provisions for the case when the problems is nonconvex". The nonconvex line-search methods modify the KKT system through a factorization, and they are too recent for it to be clear "whether they will lead to robust general purpose codes". Trust-region interior methods exist, but Plantenga's differs in how it treats the trust region, in its purely primal step and in reverting to an active-set method near the solution (p. 3). The entry is to embed SQP and trust regions in a barrier method so that convex and nonconvex problems are treated uniformly, second derivatives are used directly and nearly dependent constraint gradients are safeguarded (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: NITRO is a barrier method whose subproblems are solved approximately by the Byrd–Omojokun SQP trust-region method, as implemented for large equality-constrained problems by Lalee, Nocedal and Plantenga (pp. 3–4). μ and the tolerance ε are cut by a constant factor (p. 4). The diagnosis is that "a straightforward application of this SQP method to the barrier problem leads to inecient primal steps" that violate slack positivity and are cut short by the trust region (p. 5). So what changes is the quadratic model in the slacks and the shape of the trust region (p. 5). The slack Hessian Σ_k is μS⁻² (primal), or S⁻¹Λ (primal-dual, a Newton step on the perturbed KKT system) (pp. 7–8). In the primal-dual version a negative multiplier makes that entry fall back to μ/s², keeping the model convex in the slacks instead of forcing the multipliers positive. The previous μ is used at the start of a new barrier problem to avoid an abrupt change (p. 9). Multipliers are least-squares estimates in a "subordinate role", so the method "could just barely be considered a primaldual method" (p. 9). The trust region is ‖(d_x, S⁻¹d_s)‖ ≤ Δ plus the fraction-to-boundary rule d_s ≥ −τs with τ = 0.995 (pp. 9–10); a box-shaped ℓ∞ region was tried and set aside (p. 10). The step splits into a vertical step and a horizontal step (pp. 10–15). The vertical step is a dogleg between the Cauchy point and the minimum-norm Newton step, inside ξΔ with ξ = 0.8, or else a truncated Newton step, whichever lowers the model more. The horizontal step is a projected CG with Steihaug stopping tests, at most 2(n − t) iterations (p. 16). Its preconditioner is the projection onto the null space of Âᵀ, so CG behaves as with an orthonormal null-space basis (p. 14). The merit function is f − μΣ ln s + ν‖(h, g + s)‖₂ (p. 6). ν is raised so that pred ≥ ρν·(vertical model decrease) with ρ = 0.3, monotone for fixed μ (pp. 16–17). A step is accepted when ared ≥ η·pred with η = 10⁻⁸ (p. 17). A second-order correction is applied only when ‖v‖ ≤ 0.1‖w‖ (pp. 17–18). One augmented-system factorization (MA27) serves the multipliers, the vertical Newton step and the projections (p. 18).
  - Transferable: lay out each iteration's linear algebra so that all its systems share one factorization (here one augmented matrix for the multipliers, the Newton component and the projections), and prefer the augmented system to the normal equations for accuracy (p. 18).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, h and g are smooth, with first and second derivatives (p. 2); convexity is not assumed. No convergence theorem is stated or proved here. Global convergence is inherited from the Byrd–Gilbert–Nocedal model algorithm, with an admitted mismatch: "The trust region does not precisely match with the model algorithm analyzed by Byrd Gilbert and Nocedal but it is not dicult to extend that analysis to our case" (p. 10). The rate is linear by design, and the control of μ and ε for fast convergence is deferred "to a future article" (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: These are design arguments, not theorems. (a) With Σ_k = μS⁻² the subproblem's KKT system is a Newton step on the barrier KKT conditions; with S⁻¹Λ it is Newton on the perturbed KKT system, whose second derivatives stay bounded as the slacks go to zero (pp. 7–8). (b) The CG iterates' norm increases monotonically, so the loop tests only the trust-region norm and checks the slack bounds afterwards, taking the last feasible intersection (pp. 15–16). Infeasible CG steps were about 2% of the total (p. 16). (c) Projection preconditioning gives the behaviour of an orthonormal null-space basis; the ill-conditioning as μ → 0 forms "one tight cluster of small eigenvalues" and so does not hurt CG (p. 14). (d) The penalty rule makes pred positive and proportional to √(−m(v)). If m(v) = 0 then v = 0 and q(v + w) ≤ 0, so ν can be kept. A monotone ν for fixed μ is "an important property in establishing global convergence" (pp. 16–17). (e) By essentially the same argument as in ref. [8], (3.53) holds for a small enough radius (p. 17). This argument is cited, not reproduced.
  - Transferable: when a CG-based trust-region step must also respect simple bounds, test only the norm constraint inside the loop, since the norm grows monotonically, and handle the bounds by a final truncation (pp. 15–16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The main set is 14 CUTE problems chosen "for its variety" (p. 20, Table 1): negative curvature (OPTMASS), ill-conditioned constraint Jacobians (HAGER4), bounds only (OBSTCLAE, TORSION1), highly nonlinear equalities, and size (n up to 2001, m up to 1273). It is kept "small enough" to know each problem and run (p. 20). The comparator is LANCELOT with second derivatives at all default settings. Stopping is aligned: NITRO at E ≤ 10⁻⁷, and LANCELOT at projected gradient and constraint violation < 10⁻⁷, "very similar" (p. 20). The limit is 10,000 iterations, on a Sparcstation 20, with f evals, CG iterations and CPU seconds reported (pp. 20–21). Table 2 (p. 21) shows NITRO with fewer function evaluations on 9 of 14 problems; LANCELOT needs fewer on GAUSSELM, HAGER4, OBSTCLAE, OPTCNTRL and TORSION1, and fails on OPTMASS. CPU time splits 7–7, and NITRO loses heavily on SVANBERG (2704.77 vs 120.96 s) and OBSTCLAE (543.22 vs 12.98 s). The paragraph calls the results "highly encouraging particularly the number of function evaluations" (p. 21) and does not discuss these time losses there. The only related remark is the conservative iterative refinement, which "leads to high execution times on some problems" (p. 23). Table 3 (p. 22) runs the primal and primal-dual options in one code. Primal-dual needs fewer evaluations on 11 problems, ties on 2 (DIXCHLNV, ORTHREGF) and loses on OPTCNTRL (51 vs 50); primal fails on GAUSSELM; primal-dual hits the trust region less often (p. 21). Table 4 (p. 22) is an internal diagnostic: CG iterations divided by n − t at the last iteration, with μ of order 10⁻⁷, plus the step type. Twelve steps are "full", NGONE "hit tr" and OBSTCLAE reaches the CG limit, so the subproblems do not get harder near the solution (p. 21). The robustness test is Hock–Schittkowski via CUTE, "all the problems that we tested", f evals only (pp. 23–24, Table 5). NITRO "failed on very few", but it is worse than LANCELOT (p. 23); for example HS95 needs 156 vs 8 and HS96 224 vs 8 (p. 24).
  - Transferable: pair a small, varied, hand-picked set, analysed run by run with internal diagnostics such as the CG ratio and step type at the last iteration (pp. 20–22), with a broad robustness set reported in full, losses included (pp. 23–24).
- **D6 Positioning in the literature**: Interior methods are offered as "an alternative to active set methods" (p. 2). The convex line-search methods are, in a sense, direct extensions of LP methods (p. 2). The nonconvex line-search methods are judged too recent to assess (p. 3). Coleman–Li and Plantenga are credited, with their differences stated (p. 3), and the design is related to Yamashita's SQP line-search method (p. 3). The case for primal-dual relies on LP research and on Jarre–S. Wright, M. Wright and Conn–Gould–Toint for the weakness of the primal step (pp. 7–8). The final remarks reject imitation: "Rather than trying to mimic primaldual interior point methods" for LP, they develop "a fairly standard SQP trust region method" with primal-dual features (p. 23). The augmented system is preferred to the normal equations on the strength of their own analysis with Gould and Hribar (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The novelty sentence comes early (p. 5): the novelty "lies in the formulation of the quadratic model in the SQP iteration" and in the definition of the scaled trust region. Hedging is steady: "suggest that the new algorithm holds much promise" (p. 2), "appears to be robust" (p. 2), "Our tests seem to indicate that our code is competitive on large problems" (p. 23). Design choices come with reasons and alternatives: dogleg rather than Steihaug-CG for the vertical step, to use direct linear algebra and reuse the factorization (p. 12); the box trust region tried and set aside (p. 10). Losses on small problems are stated with a diagnosis and a conjecture: the barrier decrease is "overly conservative", and accelerating it should cut evaluations (p. 23). Robustness first, in so many words: "No attempt was made to obtain a rapidly convergent method" (p. 23). There are no theorems, and the text is mostly algorithm description with numbered procedures (pp. 4–19).
- **D8 Contribution and limitations**: The contribution is an algorithm and code (NITRO, with primal and primal-dual options) plus a numerical study; the theory is delegated to [8]. The stated limits: linear convergence by design (pp. 4, 23); worse than LANCELOT on small Hock–Schittkowski problems, a behaviour "We do not yet have a complete understanding" of (p. 23); and very conservative iterative refinement, which raises execution times (p. 23). The method is only weakly primal-dual (p. 9), and the trust region differs from the analysed model (p. 10). Future work: higher-order corrections and superlinear barrier-parameter rules (ref. [7], p. 23; also p. 4), and cheaper error detection and refinement in the linear systems (ref. [24], p. 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a barrier SQP, clip the primal-dual slack Hessian entry to μ/s² when a least-squares multiplier is negative. This keeps the model convex in the slacks without forcing the multipliers positive (p. 9).
- Precondition projected CG by the orthogonal projection, computed through the augmented system, so that CG behaves as with an orthonormal null-space basis and Z is never formed (pp. 14–15, 18).
- Pick the penalty parameter so that the predicted merit reduction is at least ρν times the vertical (feasibility) model decrease. ν is then monotone for a fixed barrier problem (pp. 16–17).
- Report a last-iteration diagnostic table (CG iterations per dimension and step type) to show that the subproblems do not get harder as μ → 0 (pp. 21–22).
