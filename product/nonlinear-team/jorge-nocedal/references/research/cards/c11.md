### S072 · Analysis of the BFGS method with errors (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2020, DOI 10.1137/19m1240794; arXiv:1901.09063)
**Read level**: full. All 28 file pages were read: abstract, §§1–5, Appendix A (proof of Lemma 3.7, pp. 26–27, read in full) and references (p. 28). The version read is arXiv v1 of 25 Jan 2019 (p. 1), not the typeset journal version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yuchen Xie (Northwestern IEMS), Richard H. Byrd (Colorado, Computer Science) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the theory paper of the 2017/18–2025 noise programme. It cites the group's finite-difference quasi-Newton DFO paper (Berahas, Byrd & Nocedal, arXiv:1803.10173) as [2] (pp. 2, 28) and announces the practical companion, which became S071 (p. 26; S071 p. 1). Nocedal's support is listed as DARPA (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap in theory, not a failed application. The classical analysis of quasi-Newton methods assumes exact function and gradient values (abstract, p. 1), and BFGS with errors "has received little attention in the literature" (p. 1). The drivers named are noise in machine learning and simulations with computational errors (p. 1). The goal is stated as a theory goal: to "extend the theory of quasi-Newton methods to the case when there are errors in the function and gradient evaluations" (p. 1). Two failure mechanisms are named. Gradient differences give misleading curvature, and the line search is confused by inaccurate function values (p. 1). The concrete mechanism is that when ‖s_k‖ is small compared with the gradient error ε_g, y_k is contaminated and the curvature bounds (2.4) fail (pp. 3–4). The gap is mapped by which assumption each prior line uses to dodge the hard part. Implicit filtering lets noise be diminished at will. Dennis–Walker and Ypma are local, starting near the solution with a good Hessian. Berahas et al. analyse a gradient method with a relaxed Armijo test and "do not study the effects of noise in BFGS updating". Stochastic quasi-Newton papers assume that gradient-difference quality is controlled (p. 2).
  - Transferable: list, for each prior line of work, the assumption that lets it avoid the hard part (noise decays on demand, local start, controlled differences). State your problem as removing exactly those assumptions (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The BFGS update and the standard Armijo-Wolfe line search are kept unchanged. The only change is the curvature pair. If ‖α_k p_k‖ < l, set s_k = l·p_k/‖p_k‖ and y_k = g(x_k + s_k) − g(x_k) (Algorithm 2.1, steps 9–13, p. 4). This costs one extra gradient evaluation (p. 5). The lengthened pair is computed even when the line search fails (α_k = 0), and lengthening guarantees s_kᵀy_k > 0 (pp. 4–5). l is "the only unspecified parameter", of order ε_g (p. 4). The theory needs l > 2ε_g/m (Lemma 3.8, p. 12; (3.42), p. 19). Three alternatives are rejected, each with a reason. Relaxing the Armijo-Wolfe conditions as Berahas et al. do: the standard search is kept "as this has practical advantages" (p. 2; also p. 3). Skipping the update "can prevent the algorithm from building a useful Hessian approximation" (p. 5). Modifying y_k (Powell damping) is hard to design under noise (pp. 1–2, 5).
  - Transferable: when a secant update is corrupted by noise, change where the curvature is sampled (the differencing interval), not the update formula or the step acceptance test (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: φ is bounded below and C², with an M-Lipschitz gradient. The C² requirement is admitted to be "only to simplify the proof of one of the lemmas below" (Assumption 3.1, p. 5). Function and gradient errors are uniformly bounded, |ε(x)| ≤ ε_f and ‖e(x)‖ ≤ ε_g (Assumption 3.2, p. 5). The authors note that Gaussian noise violates this. They defer unbounded errors as complex [Paquette–Scheinberg], because "this is best done, at first, in a benign setting" (p. 5). Strong convexity with modulus m (Assumption 3.6, p. 12) is needed for the curvature-pair bounds (3.24). The algorithm's l needs both ε_g and m. Page 19 says there are procedures for estimating them "as discussed in Section 4". Section 4, however, sets l = 4ε_g/m with both constants known (p. 24), and the final remarks confirm that both are assumed known (p. 26). Theorem 3.17 also needs max{ε_f, ε_g} > 0 (p. 21).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The skeleton of §3 (pp. 5–23) has seven parts.
  - (a) A generic two-oracle perturbation lemma (Lemma 3.3, pp. 6–8) is used twice, with the roles swapped. It relates (F_A, G_A) and (F_B, G_B) when G_A satisfies an affine-Lipschitz bound ‖G_A(y) − G_A(z)‖ ≤ L‖y − z‖ + Λ. First use: a noisy Armijo-Wolfe step exists when the true gradient is large (Theorem 3.4, Λ = 0, pp. 8–10). Second use: any noisy Armijo-Wolfe step satisfies Armijo-Wolfe for φ with loosened constants (Theorem 3.5, Λ = 2ε_g, pp. 10–11). The angles are first left as free parameters ("Hence, this result is preliminary.", p. 8).
  - (b) A spectral-ball characterization: y = Hs for some H with λ(H) ⊆ [µ, L] if and only if ‖y − (L+µ)/2·s‖ ≤ (L−µ)/2·‖s‖ (Lemma 3.7, p. 12). The converse is proved by constructing a reflector Q = 2eeᵀ − I (Appendix, pp. 26–27). With the average Hessian and the 2ε_g perturbation, this gives m̂ = m − 2ε_g/l and M̂ = M + 2ε_g/l (Lemma 3.8, pp. 12–13).
  - (c) The Byrd–Nocedal 1989 tool [4, Thm 2.1] is reused. If every pair satisfies the bounds, a fraction q of iterates have cos Θ_j ≥ β1(q), with β0(q) = (tr B0 − log det B0 + M̂ − 1 − log m̂)/(1 − q) (Lemma 3.9 and Corollary 3.10, pp. 13–14). This side-steps the condition-number bound on B_k, which cannot be had for full BFGS before convergence is proved (pp. 3, 11).
  - (d) Angle transfer from the noisy to the true gradient: if ε/‖g2‖ ≤ β/4, then cos ϑ2 ≥ β/2 (Lemma 3.11, pp. 14–15).
  - (e) Decrease of order ‖∇φ‖² on good iterates outside a neighbourhood (Theorem 3.13 and Corollary 3.14, pp. 16–19). The constants A and B depend only on c1 and c2, not on the function or the noise level (p. 17).
  - (f) A running-minimum sequence ξ_k = min_{i≤k} φ(x_i) satisfies φ(x_k) ≤ ξ_k + 2ε_f, which handles a true φ that need not decrease monotonically (Theorem 3.15, pp. 19–20). Built on it: an R-linear rate ρ = (1 − mζ)^q down to 2ε_f before the iterates enter N1 (Theorem 3.16, pp. 20–21); finite entry into N1, by contradiction (Theorem 3.17, pp. 21–22); and confinement afterwards to N2, a 2ε_f band (Theorem 3.18, p. 22).
  - (g) Lengthening is never triggered on good iterates far from N1 (Theorem 3.19, p. 23).
  - Transferable: device (a). Write one perturbation lemma between a proxy pair (F_A, G_A, standing in for the true φ and ∇φ, p. 5) and an observed pair, with an additive slack Λ in the Lipschitz bound, and apply it in both directions (pp. 6–11). Device (f): track the running minimum of the true function when only the noisy function is monotone (pp. 19–20).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There is one problem, a 4-D quadratic with eigenvalues {10⁻², 1, 10², 10⁴}, so m = 10⁻² and M = 10⁴ (p. 23). Function noise is uniform on [−ε_f, ε_f], gradient noise is uniform on the ball of radius ε_g, and ε_f = ε_g = 1 (p. 24). The lengthening parameter is l = 4ε_g/m, twice the bound (p. 24). The line search is a bisection Armijo-Wolfe search with c1 = 0.01 and c2 = 0.5, declared failed after 64 trials (then α = 0). The run stops at ‖g‖ ≤ 10⁻⁵, after 30 consecutive line-search failures, or at 60 iterations. The start is x0 = 10⁵·(1,1,1,1), with ‖∇φ(x0)‖ ≈ 10⁹ (p. 24). Twenty runs are plotted: log true gap and log true-gradient norm, with the noise level and the first lengthening (k = 8) drawn in (Figs. 4.1–4.2, pp. 24–25). A third plot shows the log condition number of H_k^{1/2}∇²φH_k^{1/2}. It shows that the Hessian approximation stays accurate after noise dominates (Fig. 4.3, pp. 24–25). There is no baseline (no unmodified BFGS run) and no test set. The authors say the results are designed to "verify only the behavior predicted by the theory" (p. 26).
  - Transferable: in a theory paper, use one small, badly conditioned instance and many seeds. Plot the true (not the observed) gap and gradient, with the noise level and the iteration where the new mechanism first fires marked. Add a matrix-quality diagnostic such as cond(H^{1/2}∇²φH^{1/2}) (pp. 24–25).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents itself as an extension of Byrd–Nocedal 1989 [4], "one of the hallmark results of BFGS" (p. 25), and of Powell's result that Armijo-Wolfe searches stabilize BFGS (p. 2). The neighbourhood-type result is framed "along the lines of Nedic and Bertsekas" (p. 26). Prior work is restated by its assumptions, not attacked (p. 2). This includes the group's own work: Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal [2], and the stochastic quasi-Newton papers [3, 8, 12, 17], among them Byrd–Hansen–Nocedal–Singer [3]. Those papers "avoid potential difficulties with BFGS or L-BFGS updating by assuming that the quality of gradient differences is always controlled" (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The intro's fifth sentence states both the single modification and everything that stays unchanged (p. 1). After each theorem comes a plain paragraph on what is now established and what is still open (pp. 8, 11, 17, 22–23). Hedging is placed where the scope narrows: the benign-setting sentence (p. 5) and "this result is preliminary" (p. 8). Each rejected alternative gets a one-sentence reason (pp. 2, 5). There is one internal inconsistency in v1: the estimation procedures promised for Section 4 (p. 19) do not appear there (pp. 23–24, 26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory. A slightly modified BFGS with bounded errors in f and g converges globally, R-linearly, to a noise-determined neighbourhood, and its Hessian approximation is not corrupted there (pp. 20–23, 26). The limits are stated concretely. The objective must be strongly convex and the noise bounded (pp. 5, 12), ε_g and m must be known (p. 26), and no superlinear rate is claimed, because that "would require errors to diminish very rapidly" (p. 25). The future work is specific: a separate paper with adaptive l, nonconvexity and a limited-memory version (p. 26). S071 delivered it (S071 p. 1).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (evidence ✅, steps 1, 3 and 6): the classical BFGS and its Armijo-Wolfe search are kept (pp. 1–2). The failing component is named: curvature pairs from a too-short differencing interval (pp. 3–4). It is replaced by a version driven by explicit constants (ε_g, m; pp. 4, 12, 19). Convergence is proved to a noise-determined neighbourhood (pp. 20–22). The practical version is announced as a separate paper (p. 26), which confirms Method 3 step 6's two-paper pattern.
- Method 3 (variant ⚠, the 🔴 checkpoint in SKILL.md on constants users cannot know): this theory paper knowingly depends on m and assumes it known (pp. 19, 24, 26). It has no recovery path or exit flag beyond α = 0 on a failed search and a stop after 30 consecutive failures (pp. 4, 24). The checkpoint is met only by the companion paper (S071 pp. 1, 7).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅, steps 1 and 3): it analyses the unmodified Armijo-Wolfe line search people run, "as this has practical advantages" (p. 2), and proves an R-linear rate, not only convergence (p. 20). Variant ⚠ on step 4: Fig. 4.1 draws the noise level, not the theorem's neighbourhood N1 or its rate, so there is no bound-versus-run plot (p. 24).
- new-pattern candidate: **Extend your own classical analysis tool to the new regime.** The 1989 good-iterates theorem is the backbone (pp. 3, 13–14, 25); S071 reuses it for BFGS and L-BFGS (S071 pp. 7, 10–11).
- new-pattern candidate: **Benign setting first, as declared staging.** Bounded noise, strong convexity and known constants come first, with the practical, nonconvex, limited-memory version promised for a later paper (pp. 5, 26).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Lengthened differencing: if the accepted step is shorter than l, recompute y at x_k + l·p_k/‖p_k‖ (one extra gradient). With l > 2ε_g/m, the curvature ratios satisfy yᵀs/sᵀs ≥ m − 2ε_g/l and yᵀy/yᵀs ≤ M + 2ε_g/l (pp. 4, 12–13).
- A two-oracle perturbation lemma with an affine-Lipschitz slack Λ, used once true→noisy (Λ = 0) and once noisy→true (Λ = 2ε_g) (pp. 6–11).
- Ball characterization of y = Hs with spectrum in [µ, L], to turn a perturbation bound on y into bounds on yᵀs/sᵀs and yᵀy/yᵀs (p. 12; proof pp. 26–27).
- A running minimum of the true objective, to get linear convergence to a 2ε_f band when only the noisy objective is monotone (pp. 19–21).
- To show the quasi-Newton matrix is not corrupted, plot cond(H_k^{1/2}∇²φ H_k^{1/2}) against iterations (p. 24).

**Quotes**:
- "We prefer to retain the standard Armijo-Wolfe line search without any modification, as this has practical advantages." (p. 2)
- "The numerical results presented here are designed to verify only the behavior predicted by the theory." (p. 26)

### S071 · A noise-tolerant quasi-Newton algorithm for unconstrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2022, DOI 10.1137/20m1373190; arXiv:2010.04352)
**Read level**: full. All 27 file pages were read: abstract, §§1–6, acknowledgements and references (pp. 26–27). Figure panels are image text in the extraction, so they were read from their captions and the body text only. The version read is arXiv v3 of 9 Sep 2021 (p. 1), not the typeset journal version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Hao-Jun Michael Shi and Yuchen Xie (Northwestern IEMS), Richard H. Byrd (Colorado) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the practical companion promised by S072 (S072 p. 26). It builds directly on S072, cited as [29] (pp. 1, 3). Shi was supported by a grant from Facebook, Nocedal by ONR and NSF (p. 1). Code was released on GitHub (hjmshi/noise-tolerant-bfgs, p. 25).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters through the practical gap left by S072. S072's algorithm "is not practical as it requires knowledge of the strong convexity parameter m of the objective function, which is normally not known", and "An overestimate of m may lead to an unstable iteration, whereas an underestimate can slow down convergence." (p. 1). S072 also proved only that an Armijo-Wolfe step exists and gave no practical line search (p. 4). The applications named are computational noise, low-precision arithmetic and statistical noise (abstract, p. 1). More specifically: PDE-constrained optimization with inexact linear solvers or adaptive grids, Monte Carlo simulation, and finite-difference gradients in DFO (p. 2). The mechanism is that gradient differences can cancel and leave only noise (p. 2). The authors concede that this case may be unlikely but note that it blocks any guarantee (p. 2). The failure is then shown on ARWHEAD with U[−10⁻³, 10⁻³] noise in each gradient component. The condition number of the BFGS matrix rises sharply, which does not happen without noise, and afterwards the line search takes tiny steps (pp. 2–3, Fig. 1). "The ARWHEAD problem is chosen because it is easily solved" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open with a failure demo on an easy problem and an internal diagnostic, κ(H_k) with and without noise. Say why the problem was chosen (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: S072's m-dependent lengthening is replaced by a noise control condition (2.9) on a separate lengthening parameter β_k ≥ α_k: (g(x_k + β_k p_k) − g(x_k))ᵀp_k ≥ 2(1 + c3)ε_g‖p_k‖ (pp. 4–5). This condition guarantees that the true change in directional derivative is at least 2c3ε_g‖p_k‖ (2.10). It also keeps the observed curvature sᵀy within the factors (1 ± 1/(1+c3))⁻¹ of the true sᵀỹ (2.12) (pp. 5–6). c3 trades accuracy against locality (p. 6). The condition is compatible with S072's β = O(ε_g/(m‖p‖)) via (2.14) (p. 6).
  - **Two-phase line search** (§4.1, pp. 12–14; Algorithms 4.1–4.2): the initial phase is a bisection with α = β. It checks Armijo, then the absolute noise test (4.3), then Wolfe. If (4.3) fails or N_split trials pass, the split phase starts: α backtracks on Armijo by a factor of 10, and β doubles until (4.5) holds. With ε_g = 0 the search reduces to the standard Armijo-Wolfe search (p. 13).
  - **Heuristics** (pp. 15–16): a relaxed Armijo rule (4.6). When g(x_k)ᵀp_k ≥ −ε_g‖p_k‖, the gradient is treated as unreliable and only simple decrease is required. After the first failed trial, 2ε_f is added to the right-hand side. The best Armijo α seen is reused. The initial β comes from a local strong-convexity estimate μ_k, the minimum of the last h curvature estimates (4.9)–(4.10).
  - **Algorithm 4.3** (p. 15): it moves only if α satisfies (4.1) and updates only if β satisfies (2.9).
  - **Rejected alternatives**: skipping is tested (Remark 1). Powell damping is rejected as dangerous (p. 6). Remark 2 (pp. 13–14) rejects a split-only search, which loses the longer steps the Wolfe test allows. It also rejects S072's sequential scheme: there the Armijo-Wolfe search can be fooled for many iterations, and enforcing Wolfe on a noise-dominated gradient leads to "ill-advised or unnecessary changes to the steplength" (p. 14).
  - **Change from S072**: S072 kept the line search unmodified (S072 p. 2). Here it is redesigned and Armijo is relaxed (pp. 12–16).
  - Transferable: write the safeguard as a test on observed quantities relative to the noise bound, as in (2.9), not on unknown problem constants. Then use the same test as the switch that tells the line search when the Wolfe test has become unreliable (pp. 4, 14).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The analysis assumes φ m-strongly convex with an M-Lipschitz gradient (Assumption 3.1, p. 7). Unlike S072, it does not need C². The errors are bounded and their bounds are known or estimated (ECNoise, sample variance, the Hamming procedure). The algorithm "has access to" ε_f and ε_g (pp. 2, 5; Assumption 3.2, p. 7). If the noise is unbounded or its bounds depend on x, the methods "cannot be directly applied" (p. 2). Theorem 3.5 also assumes that the line search finds an Armijo-Wolfe α on every good iterate outside N1 (p. 12). Lengthening terminates finitely if ∇φ(x + βp)ᵀp → ∞ as β grows. That holds for strongly convex functions and "many other (but not all) functions" (p. 6). The method is meant for general nonlinear problems, while the analysis covers only strongly convex ones (pp. 1, 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: The analysis has three parts.
  - (a) Lemma 3.3 (pp. 8–9): if sᵀ[g(x+s) − g(x)] ≥ c·ε_g‖s‖ with c > 2, then sᵀy/sᵀs ≥ c/(c+2)·m and yᵀy/sᵀy ≤ c/(c−2)·M. The proof uses the co-coercivity inequality for strongly convex functions with Lipschitz gradient (Bertsekas, Prop. 6.1.9(b)) to get the ball ‖ỹ − (M+m)/2·s‖ ≤ (M−m)/2·‖s‖ without C². It adds the 2ε_g perturbation and the lower bound ‖s‖ ≥ (c−2)ε_g/M, and splits into two cases on the sign of 2ε_g − m‖s‖. With c = 2(1 + c3) this gives m̂ = (1+c3)/(2+c3)·m and M̂ = (1 + 1/c3)·M (3.9). Neither depends on l or on the noise level (p. 10).
  - (b) Lemma 3.4 (pp. 10–11) covers both variants. For BFGS, a fraction of iterates are good, by Byrd–Nocedal [11, Thm 2.1]. For L-BFGS every iterate is good: the potential ψ(B) = tr B − log det B grows by at most M̂ − log m̂ per update, so κ(B_k) ≤ exp[(d + t)(M̂ − log m̂)].
  - (c) Theorem 3.5 (pp. 11–12) is proved by reduction. The algorithm differs from S072's "only in the quasi-Newton updating strategy and lengthening procedure". So S072's line-search results, and its Theorems 3.16–3.18, carry over with γ* in place of β1 (p. 12). The constants A and B are the same as S072's (p. 11; cf. S072 p. 18).
  - Transferable: build the proof in modules. Isolate the update-specific lemma (here the curvature-pair bounds), so that a new update rule needs only a new version of that lemma while the line-search and rate chain is reused verbatim (p. 12).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Six methods are compared: BFGS, L-BFGS (t = 10), BFGS (Skips) and L-BFGS (Skips), which use rule (5.1) with c3 = 0, and BFGS-E and L-BFGS-E (pp. 16–17). All use bisection without interpolation, "because it is more robust in the presence of noise" (p. 17). The parameters are c1 = 10⁻⁴, c2 = 0.9, c3 = 0.5, t = 10 and N_split = 30 (Table 1, p. 17). The test set is 41 unconstrained CUTEst problems with d = 30–110 (Table 2, p. 18), with uniform noise injected per component, so ε_g = √d·ξ_g (p. 17). φ* comes from running BFGS on the noise-free problem until it makes no more progress (p. 17). The protocol runs in eight steps.
  - (1) Gradient noise only (ε_f = 0), "a benign setting that avoids the effects of the noisy line search", for a fixed number of iterations (§5.1, p. 18).
  - (2) ξ_g = 10⁻¹, 10⁻³ and 10⁻⁵, plotted against gradient evaluations (Fig. 3, p. 19).
  - (3) Cost accounting: once the split phase starts, each iteration costs 2–4 gradient evaluations (Fig. 4, pp. 19–20).
  - (4) A misspecification sweep with ε̄_g = ωε_g, ω ∈ {1/10, 1/5, 1/2, 1, 2, 5, 10}. Underestimation causes early stagnation; overestimation slows progress (§5.1.1, Fig. 5, p. 20).
  - (5) Intermittent noise switched on and off every N_noise ∈ {10, 25, 50} iterations on CRAGGLVY. Plain L-BFGS recovers when N_noise exceeds its memory t (§5.2, pp. 20–21).
  - (6) Skipping against lengthening. Skipping wins on the easy ENGVAL1 and loses on EIGENCLS and the intermittent CRAGGLVY, and it is then dropped (§5.3, pp. 21–23).
  - (7) Function plus gradient noise on DIXMAANH, 3000 gradient evaluations (Figs. 10–11, pp. 23–24).
  - (8) Aggregate pairwise Morales profiles over the 41 problems, averaged over 5 seeds, at ξ_f = ξ_g = 10⁻³ only. One profile is the log₂ ratio of the gaps after 3000 iterations; the other is the log₂ ratio of the evaluations needed to reach (5.3) (pp. 23, 25).
  Single-problem figures are chosen because each "illustrates typical behavior over the whole test set" (pp. 17–18). All comparators belong to the same family. There is no implicit-filtering code, no relaxed-line-search gradient method and no DFO solver (pp. 16–17).
  - Transferable: sweep any estimated input from ×1/10 to ×10 and report which direction of error hurts (p. 20). Use intermittent noise as a probe of whether the method recovers once noise disappears (pp. 20–21). Use pairwise log₂-ratio (Morales) profiles sorted by value, read by area: "the larger the area, the more successful the method" (p. 23).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper calls S072 [29] the most relevant work (p. 3) and builds its analysis on Byrd–Nocedal 1989 [11] (p. 7). The literature map is S072's: Kelley, Dennis–Walker, Ypma, Barton and Berahas et al. (p. 3). Stochastic quasi-Newton papers for machine learning [7, 9, 10, 19, 25, 28] are said to avoid the difficulty "by assuming that the quality of gradient differences is sufficiently controlled" (p. 3). Three of those are the group's own: [7] Berahas–Nocedal–Takáč, [9] Bollapragada et al. and [10] Byrd–Hansen–Nocedal–Singer (p. 26). The relaxed Armijo rule is linked to Berahas–Cao–Scheinberg [6] (p. 15). The profile is credited to Morales [22], the Morales–Nocedal L-BFGS-B Remark (pp. 23, 26). Powell damping is dismissed without a test (p. 6). Bindel, Moré, Tang and Waechter are thanked for "suggestions of problems with computational noise" (p. 26).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Three contributions are numbered i–iii in the introduction (p. 3) and restated as "two contributions" in the final remarks (pp. 24–25). Remarks 1 and 2 answer reviewer questions in advance. Remark 2 opens by admitting the design "may seem too complex" and then argues against the simpler options (pp. 6, 13–14). Surprising plots are explained in the text: κ(H_k) falling after its spike (p. 19), and L-BFGS recovering through its finite memory (p. 21). Hedging is moderate and scoped: "encouraging numerical results" (p. 1), "robust for certain classes of noisy optimization problems" (p. 3), and "often provide a real improvement in the solution of certain classes of optimization problems" (p. 24). There is one tension. The abstract says the line search "guarantees that the Armijo-Wolfe conditions are satisfied under most reasonable conditions" (p. 1). But Theorem 3.5 assumes the line search succeeds (p. 12), and the text admits the two-phase search "may not be guaranteed to find αk and βk under certain scenarios" (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The paper contributes an algorithm, theory and software. It introduces the noise control condition, the BFGS-E and L-BFGS-E methods, and their linear convergence to a noise neighbourhood for strongly convex functions (pp. 10–12, 24–25), and releases public code (p. 25). It also claims to have turned S072's algorithm into "a robust and practical procedure" (p. 25).
  - Limits printed concretely: unbounded or x-dependent noise is excluded (p. 2). The search can fail when noise dominates or along very flat directions, and the method then stops at the line-search iteration cap (p. 13). Underestimating the noise level hurts (p. 20). Skipping is cheaper on easy, well-conditioned problems with uniform noise (pp. 21, 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- A noise control test on a separate lengthening parameter: accept the curvature pair only if (g(x+βp) − g(x))ᵀp ≥ 2(1+c3)ε_g‖p‖. This needs only the noise bound and gives curvature-ratio bounds independent of the noise level (pp. 4–6, 10).
- A two-phase line search. Run bisection with α = β; when the noise test fails, split, backtracking α on Armijo (factor 10) and doubling β on the noise test. It reduces to standard Armijo-Wolfe at zero noise (pp. 12–14).
- A gradient-reliability switch in the Armijo test. If gᵀp ≥ −ε_g‖p‖, require only simple decrease, and add 2ε_f after the first failure (p. 15).
- Seed the expensive search with a cheap local estimate (the minimum of the last h curvature estimates). In the paper's tests this cut lengthening to 1–3 extra gradient evaluations (p. 16).
- In the experiments, sweep the misspecification of the estimated noise level, test intermittent noise, and compare against the cheapest safeguard (skipping), reporting where it wins (pp. 20–22).
- For an L-BFGS analysis, bound κ(B_k) through ψ(B) = tr B − log det B over t updates from the scaled initial matrix. Every iterate is then good, not just a fraction (pp. 10–11).
