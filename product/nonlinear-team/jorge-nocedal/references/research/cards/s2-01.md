### S067 · Numerical experience with a reduced Hessian method for large scale constrained optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2000, doi:10.1023/a:1008723031056)
**Read level**: full. Read: abstract (p. 2), §1 (pp. 2–5), §2 with Algorithm RHC (pp. 5–9), §3 (pp. 10–13), §4 analytic example (pp. 13–14), §5 numerical results with Tables 2–5 (pp. 14–19), §6 conclusions (p. 19), Appendix §7 (pp. 19–21). References were skimmed (pp. 22–23). Table 1 of suggested parameter values (p. 9) is still unreadable. Tables 2–3 (p. 16) are legible. In Tables 4–5 (pp. 17–18) cells are out of row order. First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Lorenz T. Biegler, Claudia Schmid and David Ternet (Chemical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon; supported in part by Bayer Corporation) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the computational companion of the Biegler–Nocedal–Schmid reduced-Hessian theory paper (SIAM J. Optim. 1995; reference [2], p. 22). It starts from that paper's Algorithm II (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: a reduced-Hessian SQP method approximates Z^T W Z by BFGS. This pays off for many variables with few degrees of freedom (pp. 4–5). Dropping the cross term Z^T W Y p_Y works with orthonormal bases but gives poor directions with coordinate bases, which are the affordable choice at large scale (p. 4). The entry was practical: the first author's tests on chemical process control problems, with non-orthogonal bases, were "sometimes very inefficient" because the search directions were poor (p. 19).
- **D2 Core idea**: approximate the cross term by a vector w_k. There are two options: a Broyden matrix S_k ≈ Z^T W, which needs no extra gradient, or a finite difference, which costs one extra gradient. Finite differences are used "only ... when this is required to ensure a superlinear rate of convergence" (p. 5). Departures from Algorithm II of [2]:
  - the finite-difference formula is rescaled by α_k (2.7) to avoid an extra gradient. Because (2.7) can be poor far from the solution, the option is switched on only near a solution (p. 6);
  - the finite-difference correction is computed only after the KKT error falls below a threshold (step 6, p. 7);
  - a new bound (2.29) is placed on the finite-difference correction (p. 9);
  - a damping factor ζ_k keeps the direction a descent direction for the ℓ1 merit function (pp. 6–7).
  Implementation devices (§3): MA28 picks the basis. A new basis is requested when max |(C⁻¹N)_ij| grows tenfold, or when a tiny step length coincides with growth (threshold exponent unreadable) (pp. 10–11). After a basis change, B and S are mapped into the new coordinates by (3.9)–(3.10) instead of being reset, so that curvature information is kept (pp. 11–12). The watchdog line search handles the Maratos effect and is switched on only after the KKT error falls below a user tolerance (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions**: equality constraints only, "Because we wish to focus on the effects of this correction term". The ideas are said to carry over to an active-set code such as SNOPT (p. 5). A_k has full column rank (p. 3), the basis C is nonsingular (p. 4) and Z^T W Z is assumed positive definite in (1.11) (p. 4). Global and superlinear convergence are taken from [2] "under certain assumptions" (p. 2). The paper says that redefinition (2.7) affects only Lemma 5.5 of [2], whose proof "can easily be modified". That modification is not shown (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: the key argument is an analytic example in which a parameter θ is adjustable (4.1)–(4.2):
  - orthogonal bases give quadratic contraction (4.4);
  - the coordinate basis without the correction gives a one-step error ratio that grows with θ. The two-step behaviour agrees with Byrd's and Yuan's 2-step superlinear result, but the paper calls enforcing descent only every two iterations "too risky" (p. 14);
  - with the correction, the step equals the orthogonal-basis step (pp. 13–14).
  The derivations are in the Appendix (pp. 19–21). To simplify them, B_k = Z_k^T Z_k replaces BFGS (p. 12).
- **D5 Experiments**: DEC-ALPHA, double-precision FORTRAN. Three arms: No Correction, Broyden Correction and the full RHC. The watchdog and the finite differences switch on when the KKT measure is below 0.1 (p. 15). Results are iterations (function/gradient evaluations).
  - With a good basis the correction changes little: Example 3, n = 80, gives 6 (6/6) for all three arms (Table 3, p. 16).
  - With a poor basis it helps. Example 3, n = 80: 27 (39/27), 19 (28/19), 17 (21/18). n = 200: 25 (36/25), 19 (26/19), 18 (22/19) (Table 3, p. 16). Example 2, poor basis, 80 variables: 19 (34/19), 9 (12/9), 8 (11/10) (Table 2, p. 16).
  - Hock–Schittkowski problems are reported without times because they are small (Table 4, p. 17).
  - CUTE problems EIGENC2, EIGENCCO and ORTHREGA/C/D add CPU seconds (Table 5, p. 18). The Broyden-only arm uses the fewest gradient evaluations (pp. 17–18). In the Table 5 row with the largest CPU time: No Correction 341 (864/358/1294.5), Broyden 298 (681/312/1370.6), RHC 298 (681/338/1379.9) (p. 18; row labels scrambled).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm and its implementation. The correction "may be a very useful feature in any reduced Hessian SQP method" (p. 19). Limits are stated:
  - MA28 may return the same basis, and forcing a different one "is a refinement that our code does not yet include" (p. 11);
  - equality constraints only (p. 5);
  - the full method is not uniformly best: if gradients are expensive, "it may be advantageous to compute the correction term always by Broyden's method" (p. 19).
  The Table 1 defaults are said to "give good performance" (p. 9), but the values cannot be read in this file.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the reduced-Hessian SQP engine is kept, and only the component that breaks is replaced (the cross term dropped under coordinate bases). The expensive finite-difference version is used only past an explicit KKT-error trigger (0.1 in the tests) and is safeguarded by (2.27) and (2.29) (pp. 5–9, 15). The repair is ablated in Tables 2–5, which can now be read (pp. 16–18) (steps 3, 5).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the analytic example separates orthogonal from coordinate bases, and the corrected from the uncorrected step (pp. 13–14). The runs are read against the theory of [2]: the correction helps "only when the path followed by the iterates is not tangential to the constraints" (p. 16) (steps 2, 4).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the comparison is an ablation of the authors' own code with three arms, not a comparison with rival codes. It does print where the full method loses: the Broyden-only arm needs fewer gradient evaluations (pp. 17–19).
- Heuristic 1 and Heuristic 9 evidence (card only): an analytic failure example comes first (p. 13), and the work was entered from a practitioner's problem, chemical process control (p. 19).
- New-pattern candidate (existing name): *Motivate-and-revisit toy examples*. The analytic example (§4) returns as test Examples 2–3 with a dictated good or poor basis (pp. 14–16).
- New-pattern candidate: *switch on local-convergence devices only near the solution*. Finite-difference corrections and the watchdog are gated on a size-independent KKT error (pp. 7, 9, 13, 15). Compare S077's growth of the HFN cycle (p. 6).
- New-pattern candidate: *preserve curvature memory across a change of basis*. The old quasi-Newton matrices are transformed with PZ = Z̃R and B̃ = Z̃^T P^T T^T B T P Z̃ rather than reset (pp. 11–12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a two-variable analytic example whose free parameter θ makes the harm of an omitted term arbitrarily large, then show that the repair restores the behaviour of the ideal basis (pp. 13–14, 19–21).
- Ablate a repair with three arms: none, the cheap approximation only, and the full scheme. Report gradient evaluations separately from function evaluations, so that the cheap arm's advantage stays visible (Tables 2–5, pp. 16–18).
- Gate expensive or local-convergence devices on a size-independent KKT error (max norm) rather than on an iteration count (pp. 9, 13, 15).
- After a change of coordinates, map the old quasi-Newton matrix into the new ones using a permutation identity, instead of discarding it (pp. 11–12).

**Quotes**:
- "The correction term cannot be expected to improve the performance of the algorithm when the basis matrices are adequate, but should prevent poor performance from taking place." (p. 14)
- "This research was motivated by numerical tests on chemical process control problems performed, a few years ago, by the first author." (p. 19)

### S098 · Algorithm 809: PREQN: Fortran 77 subroutines for preconditioning the conjugate gradient method (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 2001, doi:10.1145/382043.382343)
**Read level**: full. Read: abstract (p. 1), §§1–5 (pp. 2–8), acknowledgements (p. 9) and references (p. 10). Table 1 (p. 8) is still unreadable apart from its IOP column labels and caption. The exponent of the CG stopping test (5.13) is garbled. The Fortran code is not in the file. First page checked: "Algorithm PREQN: Fortran 77 Subroutines for Preconditioning the Conjugate Gradient Method", José Luis Morales and Jorge Nocedal (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement (software paper) · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales (ITAM, Mexico) (p. 1); two referees thanked (p. 9) · **Position in trajectory**: the package for the preconditioner of the companion paper [5], "Automatic Preconditioning by Limited Memory Quasi-Newton Updating" (pp. 4, 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: a sequence of linear systems A_i x = b_i with symmetric positive definite A_i that vary slowly and arbitrary right-hand sides. The multiple-right-hand-side problem is the special case of constant A_i (p. 2). The target is matrices that are known only through products A_i v, or that are not sparse (abstract). Such sequences arise in Newton methods for optimization, in differential equations and in statistics (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: BFGS and CG are equivalent on positive definite quadratics (p. 4). So the CG directions and their products (p^(j), A p^(j)) serve as correction pairs (2.9); the step lengths cancel (p. 3). The m pairs collected while solving system i define a limited-memory BFGS matrix H_{i+1}(m) that preconditions system i+1 (p. 4).
  - Two pair-selection rules come from the companion paper: a uniform sample (IOP = 1) or the last m pairs (IOP = 2) (pp. 4, 7). With IOP = 1 the last pair is always added (M + 1 pairs, M even) for "a slight improvement in performance" (p. 7).
  - Interface: one call per CG iteration applies the preconditioner and hands over the newest pair. The decision whether to keep a pair, and the building of the next preconditioner, wait until the next call (pp. 5–6).
  - BUILD = .FALSE. reuses the previous preconditioner, for example when the last CG run took very few iterations ("say, less than 5") or when the matrix is constant (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions**: A_i symmetric positive definite, which gives s^T y > 0 (p. 3), and slowly varying (p. 2). No convergence theory.
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (software paper). Cost accounting takes its place: the product H(m)v costs about 4mn flops, and the pairs take 2mn storage (pp. 3–4).
- **D5 Experiments**: three finite-element matrices from [5], each with 51 right-hand sides (b_2, …, b_51 are perturbations of b_1). The preconditioner is built from the first CG solve and reused for the other 50 systems. Reported: average CG iterations over the 50 solves, for several m and both IOP values. The stopping test differs from the one in [5] (pp. 7–8). In these tests uniform sampling "performs much better", although the two rules are comparable in most of the authors' experiments. The preconditioner "is not always beneficial", and its effect grows with the stored information (p. 8). The table values cannot be read.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: software. Advice comes as numbers: m in [4, 20], independent of n (p. 4); smaller values precondition poorly, and values above 20 cost time (p. 7). The scope is printed as a break-even point. For one of the test matrices with IOP = 1 and m = 16, preconditioned CG wins only if a matrix–vector product costs more than 24n operations, against 10n flops per unpreconditioned CG iteration. Hence it is "unlikely to be useful for very sparse problems", although in optimization a product costs about as much as a gradient (p. 8). BUILD = .FALSE. "must be used with caution" (p. 7). Users are told to experiment with m (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: two selection rules that both "performed well" become the user option IOP instead of one hard-coded rule, and BUILD is a further switch (pp. 4, 7) (step 2).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the authors scope their own preconditioner by structure. They advocate it only when A_i is unknown, dense or costly, and point to incomplete Cholesky as cheaper to apply (p. 4). A flop break-even says when it will not pay (p. 8) (steps 1–3).
- Heuristic 5 and Heuristic 7 evidence (card only): parameter advice as numbers (m ∈ [4, 20], p. 4); a numbered TOMS algorithm with a documented calling sequence (pp. 6–7).
- New-pattern candidate (existing name): *recycle inner-solver by-products as curvature memory*. The CG directions of one solve become the quasi-Newton preconditioner of the next (p. 4).
- New-pattern candidate (existing name): *algorithm information structure as a deployment interface*. A single call per CG iteration both applies the preconditioner and passes on the data for the next one (pp. 5–6). Compare S119.

**Transferable techniques**:
- State a break-even inequality for your preconditioner (its extra flops per iteration against the cost of a matrix–vector product) and name the problem class for which it will not pay (p. 8).
- Design the library call so that one invocation per inner iteration both applies the operator and hands over what the next one needs. Defer bookkeeping decisions to the next call (pp. 5–6).
- When two selection rules perform comparably across tests, ship both behind a flag and report the test in which they differ (pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "Values of m, in the range [4, 20], are recommended for most problems, independently of the value of n." (p. 4)
- "Therefore the preconditioner is unlikely to be useful for very sparse problems." (p. 8)

### S111 · Feasibility control in nonlinear optimization (Foundations of Computational Mathematics 2001, doi:10.1017/cbo9781107360198.007)
**Read level**: full. Read: abstract (p. 1), §§1–5 (pp. 2–23) and acknowledgements (p. 23). The references were glanced at (pp. 23–26). Some displayed equations are garbled. Figures 1–4 are not in the text layer, but Table 1 (p. 21) can be read. First page checked: title, "M. Marazzi" and "Jorge Nocedal", Report OTC 2000/04 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement (survey with analysis) · **Co-authors**: M. Marazzi (Northwestern IEMS) (p. 1). Richard Byrd, Andreas Wächter and Richard Waltz are thanked, and "Mike Powell for an illuminating discussion concerning Example 1" (p. 23) · **Position in trajectory**: written after NITRO, which serves as its trust-region interior example (pp. 16–21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: methods that demand exact satisfaction of the constraint linearizations can converge to spurious points that are not stationary for the merit function or for the infeasibility measure (abstract). The way in is two counterexamples. The first is Powell's example for line-search Newton on nonlinear equations (pp. 2–4). The second is Wächter and Biegler's example for line-search interior methods, the case the paper calls "more surprisingly" affected (pp. 2, 13–15).
- **D2 Core idea**: name the mechanism, "feasibility control": mechanisms that regulate progress toward feasibility, so that the gain in linear feasibility is at least comparable to that of a Cauchy step (pp. 2, 5–6, 9–10). Then sort methods by whether they have it:
  - Levenberg–Marquardt and dogleg have it (pp. 4–6);
  - Vardi's method does not, because θ fixes the length of the step but not its direction (p. 8);
  - Byrd–Omojokun has it: a normal step, then a total-step region of 1.2Δ (pp. 8–9). So does Celis–Dennis–Tapia (pp. 9–10);
  - SNOPT's elastic mode applies it "only when needed" (p. 10). Biggs-type relaxation is also discussed (pp. 10–11);
  - line-search primal–dual interior methods (4.4)–(4.5) lack it (pp. 12–15);
  - NITRO has it, with τ = 0.995 and a normal-step radius ξΔ, ξ = 0.8 (pp. 16–19);
  - NUOPT's blend of the primal–dual and "steepest descent" steps lacks it, because both steps satisfy the linearization (pp. 21–22).
  Regularization is kept apart from feasibility control: σ "must be tied in with the convergence properties of the optimization process" (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions**: smooth problem functions. The trust-region result (2.7) needs A(x) Lipschitz and bounded on the level set (p. 5). A footnote summarizes the assumptions of the Byrd–Omojokun theory: smooth f and c, f bounded below, bounded gradients, constraint values, Jacobians and Hessian approximations (p. 9). The paper surveys more than it proves.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: the arguments run through examples.
  - Powell's example: from (3, 1) with an exact line search, the iterates converge to ≈ (1.8016, 0.000), a point where the merit function still has a descent direction (pp. 3–4).
  - The Wächter–Biegler argument uses only the third block of the Newton equations and the bounds, so the merit function and the barrier-update rule are irrelevant (p. 14).
  - In Example 2 the KKT matrix is singular on the limit manifold. Yet the step lengths go to zero because of the bounds, whereas in Example 1 the merit function forces them, a difference left "worthy of further investigation" (p. 15).
  - Example 3 (min x s.t. x ≥ b, with b = 500, μ = 0.1, x0 = 1, s0 = μ): a range-space normal step cut back by the fraction-to-the-boundary rule makes NITRO's primal version approach feasibility slowly. A lower bound (4.23) on the iteration count follows from a closed-form recurrence under Δ0 > (9/4)μ and s0 = μ (pp. 19–21).
- **D5 Experiments**: only small demonstration runs.
  - On Powell's example a relaxed backtracking line search succeeds: the first full step reaches (0, −1.841), and convergence is then linear (p. 4).
  - NITRO leaves the trap region of Example 2 after three iterations with an active trust region (Figure 3, p. 18).
  - NITRO's line-search option solves Example 3 in five steps. Infeasibility falls from 5.000E+02 to 6.706E-14 with step lengths 8.95E-04, 2.75E-03, 2.58E-02, 2.01E-01 and 1.00 (Table 1, p. 21).
  - The slow-feasibility behaviour was also seen on CUTE problems such as GOFFIN (p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**: a review, not a polemic. It credits Powell's analysis as "one of the founding blocks of trust region convergence theory" (p. 6) and credits Wächter and Biegler with the interior counterexample (p. 13). It credits NUOPT's "excellent numerical results" (p. 21) while showing that the method fails Example 2 (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: hedges are placed where knowledge stops: "We speculate" about Vardi's method on Example 1 (p. 8); "We do not know whether failures of this type are rare in practice" (p. 15); "To our surprise" when the relaxed line search succeeds (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a survey with analysis. Open questions are printed. Can a simple modification of line-search interior methods remove the Wächter–Biegler failure, and does Forsgren and Gill's method converge better (p. 15)? New techniques for solving the subproblems inexactly are needed to handle Example 3 (p. 21). Feasibility control in interior methods is "only beginning to be investigated" (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: an outside counterexample (Wächter–Biegler) is analysed as a specification. The paper also writes down a failure mode of the authors' own solver: NITRO's inexact normal step is inefficient on Example 3 and on GOFFIN, followed by a call for new subproblem techniques (pp. 13–15, 19–21) (steps 1, 4).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the Cauchy-decrease criterion separates methods that cannot fail on the examples from methods that can. Cannot fail: Levenberg–Marquardt, dogleg, Byrd–Omojokun, NITRO. Can fail: line-search Newton, Vardi, line-search interior methods, NUOPT (pp. 5–10, 13–22) (step 2).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the paper compares the integrated parts like with like and records where its own trust-region method loses. Example 3 is run with "an option in the NITRO package that computes steps of the form (4.4)-(4.5)", and the line-search step wins (p. 21) (step 5).
- New-pattern candidate (existing name): *property abstraction*. One property, feasibility control, is named, and about ten methods are classified by whether they have it, each against the canonical counterexamples (pp. 2, 8–10, 16–22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Organize a survey around one named mechanism and a short list of canonical counterexamples. Then classify each method by whether it has the mechanism, and check the classification on the examples (pp. 2–22).
- When a counterexample is proved for one line-search variant, rerun it with a relaxed (backtracking) line search and from other starting points before generalizing. Here the relaxed search succeeds from the published start but fails from others (p. 4).
- Turn a suspected inefficiency into a closed-form recurrence on a one-variable barrier problem, which gives a lower bound on iterations (pp. 19–21). Then confirm with the alternative option in the same code (Table 1, p. 21).

**Quotes**:
- "We should stress that failures of the line search Newton iteration (2.2) are observed in practical applications, and are not purely of academic interest." (p. 4)
- "We should stress that the interior method will not fail on Example 3, but that it will be inefficient." (p. 21)

### S077 · Enriched methods for large-scale unconstrained optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2002, doi:10.1023/a:1013756631822)
**Read level**: full. Read: abstract (p. 1), §§1–4 with the ENRICHED ALGORITHM and the ADJUST procedure (pp. 2–8), Tables 1–2 (pp. 9, 11), conclusion (p. 10) and references (pp. 10, 12). In both tables each problem row shows only two of the three columns. My check: the legible pairs sum to about 76.2k and 75.7k (after splitting the fused FLETCHCR and PENALTY2 entries). That is close to the totals 76224 and 75089 that the total row gives for HFN1 and HFN2. So the per-problem Enriched column is the one lost, and only the Enriched total, 34371, can be read (p. 11). The exponents of the gradient tolerance and of the HFN2 forcing rule are garbled (p. 8). First page checked: title and "José Luis Morales" and "Jorge Nocedal" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales (ITAM) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the "sophisticated implementation" (p. 4) that replaces the rigid earlier attempt of Byrd, Nocedal and Zhu, [2] in its references (pp. 3–4, 10). It builds on the preconditioners of [8, 9] (pp. 2, 4, 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: "much effort is expended in designing algorithms that do not require the setting of parameters" (p. 2). Hessian-free Newton (HFN) methods are notoriously sensitive to the termination rule of their inner CG iteration (p. 2). The strengths and weaknesses of HFN and L-BFGS are complementary: HFN needs few iterations but discards the curvature it gathers, while L-BFGS iterations are cheap but can be poor on ill-conditioned problems (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: interlace l L-BFGS steps with t HFN steps. One limited-memory matrix H(m) does two jobs: it preconditions the inner PCG of HFN and warm-starts the L-BFGS cycle (abstract, p. 3). L-BFGS and HFN are the special cases t = 0 and l = 0 (p. 3). Every member of the family is a preconditioned HFN iteration with a varying number of CG iterations; L-BFGS allows exactly one (pp. 4–5). The earlier attempt in [2] was rigid: 20 L-BFGS steps, then 1 HFN step with unpreconditioned CG and always 5 CG iterations (p. 3). The new ADJUST procedure sets l and t from in-run signals (pp. 5–7):
  - a Newton step is "profitable" when its step length lies in [0.8, 1];
  - an indefinite Hessian sends the method back to L-BFGS with t = 1;
  - a full cycle of profitable steps increases t by one;
  - force2 guarantees two consecutive HFN steps, because "the full benefit of limited memory preconditioning is obtained only if more than one HFN iteration is performed in succession" (p. 6);
  - the very first HFN step is capped at 5 CG iterations, and t starts at 2 (p. 6).
  The prose and the pseudocode, as extracted, disagree in two places. After indefiniteness the prose increases l "by 1" (p. 5), while ADJUST sets l ← min{(3/2)l, 30} (p. 7). After a small step the prose sets t = max{2, t − 1} (p. 6), while ADJUST uses max{2, k − 1} (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions**: the gradient is available but second derivatives are not (p. 2). Hessian–vector products come from finite differences or automatic differentiation (p. 4). CG stops at negative curvature without exploiting it (p. 4). There is no convergence theory.
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no proofs). The framing device is that the whole family is viewed as preconditioned HFN (pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Experiments**: all large unconstrained CUTE problems, starting from m = 20 and l = 15 (p. 7). The inner CG stops after 30 iterations (5 in the very first) or when the preconditioned residual ratio falls to 1/10 (p. 8). The competitors are HFN1 (Nash's rule) and HFN2 (a forcing rule from Dembo and Steihaug), with "the same quasi-Newton preconditioner as in the enriched method" (p. 8). DEC Personal Workstation, 512 Mb. The stopping rule is ‖g‖/max(1, ‖x‖) below a tolerance whose exponent is garbled (p. 8). Function/gradient evaluations total 34371 for Enriched against 76224 and 75089 for the two HFN methods (Table 2, p. 11). Other HFN variants were tried "but their performance was not better" (p. 10). Plain L-BFGS has no column in Tables 1–2 (pp. 9, 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm with a numerical study. The enriched method "should be considered as a serious competitor to Hessian-free Newton methods" (p. 10). ### S119 · iNEOS: An interactive environment for nonlinear optimization (Applied Numerical Mathematics 2002, doi:10.1016/s0168-9274(01)00063-0)
**Read level**: full. Read: abstract (p. 1), §§1–4 (pp. 2–9, 12), the figure pages (pp. 10–11, rotated and garbled) and the references (pp. 12–14). First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement (software-infrastructure note) · **Co-authors**: Marcel Good (Northwestern ECE), Jean-Pierre Goux (Optimization Technology Center) and Victor Pereyra (Weidlinger Associates) (p. 1); Todd Plantenga thanked (p. 12) · **Position in trajectory**: a side line of the OTC/NEOS years. It reuses the reverse-communication design of L-BFGS-B (pp. 3–4, 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: NEOS makes the user submit the whole model as AMPL, Fortran or C files (p. 2). That rules out three kinds of user: those whose models are proprietary, those whose evaluations are too slow to delegate, and those whose models are written in several languages (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: use the structure of the algorithm. A quasi-Newton iteration needs only x_k, f_k and g_k (and line-search trial values) to produce the next point, and "These arrays of real numbers do not reveal the nonlinear model" (p. 3). So the client keeps the simulation and the server runs the optimizer, here L-BFGS-B for bound constraints (pp. 3–4). The implementation uses CORBA. A Factory object lets new solvers be added, and the client–solver interface reduces to two methods, Init and NextIterate (pp. 5–7). A C/C++ library hides CORBA, so Fortran users can call it too (p. 7). Rejected alternatives are recorded with reasons. A Nexus prototype lacked object orientation, built-in multiple-client support and easy deployment. DCOM would have had to be bought and installed on Unix clients (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions (requirements)**: bound constraints only for now. Constrained solvers would need second derivatives sent from client to server (p. 4). Communication is assumed cheap next to the simulation, which takes minutes or hours: each exchange sends 2n + 1 floating-point numbers, and problems "of up to 1 million variables" are envisioned (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: parameter identification for a piezoelectric crystal model. There are 10 bound-constrained parameters in a weighted least-squares fit to FFTs of impedance samples, with finite-difference derivatives (pp. 8–9). The test is controlled: impedances are generated from target parameters, the start is perturbed by 7.5%, and each parameter should be recovered to within 1%. Each f/g evaluation takes about 10 minutes. The client was a 300 MHz Pentium II in California and the server a Sun Ultra in Illinois (p. 9). The result is reported only as "successfully completed", with fitted impedance curves "After 20 LBFGS iterations" (pp. 9–11). The quantitative recovery is deferred to [3] (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: software and infrastructure. Limits are printed. There is no user-level security yet: "Anyone has access to iNEOS and can view the data submitted to it" (p. 12). The API still exposes solver-specific details, and an XML common interface is proposed, with its overhead still to be measured (p. 12). General constraints are left for the future (pp. 4, 9, 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: the property that makes L-BFGS the general method, its need for only function and gradient values (Method 6, step 3), is reused here as a deployment property: the model stays hidden from the server (p. 3).
- Heuristic 9 evidence (card only): the test application comes from a practitioner's problem. The proprietary piezoelectric model (p. 9) is described in a Weidlinger Associates report co-authored by Pereyra ([3], p. 13), and the paper notes that ultrasound companies' finite-element models have had "only limited success" (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate (existing name): *algorithm information structure as a deployment interface*. Reverse communication becomes a remote service that hides the model (pp. 3, 7). Compare S098's single-call interface.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before building infrastructure, list what the algorithm needs per iteration (here 2n + 1 numbers). Size the communication against the evaluation time and decide on that basis whether a heavier but more robust middleware is acceptable (p. 5).
- Validate an identification pipeline on synthetic truth: generate data from known parameters, perturb the start by a stated amount (7.5%) and fix the recovery target in advance (1%) (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "In nonlinear optimization, an improved solution can be generated just by knowing the current iterate and the numerical values of the function and gradient at the current point." (p. 3)

### S075 · Feasible interior methods using slacks for nonlinear optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2003, doi:10.1023/a:1025136421370)
**Read level**: full. Read: abstract (p. 1), §§1–7 (pp. 2–20), acknowledgements (p. 20), the appendix table (pp. 20–25: some entries scrambled, legend read on p. 25) and the start of the references (p. 25). The threshold τ and the stopping tolerance have garbled exponents (p. 17). First page checked: title and "Jorge Nocedal" among the authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado) and Richard A. Waltz (Northwestern) (p. 1). Guanghui Liu (initial experiments), Marcelo Marazzi and José Luis Morales (the way the results are displayed) are thanked (p. 20) · **Position in trajectory**: KNITRO's feasible mode, built on the composite-step trust-region interior method (p. 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: some applications need iterates that satisfy the inequalities, either because the objective is defined only there or because an early stop must still return a feasible point (p. 2). Most NLP interior codes are infeasible (p. 2), and "To our knowledge there have been no studies comparing the performance of feasible versus infeasible interior point methods" (p. 17).
- **D2 Core idea**: turn an infeasible slack-based method into a feasible one by resetting the slacks, s_T ← g(x_T), after each trial step and before the merit function is evaluated (pp. 4–5, 20). The two modes differ only in "the two instructions enclosed in boxes" and use the same linear systems and data structures. Solving the larger system with MA27 costs little more, "if at all" (p. 5). The reset can be applied to any subset of the inequalities (pp. 4, 20). For exact primal–dual Newton steps, the reset method is equivalent to a classical Fiacco–McCormick barrier method (pp. 6–7).
  In a trust-region method the reset can stall when equalities are present. The remedy is the Feasible Step Conditions: set r_g = 0 whenever g(x) − s = 0 (p. 12). In KNITRO only the Cauchy direction of the normal step needs to change. It is recomputed from system (5.30) so that it satisfies the linearized inequalities, makes standard Cauchy-like progress on h, and lies in the range of the scaled Jacobian (pp. 12–14).
- **D3 Assumptions**: smooth f, h and g (p. 3). The merit function (2.4) uses an infeasibility measure χ, with value ∞ for s ≤ 0 (p. 3). Step generation and merit function are assumed compatible for small steps (p. 4). Theorem 4.1 needs χ and the Jacobian Lipschitz, and a step that satisfies (4.15) (p. 10). Corollary 5.1 needs ν sufficiently large, which the paper calls "not restrictive since the type of adjustment described above is commonly done in practice"; it also needs a point that is not stationary for ‖h‖² and a small enough radius (pp. 14–15).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Theorem 4.1: if the step satisfies the linearized inequalities, the reset perturbation is O(‖d_x‖²), which the first-order decrease γ‖d‖ dominates, so a small enough step is accepted (pp. 10–11).
  - Backtracking cannot remove a violation of (4.15), because every scalar multiple of d keeps it (p. 12).
  - Corollary 5.1 transfers the result to the modified dogleg (pp. 14–15).
  - When A_h is rank-deficient, dependent rows and columns are deleted from (5.30) (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**:
  - GAUSSELM (385 nonlinear equalities, 750 linear inequalities), before the repair: from iteration 18 every trial point is feasible, yet the merit function rises. The reset perturbation ‖δ_s‖ jumps to 2.345e-01 at iteration 5 and later stays comparable to Δ, which falls to about 1.2e-14 by iteration 57, when the run fails (Table 1, pp. 8–9).
  - GAUSSELM after the repair: solved in 102 iterations, with ‖δ_s‖ at unit-roundoff level (Table 2, p. 16).
  - On 153 problems with equalities, the modified and unmodified steps differ on 45. The modified step fails on 3 of them and the unmodified one on 17. On the 25 solved by both, the modified step needs "significantly fewer iterations" (p. 16).
  - Feasible against infeasible KNITRO on 216 CUTE problems: Sun Ultra 5/10, KNITRO's default stopping test on a normalized KKT condition (p. 17). They solve 181 and 179 problems, and mostly fail on the same ones. On 58 problems feasible mode was never entered. Figure 1 shows sorted log2 ratios of function evaluations for the 119 problems where both versions found the same solution and feasible mode was entered. On three problems the feasible version was dramatically better, but the runs do not suggest this "is due to the properties of the feasible iteration" (p. 18). Limits were 1000 iterations and 60 CPU minutes, and failure codes are listed (Table 3, p. 25).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: a null result is reported as a surprise and then read as good news for the option ("very positive", p. 18). A confound is disclosed in the same section: the slack adjustment (6.35) used by both versions "dampens the disparity between the two methods" (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithmic framework, a convergence safeguard and a feasible option in KNITRO (pp. 17–19). Design rules are printed: keep the identity of inequalities and slacks in the problem formulation (pp. 19–20); reset before the merit function is evaluated (p. 20). Limits: the simpler alternative of rejecting infeasible trial points can also fail, and a step modification for it was "not explored", although it is called "plausible" (pp. 16–17). Using the KNITRO slack adjustment in both arms narrows the comparison (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the infeasible slack-based engine is kept and only one component changes, the slack reset (p. 5). In KNITRO only the Cauchy direction of the normal step changes: "the change needed in the step computation of KNITRO occurs only in the normal component" (p. 14) (step 3).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: method-level comparison inside one code base, "while keeping all other algorithmic features the same" (p. 17). - Method 4 ✅ evidence: the authors' own failure case (GAUSSELM, Table 1, p. 9) becomes the specification for the Feasible Step Conditions and the new Cauchy step (pp. 12–16). **Transferable techniques**:
