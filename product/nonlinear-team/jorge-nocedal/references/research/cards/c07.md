### S018 · Sample size selection in optimization methods for machine learning (Mathematical Programming 2012, 10.1007/s10107-012-0572-5)
**Read level**: full (preprint pp. 1–30). Read: abstract, §§1–3, all of §4 including the proofs of Theorems 4.1, 4.2 and Corollary 4.3 line by line, §5 (Algorithms 5.1–5.2, Table 5.1), §6 (Algorithm 6.1), §7 related work, §8 final remarks. Reference list skimmed. Figures 5.1, 5.2, 6.1 and 6.2 exist only as captions in the text layer (pp. 19, 21, 26), so the curves were not read; statements about them come from the authors' text.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado CS), Gillian M. Chin (Northwestern IEMS; NSERC fellowship and a Google grant), Yuchen Wu (Google) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the early machine-learning period. The paper presents itself as "a continuation of the work in [7]" (Byrd, Chin, Neveitt & Nocedal, SIOPT 2011, stochastic Hessian information) (pp. 2–3). Google supplied the speech data (p. 18) and Will Neveitt is thanked (p. 28). S006 later cites this paper as a possible remedy for large-batch training (S006 p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a changed trade-off. Parallel computing makes batch steps whose function and gradient are computed in parallel competitive with cheap sequential SGD steps (p. 2). The question is "the delicate issue of sample selection in the evaluation of function and gradients" (p. 2). The sole focus is batch and mini-batch methods, "motivated by the potential of function/gradient parallelism" (p. 3). A second entry is the subsampled Hessian Newton-CG method of [7]. There the open question is how small the Hessian sample can be, and [7] fixed both the gradient sample and the number of CG iterations (pp. 2, 15). A third entry is L1-regularized models (pp. 2, 21–22). The rival claim, stated in §4, is Bottou & Bousquet's conclusion that, for large-scale learning, "the stochastic gradient descent method is to be preferred" (pp. 6–7).
  - Transferable: enter a field dominated by one method by naming the change in hardware or cost model that shifts the trade-off, then attack the specific decision (here, when to grow the sample) that the rival analysis did not cover (p. 7: Bottou–Bousquet "does not, however, cover dynamic batch algorithms").
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The descent condition δ_S(w) = ‖∇J_S − ∇J‖ ≤ θ‖∇J_S‖ (3.2) needs ∇J, which is unavailable. It is replaced by a variance test: E[δ_S²] = ‖Var(∇J_S)‖₁ (3.4), with the finite-population formula (3.5), the sample variance (3.6), and N → ∞ giving test (3.9) (pp. 4–5). If (3.9) fails, the new size is |Ŝ| = ‖Var‖₁/(θ²‖∇J_S‖²) (3.12), under the stated assumption (3.10) that variance and gradient norm change little (p. 5). Algorithm 3.1 keeps the gradient step and a line search "deliberately vague" (p. 6). For Newton-CG (Algorithm 5.2), the Hessian sample H_k ⊂ S_k is held at a fixed ratio R = |H_k|/|S_k| "to avoid having too many parameters to tune" (p. 15). The new CG stopping test balances the CG residual against the Hessian subsampling error Δ_H(w; d) (5.4)–(5.5). That error is estimated by the variance of the Hessian-vector products (5.6), computed once, for d = p₀, and scaled by ‖d_j‖²/‖p₀‖² (pp. 15–17, Algorithm 5.1). The scaling also stops CG before the direction becomes too long when the sampled Hessian is nearly singular (p. 17). For L1 (Algorithm 6.1, p. 25): an "infinitesimal search" along the minimum-norm subgradient picks the orthant (6.3)–(6.7), a subsampled-Hessian CG solves on the free variables (6.10), and a projected backtracking line search follows (6.12) (pp. 22–24). The orthant rule "coincides with that generated by the OWL method" (p. 24). What changed relative to OWL: Newton-CG replaces L-BFGS in the subspace, and OWL's sign-alignment step (6.14) is dropped (p. 24). What did not change: gradient or Newton-CG steps with Wolfe or Armijo line searches on J_S (pp. 6, 18, 24).
  - Transferable: when a test needs an unavailable exact quantity, write the test, take its expectation, and replace the expectation with a sample variance from data you already hold. Use the same inequality to size the next sample (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Linear prediction f(w; x) = wᵀx and a convex loss (p. 3). For the analysis, J is twice continuously differentiable and uniformly convex, λ‖d‖² ≤ dᵀ∇²J d ≤ L‖d‖² (4.2) (p. 7). The authors admit this "may be seen as a limitation" for merely convex losses such as logistic ones, and defend it by the usual ½γwᵀw regularization and by its showing the role of L/λ (p. 10). A fixed step α = (1−θ)/L is used "because the latter [Armijo] is more difficult to analyze", a convenience assumption they state (p. 7). The stochastic analysis assumes a bounded per-sample gradient variance ‖Var(∇ℓ(w_k, i))‖₁ ≤ ω (4.22) and imposes geometric growth n_k = a^k (4.23) rather than the variance test, "because of sample variation" (p. 11). The L1 part assumes a fixed sample |S| "for simplicity" (p. 22).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: Theorem 4.1 (deterministic; pp. 8–10): the relative-accuracy condition ‖g_k − ∇J‖ ≤ θ‖g_k‖ gives ∇Jᵀg_k ≥ (1−θ)‖g_k‖² (4.14). Taylor's theorem with step (1−θ)/L then gives J_{k+1} ≤ J_k − (β/L)‖∇J_k‖² (4.16), with β = (1−θ)²/(2(1+θ)²). Combined with the gradient-dominance inequality ‖∇J‖² ≥ λ(J − J*) (4.5), derived from uniform convexity through the averaged Hessian H_k (4.3)–(4.4), this gives a linear rate and O((L/βλ) log(1/ε)) iterations (4.11) (pp. 7–10). Derivation of the analysable rule (p. 11): imposing the variance test at every iteration together with bound (4.12) implies n_k ≥ O((1−βλ/L)^(−k)). Hence "the sample size n_k should grow geometrically", and that rate is imposed as (4.23). Theorem 4.2 (p. 12): induction on E[J(w_k) − J*] ≤ Cρ^k, with ρ = max{1−λ/(4L), 1/a} and C = max{J(w₀) − J*, 2ω/λ}, from the one-step bound (4.27), which uses E‖g_k‖² = ‖∇J‖² + ‖Var(g_k)‖₁ and Var(g_k) ≤ ω/n_k. Corollary 4.3 (pp. 13–14): sum the geometric series of sample gradient evaluations. Choosing a ≤ (1−λ/(4L))⁻¹ makes τ = log a/(−log ρ) = 1 and the total work O(Lmω/(λ²ε))-type, i.e. O(1/ε) gradient evaluations. "any overestimate" of κ suffices (p. 14). Table 4.1 compares this bound with Bottou–Bousquet's fixed-sample and SGD bounds, using the stated approximation ν̄ ≈ ω/λ. The conclusion is that the bounds differ by κ against κ², so the method is "competitive" with SGD (p. 14). Gap: the analysed iteration (fixed step 1/L, imposed a^k growth) is not the algorithm that is run. The authors say the Newton-CG method is "an extension of Algorithm 3.1 and not of the idealized variant" (p. 15). No convergence result is given for Algorithm 6.1 (pp. 24, 27). Minor slip: p. 11 refers to "the deterministic Theorem 6.1" for Theorem 4.1.
  - Transferable: to analyse an adaptive (test-driven) sample-size rule, first derive the growth rate the test would force under a bounded-variance assumption, then analyse the idealized variant that imposes that rate; total work comes from summing a geometric series (pp. 11–14).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: There is one application, a Google speech-recognition multi-class logistic model: N = 168,776 training points, m = 10,191 parameters, 129 classes, 79 features (p. 18). Dynamic Algorithm 5.2 runs against Algorithm 5.2-Static (the method of [7], fixed 10 CG iterations) at |S| = N and |S| = 0.05N. Settings: R = 0.1, initial sample 1% of N, θ = 0.5 (p. 19). The x-axis is "the number of accessed data points", counting function, gradient and Hessian-vector products. The y-axis is the probability of correct classification exp(−J) (p. 19). Estimator check, Table 5.1 (p. 20): the sample-variance estimate A is tabulated against the true gradient error B, and the Hessian estimate Y against the true Z, for 17 iterations, after which |S_k| = N. Objective 0.123 against an optimum of 0.136 shows that "most of the improvement" came in the dynamic regime (p. 20). Sensitivity: four θ values, larger θ better, θ = 0.75 "more erratic" (pp. 20–21). L1 test: a larger speech problem, N = 191,607, m = 30,315, chosen "to admit a useful sparse solution" (p. 24). The comparison is OWL from the third-party LibLBFGS at memory 5 and 20 against Algorithm 6.1 with maxcg = 10, R = 0.05, |S| = N, w₀ = 0 (p. 25). Outcome: "only moderately faster than OWL" in objective, "much more effective" in sparsity (p. 25). No repeated runs, no other data sets, and no SGD baseline in any experiment, although SGD is the rival of §4 (my reading of pp. 18–25).
  - Transferable: before trusting an estimator inside an algorithm, tabulate it next to the true quantity on one problem where the truth is computable (Table 5.1, p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against Bottou & Bousquet [6]: their analysis "does not, however, cover dynamic batch algorithms", and the paper shows a dynamic method "as good as" SGD in complexity (p. 7). Toward its own line: an extension of [7] in two respects, dynamic gradient samples and a CG stop test (p. 15). Against OWL [2], a direct critique: "the convergence proof given in [2] is not correct, due to an invalid assumption made in Proposition 4", and "A simple counter-example can be constructed" (p. 24); the counterexample itself is not given. The OWL orthant selection is re-derived as "a version of a gradient projection step", which "has the advantage of suggesting variants of the algorithm and mechanisms for establishing global convergence" (pp. 25–27). Related work comes last (§7, p. 27): Shapiro–Wardi, Kleywegt et al., Shapiro–Homem-de-Mello, Homem-de-Mello, Deng–Ferris, and Bastin–Cirillo–Toint. Shapiro & Homem-de-Mello's confidence-ellipsoid rule is dismissed as "not computationally feasible" at scale because it needs the sample covariance matrix (p. 27). First-order L1 methods are acknowledged as "very effective", but excluded "in keeping with the main theme of this paper" (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract is a three-part list ("The first part ... The second part ... The third part", p. 1). Practical algorithm and analysed variant are separated explicitly (pp. 6, 15). Hedging in the body is measured: "reasonably accurate measures" (p. 20), "only moderately faster" (p. 25). The final remarks are stronger: "our algorithm is able to outperform the OWL algorithm ... both in terms of sparsity and objective value" (p. 28). Each design choice is justified in one sentence, for example the fixed R "to avoid having too many parameters to tune" (p. 15) and a model rather than F_S "due to the high cost of evaluating the objective function" (p. 22). The running head differs from the title (pp. 2–30), which suggests an earlier working title (inference).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic (a variance-based sample-growth test and rule; a CG stop test matched to the Hessian sampling error; a two-phase L1 Newton-CG), theoretical (linear convergence in expectation and O(1/ε) total work for the idealized variant), and numerical on Google data (pp. 1, 27–28). Stated limits and future work: dynamic sampling for L1 "is the subject of a future paper" (p. 22) and "remains to be explored" (p. 28); a more elaborate gradient-projection search is deferred because its design "is, however, not simple" (p. 27); uniform convexity (p. 10). Unstated limits: one data set per experiment, single runs, no SGD comparison, no convergence result for Algorithm 6.1 after OWL's alignment safeguard is removed (pp. 24–27).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ (evidence): the classical engine (gradient or Newton-CG step with a Wolfe/Armijo line search) is kept, and only the sampling decision and the CG stopping test become regime-aware. Each is driven by an explicit sample-variance estimate: (3.9)/(3.12) for the gradient, (5.6) for the Hessian (pp. 4–6, 15–17). Recovery is weaker than Method 3 asks: the sample only grows, and nothing re-estimates or backs off (Algorithms 3.1 and 5.2, pp. 6, 18).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): the field's conclusion that SGD "is to be preferred" (p. 7) is put on trial with a dynamic batch method whose bound is "competitive" (Table 4.1, p. 14). OWL's alignment step, a safeguard motivated by convergence, is dropped because "it slows down the iteration" (p. 24).
- Method 5 ⚠ (variant): the analysed method is an idealized variant (fixed step 1/L, imposed a^k growth), not the variance-test algorithm with a line search that is run (pp. 6, 11, 15). A complexity bound is used to argue competitiveness with SGD (p. 14), the use SKILL.md warning sign 3 cautions against. The L1 method has no convergence result (pp. 24, 27).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): cost is counted in accessed data points (pp. 19, 25), and the rival OWL runs in a public third-party implementation at two memory sizes (p. 25). But there is one problem and one run per comparison, no handicap on the authors' side, and the main rival (SGD) is absent from the experiments (pp. 18–25).
- Method 4 ✅ (evidence, link inferred): the two deferred items, dynamic sampling for L1 and a better active-set prediction, are written down as future papers (pp. 22, 27–28). INDEX.md lists later ℓ1 second-order works (S087, 2015, "flexible active-set strategy"; S089, 2016, "active-set prediction"); the link rests on their titles only, since those papers are not read here.
- new-pattern candidate: "Error balancing for inner solves": stop the inner iterative solver when its residual reaches the error already committed by the outer approximation (here the Hessian subsampling error), estimated from data in hand (pp. 15–17).
- new-pattern candidate: "Re-derive a rival heuristic as a classical method": OWL's orthant choice recast as a gradient-projection step, to expose variants and convergence mechanisms (pp. 24–27).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Replace an unavailable relative-accuracy test ‖g − ∇J‖ ≤ θ‖g‖ by its variance form ‖Var_S‖₁/|S| ≤ θ²‖g‖², and size the next sample from the same inequality (3.9)–(3.12) (pp. 4–5).
- Stop CG when ‖r_j‖² ≤ γ‖d_j‖², where γ is the per-sample variance of Hessian-vector products along p₀ divided by |H|‖p₀‖², computed once per outer iteration (Algorithm 5.1, pp. 16–17).
- Fix the Hessian/gradient sample ratio so that the CG Hessian-vector cost does not exceed one gradient evaluation, which makes a Newton-CG iteration cost less than two gradient steps (p. 15).
- Plot every method against accessed data points (function, gradient and Hessian-vector products counted together) instead of iterations or wall time on one machine (p. 19).
- Tabulate estimator against truth over the iterations of a real run (Table 5.1, p. 20).

**Quotes**:
- "The focus of this section is on the development of a practical algorithm, not on establishing complexity bounds, and therefore our method is an extension of Algorithm 3.1 and not of the idealized variant given by (4.23)-(4.24)." (p. 15)
- "The alignment is motivated by the need to achieve global convergence properties, but our computational experience indicates that it slows down the iteration." (p. 24)

### S053 · An interior point method for nonlinear programming with infeasibility detection capabilities (Optimization Methods and Software 2014, 10.1080/10556788.2013.858156)
**Read level**: full for the body (manuscript pp. 1–19): §1 introduction, §2 solver tests (Tables 1–3), §3 certificates, §4 algorithm (Algorithms 1–3, initialization, extra switching rules), §5 experiments (Tables 4–5, Fig. 1 caption), §6 line-search extension (Algorithm 4, Tables 6–7, Fig. 2 caption), §7 final remarks, references. Appendices A–B (Tables 8–9, pp. 20–35, every CUTEr run) were skimmed: I filtered for the rows where the old and new terminations differ, and read the first page of each table in full. Figures 1–2 exist only as captions in the text layer (pp. 14, 18), so the plots were not read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Figen Oztoprak (Sabanci University and Northwestern EECS), Richard A. Waltz (USC Epstein ISE); Nocedal is the corresponding author, at Northwestern EECS (p. 1). Richard Byrd is thanked "for many useful suggestions" (p. 17) · **Position in trajectory**: the interior-point counterpart of the Byrd–Curtis–Nocedal infeasibility-detection SQP work (cited as the 2008 TR [2], p. 17). It is built inside KNITRO 8.0 as extensions of knitro/cg and knitro/direct (pp. 1, 12–13). Test Set 2 comes from the Curtis–Oztoprak collection of infeasible problems (Northwestern OTC TR 2011/05, [12], pp. 3, 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The application pressure comes first. Infeasible problems "arise often in practice": parameter sweeps, and NLP subproblems inside branch-and-bound or branch-and-cut, where the solver "is typically run thousands of times", with many infeasible subproblems (p. 2). The entry is an empirical snapshot of the state of the art (§2, pp. 3–4). Seventeen small infeasible problems are run on ten solver/algorithm combinations (eight reported in the tables), and the interior-point codes knitro/direct and knitro/cg (the authors' own) come out "quite poor" (p. 4). Stated motivation: "the desire to build effective feasibility detection capabilities for interior point methods" (p. 4).
  - Transferable: open a solver paper with a reproducible snapshot of current codes (your own included) on the failure class, and let your own code's losses motivate the paper (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The key insight is that the normal component of a step-decomposition (trust-region) interior-point step already estimates θ(x), the best first-order reduction of infeasibility inside the trust region. Line-search primal-dual methods lack this, because the fraction-to-boundary rule and the line search shorten the step, so the achievable reduction cannot be measured (pp. 2, 5). The engine is kept: the knitro/cg algorithm of [5, 3], with normal step (4.3) (ξ = 0.8, κ = 0.005), tangential/total step (4.4), merit function φ = ϕ + ν‖c‖ (4.6), slack reset (4.8) and least-squares multipliers (p. 6). What is added: a feasibility mode that minimizes ‖w(x)‖₁ through relaxation variables and its own barrier problem (4.10)–(4.11). It is solved by the same step decomposition after re-writing (4.13)–(4.14) in the form of (4.2) (pp. 7–8). Switching rules: Main→Feasible when the linearized residual after the step is ≥ δ‖c‖ (4.15). Feasible→Main when it is ≤ δ̄‖c‖ (4.16). δ = 0.9 and δ̄ = 0.1 (p. 8). Extra anti-cycling rules (p. 12) require: ‖w_k‖ ≥ ζ‖w_{k−1}‖ with ζ = 0.99; at least 3 main-mode iterations; conditions holding on two consecutive iterations; and, before returning to main mode, ‖w‖ ≤ (1−σ)‖w(x_j)‖. Explicit warm-start rules cover each mode switch: barrier parameter, trust radius scaled by √(n+m+2t)/√n, penalty, slacks, multipliers (pp. 10, 12). Three exits: KKT point, infeasible stationary point, LICQ failure (Algorithm 1, pp. 9–10). Line-search extension (§6, Algorithm 4): knitro/direct already discards a primal-dual step when the step length is tiny or the inertia is wrong, and falls back to the knitro/cg step. The switching test (4.15) is attached to that fallback (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable: choose (or keep) the algorithm architecture whose by-products measure what your new feature needs. Here the step decomposition was chosen over a line-search primal-dual method because only it yields θ(x) (pp. 2, 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Smooth f, g, h (3.1) (p. 4). Infeasibility measure ‖w(x)‖ with w = (g⁺, h) (3.2) (p. 4). The algorithm starts in main mode "assuming that the nonlinear program has a feasible solution" (p. 9). No convergence assumptions are stated, because no analysis is given (p. 16). The constants δ, δ̄ are admitted to be a sensitivity: the principal conditions "can be sensitive to the choice of the constants" (p. 12). Initialization is admitted to be fragile in general: "It is well known that interior point methods are very sensitive to the initialization of some of these parameters" (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a for theorems; the paper proves nothing. The design device is the first-order characterization of an infeasible stationary point, ‖w(x)‖ > 0 and θ(x) = 0 for any Δ > 0 (3.3), turned into a relative test θ(x_k) ≤ δ‖w(x_k)‖ (p. 5). The termination logic uses the KKT residuals F^M and F^F of the NLP and of the feasibility problem; ‖w‖ > 0 at a stationary point of (4.10) is accepted "as a certificate of local infeasibility" (p. 9). The LICQ-failure exit relies on Theorem 3 of [3] (p. 9). The convergence analysis is deferred: "In a future study, we analyze the convergence properties of this approach" (p. 16).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: §2 snapshot (pp. 3–4): SNOPT 7.2-10, FILTER 20020316, KNITRO 7.0 (active, CG, direct), IPOPT 3.8.3, LANCELOT and MINOS 5.51 were run on NEOS through AMPL at their default stop tests. LOQO and PENNON detected no infeasibility at all and are omitted from the tables (p. 3). Iterations are counted in each solver's own unit: Jacobian evaluations for SNOPT, restoration iterations in parentheses for FILTER (p. 3). Verdict: FILTER and knitro/active "the most efficient and show the most consistent performance". Surprise is recorded: knitro/active's strength "was somewhat unexpected" (pp. 3–4). §5 (pp. 12–14): KNITRO 8.0 runs with options alg=2, presolve=0, maxiter=3000, maxtime_real=500, with bar_switchrule=1 (old) against bar_switchrule=2 (new) (pp. 12–13). By my count of Table 4 (p. 13), the old code detects infeasibility on 8 of the 17 problems and the new one on 17 of 17, with far fewer iterations (e.g., isolated 3000 → 14). Non-regression on all 430 constrained CUTEr problems, Table 5 (p. 14): 398 optimal/optimal; 1 optimal → infeasible; 1 failure → infeasible; 4 failure → optimal; 4 optimal → failure; 20 failure/failure; 2 unbounded. The 391 problems on which both runs reach the same objective (relative difference ≤ 0.01) are compared with Morales's log₂ iteration-ratio plot, "more informative than the Dolan-Moré performance profiles ... when comparing only two solvers" (pp. 13–14). §6 line search, Table 6 (p. 17), my count: detection rises from 11 of 17 to 14 of 17. One regression goes unmentioned in the text: powellbs modified is detected by the old code but not by the new. Portfolio and peigen are missed by both. Table 7 (p. 17): 410 optimal/optimal, 1 optimal → infeasible, 4 failure → infeasible, 3 failure → optimal, 3 optimal → failure. The text summarizes these as "greatly improves" and "does not adversely affect" (p. 15). There is no CPU time anywhere. Appendix rows show per-problem costs that the averages hide: knitro/cg aljazzaf 56 → 504 iterations, core1 96 → 415, corkscrw 412 → 81, cresc132 "term infeas" → optimal (pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable: show a new feature's harmlessness with a full termination cross-table (old outcome × new outcome) over the whole standard set, then compare cost only on the problems where both runs end at the same objective (Tables 5 and 7, pp. 13–14, 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper surveys how each school handles infeasibility (p. 2). Active set: SNOPT's switch to an SL1QP penalty mode, FILTER's restoration phase triggered by an infeasible trust-region subproblem, the single-phase penalty SQP of Gould–Robinson, of Byrd–Curtis–Nocedal with steering, and of Yamashita–Yabe. SLQP: Chin–Fletcher's filter; knitro/active's penalty. Interior: LOQO's penalty, IPOPT's filter plus restoration, Curtis's interior-penalty method. Augmented Lagrangian: LANCELOT, MINOS. Its own place is asserted, not argued: "distinct from other methods proposed in the literature" (p. 2). Rivals are credited where they win: FILTER "performs well thanks to a carefully designed feasibility restoration phase" (p. 4). IPOPT's restoration phase is judged "not consistently successful" (p. 4). Citation slip: "Curtis [2] proposes an interior-penalty method", but [2] is the Byrd–Curtis–Nocedal SQP TR (pp. 2, 17). Self-citation is dense ([3]–[7], [30]) because the method lives inside KNITRO (pp. 6, 17–19).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Short body (pp. 1–17) built around pseudo-code (Algorithms 1–4, pp. 10–11, 16). The contribution is stated in the abstract: "We give particular attention to the reliability of the switching mechanism" (p. 1). Results are summarized in one-line verdicts: "It is clear that the new algorithm has much improved feasibility detection capabilities" (p. 13); "there is no loss of robustness" (p. 13). The switching rules are said to have "a solid theoretical underpinning" (p. 12), with no proof in the paper. The final remarks are two sentences plus one design idea (pp. 16–17). Self-criticism of the authors' own codes is plain (p. 4). Slip: the switching constants are introduced as "¯δ > δ > 0" but set to δ = 0.9 and δ̄ = 0.1, the reverse order, which is the one the hysteresis logic of (4.15)–(4.16) needs (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and software: two KNITRO extensions plus a benchmark snapshot (pp. 1, 3–4, 12–17). Stated limits: no convergence analysis yet (p. 16); sensitivity to δ, δ̄, handled by the extra rules (p. 12); general initialization sensitivity of IPMs (p. 10). Future direction: a feasible interior method in feasibility mode that keeps satisfied inequalities satisfied, so that the relaxation variables r are no longer needed (p. 17). Unstated limits: small infeasible test sets (n ≤ 251), iteration counts only, and line-search losses left in the tables (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ (evidence): a known failure mode of KNITRO's interior algorithms (no infeasibility handling, shown in Tables 2–3, p. 4) becomes the paper and ships as KNITRO extensions selected by an option, bar_switchrule=2 (pp. 1, 13). The paper's own diagnosis, that these algorithms "do not contain any features for handling infeasibility" (p. 4), restates the 2006 KNITRO limitation (doi:10.1007/0-387-30065-1_4) and so supports the timing-inferred arrow in SKILL.md Method 4; it does not quote the 2006 text.
- Method 3 ✅ (evidence): the step-decomposition engine is kept, and one component (mode control) is added. The old and new versions are compared inside one code base with identical options except the switch rule (pp. 12–13). Against Method 1 step 7: iteration units differ across solvers in §2 (p. 3); the line-search regression on powellbs modified and four feasible CUTEr problems ending "infeasible" appear only in Tables 6–7 (p. 17); there is no CPU time.

### S006 · On large-batch training for deep learning: Generalization gap and sharp minima (arXiv preprint 2016; ICLR 2017, arXiv:1609.04836)
**Read level**: full (arXiv v2 pp. 1–16): main text §§1–4, references, Appendices A (data sets), B (architectures), C (performance model), D (curvilinear plots), E (remedies: data augmentation, conservative training, robust/adversarial training). Figures 1–8 exist only as axis labels and captions in the text layer (pp. 3, 5–9, 14, 16), so the curves were not read; statements about them come from the authors' text. Tables 1–7 were read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Nitish Shirish Keskar (Northwestern; the work was done "when author was an intern at Intel Corporation"), Dheevatsa Mudigere (Intel, Bangalore), Mikhail Smelyanskiy and Ping Tak Peter Tang (Intel, Santa Clara) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the deep-learning end of the machine-learning period. It cites the group's sampling paper (Byrd et al. 2012 = S018) as a prospective remedy (p. 9) and the Bottou–Curtis–Nocedal survey (pp. 2, 10). The paper is an empirical study with no algorithm of its own.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a practitioners' observation: with larger batches "there is a degradation in the quality of the model, as measured by its ability to generalize" (abstract, p. 1; LeCun et al. 2012 cited on p. 2). The cost model is the motivation: SGD's small batches limit parallelism, and a large-batch method without the gap "would have the ability to scale to a much larger number of nodes" (p. 2; App. C, p. 13). The size of the problem is measured first, with a gap "as high as 5% even for smaller networks" (p. 2). The question is causal: "We investigate the cause for this generalization drop" (p. 1).
  - Transferable: start an empirical paper by measuring the size of the practitioners' effect on your own runs before explaining it (p. 2; Table 2, p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and design of the study**: Four candidate causes are listed (p. 3): (i) over-fitting, (ii) saddle points, (iii) lack of exploration (zooming in on the minimizer closest to x₀), (iv) qualitatively different minimizers. The data "supports the last two conjectures" (p. 3). The main claim, set off in a box, is that large-batch methods converge to sharp minimizers, with "a significant number of large positive eigenvalues", and small-batch methods to flat ones (p. 3). The study uses instruments, not an algorithm: (a) 1-D parametric plots along the line, and along a curved path, between the small-batch (SB) and large-batch (LB) solutions (pp. 4–6, 13–14); (b) a sharpness metric (4), the relative rise of f maximized over a box C around x, with half-widths ε(|(A⁺x)_i| + 1) "to ensure invariance of sharpness to problem dimension and sparsity", in the full space (A = I) and in random 100-dimensional subspaces. It is computed inexactly by 10 iterations of L-BFGS-B, and is interpreted as related to the largest eigenvalue or to a Ritz value of ∇²f (pp. 5–6). The probes are designed to discriminate between causes. Over-fitting is excluded by training/testing curves with no peak-then-decay (pp. 4–5). A batch-size sweep finds thresholds (≈ 15000 for F2, ≈ 500 for C1) (p. 7). Warm-started ("piggybacked") LB runs from each of 100 SB epochs show accuracy improving and sharpness dropping after enough SB epochs (p. 7). The ratio ‖x_s − x₀‖/‖x_l − x₀‖ lies in 3–10 (p. 9). Sharpness-versus-loss trajectories suggest "an exploration phase followed by convergence to a flat minimizer" for SB (p. 9).
  - Transferable: list the rival explanations as numbered conjectures and, for each experiment, say which conjecture it supports or rules out (pp. 3–4, 7, 9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Finite-sum nonconvex training (1) (p. 1). "SB" means ADAM with 256 points and "LB" means ADAM with 10% of the training data, in both regimes (p. 4). Networks are trained "without any budget or limits, until the loss function ceased to improve" (p. 4). "Minimizer" means the final iterate. The term is used "loosely" (p. 5), and second-order optimality is not checked anywhere in the text. The link from sharpness to generalization is taken from Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, MDL theory, the Bayesian view and free Gibbs energy (p. 3), not tested. The metric is called "although imperfect" (p. 5), and its spectral interpretation holds "Assuming ε to be small enough" (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a for proofs; there are no theorems. Analytical devices: the box-constrained sharpness definition (3)–(4) with its dimension- and sparsity-invariance scaling (p. 5); the reading of the metric as the top Hessian eigenvalue (A = I) or a projected Ritz value (random A) (p. 6); the idealized parallel performance model I_l/I_s < B_s/(f_s(P)B_l). For example, with f_s(P) = 0.2 and B_s/B_l = 0.1, the LB method must converge in at most half as many iterations (App. C, p. 13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Six networks: F1 MNIST and F2 TIMIT (fully connected); C1 and C3 (AlexNet-like) and C2 and C4 (VGG-like) on CIFAR-10/100 (Table 1, p. 4; App. A–B, p. 12). Each experiment was run 5 times from random starts, with mean ± standard deviation reported (p. 4). Table 2 (p. 4): training accuracy is similar in both regimes, and testing accuracy is lower for LB on all six networks (e.g., C4 63.08% against 57.81%). Sharpness differs by 1–2 orders of magnitude between SB and LB (p. 6; Tables 3–4, p. 7). The parametric plots use one randomly chosen SB/LB pair (p. 5). Remedies (App. E). Data augmentation (Table 6, p. 14): augmented LB on C1–C4 reaches 82.50/90.26/53.03/65.88% against SB baselines (also trained on augmented data) of 83.63/89.82/54.55/63.05%, so it matches or beats SB on C2 and C4, while "the sharpness of the minima still exists" (p. 13). Conservative training (proximal subproblem, 3 ADAM iterations, λ = 10⁻³; Table 7, p. 15): a "statistically significant improvement" that "does not solve the problem of sensitivity" (p. 15). Adversarial and stability training: "did not improve generalization" (p. 16). Robust optimization: "not feasible in our context" (SOCP per iteration) (p. 15). Claims made without data: other optimizers (ADAGRAD, SGD, adaQN) "led to similar results" (p. 4); other networks and settings "showed similar behavior" (pp. 3–4). Code was released for the parametric plots only (footnote, p. 4). Slips: the conservative-training results are said to be "presented in Figure 7" but are in Table 7 (p. 15); App. B.4 describes C2 as "The C3 network" (p. 12).
  - Transferable: probe a basin hypothesis with warm starts. Run the competing regime from the other regime's iterate at each epoch, and plot accuracy and the diagnostic against the warm-start epoch (Figure 5, p. 8).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on flat-minima work (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997), MDL (Rissanen), MacKay's Bayesian view and Chaudhari et al.'s Gibbs-energy view (p. 3), and uses Goodfellow et al.'s visualization (p. 3). It sets aside the convex-analysis sense of "sharp minimum" (Ferris 1988) as "not useful for our purposes" (p. 5). It reports itself "in alignment" with loss-surface theory (Choromanska et al.; Soudry & Carmon; Lee et al.), but does not know whether those models say anything about sharp minimizers (p. 9). Remedies are borrowed from ML practice: Li et al. 2014's proximal "conservative" step, Zhang et al., Mobahi, Goodfellow et al.'s adversarial training, and Zheng et al.'s stability training (pp. 15–16). The group's own dynamic sampling (Byrd et al. 2012; Friedlander & Schmidt 2012) is offered as a "prospective remedy", supported by the warm-start result (p. 9). The paper is written for an ML venue: the related work is the ML loss-surface literature, not optimization theory.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The main observation sits in a box at the top of §2 (p. 3). Hedging is asymmetric. The abstract says "and as is well known, sharp minima lead to poorer generalization" (p. 1). The body says "loosely" (p. 5), "apparently" (p. 7) and "(In this paper, we only provided some numerical evidence.)" (p. 9). The open questions are lettered (a)–(e) (p. 9). Figures carry the argument and there are no theorems. Remedy results are reported as partial or negative rather than hidden (pp. 6, 9, 13–16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is empirical and diagnostic: a computable sharpness measure, parametric-plot and warm-start evidence across six networks, and negative or partial results for four remedies (pp. 3–9, 13–16). Stated limits: numerical evidence only (p. 9); the remedies "do not completely remedy the problem" (p. 6) and "do not correct the problem" (p. 9); the performance model is "idealized" (p. 13). Open questions (p. 9): (a) proof that LB methods typically converge to sharp minimizers; (b) the relative density of the two kinds of minima; (c) architectures suited to LB methods; (d) initialization that lets LB succeed; (e) steering LB methods away from sharp minimizers by "algorithmic or regulatory means". Future remedy: dynamic sampling (p. 9).
