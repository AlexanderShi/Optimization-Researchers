### S032 · Exact and inexact subsampled Newton methods for optimization (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2019, DOI 10.1093/imanum/dry009; read as arXiv:1609.08502 v1)
**Read level**: full, of arXiv v1. Read the abstract and §1 with the related work (pp. 1–4), the assumptions (pp. 5–6), Theorem 2.1 and its proof (pp. 6–8), Lemmas 2.2–2.3 and their proofs (pp. 8–10), the statement of Theorem 2.4 and the comparison after it (pp. 11, 13), §3 with Lemma 3.1, Theorem 3.2 and its proof, and the residual-test variant (pp. 13–17), §4 (pp. 18–21), §5 (pp. 21–27) and §6 (p. 27). The proof of Theorem 2.4 (pp. 11–12) and the algebra of the Lemma 3.1 proof (p. 15) were followed only in outline. The Appendix A figures (pp. 30–37) were read through their captions. The IMA JNA version was not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Raghu Bollapragada (Northwestern IEMS), Richard Byrd (University of Colorado, Computer Science) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the analysis paper of the 2016 subsampled-second-order line. Card S041 (c10) records that the later Newton-sketch study cites this paper and refines its worst-case CG analysis (S041 p. 11).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is expected risk F or empirical risk R (eqs. 1.1–1.2, p. 2), solved by Newton steps that subsample the gradient on X_k and the Hessian on S_k (eqs. 1.3–1.5, p. 2). The stated goal is to find which choices of {X_k} and {S_k} are most promising, "in theory and in practice" (p. 2). The second entry is a received view: in deterministic optimization CG is the accepted inner solver, but under subsampling a stochastic gradient iteration (SGI) can change the sample at every inner step, which CG cannot (p. 3). The question asked is which of Newton-CG and Newton-SGI is more efficient, and how to measure that. The measure, taken from Agarwal et al. [1], is the effort needed for a local linear rate with constant 1/2 (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Coordinate the two sample sizes with the target rate. For R-linear convergence the gradient sample must grow geometrically, |X_k| = η^k, while the Hessian sample can stay constant (Theorem 2.1, p. 6). For R-superlinear convergence the gradient sample must grow faster than geometrically, while the Hessian sample may grow at any rate as long as it tends to infinity (Theorem 2.4, pp. 8, 11). In the inexact part the gradient is exact and the Hessian is subsampled. A fixed number r of CG steps and a fixed sample size β are chosen so that each error term is at most 1/8 of the contraction (Theorem 3.2, p. 16). With a residual test, the forcing constant must satisfy ζ ≤ μ_β/(8L), inversely proportional to a condition-number-like quantity (p. 17).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Every component F_i is strongly convex. This is a stronger assumption than needed, made for brevity, and regularization can ensure it (p. 5). The other assumptions are bounded subsampled-Hessian eigenvalues (A1), bounded trace of the gradient covariance (A2), a Lipschitz Hessian (A3) and bounded Hessian-component variance (A4) (pp. 5–6). Assumption B1 bounds the second moment of the error by γ times the squared first moment. The authors call it arguably restrictive (p. 11) and name it again in §6 as the price of results in expectation (p. 27). Sampling is uniform only (p. 27), and the gradient and Hessian samples are independent (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The superlinear analysis starts from a three-term split of the expected step error into a Lipschitz-Hessian term, a Hessian-subsampling term and a gradient-sampling term (Lemma 2.2, pp. 8–9). The sampling terms are bounded by the variance of a sample mean through the trace of a covariance (eq. 2.11, p. 7; Lemma 2.3 via Jensen, p. 10). Lemma 2.3 measures the Hessian error along w_k − w* and not with matrix concentration inequalities, which the authors say gives a more precise estimate (p. 10). The inexact analysis plugs in the worst-case CG bound (eq. 3.4, p. 14) and proves a rate of 1/2 by induction (pp. 16–17). Work is counted in Hessian-vector products: O((κ̂_max)² min{d, √κ̂_max log κ̂_max}) for Newton-CG (eq. 4.1, p. 18), against O((κ̂_max)² κ̂ log κ̂ log d) for LiSSA (eq. 4.4, p. 19). So the square root of a condition number replaces the condition number, and a log d factor disappears because the proof avoids concentration bounds (p. 19).
- **D5 Experiments**: ℓ2-regularized logistic regression (λ = 1/N) on six binary datasets (Table 5.1, p. 22), comparing GD, Newton, Newton-CG at |S| = 5%, 10% and 50%, and Newton-SGI. All use Armijo backtracking and full gradients (p. 22). Plots show training error against iterations and against effective gradient evaluations, with each Hessian-vector product counted as one gradient evaluation, and testing error against CPU time (pp. 23–24). CG's ζ = 0.01 and maxcg = 10 were fixed beforehand and not tuned (p. 22). SGI got It = β × maxcg inner steps, to match Hessian-vector products per iteration (p. 24), and its step length was picked as the best value on a grid from 2⁻²⁰ to 2³ for unscaled data (pp. 24, 26). Result: the two are similar in effective gradient evaluations, and SGI is slower in time because of communication cost (p. 24). Repeated runs or variability were not mentioned in §5.
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**: An "N.B." paragraph separates this paper from Xu et al.'s concurrent work: theirs gives one-iteration results in probability, this one the whole sequence in expectation (p. 3). The same contrast is drawn with Roosta-Khorasani and Mahoney (pp. 4, 13). Table 4.1 is labelled a summary of the literature, "not as a simple way to rank methods", because the results differ in type and in the accuracy measure (pp. 20–21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theoretical (sampling rates for linear and superlinear rates in expectation; complexity of Newton-CG) plus a comparison with LiSSA and Newton sketch (pp. 18–21, 27). The limits are written specifically: assumption B1 (pp. 11, 27); uniform sampling only, while [26] studies non-uniform sampling (p. 27); the worst-case CG bound "may not reflect the full power" of the method (p. 17); the theory asks for κ² samples while a few suffice in practice (p. 20); the numerical results are "preliminary" (pp. 1, 27). Future work: a more comprehensive numerical investigation (p. 27) and proof techniques other than concentration bounds (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: his own inner solver's parameters were fixed in advance and not tuned (p. 22), while the rival inner solver got a step-length grid (p. 26) and a budget matched in Hessian-vector products (p. 24).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the theory does separate the two inner solvers (√κ̂ against κ̂, and log d, p. 19). But the runs find them similar in effective gradient evaluations (p. 24), and no bound is plotted against runs. The authors admit the theory is pessimistic in sample size (κ² against small samples in practice, p. 20) and warn that Table 4.1 is not a ranking (p. 20). The theory discriminates on paper only.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the received view that CG is the inner solver of choice is put on trial in a new regime (p. 3). Here the classical tool is defended against a newer challenger (LiSSA-type SGI), not a dismissed simple tool rehabilitated. The verdict is scoped: both methods are "worthy of further investigation" (p. 27).
- New-pattern candidate: asymmetric accuracy (cheap curvature, accurate gradient). A constant Hessian sample suffices for a linear rate, and any growth to infinity suffices for a superlinear rate, while the gradient sample must grow geometrically or faster (pp. 6, 8, 11). This matches the existing candidate from S023.
- New-pattern candidate: error balancing for inner solves. β and r (or ζ) are chosen so that the Hessian-sampling and CG-residual terms are each at most 1/8 (pp. 16–17). This matches the existing candidate from S018.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To compare two inner solvers, fix a common target (a local linear rate with constant 1/2) and count the work each needs to reach it in one shared unit (Hessian-vector products) (pp. 3, 18).
- Bound a subsampled-matrix error along the one vector that matters (w_k − w*) through the trace of a covariance, not with a matrix concentration inequality. It is simpler and removes a log d factor (pp. 10, 19).
- Prove a target contraction by giving each error term a fixed share (1/4 for the quadratic term, 1/8 each for the sampling and solver terms) and solving for the sample size and iteration count each share implies (p. 16).
- When a concurrent paper appears with similar-looking results, add a dated paragraph stating the difference in result type (in expectation for the whole sequence against one step in probability) and in scope (pp. 3, 13).
- In a work-complexity comparison table, state for each row the type of convergence (deterministic, in expectation, in probability) and the accuracy measure (function value or iterate), and say that the table is not a ranking (pp. 20–21).

**Quotes**:
- "all the results just mentioned are useful and shed light into the properties of subsampled Newton methods, but none of them seem to be definitive." (p. 13)
- "This suggests that the theory could be improved and that techniques other than concentration bounds might help in achieving this." (p. 20)

### S091 · Adaptive finite-difference interval estimation for noisy derivative-free optimization (SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing 2022, DOI 10.1137/21m1452470; read as arXiv:2110.06380 v2)
**Read level**: full, of arXiv v2. Read the abstract and §§1–5 in full (pp. 1–19), including Algorithms 2.1 and 3.1, §3.1 Practical Considerations, and Theorem 3.4 with its proof (pp. 12–13). Of Appendix A, only its opening (p. 21) was read; Appendix B's prose was read for B.1–B.5 (pp. 26–27, 29–31), with Tables 7, 9, 12 and 13 sampled (pp. 26, 28, 31, 32). The remaining rows of Tables 13–20 (pp. 32–39) were not checked. The SISC version was not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Hao-Jun Michael Shi, Yuchen Xie, Melody Qiming Xuan, all Northwestern IEMS (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a component paper of the noisy finite-difference DFO programme. It cites S080 (ref. [21]) for finite-difference methods with a known noise level and for two difficult test functions (pp. 3, 14), Berahas–Byrd–Nocedal (S038, ref. [2]) for FD L-BFGS with ECNoise and the Moré–Wild heuristic (p. 3), and S071 (ref. [20]) for the relaxed line search (p. 17).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Finite-difference DFO spends at least n + 1 evaluations per iteration, against one for interpolation methods, so its gradients must be good (p. 1). The authors say noise is what has kept finite differences out of favour in the DFO community, because the interval matters more as noise grows (p. 1). The near-optimal interval needs the noise level and a higher derivative. The noise level can be known or estimated (difference tables, sampling), but the higher derivative "is not normally available" (p. 2). The default h = max{1, |x|}√ε_M is poor under noise (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The procedure estimates the interval directly, not through a separate estimate of the second derivative. A bisection on h drives a testing ratio, which estimates the ratio of truncation error to measurement error from noisy values and ε_f, into a band [r_l, r_u] with r_l > 1 and r_u > r_l + 2 (eqs. 2.3–2.4, p. 4). For forward differences, r = |f(t+4h) − 4f(t+h) + 3f(t)|/(8ε_f), and r_l = 1.5, r_u = 6 put h within a constant factor of the optimum (eq. 2.8, p. 5). Scaling h by 4 means one new evaluation per monotone trial, and the ratio is affine-invariant (p. 5). Against Gill et al., it avoids their nested estimation. Against Moré–Wild, their condition (2.12) is not affine-invariant (p. 6). The general version handles any scheme (w, s) for a d-th derivative (Algorithm 3.1, p. 8). Testing ratios come from combining the scheme at h and at αh, so the difference values are already available at no extra cost (eq. 3.18, pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions**: The noise is bounded, |ε(t)| ≤ ε_f (Assumption 3.1, p. 12), and its level ε_f is assumed known (pp. 15, 19). Assumption 3.2 is the ratio-perturbation bound implied by ‖w̃‖₁ = 1 (p. 12). Assumption 3.3 requires continuity and r_S(2^K h_0) ≥ r_u − 1 for some K, which holds when |φ^(q)| is bounded away from zero (p. 12). The accuracy claim ignores the o(h^q) term (pp. 4, 9). The experiments use uniform noise (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The analysis has two parts. Accuracy rests on a Taylor argument bounding the noise part of the ratio by 1, so h lands in a band of the optimal order (eqs. 2.6–2.8, pp. 4–5; eqs. 3.7–3.10, pp. 8–9). The theorem covers finite termination only (Theorem 3.4, p. 12). It is proved by contradiction, with invariants r_S(l_k) < r_l + 1 and r_S(u_k) > r_u − 1 on the true function, a finite expansion phase and uniform continuity on [0, u_K1] (pp. 12–13). As printed, the theorem asks for 0 < r_l < r_u − 2 while Algorithm 3.1 requires 1 < r_l (pp. 8, 12), and the proof doubles h (h_k = 2^k h_0, p. 13) while the algorithm expands by a general η (p. 8). Failure cases are analysed in prose. o(h^q) can dominate at large h, which was not common in the tests. When φ^(q) ≈ 0, h grows until max iter = 20 and the code warns without flagging failure. If ε_f is underestimated, termination is not guaranteed (pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Experiments**: In 1-D, six schemes (Table 1, p. 14) are tested on cos(t) at noise levels from 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹ and on four difficult functions taken from Gill et al. and S080 (pp. 14–16). Each output h† is plotted on the deterministic worst-case relative-error curve δ_S(h) (Figs. 1–2, pp. 15–16). In FD L-BFGS (memory 10, relaxed Armijo–Wolfe) the test set is 54 CUTEst problems (Table 2, p. 16) at ε_f ∈ {10⁻¹, 10⁻³, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁷}. Three interval rules are compared: an idealized Fixed interval using true Hessian information, Moré–Wild, and Adaptive (pp. 15–18). Optimality gap is plotted against function evaluations for four representative problems (Figs. 3–4, pp. 18–19), and full tables are in App. B (pp. 32–39).
- **D6 Positioning (one line)**: The paper places itself in the numerical-differentiation tradition (Curtis–Reid, Stepleman–Winarsky, Gill–Murray–Saunders–Wright, Barton, Moré–Wild; p. 3). Gill et al. are left out of the benchmark, with the stated reason that Moré–Wild is regarded as an improvement on them (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing (one line)**: Practical considerations are numbered I–V (rule choice, ratio generation, initialization, special cases, sensitivity to ε_f; pp. 10–12), so each failure case and its symptom sits next to the design rule.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic contribution (a scheme-agnostic interval search), a termination theorem and a numerical study (pp. 1–2, 12, 19). The limits are specific: the noise level is assumed known (p. 19); poor h_0 can cause large errors on rare occasions (p. 11; BOX2, p. 31); it costs more than Moré–Wild (p. 19) and more than the idealized Fixed interval for central differences (p. 19); termination needs ε_f not underestimated (p. 12). Future work: unbounded noise with finite variance via Chebyshev, with high-probability results (p. 19), and periodic rather than per-gradient re-estimation to save evaluations (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the FD L-BFGS engine is kept and only the difference interval is replaced, driven by the noise level ε_f (pp. 2, 15–17). The unknowable constant (φ^(q)(t)) is replaced by a quantity the noisy values can test (abstract; p. 4), which is the 🔴 rule of Method 3. The answer to a misestimated ε_f is analysed, not assumed away (p. 12). The special case φ^(q) ≈ 0 ends in a warning, not a failure (pp. 11–12).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the Fixed baseline is idealized with true Hessian diagonals, or true-Hessian third derivatives for central differences, and is "not generally viable in practice" (pp. 17–18). The rival heuristic got a safeguard threshold tuned on the test set (p. 17). His own losses are printed: higher cost (p. 19), and the BOX2 failure (p. 31).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (programme context): the paper opens with the DFO community's dismissal of finite differences under noise (p. 1) and closes by saying accuracy and robustness are what make finite differences reliable enough for established nonlinear optimization algorithms (p. 19). The broad test itself is in S080.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the only theorem is finite termination (p. 12). The accuracy claim is a Taylor argument (pp. 4–5, 9), checked by plotting the error model δ_S(h) with the output h† marked (pp. 15–16). This is a bound-against-run plot for the estimator, but no theory separates the procedure from Moré–Wild; that separation is purely empirical (pp. 29–31).
- Heuristic 1 ✅: deliberately difficult 1-D functions (pp. 14–15) and a near-central-symmetry probe that breaks Moré–Wild (p. 29) come before the CUTEst runs.
- New-pattern candidate: computable proxy for an unavailable quantity. The testing ratio stands in for the unknown truncation-to-measurement error ratio, and through it for |φ^(q)(t)| (pp. 4–5, 8–9). This matches the existing candidate from S018 and S053.
- New-pattern candidate: arm with an idealized estimate. The Fixed arm uses true-Hessian information "for benchmarking purposes only" (pp. 17–18). This matches the existing candidate from S080.
- New-pattern candidate: invariance as a design requirement and a test. The testing ratio is built to be affine-invariant (p. 5), the rival's condition is criticized for not being so (p. 6), and invariance is tested on a·sin(b·t) (App. B.2, p. 27).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To choose a parameter that balances two error sources, build a ratio of the two that can be computed from available data. Then bisect the parameter until the ratio lies in a band whose width exceeds the ratio's noise bound (here r_u > r_l + 2 because the noise part is at most 1), instead of estimating the constant in the optimal formula (pp. 4–5, 8–9).
- Generate valid testing ratios for any finite-difference scheme by combining the scheme at h and at αh. This cancels the derivative term, leaves the order-q remainder and reuses the evaluations (eq. 3.18, pp. 10–11). Pick α as a power of the expansion factor so that monotone trials need one new evaluation (pp. 5, 11).
- Evaluate an estimator with a deterministic worst-case error curve, plotted against the parameter with the estimator's output marked. This needs no noise realization (eq. 4.1, p. 14; Figs. 1–2, pp. 15–16).
- Add an oracle arm (true derivatives) labelled "for benchmarking purposes only", to show how far a practical rule is from the ideal (pp. 17–18).
- Write a sensitivity paragraph for each user-supplied estimate, giving the direction of failure: overestimating ε_f still terminates with h > h*, underestimating it may not terminate and gives h < h* (p. 12).

**Quotes**:
- "Our procedure produces reliable estimates of the finite-difference interval at low cost without explicitly approximating higher-order derivatives." (abstract, p. 1)
- "We created this idealized option for benchmarking purposes only." (p. 17)
