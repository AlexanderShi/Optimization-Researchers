### S031 · A progressive batching L-BFGS method for machine learning (International Conference on Machine Learning 2018, arXiv:1802.05374)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole arXiv v2 text: main paper pp. 1–8, references pp. 8–10, supplement A (Lemma A.1, p. 11), B (convergence proofs, pp. 11–14), C (dataset table p. 15; network architectures p. 19) and D (performance model, p. 24). The figure pages of supplement C (pp. 15–23) were skimmed through captions and axis labels only.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Raghu Bollapragada and Hao-Jun Michael Shi (Northwestern IEMS), Dheevatsa Mudigere and Ping Tak Peter Tang (Intel) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: continues the group's sampling line: dynamic sample sizes (Byrd, Chin, Nocedal & Wu 2012), multi-batch L-BFGS (Berahas, Nocedal & Takáč 2016) and the inner product test (Bollapragada, Byrd & Nocedal 2017) (pp. 2, 9). It comes after the large-batch / sharp-minima paper (Keskar et al. 2016), which it cites as motivation and for the performance model (pp. 1–2, 24).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a piece of received wisdom in machine learning: L-BFGS has traditionally been regarded as a batch method in the ML community because quasi-Newton updating and line searches need high-quality gradients (p. 1); a well-tuned SG method beat batch L-BFGS on logistic regression, and some researchers credit SG with regularization properties (p. 1). The paper refuses the dichotomy ("One need not, however, choose between the two extremes", abstract) and postulates that the best methods use progressive batching (p. 1). A second gap is named: stochastic line searches are "poorly understood and rarely employed in practice" (p. 4) and have been dismissed in the literature (p. 1); the paper studies one component of such a search, the initial trial steplength (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: when a community dismisses a classical tool because its preconditions fail in one regime (accurate gradients), look for an interpolating regime (here a growing sample) in which the preconditions are met progressively, and design the method there (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Three components are combined (p. 2; Algorithm 1, p. 5):
  1. *Sample size*: the inner product quasi-Newton (IPQN) test asks that the sampled direction −H_k g_k^S make an acute angle with the true quasi-Newton direction −H_k∇F(x_k) with high probability, through the variance condition (5), its bound (6) and the practical sample version (7)–(8), which costs one extra Hessian-vector product (p. 3). A plain extension of the first-order inner product test (descent with high probability) is rejected because it "would underutilize the curvature information" (p. 3). When (7) fails, the new size is b_k from (9) (p. 4). When the sample is too small for the estimate to be reliable, a moving-window technique from Bollapragada et al. (2017) is used (p. 4).
  2. *Line search*: an Armijo backtracking search on the sampled function (16), halving the step (Algorithm 1, lines 10–12, p. 5), whose initial trial step (14) is α_k = (1 + Var/(|S_k|·‖g_k^S‖²))^{-1}. It comes from the expected-decrease inequality (11), in which the only difference from the deterministic case is the variance term in W_k, and from approximating the variance-bias ratio (12)–(13) by sample quantities (p. 4).
  3. *Stable updating*: curvature pairs from the same sample at both ends (18) (Schraudolph et al., full overlap, "FO") or from the overlap O_k = S_k ∩ S_{k+1} (19) (Berahas et al. 2016, multi-batch, "MB"), which costs no extra gradients (pp. 4–5).

  Kept from classical L-BFGS: the two-loop recursion, memory m = 10, H_k^0 = γ_k I with γ_k = y^T s / y^T y, and skipping the update when y^T s ≤ ε‖s‖² with ε = 10⁻² (p. 5). Changed: how the sample, the initial step and y_k are chosen. Defaults: θ = 0.9 (logistic regression), c1 = 10⁻⁴ (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: derive a stochastic line search's first trial step from the expected-decrease inequality of the analysis. The unknown Lipschitz constant drops out, a sample variance-to-squared-gradient ratio remains, and the formula returns α = 1 in the noise-free limit (pp. 4, 11).
  - Transferable technique: when moving a sample-size test from a first-order to a second-order method, test the angle with the true preconditioned direction rather than mere descent, so that the curvature information is not wasted (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: Expected risk (1) and its empirical version (p. 3). The theory freezes parts of the algorithm. It assumes a fixed steplength α_k = α instead of the line search, eigenvalue bounds Λ1 I ⪯ H_k ⪯ Λ2 I (21)/(33), and the exact-variance IPQN test (31) (pp. 5, 11–12). It also adds an orthogonality condition (22)/(32) that does not appear in Algorithm 1, because the IPQN test "in rare situations could allow for the generation of arbitrarily long search directions" (p. 5). The settings are strongly convex with μI ⪯ ∇²F ⪯ LI (23) (Theorem 3.1) or nonconvex, bounded below, with ∇²F ⪯ LI (26) (Theorem 3.2) (pp. 5–6). The authors justify the eigenvalue bound by regularized convexity or by cautious updating (p. 12) and say the fixed-step assumption follows common practice, so that the analysis can focus on progressive sampling (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Lemma A.1 (p. 11): the Lipschitz descent inequality, plus the second moment of H_k g^S written as the squared mean plus the variance, gives the W_k matrix of (11). This is the derivation behind the initial step (14).
  - Lemma B.3 (pp. 12–13): the orthogonality condition (32) bounds the part of H_k g^S orthogonal to H_k∇F (38)–(39). The IPQN test (31) bounds the part along it (40)–(42). Together they give E‖H_k g^S‖² ≤ (1 + θ² + ν²)‖H_k∇F‖² (35), and the step bound α ≤ 1/((1 + θ² + ν²)LΛ2) (36) gives descent (37).
  - Theorem B.4 (pp. 13–14): with ‖∇F‖² ≥ 2μ(F − F*), linear convergence in expectation with ρ = 1 − μΛ1α (44).
  - Theorem B.5 (p. 14): a telescoping sum gives E‖∇F(x_k)‖² → 0 and min_k E‖∇F(x_k)‖² ≤ 2(F(x0) − F_min)/(αTΛ1).

  The rates are per iteration only; no sample or work complexity is given (pp. 5–6, 13–14). Inference, not stated by the authors: the constants depend only on Λ1, Λ2, θ, ν, so the bound does not separate L-BFGS from an H_k = I method.
  - Transferable technique: control the second moment of a stochastic direction by splitting its variance into the component along the true direction (an inner-product test) and the orthogonal component (an orthogonality test), each bounded relative to ‖H∇F‖² (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Logistic regression* (ℓ2, λ = 1/N) on 8 data sets (p. 6; Table 2, p. 15). R* comes from full-batch L-BFGS run to ‖∇R‖∞ ≤ 10⁻⁸ (p. 6). PBQN-MB (25% overlap) and PBQN-FO use θ = 0.9, |S0| = 512 and defaults; "none of the parameters in our PBQN method were tuned for each individual dataset" (p. 6). SG (batch 1) and SVRG (inner loop N) use constant steps tuned per problem over 2^j, j ∈ {−10, …, 10} (p. 6). The cost unit is full gradient evaluations; training error, test loss and test accuracy are plotted (p. 6), plus batch-size and steplength traces for every data set (pp. 15–19).
  - *Logistic-regression findings*: PBQN competes well; the trial step (14) is accepted in most iterations, so the line search costs few extra function values (p. 6). SVRG reduces training error fast in later epochs without matching test gains, and "Neither the PBQN nor SVRG significantly outperforms the other" on training error across data sets (p. 6). MB is preferred to FO (p. 6). On url, SG and SVRG were run for only 3 gradient evaluations because they had already reached about 10⁷ iterations (p. 19).
  - *Neural networks*: a small ConvNet, AlexNet-like networks on MNIST and CIFAR-10, and ResNet18 on CIFAR-10, in PyTorch, with batch normalization and dropout removed from all methods (p. 7; architectures p. 19). PBQN starts at |S0| = 512, while SG and Adam use batch 128 with a development-based decay of the step length on validation loss. PBQN's θ was "tuned lightly" over {0.9, 2, 3} (p. 7). PBQN reaches similar test accuracy but needs more gradient evaluations (p. 7). Table 1 (best test accuracy over 5 runs): MB is best on C (67.37) and R (70.28); Adam is best on A1 (99.34) and A2 (73.59) (p. 8). Overfitting sets in once the full batch is reached (p. 8).
  - *Parallel potential*: argued only through an idealized performance model (cost ratio 4/3, SG parallel efficiency 0.2, effective batch ratio 4), which concludes that PBQN needs about as many iterations as SG to win (p. 24). No parallel implementation was run (pp. 2–3, 24).
  - Transferable technique: in an ML benchmark, give the baselines a per-problem log grid of step sizes (21 points) and run your own method at one default setting across all data sets. Report the internal adaptive quantities (batch size, accepted steplength) next to the error curves (pp. 6, 15–23).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Builds on and extends the group's own tools: the inner product test is extended to second-order methods, which "leads to a significant modification" of the original procedure (p. 2); the overlap scheme of Berahas et al. is preferred over Schraudolph et al.'s scheme, presented as a "straightforward adaptation of classical quasi-Newton methods" (p. 2). It uses the ML large-batch literature (Keskar, Goyal, Smith, Hoffer, You) as motivation (p. 2). K-FAC and the Neumann optimizer are mentioned but not compared (p. 2). Observation: the experiments compare only against SG, SVRG and Adam (pp. 6–7), not against the other stochastic quasi-Newton methods listed on p. 2 (Schraudolph et al., Mokhtari & Ribeiro, Byrd et al. 2016, Curtis 2016).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution list sits in §1.2 (p. 2); the main thesis is a "postulate" (p. 1). The optimism outruns the deep-learning evidence: the paper claims "the potential to become one of the leading optimization methods for training deep neural networks" (p. 2), "almost no parameter tuning" (p. 2) and a method that does not expose free parameters (Final Remarks, p. 8). Yet θ is a tunable parameter, chosen in the interval [0.9, 3], for the networks (p. 5), and PBQN needs more gradient evaluations than SG and Adam there (p. 7). The abstract says the method performs well on deep networks and does not mention this loss (abstract). The theory is labelled "supporting convergence theory" (abstract). The main-text cross-references point into supplement numbering, e.g. "Figures 9 and 2" (p. 6), "Figures 15 and 16" (p. 7), and (B.2), (29), (31) in Theorems 3.1–3.2 (pp. 5–6).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: The contribution is algorithmic, with supporting theory. The limitations are concrete: work on fast NN performance is "still underway" (p. 7); finer control of the sample size in the small-batch regime is needed (p. 7); batch normalization and dropout conflict with the gradient consistency that the curvature pairs need (p. 7); the parallel implementation was only "briefly explored" (p. 3). Future work: a more robust batching mechanism, a redesign of batch normalization and dropout, and "most importantly" a parallel implementation (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: puts two dismissals on trial, that L-BFGS is not a method of choice for ML (abstract; p. 1) and that stochastic line searches are inappropriate (pp. 1, 4), and tests them with a progressive-batching L-BFGS (pp. 6–8).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the L-BFGS engine is kept (p. 5). Only the sample size, the initial step and the curvature pairs are made regime-aware, driven by explicit sample-variance estimates (7)–(9) and (14) (pp. 3–4). The recovery path is thin: halving backtracking, skipped updates and the moving-window fallback (pp. 4–5).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: own method untuned, competitors tuned over a 21-point grid (p. 6). Also ⚠ variant of step 7: the deep-network loss appears in the body (p. 7) and Table 1 (p. 8) but not in the abstract, and θ was tuned lightly for the networks (p. 7).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the analysed iteration (fixed step, an extra orthogonality test not in Algorithm 1) is not the one shipped (pp. 5, 12), there is no bound-versus-run comparison, and the theory is called "supporting" (abstract).
- Also Heuristic 2 (instrument the inside): batch-size and steplength traces for every run (pp. 15–23).
- New-pattern candidate: *component-isolating analysis on a simplified iteration* (fixed step, bounded H_k, extra test, with the gap stated) (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: *idealized performance model to argue an untested parallel speedup* (p. 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Initial trial step for a stochastic Armijo search taken from the expected-decrease inequality: α = (1 + sample variance / (|S|·‖g_S‖²))⁻¹ (pp. 4, 11).
- An angle-based sample-size test for preconditioned stochastic directions, costing one extra H-vector product (pp. 3–4).
- Overlapping consecutive batches (25%) to form curvature pairs at no extra gradient cost (pp. 5–6).
- A parallel/orthogonal variance split to bound E‖H g_S‖² in the analysis (pp. 12–13).
- An untuned own method against grid-tuned baselines, with traces of the adaptive internals (pp. 6, 15–23).

**Quotes**:
- "we postulate that the most efficient algorithms for machine learning may not reside entirely in the highly stochastic or full batch regimes" (p. 1)
- "We observe from our results that the PBQN method achieves a similar test accuracy as SG and Adam, but requires more gradient evaluations." (p. 7)

### S038 · Derivative-free optimization of noisy functions via quasi-Newton methods (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2019, DOI 10.1137/18m1177718; arXiv:1803.10173)
**Read level**: full. Read the whole arXiv v2 text, pp. 1–26: sections 1–6 (pp. 1–22), Appendix A (pp. 23–24; the profile figures were skimmed through captions) and the references (pp. 24–26).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Albert S. Berahas (Northwestern IEMS), Richard H. Byrd (University of Colorado) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: opens the group's noisy-DFO programme; submitted 3/27/2018 (p. 1). It thanks Jorge Moré and Stefan Wild for suggestions on noise estimation (p. 24) and builds on their ECnoise and difference-interval work (pp. 6–8).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The received wisdom is that finite differences in the noisy case are "considered problematic". This motivated direct-search and interpolation methods, which "do not, however, scale well with the number of variables" (p. 1). The entry is two observations. L-BFGS builds quadratic models at a cost linear in n (p. 1). And finite-difference BFGS is regarded by some (citing a private communication from N. Gould, ref. [16], pp. 1, 25) as one of the best methods for non-noisy DFO, "Although not a mainstream view"; Matlab's fminunc invokes it whenever only function values are supplied (p. 2). The problem is f(x) = φ(x) + ε(x) with non-decaying noise, contrasted with Kelley's implicit filtering, which assumes decaying noise. Convergence to a neighbourhood is "the best we can hope for in this setting" (p. 3). The noise level is unknown, contrasted with Barton's known noise bound (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: locate the gap by listing the closest predecessors' assumptions (noise that can be driven to zero; a known noise bound) and dropping exactly those (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: FDLM (Algorithm 3.1, p. 9) has four parts:
  1. *Noise estimate*: the noise level ε_f is estimated by ECnoise, using Hamming's difference table as extended by Moré and Wild, (3.5)–(3.8) (pp. 7–8). The difference interval h comes from ε_f and coarse second- or third-derivative estimates, (3.3) for forward and (3.4) for central differences (p. 7). They prefer the Hamming table to repeated sampling because it also works for deterministic noise (p. 8).
  2. *Line search*: an Armijo–Wolfe search capped at a_max trials. After the first trial (α = 1) fails, the Armijo test is relaxed by +2ε_f (3.11) (p. 8). The line search has a dual role: it computes the step, and its failure signals that h may be wrong. The method must tell a poor gradient (increase h) from nonlinearity (shorten the step) and from noise (p. 2).
  3. *Recovery* (Algorithm 3.2, p. 10), five cases. Case 1: re-estimate the noise along d_k; if the new h̄ differs from h by a factor outside [γ1, γ2], replace h and keep the iterate. Cases 2–3: try a step of size h along d_k. Case 4: move to the best point on the finite-difference stencil (3.9), an idea taken from pattern search that costs no extra evaluations. Case 5: re-estimate the noise along a random direction (pp. 9–11).
  4. *Hessian approximation*: curvature pairs are stored only if s^T y ≥ ζ‖s‖‖y‖ (pp. 9, 11).

  There are two stopping tests, a gradient test (3.13) and moving-average function-value tests (3.14)–(3.15), offered "in isolation or in combination" because "no single test is best suited for all situations" (p. 11). Defaults as numbers: ECnoise uses 4 evaluations for stochastic and 6 for deterministic noise, below Moré–Wild's 4–8 / 6–10, which the authors found conservative; ν2 costs 2–4 evaluations, with the difference table as a back-up; ν3 ← ν2 (p. 12). Kept: BFGS/L-BFGS updating. Changed: the gradient (differencing with a noise-based h), the line-search acceptance test (relaxed), and an added recovery tree.
  - Transferable technique: give the line search a second job. Its failure triggers a diagnosis (re-estimate the noise along the search direction and compare the new h̄ with h) before any new step is tried (pp. 2, 9–10).
  - Transferable technique: keep the best point of the finite-difference stencil as a free fallback iterate when the line search fails (pp. 8, 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: φ is twice continuously differentiable, and ε(x) is a random variable whose distribution does not depend on x; multiplicative noise fits the model (3.1) (p. 6). The theory assumes i.i.d. bounded noise (p. 6): A.1 strong convexity μI ⪯ ∇²φ ⪯ LI; A.2 bounded gradient error ‖e(x)‖ ≤ ε̄_g; A.3 bounded function noise |ε(x)| ≤ ε̄_f (pp. 13, 15). The authors admit three conveniences:
  - H_k = I, because the general case is "longer and more cluttered" (p. 12);
  - plain backtracking with a relaxation 2ε_A (A > ε̄_f) instead of Armijo–Wolfe, "because this simplifies the analysis" (p. 19);
  - the parameter A could be removed at the cost of a more complex analysis (p. 19).

  The noise-estimation guarantee needs i.i.d. noise, but the algorithm is "often effective" without it (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Theorem 4.1 (pp. 13–14): fixed step α ≤ 1/L. The descent lemma plus a completed square gives φ(x_{k+1}) ≤ φ(x_k) − (α/2)‖∇φ‖² + (α/2)‖e‖² (4.6). With ‖∇φ‖² ≥ 2μ(φ − φ*) (4.7), this gives Q-linear convergence to φ* + ε̄_g²/(2μ) (4.5), which is read as the best value attainable under noise. An R-linear restatement follows (4.8) (p. 14).
  - Theorem 4.2 (pp. 15–18): the relaxed Armijo test is always satisfiable for small α (4.16) (p. 15). The analysis then splits iterations by β_k = ‖e(x_k)‖/‖∇φ(x_k)‖ (4.17). Case 1 (β_k ≤ β): every α ≤ 1/L passes, so α_k > τ/L and the decrease (4.20) holds. Case 2 (β_k > β): add and subtract c1(τ/L)(1 − β)²‖∇φ‖², then bound ‖∇φ‖² by ‖e‖²/β², which gives (4.23) with a constant η (p. 16). The two cases share one recursion (4.25). Shifting by η/(1 − ρ) gives (4.28) (p. 17). The choice β = 1 − 4c1 gives ρ = 1 − 32μτc1³/L (4.14) (pp. 15, 18).
  - Forward-difference specialization: ‖e‖ ≤ Lh/2 + 2ε̄_f/h = 2√(Lε̄_f) at the optimal h (4.30), so the asymptotic accuracy η̄ is proportional to ε̄_f times the condition number L/μ (4.32)–(4.33) (p. 18).

  Stated pessimism: the line-search theory "only uses the fact that α cannot be much smaller than 1/L" (p. 19). With a general H_k the provable rate would be "closer to 1", just as the provable L-BFGS rate is no better than steepest descent's without noise (p. 14).
  - Transferable technique: for a noisy line search, split iterations by the relative gradient error β_k. Where it is small, the Armijo test behaves as in the deterministic case; where it is large, the gradient is bounded by the error, and the possible increase is absorbed into a constant η (pp. 15–17).
  - Transferable technique: chain the function-noise bound to the gradient error through the optimal difference interval (4.30), so that the final neighbourhood is stated in a quantity the user can estimate, ε̄_f (p. 18).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Noise-free first* (§2). FDLM with forward and central differences is compared with DFOtr (the Conn–Scheinberg–Vicente interpolation code) and NOMAD on four Hock–Schittkowski problems (Fig. 2.1, p. 4). NOMAD is "by far the slowest"; s271 is quadratic, which favours the interpolation code's finite termination (p. 4). Function evaluations are counted per iteration as n + t_ls or 2n + t_ls (p. 4).
  - *CPU accounting*. No CPU times are compared, because DFOtr's O(n⁶) linear algebra is "not designed to be efficient". FDLM alone gets a CPU table on extended Rosenbrock for n = 10 to 5000, where DFOtr already needs more than 1,800 s at n = 100 (Table 2.1, p. 5). Profiles are in Appendix A.1 (p. 23).
  - *Noisy tests* (§5): 49 Hock–Schittkowski problems, n = 2 to 100 (Table 5.1, p. 19). The budget is 100×n evaluations and 30 CPU minutes, with a 10⁻⁸ tolerance "to display the complete evolution of the runs"; NOMAD runs at defaults (p. 19). Four noise types (stochastic/deterministic × additive/multiplicative) are run at four levels {10⁻⁸, 10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁴, 10⁻²} (p. 19). Deterministic noise follows the Moré–Wild Chebyshev construction (p. 23). Performance profiles over 392 problem instances use the Moré–Wild decrease test (5.2) with τ = 10⁻⁵; data profiles are in the appendix (pp. 20–21, 24). Only the 10⁻⁸ and 10⁻² levels are shown (pp. 20–21). NOMAD is dropped from the profiles as the slowest method (p. 20).
  - *Verdict*: FI and FDLM are "roughly comparable in terms of function evaluations". "Contrary to conventional wisdom", the per-iteration cost of finite differences is offset by faster convergence (p. 21). Forward differences are better at low noise, central differences more robust at high noise (p. 21).
  - *Ablation*: without Recovery, performance "deteriorates substantially". Recovery is invoked more often at high noise and with forward differences, and is crucial for multiplicative noise (p. 21; Fig. 5.4, p. 22; data profiles Fig. A.4, p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: a three-stage protocol. First a noise-free comparison, which separates the power of the method from its noise handling (§2). Then the noisy comparison with the rival community's own profile machinery (the Moré–Wild test, performance and data profiles). Finally an ablation of the new safeguard (pp. 3–5, 19–22).
  - Transferable technique: when the rival code is not built for speed, compare evaluations only. Add a separate scalability table on a problem family the rival cannot reach (p. 5).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: Openly contrarian against the DFO mainstream. Powell's 1972 remark, "I believe that eventually the better methods will not use derivative approximations.", serves as the foil (p. 6). The paper concedes the rivals' strengths: Moré–Wild showed interpolation methods are the most robust under noise (p. 3), Powell's O(n⁴) codes are faster than the code tested (p. 5), and conclusive remarks are hard because interpolation methods vary so much (p. 5). DFOtr was chosen because it is state of the art yet "simple enough to allow us to evaluate all its algorithmic components" (p. 4). Implicit filtering (Kelley) and Barton are credited as the closest finite-difference predecessors (p. 3). The success of BFGS on nonsmooth problems (Lewis–Overton; Keskar–Wächter) supports the opening claim (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The opening is a strong claim for BFGS, softened later: it is "the power of quasi-Newton methods, in general, rather than the specific properties of BFGS" that matters (p. 2). The main claim is hedged as "competitive" (p. 2) and "roughly comparable" (p. 21). Theorems come with interpretations of the accuracy constants (pp. 14, 18–19). A roadmap paragraph follows (p. 2), and the final remarks restate the design logic in one paragraph (p. 22).
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: Algorithm, analysis and numerical study. The limits are written down:
  - the noise-estimate guarantee needs i.i.d. noise (p. 2);
  - the analysis covers H_k = I and backtracking only (pp. 12, 19);
  - the comparison with interpolation methods is not conclusive (p. 5).

  Open directions: switching from forward to central differences, where "How to do this in an algorithmically sound way remains a topic of investigation." (p. 22), and higher-order differences, both as a last-resort action in Recovery (p. 11) and for hard problems (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (strong): the paper names the missing comparison (p. 6), uses Powell's 1972 remark as the received wisdom (p. 6), and reports a "Contrary to conventional wisdom" result (p. 21).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the BFGS engine is kept. h is set from an explicit noise estimate (pp. 6–7), the Armijo test is relaxed by 2ε_f (p. 8), and a recovery tree is added (pp. 9–11), with an ablation of the recovery (p. 21).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the rival community's own protocol (Moré–Wild test, profiles, a 100×n budget; pp. 19–21), and accounting in the rival's favour (no CPU comparison for an O(n⁶) code; p. 5).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant of step 1: the baseline is not the strongest rival code. It was chosen for transparency, the faster Powell codes are acknowledged, and the conclusion is scoped (pp. 4–5).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the analysis is of an H_k = I gradient iteration with plain backtracking, not the shipped L-BFGS / Armijo–Wolfe method (pp. 12, 19). The authors say the provable quasi-Newton rate would be no better (p. 14), so the theory separates noise-aware from naïve line searches, not L-BFGS from steepest descent. There is no bound-versus-run plot.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak): undecided choices are recorded as options or open questions: two stopping tests (p. 11), the forward-to-central switch (p. 22), higher-order differences (pp. 11, 21).
- Also Heuristic 3 (noise-free before noisy): §2 before §5 (pp. 3–5, 19); Heuristic 5 (numbers as defaults): ECnoise 4/6 evaluations (p. 12).
- New-pattern candidate: *component-isolating analysis on a simplified iteration* (H_k = I and backtracking, the gap stated) (pp. 12, 14, 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Set the difference interval from an estimated noise level (ECnoise) and re-estimate it when the line search fails (pp. 7–11).
- Relax the Armijo test by twice the noise level only after the unit step fails (p. 8).
- A recovery tree that first tests whether the noise estimate is stale, then tries cheap moves (a step of size h; the best stencil point), and only then re-samples the noise (p. 10).
- A case split on the relative gradient error in noisy line-search proofs (pp. 15–17).
- Noise-free comparison, then noisy profiles, then an ablation of the safeguard (pp. 3–5, 19–22).

**Quotes**:

### S041 · An investigation of Newton-sketch and subsampled Newton methods (Optimization Methods and Software 2020, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2020.1725751; arXiv:1705.06211)
**Read level**: full. Read the arXiv v4 main text pp. 1–17, the references pp. 17–19, the prose of Appendix A (pp. 20–21, 23, 27) and of Appendix B (pp. 32–33). The figure pages (pp. 22–36) were skimmed through captions and legends; the text layer repeats some captions and figure blocks, which is harmless.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Albert S. Berahas (Lehigh ISE), Raghu Bollapragada (Northwestern IEMS) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: follows Bollapragada, Byrd & Nocedal, *Exact and inexact subsampled Newton methods* (ref. [2]; pp. 2, 11, 17), whose worst-case CG analysis it refines (p. 11). It answers Pilanci & Wainwright's Newton-Sketch [16] and Agarwal et al.'s LiSSA [1] (pp. 2, 7).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a missing comparison. Newton-Sketch was analysed with exact solves for problems where d is not large, and its authors "do not present extensive numerical results, nor do they compare their approaches with Hessian subsampling" (p. 2). The relative tradeoffs of the two "have not been investigated in the optimization literature" (abstract). Two more questions come from fashion. Would a stochastic gradient iteration beat CG as the inner linear solver (p. 2)? And, given the popularity of first-order methods, do stochastic second-order methods have much to offer (p. 2)? The paper argues they do, through the steplength selection that second-order information makes easier and through robustness to ill conditioning (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable technique: when a method is published with theory but without a head-to-head against its own simplest special case (subsampling is a special case of sketching, p. 4), make that comparison the paper (pp. 2, 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: All methods share the framework A_k p_k = −∇F(w_k) with an exact gradient (2.1). The gradient is made exact on purpose, because it "allows us to study Hessian approximations in isolation" and gives a Q-linear rate comparable with first-order methods (p. 3). Three methods:
  1. *Newton-Sketch*: a randomized Hadamard sketch of a square-root Hessian (2.2), available for generalized linear models (pp. 3–4). It is run with CG through two products with the sketched square root (3.2), with parameters m_NS and maxCG (p. 6). The cost of forming the square root and multiplying by the sketch is ignored, so the results are "the most optimistic for Newton-Sketch" (p. 6).
  2. *SSN-CG*: an averaged subsampled Hessian (2.3) used Hessian-free (3.3), with parameters T and maxCG (p. 6).
  3. *SSN-SGI*: a LiSSA-like inner stochastic gradient iteration (3.5) on quadratic models built from single component Hessians (p. 7).

  All second-order methods use Armijo backtracking from the unit step, and CG stops on the relative residual test (4.1) (p. 7). Kept: inexact Newton-CG with a line search. Varied: the Hessian approximation and the inner solver.
  - Transferable technique: make every ingredient except the one under study exact (here the gradient), so that the comparison isolates that ingredient (the Hessian approximation) (p. 3).
- **D3 Assumptions & model**: A finite sum of smooth convex components (p. 2). The analysis (§5) assumes:
  - A.1: every component Hessian satisfies μI ⪯ ∇²F_i ⪯ LI;
  - A.2: a Lipschitz Hessian;
  - A.3: bounded variance of the component Hessians;
  - A.4: bounded moments, E‖w_k − w*‖² ≤ γ(E‖w_k − w*‖)² (pp. 11–12).

  Each assumption is justified (p. 12). The authors note that [18] weakens A.1 but obtains only per-iteration improvement in probability. They keep A.1 "to focus instead on the interaction between the CG linear solver and the efficiency of method" (p. 12). The theorem further assumes a constant sample size T (p. 11), a unit step and a start close to the solution, ‖w0 − w*‖ ≤ min{μ/(2M), μ/(2γM)} (p. 12). The complexity comparison makes the implicit assumption n > κ² explicit (p. 16).
- **D4 Analysis & proof technique**:
  - Theorem 5.1 (pp. 12–14): the error splits into Term 1, the exact subsampled-Newton step, and Term 2, the CG residual (5.9). Term 1 follows from [2] plus Lemma 2.3 of [2]. With T ≥ 64σ²/μ² (5.6) it is at most (M/2μ)‖w_k − w*‖² + (1/8)‖w_k − w*‖ (5.10)–(5.11) (pp. 12–13). Term 2 uses CG's eigenvalue-gap bound (5.1) instead of the worst case. The A-norm is converted to the Euclidean norm at a cost of √κ, and r_k is taken as the smallest number of CG steps for which the ratio in (5.7) is at most 1/(8κ^{3/2}), which gives 1/8 (5.12) (p. 13). Summing gives (5.13), and induction with A.4 gives linear convergence with constant 1/2 (5.8) (pp. 13–14).
  - Corollary 5.2: total work O((n + r̄σ²/μ²) d log(1/ε)), with r̄ = max_k r_k (p. 14).
  - Newton-Sketch, from [16]: sketch size m = O(κ² min{n, d}) and per-iteration cost O(nd log(κd) + d³κ⁴), so total work O((n + κ⁴d²) d log(1/ε)) (5.15) (p. 16). In the worst case, r̄ = d, and the two bounds are "similar", with Newton-Sketch slightly more expensive (p. 16).
  - The bound is tied to data: the spectra of the true, subsampled and sketched Hessians on four data sets (Fig. 2, p. 15) show r̄ ≪ d for clustered spectra. On cina, CG gains two orders of magnitude in iterations 20–24 (p. 14); the wide-spectrum synthetic3 behaves like the worst case (p. 16). Appendix B averages the spectra over 10 replications with error bars and sets T = m_NS for fairness (p. 32).
  - Transferable technique: replace a worst-case Krylov bound in a complexity result by CG's eigenvalue-gap bound. This leaves a data-dependent iteration count r̄; then plot the actual spectra of the test problems to show r̄ ≪ d (pp. 11, 14–15, 32–33).
  - Transferable technique: budget the per-iteration contraction of an inexact Newton analysis. Split the error into the exact-step error and the inner-solver residual, and give each a fixed share (1/8 + 1/8) through the sample size and the number of inner iterations (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - *Problems and baseline*: logistic regression with ℓ2, λ = 1/n (4.2) (p. 8), on 4 synthetic data sets (κ from 10² to 10⁵) and 12 machine-learning data sets (Tables 1–2, p. 20; p. 23). The first-order baseline is SVRG (p. 7).
  - *Cost unit*: "effective gradient evaluations", meaning function evaluations plus gradient evaluations plus Hessian-vector products, which is valid because a gradient and a Hessian-vector product cost the same for this loss (p. 8).
  - *Tuning*: every method gets an iteration budget b of work between gradient evaluations, swept over 9 values {n/100, …, 10n}. All combinations of each method's tuning parameters are tried for each b, and the best by training error versus effective gradient evaluations is shown (p. 8; Fig. 1 caption, p. 10).
  - *Findings*: SSN-SGI is not competitive with SSN-CG, and "This was not easily predictable at the outset" (p. 8). On gisette and australian, Newton-Sketch and SSN-CG are much faster than SVRG; on the well-conditioned australian-scale all are on par; on rcv1 curvature does not pay (p. 9). Newton-Sketch and SSN-CG are on par in effective gradient evaluations, but CPU time would show a "dramatic advantage" for SSN-CG. This claim is not backed by CPU numbers in the paper (pp. 9, 16–17). The best m_NS is much smaller than the best T, which the spectra explain (p. 9). On the synthetic sets Newton-Sketch is slightly better (dissimilar components), and SVRG leads early and then stagnates (p. 21). The paper reports an "SVRG sweet-spot" (p. 23). Newton-Sketch was not run on cov because of memory (p. 23).
  - *Sensitivity* (pp. 27–31): divergent SVRG and SSN-SGI runs are excluded from the figures, with an explicit warning that this makes the methods look equally sensitive when they are not. Newton-Sketch and SSN-CG are more robust and easier to tune (p. 27).
  - Transferable technique: compare stochastic methods at an equal work budget b between full-gradient evaluations. Sweep b over a log grid and tune every method's parameters for each b (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: in a sensitivity study, state which runs were excluded (divergent ones) and say that their exclusion reverses the apparent conclusion (p. 27).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper corrects others and the group's own earlier work:
  - Pilanci–Wainwright: no numerical study, no comparison with subsampling, and direct solvers that are practical only for small d (pp. 2, 5);
  - LiSSA and the complexity comparison in [2]: "None of those papers report detailed numerical results" (p. 7);
  - the group's own worst-case analysis [2], which "underestimates" SSN-CG (p. 11);
  - the popularity of first-order methods (p. 2).

  It credits Roosta-Khorasani & Mahoney for weaker assumptions (p. 12) and describes Hessian subsampling as well understood theoretically (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Framed as an "investigation" (title; p. 2). Surprise is recorded as surprise: SGI's effectiveness "was not known to us at the outset of this investigation" (p. 7); "not easily predictable at the outset" (p. 8). The conclusion is hedged: "generally more efficient than SVRG (a popular first order method), on some logistic regression problems" (p. 17); this last sentence of the paper has no full stop. The CPU-time advantage is asserted three times (pp. 9, 16–17) without a CPU table.
- **D8 Contribution & limitations**: A numerical study plus a spectrum-dependent complexity analysis of SSN-CG (pp. 16–17). Limits, as stated or visible:
  - only convex logistic regression is tested (p. 8);
  - there is an SVRG sweet-spot (p. 23);
  - Newton-Sketch needs a square-root Hessian (p. 3), and its sketch could not be built for cov (p. 23);
  - the analysis is local, with a unit step and a constant T (p. 12);
  - the comparison assumes n > κ² (p. 16).

  The final remarks give no future directions (pp. 16–17).
