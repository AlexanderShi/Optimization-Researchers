### S009 · A trust region method based on interior point techniques for nonlinear programming (Mathematical Programming 89(1), 149–185, 2000, doi:10.1007/pl00011391)
**Read level**: full. All 37 file pages: pp. 1–35 (introduction, KKT connections, Algorithm I, well-posedness, global analysis, Algorithm II, final remarks) read; the reference list (pp. 35–37) skimmed. Because digits and symbols are stripped, I followed the argument from the prose and the order of the lemmas. Individual inequalities, constants and parameter values could not be checked.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Jean Charles Gilbert (INRIA) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the global convergence theory for the trust-region interior method whose implementation, by Byrd, Hribar and Nocedal, is cited as showing "much promise" (p. 2). S021 calls that code NITRO (S021 p. 25). S078 says a global theory exists for "an algorithm similar to that implemented in KNITRO" (S078 p. 5), but its reference number is stripped, so the link from KNITRO to S009 is inferred.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: SQP methods need few iterations, but each step solves a QP, which is costly when there are many variables and constraints. Interior methods succeeded on large LPs, and "it is natural to ask whether they can be extended" (p. 2). The entry is to "view interior point methods from the perspective of sequential quadratic programming" and to build the QP subproblem so that steps are discouraged from violating s > 0 (p. 2). The problem is an inequality-constrained NLP, rewritten as a log-barrier problem in (x, s) with slacks. Algorithm I solves it for fixed μ, and Algorithm II repeats this for decreasing μ (pp. 2, 6, 33). The paper fences its scope at the start: "we do not address the important issue of how fast to decrease the barrier parameter" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The paper applies the Byrd–Omojokun trust-region SQP method for equality constraints to the barrier problem: first a normal step inside a contracted region, then a tangential step (pp. 5–6). Three things are kept: the equality-constrained SQP engine, its non-squared Euclidean-norm merit function, which is exact and nondifferentiable (p. 5), and the Lalee–Nocedal–Plantenga direct and iterative subproblem solvers (p. 5).
  Four things change:
  - The slack component of the trust region is scaled by S⁻¹, because the slack block of the Hessian is diagonal and positive definite: "it seems to be the best scale" (p. 4).
  - A fraction-to-the-boundary rule keeps the slacks positive, so small trust regions are not needed for that purpose (p. 4).
  - The normal subproblem tightens both the radius and the slack bounds by a contraction parameter (p. 5).
  - After an accepted step, the slacks of satisfied constraints are reset by a componentwise max rule. The new slack is then not unnecessarily small, and the merit value does not increase (pp. 17–18).

  Relation to known methods: when the QP is strictly convex, the SQP step equals a primal interior Newton step (p. 7). The primal-dual variant differs only in the slack block of the Hessian (p. 8). This equivalence needs a strictly convex QP, an exact solve and an inactive trust region, and those conditions fail in most iterations. Near the solution the method should behave like other interior methods, but its global behaviour is likely to be very different (pp. 8–9).

  The exact Hessian, or a BFGS or SR1 approximation, may be used, and no positive definiteness of ∇²L is required (p. 8). The penalty parameter is increased, always by at least a constant factor, until the predicted reduction is at least a fraction of ν times the normal predicted reduction (pp. 17, 26).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - Hypotheses (p. 20): (a) f and g are differentiable, with ∇f, g and A Lipschitz on an open convex set containing the iterates; (b) {f_k} is bounded below and {∇f_k}, {g_k}, {A_k}, {B_k} are bounded.
  - Full rank of A_k is not assumed, on purpose: the authors want to see how the algorithm behaves when constraint gradients are dependent (p. 20). They call (b) the "most restrictive assumption", since it fails if the iterates are unbounded (p. 20).
  - B_k may be any bounded Hessian approximation, and the choice of multiplier estimate is left open (p. 18).
  - The primal version is analysed "because of its simplicity". The primal-dual extension is only said to be possible "if appropriate safeguards are applied" (p. 8).
  - The trust region uses the Euclidean norm for simplicity (p. 4). LICQ is redefined for limit points of the sequence {g_k, A_k}, and the authors say this definition is "not standard" (p. 21).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**:
  1. A generalisation of a one-dimensional result of Powell (minimising a quadratic on an interval, p. 12) gives lower bounds on the normal predicted reduction (pp. 12–13) and on the tangential one (p. 16).
  2. Inexactness is specified by a range-space condition and by normal and tangential Cauchy-decrease conditions. Steihaug's truncated CG satisfies all of them (pp. 11–12, 15).
  3. Any null-space basis Z_k is allowed, provided its singular values are bounded above and away from zero (pp. 14–15).
  4. Model accuracy: |pred − ared| is bounded by a quadratic in the scaled step (pp. 18–19). From this, well-posedness follows: at a non-stationary point the trust-region radius cannot shrink to zero (Proposition, proof by contradiction, pp. 19–20).
  5. The slacks stay bounded above because ln‖s‖ / ‖s‖ → 0 (pp. 22–23).
  6. A ball argument: the iterates must leave a ball around a non-stationary iterate, otherwise the merit function would go to −∞ (pp. 24–26). The same argument is reused for the stationarity of the barrier problem (pp. 31–32).
  7. The main theorem is a trichotomy (p. 21, restated with a proof map on pp. 32–33):
     - (i) the iterates are not asymptotically feasible: they approach stationarity of the infeasibility measure, and ν → ∞;
     - (ii) the iterates are asymptotically feasible, but a limit point fails LICQ: ν → ∞;
     - (iii) LICQ holds at every limit point: the slacks stay away from zero, ν is eventually constant, and the barrier stationarity conditions hold in the limit.
  8. The outer-loop theorem (p. 34) has two outcomes: either some inner problem ends in (i) or (ii), or all limit points are feasible and satisfy the KKT conditions under LICQ.

  The results are global only: "We are not concerned here with conditions assuring a good rate of convergence" (p. 33).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: none in this paper. The authors point to the Byrd–Hribar–Nocedal implementation, whose experiments "show that this approach holds much promise" (p. 2), and to companion papers with results for primal and primal-dual versions and for the second-order correction (p. 35).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper builds on the Byrd–Omojokun approach, "which we have found suitable for solving large problems" (p. 5). It credits Yamashita and others with noting the SQP–interior equivalence (p. 7). It lists nine author groups working on interior methods for NLP and separates itself on one technical point: those studies assume or enforce positive definiteness of ∇²L, while "In our approach there is no such requirement" (p. 8). Plantenga's related method is set apart by three listed differences (p. 8): its trust region has no scaling, it takes affine-scaling steps near the solution, and it reverts to an active-set method when progress is slow. The tone is restatement and contrast, with no criticism. Gonzaga and Mehrotra are thanked for discussions (p. 35).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper starts with an outline algorithm (p. 6). It then connects the outline to the KKT / Newton view (pp. 7–9). Only then comes the precise Algorithm I (pp. 16–17), followed by a paragraph that clarifies two of its steps (p. 17). The theorem is stated before its sequence of lemmas and restated with a proof map at the end (pp. 21, 32–33). The analysis is presented as the justification of design details whose role "may not be apparent to the reader at this point" (p. 20). Each outcome of the theorem is interpreted for a user: outcome (ii) "may be just as relevant to the problem as satisfying the KKT conditions" (p. 21). Hedges are few and local ("it seems to be the best scale", p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is at the algorithm and theory level: a framework and its global theory, with no software in this paper. The limits are concrete:
  - the barrier-parameter update and the rate of convergence are not treated (pp. 2, 33, 35);
  - the choice of B_k and of the multipliers "is not addressed here" (p. 18);
  - the primal-dual analysis is not carried out (p. 8);
  - the nondifferentiable merit function may need a second-order correction or a watchdog against the Maratos effect; their experience favours the second-order correction (p. 35).

  The stated future work is the rate of convergence, the barrier strategy and how accurately to solve the barrier subproblems (p. 35).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: failure modes are written into the main theorem. Outcomes (i) and (ii) are named as problem properties a user can act on, and both are signalled by a diverging penalty parameter (pp. 20–21). The undecided choices (barrier update, B_k, multipliers) are listed openly (pp. 2, 18, 35).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the classical engine is kept. The final remarks describe the strategy as using a well-developed algorithm for equality-constrained optimization and enforcing s > 0 through the trust region and the barrier term (p. 35). Only the slack handling is new: scaling, fraction to the boundary and the slack reset (pp. 4, 17–18). But there is no repair driven by an estimate and no recovery path, and the new regime is inequality constraints, not noise or inexactness.
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the theory covers the method as it would be run, with inexact CG or dogleg steps under Cauchy decrease (pp. 11–15). But it is global convergence only (p. 33), the kind of result Method 5 says would also bless steepest descent. Its stated job is to guide and justify the design (pp. 20, 35) and to classify failures, not to separate good methods from bad ones.
- Heuristic 6 evidence (robustness before speed): global behaviour is analysed and the rate is explicitly deferred (pp. 9, 33).
- New-pattern candidate: **failure outcomes as theorem cases** (pp. 20–21, 33–34; see also S061).
- New-pattern candidate: **map the new method onto known methods** (exact conditions under which it reduces to primal or primal-dual Newton, pp. 7–9; see also S021 pp. 4–5, S078 pp. 7, 10, S061 pp. 18, 23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Specify subproblem inexactness as Cauchy-decrease conditions, plus a range-space condition for the normal step, that truncated CG or dogleg satisfy automatically. The theory then covers the solver the code actually uses (pp. 11–12, 15).
- (D4, D7) State the global theorem as an exhaustive list of outcomes. Make every non-KKT outcome a recognisable problem property (no first-order direction that improves feasibility; LICQ failing at a limit point), each flagged by a quantity the code can print (penalty parameter → ∞) (p. 21).
- (D3) Leave constraint-rank assumptions out of the hypotheses, so that their failure becomes an outcome of the theorem. Then name your most restrictive remaining assumption and say when it fails (p. 20).
- (D2) When bounds are added to a trust-region SQP method, scale the bounded variables by their current values inside the trust region, and keep them positive with a fraction-to-the-boundary rule rather than by shrinking the radius (p. 4).
- (D2) Write down the exact conditions under which the new step equals the established Newton step: strict convexity, an exact solve and an inactive trust region. The local theory can then be borrowed, and the global behaviour is the part left to analyse (pp. 7–9).

**Quotes**:
- "The practical value of our analysis, as we will show, is that the situations under which Algorithm I can fail represent problem characteristics that are of interest to a user and that can be characterized in simple mathematical terms" (p. 20)
- "In this paper we have focused on primal methods because they are easier to analyze and we have devoted much attention to their global convergence properties because the analysis provides important clues on how to design the algorithms" (p. 35)

### S021 · On the solution of equality constrained quadratic programming problems arising in optimization (SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing 23(4), 1376–1395, 2001, doi:10.1137/s1064827598345667)
**Read level**: full. All 30 file pages: pp. 1–27 (introduction, CG without a null-space basis, error analysis, iterative refinement, residual update, numerical results, conclusions, acknowledgements) read; the reference list (pp. 28–30) skimmed. The digits are stripped, so the entries of the four result tables (pp. 23–26), the problem dimensions, the iteration counts and the cosine threshold are unreadable. Figure axes partly survive (pp. 10, 14, 16, 20).
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Nicholas I. M. Gould (Rutherford Appleton Laboratory), Mary E. Hribar (Rice) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the linear-algebra kernel of the tangential (CG) step in the Byrd–Hribar–Nocedal interior code. The test QPs were generated by that code (p. 22). The techniques went into NITRO, GALAHAD and HSL QP modules (p. 25).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Many constrained-optimization algorithms use CG to solve equality-constrained QPs, min ½xᵀHx + cᵀx subject to Ax = b (p. 2). The paper gives two reasons to avoid computing a null-space basis Z (p. 2):
  - With Z, the algorithm needs two different linear systems per iteration, and Z combined with the trust-region scaling raises the difficult issue of preconditioning the dense reduced Hessian ZᵀHZ. Without Z, one system suffices.
  - Z can cause unnecessary ill-conditioning, and the carefully built LUSOL basis needs two LU factorizations.

  The price is named up front: rounding errors can make the iterates violate Ax = b and can even make CG fail (pp. 2–3). A practical case is CUTE problem ORTHREGA, which "cannot be solved to a prescribed accuracy" (p. 10).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Reduced-space preconditioned CG (Algorithm I, p. 4) is rewritten in expanded form (Algorithm II, p. 5), which does not depend on the choice of Z. The scaled projection is applied either through the normal equations (a Cholesky factorization of AAᵀ) or through an augmented system (a symmetric indefinite factorization). The augmented system with a G block is the convenient form for a general preconditioner G; the explicit normal-equations formula is unappealing unless G is simple, and multiple projections need G = I or a diagonal G (pp. 6–7, 13).

  Diagnosis: near the solution, r = Hx + c approaches the range of Aᵀ while its projection g goes to zero, so the projection suffers cancellation (p. 8).

  Remedies:
  - multiple projections, i.e. fixed-precision iterative refinement (pp. 13–17);
  - the residual update: replace r by r − Aᵀy, with y the least-squares multiplier estimate. This leaves the iterates unchanged in exact arithmetic (p. 17). In optimization terms, CG starts from the gradient of the Lagrangian instead of the gradient of the objective (p. 18). For G = I it costs one projection per iteration (p. 19);
  - the steplength α = gᵀg / pᵀHp and a stopping test on gᵀg, whose numerators cannot turn negative (pp. 20–21);
  - Algorithm IV: the residual update plus refinement, applied only when the cosine test fails (pp. 20–21).

  What is kept: the CG iteration and the factorization. What changes: which of the mathematically equivalent residuals is projected.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A has full row rank. H is assumed positive definite on the null space "for convenience". This is not needed in trust-region use, where CG stops at negative curvature (p. 2). The error analysis scales A to unit norm, takes G = I, and counts rounding only in forming the factor and in the solves (pp. 10–11, 14). All examples use G = I, because the efficiency of preconditioners is not the subject (p. 9). Arithmetic is double precision throughout. Extended precision is rejected as unsuitable for general-purpose software (p. 15).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**:
  - With the normal equations, the relative error in g is governed by unit roundoff times the square of the condition number of A, times ‖v‖/‖g‖, which is roughly ‖r‖/‖Pr‖ (pp. 11–12). The augmented system gives a similar bound, linear in the condition number, and is "usually but not always more stable" (p. 12).
  - With multiple projections the error contracts R-linearly (pp. 14–15). A comparison with the least-squares perturbation bound shows that the normal-equations approach is not backward stable (p. 15).
  - The analysis explains why fixed-precision refinement of the normal equations cannot help: the Cholesky factorization is already backward stable. The authors tested it and found no improvement (p. 15).
  - The residual update is interpreted as a limited "iterative semirenement" (p. 18).
  - Accuracy diagnostic: the cosine of the angle between g and the rows of A, linked to the Arioli–Demmel–Duff backward error. It is required to stay a modest multiple of machine precision; the threshold value is stripped (pp. 9–10).
  - The bound is checked against a run: it "can often be overly pessimistic it appears to be reasonably tight in this example" (p. 12).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - Small examples: a constructed example with an ill-conditioned A (pp. 8–9), and a CUTE CVXEQP problem with the residual and the cosine plotted at each iteration (pp. 9–10). The CVXEQP run is repeated with each remedy (figures on pp. 14, 16, 20) and with the new α and stopping formulas inside plain Algorithm II (p. 21).
  - Main test: QPs harvested from the last iteration of the interior code on CUTE problems, solved with unpreconditioned CG. Three variants (stand, ir, update) are each run with the augmented and the normal-equations projections, under a deliberately demanding residual test. Superscripts mark which stop occurred instead: iteration limit, trust-region bound, negative curvature, or rᵀg < 0 (p. 22).
  - Result: standard CG met the test on none of the problems, while refinement and the residual update succeeded in most (p. 22). CPU times come with a caution that the standard runs stopped early (p. 22). For three problems no variant met the test; the best residuals are reported (pp. 22, 24).
  - Indirect test inside the NLP code: function evaluations and number of projections (pp. 25–26). With the augmented system the residual update changes the projection count in only a few problems, but there "the improvements are very substantial". With the normal equations it gives a substantial reduction in about half the problems (p. 25).

  None of the table values are readable in this text.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper respects the null-space approach, which has been successful in many algorithms, and calls the LUSOL basis "largely successful" (p. 2). Of Coleman and Verma's approach it says it "has considerable merit" and that "It remains to be seen how such an approach compares" with the new methods (p. 7). It traces the projected-CG idea back to earlier work (p. 7). It declines Björck's shifted augmented system because a good shift is hard to choose (p. 12). The analysis rests on Higham, Björck and Arioli–Demmel–Duff (pp. 9, 12, 15–16). Conn, Toint, M. Wright, Gill and Saunders are thanked (p. 27).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Examples come before analysis: "These arguments will be made more precise in the next section" (p. 8). The central claim is phrased "We thus contend" (p. 2). Each remedy is shown as a rerun of the same example, with a figure. Untested ideas are flagged: "Although we have not experimented on this idea for this paper" (p. 22). The conclusions rank the remedies: the residual update almost always suffices, refinement alone is weaker, and the combination is both cheap and accurate (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm and error analysis for a solver kernel, plus software. The techniques are ingredients of NITRO, GALAHAD and HSL QP modules (p. 25), and the general-G variant is the backbone of HSL nonconvex QP packages, both interior-point and active-set (p. 22). The limits:
  - preconditioners are not evaluated (p. 9);
  - the residual update for general G is untested (p. 22);
  - three problems were solved by no variant (p. 22);
  - the comparison with Coleman–Verma is left open (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the design choice of working without a null-space basis is offered as an option for general-purpose codes (p. 2), and the remedies were shipped in production packages (pp. 22, 25).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence, with rounding error as the regime instead of noise. The CG engine is kept. The failing component, the projection under cancellation, is named (p. 8) and repaired alone (pp. 17–21). The repair is triggered when an internal diagnostic, the cosine, exceeds a threshold, with refinement as the recovery (pp. 13–14, 20–21). The repair is ablated: resetting the residual only at the start was not enough (pp. 18–19).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (partial): the error bound is compared with an observed run, and its pessimism is stated (p. 12).
- Heuristic 1 evidence (failure demo first, with an internal diagnostic): the constructed example and CVXEQP with the cosine plotted (pp. 8–10). Heuristic 2 (instrument the inside): termination-type superscripts (p. 22). Heuristic 5 (cheapest adequate option): the G = I update needs one projection per iteration, and extended precision is rejected (pp. 15, 19).
- New-pattern candidate: **pick the numerically best of mathematically equivalent forms** (residual representative, steplength and stopping formulas; pp. 17–21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) When a projected iteration loses accuracy, look for an invariance (here r → r − Aᵀy leaves CG unchanged) and pick the representative of smallest norm. Here that is the gradient of the Lagrangian with least-squares multipliers (pp. 17–18).
- (D4) Attach a cheap scalar diagnostic, computed at every iteration, to an invariant of exact arithmetic (the cosine between the projected residual and the rows of A), and trigger the costly fix only when the diagnostic is violated (pp. 9–10, 20–21).
- (D2) Prefer formulas whose numerators cannot change sign under rounding: α = gᵀg / pᵀHp, and a stopping test on gᵀg (pp. 20–21).
- (D5) Evaluate a kernel at two levels. First, run it on subproblems harvested from the real outer solver, with a deliberately demanding tolerance and marks for each type of failure. Second, report the full outer solver's counts (function evaluations, projections) (pp. 22, 25).
- (D5) Ablate how often a fix is applied (once at the start versus every iteration) to show that it must be repeated (pp. 18–19).

**Quotes**:
- "We thus contend that it can be very useful for general-purpose optimization codes to provide the option of not computing with a null-space basis, and the development of suitable methods is our goal in this paper" (p. 2)
- "We believe it is reasonable to attribute the failure of the CG algorithm to the deviation of the iterates from the constraint manifold" (p. 9)

### S078 · Assessing the potential of interior methods for nonlinear optimization (Large-Scale PDE-Constrained Optimization, 167–183, 2003, doi:10.1007/978-3-642-55508-4_10)
**Read level**: full. All 17 file pages: pp. 1–16 (introduction, overview of the four codes, testing environment, results for the three problem classes, final remarks, acknowledgements) read; the reference list (pp. 16–17) skimmed. The digits are stripped, so many numbers are unreadable: the problem counts, the size threshold for CPU reporting, the stopping tolerance, the CPU and iteration limits, the percentage on p. 14 and the whole robustness table on p. 15. The performance-profile plots survive only as axis labels and legends. The figures that SKILL.md Method 1 attributes to this paper (10⁻⁶, 30 CPU minutes, 1000 major iterations, n > 100) cannot be checked against this file.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales (ITAM), Richard A. Waltz, Guanghui Liu, Jean-Pierre Goux (Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: KNITRO's own authors benchmark it against LOQO, SNOPT and filterSQP. The observations become a to-do list for KNITRO: a preconditioner and an adaptive initial barrier parameter (pp. 15–16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The goal is to evaluate how efficient and robust interior methods are for NLP and what potential they have on very large problems. The paper also aims to show their strengths and weaknesses, suggest improvements, and find the problem classes they suit (p. 1). The trigger is that many interior methods had been proposed but few implementations were publicly available (p. 2). The question: are the interior methods in LOQO and KNITRO competitive with established large-scale NLP codes (p. 2)?
- **D2 Core idea and study design**:
  - Choice of codes: two interior codes that differ in globalization (line search vs trust region) and in linear algebra (direct factorization vs projected CG) (p. 2). They are compared with two SQP codes, chosen "because we have experience with both codes and because they constitute two complementary approaches" (p. 2): SNOPT uses a line search with convex quasi-Newton models, filterSQP a trust region with nonconvex models.
  - Problem classes: the problems are split into unconstrained, equality-constrained and general problems "To focus on algorithmic features" (p. 3). For each class the paper states what each code reduces to: three Newton variants and a dense quasi-Newton method on unconstrained problems (p. 7), four SQP variants on equality-constrained problems (p. 10), interior versus active-set methods on general problems (pp. 12–13). Differences can then be traced to the component active in that class. For example, KNITRO's CG-and-projection step computation is "fully active" on equality-constrained problems (p. 11).
  - Theory status: the overview records each code's theoretical standing. LOQO has no global convergence theory (p. 4). An algorithm similar to KNITRO's has one (p. 5). SNOPT's is hard to establish (p. 5). Filter theory is still ongoing research (p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model (test environment)**:
  - Problems: smooth only (p. 2), drawn from CUTE as translated to AMPL by Benson and Vanderbei. LPs and QPs are excluded because specialised methods treat them better, and so are feasibility problems and problems with only bound constraints. Default dimensions are used (p. 6).
  - Settings: stopping tests could not be made uniform, so they were made "as similar as possible", and KNITRO's test was modified to match SNOPT's (p. 6). All codes ran with default settings and the same tolerance; hitting the CPU-time or major-iteration limit counted as a failure (pp. 6–7). The values are unreadable.
  - SNOPT is first-order only. The authors use this disparity to assess quasi-Newton approximations; no second-derivative version of SNOPT existed when the tests were run (p. 7).
  - Measures: CPU time is reported only above a size threshold, because set-up costs dominate small problems; function evaluations are reported for all problems (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis techniques (no proofs)**:
  - Dolan–Moré log₂ performance profiles of CPU time and of function evaluations, per class (p. 8).
  - Robustness is judged from the evaluation profiles and the solved-count table, because the CPU profiles leave out small problems (p. 9).
  - Problem-by-problem reading of runs and solver output to attribute effects to components: ill-conditioning causes many CG iterations in KNITRO (p. 9), and inefficient factorization makes filterSQP slow (pp. 9, 11). Second-order corrections were checked and excluded as the cause of filterSQP's times (p. 11).
  - Accounting corrections in both directions: LOQO's one unnecessary evaluation per iteration is discounted (p. 10). LOQO's unconstrained stopping test is often less demanding because of scaling differences (p. 10). SNOPT may undercount evaluations when the objective is linear (p. 14).
  - A targeted follow-up checks whether SNOPT's strength on constrained problems was an artefact of small sizes: selected problems at three growing dimensions, with the null result reported (p. 14).
  - A separate test of KNITRO's fixed initial barrier value (p. 15).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: LOQO, KNITRO, SNOPT and filterSQP, run on a Sun Ultra in double precision with default settings (pp. 6–7). CPU and evaluation profiles are shown for each class (figures on pp. 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14), plus a table of problems solved, including the larger ones (p. 15). Failures are counted. KNITRO's own weaknesses are reported: slowness on ill-conditioned problems (p. 9), a higher time per iteration than LOQO on many problems (p. 15), and an initial barrier value that "can be highly detrimental in some cases" (p. 15).
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper refuses to rank. It warns readers against using its results to rank the codes (p. 2). ### S061 · On the convergence of Newton iterations to non-stationary points (Mathematical Programming 99(1), 127–148, 2004, doi:10.1007/s10107-003-0376-8)
**Read level**: full. All 25 file pages: pp. 1–24 (introduction, nonlinear equations with regularized and trust-region variants, unconstrained minimization, constrained optimization in the feasible and infeasible cases) read; the reference list (pp. 24–25) skimmed. The file is a Northwestern Optimization Technology Center report dated "April"; its number and year are stripped. Digits and symbols are stripped, so theorem and example numbers, all example data and the three numerical tables (pp. 12, 13, 18) are unreadable.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Marcelo Marazzi (Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the answer by analysis to the Wächter–Biegler failure example for interior methods (p. 2) and to Fletcher's question about Newton's method for minimization (p. 14).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In the authors' view, a method that fails in practice usually seems to converge to a spurious solution, or makes negligible progress. So convergence to a non-solution point is the failure of practical interest (p. 2). There are three entry points:
  - Powell's nonlinear-equation example, in which Newton's method with an exact line search converges to a singular nonstationary point (pp. 2–4);
  - Wächter and Biegler's three-variable problem, on which a class of interior methods never becomes feasible (p. 2);
  - Fletcher's question whether the same can happen in minimization, where "to the best of our knowledge this question has not been investigated" (p. 14).

  The paper studies three problem classes: nonlinear equations, unconstrained minimization and constrained optimization (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and design**: The key insight is that failure comes from the interaction between the Newton direction and the merit function. Near the singular set, the directions become very long and nearly perpendicular to it. Along these directions the merit function is locally convex, so the line search keeps the iterates close to the set (pp. 4–5). The results, by problem class:
  - Nonlinear equations: the sign of the second directional derivative of φ = ½‖F‖² along the null vector decides whether the Newton displacements shrink to zero or stay bounded below (Theorem, pp. 5–8). The result is extended to any rank deficiency and to Wolfe line searches (pp. 8–10). The range assumption is shown to be necessary by two examples (pp. 10–11).
  - Unconstrained minimization: with positive-definite Hessians and Wolfe line searches, Newton's method cannot converge to such a point (rank n−1, gradient outside the range of the Hessian; Lemma, pp. 14–16). The authors attribute the difference to the line search acting on f rather than on the norm of the gradient (p. 16).
  - Constrained optimization: a primal-dual interior method whose merit function is the norm of the KKT residual can stall at a singular nonstationary point even when all iterates are feasible (pp. 17–18). A feasible primal-dual method that uses the barrier function as merit, Wolfe steps and a bounded modified Hessian cannot (pp. 19–22). Infeasible Newton-type methods can fail to reach feasibility even with no bounds involved (pp. 22–24).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - F is twice continuously differentiable (p. 3).
  - The line search takes the first local minimizer or a step in the first Wolfe interval. Backtracking is not covered; that would need the merit function to be unimodal along the search rays (p. 8).
  - The Jacobian rank is n−1 for simplicity, and the result is then extended (p. 9).
  - Minimization: the Hessian is positive definite at every iterate. This "may appear to be restrictive", but practical methods modify the Hessian, so the lemma covers an "unfavorable situation" (p. 16).
  - Constrained case: μ is fixed until the barrier problem is solved (p. 17). Assumptions A1–A3 (p. 20): f is bounded below on the feasible region; derivatives are bounded on its convex hull; the modified matrices have eigenvalues bounded above and away from zero. The iterates are strictly feasible (p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis and proof techniques**:
  - From the SVD of the Jacobian, the Newton direction becomes arbitrarily long and parallel to the null vector (pp. 5–6).
  - The merit function is restricted to the normalised Newton ray, h(τ) = φ(x + τ d/‖d‖), and its first local minimiser is bounded by a Taylor expansion. There are two cases, according to the sign of the second derivative (pp. 6–8).
  - A grid plot of the estimated step length |h′/h″| shows the theorem's prediction (p. 9).
  - Counterexamples show that the range condition is necessary (pp. 10–11).
  - The minimization lemma is proved by contradiction, from the curvature Wolfe condition and the mean-value theorem (pp. 15–16).
  - The positive result uses a Zoutendijk-type argument on a level set that the barrier keeps away from the boundary, where the gradient is Lipschitz (pp. 20–22).
  - Capping the multipliers keeps the Hessian modification inactive near a regular solution, so quadratic convergence is not harmed (p. 22).
  - Using the affine invariance of Newton's method, a change of variables maps the new constrained example onto the earlier equation example (p. 23).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: illustrations rather than benchmarks.
  - Powell's example is plotted (pp. 4, 9).
  - Regularized Newton with several thresholds stays very inefficient even with a large regularization parameter (p. 12). An SVD-based regularization "does not perform any better" (p. 12).
  - MINPACK's LMDER, a Levenberg–Morrison–Marquardt trust-region code, solves the same problem in few iterations (pp. 12–13).
  - An iteration table shows a primal-dual method with the KKT-norm merit function on a one-variable problem (pp. 17–18).

  The table values are unreadable here.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper builds directly on Powell's example and the Wächter–Biegler example. Its own constrained examples are presented as different from Wächter and Biegler's: in the second one the bounds play no role (pp. 2–3, 22–24). It quotes Fletcher's question from his textbook and gives a partial answer (pp. 14–16). Trust-region methods are presented as unaffected in theory and efficient in practice on the example (p. 12). The interior-method class is not attacked; the merit function is identified as the cause (pp. 18, 24).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Open questions are marked as such throughout. Whether the conditions always lead to convergence "is an open question" (p. 8). The weaker-assumption case "must be considered open", and no counterexample was found for the remaining case (p. 16). For primal-dual systems it is "not clear" whether convergence can be proved (pp. 18–19). The theorem is translated into what an implementation shows: a step-size stopping test fires, or the line search fails (p. 8). The paper alternates theorem, example and plot, with an "Example Revisited" (p. 9), and ends with a summary paragraph (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a theory of failure: conditions under which convergence to singular nonstationary points can and cannot occur, new counterexamples, and a positive result for feasible interior methods with the barrier merit function (pp. 19–22). The limits:
  - backtracking line searches are not covered (p. 8);

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To analyse a stall, restrict the merit function to the normalised search ray and study the sign of its second directional derivative along the singular direction. The sign separates "steps shrink to zero" from "steps stay bounded below" (pp. 5–8).
- (D4) Show that a hypothesis is needed with a pair of examples: one where its failure breaks the conclusion, and one where the conclusion survives anyway (pp. 10–11).
