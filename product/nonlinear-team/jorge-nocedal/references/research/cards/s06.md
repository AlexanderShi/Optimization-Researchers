# Batch s06 · Jorge Nocedal · round 1, supplement

Batch intent: read eight 2008–2017 works on two topics. The first is the active-set programme after SLQP: SQP+ with an EQP phase, the piecewise-linear-model method, two-phase matrix-splitting methods for BQP and LCP, and three second-order methods for ℓ1 problems (iiCG, inexact SQA, OBA). ### S060 · A sequential quadratic programming algorithm with an additional equality constrained phase (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 2012, 10.1093/imanum/drq037)
**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract and §1 (pp. 1–2); §2 with Algorithm I and the remarks on safeguards and motivation (pp. 2–6); Assumptions 3.1 with the authors' comment, Lemmas 3.2–3.4 with the proofs of 3.2–3.3, and Theorem 3.5 (pp. 6–12); Assumptions 4.1, Lemma 4.2, the Theorem 4.3 statement and the remarks after its proof (pp. 12–17); §5 implementation, Algorithm SQP+ and experiments (pp. 17–21); §6 (pp. 21–22); the first appendix table (p. 26). Not followed: the proofs of Lemma 4.2 and Theorem 4.3 (pp. 13–17) and appendix tables pp. 27–29. First page checked: title, and "Jorge Nocedal" second of three authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales, Yuchen Wu (Richard Byrd thanked, p. 22) · **Position in trajectory**: the SQP branch of the active-set programme. It follows the SLQP method of Byrd, Gould, Nocedal & Waltz (refs. [4, 5], p. 6). S114 (p. 3) groups it with Gould and Robinson's method under the name SQP+.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Contemporary SQP has two drawbacks. Convex quasi-Newton QPs "can be slow when solving large or badly scaled problems", and exact-Hessian SQP must find global solutions of indefinite QPs (p. 2). The paper gives two practical motivations (p. 6). One is the difficulty of putting second derivatives into SQP codes built for convex subproblems, such as SNOPT. The other is the SLQP experience: its LP phase picks good working sets, but it is erratic under degeneracy and hard to warm start, because the box trust region is active at every iteration.
- **D2 Core idea**: Each iteration has two phases (pp. 2–3, 6). A convex IQP with a simple positive definite B_k estimates the working set W_k and drives global convergence. An EQP on W_k with the exact Hessian of the Lagrangian supplies the fast rate. The EQP step is contracted by β so that all linearized constraints hold. The line search runs along the segment x_k → x_k + d^IQ → x_k + d^IQ + βd^EQ and falls back to backtracking along d^IQ (pp. 3–5). The penalty parameter is updated from the IQP step by rule (2.9)–(2.11) (p. 4). Unchanged from classical SQP: the ℓ1 merit function and the BFGS IQP. The multipliers are taken from the EQP (pp. 2, 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: For global convergence: bounded iterates and derivatives, feasible IQPs, bounded IQP multipliers, and uniformly bounded positive definite B_k (pp. 6–7). The authors call these "fairly restrictive". They adopt them to get "a fairly compact convergence analysis" instead of an analysis built on relaxations and other devices (p. 7). For local convergence: C², full-rank active Jacobian, strict complementarity, IQP Hessians bounded away from singularity, and second-order sufficiency (pp. 12–13). Unit steps are assumed; the Maratos effect is left to second-order corrections or watchdog steps (p. 17).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Bounded IQP multipliers make the penalty parameter eventually constant (Lemma 3.2, pp. 7–8). The sufficient-decrease test uses the model reduction instead of the directional derivative, which "allows the algorithm to take longer steps" (p. 7). A backtracking bound then gives qred(d^IQ) → 0 (Lemma 3.3, pp. 8–10), which leads to asymptotic feasibility and KKT limit points (Lemma 3.4, Theorem 3.5, pp. 10–12). Robinson's active-set identification result (Lemma 4.2, p. 13), together with Newton steps on W*, gives quadratic convergence (Theorem 4.3, p. 15). The authors contrast this with classical exact-Hessian SQP, which must assume that the QP solver picks a particular local minimizer, while their result "relies only on the second-order sufficiency conditions" (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: A Matlab prototype built on Gilbert's sqplab, with KNITRO/Active as the QP solver and penalty rule (2.10)–(2.11); both changes are also given to the sqplab baseline (pp. 17–18). The EQP is skipped when constraint gradients are dependent or the KKT inertia is wrong (pp. 18–19). Test set: small and medium CUTEr problems, fixed in advance. It reports iteration performance profiles against sqplab, then major iterations against SNOPT 7.2-1 with LPs and QPs removed, plus a warning that the two codes use different scalings and termination tests (pp. 20–21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are algorithmic and theoretical, and "A fully developed implementation of the proposed approach is outside the scope of this paper." (p. 6). Stated limits: solving large QPs remains the main cost of SQP, so SQP+ targets "the class of problems that is currently solved by SQP methods" (p. 22); the cost of the EQP phase is argued from the SLQP literature, not timed (p. 20); the projection variant gained little except on bound-constrained problems (p. 21). Future direction: projected CG with Hessian-vector products or gradient differences, which would need first derivatives only (p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the EQP phase is ablated inside one code base, since "the other aspects of these two algorithms are identical" (p. 20). The baseline got the same QP solver and penalty rule (pp. 17–18). The SNOPT comparison is presented as not like-for-like (pp. 20–21).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the classical convex-QP SQP engine is kept. The repair, however, adds a second-order phase instead of replacing the failing component, and there is no estimate-driven recovery path (pp. 2, 6).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the stated motivations are SNOPT-type codes' difficulty with second derivatives and SLQP's erratic, hard-to-warm-start LP phase (p. 6).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence and ⚠ variant: the local theory is used to separate SQP+ from exact-Hessian SQP, since it needs no assumption about which local QP solution the solver picks (p. 17). But the global analysis covers a "somewhat idealized" algorithm (p. 6) under assumptions the authors call restrictive (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: *cheap predictor carries the proof, second-order phase carries the rate*: the IQP gives global convergence and the working set, the EQP the speed (p. 6). The same split recurs in S114, S113, S087 and S089 in this batch.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ablate a new phase by adding it to an existing simple code and giving the baseline every other improvement you made (QP solver, penalty rule), so the difference isolates the phase (pp. 17–18, 20).
- Fix the test set before running, and say so in the paper (p. 20).
- When comparing a prototype with a mature code, remove the problem classes the mature code treats specially (LPs and QPs, solved in one major iteration by SNOPT) and print the caveats (pp. 20–21).

**Quotes**:
- "One of the main questions we wish to investigate is whether the EQP phase does, in fact, add significant benefits to the standard SQP approach." (p. 6)
- "The test set was selected before the numerical experiments were performed; no problems were removed or added during the testing process." (p. 20)

### S113 · Subspace accelerated matrix splitting algorithms for asymmetric and symmetric linear complementarity problems (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2013, 10.1137/110845094)
**Read level**: partial, preprint version with a different title. Read: abstract and §1 with Algorithms 1.1–1.2 and Table 1.1 (pp. 1–3); §2.1 and Algorithm 2.1 (pp. 4–5); §2.2 with Lemmas 2.1–2.2, Theorem 2.4, the end of its proof and the remarks (pp. 6–9); §2.3 with Tables 2.1–2.4 (pp. 9–12); Assumption 3.1, §3.1 and Algorithm 3.1 (pp. 11–14); the Theorem 3.4 statement and remarks (pp. 18–20); §3.3 with Tables 3.1–3.2 (pp. 20–22); §4 (pp. 21–22). Not followed: the proofs of Lemmas 3.1–3.3 and Theorem 3.4 (pp. 14–19) and Corollary 2.3 (p. 7). First page checked: the file is titled "Subspace accelerated matrix splitting algorithms for bound-constrained quadratic programming and linear complementarity problems", with "JORGE M. NOCEDAL" third of four authors (p. 1). It has the same authors and subject as the SIOPT 23(3) article listed under S113, so it is read as that article's preprint. The journal title page and pagination were not seen.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Daniel P. Robinson, Liming Feng, Jong-Shi Pang · **Position in trajectory**: a convergent, general-purpose successor to the two-phase complementarity solvers of Feng et al. (American options) and Morales, Nocedal & Smelyanskiy (contact mechanics), which "have no convergence guarantees" (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two-phase methods work well in practice, but none covers the hard cases (pp. 1–2). Moré–Toraldo solve BQPs with a projected-gradient phase. Kočvara–Zowe use SOR or CG phases but assume M positive definite. The FLMN method solves LCPs from finance and mechanics without guarantees. The open cases are nonconvex BQPs and asymmetric LCPs. An asymmetric LCP has no "convenient relationship" with a BQP, so two algorithms are needed (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Phase 1 predicts the variables that are zero at the solution by matrix-splitting fixed-point iterations. Projected gradient, Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel and SOR are special cases (Table 1.1, p. 3). Phase 2 solves a subspace problem to accelerate convergence (p. 1).
  - BQP (pp. 4–5): a Cauchy step built from one splitting iteration, with an exact projected search, "drives convergence" (p. 4). Further splitting iterations and a second projected search define the active set, and a trust-region-bounded subspace step follows.
  - LCP (pp. 13–14): there is no objective to decrease. Steps are accepted by contraction tests on the splitting iterates, or by a merit function φ(x) = ‖min(x, Mx+q)‖ with a halving forcing sequence. When both fail, the trust region shrinks. Iterations are classed as C-, M- or F-iterates accordingly.
  - The paper presents this as a generalization of Moré–Toraldo, since projected gradient is "the most basic" splitting (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions**: BQP: M symmetric, possibly indefinite, with a splitting whose B is positive definite (p. 8). LCP: Assumption 3.1, that the splitting iterations are contractions (p. 11). It holds, for example, for strictly diagonally dominant or symmetric positive definite M, and the algorithm "depends crucially" on it (p. 11). Limit points exist if level sets are bounded (pp. 9, 20). The tests drop the trust region and solve the subspace system with Matlab backslash. The authors state that this "does not alter the convergence theory in any way" (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: BQP: a descent property of splitting iterations (Cottle–Pang–Stone, Lemma 5.5.1) and a Cauchy-decrease bound (Lemmas 2.1–2.2, p. 6). Then the algorithm stops at a solution, finds an unbounded ray, or every limit point is first-order (Theorem 2.4, p. 8). LCP: contraction measures computed from the observed ratios, plus the merit-function forcing sequence, give lim inf φ(x_k) = 0 (Theorem 3.4, p. 18). Both results extend to iteration-dependent splittings with uniformly positive definite B_k (pp. 9, 20).
- **D5 Experiments**: In Matlab, against the authors' own implementations of a two-phase projected-gradient method with Barzilai–Borwein steps and of FLMN. For fairness, all methods use the same maximum of three recursive subspace steps and the same termination test (pp. 9–10, 20). Test problems: CUTEr BQPs (strictly convex, convex, nonconvex) and ten random strictly convex BQPs (Tables 2.1–2.4, pp. 10–12); four American-put scenarios (Table 3.1, p. 21); ten random diagonally dominant asymmetric LCPs, run with and without the subspace phase (Table 3.2, pp. 21–22). The tables report iterations, subspace steps and splitting iterations, not CPU time.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contribution is convergence "under weaker assumptions than those assumed for other methods" (abstract, p. 1), with no loss of speed on the home application: Algorithm 3.1 performs "exactly the same as" FLMN (p. 21). Stated limits: the splitting iteration must be inexpensive for the method to pay off (p. 22); the nonconvex tests use the simplest splitting B = I, and a better one is left to future research (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the splitting is chosen from the matrix structure: SOR for positive definite M, B = I for indefinite M (pp. 10–11). The LCP guarantee comes from the structure of the application, whose M_j is diagonally dominant (p. 21).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (outside the noise programme): the two-phase engine of Moré–Toraldo and FLMN is kept. Only the prediction iteration is generalized, and for LCPs an acceptance test with a merit-function fallback and a trust-region shrink is added (pp. 2, 13–14).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: here theory certifies a heuristic that already works; it does not separate a good method from a bad one. Algorithm 3.1 matches FLMN, and what it adds is the guarantee (p. 21).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the baselines were reimplemented in the same environment, with equal subspace steps and termination, "To allow for a fair comparison" (p. 10).
- New-pattern candidate: *cheap predictor carries the proof, second-order phase carries the rate* (the Cauchy point "drives convergence", p. 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Parameterize the prediction phase by a matrix splitting, so that the field's standard method (projected gradient) is its simplest member and one analysis covers every member (pp. 2–3).
- When the problem has no objective (asymmetric LCP), globalize with the observed contraction ratios and keep a merit-function forcing sequence as the way to accept long productive steps (pp. 13–14).
- Before claiming gains elsewhere, show that the provably convergent method reproduces the heuristic incumbent exactly on the incumbent's home application (Table 3.1, p. 21), then ablate the subspace phase on a new problem class (Table 3.2, p. 22).

**Quotes**:
- "Our key contribution in this area is the development of a provably convergent two-phase general purpose method that is efficient at solving the problems considered in [6] as well as more general problems." (p. 2)
- "We find comfort, however, in knowing that Algorithm 3.1 is guaranteed to converge since Assumption 3.1 holds as a result of Mj being diagonally dominant" (p. 21)

### S114 · On the use of piecewise linear models in nonlinear programming (Mathematical Programming 2013, 10.1007/s10107-011-0492-9)
**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract and §1 (pp. 1–3); §2 on the PLA subproblem, EQP phase, line search, Algorithm 2.1 and penalty update (pp. 3–7); §3 and §3.1 with Procedure 3.1 (pp. 7–11); from §4, Assumptions I, the Growth Conditions, Placement Conditions A, Theorem 4.1 and the start of its proof (pp. 11–13), and Theorem 4.8 with the remark after it (p. 18); from §5, Assumptions II, Lemma 5.1, the Theorem 5.2 statement, and Theorem 5.3 with the remark after it (pp. 18–24); the openings of §6 and §6.1 (pp. 25–28); §7 and the references (pp. 32–34). Not followed: the proofs of Lemmas 4.2–4.7 (pp. 14–18) and of Theorem 5.2 (pp. 22–24), and the BFGS-PLA analysis (pp. 28–31). First page checked: title, and "Jorge Nocedal" second of four authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd, Richard A. Waltz, Yuchen Wu · **Position in trajectory**: the third design in the SLQP / SQP+ family; it cites SLQP [2, 3] and SQP+ [19] (pp. 2–3). Implementation and experiments are left to a companion report [7] (pp. 6, 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In the large-scale case, SQP is limited by the cost of solving an inequality-constrained QP at each iteration, whereas interior methods "scale up well with the dimension of the problem" (p. 1). The SL-QP method of Fletcher and Sainz de la Maza predicts the active set with an LP. That LP needs a trust region whose radius is delicate, which is typically active at the LP solution, and which prevents warm starts (p. 2). The entry point is a model between linear and quadratic.
- **D2 Core idea**: The LP's linear objective is replaced by ∇fᵀd + Γ_k(d). Γ_k is a convex piecewise-linear outer approximation of a separable quadratic ½dᵀB_kd, built coordinate by coordinate by Hermite interpolation at a few nodes, one of them at zero (pp. 4, 8–9). The subproblem is still an LP, and the trust region can be dropped altogether (p. 2). Taking Γ_k to be the indicator of a box recovers SL-QP (p. 8). An EQP with the exact Hessian on the working set follows. The ℓ1 merit function, the penalty rule and the segment line search are as in SQP+ (pp. 4–7). A BFGS-PLA variant makes a compact limited-memory BFGS model separable: it orthogonalizes the model's columns and diagonalizes a small matrix (pp. 25–26).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Global: iterates in a convex set with bounded derivatives, feasible PLA subproblems, bounded multipliers, and uniformly bounded positive definite B_k (Assumptions I, p. 11). Dependent working-set gradients and incompatible constraints are assumed not to arise (p. 6). Local: LICQ, strict complementarity and bounded B_k (Assumptions II, p. 19). The local analysis is "self-contained and independent of the analysis in Section 4" (p. 19).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The analysis first states abstract Growth Conditions P1–P3 on Γ_k and m_k (p. 12) and proves global convergence from them (Theorem 4.8, p. 18). A separate result shows that Placement Conditions A on the interpolation nodes imply them (Theorem 4.1, p. 12). Active-set identification needs Γ_k to be flat near zero and to grow at least linearly far away (Lemma 5.1, pp. 19–20; Theorem 5.2, p. 22). Nodes in two intervals proportional to ‖x_k − x*‖ guarantee this (Theorem 5.3, p. 24). The authors note that these intervals resemble Oberlin and Wright's trust-region radius for SL-QP. A node of order ‖x_k − x*‖^1/2 meets the condition, and computable quantities such as the KKT error have that order (p. 24).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a. The paper has no numerical experiments; the implementation and computational results are in the companion paper [7] (pp. 6, 27, 32). Figure 1 only illustrates Γ (p. 9).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The stated contribution is the design of the piecewise-linear model and the theoretical foundations of the method (p. 6). Deferred: incompatible constraints, dependent working-set gradients, and a practical BFGS-PLA implementation with its safeguards (pp. 6, 27). The final remarks give a node-placement procedure that satisfies both the global and the local conditions, with example constants c1 = 0.1, c2 = 10 (p. 32). The authors add that all the new SQP methods, theirs included, are untested in production-quality software (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the starting point is SL-QP's known failure modes, a delicate radius and no warm starts, documented in the group's own SLQP work [2, 23] (p. 2). The paper also states that comparison in production software is still open (p. 3).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the theory here specifies the design (where to put the interpolation nodes) rather than discriminating between methods that are run. The abstract says the analysis "highlights the importance of the placement of the interpolation points" (abstract). With no runs, there is nothing to set the bounds against.
- New-pattern candidate: *model family with two classical methods at its ends*: with Γ the box indicator the method is SL-QP, and as Γ approaches ½dᵀBd it approaches SQP (pp. 8, 2). Compare the storage knob of S138 in s01.
- New-pattern candidate: *cheap predictor carries the proof, second-order phase carries the rate* (the PLA step gives the working set and global convergence, the EQP the rate, pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split a convergence proof into abstract conditions on the model (here growth conditions) and a separate lemma that a concrete construction satisfies them. Other constructions, such as the BFGS-PLA model, then inherit the theory by checking the conditions (pp. 11–12, 18, 27).
- Make a high-dimensional quadratic separable before approximating it coordinate by coordinate: use a diagonal B, or a cheap change of variables for a compact L-BFGS matrix (pp. 8, 25–26).
- Replace constants of the local analysis that are not available (γ_s, γ1) by a rule based on a computable quantity of the right order, such as the square root of the KKT error (pp. 24, 32).

**Quotes**:
- "By using a piecewise linear (as opposed to linear) model in the active-set prediction phase, the new method becomes insensitive to the choice of the trust region constraint — in fact, one can dispense with this constraint altogether." (p. 2)
- "All these new SQP methods have been tested only on prototype implementations and their relative efficiency with respect to SL-QP methods and established SQP methods such as [13, 16] is yet to be explored in production-quality software." (p. 3)

### S087 · An algorithm for quadratic ℓ1-regularized optimization with a flexible active-set strategy (Optimization Methods and Software 2015, 10.1080/10556788.2015.1028062; arXiv:1412.1844)
**Read level**: partial, arXiv v1. Read: abstract and §1 (pp. 1–2); §2 on iiCG-1 with ω, ψ, the gradient balance condition and the cutback (pp. 2–5); §3 on iiCG-2 (p. 5); from §4, the opening, Theorem 4.5 with its proof, Corollary 4.6, and Theorem 4.7 with its proof (pp. 5, 10–12); §5 with the test problems, ISTA-BB-LS, Table 1 and the discussion of Figures 1–8 (pp. 12–19); §6 (p. 19); Appendices 7–8 (pp. 22–23). Not followed: the proofs of Lemmas 4.1–4.4 (pp. 5–10). The Algorithm iiCG-2 listing (p. 6) was only skimmed. First page checked: title, and "Jorge Nocedal" among three authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd, Stefan Solntsev · **Position in trajectory**: one of three ℓ1 papers in this batch. Its "proxnewt" test problems are subproblems extracted from the proximal Newton method of ref. [7] (p. 13), which is S050 (Byrd, Nocedal & Oztoprak).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is a quadratic plus τ‖x‖1 with A symmetric positive semidefinite. The focus is on methods that use second-order information "as an integral part of the iteration" (p. 1). Two-phase methods follow an identification step with a whole cycle of CG iterations; the entry point is to allow a different kind of step at every iteration (abstract; p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Each step is either an ISTA step, which improves the active-set prediction, or a subspace CG step. The choice follows a gradient balance condition, ‖ω‖ ≤ ‖ψ‖, which compares the parts of the minimum-norm subgradient for zero and free variables (pp. 1, 3). The condition is adapted from Dostál and Schöberl's bound-constrained method, but their step along −ω is dropped, "as we have observed that it is more effective to release variables through the ISTA iteration" (p. 5). CG may leave the current orthant if F decreases by c‖v‖²; otherwise the step is cut back (p. 4). iiCG-2 replaces ISTA with a subspace ISTA step, which releases no variables, whenever the balance condition holds (p. 5). Every step costs about one matrix-vector product (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: The analysis assumes A nonsingular, with extreme eigenvalues λ and L (p. 5), and an ISTA steplength with 1/(8L) ≤ α ≤ 1/L (p. 10). Finite identification needs strict complementarity (p. 11). In the tests, B and C are rank-deficient, so the Hessians are singular when γ = 0 (p. 13). Those test problems therefore lie outside the analysed setting (card's observation).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Each constituent step gives sufficient decrease. A cut-back CG step is followed by an ISTA step, which gives a 2-step Q-linear rate (Theorem 4.5, pp. 10–11). The work complexity is counted in matrix-vector products (Corollary 4.6, p. 11). Finite identification of the optimal orthant uses strict-complementarity margins δ1, δ2 and the finite termination of CG (Theorem 4.7, pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Experiments**: Matlab; the code is on GitHub (p. 12). The test set is 4 problem families × 12 values of (γ, τ), 48 problems, with τ set by experiment to span a range of solution sparsities (pp. 13, 22). Accuracy is measured after every matrix-vector product, with a limit of 50,000 (p. 13). First comes a controlled comparison with FISTA and ISTA-BB-LS per matrix-vector product (Table 1, Figs. 1–2, pp. 13–16). Then come CPU-time profiles against SpaRSA, PSSgb, N83 and pdNCG, restricted to Matlab codes; five other codes are dropped as "not competitive" with no results shown (p. 17). The text says Figure 5 covers "all test problems" (p. 17), but its caption lists only spectra, sigrec and myrand (p. 18). Appendix 8 tests sensitivity to α (pp. 22–23).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The contributions are the method, global rates and work-complexity bounds (pp. 1, 19). Limits as printed: the complexity bound is worst case (p. 11); iiCG-2 is "the most efficient in these tests, but not uniformly so" (p. 14); Table 1 shows FISTA ahead at tolerance 10⁻⁴ on several myrand and sigrec problems (p. 15); PSSgb is competitive on high-sparsity problems (p. 19). §6 gives no future directions (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the authors say plainly that the complexity bound is worst case and that their proof device ("a CG step is no worse than a standard gradient step") hides the power of the subspace phase. The result that shows that power is finite identification (p. 11).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence and ⚠ variant: the controlled per-matrix-vector comparison comes first, the borrowed line-search parameters were not tuned to the test set (p. 12), and the losses are visible in Table 1 (p. 15) and in PSSgb's lead at high sparsity (p. 19). Against Method 1 step 6: five codes were dropped without results (p. 17), and the CPU-time figure covers three of the four problem families according to its caption (p. 18).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (weak): a component of the predecessor method (Dostál–Schöberl's step along −ω) is dropped because experiments showed it was not needed (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: *cheap predictor carries the proof, second-order phase carries the rate*, here as a switch at every iteration (pp. 3–5, 10–11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Use a computable split of the optimality measure (the zero and free components of the minimum-norm subgradient) to switch between first-order identification steps and subspace steps (pp. 3, 5).
- Count work in the dominant operation (matrix-vector products) in both the complexity bound and the experiments, so that theory and plots use the same unit (pp. 11, 13).
- Build a test set by extracting real subproblems from an outer method (proxnewt from the proximal Newton method of [7]), and vary conditioning and sparsity with two parameters (p. 13; Appendix 7, p. 22).
- Compare sparse solvers by the Pareto frontier of accuracy against number of nonzeros at several work budgets (Fig. 3, p. 16).

**Quotes**:
- "More generally, Corollary 4.6 represents worst-case analysis, and is not indicative of the overall performance of the algorithm in practice." (p. 11)
- "The choice of parameters M, ξ is as in [38]; we did not attempt to fine tune them to our test set." (p. 12)

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Much work studies how to parallelize SGD. This paper instead studies batch methods that use a sizeable part of the data and second-order information, for a better balance of computation and communication (abstract; pp. 1–2). If the batch changes at every iteration, or if some nodes fail to return gradients, the L-BFGS gradient differences are computed on different data, and "the process can be unstable" (p. 1). The common remedies are to enforce gradient consistency or to keep one batch for several iterations. The paper treats consistency as either impossible (with faults) or undesirable (in multi-batch learning), and keeping a batch as slower (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Compute y_k only on the overlap O_k = S_k ∩ S_{k+1} of consecutive batches (p. 3). The justification is that a Hessian approximation can be chosen much more freely than a gradient (p. 3). There are two sampling schemes (p. 5). In the first, batches are taken in order from shuffled data with built-in overlaps: no extra gradient cost, but the samples are not independent. In the second, random batches are drawn with a random overlap subsample, at a slightly higher cost. With faults, the overlap is the data of nodes that responded in both iterations (p. 4). The steplength is constant (p. 4). For nonconvex problems the update is skipped when yᵀs < ε‖s‖² (p. 6). The two-loop recursion and the memory m are unchanged (p. 4).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Strongly convex case (p. 5): the subsampled Hessians are uniformly bounded on every overlap set, which means each F^O is strongly convex (justified by regularization). In addition, the second moment is bounded and the samples are independent and unbiased. Nonconvex case (p. 6): Lipschitz gradients, F bounded below, and second-moment growth γ² + η‖∇F‖². The paper says the first sampling scheme produces dependent samples (p. 5), so that scheme does not satisfy the independence assumption (card's inference).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Eigenvalue bounds on H_k, proved by adapting [8] and working with B_k = H_k⁻¹ (Lemma 3.1, p. 5; App. A.2, p. 10). With a fixed step, convergence is linear to a neighbourhood whose size grows with α (Theorem 3.2, p. 6). In the nonconvex case, the average of ‖∇F‖² is bounded by a noise term plus a term of order 1/L in the number L of iterations (Theorem 3.4, pp. 6–7). Robbins–Monro step decay gives only a sublinear rate, "of little interest in our context", so Theorem 3.2 is "more relevant to practice" (p. 6). The BFGS method "is known to fail on noconvex problems", with Mascarenhas [17] and Dai [10] cited, which motivates cautious updating in the style of Li–Fukushima [15] (p. 6).

Methods compared: robust L-BFGS; multi-batch L-BFGS with y from different samples; multi-batch gradient descent; serial SGD. Runs use 10 seeds, with mean, best and worst shown, over several batch sizes r and overlaps o (pp. 7, 16). Node-failure probabilities p ∈ {0.1, 0.3, 0.5} are tested in the main text and 0.1–0.5 in Appendix B.2 (pp. 8, 27). Strong and weak scaling are measured on artificial data with up to 128 MPI processes (p. 8). A microbenchmark on Amazon EC2 and a shared cluster measures computation and communication noise, to motivate fault tolerance (p. 26).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the robust variant, its behaviour on a distributed platform, and convergence in the convex and nonconvex cases (abstract; p. 2). Limits as printed: serial SGD reaches similar accuracy at a similar rate at r = 1%, at a higher communication cost (p. 7); the overlap must not be too small (p. 2), and the behaviour holds where r·o is not too small (p. 7); the analysis covers a fixed steplength only (pp. 2, 4). §5 states no future work (p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Proximal Newton methods (here called successive quadratic approximation, SQA) have been analysed, but "the viability of the approach in practice has not been fully explored", and in practice the inner problems must be solved inexactly (p. 3). Earlier SQA papers do not address convergence with inexact subproblem solutions. The criterion of Lee, Sun and Saunders comes closest but has drawbacks (pp. 2, 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: An inner solution x̂ is acceptable if ‖F_q(x_k; x̂)‖ ≤ η_k‖F(x_k)‖ and the model decreases, q_k(x̂) < q_k(x_k). Here F is a continuous semismooth optimality measure built from the soft-thresholding step, F(x) = g(x) − P_[−µ,µ](g(x) − x/τ) (p. 4). For µ = 0 the condition reduces to the inexact-Newton condition of Dembo, Eisenstat and Steihaug (p. 3). A backtracking line search requires a fraction of the decrease of a piecewise-linear model (Algorithm 2.1, p. 5). On naming: "successive quadratic approximation" is preferred to "proximal Newton" because the quadratic is a second-order model, not a step restriction (p. 2). Inner solvers: FISTA (N83), or an orthant-based method (OBM) with Newton-CG or L-BFGS subspace steps (pp. 2, 12–13, 17–18).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Global: f smooth with Lipschitz gradient and bounded below, H_k uniformly positive definite and bounded. Convexity is not needed for the global result (pp. 6, 8). Local: Lipschitz and positive definite Hessian at x*, and τ < 1/‖∇²f(x*)‖ (pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The inexactness conditions and the contraction of the projection give a decrease of the linear model of at least γ‖F(x_k)‖² (pp. 6–7). The Lipschitz bound keeps the steplength bounded below, so F(x_k) → 0 (Theorem 3.1, pp. 6–8). Local rates come from Facchinei and Pang's theory of nonsingular strong Newton approximations (Theorems 4.2–4.4, pp. 9–11): linear for small η_k, superlinear if η_k → 0, quadratic if η_k = O(‖F‖). A sufficient-decrease variant (4.8) is satisfied by accurate enough inner solutions and makes the unit step accepted eventually (Lemma 4.5, Theorem 4.6, pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Experiments**: The benchmark is FISTA applied directly to the problem. The others are PNOPT (Lee et al.) with L-BFGS memory 50 and three SQA variants. Settings: stop at ‖F‖∞ ≤ 10⁻⁵, at most 3000 outer iterations, η_k = max{1/k, 0.1}, θ = 0.1 (pp. 12–13). Problems: inverse covariance estimation (Estrogen, Leukemia, OONR; Tables 1–5, pp. 14–16) and logistic regression (Gisette, RCV1, with µ taken from Lee et al.; Tables 6–9, pp. 16–17). Reported: outer and inner iterations, function and gradient evaluations, Hessian-vector products, and time. On Leukemia, PNOPT ran out of memory with memory 50 and stopped early on its ftol test (p. 15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions: the inexactness criterion, global convergence, a rate controlled by η_k, and acceptance of unit steps (pp. 18–19). Limits as printed: on logistic regression the advantage of SQA is "less pronounced" and needs the right H_k (quasi-Newton) and the right inner solver (OBM) (p. 17); SQA-FISTA is not efficient on Gisette and becomes competitive only at 10⁻⁶ accuracy (p. 17); convergence of OBM is unproved, because its orthant-face identification can produce arbitrarily small steps (p. 18). The introduction promises "a list of questions to explore" in §6 (p. 3), but §6 contains none (pp. 18–19).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (outside the noise programme): the SQA engine is kept, and the one component that made it impractical, the inner termination test, is replaced by a criterion built from a continuous optimality measure. The classical Dembo et al. condition is the special case µ = 0 (pp. 3–4).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the paper tests the simple option that the sophisticated method is assumed to beat, FISTA on the original problem, and prints a split verdict: SQA-FISTA wins clearly on covariance selection but not on logistic regression (pp. 12, 16–17).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the best H_k and inner solver depend on problem structure. The Kronecker-structured Hessian P⁻¹ ⊗ P⁻¹ makes Newton-CG best for covariance selection, and expensive dense Hessian-vector products make quasi-Newton best for logistic regression (pp. 14–17, 19).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the criticism of Lee et al. is a theoretical argument about a practical termination test: because the subgradient of q_k is discontinuous, their criterion can fail at points arbitrarily close to the exact minimizer (p. 5). Theorem 4.6 explains the observed unit steps (pp. 5, 11–12).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the FISTA baseline is the better-performing N83 implementation (p. 13), and PNOPT's ftol and xtol were set to 1e−16 so that those tests would not end its runs early (p. 13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the inner-solver stopping test from a continuous optimality residual of the subproblem (here via soft-thresholding), and check that it reduces to the classical inexact-Newton condition in the smooth case (pp. 3–4).
- Before claiming that a nested method helps, run the plain first-order method directly on the original problem as the benchmark, and report inner and outer work separately (pp. 12, 15–17).
- Give the baseline its strongest implementation (the better of two FISTA codes), and disable a competitor's stopping tests that would end its runs early (p. 13).

- "we prefer not to use the term “proximal” in this context since the quadratic term in (1.2) is better interpreted as a second-order model rather than as a term that simply restricts the size of the step." (p. 2)
- "Note that sqa-fista is not efficient; in fact it requires a much larger number of inner iterations than the total number of iterations in fista." (p. 17)
