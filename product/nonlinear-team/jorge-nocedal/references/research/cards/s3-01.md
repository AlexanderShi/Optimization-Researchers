### S120 · A study of conjugate gradient methods (Stanford Systems Optimization Laboratory Technical Report SOL 78-29, 1978; no DOI; DTIC ADA066391)
**Read level**: partial. All prose on file pp. 3–57 was read: the title page and abstract (pp. 3–4), §1 (pp. 5–6), §2 with the three interpretations and the generalizations (pp. 7–22), §3 (pp. 23–39), §4 (pp. 40–48), §5 (pp. 49–52), the Appendix (p. 53), the references (pp. 54–56) and the DD Form 1473 (p. 57). Most equations are garbled by the OCR, so the statements of Theorems 2.1, 3.1, 3.3 and 3.4 and Lemma 3.2 were read from their prose, and their proofs (pp. 18–21, 25–26, 31–33, 34–36, 37–38, 49–52) were followed only in outline. First page checked: title "A STUDY OF CONJUGATE GRADIENT METHODS", "L. Nazareth and J. Nocedal" (p. 5; also DD Form 1473, p. 57).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: L. Nazareth · **Position in trajectory**: the earliest work in his record (December 1978, p. 57). Funded by ONR, NSF and DOE (p. 3). It cites a companion SOL report, Nazareth & Nocedal, "Properties of conjugate gradient algorithms with inexact line searches", Report 78-1, as [25] (p. 55). That this is S162 is inferred from the title. It also announces a convergence analysis "with Variable Storage" as a forthcoming Rice University report [4] (pp. 6, 54).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Since Fletcher and Reeves, "many variants" of nonlinear CG have appeared, and the family is valued "when the number of variables is large" (p. 5). The entry is unification. Each algorithm is presented "within the context of existing algorithms", with attention to CG methods that use variable-metric information (p. 5). Linear-systems CG is excluded (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three interpretations of CG on quadratics serve as "a good staging ground for extending the conjugate gradient method" (p. 10). They are (I) the relationship to BFGS, (II) a specialized Gram–Schmidt and (III) an implicit Lanczos process (pp. 10–13). Each extension relaxes one condition. A metric H gives preconditioned CG, and a quasi-Newton H that satisfies t secant equations leaves at most n − t steps (pp. 15–16). An arbitrary starting direction gives Beale's method, and with metric H chosen by a quasi-Newton update, d0 becomes an eigenvector of HA (pp. 17, 21–22). Interpretation I yields the Variable Storage Generalized CG (VSGCG) method of Nazareth. When storage is limited, a simple H (e.g. diagonal) is used, the update vectors are saved, and the metric "can vary or stay the same from one iteration to another, depending on the storage available" (pp. 23–24). In Buckley's interleaved quasi-Newton–CG method, "as many corrections are retained as our storage capacity allows us" (p. 27). Interpretations II and III organize the inexact-line-search variants: Dixon's gradient prediction, Shanno's memoryless BFGS, the Nazareth–Nocedal multistep method and the three-term recurrence (TTR) (pp. 40–44). Algorithm 5.1 interleaves BFGS with TTR steps to avoid line searches in the CG steps (p. 49).
- **D3 Assumptions**: The termination results assume a strictly convex quadratic (SPD A) and, mostly, exact line searches (pp. 14, 18, 24, 30, 34, 49). For general functions, the report obtains only positive definiteness of the interleaved updates under "sufficiently accurate" line searches (Lemma 3.1, pp. 29–30, using Powell's rank result as Theorem 3.2, pp. 27–28).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Most proofs are inductions showing that a hybrid generates the same steps as CG with one fixed metric. VSGCG terminates for any H and any Broyden-class member (Theorem 3.1, pp. 24–26). The interleaved method with CG steps in the newest matrix is shown to be equivalent to CG with the initial metric H0 throughout (Theorem 3.4, pp. 34–35). The BFGS–TTR method reproduces the CG directions up to scaling (Theorem 5.1, pp. 49–52). There is one discriminating negative result: with any Broyden member other than BFGS "the quadratic termination property is lost" (Lemma 3.2, p. 37). The authors contrast this with VSGCG, where the result "is independent of what member of Broyden's class one chooses" (p. 26). The Beale bound (Theorem 2.1, p. 18) extends Luenberger's polynomial argument (pp. 13–14, 21). A TTR lemma (premature termination leaves the gradient conjugate to the earlier directions, p. 47) "justifies the hybrid implementation of Gill and Murray" (p. 48).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a. The report has no runs of its own. It records that others' experience with TTR conflicts: encouraging in [27], while "Shanno [26] however does not obtain good results with the method" (p. 44).
- **D6 Positioning**: a map of the field rather than a single method. Buckley's theorem is generalized "slightly" (p. 27), and Powell is credited with explaining why Polak–Ribière recovers from poor directions (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing**: a fixed per-section template, "basics", "generalizations" and "strategies" (p. 6). The authors grade their own results: an earlier theorem "is a weak result" before it is strengthened to n-step termination (p. 34).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The stated contributions are new properties and new algorithms (pp. 4–5). Deferred: convergence analysis (to [4]) and "a documented and distributable FORTRAN implementation" (p. 6). Open: it is "as yet unclear" how to use the multistep lemma in an algorithm, and a straightforward multistep method does not ensure descent (pp. 43, 45). TTR need not give descent directions even with exact line searches (p. 45). The TTR modifications are deferred "so as not to unduly lengthen an already long paper" (p. 48), and strategies for scaling and restarting are left to Powell and Shanno (pp. 22, 48).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the theory does separate methods. In the interleaved method BFGS is the only Broyden member that keeps quadratic termination (Lemma 3.2, pp. 37–38), while VSGCG works for any member (p. 26). But the discrimination is made on quadratics with exact line searches, not on "the version people run" (Method 5 step 1), and there are no runs to set it against. The SKILL's later "Broyden-class result that excludes DFP" has here an early termination-theoretic form.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: known weaknesses are listed and turned into lemmas that suggest repairs. They are TTR non-descent, TTR premature termination when d0 ≠ −g0, and the multistep method's lack of a descent guarantee (pp. 45–48). The repairs themselves are deferred (p. 48), and there are no solver options yet.
- Taste mark 2 ✅ (theory that explains codes): the TTR termination lemma is used to justify an existing code, Gill and Murray's hybrid (p. 48).
- New-pattern candidate: *equivalence on quadratics as a staging ground*. Start from an exact equivalence (CG ≡ BFGS on quadratics; "the BFGS method can be interpreted as a conjugate gradient method in which the metric is changed at each step", p. 23), relax one condition at a time (metric, storage, starting direction, line-search exactness), and ask which property survives (pp. 10–12, 15–17, 23–24, 40).
- New-pattern candidate: *storage as a design knob*. The VSGCG family runs between CG and BFGS according to "the storage available" (pp. 23–24, 27). This matches the candidate *storage knob spanning two classical methods* from S138, his 1978 Rice thesis on CG methods, and S143 (card s01). Its link to his 1980 limited-storage paper is inferred; that paper was not read here.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To show that a hybrid keeps a property such as finite termination, prove by induction that its directions coincide (up to scale) with those of a reference method with one fixed metric, then inherit the reference method's property (pp. 24–26, 34–36, 49–52).
- Test whether a result depends on the choice within a parametrized family (here Broyden's class). A family-wide result (p. 26) next to a single-member result (pp. 37–38) is a cheap way to make theory discriminate.
- When a negative property of a method is known (TTR's premature termination), prove what does still hold at the failure point, then use it to justify a practical safeguard someone already ships (pp. 47–48).

**Quotes**:
- "it can be seen that the BFGS method can be interpreted as a conjugate gradient method in which the metric is changed at each step." (p. 23)
- "we hope eventually to develop a documented and distributable FORTRAN implementation." (p. 6)

### S063 · KNITRO user's manual (Northwestern University Optimization Technology Center Technical Report OTC 2003/5, 2003; no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 44 pages were read: the title page and contents (pp. 1–2), §§1–3 (pp. 3–11) including the options table (p. 11), §4 (pp. 12–29), §§5–9 (pp. 30–43) and the references (pp. 43–44). In §4, the C driver and user-routine listings (pp. 18–26) were skimmed through their comments and the sample outputs (pp. 8–10, 26–27) were read. First page checked: "Knitro User's Manual Version 3.0", "Richard A. Waltz Jorge Nocedal", 1 April 2003, "Technical Report OTC 2003/5" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Richard A. Waltz · **Position in trajectory**: user documentation of KNITRO 3.0. The software is copyright Northwestern University (p. 1) and "licensed and supported by Ziena Optimization, Inc." (p. 4). The algorithm papers it rests on are [3] (algorithm), [2] (global convergence theory) and [7] (Knitro-Direct) (p. 4). Version note: the title page is dated 1 April 2003 (p. 1), yet [7] is listed as a June 2003 report (p. 43). This copy may therefore postdate its title-page date (not resolved here). S139 cites this manual as [24] (S139 p. 23).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: local solutions of smooth continuous problems, with or without constraints. It is designed for large-scale problems but presented as efficient across eight classes, from unconstrained problems to systems of equations, least squares, LP, QP and general inequality-constrained problems (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: An interior (barrier) method with trust regions and a merit function solves barrier subproblems while µ decreases (p. 4). There are two step computations. Knitro-CG, the default, splits the step into normal and tangential components and computes the tangential step by projected CG. It "factors a projection matrix" rather than solving the KKT system (p. 4). Knitro-Direct solves the primal–dual KKT system by direct linear algebra and falls back to the CG step when the direct step "cannot be guaranteed to be of good quality" or negative curvature is detected (pp. 4, 38). A reverse-communication design returns control to the driver at every evaluation, for monitoring and embedding (pp. 3, 43).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f, h and g smooth (p. 4); local solutions only (p. 3). Exact first derivatives are "highly recommended", since approximations "may seriously degrade the performance of the code" (pp. 32, 39).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no proofs; the theory is in [2], p. 4). The termination test is scaled by computable quantities: τ1 = max(1, initial constraint violation) and τ2 = max(1, ‖∇f(x_k)‖∞). Feasibility and KKT errors are tested against max(τ·tol, tol_abs), and the absolute tolerances default to 0 (pp. 33, 35).
- **D5 Experiments**: n/a. The only run is a three-variable example. With direct=1 and opttol=1e-8 it takes 8 iterations (pp. 8–10); with default options, from the C driver, it takes 7 iterations (pp. 26–27).
- **D6 Positioning**: n/a for rivals. CUTEr's nearly 1000 problems are called "extremely valuable for benchmarking optimization software" (p. 29). The Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt background is referred to Nocedal & Wright [8] (p. 42).
- **D7 Writing**: advice is conditional and numeric ("Typically if exact Hessians … cannot be provided … option 4 above is recommended", p. 31). The size threshold for the dense quasi-Newton options is given inconsistently: "n < 1000" in §5 (p. 31) and "n < 2000" in §9.2 (p. 40).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: software, with defaults for 16 options (pp. 11, 30–34). Failure modes are documented as exit codes. Code −2 is "Convergence to an infeasible point", which can also occur for feasible problems; the advice is to try several initial points (p. 37). Code −4 means the point "cannot be improved" (degeneracy, ill-conditioning, bad scaling), and code −5 means "appears to be optimal" within a factor of 100 of the tolerances (p. 37). Option incompatibilities are stated: Direct cannot be combined with hessopt=4 or 5 or with the feasible version, and initpt requires direct=1 (pp. 32–33, 38). The manual gives no future directions.

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: an undecided choice is exposed as a user option. "Knitro-CG is the default, but we encourage the user to try both options" (p. 4), and in §8 it is "difficult to predict in advance which one will be more effective" (p. 38). The infeasible-stationary-point failure mode, which the SKILL quotes from the 2006 Knitro paper as the start of the infeasibility-detection papers, is already documented here as exit code −2 (p. 37). Timing note for Method 4 chain (b): the manual shows Knitro-Direct shipping in version 3.0 (pp. 1, 4, 38). The date of the LOQO benchmark preprint was not checked here.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (early form): complementary step computations, iterative CG and direct KKT factorization, are combined in one package with an automatic fallback (pp. 4, 38). Structure is exploited through the existing interface. Least-squares users pass JᵀJ as the Hessian, so that Knitro "will behave like a Gauss-Newton method" with direct=1 and like Levenberg–Marquardt with direct=0 (p. 42). Square systems are treated as feasibility problems (p. 42). Interior and active-set algorithms are not yet integrated: the only algorithm options are Knitro-CG and Knitro-Direct (pp. 37–38).
- Heuristic 5 ✅: parameter advice is given as numbers: the default table (p. 11), the 10⁻⁴ interior margin for the feasible mode (pp. 32, 41), and size thresholds for the dense quasi-Newton options (pp. 31, 40). The thresholds do not agree with each other (n < 1000 against n < 2000).
- Heuristic 2 ✅: each iteration line prints Acc/Rej, Feas err, Opt err, ‖Step‖, CG its and µ (p. 36). The CG count also serves as a diagnostic: Direct may help "as evidenced by Knitro-CG taking a large number of Conjugate Gradient iterations" (p. 38).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: exit flags exist for "cannot be improved" and for near-optimality within a factor of 100 (p. 37), but none is driven by an explicit estimate (such as a noise level), and there is no recovery path.
- New-pattern candidate: *symptom-to-option map in user documentation*. The manual ties a log symptom to the option to change (many CG iterations → direct=1, p. 38) and an exit pattern to a diagnosis (−2 from several starting points → "likely the problem is infeasible", p. 37).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the stopping test's scale factors only from quantities the code always has (the initial infeasibility and the current gradient norm, each floored at 1), and keep separate absolute tolerances that default to zero (p. 35).
- Pair each exit code with a cause and an action, and include a near-success code (here, within a factor of 100 of the tolerances) so that users do not discard usable answers (pp. 36–37).
- Write the derivative-option advice as a decision rule on what the user has and what dominates the cost: exact gradients → finite-difference Hessian-vector products; finite-difference or costly gradients → quasi-Newton; dense quasi-Newton only below a stated n (pp. 31, 39–41).
- Expose problem structure through an existing callback rather than a new solver. Passing JᵀJ as the Hessian turns the general code into Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt, depending on the step option (p. 42).

**Quotes**:
- "We encourage the user to try both options as it is difficult to predict in advance which one will be more effective on a given problem." (p. 38)
- "This happens when the problem is infeasible, but may also occur on occasion for feasible problems with nonlinear constraints." (p. 37)

### S139 · An implementation of an algorithm for nonlinear programming based on piecewise linear models (Technical Report, Optimization Center, Northwestern University, 2011; no DOI; Optimization Online 2011/04/3003)
**Read level**: full. All 23 pages were read: the abstract and §1 (pp. 1–2), §2 with Algorithms 2.1 and 2.2 (pp. 2–9), §3 (pp. 10–13), §4 (p. 14), §5 with Tables 1–3 (pp. 14–18) and the final remarks and references (pp. 18–23). The plotted data of Figures 1–3 (pp. 18–19) and Figures 4–6 (pp. 20–21) are not in the text file; only their axes, legends or captions survive. First page checked: title, "Richard Byrd Jorge Nocedal Richard Waltz Yuchen Wu", April 21, 2011 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Richard Byrd, Richard Waltz, Yuchen Wu · **Position in trajectory**: the implementation companion of the theory paper [6] = S114, which is cited here as a 2009 technical report (pp. 1, 22). It reuses the penalty steering rules of [2, 4] (pp. 4–5) and is built "within the knitro software package" (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The goal is an algorithm for large-scale problems, "including those with a large reduced space" (abstract, p. 1). The paper surveys three families (p. 2). Interior methods are effective for large problems but "not always robust" and can be inefficient for a series of related problems. Active-set SQP is the most robust for small and medium problems. SLP-QP methods scale better but are "not yet as robust as SQP". The aim is to "combine some of the properties of SQP and SLP-QP methods" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Curvature information enters the LP-based active-set prediction as a separable convex piecewise-linear model Γ_k, so the prediction is still an LP (pp. 2–3). An EQP on the working set, solved by projected CG, follows (pp. 3, 6). The implementation makes two changes to the subproblem of [6]. It adds an ℓ1-penalty reformulation, so the subproblem is always feasible, and it adds a trust region "for greater control and robustness" (pp. 3–4). π is set by the steering rules, adapted from Algorithm 9.1 of [2] (Algorithm 2.1, pp. 4–5). The working set must be linearly independent, and LP codes "produce an independent working set as a byproduct" (p. 5). The other components are a Cauchy point along the PLA step in a quadratic model of the merit function (pp. 5–6); an EQP relaxed through a normal step truncated to 0.8Δ_k (p. 6); a trial point on the Cauchy–EQP segment with backtracking (p. 7); and second-order correction against the Maratos effect (pp. 7–8). The model is built from 2r + 1 interpolation points per coordinate, in geometric ratio 0.3 (p. 10). Initial ranges are min(2Δ_k/n, distance to bound), enlarged toward the one-dimensional minimizer 2υ (p. 11). The outer slopes are overridden with ±‖∇f‖ to keep the LP bounded (p. 12). The LP is assembled in "Delta-form" (p. 13). B_k is diagonal: mid(10⁻⁵, H_jj, 10⁵), with ‖H‖_F/n² for structurally zero diagonal entries (p. 14), and r = 3 (p. 14).
- **D3 Assumptions**: f, h and g twice differentiable, with bounds on the variables (p. 1). The convergence theory is that of [6]: the Cauchy point is kept because it is "crucial" for global convergence (p. 6).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (no proofs; the paper conforms to "the theoretical guidelines given in [6]", abstract, p. 1).
- **D5 Experiments**: 514 CUTEr/AMPL problems split by type (QP 141, BC 68, GC 305) and size (Table 1, p. 15). Only active-set codes are compared: knitro/active and SNOPT 7.2-1 (p. 14). PLA uses the knitro/active stopping test, SNOPT its default test, and all codes 10⁻⁶. Limits are 10000 iterations (SNOPT 50000 major) and 8 CPU hours, and reaching a limit counts as failure (p. 15). Robustness is tied at 480/514 (Tables 2–3, p. 16). Function-evaluation profiles favour PLA in all three categories (p. 17). Against SNOPT, CPU time is compared only for n ≥ 500, and indefinite QPs are omitted because SNOPT's AMPL interface fails on them (p. 18). There is no timing against knitro/active (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: SNOPT and knitro/active are chosen as "generally regarded as some of the most effective active-set codes". The derivative disparity is named: SNOPT uses first derivatives and a limited-memory BFGS reduced Hessian, while PLA and knitro/active use exact second derivatives (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing**: framed as a "progress report" (abstract; p. 18), with upbeat verdicts: "highly encouraging" (p. 17), "already very robust" (p. 18).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: the contribution is software, a PLA implementation inside knitro (p. 3). The paper states its limits. The code is not optimized for speed, so there is no timing against knitro/active (pp. 18, 22). The diagonal Hessian approximation "is not very sophisticated" (p. 22). Recognizing infeasible or unbounded problems is "outside the scope of this paper" (p. 15). Open: an efficient implementation of the diagonalized L-BFGS model of [6] (p. 22) and other choices of B_k (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: parts of the protocol are present. The new code and the incumbent share one stopping test, every code uses 10⁻⁶, and a limit reached counts as a failure (p. 15). Problems with no solution count as failures for both codes, and different local minima count as successes for both (p. 15). CPU time is reported only where it is meaningful, n ≥ 500 (p. 18). But the scales are not tipped against the authors' own side. PLA has exact second derivatives against SNOPT's quasi-Newton; the disparity is named (p. 14) but not turned into a question, as it was in 2003 (Method 1 step 5). SNOPT keeps its default stopping test (p. 15), the class SNOPT mishandles is removed (p. 18), and the abstract reports no losses.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (reuse): the penalty-steering repair of the Method 3 worked case is ported unchanged, as a module, into a new active-set algorithm (Algorithm 2.1, adapted from [2], pp. 4–5).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the stated aim is to combine SQP and SLP-QP properties (p. 2). The new algorithm is built inside knitro next to knitro/active and compared like with like, against active-set solvers only (pp. 3, 14).
- Heuristic 8 ✅: the prototype is built inside the production solver and compared with that solver's own default active-set algorithm (pp. 3, 14–17).
- Heuristic 5 ✅: numbers are given as defaults: r = 3, ratio 0.3, 0.8Δ_k, 2Δ_k/n, and the curvature clip [10⁻⁵, 10⁵] (pp. 6, 10–11, 14).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: here theory steers the implementation, which conforms to the guidelines of [6] (abstract). But the implementation reinstates a safeguard that the theory paper said could be dropped. S114 says the PLA model lets one "dispense with this constraint altogether" (card S114, S114 p. 2), while S139 adds a trust region "for greater control and robustness" (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *theory paper, then an implementation report that reinstates safeguards*. The trust region comes back (p. 3), a penalty makes the subproblem always feasible (pp. 3–4), and the EQP is relaxed (p. 6). Each departure from the theory paper is stated.
- New-pattern candidate: *cheap predictor carries the proof, second-order phase carries the rate*. The LP phase and the Cauchy point provide global convergence (pp. 3, 6), and the EQP provides the step quality. This matches the existing candidate from S060, recurring in S114 (card s06).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a solver comparison, decide in advance how to score instances with no solution (failure for every code) and convergence to different local minima (success for both), and say that this affects the codes equally (p. 15).
- Report CPU time only for problems large enough (here n ≥ 500) for timing to be meaningful, and count function evaluations everywhere (p. 18).
- Take a linearly independent working set from the LP solver's basis instead of extracting one yourself (p. 5).
- Keep a piecewise-linear model LP bounded by overriding its outermost slopes with ±‖∇f(x_k)‖ on the coordinates where the interpolation range could not be closed (p. 12).
- Clip diagonal curvature estimates into a fixed range ([10⁻⁵, 10⁵]) and use a Frobenius-norm fallback for structurally zero diagonal entries (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "Given that PLA is an active-set method, it is appropriate to compare it only with active-set solvers." (p. 14)
- "In addition, we include a trust region constraint in (2.2) for greater control and robustness." (p. 3)
