### S138 · ON THE METHOD OF CONJUGATE GRADIENTS FOR FUNCTION MINIMIZATION. (Rice University PhD thesis 1978, no DOI)
**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract (p. 4), acknowledgements (p. 5), contents (p. 6), Ch. I (pp. 7–9), opening of Ch. II (pp. 10–11), Ch. III opening (pp. 13–14), Ch. IV opening and §D (pp. 19–20, 29–31), Ch. V opening, Algorithm 2 / Theorem 5.17 statement and start of proof (pp. 32–33, 39–41), §V.B VSCG with Theorem 5.36 and proof (pp. 48–51), Ch. VI opening (pp. 52–53), Ch. VII opening and Theorem 7.6 statement (pp. 67–70), Ch. VIII (pp. 73–76), Ch. IX numerical examples (pp. 77–79), references (pp. 80–82). Proofs of Chs. VI–VII not followed. The microfilm OCR garbles most displayed formulas; prose is legible.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: none (sole author; thesis director Richard Tapia, help from Larry Nazareth, fellow student Richard Byrd thanked, p. 5) · **Position in trajectory**: the 1978 starting point. The O(k·n)-storage family between CG and quasi-Newton (p. 7) is the seed of the 1980 limited-memory update; the program used is Nazareth's (p. 78).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two families are compared on their trade-off: quasi-Newton converges faster and tolerates inexact line searches, CG needs no matrix storage and works on large problems with dense or unknown Hessian structure (p. 7). The stated aim is to speed up CG by combining it with quasi-Newton updates using O(k·n) storage, 1 < k < n; within this class CG is one extreme and quasi-Newton the other (p. 7). A second entry is recent CG methods without line searches (Dixon, Nazareth, Shanno), to be shown to keep quadratic termination when combined with QN updates (p. 7). Ch. VIII is entered from an application: for multiplier updates "Doing line searches is out of question", since evaluating the dual function needs a full minimization (p. 75).
- **D2 Core idea**: Two combined methods (p. 32): the Interleaved QN–CG method (QN steps that update a matrix kept as individual rank-two corrections, then CG steps with that fixed metric) and Nazareth's Variable Storage CG method (VSCG; only CG steps, in a metric built by QN updates, pp. 48–49). CG "with metric H" is obtained by a change of variables z = L⁻¹x with LLᵀ = H (pp. 10–11). Restarting frequency and how long each cycle runs are left as implementation choices (pp. 32, 67).
- **D3 Assumptions**: quadratic termination results assume exact line searches on a strictly convex quadratic (Theorems 5.17, 5.36, pp. 39, 49). Local results use Assumption 7.5: f ∈ C², a uniformly positive definite Hessian at x*, Lipschitz Hessian (p. 69), and "asymptotically perfect line searches" (Theorem 7.6, p. 69). The thesis notes that with less than O(n²) storage the bounded deterioration theorem cannot be used, since H_k need not approach the Hessian (p. 52).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: quadratic termination by induction showing that each combined step equals a CG step with one fixed metric (Theorem 5.17, pp. 39–41; Theorem 5.36, pp. 49–51). The interleaved result needs BFGS, while the VSCG result holds for any member of Broyden's class: "This is in sharp contrast with the Interleaved Method where BFGS has to be used" (p. 51). Local Q-linear convergence follows Stoer's framework via a bound on the condition number of H_k (pp. 52–53); n-step quadratic convergence is outlined "along the same lines as Stoer's [27] and Cohen's [12]" (p. 69).
- **D5 Experiments**: Wood, Powell singular (n = 4), Biggs exponential (n = 6) and trigonometric (n = 20) on an IBM 370/168, function evaluations (NFCALL) and final gradient norm against STORE = 0–4. The update is SR1 "for its simplicity", although the theory covers only Broyden's restricted class, which excludes SR1 (pp. 77–79).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory plus a small test. The two methods are similar on small problems; on larger ones the experiments only "seem to indicate" that VSCG is more efficient (p. 78). Limits written down: local, not global, results (p. 52), and the SR1–theory mismatch (p. 77). The multiplier formulas of Ch. VIII are untested and "should also be guarded against too long displacements" (p. 76).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the quadratic-termination theory separates update choices (the interleaved scheme needs BFGS, VSCG works for any Broyden member), pp. 39, 49–51.
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction (acknowledged): the runs use SR1 while the theorems cover Broyden's restricted class, so the analysed and the tested version differ (p. 77).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: CG is motivated as the method for large problems whose Hessian is dense or whose structure is unknown (p. 7), the role L-BFGS later takes in Method 6 step 3.
- New-pattern candidate: *storage knob spanning two classical methods*: an O(k·n) family with CG and quasi-Newton at its ends (p. 7) and STORE as the experimental variable (p. 77); compare m in S143.
- New-pattern candidate: *port a canonical convergence framework*: Chs. VI–VII are built on Stoer's analysis (pp. 52, 67–69); see also S039, S054.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define a one-parameter family by memory (k vectors, 1 < k < n) whose ends are two known methods, and make that parameter the axis of the experiment (pp. 7, 77–79).
- Prove a property of a hybrid by showing that, on quadratics, every step coincides with a step of a classical method with one fixed metric, so the classical property transfers by induction (pp. 39–41, 49–51).
- When a line search is impossible in an application (each function value needs an inner minimization), reuse methods without line searches and say what safeguards a practical version would need (pp. 75–76).

**Quotes**:
- "On the other hand, conjugate gradient methods do not require storage of a matrix and have proved themselves useful in large problems where the matrix of second derivatives is not sparse or where the structure of this matrix is unknown." (p. 7)
- "Unless STORE is also large we think that the best approach is to build the metric as soon as possible, since doing many iterations but few updates will not scale the steps well enough." (p. 77)

### S026 · The formulation and analysis of numerical methods for inverse eigenvalue problems (SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis 1987, 10.1137/0724043)
**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract and §1 with applications start (pp. 1–3), §2 Methods I–IV and operation counts (pp. 5–11), §3.1 and §3.2 up to the modified Method I (pp. 13–18), §3.3 opening, Assumption 3.1 and Lemma 3.1 statement (pp. 21–22), theorem statements 3.2–3.7 (pp. 23–30), §4 numerical results and Tables 1–3 (pp. 30–33), acknowledgements and references (pp. 33–34). Proofs of §3.3 and the remaining applications of §1.1 not read.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: S. Friedland (Hebrew University / U. Illinois Chicago), M. L. Overton (Courant) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the numerical linear algebra strand of the 1980s, continuing Nocedal and Overton (1983), which first established quadratic convergence of Method I with multiple eigenvalues (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Problem 1: find c with eigenvalues of A(c) = A₀ + Σ c_k A_k equal to given λ* (p. 1). The entry is a gap: eigenvalues are not differentiable where they coalesce, yet "the behavior of the numerical methods in these circumstances has received little attention" (p. 2). Scope is set up front: local analysis only, assuming a solution exists (p. 1). Applications are listed first (inverse Sturm–Liouville, §1.1, pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Four Newton-related methods share one step form J c^{ν+1} = λ* − b (pp. 6–8): Method I (Newton on λ(c) − λ*, exact eigenvectors), Method II (eigenvectors updated by one inverse-iteration step), Method III (new: Cayley-transform update of the eigenvector matrix, "This method is apparently new", p. 8), and Method IV (Newton on determinant equations). With a multiple eigenvalue of multiplicity t the problem is overdetermined by s = t(t − 1)/2, so the paper reformulates it as Problem 2 (specify only n − s eigenvalues) and modifies the methods: the s rotation parameters Y_ij of the multiple block are set to zero, and a tolerance Neglig guards against coalescing unspecified eigenvalues (pp. 16–17).
- **D3 Assumptions**: existence of a solution c* and bounded inverse Jacobians along the iterates (Assumption 3.1, p. 21); for the modified methods, nonsingularity of K(c*) (p. 21). The authors say a condition at c* "would be more desirable", and show that the natural one fails because J*(Q) can be made singular by a choice of eigenvectors (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: eigenvector-derivative formula for the Jacobian (p. 6); linearizing QᵀA(c)Q with a matrix exponential, then a Cayley transform (pp. 7–8); a QR factorization of the eigenprojection to build an eigenvector basis close to the current one (Lemma 3.1, pp. 21–22); quadratic convergence of c (Theorems 3.2–3.3, pp. 23–24) and of Method III's error matrices (Theorem 3.7, p. 30). Method IV is dismissed by a one-line argument: if λ(c) is linear, Method I solves it in one step while Method IV iterates on degree-n polynomials (p. 10).
- **D5 Experiments**: three examples (additive n = 8 distinct; n = 8 with multiplicity 3; additive n = 6 with multiplicity 3), started near the solution, residual per iteration to 10⁻⁸, VAX 11/780 with EISPACK; Methods I–III have similar local behaviour and all converge quadratically (pp. 30–33).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: formulation plus local theory, one new method. Limits are explicit: global behaviour "we shall not explicitly address" (p. 1); variants such as inequality constraints on eigenvalues are only mentioned (p. 2); no line search or trust region in the tests (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the Newton engine is kept and only the formulation (Problem 2) and one component (Y_ij = 0 for the multiple block, a Neglig tolerance) are changed to keep quadratic convergence (pp. 16–17). The repair has no estimate or recovery path.
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: theory and a minimal case discriminate between two locally quadratic methods (Method I vs IV, p. 10), backed by experience that Method IV needs more iterations and fails more often (p. 30).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the expectation that non-differentiable eigenvalues make Newton "at best slowly convergent" is tested and found false, "both in theory and in practice" (p. 13). The test is analytical, not a comparison against a dismissed simple tool.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before modifying a method for a degenerate case, check whether the problem itself is overdetermined there, and count the degrees of freedom: here s = t(t − 1)/2 parameters only rotate the eigenvector basis (p. 16).
- To rank two methods with the same asymptotic rate, find the simplest instance where one finishes in one step and the other must iterate (p. 10).
- Write all candidate methods in one common step form, so that they differ only in how the data (J, b) are produced and their costs can be compared per iteration (pp. 6–8, 11).

**Quotes**:
- "Interestingly enough, however, even when Method I is started at such a point, it has no difficulty with convergence." (p. 14)
- "A line search (or a trust region strategy) would be essential to make the algorithms convergent in practical applications." (p. 30)

### S039 · An analysis of reduced Hessian methods for constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming 1990, 10.1007/bf01588794; read as TR CU-CS-398-88, 1988)
**Read level**: partial, TR version. Read: abstract and §1 (TR pp. 4–8), §2 Algorithm 2.1 and the choice of s_k, y_k (TR pp. 9–11), §3 opening, Theorem 3.1, Definition 3.1, Assumptions 3.1, Lemmas 3.2–3.3 and Theorem 3.4 with proofs (TR pp. 14–18), §3.2 opening (TR p. 19), §4 Assumptions 4.1 (TR p. 22), remarks after Theorem 4.3 and Theorem 4.4 (TR pp. 26–27), §5 opening, §5.1–5.2 and Theorem 5.1 (TR pp. 27–30), end of the §5 proofs and §6 Summary (TR pp. 42–44). Not read: §2 merit-function details (TR pp. 11–14), §3.2 lemmas (TR pp. 19–21), §4 proofs (TR pp. 23–26), §5.3–5.5 (TR pp. 30–42).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: R. H. Byrd (Colorado) (TR p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: the late-1980s convergence theory of quasi-Newton methods with Byrd (it reuses Byrd and Nocedal 1987), applied to SQP before the trust-region SQP and KNITRO work.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a critique of the assumptions in earlier analyses: all of them "either assume a good initial approximation to the solution and to the Hessian of the Lagrangian at the solution, or they assume that the iterates converge and that the Hessian approximations are bounded" (TR p. 7), assumptions whose validity cannot be checked in practice (quote below). Reduced-Hessian SQP is chosen "primarily because the issues we are interested in are simpler to deal with than for full Hessian approaches" (TR p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea**: Algorithm 2.1: reduced-Hessian SQP for min f s.t. c(x) = 0, BFGS on the reduced Hessian with the Coleman–Conn y_k, a backtracking line search on the ℓ₁ or Fletcher merit function, any positive definite B₁ (TR pp. 9–10). Unchanged: the Coleman–Conn update; the more complex Nocedal–Overton update is left aside (TR p. 8). For superlinear convergence the ℓ₁ version is modified by a second-order correction or a watchdog to avoid the Maratos effect (TR pp. 30, 43).
- **D3 Assumptions**: Assumptions 3.1: iterates in a convex set with bounded f, c and derivatives, A(x) of full column rank, and y_kᵀs_k/s_kᵀs_k ≥ m, ‖y_k‖²/y_kᵀs_k ≤ M (TR p. 16). Local: second-order sufficiency at x* (Assumptions 4.1, TR p. 22). For Fletcher's merit function "additional optimistic assumptions" are needed (TR pp. 19, 27). The authors rate their own assumptions: bounded multipliers is "stronger than we would like" (TR p. 43).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: the Byrd–Nocedal (1987) theorem that BFGS bounds on cos θ_j and ‖B_j s_j‖/‖s_j‖ hold for at least ⌈pk⌉ of the first k iterates (Theorem 3.1, TR p. 15) defines a set of "good" iterates (Definition 3.1, TR p. 15); summing the merit decrease over good iterates gives lim inf(‖Zᵀg‖ + ‖c‖) = 0 (Theorem 3.4, TR p. 18). R-linear convergence then gives bounded B_k and the consistency condition for two-step superlinear convergence, given a smoothly varying null-space basis (Theorem 5.1, TR pp. 28–30).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (n/a).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory only. The authors grade their own results: the global result is "somewhat weak" (TR p. 8); a stronger local result is skipped because "the analysis of this result is much more complex" (TR p. 26); the Fletcher-function theory is "significantly weaker" (TR p. 43). Future work: the Nocedal–Overton update and full-Hessian SQP "in a similar fashion" (TR p. 44).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the paper analyses the version that is run (backtracking line search, BFGS from an arbitrary positive definite matrix, no assumption that the iterates converge or the matrices stay bounded), and calls its local and superlinear sections a "realistic and informative analysis" of the method as implemented in practice (TR pp. 4, 7–8, 44).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the reduced-Hessian SQP engine is kept and only step acceptance is repaired (SOC or watchdog) when the ℓ₁ merit function rejects unit steps, at the price of extra function evaluations (TR pp. 30, 43). The repair is driven by no estimate.
- New-pattern candidate: *port a canonical convergence framework*: "an extension of the well known results of Powell (1976) for unconstrained optimization" (TR p. 4), carried by one reusable lemma from Byrd and Nocedal 1987 (TR pp. 8, 15, 30); see S054 and S138.
- New-pattern candidate: *label unprovable assumptions as optimistic*: the paper names its assumptions "optimistic", says why they are needed, and notes that they hold a posteriori once R-linear convergence is known (TR pp. 19, 27, 43).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When an update keeps good properties only on average, prove the key bounds on a positive fraction of iterates and sum the merit decrease over those "good" iterates only (TR pp. 15, 18).
- Open the paper by listing the assumptions prior analyses needed that a user cannot verify, and make dropping them the contribution (TR p. 7).
- Pick the simpler variant (reduced rather than full Hessian) to develop the proof technique, and name the harder variant as the next target (TR pp. 8, 44).
- Grade each result's strength in the summary (strong / "somewhat weak" / needs optimistic assumptions) instead of listing theorems uniformly (TR pp. 43–44).

**Quotes**:
- "We regard these assumptions as undesirable since it is not known when they will be satisfied in practice." (TR p. 7)
- "we will be forced to make some additional optimistic assumptions in order to establish convergence." (TR p. 19)

### S197 · Algorithms and software for large scale optimization. Progress report, August 1, 1989--May 5, 1990 (Northwestern Univ., DOE progress report 1990, OSTI DOE/ER/25047-3, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–5; pp. 6–7 blank). OCR is noisy but legible.
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: none (Nocedal as PI). Cited joint reports with S. Nash, R. Byrd and D. C. Liu, J. C. Gilbert; graduate student Marucha Lalee named (pp. 4–5) · **Voice**: Nocedal's own, as PI of a DOE grant continuation request (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: large problems ("thousands of variables") and applications: L-BFGS "now being used, for example, to solve problems in meteorology and oceanography" (p. 2); transonic flow around a wing as a nonlinear least-squares problem with 461 degrees of freedom (p. 2). Two open questions frame the theory: whether BFGS is the best variable metric method (p. 3), and why theory and practice of nonlinear CG disagree, with Polak–Ribière best in practice but able to fail on nonconvex problems while Fletcher–Reeves, which performs poorly, has the only satisfactory global theory (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: no single algorithm; the report lists results and plans: parallel L-BFGS on the Alliant FX/8 (up to 50,000 variables, 13 Mflops on 8 processors, with memory-management problems noted, p. 2); an L-BFGS versus discrete-Newton study concluding that the extra gradient evaluations of discrete Newton "does not pay off" on common problems (p. 2); a Broyden-class analysis showing that some natural rules for negative φ lose superlinear convergence (p. 3); a planned column-scaling BFGS (p. 3); a line-search condition that guarantees descent for CG methods and splits them into two classes (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: n/a (report).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (results only summarized).
- **D5 Experiments**: summarized without tables: speed against the Buckley–LeNir code and Shanno–Phua's CONMIN on the transonic-flow problem (p. 2); first column-scaling tests "remarkable", typically cutting work by 50% (pp. 3–4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: plans for the coming year (pp. 3–5): a column-scaling BFGS code, its extension to limited memory (admitted to be not simple, p. 4), meteorology (4-D assimilation) and oceanography with bounds on the variables, to be addressed the next year (p. 4), and a better nonlinear CG. Grant-report register: "We believe that this will be the most efficient BFGS code in existence" (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the report names three best current approaches for large problems, Newton with sparse matrix techniques, Griewank–Toint partitioned quasi-Newton and L-BFGS (pp. 2–3); for 4-D assimilation it expects that new techniques may be needed to exploit the problem's structure (p. 4).
- Method 6 ✗ contradiction: the transonic-flow problem is posed as nonlinear least squares, yet it is solved with L-BFGS and compared only with general-purpose CG/quasi-Newton codes, not with a least-squares method (p. 2).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: both open questions are theory-versus-practice gaps between named methods (the BFGS/DFP and negative-φ analysis, p. 3; PR versus FR, p. 4), and the plan is theory that explains which CG methods work (pp. 4–5).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: application needs and open questions are written into the next year's plan: bounds for oceanography (p. 4), later delivered as L-BFGS-B (S143, p. 2; the link is inferred from timing); CG line-search conditions (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a grant report, pair each result with the open question it raises and the next year's deliverable (bounds, a code, a theory), so the report doubles as a research agenda (pp. 3–5).
- Frame a theory project around a named practice–theory inversion (the method that works has no theory; the one with theory works badly), which makes the target falsifiable (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "However we believe that conjugate gradient methods have not been properly implemented so far, and that their full potential needs to be assessed." (p. 4)
- "Our work shows that much care is required when implementing such strategies." (p. 3)

### S054 · Analysis of a self-scaling quasi-Newton method (Mathematical Programming 1993, 10.1007/bf01582136)
**Read level**: partial. Read: abstract and §1 (pp. 1–2), §2 with Theorem 2.1 and proof (pp. 2–5), Assumptions 3.1 and Theorem 3.1 with start of proof (pp. 5–7), end of §3 and §4 opening (pp. 10–11), end of the example and §5 with Tables 1–3 (pp. 15–17), §6 final remarks (p. 18). Not read: middle of the §3 proof (pp. 8–9) and the construction of the example (pp. 12–14).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Ya-xiang Yuan (Academia Sinica, visiting Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: early-1990s analysis of the Broyden and scaled quasi-Newton family, next to the Byrd–Liu–Nocedal Broyden-class study cited on p. 10.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the self-scaling BFGS method of Oren and Luenberger (1974), which was derived and motivated on quadratics (p. 2), is analysed on general convex functions, against "disappointing numerical results" reported by others (Shanno and Phua, p. 18). Only one scaling rule (1.8) is studied, because it simplifies the analysis and because the authors believe other choices of φ_k have no stronger convergence properties (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: the update is BFGS with the scaling factor y_kᵀs_k / s_kᵀB_ks_k on B_k (pp. 1–2). The analysis works with an auxiliary sequence B̃_k that differs by a constant factor and has a constant determinant (eqs. 2.1–2.7, pp. 2–3). Main finding: the directions approach Newton's, but the steps are not well scaled, so unit steps fail (condition (4.2) does not hold) and the best known fix costs two function evaluations per iteration (pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Assumptions**: global result: f convex, bounded below, C², Hessian bounded on the level set, Wolfe line search with 0 < σ₁ < ½ (Assumptions 2.1, p. 4; eqs. 1.4–1.5, p. 2). Linear rate: uniformly convex level set and Lipschitz Hessian at x* (Assumptions 3.1, pp. 5–6). The example takes G* = I without loss of generality (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Powell's (1976) trace–determinant argument with the infinite-product lemma and Zoutendijk's condition, by contradiction (Theorem 2.1, pp. 4–5); R-linear convergence through boundedness of [tr B̃_k]^{2n}(f_k − f*) (Theorem 3.1, pp. 7–10); cos θ_k → 1 (eq. 4.1, p. 10); a 2-D quadratic on which the steps needed for superlinear convergence alternate between 2 and 0.5 (pp. 11–15).
- **D5 Experiments**: the 2-D quadratic from (10¹⁵, 10²⁰) with exact and Wolfe (0.01, 0.9) line searches, plus unscaled BFGS for comparison (Tables 1–3, pp. 16–17); on the Moré–Garbow–Hillstrom set with well and badly scaled starting matrices, standard BFGS needs fewer function evaluations most of the time (p. 16, no table).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory plus one example and an untabulated test. The main lesson is drawn even though "our analysis is asymptotic" (p. 18). Future direction: scaling strategies without this inefficiency; column scaling (Powell 1987; Siegel; Lalee and Nocedal) is pointed to as the better route (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: the theory explains a practical observation, since the authors judge the disappointing self-scaling results reported by others consistent with their analysis (p. 18), and it separates BFGS (which satisfies (4.2) and takes unit steps) from the self-scaling variant (which does not) (pp. 10–11). The constructed example and Table 2 confirm the predicted step pattern (pp. 15–16).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: a refinement motivated on quadratics is tested against the plain method it was meant to improve, and the plain BFGS wins most of the time, with almost twice the function evaluations for self-scaling on several problems (p. 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To show that a rate needs an extra cost, build the smallest problem (a 2-D quadratic) on which the required step lengths can be computed in closed form, then print the iterate table with exact and practical line searches (pp. 11–17).
- Simplify a scaled update's analysis with an auxiliary matrix sequence that differs by a scalar, chosen so that one invariant (here the determinant) stays constant (pp. 2–3).
- When diagnosing why a superlinear method is slow, separate two conditions: the direction is asymptotically right (cos θ_k → 1), and the step length is asymptotically right (s_kᵀB_ks_k / s_kᵀy_k → 1) (pp. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "We find that the method has a major drawback: to achieve superlinear convergence it may be necessary to evaluate the function twice per iteration, even very near the solution." (p. 1)
- "the standard BFGS method is superior to the self-scaling method most of the time." (p. 16)

### S115 · Automatic differentiation and the step computation in the limited memory BFGS method (Applied Mathematics Letters 1993, 10.1016/0893-9659(93)90032-i; read as HAL author version hal-04140351)
**Read level**: full, HAL version (pp. 1–6: cover, abstract, §§1–3, references).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Jean Charles Gilbert (INRIA Rocquencourt) (HAL p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: a short note in the 1992–93 limited-memory cluster (compact representations, L-BFGS-B), interpreting his 1980 two-loop recursion [6].

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: two subjects judged "apparently unrelated": the step computation of a limited-memory method, for which several equivalent formulas exist, "some of which are more economical than others" (HAL p. 2), and reverse-mode automatic differentiation, which gives a gradient at a small multiple of the function's cost (HAL pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: write H_m g as the gradient of f_m(g) = ½ gᵀH_m g; use the recursion f_m(g) = f_{m−1}(V_{m−1}g) + (ρ_{m−1}/2)(s_{m−1}ᵀg)² to obtain a one-loop program for f_m (3.4); its adjoint code (3.5), stripped of instructions not needed for H_m g, is exactly the two-loop recursion (3.6) (HAL pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Assumptions**: H₀ symmetric positive definite and ρ_i = (y_iᵀs_i)⁻¹ > 0 (HAL p. 3); the reverse-mode cost bound T(f, ∇f) ≤ C·T(f) with C ≤ 5 for Fortran operations, cited from Griewank (HAL p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: derivation by induction on the recursion, then mechanical adjoint construction and dead-code elimination (HAL pp. 4–5).
- **D5 Experiments**: none (n/a).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a conceptual explanation in four manuscript pages. No limitation or future direction is stated. The paper claims only that the derivation "shows why algorithm (3.6) is efficient" (HAL p. 5), whereas his DOE reports call the recursion "optimal in a sequential machine" (S197 p. 2) and the limited-memory operations "optimal in terms of arithmetic operations" on the strength of this note (S193 p. 3). The grant reports claim more than the paper.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the theory explains why a coded computation is the economical one among equivalent formulas (HAL pp. 2, 5). It concerns arithmetic cost, not convergence, and discriminates between implementations rather than methods.
- New-pattern candidate: *cross-field reinterpretation explains efficiency*: the paper reads an existing optimization kernel as an instance of another field's tool (reverse-mode AD) and inherits that tool's efficiency argument (HAL pp. 2, 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To explain why an implicit matrix–vector product is cheap, write the product as the gradient of a scalar quadratic form, write a short program for that scalar, and derive the product as its adjoint code (HAL pp. 4–5).

**Quotes**:

### S143 · LBFGS-B: Fortran Subroutines tor Large-Scale Bound Constrained Optimization (Northwestern EECS report 1994 (listed as Report NAM-11), no DOI)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–16; reference list pp. 16–17 skimmed). The extraction replaces the ff/fi/ffi ligatures with a control character (byte 0x0E, as in e·cient for efficient), so quotes are taken only from ligature-free sentences. The text carries no report number; it is dated December 31, 1994 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Ciyou Zhu, Richard H. Byrd, Peihuang Lu (p. 1; Lu is missing from the work-list author string) · **Position in trajectory**: the user documentation and benchmark of the L-BFGS-B code; the algorithm itself is in reference [8], Byrd, Lu, Nocedal and Zhu (p. 2). Delivers the bounds capability planned in S197 (p. 4) and reported in S193 (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: minimize f subject to simple bounds l ≤ x ≤ u when the Hessian is hard to compute or dense (pp. 1–2). The gap: no other limited-memory quasi-Newton code handles bounds, and nonlinear CG "has not been adequately extended to handle bounds" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: at each iteration the L-BFGS matrix defines a quadratic model; gradient projection identifies the active set, the model is minimized approximately over the free variables, and a Moré–Thuente line search follows; the L-BFGS matrices are held in compact form (p. 2). Storage is (12 + 2m)n; cost per iteration ranges from 4mn + n to about m²n multiplications (p. 2). The code unifies the primal and dual subspace minimizations through one matrix, so their run times are now similar (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions**: the user supplies f and g; no Hessian or structure information is needed (p. 2). Reverse communication (p. 3).
- **D4 Analysis**: n/a (software report; the unified primal/dual matrix identity is stated without proof, p. 8).
- **D5 Experiments**: 33 bound-constrained (n ≥ 5) and 34 unconstrained (n ≥ 100) CUTE problems against LANCELOT SR1 and exact Hessian at default options, both stopped at ‖proj g‖∞ < 10⁻⁵; evaluations (nfg / nf) and Sparcstation-2 CPU time; m = 3, 5, 17, 29; primal, dual and CG subspace options; failure codes for 9999 evaluations, no progress, and C1/F1 (pp. 8–15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: software plus benchmark. Drawbacks are listed next to the advantages: not rapidly convergent, may not reach high accuracy on highly ill-conditioned problems, cannot use problem structure (p. 3). Also written down: incomplete scale invariance (first iteration; the factr test, p. 7); the restart strategy succeeds in some of these hard cases but not all (p. 7); the C1 phenomenon (good f, gradient test unmet) "deserves further study" (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the benchmark is a structure-using rival (LANCELOT with exact Hessian or SR1) at its defaults, with the same projected-gradient stopping test (p. 8), and the result is stated with its cost: more function evaluations, comparable but highly variable CPU times (p. 8). But runs that miss the stopping test with a function value at least as good as LANCELOT's are labelled C1 and explicitly not counted as failures (p. 9), which classifies borderline runs in the author's favour, against Method 1 step 4.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the undecided subspace-minimization choice is exposed as the user option isbmin (primal / dual / CG, p. 5) and compared in Tables 1.5–1.6 (pp. 9, 14–15); the memory m is varied in Tables 1.3–1.4, with the advice 3 ≤ m ≤ 20 (pp. 2–3, 9).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: his own code is scoped to the case where structure is unknown: it is recommended for large problems whose Hessian is dense or hard to compute (p. 3), and "cannot make use of knowledge about the structure" is listed as a drawback (p. 3).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: a recovery path is built in (after 20 failed line-search evaluations, or when the matrix becomes singular or indefinite, the corrections are discarded and the method restarts along steepest descent; updates are skipped by a machine-precision test, p. 7). The safeguard uses an explicit estimate (machine precision) but no noise estimate.
- New-pattern candidate: *storage knob spanning two classical methods*: m sets the memory; small values are recommended and larger ones improve reliability at extra CPU time (pp. 2–3, 9); compare STORE in S138.

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a software paper, print advantages and drawbacks side by side, with the drawbacks concrete (no fast convergence, limited accuracy on ill-conditioned problems, no use of structure) (p. 3).
- Benchmark against a rival that uses more problem information, with the same stopping test, and report both evaluation counts and CPU time, saying which one you lose (p. 8).
- Give every exit a named status string and a counter the user can read (CONV / ABNO / ERROR; isave(26) counts skipped updates), and give tolerance advice as concrete values (factr = 1.d+12, 1.d+7, 1.d+1) (pp. 4–7).
- Define failure categories in the table legend (limit reached, no progress, C1, F1), so a reader can recount (pp. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "(iii) the algorithm cannot make use of knowledge about the structure of the problem to accelerate convergence." (p. 3)
- "We do not interpret these as failures of the algorithm, but feel that this property of L-BFGS deserves further study." (p. 9)
