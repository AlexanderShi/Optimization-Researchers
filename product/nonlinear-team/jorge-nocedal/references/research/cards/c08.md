### S015 · A stochastic quasi-Newton method for large-scale optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 26(2), 1008–1031, 2016, doi:10.1137/140954362; arXiv:1401.7020)
**Read level**: full. Read pp. 1–29 (abstract, §1–§6, all figures and captions) in the arXiv v2 text; reference list pp. 30–31 skimmed for the identities of [4], [12], [13], [14], [16], [24], [25].
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: R. H. Byrd (Colorado), S. L. Hansen (Northwestern ESAM), Y. Singer (Google Research) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the stochastic quasi-Newton paper of his 2010–2014 turn to machine learning; it follows his group's batch (SAA) Hessian-free methods [6, 7], from which it distinguishes itself (pp. 28–29).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap between theory and the dominant method. SGD needs about nνκ²/ε iterations, so it is "adversely affected by ill conditioning", while Murata's result that H_k = ∇²F(w*)⁻¹ removes κ "suggests that an appropriate choice of Hk may result in an algorithm that improves upon the stochastic gradient descent method" (p. 3). The obstacle is named in the abstract: classical quasi-Newton updating applied directly "leads to noisy curvature estimates that have harmful effects on the robustness of the iteration" (p. 1). The problem is min F(w) = E[f(w; ξ)] for convex F (1.1), with the empirical average (1.3) and mini-batch gradients of size b ≪ N (1.4) (pp. 2–3). Requirements are set before the design: update the inverse H_k directly and spend only O(n) operations per step (p. 3). The setting is data streamed to the learner (network traffic, web search, advertising) (p. 2).
  - Transferable: motivate a new method with an ideal but infeasible oracle result (here the κ-free complexity of the exact inverse Hessian, p. 3), then write down the scalability requirements (inverse updated directly, O(n) per step) before proposing anything.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The diagnosis is mechanistic. Quasi-Newton updating overwrites instead of averaging, so y must represent the Hessian of the whole objective, which differences of small-sample stochastic gradients cannot give (p. 5). The repair is to "decouple the stochastic gradient and curvature estimate calculations" (p. 5). Curvature pairs are formed every L iterations (counter t, separate from the gradient counter k). s_t is the difference of averaged iterates over the last 2L iterations (2.1). y_t = ∇̂²F(w̄_t)s_t is a sub-sampled Hessian-vector product on its own sample S_H, which "should be relatively large" whatever b is (2.2)–(2.3) (p. 5). The first 2L iterations are plain SGD (Algorithm 1, steps 7–8, p. 6). What is kept: the BFGS formula, limited memory with initial scaling sᵀy/yᵀy, and the two-loop recursion (Algorithm 2, p. 7). In the paper's words it "builds upon the strengths of BFGS updating, but deviates from the classical method" only in the correction pairs (p. 7). The paper says which parts are incidental: averaging "is not essential" (p. 6), and Hessian-vector products "may not be essential" if gradient differences used the same sample and guarded against round-off (p. 29). Cost is argued by formula. The SQN/SGD cost ratio is 1 + 2M/b + 2b_H/(3bL) (2.11), small for M = 5, b ≥ 50, L = 10–20, b_H ≥ 300 (p. 8). The compact L-BFGS form parallelises to about 3n, independent of M, and the Hessian-vector product may lag behind the iteration (p. 9). Parameter guidelines: b is exogenous and the method "should work well with any value of b"; Lb/b_H in [2, 20]; M in [4, 20] (p. 9).
  - Transferable: when a deterministic update breaks under sampling, put the estimate that needs accuracy (curvature) on its own larger sample and a slower clock, so its cost is amortized. Then write the per-iteration cost ratio as a function of the algorithm parameters and turn it into a parameter guideline (pp. 5, 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Convex stochastic objective (p. 2). The analysis assumes strong convexity and C², and the authors admit the first "may appear to be unduly strong", because logistic-regression components are convex but not strongly convex. They justify it by ℓ2 regularization (3.1)–(3.2) or by iterates staying in a strongly convex region, and "do not specify the precise mechanism" (pp. 9–10). Assumptions 1 require λI ≺ ∇̂²F(w) ≺ ΛI for every w and every subset S_H (3.3) (p. 10). Assumptions 2, for the general iteration, add a uniform bound γ² on the gradient's second moment (3.14) (p. 12). α_k = β/k is chosen "for convenience" instead of the Robbins–Monro conditions, to get a short proof in the style of Nemirovski et al. (p. 12). Nonconvex problems are covered only conditionally, given a mechanism that keeps s_tᵀy_t > 0 (p. 29). Two observations by this reader, not stated in the paper: (a) the test objectives (4.1) and (4.4) carry no ℓ2 term as written (pp. 15, 22), so the experiments do not visibly satisfy Assumptions 1; (b) (3.13) and (3.14) imposed for all w ∈ ℝⁿ cannot both hold globally (strong convexity makes ‖∇F(w)‖ unbounded, and by Jensen the second-moment bound caps it), so the assumption set is consistent only on a bounded region. (b) is a deduction from p. 12, not a claim of the paper.
  - Transferable: when a convenience assumption is strong, say so in the text and name the practical mechanism that would enforce it (regularization, a bounded region) (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Analysis and proof technique**: Two pieces. (1) Lemma 3.1 (pp. 10–12): the L-BFGS matrices have eigenvalues uniformly bounded above and away from zero. The proof switches to the direct matrix B_t "because this allows us to easily quote a result from the literature" (p. 10). Because y_j is an exact sub-sampled Hessian times s_j, λ‖s‖² ≤ yᵀs ≤ Λ‖s‖² (3.7) and λ ≤ ‖y‖²/yᵀs ≤ Λ (3.8) follow at once, the second via the Hessian's square root (p. 11). The standard trace bound (3.9) caps the largest eigenvalue, and Powell's determinant formula (3.10)–(3.11) keeps the smallest away from zero (pp. 11–12). No line search or Wolfe condition is needed, because the construction of y guarantees the curvature condition. (2) Theorem 3.2 (pp. 12–13) covers any Newton-like iteration w^{k+1} = w^k − α_kH_k∇f(w^k, ξ^k) with µ1I ≺ H_k ≺ µ2I. It proves E[F(w^k) − F*] ≤ Q(β)/k for β > 1/(2µ1λ), where Q(β) = max{Λµ2²β²γ²/(2(2µ1λβ − 1)), F(w¹) − F*} (3.16)–(3.17). Skeleton: the descent lemma with Λ, then expectation with the γ² bound (3.18); a minorizing quadratic gives 2λ[F − F*] ≤ ‖∇F‖² (3.19); this yields the recursion φ_{k+1} ≤ (1 − 2α_kµ1λ)φ_k + (Λ/2)(α_kµ2)²γ² (3.22), closed by induction (p. 13). The general result is proved because it "is of interest beyond this paper" and no self-contained proof was known to them (c.f. [25]) (p. 12). Corollary 3.3 specializes it to SQN through Lemma 3.1 (p. 14). Form of the result: an O(1/k) sublinear rate in expectation. The constants depend on µ1, µ2, λ, Λ and γ. Nothing in §3 shows SQN's constant beats SGD's (H_k = I satisfies the theorem too) (pp. 12–14).
  - Transferable: choose the estimator so the analysis comes for free. With y = (sub-sampled Hessian)·s, the curvature ratios are bounded by the Hessian bounds, and the trace–determinant argument applies directly (pp. 10–12).
  - Transferable: prove the rate for the general class "H_k with bounded eigenvalues", then obtain the method-specific result as a corollary through an eigenvalue lemma (Lemma 3.1 + Theorem 3.2 → Corollary 3.3, pp. 10–14). Keep in mind that such a theorem cannot show the method beats SGD.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Baselines: SGD with β/k (2.5); a coordinate-descent reference value from an implementation [13] of a Tseng–Yun method (pp. 15–16); oLBFGS [24], reimplemented by the authors with stated settings (no damping, ε = 10⁻⁶ first-step rescaling, the initial scaling recommended in [24]) (pp. 26–27). Tuning protocol for β, the same for SGD and SQN: try several values and keep the one where a fixed increment up or down is worse. This isolates the effect of H_k "in a controlled setting" (p. 14). Tuned values differ, e.g. SGD β = 7 against SQN β = 2 (p. 16). Cost axes: "accessed data points" (adp), counting b + b_H/L per SQN iteration (p. 15); iteration counts in some plots; a flop-model work axis (2.10), scaled by 1/n, for small batches (p. 25). No CPU or wall-clock time is reported (§4). Test problems: synthetic binary logistic regression, N = 7000, n = 50 (p. 15); RCV1, n = 112919, N = 688329, binary "Markets" label (p. 17); a Google speech set with multi-class logistic regression, N = 191607, n = 30315 (p. 22). Gradient samples are drawn without replacement within each epoch, Hessian samples independently (p. 15). Parameter studies: b_H from 10 to 10000 (Fig. 4, pp. 18–19; Fig. 8, p. 23); M from 0 to 50 (Fig. 2, p. 17; Fig. 5, p. 20; Fig. 9, pp. 23–24); b = 10, 20 with L = 200, which was "crucial" (Fig. 11, pp. 25–26); test error on a 75/25 split (Fig. 10, pp. 23–25). Failures reported and diagnosed: the b_H = 10 spike, where the monitored angle between s and y approached 90 degrees between iterations 3100 and 3200, with the proposed safeguard to monitor sᵀy relative to sᵀBs and enlarge b_H or skip the update (p. 19). M = 0 is "poor and also unstable" (p. 20). Why M matters less on RCV1 than on the synthetic set is "not clear" (pp. 19–20). Internal diagnostics: GradError and HvError relative to full-data quantities (4.2)–(4.3), computed every 20 iterations of one run (pp. 20–21). Each configuration appears as one curve; the text mentions no repeated runs or variance bands (§4, pp. 14–27).
  - Transferable: tune the step parameter of every method by the same rule (a local optimum under ± one increment) on a fixed β/k schedule, so the comparison isolates the new component (p. 14).
  - Transferable: plot against a cost unit that charges your method its overheads (b + b_H/L data points per iteration), and add a flop-model work curve on the same axes when the overhead is not data access (pp. 15, 25).
  - Transferable: when a run spikes, log the angle between s and y (or sᵀy against sᵀBs) to pin the bad update, and turn the diagnosis into a skip or resample rule (p. 19).
  - Transferable: run the degenerate setting M = 0 (a scalar multiple of I) to show that the gain comes from full-matrix curvature, not rescaling (pp. 20, 23, 29).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends his own L-BFGS [11, 17] to the stochastic-approximation regime (pp. 4–5). The notation paragraph links three communities (Robbins–Monro in statistics, SA in stochastic programming, SGD in ML) and adopts the ML name (p. 4). Earlier stochastic quasi-Newton methods [24, 12, 4, 22] "have not been entirely successful" (p. 27). Their common fault is a Hessian approximation "updated with a high level of noise", and two of them need an extra gradient per iteration for sample uniformity (p. 28). The shared design choice is credited: [4] also updates "at fixed intervals" (p. 28). AdaGrad (diagonal) is distinguished from the full-matrix approach, and the natural-gradient line (Amari, TONGA, Roux–Fitzgibbon) and the Bach–Moulines two-stage Newton are placed as different in kind (p. 28). The group's own batch Hessian-free methods [6, 7] are set apart as SAA, not SA (pp. 28–29). oLBFGS is named "the best known stochastic quasi-Newton method in the literature" and is beaten in a direct comparison. oLBFGS is recast as SQN with L = 1 and b = b_H, which "is not an efficient allocation of effort" (pp. 26–27). Proof sources: Nemirovski et al. [16] (p. 12), Powell [20] (p. 11).
  - Transferable: recast the strongest rival as a parameter setting of your own scheme (oLBFGS ≈ SQN with L = 1, b = b_H), then show that this setting is the wasteful one (p. 27).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is in the abstract ("efficient, robust and scalable"), with hedged conclusions: results "suggest that the proposed method shows much promise" (p. 1); "suggest that the new method is robust and efficient" (p. 4). The key idea appears in the second paragraph of the introduction: "The key is to compute average curvature estimates at regular intervals" (p. 2). The burden of proof is stated explicitly: "Our task in the remainder of the paper is to argue that" SQN is competitive with SGD in computing time and reaches a lower objective (p. 7). The mechanism is explained in prose (overwriting vs averaging, p. 5) rather than through a theorem. Theory is about 4 pages (one lemma, one theorem, one corollary; pp. 10–14), experiments about 14 (pp. 14–27). Incidental components are flagged as such ("not essential", pp. 6, 29). Small slips survive in v2: "can a useful indicator" (p. 19), "a task that is not often not onerous" (p. 29).
  - Transferable: state in one sentence what the rest of the paper must argue, including the cost measure ("competitive ... in terms of computing time (even in the early stages of the optimization)"), so that readers can check each experiment against it (p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Contributions are an algorithm (SQN), a convergence result for a general class of iterations, and a numerical study on three ML problems (§2–§4). No code release is mentioned in the text. Stated limits and futures: convex case only, with the nonconvex extension conditional on keeping sᵀy > 0 (p. 29); Hessian-vector code is required (p. 29); a gradient-difference variant with the same sample is left open (p. 29); the s–y monitoring safeguard is proposed but not implemented (p. 19); the M effect is unexplained (p. 20). Limits this reader sees in the text: per-problem β tuning with no rule for choosing it ("no generally accepted rule", p. 14); a rate of only O(1/k) that does not reflect the curvature gain (pp. 12–14); single runs; no wall-clock time. The later step-size and no-linear-rate critique by the SLBFGS authors is logged in 05-peer-critique.md and matches the per-problem β tuning seen here (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the classical engine is kept (BFGS formula, limited memory, two-loop recursion) and only the failing component, the correction pair, is replaced (pp. 1, 5–7). But the repair is a design of sampling and schedule, not one driven by an explicit noise-level estimate. The recovery path (monitor sᵀy, enlarge b_H or skip) is only proposed (p. 19), and there are no exit flags.
- Method 5 ✗ contradiction: Theorem 3.2 holds for any H_k with bounded eigenvalues, so it blesses SGD (H_k = I) equally and cannot discriminate SQN from SGD. The superiority claim rests on numerics: "Our numerical results suggest …" (p. 29) (pp. 12–14, 29). This is taste warning sign 2 in his own work. It does analyse the version as run (H_t constant for L iterations, p. 12).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the fair-accounting core is present. The same β rule is used for every method (p. 14). The cost axis charges SQN's Hessian samples (p. 15) and a flop model (p. 25). The strongest rival is reimplemented with the settings its authors recommend (p. 27). But there is no handicap of the own method (L, M, b_H set per problem; L = 200 "crucial", p. 26), no losses in the abstract (p. 1), and only single runs (§4).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (weak): failure modes are written into the paper with a diagnosis and a proposed safeguard (b_H = 10 spike, p. 19; M = 0 instability, p. 20). Open choices are exposed as parameters with numeric guidelines (p. 9), and the open questions are listed (nonconvex, gradient differences; p. 29).

- New-pattern candidate: **decouple and amortize noisy estimates** (separate sample and slower clock for curvature, plus a cost-ratio formula; pp. 5–9, 27).
- New-pattern candidate: **make the safeguard unnecessary by construction** (y as a Hessian-vector product gives yᵀs ≥ λ‖s‖² automatically and handles sample uniformity; pp. 5, 10–12, 29).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give the accuracy-sensitive estimate its own sample and a slower update clock. Then choose its size and frequency from an explicit cost ratio (1 + 2M/b + 2b_H/(3bL)) and turn the ratio into a numeric guideline (Lb/b_H ∈ [2, 20]) (pp. 5, 8–9).
- Replace a noisy gradient difference with a sub-sampled Hessian-vector product at an averaged point. The curvature condition then holds by construction, and the trace–determinant eigenvalue lemma applies without a line search (pp. 5, 10–12).
- Prove an O(1/k) result once for the general iteration w − α_kH_k∇f with bounded H_k, then specialize through an eigenvalue lemma (pp. 12–14). Say plainly that it does not separate your method from SGD.
- Use the same step-tuning rule for every method (keep the β where ± one increment is worse) on a fixed β/k schedule (p. 14).
- Use an accessed-data-points axis that charges the new method's extra samples, and a flop-model work curve when overhead is arithmetic (pp. 15, 25).
- Diagnose spikes by logging the angle between s and y, and propose a skip or resample rule from that diagnosis (p. 19).
- Run the M = 0 ablation to show that the gain is curvature, not scaling (pp. 20, 23).
- Recast the strongest rival as a parameter setting of your own method (oLBFGS ≈ L = 1, b = b_H) and show that the setting is inefficient (p. 27).
