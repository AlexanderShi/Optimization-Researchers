### S030 · Automatic preconditioning by limited memory quasi-Newton updating (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2000, DOI 10.1137/s1052623497327854)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–23 was read: abstract, §§1–5, acknowledgements, the Appendix (the sampling algorithm, pp. 20–21, read in full) and the references. The OCR has lost most table bodies. Tables 1–2 (p. 7) are fragments, Tables 3 and 7–17 (pp. 8, 12, 14–19) keep only their captions, and Tables 4–5 (pp. 9–10) are partial. Several exponents (the stopping tolerance (3.6), the TOL values) are garbled. No numbers are taken from the tables; results come from the prose.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: José Luis Morales (ITAM, Mexico) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: L-BFGS (refs. [12], [19]) moves from an optimizer to a preconditioner for the inner CG solver of a Hessian-free Newton method (pp. 2–3). The paper grows out of the Byrd–Nocedal–Zhu discrete Newton method with memory (ref. [6], p. 3), and the test problems come from the Nash–Nocedal and Liu–Marazzi–Nocedal studies (p. 6). It thanks Richard Waltz for running the Table 5 tests and a referee for suggesting the experiments of Tables 15–17 (p. 20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry was an observation made "while performing numerical tests with a Hessian-free Newton method": Nash's 2-step preconditioner "was effective only in a few test problems", while the new technique "gave improvements over a wide range of problems" (p. 3). The problem is stated in general form: CG for a sequence of SPD systems with a fixed matrix and varying right-hand sides (1.1), or with slowly varying matrices (1.2), when only products A·v can be computed (abstract; p. 2). What drives the cost is that in Hessian-free Newton each product is a finite difference of gradients, so one CG iteration costs one gradient evaluation (pp. 5–6).
  - Transferable: when a nested method is expensive, look at what its inner solver computes and then throws away.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Run unpreconditioned CG on the first system, save m pairs sᵢ = xᵢ₊₁ − xᵢ and yᵢ = rᵢ₊₁ − rᵢ, and use the L-BFGS matrix H(m) built from them to precondition the next system (pp. 2, 4). Several parts are left unchanged: the limited-memory representation (2.3) with its 4mn-flop product recursion, the scaled initial matrix (2.4) (pp. 4–5), and Nash's Hessian-free Newton code with the Moré–Thuente line search and inner test (3.4) (p. 5). The new choice is which pairs to keep. The options are the last m pairs, or a uniform sample over the CG run that Algorithm SAMPLE maintains online with no extra storage or computation (pp. 2, 4, 20–21). Inside Hessian-free Newton the preconditioner is rebuilt at every outer iteration and used at the next one (p. 6). In the CUTE runs the outer correction pair is stored as well (p. 8).
  - Transferable: build the preconditioner from pairs the inner solver already produces, so it costs no extra matrix-vector products.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: The coefficient matrices are SPD (p. 2), and f is twice continuously differentiable (p. 5). Hessian-vector products are forward differences with h = (1 + ‖x‖)√ε_m (pp. 5–6). After the finite-element tests the paper assumes explicitly that computing A·v costs much more than applying the preconditioner (p. 12). No theoretical assumptions are made, because there is no theory.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a for proofs: there is no theorem. The paper offers explanations instead. (a) The spectrum of H(49)A1n, one small eigenvalue and a cluster of 49 eigenvalues at 1, is "expected, given the properties of quasi-Newton updating", because A1n has dimension 50 (p. 12). (b) A structural argument about A1n (its first row is e1ᵀ, so x1 = b1 and the other components are tiny) explains why the last-m preconditioner works very well for random b at the tight tolerance, and why it gains little once b1 is set to zero (pp. 16–17). (c) Qualitative properties of Algorithm SAMPLE: there is a transition period between uniform distributions, and it is longer when m is larger (p. 21). The authors concede that "It is difficult to provide theoretical arguments in favor of either strategy" (p. 17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: (1) In Hessian-free Newton, five problems with n = 100 were chosen for five distinct classes of eigenvalue distribution (p. 6). CG iterations are the main measure, because the outer counts "vary somewhat randomly" (p. 6). Every problem shows a substantial cut at m = 8, and m = 16 brings no further gain. Table 2 explains why: there are few CG iterations per Newton step (p. 6). (2) A controlled test. The Hessian was frozen at an intermediate positive-definite iterate of cvar-2 and penalty-3, and 51 systems with random right-hand sides were solved, plain CG first and then preconditioned, at two tolerances (p. 7). The gain is modest at the tight tolerance on cvar-2 and substantial at the relaxed one (pp. 7–8). (3) CUTE problems (Table 4, p. 9) with failure codes −2 (more than 3000 CG iterations) and −3 (more than 20 line-search iterations without decrease). The preconditioner reduces CG iterations and also improves reliability (p. 10). (4) Two MINPACK-2 problems with CPU time (Table 5, p. 10). (5) Finite-element matrices from Belytschko et al.: 1-D with n = 50 and n = 451, a 2-D cantilever, and five meshes perturbed by 1–10% (pp. 10–11). Right-hand sides were generated by random ±5% perturbations, and results are averaged over 50 runs from two starting points (p. 11). The finite-element tests also cover slowly varying systems with a fixed preconditioner, hot starts and refreshed preconditioners (pp. 12–15), a comparison of sampling strategies (pp. 16–17) and a sample-size test with a forced maxCG (p. 18). The metric is CG iterations, not time, because the aim is to cut gradient evaluations. The paper warns that the 4mn cost matters when gradients are cheap (p. 8), and reports that the sparse finite-element tests gave no CPU reduction for any m (p. 12). Negative results are printed: refreshing gives gains "less significant than one would expect" (p. 15), and preconditioning does not help the hot start from one of the two starting points in Table 9 (p. 16).
  - Transferable: freeze one outer iterate's Hessian and solve many random right-hand sides, so that an inner-solver device is judged apart from outer-loop variation (p. 7).
- **D6 Positioning**: "The idea of saving information from the CG iteration in the form of a quasi-Newton matrix is not new" (p. 3). The paper credits Nash's m = 2 matrix [15], the full-memory quasi-Newton preconditioners of O'Leary–Yeremin [21], and Byrd–Nocedal–Zhu [6], where the limited-memory matrix gives only a search direction, not a preconditioner (p. 3). It claims a wider scope rather than priority: "The objective of this paper is to suggest that the automatic preconditioner is well suited not only in optimization, but in a wider context" (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The claims are hedged throughout: "Numerical experiments indicate" (abstract); the preconditioner "may be useful" (p. 18); "more research is required to establish this firmly" (p. 20). Each table is followed by a reason: few CG steps explain why m = 16 adds nothing (p. 6), and the structure of A1n explains Table 12 (pp. 16–17). Things that did not win are reported briefly. One is a selection rule based on the smallest Rayleigh quotient (p. 20). Another is a variant without the outer pair, which was "slightly less successful" and so is not tabulated (p. 8). An experiment is credited to the referee (p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and empirical: a matrix-free preconditioner and an online uniform-sampling algorithm (pp. 2, 20–21). The limits are concrete. There is no CPU gain on very sparse matrices, where A·v needs about 3n or 14n multiplications against 4mn for the preconditioner (p. 12). When to refresh the preconditioner is "not simple" (p. 15). The hot start helps only sometimes (p. 16). There is no theory for choosing the sampling strategy (p. 17). Usefulness on finite-element problems needs more research (p. 20). Stated future directions: dynamic refresh strategies (p. 15), pairs taken from different systems (p. 5), and Rayleigh-quotient selection for other applications (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 6, evidence ✅ (scope): the generic, structure-blind preconditioner costs 4mn "independently of the matrix structure". It is shown to lose in CPU time on very sparse matrices, and the claim is confined to matrices that are not very sparse or are available only through products (pp. 12, 18).
- Method 1, evidence ✅ on printed losses: no CPU gain (p. 12), weak refresh gains (p. 15), a hot start that does not help (p. 16). Variant ⚠: no competing preconditioner is run in any table. Nash's 2-step preconditioner is dismissed in one sentence, without data (p. 3).
- Method 3, variant ⚠: the CG and Hessian-free Newton engine is unchanged, and one component, the preconditioner, is added from the engine's own by-products (pp. 2, 6). But no component is failing, and there is no explicit estimate with a recovery path.
- Method 5, variant ⚠: there is no theory, and the paper says so and substitutes controlled tests (p. 17). It does not present numerical experience as proof, but it offers no discriminating analysis either.
- Also Heuristic 5: m = 8 is enough, and more memory gains nothing (p. 6). Heuristic 4: several selection strategies were tried and the two simplest kept (pp. 2, 20).
- New-pattern candidate: harvest inner-solver by-products as quasi-Newton curvature pairs (pp. 2, 4, 6; precedent Byrd–Nocedal–Zhu [6], p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Evaluate an inner-solver device on a frozen Hessian with 50 random right-hand sides and two stopping tolerances, so that outer-loop noise is removed (p. 7).
- Put the device's own cost per application (4mn) next to the cost of the product it saves (3n, 14n), which states exactly when it pays (p. 12).
- Keep a uniform sample of a stream of correction pairs within fixed memory, online (Algorithm SAMPLE, pp. 20–21).
- Before generalising a selection rule, construct the matrix and right-hand side on which it fails, and explain the failure (A1n with b1 = 0, pp. 16–17).

**Quotes**:
- "the cost of applying the preconditioner is too high to offset the reduction in the number of CG iterations in these experiments" (p. 12)
- "It is difficult to provide theoretical arguments in favor of either strategy, but we now report the results of controlled tests that further support the uniform sampling technique." (p. 17)

### S040 · Wedge trust region methods for derivative free optimization (Mathematical Programming 2002, DOI 10.1007/s101070100264)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–19 was read: abstract, §§1–5, acknowledgements and references. Table 2 (p. 16) is scrambled into separate columns and cannot be read row by row. Table 1 (p. 14) has a few garbled entries, and its "boxes" marking the winners are lost. Table 3 (p. 17) is readable, except that three problem names are missing.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Marcelo Marazzi (Northwestern IEMS). His PhD thesis, "Nonlinear Optimization With and Without Derivatives", was expected in June 2001 (ref. [7], p. 18) · **Place in trajectory**: an early derivative-free algorithm of his, OTC report of 26 October 2000 (p. 1). It comes nine years before the 2009 finding that the geometry phase can be omitted (SKILL Method 2) and 21 years before the finite-difference study (arXiv:2102.09762). It thanks Katya Scheinberg "for some useful observations on testing derivative-free methods" and for DFO results, and Mike Powell for supplying COBYLA (p. 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is one component of the incumbent model-based trust-region methods (refs. [4, 5, 11, 12]). After a trust-region step the interpolation set may become degenerate, so these methods alternate "minimization" iterations with "simplex" iterations that repair the geometry, and they also shrink the radius (pp. 2–3). The setting is a smooth f with a moderate number of variables, no derivatives, and evaluations that cost much more than the linear algebra. Physical measurements and proprietary code are given as reasons why derivatives may be unavailable (p. 2). Finite-difference gradient methods are named up front as the baseline (p. 2).
  - Transferable: find the second kind of iteration a method needs to repair its own model, and ask whether that requirement can be written into the main step.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: "Our method performs only one type of iteration" (p. 3): the geometric (non-degeneracy) condition is imposed as a constraint in the step subproblem (2.2) (pp. 3–4). The satellite farthest from x is marked to leave first (p. 4). The "taboo region" of steps that would make the new set degenerate is the hyperplane bᵀs = 0 for linear models (pp. 6–7) and the quadric bᵀs + ½sᵀBs = 0 (4.10) for quadratic models (p. 9). The wedge constraint asks that the cosine of the angle between the step and the normal be at least γ: (3.4) on p. 7 and (4.12c) on p. 10. The constraint has the shape of a wedge, hence the method's name (p. 2). The second novelty is Step 6: an unsuccessful trial point is kept as a satellite if it is no farther from x than the leaving point, so the model improves while steps are rejected (pp. 5–6). Several parts are unchanged: trust-region acceptance and the radius update (Step 5, p. 5), the Moré–Sorensen subproblem solver (p. 11), and one QR factorization for the interpolation (p. 10). In the quadratic version the step is rotated away from the taboo region. If the rotation raises the model value too much, by test (4.13) (its constant is garbled in the OCR), the authors "close the wedge", i.e. reduce γ by (4.14), so γ never increases (p. 11; Procedure QuadStep, pp. 11–12).
  - Transferable: give a safeguard that competes with progress an adaptive width that shrinks only when it is shown to cost model decrease.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is smooth, the number of variables is moderate, and the evaluation cost dominates (p. 2). The model needs m = n satellites for a linear model and (n+1)(n+2)/2 − 1 for a quadratic model. The method assumes an initial non-degenerate set and f(x) ≤ f(y) for every satellite y (p. 4). There is no noise model: the only arithmetic caveat is that COBYLA runs in single precision (pp. 12–13). Randomness enters only through the random signs of the initial satellites (5.1) (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No theorem is proved in this paper. Global convergence is asserted ("satisfactory global convergence properties") and deferred to a paper in preparation [8] and to the thesis [7] (p. 6). The design argument behind Step 6 is a property taken from the proofs: gradient-based trust-region methods guarantee a successful step once the radius is small enough, and the rejected points are kept "In order to retain this important property in interpolation-based models" (p. 5). After repeated failures with a shrinking Δ, trial points become satellites and the model becomes accurate (p. 6). Design devices: (a) a closed-form global solution of the linear wedge subproblem by rotation in span{g, b}, unique except when bᵀg = 0 (p. 7); (b) the quadratic wedge rewritten through ‖ssᵀ‖_F = ‖s‖² (p. 9); (c) one QR factorization that gives both the model coefficients and the taboo normal (p. 10).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The tests use CUTE problems with n = 2–16 (Tables 1–3, pp. 14, 16–17), run on a Sun Ultra 5 with the wedge code in Matlab (p. 12). The radius rule uses α = 0 and β = 1/2, with Δ doubling after a boundary step (p. 12). Each problem was run five times and the median reported, because the initial satellites are random (p. 12). The stopping test works as follows. NITRO, which here is Newton's method with exact second derivatives, is first run to obtain f*. The derivative-free codes then stop when f0 − fk ≥ (1 − ε)(f0 − f*) with ε = 10⁻⁶ (5.3), a test also used by Elster–Neumaier [6], or at 8000 evaluations (pp. 12–13).
  - **WEDlin against COBYLA** (γ = 0.4 fixed; rhoend = 10×macheps; WEDlin stops at Δ < 10×macheps; p. 13): the authors call WEDlin "competitive" (p. 13). Table 1 shows wins both ways. WEDlin uses fewer evaluations on HAIRY (98 vs 3195) and SNAIL (1915 vs 8000); COBYLA uses fewer on BOX3 (375 vs 2276) and BROWNAL (11 vs 629) (p. 14). The wedge constraint is active in a large share of iterations (p. 13). COBYLA's abnormal terminations are explained: single-precision AKIVA, an undefined f, and rhoend (p. 13).
  - **WEDquad against QNfd**, i.e. L-BFGS-B with forward-difference gradients at "all its default settings" (p. 13): WEDquad is "sometimes, but not always, more efficient", with one failure against five. The authors attribute QNfd's failures to finite-difference errors that stall the line search (p. 15).
  - **WEDquad against DFO**: DFO was not public, so the comparison uses the published results [5] plus runs supplied by Scheinberg. WEDquad stops when fk ≤ f_DFO (5.4), and the authors warn that this test "may be adverse to DFO", because DFO spends evaluations recognising convergence (p. 15). In my count of the 39 readable rows of Table 3 (p. 17), WEDquad uses fewer evaluations on 30, DFO on 8, with one tie.
  - On most problems γ falls well below 0.4 (p. 15).
  - Transferable: when cross-class codes cannot share a stopping rule, define a target from a derivative-based reference solve and stop everyone at the same fraction of the attainable decrease (pp. 12–13).
- **D6 Positioning**: The work sits within interpolation-based trust-region DFO, with pattern search and simulated annealing as the other classes. It cites Powell's survey and M. H. Wright's "Once scorned, now respectable" (pp. 2, 19). The difference is stated as "In contrast to the methods described in [4, 5, 11, 12]": the step itself carries the geometric constraint (p. 2). The rivals are described neutrally, without direct criticism. The DFO comparison notes how the test may be unfair to the rival (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The novelty is stated in one sentence: "Its novelty lies in the use of the wedge constraint, and in in the acceptance strategy in step 6" (p. 5). Three figures make the geometry visible (pp. 7–8, 10). The results are hedged: "these results suggest that WEDlin is competitive" (p. 13); "sometimes, but not always" (p. 15). The paper ends on weaknesses and refinements rather than a summary (pp. 15, 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm in two versions with numerical evidence; the theory is deferred (p. 6). Named limits: starting from the vertices and midpoints of a simplex is "not an efficient strategy" (p. 15), and the subproblem acceptance test (4.13) is "quite demanding" (p. 15). Future directions: linear or underdetermined quadratic models in the early iterations, increasing γ on some iterations, and a test against the Cauchy point (pp. 15, 18). Two limits are not stated: the Matlab implementation runs against compiled rivals, one of them in single precision (p. 12), and all tests have n ≤ 16 (pp. 14, 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 1, evidence ✅: one rival-neutral target from a derivative-based reference solve (5.3), one evaluation budget (pp. 12–13), the median of five runs (p. 12), rivals' abnormal terminations explained (p. 13), and the bias of the DFO comparison against the rival named (p. 15). Correspondence with the rival school is acknowledged: Scheinberg on testing, Powell for the code (p. 18). Variant ⚠: QNfd runs at defaults with forward differences, and the difference interval is not studied (pp. 13, 15). The 2021 study took that care.
- Method 3, variant ⚠: the trust-region engine and interpolation model are kept, and only the geometry-maintenance component is replaced, by a constraint in the step and an acceptance rule (pp. 3, 5). The adaptive γ is a mild self-correction (p. 11). The change is driven neither by a new regime nor by an explicit estimate.
- Method 2, variant ⚠ (trajectory): in 2000 geometry is treated as necessary and enforced at every step (pp. 2–3, 7). The 2009 finding that the geometry phase can be omitted (SKILL Method 2) reverses this, so this paper is an earlier point on the list of positions that moved. The finite-difference quasi-Newton baseline is already present (p. 2), but here it loses on reliability (p. 15), unlike in 2021.
- Method 5: n/a (no theory in this paper; deferred to [7], [8], p. 6).
- New-pattern candidates: a proof-driven safeguard, i.e. a mechanism that restores the property the classical convergence proof needs, here a successful step at a small radius (pp. 5–6; also S035 pp. 3, 8); a reference-solver target for comparisons across method classes (pp. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Compare derivative-free codes against a common target defined by a derivative-based solve: f0 − fk ≥ (1 − ε)(f0 − f*), with f* from a Newton code (pp. 12–13).
- Write the non-degeneracy condition as a cosine bound against the normal of the "taboo" set, and add it to the trust-region subproblem (pp. 6–7, 9).
- Keep rejected trial points that are closer than the point you would discard, so that the model improves while the radius shrinks (pp. 5–6).
- Report the percentage of iterations in which the safeguard is active, to show that it does work (p. 13).
- When a comparison relies on a rival's published results, adapt your stopping test to theirs and say which way the bias goes (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "In order to retain this important property in interpolation-based models, we deviate from the standard practice of discarding trial points that give rise to an increase the objective function" (p. 5)
- "It is difficult to design an adequate stopping test for comparing derivative free methods, and this last comparison is an example of that." (p. 15)

### S057 · On the behavior of the gradient norm in the steepest descent method (Computational Optimization and Applications 2002, DOI 10.1023/a:1014897230089)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–27 was read: abstract, §§1–6 and references. Several displayed formulas are garbled ((2.7), (3.9)–(3.14), (4.1), (5.1)–(5.2), (5.8)–(5.10), (6.2)–(6.6)); their content is taken from the prose that states them. Figures 1–12 are only partly legible.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Annick Sartenaer (FUNDP Namur) and Ciyou Zhu (Northwestern ECE); travel support came from a NATO grant (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a by-product of the L-BFGS-B development (refs. [5], [14]); preprint of 30 May 2000 (p. 1). It cites private communications from J. Ch. Gilbert (1994) and J. Moré (1995), who had seen the same behaviour (p. 2; refs. on p. 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an anomaly in his own code. L-BFGS-B could not reduce ‖g‖∞ as far as LANCELOT, and this was reported as a failure until a closer look showed that the limited-memory code had sometimes reached a lower f (p. 2). Table 1 gives a striking case on PENALTY3: L-BFGS (m = 5) ends with a lower f than an inexact Newton method but a larger final squared gradient norm (p. 2). The paper asks whether the oscillations of ‖g‖ are related to conditioning and to the rate of convergence, and when ‖g‖ is a good estimate of the accuracy in f (p. 2). The practical stake is that ‖g‖ drives automatic stopping tests, including the subproblem tests of augmented Lagrangian and barrier methods (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a code "fails" its stopping test, compare the quantity that matters (f) across codes before accepting the label.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: No new algorithm. The paper analyses steepest descent with exact line searches on strongly convex quadratics (p. 5), with general observations for nonlinear f (§2) and for finite precision (§6). Its results:
  - (i) f − f* and ‖g‖² are both proportional to ‖x − x*‖², with Rayleigh-quotient factors, so their ratio can vary by as much as the condition number (pp. 3–4). The path taken therefore decides whether the final gradient norm is small or large (p. 4).
  - (ii) Theorem 4.1 bounds the one-step growth of ‖g‖². As a consequence ‖g‖ decreases monotonically when γ < 3 + 2√2, and large oscillations imply ill-conditioning (p. 12).
  - (iii) Theorem 5.1 gives the limits of the one-step ratios in terms of γ and Akaike's constant c. The two-step rate of ‖g‖ equals the one-step rate of f (pp. 13–14).
  - (iv) The possible values of c are confined by the deviation δ of the inner eigenvalues (Lemma 3.5, pp. 9–10). So for most problems the asymptotic oscillation stays well below the bound (pp. 15, 18), and the larger the oscillation, the faster the convergence in f (p. 18).
  - (v) The iterates approach along the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue, which gives small final gradient norms (pp. 19–20).
  - (vi) The 2-D case is solved exactly (pp. 21–24).
  - (vii) The error in the final f grows linearly with γ (pp. 24–26).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: A strongly convex quadratic and exact line searches (p. 5). Assumption 1 rules out termination in one step. Assumption 2 (distinct eigenvalues, and a starting gradient with components along the extreme eigenvectors) is made for exposition: (3.6) can be relaxed following Forsythe, and (3.7) is without loss of generality (pp. 6, 10). §2 holds for any twice continuously differentiable f (pp. 3–4). §6 ignores rounding errors in the iterates, which the paper concedes (p. 25). The n-dimensional observations "apply to most, but not all, problems" (p. 14).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - (a) Mean-value Taylor expansions express f − f* and ‖g‖² through Rayleigh quotients of G and G² in the direction x − x* (eqs. (2.3)–(2.7), pp. 3–4). For quadratics the change of variable z = G^{1/2}(x − x*) is used (2.8) (p. 4).
  - (b) The Kantorovich inequality is applied to the exact one-step identity for ‖g⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾‖², "similar to that used in [11]" (Luenberger) for the rate in f. This gives Theorem 4.1 (p. 12).
  - (c) Akaike (1959) and Forsythe (1968) asymptotics: the iteration is reduced to the two extreme eigenvectors and alternates between two directions (Proposition 3.1, pp. 6–7). The constant c is reinterpreted as the limiting ratio of gradient components (Lemma 3.4, pp. 8–9), with step-length limits (Lemma 3.3, p. 8).
  - (d) The interval for c² is restricted using the minimum deviation δ of the inner eigenvalues from the mean (Lemma 3.5, pp. 9–10; Figure 2, p. 11).
  - (e) The paper solves for the values of c at which the bound (4.1) is attained asymptotically (c² = 1/γ and c² = γ) and derives the intervals (5.8)–(5.12) in which oscillation occurs (pp. 14–15). Monotonicity arguments in c² follow (p. 18).
  - (f) In 2-D, the recurrence p⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = −γ²/p⁽ᵏ⁾ (credited via [3]) turns the asymptotic limits into identities, (5.22)–(5.25) (pp. 21–22).
  - (g) The final-accuracy argument: with linear rate a and a stop when the relative decrease falls below u, the error in f is about u/(1 − a). With a = ((γ − 1)/(γ + 1))² this is about γu/4 (pp. 24–26).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Table 1 compares L-BFGS-B (m = 5) and inexact Newton on PENALTY3, both run until no further progress (p. 2). The figures show a 2-D contour picture (p. 5), and a 3-D example f = (x1² + 4x2² + 16x3²)/2 from x0 = (3.1, 1, 0.39), with γ = 16, δ = 0.6 and c = 1.2 (pp. 19–20). They also show the 2-D regions for γ = 9 (pp. 22–24) and the rate as a function of γ (p. 25). The §6 prediction is tested once on a non-quadratic: a 100-variable quartic minimised by steepest descent with the Moré–Thuente Wolfe line search, run until no further decrease. The result is f − f* = 9.3D−11 and ‖g‖² = 4.4D−13, in "good agreement" with the predicted 10 or so correct digits (p. 26). There is no benchmark.
  - Transferable: predict the attainable final accuracy from the linear rate and the unit roundoff, then check the prediction on a non-quadratic problem with a practical line search (pp. 24–26).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds on Akaike (1959) and Forsythe (1968), and extends the interpretation of Akaike's constant (pp. 7–9). The material of §2 is credited to Gill–Murray–Wright: "Most of the results given in this section can be found in [10]" (p. 3). It confirms the "well-known fact" that the gradient norm is unreliable rather than overturning it (p. 2). It questions Barzilai–Borwein's suggestion that their 2-D rate analysis explains their advantage in n dimensions: the rate improves with γ in 2-D, but this "is not true in the n-dimensional case", so the 2-D case would have to be shown representative (p. 24).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The paper opens with the story of a mislabelled failure (p. 2). Theorems are followed by practical readings: "if large oscillations in the gradient are observed, the problem must be ill-conditioned" (p. 12); use two steps when monitoring ‖g‖; the amplitude of the oscillations cannot reveal the worst starting point (p. 14). Surprise is recorded as surprise (p. 18). There are twelve figures. The quasi-Newton case is openly left unresolved (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory that explains a stopping-test phenomenon for steepest descent on quadratics, together with two rules of thumb: monitor ‖g‖ over two steps, and read large oscillations as a sign of ill-conditioning (pp. 12, 14). The limits: the analysis covers steepest descent with exact line searches on quadratics only. The methods that motivated it are not analysed: for quasi-Newton methods, "a description of the behavior of their gradient norms remains an open question" (p. 5). §6 ignores rounding in the iterates (p. 25). No new stopping test is proposed, and the only future direction is the quasi-Newton question.

**Method links**:
- Method 4, evidence ✅: a failure seen while developing L-BFGS-B became a paper, and the failure label was revised after examination (p. 2).
- Method 5, evidence ✅: the theory explains behaviour seen in codes, and its accuracy prediction is checked against a run (p. 26). The critique of Barzilai–Borwein insists that an analysis must be about the case that matters, n dimensions rather than 2-D (p. 24). Variant ⚠: the method analysed is steepest descent with exact line searches, not the L-BFGS-B or quasi-Newton codes that raised the question (pp. 2, 5).
- Method 2, variant ⚠: surprise is recorded as such ("contrary to our initial expectations", p. 18), and a rival's explanatory reasoning is put on trial (p. 24). But no dismissed simple option is tested.
- Also Heuristic 2 (instrument the inside): final f and ‖g‖ are compared across codes (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidates: check a failure label against the quantity that matters before calling a run a failure (p. 2); check that a 2-D analysis is representative of the n-dimensional case before using it to explain numerical results (p. 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before reporting that a code fails to reach accuracy, compare final objective values across codes (p. 2).
- Bound the one-step growth of a monitored quantity by applying the Kantorovich inequality to the exact one-step identity (p. 12).
- In 2-D, find the invariant ratio that makes the asymptotics exact (p. 21), then state explicitly what does not carry over to n dimensions (p. 24).
- Translate each theorem into a monitoring rule a practitioner can use, such as two-step gradient ratios (p. 14).

### S035 · An algorithm for nonlinear optimization using linear programming and equality constrained subproblems (Mathematical Programming 2003, DOI 10.1007/s10107-003-0485-4)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–33 was read: abstract, §§1–12 and references. The version read is OTC Report 2002/4 of 30 September 2002, whose title page reads "An Active-Set Algorithm for Nonlinear Programming Using Linear Programming and Equality Constrained Subproblems" (p. 1). It has the same authors and subject as the listed journal paper. The bodies of Tables 1 and 5 (pp. 20, 28) are lost, and the total row of Table 2 (p. 20) is garbled. The performance-profile plots (pp. 22–27) are illegible, so their reading comes from the prose. The exponent of the common stopping tolerance (p. 19) is garbled.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Nicholas I. M. Gould (RAL) and Richard A. Waltz (Northwestern ECE) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: SLIQUE 1.0 (pp. 17–18), which draws on Waltz's 2002 thesis (ref. [22], p. 33). It is the active-set counterpart of the group's interior-point code KNITRO 2.1 (p. 18). The penalty update of §9, driven by an LP, is a 2002 precursor of the 2007/2008 penalty-steering rules (SKILL Method 3 worked case). The companion convergence theory is Byrd, Gould, Nocedal & Waltz, OTC 2002/5 (ref. [3], p. 32), not read.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Active-set SQP and interior-point methods are "likely to become very expensive as the problems they are asked to solve become larger and larger" (p. 2), and solving inequality-constrained QPs at every iteration "can be prohibitively expensive" (p. 2). The authors revive Fletcher–Sainz de la Maza's SLP-EQP (1989), which "has received little attention beyond this" (p. 2), because large-scale LP solvers (over a million variables) and CG for the EQP are available off the shelf (p. 4). Two questions frame the study: how well the LP predicts the optimal active set, and what an iteration costs compared with the interior-point and active-set competitors (p. 4).
  - Transferable: revive a dormant method when the enabling software has changed (here, million-variable LP codes), and frame the study by the two questions that decide whether it is viable.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The step has seven parts:
  1. An LP gives the working set W: the linearized ℓ1 penalty function minimized in an ℓ∞ trust region Δ_LP, written as a smooth LP with slack variables (3.6) (pp. 5–6).
  2. A Cauchy step along the LP direction on a quadratic model of the merit function, (4.9)–(4.11), which "plays a crucial role" in global convergence (pp. 3, 8).
  3. An EQP on W inside the master trust region Δ, (5.12)/(5.14), solved inexactly by projected CG/Lanczos (GLTR) (pp. 8–11).
  4. A dogleg-like search on the segment from the Cauchy point to the EQP point (pp. 11–12).
  5. An ℓ1-merit ratio test with a small threshold η (its exponent is garbled in the OCR, p. 12), a second-order correction (SOC) after a rejected step, and the radius rule (7.22) (pp. 12–13).
  6. Least-squares multipliers (p. 14).
  7. A penalty update driven by the LP (Algorithm 9.1, pp. 14–16).

  Against Fletcher–Sainz de la Maza the changes are in step generation, penalty management and the LP trust-region update (p. 2). Against Chin–Fletcher, the paper uses an ℓ1 merit function instead of a filter, and a dogleg path instead of choosing between the EQP step and the Cauchy step (pp. 2, 30). The EQP Hessian adds signed curvature of violated constraints that W ignores, because with zero multipliers the Lagrangian "would ignore curvature information concerning violated constraints" (pp. 10–11). The LP radius may shrink after an accepted step, so that the LP sees only locally active constraints, (3.7)–(3.8) (p. 7). The penalty rule has four cases (pp. 15–16):
  - Case 1: keep ν if the LP step is linearly feasible.
  - Otherwise, re-solve the LP with ν∞, in practice by setting ∇f = 0, to get the smallest attainable infeasibility.
  - Case 2: if that LP reaches feasibility, raise ν by factors of ten until feasibility is reached.
  - Case 3: keep ν when no significant progress in feasibility is possible.
  - Case 4: otherwise, raise ν until a fraction ε = 0.1 of the attainable reduction is obtained (9.24).

  A safeguard resets ν when it grows too large: after five consecutive successful feasible iterations with ν > 1000(‖λ‖∞ + 1), set ν = ‖λ‖∞ + 10. This can happen at most twice (p. 16).
  - Transferable: set a penalty parameter against the best attainable linearized feasibility, computed by re-solving the step LP with the objective gradient switched off.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f, h and g are twice continuously differentiable (p. 3). Simple bounds are handled explicitly, and all iterates satisfy them (p. 6). The LP software is relied on to return a linearly independent working set (p. 6), and degeneracy is not handled (p. 31). The ℓ1 norm was chosen "because it is less sensitive to outliers", and the ℓ∞ trust region because it reduces to simple bounds (p. 6). The constants of the LP-radius rule lie in the range given by the companion theory [3] (p. 7). Infeasible or unbounded problems are not evaluated: every run that does not find an optimum counts as a failure (p. 20).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No proofs in this report. Global convergence is in [3], which builds on Yuan (1985) (pp. 7, 30); not read. Devices tied to the analysis: the Cauchy decrease condition (4.10) on the merit-function model, checked with model evaluations only (p. 8), and the dogleg-type combination, which keeps at least the Cauchy decrease (pp. 11–12). Cost devices: one factorization of the augmented system (MA27) serves the projections, the SOC step (7.21) and the least-squares multipliers (pp. 11, 13–14). The SOC is taken from the trial point to avoid an extra evaluation of the problem functions (p. 13).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **Test set**: CUTEr as of 15 May 2002, filtered to problems where active-set identification matters. Unconstrained, equality-only, LP and feasibility problems are excluded, and eight problems were removed for memory reasons. That leaves 560 problems, split by class (QP/BC/GC) and by size n + m (VS/S/M/L) (pp. 19–20).
  - **Rivals**: KNITRO 2.1 (interior point, second derivatives) and SNOPT 6.1-1(5) (line-search SQP with a quasi-Newton reduced Hessian, first derivatives only). SNOPT is kept as the benchmark despite the derivative disparity, because it "is generally regarded as one of the most effective active-set SQP codes" (pp. 18–19).
  - **Protocol**: all codes run at default settings. SNOPT's superbasics limit was raised to 2000, and further where needed. The limits were 1 hour of CPU and 3000 major iterations, and reaching either counts as a failure. All solvers used the same tolerance, and the stopping tests of SLIQUE and KNITRO were built to be very similar to SNOPT's (p. 19). SNOPT's 7 exits with "optimal, but the requested accuracy could not be achieved" are not counted as successes, for consistency with how SLIQUE and KNITRO report (pp. 20–21).
  - **Robustness** (Table 2, p. 20; solved by SLIQUE/KNITRO/SNOPT): QP 89/113/99 of 117; BC 96/98/71 of 107; GC 253/295/285 of 336. By my sum, 438, 506 and 455 of 560. On the 100 large problems (Table 3, p. 21): SLIQUE 47%, KNITRO 85%, SNOPT 52%. Of SLIQUE's large-problem failures, 10 are memory failures on QPs, and 42 of the other 43 hit the CPU limit (p. 21).
  - **Efficiency**: Dolan–Moré log2 performance profiles of evaluations (the larger of the function and constraint counts) and of CPU time, on problems with n + m > 1000 only, timed on a dedicated machine. There are 43 BC, 63 QP (equality-only QPs excluded) and 112 GC problems (pp. 20–26). SLIQUE does well on BC (p. 23). On QPs it is close in evaluations but slower in CPU time, which is "a bit surprising"; the authors explain it by the many simplex iterations and poor warm starts (pp. 23, 26). On GC it lags, especially in CPU time (pp. 26, 28).
  - **Internal timing** (Table 4, p. 28): solved problems with at least 1 s of CPU, each problem weighted equally. The LP takes 75.9% of the time on QP, 33.6% on BC and 61.1% on GC; the EQP takes 8.6%, 37.0% and 13.8%.
  - **Penalty statistics**: Algorithm 9.1 adds less than 4% extra simplex iterations on average (p. 16). 86% of iterations used the final ν, and ν was increased less than once per problem on average (p. 29).
  - Transferable: when your code sits in the comparison, define success identically for all codes, and say how many rival exits this reclassifies (pp. 20–21).
- **D6 Positioning**: The lineage is Fletcher–Sainz de la Maza [9] and Chin–Fletcher [4] (pp. 2, 30). The differences are stated item by item. On filter versus penalty, the paper concedes that "testing would be needed to establish which approach is preferable" (p. 30). SNOPT is praised as a benchmark (p. 19). Specialised bound-constrained methods are acknowledged but not run (p. 23). KNITRO, the authors' own interior-point code, is the strongest rival in the tables (pp. 20–21, 29).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The final remarks number the novel features (i)–(iv) (p. 30), and the verdict is hedged: the results "indicate, in our opinion, that the algorithm holds much promise" (p. 30). Losses are stated in plain words, including the loss to the authors' own code (p. 21). The summary bullets mix strengths and weaknesses (pp. 29–30). The prototype status is declared: the software is "not a finished product but represents the first stage in algorithmic development" (p. 31). Open choices are flagged where they arise: the LP radius "remains an open subject of investigation" (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an algorithm and prototype software with a numerical study; the theory is in the companion paper (pp. 17–18, 30). The limits are concrete. The code is inefficient on large problems because of LP time, and warm starts fail because the LP active set includes trust-region bounds that change from one iteration to the next (p. 29). On some problems the active set is not identified near the solution (p. 29). It is unknown whether an interior-point LP solver would be enough (p. 30). The EQP has no preconditioning (p. 11). Five future items are listed: faster LP solves with better initial active sets, better LP trust-region updates, a better SOC or a non-monotone strategy, preconditioning of the EQP, and handling degeneracy (p. 31).

**Method links**:
- Method 3, evidence ✅: the merit-function engine is kept, and the penalty parameter is set from an explicit estimate, the best attainable linearized infeasibility from the ν∞ LP. The rule demands a fixed fraction ε = 0.1 of that reduction, keeps ν when no progress is possible, and has a reset path when ν grows too large (pp. 14–16). This pushes the penalty-steering lineage of SKILL's worked case (2007 preprint; OMS 2008) back to 2002. The factor-of-ten increases and the low warm-start cost already appear here (p. 16).
- Method 1, evidence ✅: stopping tests are built to match the rival's (p. 19), the alignment move of the 2003 protocol one year earlier. Codes run at defaults, hitting a limit counts as a failure, the rival gets extra superbasics (p. 19), success is defined consistently (pp. 20–21), and losses are printed (p. 21). Variant ⚠: KNITRO, the authors' own code, is a comparator, and SNOPT's first-derivative disparity is named but not turned into a question (p. 19).
- Method 4, evidence ✅: open choices and failure modes are written into the paper (the LP radius, p. 7; warm starts, p. 29; five improvement items, p. 31). That SLIQUE became the active-set algorithm in KNITRO is inferred from SKILL (Knitro-Active); this report does not say so.
- Method 6, evidence ✅: the owners of an interior-point code build and benchmark an active-set alternative, and record why each class fails at scale. SNOPT suffers from large reduced spaces, SLIQUE from LP time, and KNITRO wins on large problems (pp. 2, 29–30).
- Method 5, evidence ✅ (weak): the LP-radius rule as implemented is the one the companion theory covers, with admissible constants (p. 7). The theory itself was not read.
- Also Heuristic 2 (instrument the inside): the per-task timing breakdown (p. 28).
- New-pattern candidate: proof-driven safeguard. Here the Cauchy point is the global-convergence device inside a practical step (pp. 3, 8); see also S040 pp. 5–6.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Estimate the best achievable linearized feasibility by re-solving the step LP with the objective gradient set to zero, and set the penalty so that the step captures a fixed fraction of it (pp. 15–16).
- Reuse one factorization for every auxiliary solve (projections, SOC, multipliers), and say so (pp. 11, 13–14).
- Report average per-task time shares with each problem weighted equally, so that a few long runs do not dominate (p. 28).
- Filter the test set to the problems where your mechanism matters, and state the filter (p. 19).
- Break failures down by size class and explain each kind (memory, CPU limit) (p. 21).
