### S110 · Steplength selection in interior-point methods for quadratic programming (Applied Mathematics Letters 20(5), 516–523, 2007, doi:10.1016/j.aml.2006.05.020)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–9: abstract, §§1–5, Table 1, references). First page checked: title and "Frank Curtis† Jorge Nocedal‡" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Frank Curtis (Northwestern IEMS) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an early Curtis–Nocedal paper, "supported in part by a grant from the Intel Corporation" (p. 1). It is a single-component repair inside a primal-dual QP interior method and comes before the inexact-SQP line (S045).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For convex QP (Q positive semidefinite, A full row rank, p. 1), implementations use one common steplength for primal and dual steps. The literature recommends this ([1, 4, 11]) because it guarantees a reduction in infeasibility (p. 1). The paper's entry is the claim that this rule is "unnecessarily conservative in numerous cases" (p. 2). With Q ≠ 0 the primal and dual steplengths are coupled in the dual residual (2.8), so large unequal steps can increase dual infeasibility and cause divergence. In LP this coupling is absent and the largest steps can be taken (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Use three multipliers (αx, αy, αz), with αy unrestricted. They are chosen as the global minimizer of the merit φ = ‖Ax−b‖² + ‖Aᵀy+z−Qx−c‖² + xᵀz (2.9) over A1 ∪ A2: the equal-step segment plus one boundary segment (pp. 3, 5–6). Every feasible equal truncated step lies in this set, so the merit decrease is at least that of equal steps (p. 6). Remark 4.1: the subproblem reduces to two 2-D bound-constrained QPs (p. 6). The extra work is "a handful of inner products", because A∆x, Q∆x and Aᵀ∆y are already computed for iterative refinement (p. 7). Unchanged: the Newton system, centering and the fraction-to-the-boundary rule (pp. 2–4). Against Mészáros's rule [8] (efficient set for primal and dual feasibility only, αy = αz, p. 4), it names the risk of choosing (ᾱx, 0, 0) repeatedly, so that no step is taken in the dual space (p. 5).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Standard-form convex QP (p. 1). The efficient-set characterization assumes rp ≠ 0, rd ≠ 0 and Q∆x ≠ 0, "conditions which are normally achieved in practice" (p. 4). No convergence theory.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a. There are no convergence proofs, only Remark 4.1 and the set-inclusion argument that the merit decrease is at least that of equal steps (p. 6).
- **D5 Experiments**: Matlab, 56 Maros–Mészáros QPs (counted from Table 1) with AMPL models and fewer than 10,000 variables. Arms: equal steps, Mészáros and the new rule. Only iteration counts are compared, justified by the cost argument (p. 7). Stopping test: KKT residual < 10⁻⁶, with a 200-iteration limit. Table 1 prints relative differences with their signs, including losses (dual3 +22%, qpcboei2 +25%). Mészáros's rule fails on qsierra, and a log performance profile follows (pp. 7–8).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: One algorithmic component with computational results. The reduction is "fairly consistent" and the gains larger "on more difficult problems" (p. 8). The cheaper variant with αy = αz is "slightly less efficient" (p. 8). There is no theory, no timing, no limitations paragraph and no future work.
- **D6 (one line)**: Placed against the textbook equal-step rule (p. 1) and Mészáros [8] (p. 4). Unequal steps are noted as common in LP [7] and NLP codes [2, 3, 10, 12] (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the received rule is written down with its sources ("Scaling steps equally in this manner has been recommended in the literature", p. 1). It is then tested against the bolder option under a merit-function safeguard (pp. 5–8).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the interior method is kept and only the steplength selection is replaced (pp. 4–6). The safeguard is a merit decrease at least as large as the classical rule's, not an explicit estimate with recovery (p. 6).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (partial): losses against the default rule are printed problem by problem (p. 8). It is weak on cost, though: iteration counts stand in for time on an argued, unmeasured overhead (p. 7).
- Heuristic 5 evidence: the cheaper variant (αy = αz) is tested and reported as slightly less efficient (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: **contain the classical choice in the search set**. The new rule minimizes the merit over a set that includes every equal-step choice, so it can never do worse on the merit than the incumbent (p. 6). Compare S081, where the classical penalty update survives as π^u (p. 7).
- New-pattern candidate: **Degenerate-parameter ablation to attribute the gain**. The αy = αz run removes the free dual multiplier (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a safeguard is too conservative, enlarge the candidate set so that it still contains the conservative choice, and take the merit minimizer over the enlarged set. The new rule then cannot lose on the merit (p. 6).
- Before reporting iteration counts only, show that the new rule's extra work reuses products the solver already computes (A∆x, Q∆x, Aᵀ∆y from iterative refinement) (p. 7).
- Print per-problem relative differences against the default with their signs, so that losses stay visible next to the profile (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Empirical evidence has shown, however, that this approach is unnecessarily conservative in numerous cases." (p. 2)
- "We conjecture that such a choice can be beneficial in convex quadratic programming as well, as long as necessary globalization techniques are implemented" (p. 5)

### S045 · An inexact SQP method for equality constrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Optimization 19(1), 351–369, 2008, doi:10.1137/060674004)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–20, preprint: §§1–6 including all proofs of §§3–4, Tables 5.1–5.2, references). First page checked: title and "RICHARD H. BYRD∗, FRANK E. CURTIS †, AND JORGE NOCEDAL ‡" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Frank E. Curtis (Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the first paper of the matrix-free inexact SQP line (S045 → S068 nonconvex → S073 rank-deficient), motivated by PDE-constrained problems (p. 1). Its penalty update is taken from the Knitro interior algorithm paper [23] (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: For very large equality-constrained problems, for example with PDE constraints, exact SQP steps are "prohibitively expensive" and large iteration matrices cannot be factored (p. 1). Iterative solvers give inexact steps, and the question is "the accuracy with which the SQP subproblems must be solved in order to ensure global convergence" (pp. 1–2). The inexact Newton forcing rule for equations [8] suffices for convex problems with the exact Hessian. For nonconvex problems a step that decreases the first-order error "may move away from a minimizer" (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Keep a standard line-search SQP on the merit φ = f + π‖c‖ (2.7), and judge an inexact step by the reduction it gives in a local model of the merit (3.1), not by its residual (pp. 4–5). The Model Reduction Condition is (3.2) (p. 5). Two termination tests for the iterative solver, named "SMART tests" (p. 6):
  - Test I: model reduction for the current π plus a loose residual bound κ (pp. 5–6).
  - Test II: a sufficient reduction of linearized infeasibility (3.5a) and a dual residual bounded by β‖c‖ (3.5b), followed by the π update (3.6) (p. 6).

  Primal and dual residuals are handled separately, so one may grow if the other falls substantially (p. 2). The directional derivative is replaced by its computable upper bound Dφ̃ (3.9) (p. 8). The rule for raising π "has proved to work well in practice [23]" (p. 7).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumptions 4.1 (p. 8): the iterates lie in a convex set with f, c and their derivatives bounded; multipliers are bounded; the Jacobians have full row rank with singular values bounded away from zero; W_k is bounded and uniformly positive definite on the null space. (b) and (c) are called "strong", used "to simplify the analysis in order to focus on the issues related to inexactness" (pp. 8–9).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The proof uses a decomposition d = u + v into tangential and normal components that is "only for analytical purposes" (p. 9). Index sets K1 (tangential component dominant) and K2 define a quantity Θk (p. 10). Θk bounds both ‖d‖² (Lemma 4.5) and −Dφ̃ (Lemma 4.6) (p. 11). Bound (3.5b) keeps π bounded (Lemma 4.7, p. 12), and the steplengths are bounded away from zero (Lemma 4.8, p. 13). A telescoping sum gives ‖c‖ → 0 and ‖d‖ → 0 (Lemma 4.9, pp. 13–14), and a contraction argument on the dual residual gives Theorem 4.10 (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Experiments**: Matlab "isqp" with Kelley's GMRES and no preconditioner, run on 44 equality-constrained problems: CUTEr problems with AMPL models and fewer than 10,000 variables, plus two COPS problems. W_k is the exact Hessian, shifted by a multiple of the identity where needed to satisfy Assumption 4.1(e) (p. 17). The rival "ires" terminates on the full residual only and is run at ten values κ = 2⁻¹…2⁻¹⁰. It solves 45–86% of the problems, against 100% for isqp (Table 5.2, p. 17). Naïve failure tests are used throughout (p. 16). Stacking the SMART tests on the ires rule costs on average 0.5 extra GMRES iterations on the 35 problems both solve, and it solves all 9 problems that ires fails on (p. 18).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithm, theory and a robustness illustration. Efficiency is left to a separate PDE study [7] (p. 15). Limits are stated:
  - the scale dependence of β is "not ideal" (p. 7);
  - (κ, ε, β) "should be tuned for a specific application" (p. 7);
  - bounded multipliers are not guaranteed (a residual condition or a reset of λ is proposed, p. 18);
  - the rate may be slow, and a fast rate via (6.1) with κk → 0 is sketched (p. 18).

  Jacobian singularity and Hessians that are not positive definite on the null space are deferred: "in the benign context of Assumptions 4.1" (pp. 18–19).
- **D6 (one line)**: Set apart from inexact Newton for equations [8] and from Heinkenschloss–Vicente's conditions for decomposed trust-region steps (pp. 2, 5). The full-space line-search methods of Biros–Ghattas, Haber–Ascher and Prudencio–Byrd–Cai are credited, but "Unlike these papers" this one gives convergence conditions (p. 2).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the line-search SQP engine is kept; only the inner solver's stopping rule is replaced by a merit-model test, while the Armijo search and the penalty-increase rule stay standard (pp. 4, 6–7).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper writes its limits down (singular Jacobians, nonconvex W_k, pp. 18–19), and they are the targets of S068 (nonconvex, which builds on this paper, S068 p. 2) and S073 (rank-deficient, S073 p. 3).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: the rival rule gets a sweep of ten accuracy values while isqp runs one setting (Table 5.2, p. 17). Heuristic 6 evidence: "naïve failure tests ... to aggressively challenge the robustness of our approach" appear in the paper itself (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: **Computable proxy for an unavailable quantity**. The upper bound Dφ̃ replaces the directional derivative in the Armijo test (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate: **Benign setting first, as declared staging**. Strong assumptions are named as simplifications, and the hard cases are deferred (pp. 8–9, 19).
- New-pattern candidate: **experiments kept inside the theorem's assumptions**. The Hessian is shifted to satisfy Assumption 4.1(e) during the tests (p. 17); S068 and S081 do the same, and S073 then builds test sets outside them.
- New-pattern candidate: **Price devices in base-iteration units**. The robustness device is costed in GMRES iterations against the rival rule (p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Judge an inexact subproblem solution by the reduction it gives in the merit-function model, not by the size of its residual, and write the test so that it can be checked at every inner iteration (pp. 5–7).
- Run the field's natural rival rule over a sweep of its accuracy parameter and report the success rate for each value. Then price your robustness by stacking your test on the rival rule and counting the extra inner iterations (pp. 17–18).
- Prove convergence with a decomposition used only in the analysis (normal and tangential components never computed), split by which component dominates the step (pp. 9–11).

**Quotes**:
- "Assumptions 4.1(b) and (c) are strong; we use them to simplify the analysis in order to focus on the issues related to inexactness." (p. 8)
- "Indeed, the extra cost is minimal with respect to the added robustness." (p. 18)

### S076 · An algorithm for the fast solution of symmetric linear complementarity problems (Numerische Mathematik 111(2), 251–266, 2008, doi:10.1007/s00211-008-0183-5)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–17, preprint dated August 23, 2008: §§1–5, Tables 1–4, references; figure data not in the text layer). First page checked: title and "Jos´e Luis Morales∗ Jorge Nocedal† Mikhail Smelyanskiy‡" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales (ITAM), Mikhail Smelyanskiy (Intel Corporation) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an application paper driven by real-time physics simulation in computer games, with an Intel co-author; Nocedal's support includes "a grant from the Intel Corporation" (p. 1). The method moves to American option pricing in S092.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Some LCPs must be solved under strict time limits, and approximate solutions are acceptable (p. 1). In game physics at 30 frames per second the LCP gets "say 0.005 seconds", and accuracy may be low "provided a significant proportion of the optimal active set components are identified" (p. 2). The incumbents (p. 2):
  - pivoting methods, which do not exploit sparsity;
  - projected Gauss–Seidel (PGS), popular in games but slow;
  - interior-point methods, expensive per iteration and poor when stopped early;
  - gradient projection.

  Ferris et al. studied game LCPs but "did not, however, study projected Gauss-Seidel methods" (p. 3). PGS needs more than 50,000 iterations on ill-conditioned problems at tol = 10⁻² (Table 1, p. 6), yet it identifies most of the optimal active set within a few iterations (Table 2, pp. 6–7).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Alternate k_gs PGS sweeps, which estimate the active set, with a recursive subspace minimization (pp. 7–9). The subspace phase solves the reduced square system (3.3), projects v̂ onto v ≥ 0 (3.4), removes the components that became zero and repeats, at most k_sm times (pp. 7–9). A safeguard point z^b, obtained by backtracking from the first subspace solution, is used if φ(z^s) > φ(z^b). It guarantees φ(z^b) ≤ φ(z^0), so the subspace phase "does not undo the progress made in the PGS phase" (pp. 8–9). Kocvara–Zowe's method is the special case k_sm = 1 and always backtracks, whereas projection identifies the active set much better (p. 9). The method is nonmonotone: the safeguard was never needed in the tests, although φ sometimes rose after the first subspace step (p. 10).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Mixed LCP with Q symmetric, B with a positive diagonal and A with a nonzero diagonal (p. 2). It is equivalent to a bound-constrained QP when Q is positive definite (p. 3). Global convergence is claimed only in the positive definite case and "cannot be guaranteed" otherwise (pp. 8, 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a. There is no proof, only the remark that z^b cannot raise φ and the cited global convergence of PGS for symmetric positive definite Q [13] (pp. 5, 9).
- **D5 Experiments**: LCPs extracted from Open Dynamics Engine destruction scenes, n = 18 to 9,534 (Table 1, p. 6). The arms are compared in one Fortran 77 package that uses the same pardiso solver for the interior method and Algorithm 3.1 (p. 10). On the five largest problems the interior method needs 0.50–4.67 s against 0.22–0.87 s, with about twice the Cholesky fill (Table 3, p. 11). Ablation k_sm = 1: 387 iterations against 7 (p. 11). Error-versus-CPU plots follow (pp. 11–12). Against tron, the CPU time is adjusted to remove its repeated Hessian evaluations, and three parameter settings of Algorithm 3.1 perform alike (Table 4, p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithm plus application study. The tests "suggest that it has far better performance on ill-conditioned problems than competing methods" (p. 14). A parallelism argument rests on a 64-core simulator: PGS uses less than 15% of the cores, while their pardiso adaptation exceeds 65% (p. 14). Future work: studies on emerging chip multiprocessors (p. 14). Limits: no convergence for an indefinite Q (p. 10), parameters to be tuned per application (p. 13), and Kocvara–Zowe not run (p. 14).
- **D6 (one line)**: Kocvara–Zowe is credited as the base method. It is not run because "their numerical results report only moderate improvements" over an unpreconditioned gradient projection code (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the application's structure (time limit, approximate answers acceptable, active set as the useful output) shapes the design, which combines a cheap identification method with a Newton-like subspace phase instead of crowning one (pp. 2–3).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: implementation effects are controlled by one package and one sparse solver for both arms, "In order to make the numerical comparisons as accurate as possible" (p. 10), and tron's CPU time is corrected in its favour (p. 13).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the closest predecessor (Kocvara–Zowe) is not run; its own reported results are used instead (p. 14).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the practitioners' default (PGS) is tested and found poor (Table 1, p. 6). Its popularity is then explained before it is replaced (Table 2, pp. 6–7).
- Heuristic 9 evidence: the paper enters a field through a practitioner's problem with an industry co-author (pp. 1–2). Heuristic 5 evidence: Gauss–Seidel is preferred to SOR because it needs no parameter (p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: **explain the incumbent's popularity, then keep its strength**. It measures what the popular method does well (active-set identification) and builds the new method around that strength (pp. 6–7; S092 p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: **Degenerate-parameter ablation to attribute the gain**. The predecessor's restriction k_sm = 1 is run inside the new code (p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before replacing a practitioners' method that looks poor on your metric, measure what it is good at (here, the fraction of the optimal active set identified after k iterations) and design around that strength (pp. 6–7).
- Compare against interior-point methods inside one package with the same sparse direct solver, and report factor fill and factorization counts next to CPU time (pp. 10–11).
- When a rival code repeats work that is constant for your problem class (the Hessian of a QP), report an adjusted time that removes it (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "Given this poor performance, it may seem surprising that the projected Gauss-Seidel method is popular in computer game software." (p. 6)
- "The refinements introduced in our algorithm are crucial; without them the method can be very inefficient on ill-conditioned problems (as we report in §4)." (p. 9)

### S081 · Flexible penalty functions for nonlinear constrained optimization (IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis 28(4), 749–769, 2008, doi:10.1093/imanum/drn003)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–19 of the author manuscript: §§1–5, Algorithm 3.1, Assumptions 3.1, all lemma statements, the proofs of Lemmas 3.2 and 3.4 and of Theorem 3.2, and Table 1). The proofs of Lemmas 3.3, 3.5, 3.6 and 3.7 (pp. 11–14) were skimmed, and the profile data are not in the text layer. First page checked: title, "FRANK E. CURTIS" and "JORGE NOCEDAL" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Frank E. Curtis (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: one of two repairs of the same component, the penalty parameter. It sits between steering for trust-region SLQP (S042) and line-search steering (S051), and is tested inside KNITRO-Direct (p. 15). Its analysis follows S045 ("Byrd et al. (2007)", p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Both main globalization tools "block" productive steps (pp. 2–4):
  - A penalty function with a monotone π can be pushed to an excessively large value far from the solution, which forces the iterates to "hug the feasible region" (pp. 3–4).
  - A filter can block feasibility-improving steps through old entries; in the extreme case one Pareto-optimal entry blocks every path to the feasible region (pp. 3–4).

  The authors' view: "the main weakness of penalty-based strategies is the blocking effect" (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Accept a step if it gives sufficient decrease for at least one π ∈ [π^l, π^u] (2.1) (p. 5). With π^l = π^u this is a fixed penalty; with π^l ≈ 0 and π^u very large it is a one-element filter (p. 5). The two ends are updated differently:
  - π^u follows the model-based rule χk (3.8)–(3.9), "in a manner currently used for setting π in some contemporary penalty function approaches" (p. 7).
  - π^l rises only after a step into region II, from the actual reductions ν (3.11), with a damping factor 0.1 (pp. 8–9).

  The Armijo test uses π^m = max{π^l, χk} (3.14) (p. 9), and the line search needs to check only the two ends of the interval (p. 10). Unlike filter envelopes (Chin–Fletcher, Li), no history of past pairs is kept (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Equality constraints, with inequalities only in the closing sketch (pp. 1, 17). Assumptions 3.1 (p. 10): f, c and derivatives bounded on a convex set; full-row-rank Jacobians with singular values bounded away from zero; W_k bounded and positive definite on the null space. (b) is "strong", used "to simplify the analysis" (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: The analysis reuses S045's devices: the normal/tangential decomposition, the sets K1/K2 and the quantity Θk (pp. 10–12). π^u is bounded and eventually constant (Lemma 3.6, pp. 12–13), and so is π^l (Corollary 3.1, p. 13). The steplengths are bounded below (Lemma 3.7, p. 13). Theorem 3.2 then gives a KKT residual tending to zero (p. 14).
- **D5 Experiments**: Implemented in KNITRO-Direct (KNITRO 5.0), with its trust-region fallback disabled "to ensure that our implementation performs as a pure line search algorithm" (p. 15). Test set: 85 equality-constrained CUTEr/COPS problems. Arms: the default penalty rule against four flexible variants with π^u_{−1} = 1, 10, 100, 1000. Iterations and function evaluations are counted and shown in log performance profiles only, with no per-problem table and no failure analysis (pp. 15–16).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Globalization mechanism, global theory and KNITRO results, called "encouraging": slightly fewer iterations and considerable savings in function evaluations, attributed to fewer backtracks (p. 16). The initial π^u is "inconsequential" (p. 16). Extensions to barrier subproblems and to a trust-region ratio test are sketched without analysis, and "various algorithmic components must be added" (pp. 17–18).
- **D6 (one line)**: The filter (Fletcher–Leyffer 2002) and the "slanting envelope" (Chin–Fletcher 2003) are credited (pp. 2, 5). Nonmonotone penalty updates are dismissed as relying "on ad hoc heuristics" (p. 3). Discussions with Leyffer, Toint and Wächter are acknowledged (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the line-search SQP engine is kept, and one component (the single penalty value) becomes an interval. The interval's lower end is driven by a measured quantity, the actual reduction ratio ν (pp. 7–9).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant (component level): features of the two rival globalization schools are merged into one mechanism instead of one being chosen ("we hope to emulate some of the step acceptance behavior of both methods", p. 4).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: profiles only, with no per-problem results and no classification of failures (pp. 15–16).
- Heuristic 8 evidence: the idea is prototyped inside the production solver and compared with its default, with the solver's fallback switched off to isolate the component (p. 15).
- New-pattern candidate: **one-parameter family spanning two rival methods**. The two ends of the interval recover a fixed penalty and a one-element filter (p. 5); compare the s01 candidate *storage knob spanning two classical methods* (S138, S143).
- New-pattern candidate: **Collapse check to the classical case**. With π^l = π^u the mechanism is the classical penalty (p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: **Modular proof by reduction to the companion theory paper**. The analysis follows S045 (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Draw rival step-acceptance mechanisms in the (‖c‖, f) plane: the region each accepts and the productive steps each blocks. Then design the new acceptance region from the pictures (pp. 2–5).
- Drive one end of an adaptive parameter by the model, as before, and the other end by actual measured reductions, damped so that it rises gradually (pp. 7–9).
- When testing a globalization component inside a production solver, switch off the solver's fallbacks so that the runs measure the component alone (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "In summary, both penalty functions and filters can be shown to block different types of productive steps." (p. 4)
- "Not only does an algorithm with a flexible penalty function approach often require slightly fewer iterations to find a solution, but a considerable amount of savings is often experienced in terms of function evaluations." (p. 16)

### S068 · An inexact Newton method for nonconvex equality constrained optimization (Mathematical Programming 122(2), 273–299, 2010, doi:10.1007/s10107-008-0248-3)
**Read level**: full (pp. 1–23 of the "Noname manuscript": §§1–7, Assumption 1, Lemmas 1–6 (proof of Lemma 4 on pp. 13–14 skimmed), Theorem 5.1, Tables 6.1–6.4, references; profile data not in the text layer). First page checked: title and "Richard H. Byrd · Frank E. Curtis · Jorge Nocedal" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Richard H. Byrd, Frank E. Curtis (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the second step of the inexact-SQP line. It removes S045's assumption of a positive definite reduced Hessian, and "For strictly convex problems, the method reduces to" S045 (abstract).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Line-search methods convexify by modifying the Hessian when the reduced Hessian is not positive definite. This "is not entirely practical in our framework", because without factorizations there is no inertia information (p. 2). Methods that converge globally for nonconvex problems need a factorization of the Jacobian or of the primal-dual matrix (p. 3), and inexact full-space nonconvex methods "have not received much attention" (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Forgo the interpretation of the step as a QP solution and "jump straight to its effect on our globalization mechanism", the merit f + π‖c‖₂ (p. 4). The design has three parts:
  - The Model Reduction Condition uses max{½dᵀWd, θΥk} (3.12), motivated by the unconstrained rule (3.4) (pp. 6–7). Υk is a computable upper bound on ‖u‖² (6.7) and νk a computable lower bound on ‖v‖² (6.6), both obtained from ‖A_k d‖ and ‖A_k‖ (p. 17).
  - Termination Tests I and II are extended, and π is raised only when the step is convex enough or sufficiently normal (pp. 8–9).
  - The Hessian Modification Strategy (4.7) is triggered inside the iterative solve: W_k ← W_k + µI, with µ multiplied by 10 at each further modification (pp. 10, 17).

  Exact second derivatives are not required (abstract).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 1 (p. 11) repeats S045's assumptions but drops positive definiteness on the null space. In its place, (e) requires system (2.2) to be consistent for each W_k. Bounded multipliers and full-row-rank Jacobians stay.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Lemmas 1–6 follow S045, with the bound Υk replacing S045's K1/K2 split in the step and directional-derivative bounds (Lemmas 2–3, pp. 12–13). The penalty parameter is shown to be bounded (Lemma 4, pp. 13–14). Theorem 5.1 is obtained by "the same analysis as in Theorem 4.10 in [2]" (p. 16).

All 44 are solved, and Table 6.3 reports which test fired and how convex the modified matrices were (pp. 18–19). The comparison arms are ISQP-naive, which ignores nonconvexity and fails 8 problems, and ISQP-ideal, which uses inertia (pp. 19–21). The losses are printed per problem, for example eigena2 with 148 iterations for INS against 44 for ISQP-ideal, and eigenc2 with 130 against 39 (Table 6.4, p. 21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithm, theory and a robustness illustration. Effectiveness "is best studied in the context of practical applications", which is outside the scope (p. 18). Extensions: κ ≥ 1, a more flexible Hessian-modification strategy, and inequality constraints through barrier subproblems (pp. 21–22).
- **D6 (one line)**: Inexact reduced-space SQP methods (Heinkenschloss–Vicente, Ulbrich, Heinkenschloss–Ridzal) are credited with good results (p. 4). The authors are "unaware of another globally convergent line search algorithm that does not require inertia information" (p. 19).

Target: very large problems, such as discretized PDEs, whose constraints "may be ill-conditioned or even inconsistent" (p. 1).   - A tangential step and multiplier step from the modified primal-dual system (2.6), whose right-hand side is −A_k v_k (p. 5).

  The tests:
  - A tangential component condition (2.10)/(2.11) (pp. 5–6).
  - A model reduction condition written in u_k (2.12) (p. 6).
  - Three SMART termination tests (pp. 6–8). Test 2 accepts a step in the multipliers only, for points where only dual feasibility needs work (p. 7).

  The Hessian is modified when (2.10)/(2.11a) fail (p. 8), and the multiplier steplength comes from a one-dimensional least-squares problem (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Assumption 3.1 (p. 9): f, c and first derivatives bounded on a convex set, W_k bounded, and an iterative solver able to solve (2.6) to any accuracy. Full row rank is not assumed.
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: Theorem 3.3 (pp. 11–12) states the possible outcomes:
  - if all limit points of {A_k} have full row rank, π stays bounded and the KKT residual → 0;
  - otherwise ‖A_kᵀc_k‖ → 0, and dual feasibility follows if π is bounded.

  Devices: a one-dimensional lemma for the Cauchy decrease, whose proof is taken from [5] (Lemma 3.4, p. 12), and a "scaled and shifted" penalty φ̃ = (f − f_min)/π + ‖c‖ that decreases monotonically even if π grows (3.15, Lemma 3.11, p. 14). The pieces are assembled on p. 20.
- **D5 Experiments**: Matlab, with LSQR (Steihaug-type stops) for the normal step and MINRES for the primal-dual step, falling back to GMRES on (near) singular systems; no preconditioner (pp. 21–22). The 73 CUTEr equality problems with at least one degree of freedom are run in three versions (p. 23):
  - original;
  - perturbed, with the first constraint split into c1 = 0 and c1 − c1² = 0, which makes the Jacobian rank-deficient everywhere and the linearizations inconsistent;
  - infeasible, with right-hand side 1 in the added constraint.

  Functions are prescaled following [26] so that no "false infeasible" exit occurs (p. 21). The predecessor INS (S068) fails on many perturbed instances and reaches a termination test on only "a few problem instances" of the infeasible set (pp. 23–25). Iteration-limit failures are marked (Tables 4.2–4.3, pp. 24–25).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithm, theory and "preliminary numerical experiments" (p. 3). The drawback is stated: the trust region may keep the step from nearly satisfying the linearized constraints and so hinder fast local convergence. The suggested fix is to try the Newton system with S068's tests first and revert only when ill-conditioning shows (pp. 25–26). Also listed: a watchdog against the Maratos effect, preconditioners and MINRES-QLP (p. 26). The unsuccessful runs are conjectured fixable by "a more sophisticated implementation" (p. 24).
- **D6 (one line)**: Presented as extending the inexact Newton algorithms "developed by Byrd, Curtis, and Nocedal" ([3, 4], p. 3). Trust-region and penalty treatments of rank deficiency are cited as needing factorizations (p. 1).

**Method links**:

**Transferable techniques**:
