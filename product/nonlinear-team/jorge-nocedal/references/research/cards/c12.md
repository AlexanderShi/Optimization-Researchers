### S080 · On the numerical performance of finite-difference-based methods for derivative-free optimization (Optimization Methods and Software 2023, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2022.2121832; arXiv:2102.09762)
**Read level**: full, of the arXiv version.
- **Version read**: arXiv:2102.09762v1. It is dated February 22, 2021 on p. 1 and carries the arXiv stamp of 19 Feb 2021. Its title is the earlier one, *On the Numerical Performance of Derivative-Free Optimization Methods Based on Finite-Difference Approximations* (p. 1). The four authors (Shi, Xuan, Oztoprak, Nocedal) match the work list, so this is the preprint of the work named, not a wrong text. The OMS 2023 version was **not read**. Cite pages as [S080 arXiv pp. …].
- **Read in full**: the main body pp. 1–32 (§§1–5, acknowledgements), the figure captions on pp. 33–37, the references pp. 34–37, App. A (pp. 38–42) and App. B (pp. 42–44). Figures in these parts were read through their captions and the surrounding text.
- **Skimmed**: App. C (pp. 44–82, the complete result tables). I read its section prose and table captions, the outcome commentary (pp. 54, 67, 79) and one sample table (Table 4, p. 49). Individual table rows were not checked.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Hao-Jun Michael Shi and Melody Qiming Xuan (Northwestern IEMS), Figen Oztoprak (Artelys Corporation). Nocedal is the last author (p. 1). Funding: ONR N00014-14-1-0313 and NSF DMS-1620022 (p. 1).
**Place in trajectory**: This is the benchmarking paper of the Northwestern noise programme.
- It builds on Berahas, Byrd & Nocedal [7] and Nesterov & Spokoiny [41] (p. 4).
- It takes the relaxed Armijo condition from Shi, Xie, Byrd & Nocedal, arXiv:2010.04352 [53] (pp. 13, 37).
- It defers the design of the curvature estimator to a forthcoming paper, Shi, Xie, Xuan & Nocedal [54] (pp. 13, 16, 37).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**:
  - **The incumbent**: The paper accepts the field's verdict first. Moré & Wild's benchmark, confirmed by Rios & Sahinidis, makes Powell's model-based trust-region approach the leading DFO method (p. 4).
  - **The gap**: Finite differencing, perhaps the simplest alternative, is dismissed at the outset as too expensive or as impractical under noise. Most DFO papers therefore present no comparison with it (p. 4).
  - **The practice/research split**: Practitioners use finite differencing anyway, often without knowing it, because established codes fall back on it when no derivatives are supplied. No systematic effort has bridged the two (p. 4).
  - **Evidence of neglect**: The 2019 survey by Larson, Menickelly & Wild gives finite differencing two short paragraphs and focuses on complexity (p. 5). The adaptive interval of Gill et al. (1983) had no follow-up in DFO (pp. 4–5).
  - **The question**: Is finite-difference DFO competitive with established methods, with and without noise, on three problem classes (p. 5)?
  - **Secondary motivations**: parallelism; and extending existing constrained solvers, instead of the two decades it has taken to extend interpolation methods to constraints (p. 5).
  - Transferable: before attacking a field's leading method, name the option the field dismissed without measuring it. Show from the field's own surveys and benchmarks that the comparison was never run (pp. 4–5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: There is no new algorithm. The approach (FD-DFO) inserts finite-difference gradients of a smoothed approximation into established codes (abstract, p. 1). The pairings are L-BFGS against NEWUOA, lmder against DFO-LS, and the KNITRO SQP algorithm against COBYLA (p. 5).
  - **Changed: the difference interval only.**
    - Noiseless runs use h = max{1,|x_i|}·√ε_M for forward differences and a cube root for central differences, with no curvature term (pp. 8–9). The aim is to identify the simplest formula for h that makes a method competitive (p. 20).
    - Noisy unconstrained runs use the Moré–Wild interval h_i = 8^{1/4}(σ_f/L_i)^{1/2}. It comes from minimizing the mean-squared-error bound of Theorem 2.1 (pp. 11–12).
    - Procedure I sets L_i = max{0.1, |Δ(t_i)|/t_i²} from the MW heuristic at the first iteration, with the fallback L_i = 0.1. It re-estimates L whenever the line search returns α_k < 0.5, which is read as a sign that the curvature changed (p. 12).
    - Least squares uses a separate h_ij for each residual and coordinate, estimated once at the start, with the fallback L_ij = 1 (p. 22).
    - Noisy constrained runs use h_i = max{1,|x_i|}·√σ_f with no Lipschitz constant (p. 31).
  - **Changed: one line-search test.** The Armijo condition (2.19) adds 2σ_f after the first backtrack. When the directional-derivative estimate is not reliably negative, it requires only f(x_k+αp_k) ≤ f(x_k). Otherwise "no other changes" are made to L-BFGS (p. 13).
  - **Unchanged**: the solver engines. These are L-BFGS with memory t = 10 and a bisection Armijo–Wolfe line search (p. 8), lmder at scipy defaults (pp. 19–20), and KNITRO alg=4 (p. 27).
  - **Why this change**: algorithms then need not be built from scratch (p. 26), and constrained DFO comes almost for free (p. 5).
  - Transferable: to carry an established solver into a new regime, change only the derivative oracle and the one acceptance test that the regime breaks. State in one sentence that nothing else was changed (p. 13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**:
  - **Smoothness**: φ is smooth (p. 8); in the constrained case φ and ψ are twice continuously differentiable (p. 26).
  - **Noise model**: additive i.i.d. noise σ_f·U(−√3, √3), independent of x, at four levels σ_f ∈ {10⁻¹, 10⁻³, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁷} (p. 11). In least squares the noise is put on each residual, so f carries both multiplicative and additive noise (pp. 18, 22). The objective and the constraints share one noise model "for simplicity" (p. 26).
  - **Noise level**: σ_f is assumed known, or estimable by difference tables or sampling (abstract p. 1; p. 5).
  - **Accounting assumption (least squares)**: residuals can be evaluated individually, and m residual evaluations count as one function evaluation even at different points (p. 20). The authors later concede that per-residual Lipschitz estimation is affordable only under this assumption (p. 26).
  - **Idealized pieces**: two pieces use true-function information and are labelled idealized. The central-difference bound M comes from true Hessian changes, and its cost is not counted (p. 13). The Lipschitz arm for lmder uses the true γ_i (p. 23).
  - **Admitted for convenience**: a single noise model, which the authors call perhaps the most important limitation (p. 7). Smooth problems only, and local methods only (p. 7).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No theorem is proved. Theorem 2.1 is quoted from Moré & Wild [38] (p. 11).
  - (a) **Interval from an error bound**: the MSE bounds (2.9) and (2.11) are minimized over h to give the intervals (2.13) and (2.14). The resulting gradient noise levels, of order (Lσ_f)^{1/2} for forward and M^{1/3}σ_f^{2/3} for central differences (p. 12), are used to explain why central differencing reaches higher accuracy (pp. 13–14).
  - (b) **Cost counts backed by timing tables**: per-iteration linear algebra is O(n²) for NEWUOA against 4tn for L-BFGS (p. 10). It is O(mn²) for DFO-LS against O(mn) for finite-difference lmder (p. 22). Each count is backed by a CPU-time table across dimensions (Tables 1–2, pp. 10, 22).
  - (c) **A failure computed by hand**: DENSCHNE at x₃ = −8 with σ_f = 0.1 (p. 17).
    - The pointwise curvature is L ≈ 6.705×10⁻⁵, which gives h ≈ 20.539.
    - The derivative estimate is then ≈ 3.791×10⁹, against the true value ≈ −6.707×10⁻⁴.
    - Diagnosis: L was taken at the flat end of an interval over which the curvature changes rapidly.
    - Remedies: estimate L along an interval, and treat a tiny step length as a warning signal (p. 17).
  - (d) **Arms with idealized estimates**: these separate how good the estimator is from whether the approach works. Nine Hessian-based L schemes are compared, fixed against re-evaluated at every differencing (pp. 38–40). Lipschitz values exact to the true function are given to lmder (pp. 23–24).
  - Transferable: for a parameter rule that rests on a local estimate, compute one failure by hand. Show the estimate, the parameter it implies and the resulting error against the true value, then say which assumption broke (p. 17).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **Test sets**:
    - Unconstrained: 73 CUTEst problems with n up to 300 (p. 8).
    - Least squares: the 53 Moré–Wild problems, n ≤ 12 (p. 20).
    - Constrained: 49 inequality-only CUTEst problems with n, m ≤ 100, plus 3 variable-size problems up to n, m = 500 (p. 28).
  - **Measures**: function evaluations, shown as log-ratio profiles (Morales [34]) sorted by problem (p. 9). Accuracy (best optimality gap) and efficiency (evaluations to reach a tolerance τ) are reported separately (pp. 13, 15). Under noise, targets are measured against the best value NEWUOA found, or the best found by either solver (2.21, p. 15; 3.7, p. 24; 4.9, p. 31).
  - **Budgets and stopping**: noiseless runs stop at gap ≤ 10⁻⁶·max{1,|φ*|} or 500n evaluations, with φ* from BFGS on exact gradients (p. 9). Constrained runs get 500·max(n, m) evaluations (p. 28).
  - **Noise**: four levels. The figures show σ_f = 10⁻⁵ as representative (pp. 13, 16). The number of noise realizations per instance is not stated; a search for seeds, averages or repeated runs found nothing.
  - **Fairness rules**:
    - Runs where NEWUOA's initial 2n+1 sample already meets the target are excluded, as are runs that converge to different minimizers and failures (p. 9).
    - Constrained problems are sorted into four outcome classes. Only the 32 that converged to the same feasible point are profiled (p. 28), and the other classes are explained problem by problem (pp. 54, 67).
    - Accuracy ratios are capped at 8 digits, because a ratio like 10⁻¹²/10⁻¹⁵ would mislead when feasibility errors differ (p. 29).
    - A noisy run counts as a failure if its infeasibility is at least √3·√σ_f (p. 31).
    - The cost of estimating Lipschitz constants is counted (pp. 12, 22); the idealized M is flagged as not counted (p. 13).
  - **The rival at non-default settings**: App. B runs NEWUOA with p = n+2 and p = 3n+1 interpolation points and with ρ_end = 10⁻¹² (pp. 42–44). Powell's 2n+1 is found to strike the right balance (p. 14), and changing ρ_end makes almost no difference (p. 44).
  - **The own side handicapped**: KNITRO's L-BFGS memory is set to 1 (p. 27). A rerun with memory 10 helps, but not dramatically (p. 29; Fig. 29, p. 79).
  - **Results**:
    - Noiseless, unconstrained: forward-difference L-BFGS beats NEWUOA on most problems in evaluations (p. 9). At n ≈ 100 NEWUOA takes 5–10 times longer in wall-clock time; on NONDIA at n = 500 it takes 242 s against 0.467 s (p. 10).
    - Noisy, unconstrained: NEWUOA is more accurate and more efficient than forward differences, and central differences are the most accurate of the three (pp. 13, 15).
    - Least squares: comparable without noise, with DFO-LS better early (p. 21). Under noise DFO-LS is more accurate, but the idealized Lipschitz arm reverses this (pp. 23–24). Efficiency splits by tolerance (pp. 24, 26).
    - Constrained: without noise KNITRO is more accurate. COBYLA is slightly more efficient at low accuracy and KNITRO significantly more at high accuracy (p. 29). KNITRO solves n = 500 within the 1-hour limit where COBYLA times out (Table 3, p. 30). Under noise the two are comparable (p. 31).
  - **Not measured**: the parallelism advantage is argued as a hypothesis (p. 10); no parallel run is reported.
  - Transferable: in a two-solver benchmark, profile only the runs where both reach the same feasible point. Explain every other outcome class problem by problem in an appendix, and cap accuracy ratios at a fixed number of digits (pp. 28–29, 54, 67).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Builds on**: Berahas et al. and Nesterov & Spokoiny (p. 4). The most direct influence is Moré & Wild's paper on noisy derivative estimation [38] (p. 5).
  - **Revives**: Gill et al.'s 1983 adaptive interval (pp. 4–6).
  - **Direct criticism**: the DFO literature's dismissal of finite differences (p. 4). The two-paragraph treatment in the survey (p. 5). Cartis & Roberts, who dismiss finite differences at the outset and give no comparison (p. 21).
  - **A claim with no reference**: the penalty or augmented-Lagrangian route to constrained DFO is dismissed with an appeal to "modern research in nonlinear programming" and no citation (p. 5).
  - **Rivals chosen from their own community's benchmarks**: NEWUOA on Moré & Wild's results (pp. 4, 8), DFO-LS on Roberts et al.'s (p. 19), COBYLA on [2] and [5] (p. 27).
  - **Credit to rivals**: NEWUOA and DFO-LS are credited with robustness under noise without knowing the noise level. The authors know of no study that comments on this (pp. 6–7, 15, 23, 26). DENSCHNE is noted to give NEWUOA no difficulty (p. 17).
  - **Out of scope**: global methods (Bayesian, surrogate, evolutionary), with a scalability reason (p. 7).
  - **Rival community consulted**: seven DFO researchers are thanked for correspondence that shaped the experimental design (p. 32).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - **Claim forms**: the contribution is claimed as the first systematic investigation, "to our knowledge" (p. 6). The core claim appears in the abstract as a call to re-examine the dismissal (p. 1). A strong counterfactual follows: with such comparisons the field would have taken a different path (p. 4).
  - **Surprise recorded as surprise**: at each counterintuitive result the text says so (pp. 6, 9, 21, 39).
  - **Limitations first**: a "Limitations of this Work" subsection (§1.3) comes before any results (p. 7).
  - **Hedging**: calibrated and specific. For example, no single set of experiments can settle the noiseless claim, so other researchers should check it (p. 11).
  - **Commentary after results**: each problem class has a commentary subsection after its results (§2.2.3 p. 16; §3.2.1 p. 26).
  - **Three slips in v1**:
    - The abstract names the third class as general nonlinear programs *with equality constraints* (p. 1). The body tests inequality-only problems and declines problems with both kinds of constraint (pp. 5, 26, 28).
    - Procedure I calls ‖L‖/√n a "second-order differencing interval" (p. 12). App. A.2 describes the same quantity as the root mean square of the component-wise Lipschitz estimates, used for the directional derivative (p. 41).
    - The abstract advertises ease of parallelization (p. 1), which is never measured (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - **Contribution**: empirical and software. The paper gives a benchmark across three problem classes and finite-difference modules for L-BFGS, lmder and KNITRO. There is no theory.
  - **Limits, stated concretely**:
    - One established code per class, run at default options; local methods only; no nonsmooth problems; one noise model (p. 7).
    - The least-squares problems are easier and small (pp. 20, 26).
    - The central-difference M is idealized and its cost not counted (p. 13).
    - Component-wise estimation sometimes underestimates L, and is too expensive if repeated at every iteration (p. 40).
    - No conflict-of-interest statement was found, although co-author Oztoprak is at Artelys and the platform is Artelys Knitro 12.2 (pp. 1, 27).
  - **Future directions**:
    - Robust, affordable curvature and Lipschitz estimation (pp. 6, 16, 24), deferred to [54].
    - A study of NEWUOA's behaviour under noise (p. 15).
    - Finite differencing in the nonsmooth setting (p. 7).
    - Globalization (line searches, trust regions) under noise (p. 32).
    - Other noise models (p. 7).
    - Cheaper Lipschitz schemes for least squares: one constant, n constants or m constants (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- **Method 1 (evidence ✅)**: almost every step is practised.
  - Rivals are taken from their own community's benchmarks (pp. 4, 19, 27), and the DFO community was consulted on the design (p. 32).
  - The own side is handicapped: L-BFGS memory 1 in KNITRO; KNITRO's interior-point algorithms and SNOPT are avoided because they could disadvantage COBYLA; GALAHAD nls is avoided because its tensor could favour lmder over DFO-LS (pp. 19, 27).
  - Termination is aligned with COBYLA (opttol = 1e-16, xtol = 1e-8, findiff_terminate = 0; pp. 27–28).
  - The rival is rerun at non-default settings in App. B (pp. 42–44).
  - Estimation costs are counted (pp. 12, 22).
  - Few codes are used, so they can be understood (p. 7).
  - CPU time is paired with evaluation counts (pp. 10, 22, 30).
  - It also bears out the limitation already recorded in SKILL.md: KNITRO is platform and comparator, there is an Artelys co-author, and there is no conflict-of-interest statement (pp. 1, 27).

- **Method 3 (evidence ✅)**:

  - Monitoring the step length is the recovery signal in the DENSCHNE analysis (p. 17).
  - Methods are built as adaptations of existing codes (p. 26).
  - Page correction for the SKILL.md stop line: "are not robust" is in §1.2 on p. 6, not in App. A. App. A (pp. 38–42) holds the experiments behind it.

- **Method 6 (evidence ✅)**: least-squares structure is treated as its own class with structure-exploiting codes on both sides (pp. 18–20). The authors argue that unconstrained DFO applied to a penalty reformulation will not give a general-purpose method (p. 5).
- **Method 5**: no link. The paper proves nothing, and the one theorem is cited (p. 11).
- **Heuristics**:
  - Heuristic 1 (failure demo): evidence. DENSCHNE is a hand-computed failure (pp. 16–17), although here it comes after the broad runs, as commentary.
  - Heuristic 3 (noise-free before noisy): evidence in every section (§§2.1/2.2, 3.1/3.2, 4.3/4.4). There is no third arm of the unmodified code on noisy data.
  - Heuristic 8 (prototype inside the production solver): evidence through KNITRO's own finite-difference option (p. 27).
- **New-pattern candidate: arm with an idealized estimate**. Run the method with an exact or idealized value of the troublesome constant. This bounds what a better estimator could gain and locates the remaining gap in the estimator, not in the approach (pp. 13, 23–24, 38–40).
- **New-pattern candidate: stratify outcomes before profiling**. Profile only like-for-like outcomes and explain every other outcome class problem by problem (pp. 9, 28, 54, 67).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Log-ratio profiles of log₂(evals_A/evals_B), or of the ratio of optimality gaps, sorted across problems. The shaded area shows overall success in a two-solver comparison (p. 9).
- Cap accuracy ratios with max{gap, 10⁻⁸} so that ratios of tiny gaps do not mislead (p. 29).
- Under noise, measure efficiency against the best value found by either solver, φ − φ̃* ≤ τ(φ(x₀) − φ̃*), rather than against an unattainable φ* (pp. 15, 24, 31).
- Tie the feasibility-failure threshold to the noise level, ‖max{ψ, 0}‖∞ ≥ √3·√σ_f (p. 31).
- Re-estimate curvature only when a cheap trigger fires (line-search step α_k < 0.5), and count the evaluations used to do it (p. 12).
- Choose the variant of your own solver closest in spirit to the rival, and say which stronger options you left out and why (pp. 19, 27).
- Pair evaluation-count profiles with a CPU-time table over n for one representative problem, to expose per-iteration linear-algebra cost (Tables 1–2, pp. 10, 22).
- Put a non-default study of the rival's own parameters in an appendix (App. B, pp. 42–44).
