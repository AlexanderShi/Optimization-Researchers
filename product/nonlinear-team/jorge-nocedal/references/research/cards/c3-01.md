### S016 · Remark on "Algorithm 778: L-BFGS-B: Fortran subroutines for large-scale bound constrained optimization" (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 2011, DOI 10.1145/2049662.2049669)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–4 was read: the abstract, §1 with §§1.1–1.3, Figures 1–2 and the six references. The text file is the authors' preprint dated March 9, 2011 (p. 1). Only the axes, labels and captions of Figures 1–2 (p. 3) survive in the text layer, so the bar heights cannot be read and no per-problem numbers are taken from them.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: José Luis Morales (Instituto Tecnológico Autónomo de México) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a revision of the 1997 code Algorithm 778 (ref. [6], Zhu, Byrd, Lu & Nocedal) and of the algorithm paper behind it (ref. [1], Byrd, Lu, Nocedal & Zhu 1995) (p. 4). It appeared 14 years after the code (dates on pp. 1, 4). Morales is the coauthor of the 2000 preconditioning paper (S030, card c2-01).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is two defects in a released code, not a new problem. (a) In the subspace minimization phase the step toward the subspace minimizer is truncated so that it satisfies the bounds. "On some problems" this gives "a very short subspace minimization step that provides negligible progress toward the solution" (p. 2). (b) The MINPACK-2 routine dpmeps "produced an incorrect estimate of machine accuracy on some compilers" (p. 1). On some Linux compilers it underestimated machine precision by two orders of magnitude (p. 2). The text does not say who reported either defect or how it was found.
  - Transferable: treat a released code's short, unproductive steps and its platform-dependent constants as research objects. A remark-length paper is a legitimate outlet for the fix.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: One step is changed. After the subspace minimizer x̂ is computed, it is projected orthogonally onto the box l ≤ x ≤ u to give x̄_{k+1}. The direction is d_k = x̄_{k+1} − x_k, and the new iterate comes from a backtracking line search along d_k ([1, eqn (2.5)]). If d_k "is not a direction of strong descent", the algorithm reverts to the old truncation (p. 2). The exploration is minimal by design: only the cut-back and projection points are considered, to keep the phase cheap. The authors report that a more sophisticated minimization along the boundary "does not yield significantly better performance" (p. 2), with no data shown. The second change replaces dpmeps with the Fortran 90 intrinsic EPSILON(x) (p. 2). The text describes no other changes to Algorithm 778.
  - Transferable: when a projected step may fail to descend, keep the old step as an automatic fallback rather than deleting it.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Bound-constrained problems with l ≤ x ≤ u (p. 2). "Strong descent" is not defined in the remark (p. 2). Machine precision enters two components: a safeguarding rule in the quasi-Newton updating formula and one of the stop tests ([6, eqn (3)]) (p. 2). There is no theory and no theoretical assumption.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a: there is no theorem or proof. The projection is justified by the literature: "it it known that it is productive to explore the boundary of the feasible region during the subspace minimization phase [5, 4]" (the doubled "it" is in the original), that is, Lin–Moré and GALAHAD (p. 2). A second support is an unreported experiment with a more elaborate boundary search (p. 2).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: 38 bound-constrained CUTEr problems written in AMPL, "selected to be large enough so that CPU times could be measured reliably", with n from 2,000 to 100,000 (p. 2). The key control is that "The old version of Algorithm 778 makes use of the correct machine precision" (p. 2), so the comparison isolates the subspace change. The measures are per-problem ρ_f = log2(FEVnew/FEVold) and ρ_t = log2(CPUnew/CPUold), plotted in Figures 1 and 2, with m = 20 for both versions (pp. 2–3). The reading rule given is "One can gauge the success of an algorithm by the area of the graphs on its side of the half-space" (p. 3). The verdict, "rather substantial" improvements (p. 3), rests on the figures alone. There is no table, no count of wins and losses, no separate experiment on the dpmeps effect, and no other code (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: before a version-to-version comparison, port the bug fix into the old version, then plot per-problem log2 ratios of function evaluations and of CPU time.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper refers mainly to the group's own work: the 1995 algorithm [1] and the 1997 code [6] (p. 4). It credits Lin & Moré [5] and GALAHAD [4] with the boundary-exploration idea and says the improvement is "not surprising" in their light (p. 2). The test environment is CUTEr [3] with AMPL [2] (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract states both changes and the outcome in three sentences (p. 1), and the whole remark runs four pages including references. The result is stated with little hedging: "It is clear that the improvements in performance are rather substantial on these problems" (p. 3). There are small slips: the keyword "infeasibility" (p. 1) does not match the content, there is "it it known" (p. 2), and "[6])" has a stray parenthesis (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is software maintenance of a widely used code: one algorithmic refinement and one portability correction (pp. 1–2). The authors write no limitations. Unwritten limits are one test set of 38 problems, no theory, results only in figures, and no measurement of what the dpmeps error cost (pp. 2–3). No future directions are given.

**Method links**:
- Method 3, evidence ✅ (design shape): the L-BFGS-B engine is kept and only the truncation of the subspace step is replaced. The old truncation stays as the fallback when the new direction is not one of strong descent (p. 2). Variant ⚠ for the machine-precision fix. An estimate that drives a safeguard and a stop test was wrong by two orders of magnitude and caused "severe deterioration" (p. 2). The repair gives the estimate a reliable source (EPSILON), not a recovery path. This is a recorded case of an estimate-driven safeguard failing silently, which is the risk behind Method 3's 🔴 checkpoint.
- Method 1, evidence ✅: the accounting is corrected in favour of the old version, which is run with the correct machine precision (p. 2). CPU time is reported only on problems large enough to time reliably, with evaluations reported alongside (p. 2), as in step 7. Variant ⚠: the text discusses no losses, only the overall area of the plots (p. 3).
- Also Heuristic 7 (code as a numbered, maintained artefact): this is full-text evidence for the case SKILL.md already cites, a published correction of the 1997 code (pp. 1–2). And Heuristic 5 (the cheapest adequate option wins): minimal exploration with two candidate points is kept because a more elaborate boundary search gained nothing significant (p. 2).
- New-pattern candidate: two-phase active-set identification followed by a second-order subspace step. The subspace minimization phase of L-BFGS-B (p. 2) is the earliest instance in this batch (see S043, S064).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Port the bug fix into the old version before comparing old with new, so that the plotted difference measures one change only (p. 2).
- Plot per-problem log2 ratios of function evaluations and of CPU time, with problems chosen large enough for reliable timing (pp. 2–3).
- Take a projected subspace step with backtracking, and fall back to the truncated step when descent is not strong (p. 2).
- Take machine precision from a language intrinsic (Fortran 90 EPSILON) rather than a computed estimate when safeguards and stop tests depend on it (p. 2).

**Quotes**:
- "Our technique performs a minimal exploration (it only considers the cut-back and projection points) so as to keep the cost of the subspace minimization phase to a minimum." (p. 2)
- "This caused severe deterioration of performance on some problems because two important components of the algorithm rely on machine precision." (p. 2)

### S043 · Newton-like methods for sparse inverse covariance estimation (Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 25, 2012; no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–9 was read: the abstract, §§1–6, Figure 1, Algorithm 1, Tables 1–2 and the references. Table 2 survives intact in the text layer (p. 8). Some formula symbols are garbled (for example "kvec(P)k1" for ‖vec(P)‖₁ and "`1" for ℓ1) but can be read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Peder A. Olsen and Steven J. Rennie (IBM T. J. Watson Research Center) and Figen Oztoprak (Sabanci University) (p. 1). The file lists Olsen first, while the work list gives Oztoprak first. · **Place in trajectory**: a machine-learning-period collaboration with IBM. Its orthant-based Newton method is generalized in S064 (Oztoprak again, with Byrd and Chin). The L-BFGS subspace minimizer reuses the compact representation from the L-BFGS-B paper ([24], eq. 5.11) (p. 6).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an applied statistics/ML problem, covariance selection. The ℓ1-penalized log-likelihood is recast as min F(P) = L(P) + λ‖vec(P)‖₁ with L(P) = −log det(P) + trace(SP) over P ≻ 0 (pp. 1–2). The second entry point is a rival's claim. QUIC [1], a second-order coordinate-relaxation method, is reported in [1] to be "significantly faster than the ALM, GLASSO, PSM, SINCO and IPM methods" (p. 2), and it solves the LASSO subproblem by coordinate descent. The paper questions that inner choice (p. 3).
  - Transferable: enter an applied problem by finding the code currently judged strongest, on its authors' own comparisons, and question one of its inner design choices.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The paper proposes two classes of methods (p. 2; side-by-side boxes in Figure 1, p. 4).
  - **Newton-LASSO (NL)** minimizes the piecewise quadratic model (8) over the free variables (10)–(11). The free set comes from the steepest-descent direction of F through Z_k (9) (pp. 2–3). A backtracking line search requires decrease relative to the model decrease, and a Cholesky factorization checks positive definiteness (13) (p. 3). The method "can be seen as a generalization of the sequential quadratic programming method" (p. 2). Its inner solver is FISTA instead of coordinate descent (§3, pp. 4–5).
  - **Orthant-Based Newton (OBN)** fixes the orthant face from Z_k and minimizes the smooth quadratic (14) on the free variables by CG with K = O(1) steps, which makes it an inexact Newton method. A projected backtracking line search (20)–(21) follows (pp. 3, 5–6).
  - **Structure**: the Hessian H = P⁻¹ ⊗ P⁻¹ (7) is never stored. CG products use (A ⊗ B)vec(C) = vec(BCAᵀ) at O(n³) per step (pp. 5–6).
  - **OBN-LBFGS** uses the compact representation (24), at a cost of 2t²|F| + 4t|F| with memory t "between 5 and 20" (p. 6).
  - **Starting point**: P0 = (diag(S) + λI)⁻¹, taken from the optimality conditions (16) (p. 3).

  What is kept is classical: line-search Newton, CG, FISTA and the compact L-BFGS form. What changes, compared with OWL [23], is that the method uses the inverse of the reduced Hessian instead of the reduced inverse Hessian, and drops OWL's alignment procedure "as it is not needed in our experiments" (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when the Hessian has Kronecker structure, compute Hessian-vector products as matrix triple products and run truncated CG instead of forming the n² × n² matrix.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: λ is fixed, the samples are i.i.d., µ is known and S is the empirical covariance (p. 1). The problem is convex with a unique solution P* (p. 2). The ISTA/FISTA step needs a constant c > 1/(eigmin P_k)² (pp. 4–5). There are no convergence assumptions. Convergence of exact Newton-LASSO is credited to Tseng–Yun [22], and convergence of the inexact NL and of the orthant-based methods is deferred (pp. 3–4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: n/a for proofs: there is no theorem. The analytical devices are:
  - deriving the active and free sets from the steepest-descent (minimum-norm subgradient) direction, which "guarantees descent in qk(P)" (9)–(10) (pp. 2–3);
  - the Kronecker identity, which gives O(n³) reduced Hessian-vector products (22) (p. 6);
  - the compact L-BFGS formula for the reduced minimizer (24), with its operation count (p. 6);
  - the closed-form diagonal solution for λ ≥ |S_ij|, used as the starting point (p. 3);
  - a cost comparison of the inner solvers: O(Kn³) for FISTA, O(Kn|F|) for coordinate descent and O(K_CG n³) for CG (p. 7);
  - an assertion, without argument, that the convergence proof given for OWL "is not correct" (p. 4).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**:
  - **Problems**: synthetic. A random sparse inverse covariance comes from d'Aspremont's generator, data are sampled to give S, there are 10n samples and λ ∈ [0.01, 0.5] (p. 6). Table 2 has six problems: n = 500, 1000 and 2000, two each, with card(Σ⁻¹) from 1% to 20.1%, and λ = 0.5, 0.1, 0.05, 0.01. card(P*) and cond(P*) are printed for every column (p. 8).
  - **Algorithms** (seven, Table 1, p. 7): NL-FISTA; NL-Coord, a MATLAB implementation of QUIC that follows the C version "faithfully"; OBN-CG-5; OBN-CG-D (K = 5, increased by 1 every 3 iterations); OBN-LBFGS; ALM, whose termination test was changed to the ℓ∞ norm with ABSTOL = 10⁻⁶ "to match the stopping criteria of the other algorithms"; and C:QUIC (pp. 6–7).
  - **Protocol**: runs stop after 10n iterations or when ‖∇̃F(P)‖∞ ≤ 10⁻⁶, with a 5000 s time limit (p. 7). Iterations and seconds are reported (p. 8).
  - **Results**: no algorithm is consistently best. NL-Coord is the most efficient below 1% sparsity, and the new methods "seem more robust and efficient" on less sparse problems. OBN-LBFGS is called "the best choice as a universal solver". C:QUIC is roughly five times faster than its MATLAB counterpart, and best in the two sparsest settings but not in the two densest (p. 7).
  - **Failures** are printed as ">5000". On n = 2000 with 1% density at λ = 0.01, only OBN-LBFGS finished, in 610 iterations and 2163.12 s (p. 8). One cell (OBN-CG-D, n = 2000, 18.7%, λ = 0.01) reports 5007.83 s, beyond the stated 5000 s limit, without comment (p. 8).
  - Transferable: when your methods exist only in MATLAB and the rival's in C, reimplement the rival faithfully in MATLAB and report both the reimplementation and the original.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper surveys first-order methods (COVSEL, GLASSO, SINCO, PSM, first-order optimal gradient ascent [15], ALM) and second-order ones (IPM and QUIC) (p. 2). It takes QUIC as the baseline on the strength of QUIC's own report (p. 2). OWL [23] is criticised directly: "for reasons that are difficult to justify", OWL uses the reduced inverse Hessian, and its convergence proof "is not correct" (p. 4). Credit goes to Tseng–Yun for convergence of exact NL (p. 3) and to Dahl et al. [25] for a similar quasi-Newton approach (p. 6). The group's own work cited is Nocedal–Wright [9] and Byrd–Lu–Nocedal–Zhu [24] (pp. 2, 6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The contribution sentence is "The main contribution of this paper is to propose two classes of second-order methods" (p. 2), and the claim is modest: the methods are "useful tools" (abstract; p. 2). Results are hedged ("seem more robust", "appears to be the best choice", p. 7). Expectations are stated without evidence: "We expect that optimized C implementations of the presented algorithms will also be significantly faster", and "we believe the observed trends will hold even for highly optimized versions of all tested algorithms" (p. 7). The scope is stated outright: the focus is "on exploring optimization methods and ideas rather than implementation efficiency" (p. 7). There are label slips: "OBN-Coord" for the MATLAB QUIC, which Table 1 calls NL-Coord (p. 7), "OBM methods" (p. 4) and "as apposed to" (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic and empirical: two classes of methods, an L-BFGS variant and inner solvers that exploit structure (pp. 2, 6). The stated limits are these. Convergence of inexact NL and of the orthant-based methods is left to future studies (pp. 3–4). Quasi-Newton updating inside Newton-LASSO is left out: "we do not discuss this approach here for the sake of brevity" (p. 6). The MATLAB-versus-C gap is acknowledged (p. 7). The dynamic CG rule is "crude" and could be improved (p. 7). Unstated limits are synthetic data only, six problems and no real dataset (pp. 6, 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 1, evidence ✅ on several steps. The baseline is the rival community's strongest code by its own report: QUIC is "significantly faster" than five other methods (p. 2). Implementation effects are controlled with a faithful MATLAB reimplementation plus the C original (p. 7). ALM's termination test is aligned with the others (Table 1 note, p. 7). Time-outs are printed as failures (p. 8). Losses are printed: NL-Coord is best below 1% sparsity, and C:QUIC is best in the two sparsest settings (p. 7). Variant ⚠: the authors' own side gets no handicap, and the belief that the trends will hold for optimized codes is an untested extrapolation (p. 7).
- Method 2, evidence ✅: the received choice, coordinate descent as "the most effective iteration" for the LASSO subproblem according to [1], is put on trial against FISTA and CG (p. 3). The tool that looks unpromising, quasi-Newton updating for "a complicated Hessian like (7)", proves "indeed effective" (p. 6) and is named the universal choice (p. 7).
- Method 6, evidence ✅: the Kronecker structure gives O(n³) CG products without storing the Hessian (pp. 5–6). The verdict that no algorithm is consistently superior, and that the best choice depends on problem characteristics, matches the "no single approach" stance (p. 7). Variant ⚠: here the structure does not make his own brand the wrong choice. L-BFGS is crowned the universal solver (p. 7).
- Method 3, variant ⚠: the classical engines are kept (line-search Newton, CG, the compact L-BFGS representation from [24]) and only the handling of the ℓ1 term is new (pp. 3, 6). But no failing component is diagnosed, and there is no explicit estimate or recovery path.
- Method 4, evidence ✅: open questions are written into the paper: convergence of inexact NL (p. 3), convergence of the orthant-based methods (p. 4), and improvement of the dynamic CG rule (p. 7).
- Method 5, variant ⚠: the paper has no theory of its own. Theory enters only as a critique of OWL: an incorrect convergence proof, and a design argument about the reduced inverse Hessian versus the inverse of the reduced Hessian (p. 4).
- Also Heuristic 9 (inferred): the problem arrives with IBM collaborators (affiliations, p. 1).
- New-pattern candidates: (a) two-phase active-set identification followed by a second-order subspace step (Figure 1, p. 4; pp. 2–3; also S016, S064); (b) porting the compact L-BFGS representation from L-BFGS-B into a new structured problem class (p. 6; also S064, p. 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Start from the closed-form solution that holds above the regularization threshold, P0 = (diag(S) + λI)⁻¹ (p. 3).
- Define the active and free sets from the minimum-norm subgradient (steepest descent of F), so that a small step along it decreases the model (pp. 2–3).
- Make the inner stopping test relative to outer optimality: stop FISTA when the subproblem's minimum-norm subgradient falls below 1/10 of F's at the previous step (p. 7).
- Use a cheap adaptive budget for truncated CG as a first rule: K = 5, plus 1 every 3 iterations (p. 7).
- Print problem-difficulty indicators (card(P*), cond(P*)) for every column, next to iterations and time (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "It is suggested in [1] that coordinate descent is the most effective iteration for solving the LASSO problem (11). We claim, however, that other techniques merit careful investigation." (p. 3)
- "no algorithm appears to be consistently superior to the others, and the best choice may depend on problem characteristics" (p. 7)

### S064 · A family of second-order methods for convex ℓ1-regularized optimization (Mathematical Programming 159 (1) 2016, DOI 10.1007/s10107-015-0965-3)
**Read level**: full. All of file pp. 1–27 was read: the abstract, §§1–7, Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.2 with their proofs, Algorithms 3.1, 4.1 and 4.2, Table 6.1, the acknowledgements, the Appendix (§8, the derivation of BAS for (3.8), pp. 21–24, read in full) and the references (pp. 25–27). The text file is the preprint dated June 25, 2012 (p. 1), not the journal version. Figures 6.1 (p. 18) and 6.2 (p. 20) survive only as captions, so their curves are not used. Results come from the prose and Table 6.1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd and Figen Oztoprak (University of Colorado, Computer Science), and Gillian M. Chin (Northwestern IEMS, supported by an NSERC fellowship and "a grant from Google Inc.") (p. 1). · **Place in trajectory**: a machine-learning-period paper. It generalizes the orthant-based Newton method of S043 (Oztoprak in both) into a semi-smooth Newton framework. It counts the group's own Hansen & Nocedal report [14] among the second-order ISTA methods and Byrd–Chin–Nocedal–Wu [7] among the orthant-based ones (pp. 4, 25). It thanks Michael Hintermüller and Daniel Robinson for conversations (p. 20). The preprint is from 2012; the journal year 2016 is from the work list.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is (1.1), min φ(x) = f(x) + µ‖x‖₁ with f smooth and convex and µ > 0 fixed. The motivation is ML with a stochastic loss, but the methods are deterministic (p. 1). The entry has two parts. One is to bring second-order information in "to accelerate convergence" where first-order methods dominate (pp. 1–2). The other is a known failure: BAS "can fail to converge when Q in (3.8) is not an M-matrix [4]", and in practice both BAS versions are commonly seen to cycle (p. 10).
  - Transferable: take a fast but fragile method from a neighbouring field (BAS, from M-matrix LCPs and PDE control) and make its failure the problem.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The paper makes two contributions (p. 2).
  - **(1) The semi-smooth Newton framework.** The optimality conditions are written as F(x) = 0 with Fᵢ(x) = max{min{τ(∇ᵢf + µ), xᵢ}, τ(∇ᵢf − µ)} (2.2). Index sets (2.6)–(2.8) feed iteration (2.9), where τ fixes the points of non-differentiability and δᵢ the choice of generalized Jacobian (pp. 3–4). Different choices reproduce known methods:
    - second-order ISTA (δ = 0, τ = the steplength α_k) (p. 4);
    - orthant-based methods (τ small as in (2.15), δ = 0), with the same orthant face and the same subspace step (pp. 4–5);
    - BAS for quadratic f (τ > |xᵢ|/(2µ), δ = 1) (p. 6, proved in §3.1);
    - a two-metric gradient projection method and quasi-Newton variants, mentioned without derivation (p. 6).
  - **(2) Corrected BAS (Algorithm 4.1).** It keeps BAS's aggressive multiple changes per iteration. After the trial step it finds the zero variables whose predicted sign was wrong (V_k^P, V_k^N, (4.1)), moves them to the active set and recomputes the step. This repeats until V_k = ∅ (pp. 10–11). The diagnosis behind it is that "the aggressive changes in index sets is the cause of cycling" (p. 10).
  - **Safeguard.** The noncycling safeguard (Algorithm 4.2, from Júdice–Pires [19]) changes one variable per iteration if the number of violating indices has not decreased for t_max iterations (p. 15). A line search as globalization is rejected because it "changes the fundamentally discrete character of the algorithm" (p. 15).
  - **OBM-COR.** The correction is transplanted into an orthant-based method, which differs only by a final projection onto the orthant face (5.2) (p. 16).
  - **Newton-CBAS** handles general convex f. It minimizes the model (6.1) by Corrected BAS with an Armijo backtracking line search (6.2), γ = 10⁻⁴ (p. 18).
  - Transferable: when a method fails through over-aggressive set changes, add a predict–check–correct loop that undoes only the mispredicted changes, instead of falling back to one change per iteration.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is smooth and convex (p. 1). The new algorithm is "designed for the case when f is quadratic" (p. 2), and Theorem 4.1 assumes Q symmetric positive definite (p. 12). BAS is known to be efficient for M-matrices (p. 7) and to fail for non-M-matrices [4] (p. 10). The equivalence argument assumes matching initialization (3.12) (p. 9), and the BAS derivation assumes complementary initial u and v (8.7) (p. 21).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**:
  - (a) Equivalence by induction. Assume the same iterate and the index-set relation (3.12) at k − 1, then show by case analysis over A and P (N by symmetry) that both methods give the same x^k and that (3.12) still holds (pp. 9–10).
  - (b) Theorem 4.1 (p. 12): the step is nonzero at any non-stationary iterate. The proof partitions the indices by their transitions into R, S, T and W (4.4) and uses a sign vector s^k (4.5). If S^k ≠ ∅ the iterate moves. If S^k = ∅, strict convexity of the reduced QP (4.9) gives a strict descent sum supported on T^k (4.11). Corrected indices contribute nonnegative terms, so a negative term survives in T^k \ V^k (pp. 12–13).
  - (c) Corollary 4.2: d^k is a descent direction for φ. The directional derivative (4.12) splits into contributions from R (zero), S (negative) and T (nonpositive) (pp. 13–14).
  - (d) The repeat loop is finite because the sets shrink at each round (p. 11).
  - (e) The Appendix uses the u − v split, the KKT system (8.3)–(8.6) and y + z = 2µe to get Iᵘ ∩ Iᵛ = ∅. Complementarity is therefore preserved and transitions between P and N are impossible in BAS (pp. 21–24).
  - (f) Newton-CBAS descent: Dφ(x; d) = Dm(0; d) ≤ m(d) − m(0) < 0 by convexity [21] (p. 18).

  There is no rate and no global convergence proof for Algorithm 4.1 itself. Global convergence of Algorithm 4.2 rests on the borrowed safeguard [19] (p. 15).
  - Transferable: to prove that a new active-set loop always moves, partition the indices by how they change between consecutive iterations and sign each part's contribution to the directional derivative.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: The framework is coded in MATLAB with τ and δ as input parameters. The tests target the corrective mechanism, and second-order ISTA is postponed (p. 17).
  - **Random QPs**: 1000 instances of (3.8). Q comes from sprandsym with density 25%, half with condition number about 10⁴ and half about 10⁷. The entries of q match the eigenvalues of Q in magnitude, half of them positive, and µ is random in [2.5, n/3]. The dimension n is not stated in the text (p. 17).
  - **Table 6.1**: BAS (Algorithm 3.1) fails 10/500 times on the moderate class and 120/500 on the high class, 130 in total. Corrected BAS fails 0 times, with 0.92283, 1.40629 and 1.1646 corrections per iteration (p. 17). The BAS failures were cycling, as in [4]. Algorithm 4.2 with t_max = 10 never invoked its backup (p. 17).
  - **CPU performance profiles** (Dolan–Moré) compare Algorithms 3.1 and 4.1 with OBM-COR. The correction works in both methods, and OBM-COR is "somewhat more efficient". The robustness costs extra computation (p. 17).
  - **General convex case**: Newton-CBAS against OWL-QN on the StatLog Landsat Satellite dataset (UCI). The model is multinomial logistic, with pairwise feature products taking 36 features to 1296; there are 6 classes, n = 7776, memory 5 and µ = 1/n. φ and ‖F‖₁ are plotted against iterations. Newton-CBAS "outperforms OWL-QN in both of measures", and function evaluations dominate the cost (p. 19). This is one dataset, compared in iterations only.
  - Transferable: count failures by conditioning class over 1000 random instances, and report the average number of corrections per iteration as the price of the repair.
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the semi-smooth Newton methodology [12, 25, 26, 22, 29] (p. 2). It credits Hintermüller–Ito–Kunisch [16] with deriving BAS as a semi-smooth Newton method and offers "a derivation based on a different semi-smooth function" (p. 9). The BAS lineage it cites is Aganagić [1] for M-matrix LCPs, Júdice–Pires [19], Kim–Park [20] and bound-constrained QPs [5, 16] (pp. 6–7), with Ben Gharbia–Gilbert [4] for nonconvergence (p. 10). Rival methods are recast as special cases rather than criticised: [14, 30] are second-order ISTA methods, and [2, 7, 18] (including OWL-QN and QUIC) are orthant-based (pp. 4–5). OWL-QN is the comparator (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: "The contributions of this paper are two-fold." (p. 2). An interpretation comes before the algorithm: P^k is read as the set of variables "predicted to be non-negative" (p. 10). Hedging appears in "possibly due to the use of the projection" (p. 17). The rarity claims ("extremely rare", "so rare", pp. 15–16, 19) rest on one count: the backup never fired on the 1000 problems (p. 17). There are typos: "[Qx^k + k]_i" (p. 16), "in this tests" (p. 17), "Landstat" and "both of measures" (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are theoretical and algorithmic: the framework, the BAS equivalence, Corrected BAS with Theorem 4.1 and Corollary 4.2, the noncycling variant, OBM-COR and Newton-CBAS (pp. 2–19). The limits are stated concretely. Algorithm 4.1 "is not globally convergent, and in fact may be subject to cycling behavior" (p. 15). The stability comes "at the expense of increased computational cost" (p. 17). The algorithm is designed for quadratic f (p. 2), and "we have explored only a few of the methods" (p. 19). Future work: selective corrections, inexact steps and factorization updates (pp. 17–18); a safeguard built on the descent property of Corollary 4.2 (pp. 15–16, 19); an inexact subspace phase; the correction inside second-order ISTA (p. 19). The stated aim of contrasting algorithms "both theoretically and numerically" (p. 2) is only partly met, because second-order ISTA is neither tested nor analysed (p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 3, evidence ✅ (strong): BAS is kept with its aggressive multi-change character, and the failing component is named: aggressive index-set changes cause cycling (p. 10). Only that component is repaired, by checking the active-manifold estimate (the sign predictions) and correcting it (pp. 10–11). A recovery path exists in the Algorithm 4.2 backup (p. 15). A line search is rejected because it would change the engine's discrete character (p. 15). The abstract calls the device "a novel mechanism for correcting estimates, when needed" (p. 1).
- Method 4, evidence ✅: an external counterexample, the Ben Gharbia–Gilbert nonconvergence result [4], is treated as a specification and answered with an algorithm variant (pp. 10, 17). The framework exposes τ and δ as input parameters (p. 17). The failure mode (cycling) and the next papers are listed (pp. 15–19).
- Method 5, evidence ✅: Corollary 4.2 separates Corrected BAS from BAS ("unlike the original BAS algorithm", p. 13), and the experiments show the same split, 130 failures against 0 (p. 17). Variant ⚠: there is no rate and no global convergence result for Algorithm 4.1 itself (p. 15).
- Method 1, evidence ✅: OWL-QN gets the same L-BFGS approximation "to make the comparison as fair as possible" (p. 19), and the cost of the repair is printed (p. 17). Variant ⚠: the general-convex comparison is one dataset, in iterations only, with no aggregate behind it (p. 19). That is the case Method 1's 🔴 stop rule names.
- Method 6, variant ⚠: one framework generates the rival classes so that they can be compared (pp. 2–6). The paper does not integrate them into one solver with crossover.
- Also Heuristic 6 (robustness before speed): zero failures are bought at extra cost, and the speed-ups are deferred (pp. 17–18).
- New-pattern candidates: (a) a generative framework in which rival methods are parameter choices of one iteration (pp. 2–6, 17); (b) two-phase active-set identification followed by a second-order subspace step (p. 2; also S016, S043); (c) porting the compact L-BFGS representation into a new problem with matched memory for the comparator (pp. 6, 19; also S043).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the optimality conditions as a semi-smooth equation with a free scale τ, then show which choices of τ and δ reproduce known methods. Rivals then become points in one design space (pp. 3–6).
- Add a predict–check–correct loop for active-set estimates: after the trial step, demote the zero variables whose predicted sign was wrong and recompute, until no prediction is violated (pp. 10–11).
- Borrow a cardinality-based noncycling safeguard and report how often it fires; here it never did (pp. 15, 17).
- Prove that the iterate moves and descends by partitioning the indices by transition (R, S, T, W) and signing each part's contribution (pp. 12–14).
- Make the comparison fair by giving the comparator the same quasi-Newton approximation and memory (p. 19).
- Derive a single-vector algorithm from a u − v split by proving that complementarity is preserved (pp. 21–24).

### Batch observations (cross-card, for synthesis)
- **Two-phase design across 1995–2016.** The subspace minimization phase of L-BFGS-B, whose refinement the Remark ties to "faster identification of the optimal active set" (S016, pp. 1–2), the free/active split of S043 taken from the steepest-descent direction (pp. 2–4), and the "active manifold identification phase and a second-order subspace phase" of S064 (p. 2) share one shape: identify the active set, then take a second-order step on the free variables. This makes three cards in this batch for the candidate, and it adds to the abstract-level instances noted in a2-02 (S178, S139, S135). It meets the ≥3-paper threshold of the card spec §三 only if the synthesis accepts S016 as an instance, since S016 changes only the subspace phase.
- **Compact L-BFGS travels.** The group's compact representation is reused for the reduced minimizer in S043 (p. 6, citing [24]) and as B_k in Newton-CBAS with memory 5 (S064, pp. 6, 19).
- **Method 3 outside noise.** S064 is a clean pre-noise instance of Method 3. The engine is kept, the failing component is named, the repair is driven by an estimate check, and a backup provides recovery (pp. 10–11, 15). It dates the pattern to 2012, alongside the 2007 penalty-steering worked case.
