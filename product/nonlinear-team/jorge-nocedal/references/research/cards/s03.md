### S030 · Automatic preconditioning by limited memory quasi-Newton updating (SIAM Journal on Optimization 2000, doi:10.1137/s1052623497327854)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–21: abstract, all sections, final remarks, appendix algorithm SAMPLE); references skimmed (pp. 21–23). Every number in the text and tables is lost in this layer, so table entries, n, the values of m and the tolerances were not readable. First page checked: title and "Jose Luis Morales Jorge Nocedal" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales (ITAM, Mexico) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: grew out of Hessian-free Newton tests (p. 3). It is the "companion paper" of PREQN (S098 p. 4) and is cited by the enriched methods (S077 p. 10). It positions itself against Nash's preconditioner, O'Leary–Yeremin, and Byrd–Nocedal–Zhu (S088) (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Sequences of SPD systems, Ax = b_i with a fixed matrix or A_k x = b_k with slowly varying matrices, where only matrix-vector products are available (pp. 1–2). The main target is the inner CG of Hessian-free Newton, where each product costs a gradient evaluation (pp. 2, 5–6). The entry was an observation from their Hessian-free Newton tests: Nash's preconditioner helped only on a few problems, while the new one helped over a wide range (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Solve the first system with unpreconditioned CG and save m pairs s_i = x_{i+1} − x_i, y_i = r_{i+1} − r_i from the run. The L-BFGS matrix they define (two-loop formula, initial scaling sᵀy/yᵀy) preconditions the next system (pp. 2, 4–5). For a quadratic the residual is the gradient, so CG pairs are legitimate BFGS pairs (p. 4). Kept: the L-BFGS representation, and Nash's Hessian-free Newton code with the Moré–Thuente line search (pp. 3–5). Changed: where the pairs come from (the inner CG run, not outer iterates) and which pairs are kept. The default is a uniform sample across the CG run, maintained online by algorithm SAMPLE with no extra storage (pp. 4, 20–21). In the CUTE runs the outer optimization pair is stored too (p. 8).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: SPD coefficient matrices (p. 2); f twice continuously differentiable (p. 5). Hessian-vector products come from forward differences of gradients, so each CG iteration costs one gradient (pp. 5–6). The method pays only if Av costs much more than applying the preconditioner (an mn-type flop count, digits lost). The paper states this assumption openly for its remaining tests (p. 12).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: no theorems. One structural explanation: the first row of the 1-D finite-element matrix is e₁ᵀ, so for random right-hand sides the solution is nearly aligned with e₁, the most favourable case for the preconditioner. Zeroing the first and last components of b removes the effect (pp. 16–17). For the choice of sampling strategy the authors say theoretical arguments are hard to give, and they rely on controlled tests (p. 17).
- **D5 Experiments**: the order of tests: (1) Nash's HFN code on five problems with distinct eigenvalue classes, (2) CUTE and MINPACK sets (CPU time only for the two MINPACK problems), (3) finite-element matrices with multiple and slowly varying right-hand sides, (4) refresh variants, (5) last-m versus uniform sampling, (6) a forced sample-size test. CG iterations are counted, not times, and the reason is given (pp. 6–19). Some tests were run by Richard Waltz, and some experiments were suggested by a referee (p. 20).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic: a matrix-free automatic preconditioner, most useful when products are expensive and low accuracy suffices (abstract, p. 1; pp. 18–20). Stated limits:
  - the cost of applying the preconditioner may dominate when gradients are cheap (p. 8);
  - very sparse finite-element matrices show no CPU reduction for any m (p. 12);
  - refreshing the preconditioner after every solve removed the upward trend in iterations but gave smaller gains than the authors expected, and when to refresh is left open (p. 15);
  - a hot start helps only sometimes (pp. 15–16);
  - pair selection by smallest Rayleigh quotient was tried and was not better (p. 20);
  - for uses outside optimization, "more research is required" (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: losses printed in the paper: no CPU gain on sparse finite-element matrices for any m (p. 12). The counting unit is justified: the aim is fewer gradient evaluations (p. 8). CPU time appears only where it means something (p. 10) (step 7).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: undecided choices are written down: refresh timing (p. 15), last-m versus uniform sampling without theory (p. 17), other selection rules (p. 20). Both sampling strategies then became the IOP option of PREQN (S098 pp. 4, 7).
- new-pattern candidate: **recycle inner-solver by-products as curvature memory**. The CG run on one system yields quasi-Newton pairs for the next (pp. 2, 4). The same move appears in S098 (p. 4), S077 (pp. 2–3) and S088 (batch s02).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When one strategy wins unexpectedly on a test matrix, find the structural reason (here the solution's alignment with e₁). Build a right-hand side that removes it and rerun both strategies (pp. 16–17).
- To separate the effect of a wider sample from the effect of more memory, fix m and force the first solve to run maxCG iterations. Preconditioners with the same memory then differ only in how wide a sample they draw from (p. 18).
- Before a broad test set, show on a controlled case where the device pays: Hessian frozen at one iterate, random right-hand sides, two tolerances. Here the gain was modest at the tight tolerance on one problem and substantial at the relaxed one (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "Numerical experiments indicate that the preconditioner has most to offer when these matrix-vector products are expensive to compute and when low accuracy in the solution is required." (abstract, p. 1)
- "Our conclusion is that the decision of when to refresh the preconditioner is not simple" (p. 15)

### S067 · Numerical experience with a reduced Hessian method for large scale constrained optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2000, doi:10.1023/a:1008723031056)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–21: abstract, Algorithm RHC, implementation details, analytic example, numerical results, conclusions, appendix); references skimmed (pp. 22–23). All numbers in the text are lost, including the parameter table (p. 9) and the result tables (pp. 16–18), and most formulas are fragmentary. First page checked: title and "Lorenz T Biegler Jorge Nocedal Claudia Schmid and David Ternet" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Lorenz T. Biegler, Claudia Schmid, David Ternet (Chemical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the computational companion of the Biegler–Nocedal–Schmid theory paper (S028, batch s02). It develops that algorithm "into a practical method for largescale optimization" (abstract, p. 2), starting from Algorithm II of that paper (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The theory paper proved superlinear and global convergence. This paper addresses "the important implementation issues" and how to balance robustness against economy of computation at large scale (pp. 2–3). The problems: equality constraints, many variables, few degrees of freedom, first derivatives only (pp. 2, 5). The origin: Biegler's earlier tests on chemical process control problems. A reduced-Hessian SQP code with non-orthogonal bases was sometimes very inefficient, and its search directions turned out to be poor (p. 19).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: Kept: null-space SQP with coordinate (variable-elimination) bases, BFGS on the reduced Hessian, and the ℓ1 merit function (pp. 3–5). The novelty is a correction w_k ≈ ZᵀWYp_Y for the cross term, computed by Broyden (S_k ≈ ZᵀW) or by finite differences (pp. 4–5). Changes to Algorithm II:
  - the finite-difference correction is redefined so that it needs no extra gradient evaluation, and it is activated only once the KKT error falls below a threshold (pp. 6–7);
  - a bound on the finite-difference correction is added (p. 9);
  - the BFGS update is skipped when a curvature test or an update criterion fails (symbols lost, p. 9).

  Implementation devices:
  - a Harwell sparse solver (code number lost) picks the basis. A cheap heuristic requests a new basis when the largest entry of C⁻¹N grows quickly or a tiny steplength appears (pp. 10–11);
  - after a basis change, B and S are transformed to the new basis instead of being reset, which keeps their curvature information (pp. 11–12);
  - a watchdog line search handles the Maratos effect and is switched on only near the solution (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: equality constraints only, to isolate the effect of the correction. The paper says the idea could be used in an active-set method such as SNOPT (p. 5). A_k has full column rank (p. 3); the basis C(x) is nonsingular (p. 4). The bases must change smoothly between basis switches, as the quasi-Newton formulae require (p. 10).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: an analytic two-variable example with an adjustable parameter (pp. 13–14; Appendix pp. 19–21). With orthogonal bases and no correction, the error contracts quadratically. With the coordinate basis and no correction, the one-step error ratio grows with the parameter; the two-step behaviour matches the two-step superlinear rate of Byrd and Yuan. With the correction, contraction is as in the orthogonal case. The redefined correction affects only one lemma of the companion analysis, and the authors state that its proof adapts easily (p. 6; no proof given).
- **D5 Experiments**: Algorithm RHC against two ablations: "No Correction", which resembles a classical reduced Hessian method, and "Broyden Correction" (p. 15). The tests: two scalable examples with dictated good and poor bases (pp. 15–16); Hock–Schittkowski problems, with no times because they are small (pp. 16–17); CUTE EIGENC, EIGENCCO and ORTHREGA/C/D, with CPU times (pp. 17–18). Counts: iterations, function and gradient evaluations.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The correction pays when the basis is poor and changes little when it is good. This matches the companion analysis: it helps only when the iterates do not approach tangentially to the constraints (p. 16). On HS problems RHC beats No Correction in iterations and function evaluations, but the pure Broyden option is the most economical in gradient evaluations (p. 17; also p. 18). Overall the correction adds stability without a significant increase in evaluations or CPU (pp. 18–19). Limits: the code cannot yet force the sparse solver to return a different basis (p. 11); equality constraints only (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the classical reduced-Hessian SQP is kept and only the failing component is replaced: the cross term dropped under coordinate bases. The cost stays "at the same level as that of a standard SQP method" (p. 19). The failure is shown first on an analytic example (Heuristic 1, pp. 13–14), and the repair is ablated by the No Correction and Broyden Correction arms (p. 15) (steps 2, 5).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: unfinished refinements are named in the paper: forcing a new basis (p. 11), extension to inequalities (p. 5).
- Heuristic 9 evidence (card only): the work entered through a practitioner's problem, chemical process control (p. 19).
- new-pattern candidates: **predecessor as control**: the No Correction arm is the classical method (p. 15). **Motivate-and-revisit toy examples**: the analytic example (pp. 13–14) returns as Examples 1–2 with dictated bases (pp. 15–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before testing on a library, build a two-variable example in which the dropped term can be switched on and off and the basis chosen. Compute the one-step error ratio in closed form for each variant (pp. 13–14, 19–21).
- Make an accurate but expensive option (finite differences) a late-stage switch, activated only when the KKT error is below a threshold, and keep the cheap approximation elsewhere (pp. 6–7).
- When a factorization basis must change, transform the stored quasi-Newton matrices to the new basis instead of resetting them, and report that this beat resetting in the tests (pp. 11–12).

**Quotes**:
- "The correction term cannot be expected to improve the performance of the algorithm when the basis matrices are adequate but should prevent poor performance from taking place" (p. 14)
- "an examination of the results revealed that poor search directions were being generated" (p. 19)

### S188 · 3.3 Linear systems and optimization (RAL Numerical Analysis Group Progress Report January 1998–December 1999, 2000, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All of §3.3 read (file pp. 1–3 = printed pp. 21–23), including its references. The displayed equations (the Lagrange system and the preconditioning systems) are garbled in the OCR (pp. 1–2). The rest of file p. 3 belongs to other sections (§3.4 Bai, Duff and Wathen; §3.5 Meerbergen) and was not used. First page checked: the heading "3.3. Linear systems and optimization (N. I. M. Gould, M. E. Hribar, C. Keller, J. Nocedal and A. J. Wathen)" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement. A group progress report in the RAL group's "we", not Nocedal's own voice · **Co-authors**: N. I. M. Gould, M. E. Hribar, C. Keller, A. J. Wathen (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the paragraph on joint work summarises Gould, Hribar and Nocedal, RAL-TR-98-069 (1998) (pp. 1–2). That report is the earlier version of S021 (batch c04).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the approximate minimization of a quadratic subject to linear constraints is "a key computation" in nonlinear programming (p. 1). For large problems direct factorization may be too expensive and high accuracy is not needed, so the work uses preconditioned iterative methods (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: CG preconditioned by an auxiliary indefinite (constraint) system behaves as CG in the null space of the constraints, so existing convergence estimates apply (p. 1). The block M can range from the identity (a least-squares-type system) to M = H (one-iteration convergence) (p. 1). Gould–Hribar–Nocedal observed that the CG iterates can drift from the constraints in practice. The remedies are iterative refinement and an adaptive reformulation of the QP, at low overhead (pp. 1–2). The section also covers Gould's penalty variant and the Lanczos trust-region solver (Gould, Lucidi, Roma, Toint). With these preconditioners the Lanczos solver extends to the linearly constrained case, for the QP code HSL_VE12 (p. 2).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: quadratic objective with linear equality constraints. Objectives unbounded below on the constraint set need a norm constraint (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a. The null-space equivalence is stated, not proved (p. 1).
- **D5 Experiments**: none. The report says the techniques "have subsequently proved to be most effective in practice" without data (p. 2).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: at report level, a low-cost remedy for loss of constraint accuracy in projected CG (pp. 1–2). No limits are stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the classical CG engine is kept, and the component that fails in practice (drift from the constraints) is repaired at low cost (pp. 1–2). But the repair is iterative refinement, not a repair driven by an explicit error estimate with a recovery tree. This is group-report evidence only.

**Transferable techniques**:
- To run CG on an indefinite saddle-point system, precondition it with a constraint (augmented) system. The iteration is then CG on the null space, and the definite-case convergence estimates carry over (p. 1).
- In a projected iteration, monitor whether the iterates keep satisfying the constraints. If they drift, add iterative refinement or reformulate the subproblem (pp. 1–2).

**Quotes**:
- "in Gould, Hribar and Nocedal (1998), we observed that in practice the conjugate-gradient iterates sometimes wander away from the constraints Az = 0 unless care is taken" (p. 1)
- "These techniques have subsequently proved to be most effective in practice." (p. 2)

### S098 · Algorithm 809: PREQN: Fortran 77 subroutines for preconditioning the conjugate gradient method (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 2001, doi:10.1145/382043.382343)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–9) and the references (p. 10). The numbers are lost in this layer: the recommended range of M, the flop counts, the table entries. The Fortran code is not in the file. First page checked: "Algorithm PREQN Fortran Subroutines for Preconditioning the Conjugate Gradient Method" and "Jose Luis Morales Jorge Nocedal" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement (software paper) · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the package that implements the S030 preconditioner, which it calls "the companion paper" (p. 4). S077 cites it as an OTC report (S077 p. 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: make the S030 preconditioners easy to call from any CG routine. The target is sequences A_i x = b_i of slowly varying SPD matrices, which arise in Newton methods, differential equations and statistics (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: BFGS is applied to q(x) = ½xᵀAx − bᵀx. The correction pair is defined as (p_j, A p_j) because the step lengths cancel (p. 3). CG and BFGS are equivalent on positive definite quadratics, so the CG directions supply pairs for the next preconditioner (p. 4). Calling design: one call per CG iteration, at the step that computes the preconditioned residual. The call applies the current preconditioner and passes in the previous pair. The decision to keep that pair, and the building of the new preconditioner, wait until the next call (pp. 5–6). Options (p. 7):
  - M, the number of pairs;
  - IOP, uniform sample or last M pairs; the uniform scheme needs an even M and always adds the last pair, giving M + 1 pairs;
  - BUILD, to reuse a preconditioner across systems, recommended only "with caution".
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: SPD matrices of size n that vary slowly; only products A_i v are available (p. 2).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: three finite-element matrices from S030 with multiple right-hand sides, under a CG stopping test different from S030's. Reported: average CG iterations over the remaining systems for several m and both IOP values (pp. 7–8).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a software contribution. Stated limits:
  - applying the preconditioner is expensive compared with incomplete Cholesky, so it is advocated only when A_i is unknown, not sparse or expensive to apply (p. 4);
  - the preconditioner does not always help, its gains grow with the information stored, and users are told to experiment with m (p. 8);
  - a break-even estimate: the product must cost more than a multiple of n operations (number lost), so very sparse problems are unlikely to benefit, whereas in optimization a product costs about one gradient (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the choice S030 could not settle (uniform versus last-m sampling) ships as option IOP, and preconditioner reuse ships as BUILD (pp. 4, 7).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the authors scope their own method against structure. When A is known and sparse, incomplete Cholesky is the better tool (pp. 4, 8).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: losses are printed: the preconditioner does not always help, and a break-even estimate is given (p. 8).
- Heuristic 7 evidence (card only): the code is a numbered TOMS algorithm with a documented calling sequence (pp. 6–7).
- new-pattern candidate: **recycle inner-solver by-products as curvature memory** (p. 4; see S030).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Design a library routine to be called once per inner iteration, at a point every CG code already has (the preconditioned residual). Pass the data the routine needs for its next state in the same call, and defer its decisions to the next call so the user's loop does not change (pp. 5–6).
- Give users a break-even rule in operation counts: the product's cost against the preconditioner's application cost, not only timings (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "PREQN is written so that a single call from a conjugate gradient routine performs the preconditioning operation and stores information needed for the generation of a new preconditioner." (abstract, p. 1)
- "Therefore the preconditioner is unlikely to be useful for very sparse problems" (p. 8)

### S111 · Feasibility control in nonlinear optimization (Foundations of Computational Mathematics 2001, doi:10.1017/cbo9781107360198.007)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–23) and the references (pp. 23–26). Equations, example data and the table (p. 21) lose their numbers and symbols. First page checked: title, the authors M. Marazzi and Jorge Nocedal, "Report OTC" (p. 1). The batch list names only J. Nocedal, but the file names M. Marazzi as first author (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement (survey) · **Co-authors**: M. Marazzi (Northwestern IEMS) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: a survey written after NITRO (S009), which it uses as its trust-region example (pp. 16–21). It cites a paper by Byrd, Marazzi and Nocedal on failures due to rank deficiency in nonlinear optimization as in preparation (p. 24). That S061 (2004) is the published form of that work is inferred from the authors and topic, not stated.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: what properties must algorithms have so that they cannot converge to non-stationary points of the merit function (abstract, p. 1)? The entry is two failure examples: Powell's example for line-search Newton on nonlinear equations (pp. 2–4), and Wächter and Biegler's example for line-search interior methods (pp. 13–15). The common cause is demanding exact satisfaction of the constraint linearizations (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: name the mechanism, "feasibility control": mechanisms that regulate progress toward feasibility (p. 2). It is distinct from regularization (pp. 5–6). Methods sorted by it:
  - have it: Levenberg–Marquardt, dogleg (through the Cauchy step), the Byrd–Omojokun normal step, and Celis–Dennis–Tapia (pp. 4–10);
  - lack it: Vardi's scaled linearization (p. 8);
  - get it only through relaxation: line-search SQP, by SNOPT's elastic mode or Biggs's penalty relaxation (pp. 10–11);
  - lack it: line-search interior methods (pp. 12–15);
  - have it: NITRO's trust-region interior method, through its normal step (pp. 16–19);
  - lack it: NUOPT's dogleg between a steepest-descent-type step and the primal-dual step, because both satisfy the linearization (pp. 21–23).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: smooth problems. The Levenberg–Marquardt result assumes a Lipschitz, bounded Jacobian on the level set (p. 5). The Byrd–Omojokun results assume f bounded below and bounded gradients, Jacobians and Hessian approximations (footnote, p. 9).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**:
  - Powell's example recomputed. Newton with an exact line search fails, yet "To our surprise" a relaxed backtracking line search converged from the same start; other starting points make it fail (p. 4).
  - Wächter–Biegler argument: at every iterate the full step toward the linearized constraints violates a bound, so the iterates never leave an infeasible region, whatever the merit function and the barrier update (pp. 13–14). Here the KKT matrix is singular on a manifold, and the steps are cut by the bounds; in Powell's example the merit function forces them to zero (p. 15).
  - Their own code: on a one-variable barrier problem, NITRO's normal step (restricted to the range of the constraint normals and cut by the fraction-to-the-boundary rule) needs very many iterations. The iteration count is bounded below via closed-form steps (pp. 19–21), and the same behaviour appears on CUTE's GOFFIN (p. 21).
- **D5 Experiments**: illustrations only: NITRO on the Wächter–Biegler example (iterate plot, p. 18), and NITRO's line-search option on the one-variable problem (table, p. 21).
- **D6 Literature positioning (one line)**: fair to the line-search school. LOQO is called a careful implementation (p. 12), and NUOPT's scheme a "novel approach" to nonconvexity (p. 23), while both are shown to lack feasibility control (pp. 13, 22).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a survey that contributes a unifying concept and a classification (pp. 2, 10, 23). Open questions it states:
  - whether such failures are rare in practice, or appear mostly as inefficiency (p. 15);
  - whether a simple modification of the line-search scheme resolves them, and whether the Forsgren–Gill method is better (p. 15);
  - new subproblem techniques for NITRO's slow case (p. 21);
  - feasibility control in interior methods is "only beginning to be investigated" (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: theory used to separate practical methods. The classification predicts which methods can stall at non-stationary infeasible points (pp. 8–10, 13–15, 22). The analysis targets the version the code runs: NITRO's inexact range-space normal step (pp. 19–21) (step 1).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the external counterexample (Wächter–Biegler) is treated as a specification and run on their own code (pp. 13–18). Their own code's inefficiency goes into the paper as the next task (p. 21), and the follow-up analysis is announced (p. 24). Wächter is thanked for comments (p. 23).
- Workflow D evidence (card only): surprise is recorded as surprise (p. 4).
- new-pattern candidate: **property abstraction**: name one property (feasibility control) and classify methods by whether they have it (pp. 2, 10, 22–23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Organise a survey around one named property and one or two canonical counterexamples. For each method, ask whether the counterexample can defeat it (pp. 2–4, 10, 22).
- Run a published counterexample on your own code. Add a simpler example on which your code is merely slow, with an iteration-count bound derived from closed-form steps (pp. 18–21).
- When a classical failure example behaves unexpectedly, vary only the line search (exact against backtracking) and the starting point before naming the cause (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "We do not know whether failures of this type are rare in practice" (p. 15)
- "Our view is that the selection of ... should not be based entirely on linear algebra considerations but must be tied in with the convergence properties of the optimization process" (p. 6)

### S040 · Wedge trust region methods for derivative free optimization (Mathematical Programming 2002, doi:10.1007/s101070100264)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–18: abstract, the algorithm, linear and quadratic models, procedure QuadStep, numerical results, acknowledgements) and the references (pp. 18–19). The three result tables (pp. 14, 16, 17) and all parameter values lose their digits. First page checked: title, the authors Marcelo Marazzi and Jorge Nocedal, "Report OTC" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Marcelo Marazzi (Northwestern IEMS; thesis "Nonlinear Optimization With and Without Derivatives", reference list p. 18) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: a 2002 derivative-free paper, long before the noise programme, that already has a finite-difference L-BFGS-B arm (p. 13). A convergence analysis for linear models is cited as in preparation (pp. 6, 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: smooth f, a moderate number of variables, no derivatives, and evaluations that cost far more than the linear algebra (p. 2). The entry: model-based trust-region methods need two kinds of iteration, minimization steps and "simplex" (geometry-improving) steps, because a trust-region step can spoil the geometry of the interpolation set (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: one type of iteration only. The geometric condition is imposed inside the step computation (pp. 3, 5). The details:
  - the farthest satellite leaves the set (p. 4);
  - steps in the taboo region, which would make the new point set degenerate, are excluded by a wedge constraint |bᵀs| ≥ γ‖b‖‖s‖ for linear models (pp. 6–7), and by its quadratic analogue built from a QR factorization (pp. 8–10);
  - an unsuccessful trial point stays as a satellite if it is no farther from x_c than the leaving point. Repeated failures therefore improve the model while the radius shrinks (pp. 5–6);
  - the linear subproblem is solved globally by a rotation in span{s_TR, b} (p. 7);
  - the quadratic subproblem is solved approximately: a Moré–Sorensen trust-region step, then rotations away from the taboo region. If the model value worsens too much, the wedge is closed (γ decreases, never increases) (pp. 10–12).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f is smooth, derivatives are unavailable, and evaluations dominate the cost (p. 2). Global convergence is argued informally here, and a proof for linear models is deferred (p. 6; ref. p. 18).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a, no theorems. The convergence argument: when the model is poor, rejected points enter the satellite set until the model is accurate enough for a successful step (pp. 5–6).
- **D5 Experiments**: CUTE problems with a Matlab code, in three comparisons:
  - WEDlin against COBYLA, which Powell supplied (single precision);
  - WEDquad against finite-difference L-BFGS-B (QNfd) at its default settings;
  - WEDquad against DFO, using published results plus results supplied by Scheinberg.

  One stopping test for all codes: reach a fixed fraction of the decrease that NITRO with exact second derivatives obtains. There is an evaluation limit, and medians over five runs are reported because the initial satellites are random (pp. 12–15).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: an algorithm with a numerical study. The results are stated with limits:
  - WEDlin is "competitive" with COBYLA on unconstrained problems (p. 13);
  - WEDquad is sometimes, not always, more efficient than QNfd, and more reliable (one failure against five). The authors attribute QNfd's failures to forward-difference gradient errors in the line search (p. 15);
  - the stopping test may disadvantage DFO (p. 15).

  Future work: starting from the vertices and midpoints of a simplex costs O(n²) evaluations and is inefficient. Untried refinements: linear or underdetermined models early on, increasing the wedge parameter, and an acceptance test based on the Cauchy point (pp. 15, 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (strong, and early). The rival's code came from its author (COBYLA from Powell), and the DFO results from its developer (Scheinberg), who is thanked for observations on testing DFO methods (pp. 15, 18). One stopping test, anchored to a third solver, applies to all codes (p. 13). Medians over five randomized runs are reported (p. 12). ### S057 · On the behavior of the gradient norm in the steepest descent method (Computational Optimization and Applications 2002, doi:10.1023/a:1014897230089)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–26) and the references (pp. 26–27). Theorem statements and formulas mostly lose their digits, Greek letters and fractions, so the results are known from their prose explanations; the figures are not visible. First page checked: title and the authors Jorge Nocedal, Annick Sartenaer and Ciyou Zhu (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Annick Sartenaer (Namur), Ciyou Zhu (Northwestern) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: motivated by the development of L-BFGS-B (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the entry came from software development. L-BFGS-B sometimes could not reduce ‖g‖ as far as LANCELOT did. The authors first reported this as a failure, but in some of those runs L-BFGS-B had reached a lower f (p. 2). A table shows the PENALTY problem, L-BFGS against inexact Newton (p. 2). The question: when does the gradient norm measure the accuracy in f, and are its oscillations tied to conditioning and to the rate of convergence (p. 2)? Gradient-norm tests are used everywhere, including in the subproblems of augmented Lagrangian and barrier methods (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: two parts.
  - For general f: f − f* and ‖g‖² both scale with ‖x − x*‖² through Rayleigh quotients, so their ratio can vary as much as the condition number. The path to the solution decides whether the final gradient is small (pp. 3–5).
  - Main part: steepest descent with exact line searches on strongly convex quadratics, extending Akaike and Forsythe (pp. 5–26). The results:
    - a per-iteration bound on growth of the gradient norm (Theorem, p. 12);
    - asymptotic one- and two-step ratios; the two-step gradient rate equals the one-step rate in f (pp. 13–14);
    - larger oscillations go with faster convergence in f, contrary to the authors' expectations (p. 18);
    - the iterates approach along the eigenvector of the smallest eigenvalue, which gives small final gradients (pp. 18–20);
    - the 2-D case is different (pp. 20–24);
    - final accuracy: the slower the method, the fewer correct digits in f, and for steepest descent the error grows linearly with the condition number (pp. 24–26).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: strongly convex quadratic and exact line searches. Assumption 1: the initial gradient is not an eigenvector. Assumption 2: the extreme eigenvalues are simple and the starting gradient has nonzero components along them (p. 6). Part of Assumption 2 can be relaxed, as Forsythe shows (p. 10). The rounding-error argument ignores roundoff in computing the iterates (p. 25).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: the Kantorovich inequality gives the growth bound, so gradient norms decrease monotonically when the condition number is below a threshold (value lost) (p. 12). Akaike's two-direction asymptotics and Forsythe's convergence of normalized gradients are used (pp. 6–7). A minimum-deviation parameter δ of the inner eigenvalues restricts the asymptotic constant c (pp. 9–10). The 2-D case gets an exact analysis (pp. 21–23).
- **D5 Experiments**: one quartic test function, steepest descent with the Moré–Thuente Wolfe line search, run until no further decrease. The observed final accuracy agrees with the predicted number of correct digits (p. 26). Also the PENALTY table (p. 2).
- **D6 Literature positioning (one line)**: questions Barzilai and Borwein's suggestion that a 2-D rate result explains their method's n-D advantage. In n dimensions the steepest-descent rate does not improve with conditioning, so the 2-D case must first be shown to be representative (p. 24).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a theory contribution with practical messages. Monitor the gradient over two steps (p. 14). Oscillation size is no sign of the worst case, but large oscillations imply ill-conditioning (pp. 12, 14). Open: the behaviour of gradient norms for quasi-Newton methods "remains an open question" (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: theory explains a behaviour seen in codes, the gradient-norm stopping test misjudging L-BFGS-B (pp. 2–3). The prediction is checked against a run (correct digits predicted against observed, p. 26). The unproved quasi-Newton case is kept as an open question (p. 5).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a code ranking that rested on the termination measure was re-examined and reversed (p. 2). This is Taste warning sign 7 (mismatched stopping tests) caught in his own work.
- Workflow D/E evidence (card only): the surprise is recorded (p. 18), and their own earlier failure report is corrected (p. 2).
- new-pattern candidates: **audit the termination measure before declaring a failure** (p. 2); **check that the low-dimensional case is representative** (p. 24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When one code seems to fail because it cannot reach a gradient tolerance that another reaches, compare the final function values before reporting a failure (p. 2).
- For methods that zigzag, monitor progress in the gradient over two iterations, since the two-step gradient rate matches the one-step rate in f (p. 14).
- Predict the attainable accuracy in f from the linear convergence factor and the unit roundoff, then check the prediction on a non-quadratic run (pp. 24–26).

**Quotes**:

### S077 · Enriched methods for large-scale unconstrained optimization (Computational Optimization and Applications 2002, doi:10.1023/a:1013756631822)
**Read level**: partial. All prose read (pp. 1–10). The tables (pp. 9, 11) were seen, but their numbers are lost; references read (pp. 10, 12). Parameter values are lost: the initial m and l, the CG limits, the steplength interval that defines a profitable step. First page checked: title and "Jose Luis Morales Jorge Nocedal" (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: José Luis Morales (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the "sophisticated implementation" (p. 4) that replaces the rigid earlier attempt by Byrd, Nocedal and Zhu (S088, batch s02) (pp. 3–4). It uses the S030/S098 preconditioners (pp. 2, 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Hessian-free Newton methods are "notorious for their sensitivity" to the inner CG termination rule (p. 2). The goal is a method that is fairly insensitive to its parameters and efficient over a wide range of problems (p. 2). HFN and L-BFGS have complementary strengths: HFN needs few iterations, but each is costly and its curvature information is lost afterwards; L-BFGS iterations are cheap, but its curvature can be poor on ill-conditioned problems (p. 3). The earlier enriched method was rigid: a fixed number of L-BFGS steps, then one HFN step by unpreconditioned CG with a fixed number of CG iterations. It did not perform well over the CUTE set (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Key idea and algorithm design**: alternate l L-BFGS steps with t HFN steps. One limited-memory matrix H_m carries the curvature through the cycle. It is built during the L-BFGS cycle and preconditions the first HFN step; the inner PCG pairs then update it to precondition later HFN steps; finally it warm-starts the next L-BFGS cycle (p. 3). L-BFGS and HFN are the special cases t = 0 and l = 0 (p. 3), and L-BFGS is formally HFN with one CG iteration (p. 5). Procedure ADJUST sets l and t from the quality of the steps (pp. 5–7):
  - negative curvature: back to L-BFGS, with a longer l;
  - a small Newton steplength: a shorter t;
  - a full cycle of profitable Newton steps: t + 1;
  - at least two HFN steps in succession (the variable force), because limited-memory preconditioning pays off only then;
  - a cap on CG iterations in the very first HFN iteration.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: the gradient is available, second derivatives are not, and products Bv come from finite differences or AD (pp. 2, 4). CG stops at negative curvature without exploiting it (p. 4). The line search is a backtracking search enforcing the Wolfe conditions (p. 5).
- **D4 Analysis (brief)**: n/a.
- **D5 Experiments**: all large unconstrained CUTE problems. The enriched method is compared with two HFN variants, one using Nash's CG rule and one a residual rule, both with the same quasi-Newton preconditioner. Function-gradient evaluations are counted (pp. 7–11). The tables have no plain L-BFGS arm.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: claims: significantly more efficient than the two HFN methods, insensitive to the CG stopping test, and "a serious competitor to Hessianfree Newton methods" (p. 10). Only m must be chosen, and performance varies gradually with it (p. 4). There is no limitations paragraph. Other HFN variants were tried and were no better (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: complementary algorithms are integrated rather than one being crowned, and each is a special case of the hybrid (p. 3).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the next paper answers the earlier paper's open choices (S088: preconditioning CG with the pairs, when to take Newton steps) with dynamic cycle lengths and preconditioning (pp. 3–4).
- Method 1 ✗ contradiction: the obvious simple baseline is missing. L-BFGS itself, the l-only special case (p. 3), has no column in the tables (pp. 9, 11); the comparison is with two HFN variants only. In its favour, the HFN arms share the enriched method's preconditioner, so the preconditioner is controlled (p. 8).
- new-pattern candidates: **Special-case reduction as a design requirement**: L-BFGS and HFN are both special cases (pp. 3, 5). **recycle inner-solver by-products as curvature memory** (pp. 2–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
