### S019 · Theory of algorithms for unconstrained optimization (Acta Numerica 1992, DOI 10.1017/s0962492900002270)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–32 (Introduction; the most useful algorithms; basic convergence principles; conjugate gradient methods; variable metric methods including non-convex objectives, global efficiency of BFGS/DFP, "Is BFGS the best variable metric method?", SR1; methods for large problems; remarks on other methods; acknowledgements). References pp. 32–37 skimmed. Preprint version (see page mapping).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (single-authored, p. 1); thanks Marucha Lalee, Christy Hartung and Peihuang Lu for help with preparation (p. 32) · **Place in trajectory**: written after the L-BFGS paper (Liu & Nocedal, cited p. 3; 1989 per SKILL.md, the year is lost in this text layer) and before the constrained/KNITRO period. It is the survey in which he states the "as implemented in practice" test and the test of whether theory can tell efficient from inefficient methods, the two tests SKILL.md Method 5 is built on, and in which his own Broyden-class work with Byrd and Yuan is placed (pp. 17–20).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a user's question, not a gap in the literature. Preparing a lecture for engineers and numerical analysts, he asked "from the point of view of a user of nonlinear optimization routines" how interesting and practical the theory is, and made it precise by selecting "those methods that deserve to be in a subroutine library" and asking what is known about each "as implemented in practice" (p. 1). A second question follows: how useful the theory is when designing new algorithms, that is, how well it can tell efficient from inefficient methods (p. 2). The scope is narrowed on purpose to unconstrained methods (p. 1), to line search rather than trust-region methods because the latter's theory "was obtained before" and is already surveyed by Moré and Sorensen (p. 3), and rounding errors and noise are set aside: "we will not consider these aspects here" (p. 2). He finds the field "mature" (p. 1) but lists what is missing: average and worst-case behaviour, the effect of rounding errors, and tools that predict efficiency on large problems (p. 1).
  - Transferable: open a survey (or a thesis literature chapter) with a short, named list of the methods practitioners actually run, each tied to a library code (Harwell, IMSL, NAG, CONMIN, Lancelot; pp. 2–3), and ask of each what is known about the version as implemented. The list fixes what the review must explain.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The organizing idea is a four-way classification of convergence results: global convergence, local convergence, asymptotic rate, and global efficiency / global rate (p. 2). Most work is in the first three; global efficiency "requires more attention" (p. 2). The survey then shows that a few principles carry most of the analysis: the Wolfe conditions (p. 4), Zoutendijk's condition (pp. 5–6) and the Dennis–Moré characterization of superlinear convergence (p. 7). It argues that global convergence alone is a weak design target. Angle tests (turn the direction toward steepest descent whenever cos θ falls below a preset constant, p. 7) guarantee convergence but are undesirable, because they can block the asymptotic approach to the Newton direction and because "the best implementations of the methods listed ... do not need them" (p. 7). The paper proposes no new algorithm. Its algorithmic judgements are: PR+ (β = max{β^PR, 0}) is the safeguard that rescues Polak–Ribière (p. 13); conjugate gradient methods remain "the best choice for solving very large problems with relatively inexpensive objective functions" (p. 10); the partitioned quasi-Newton method is "one of the major algorithmic advances" (p. 30); L-BFGS keeps the m most recent pairs and is competitive with partitioned quasi-Newton when element functions involve more than a few variables (p. 30).
  - Transferable: classify every result you cite into global / local / rate / global-efficiency (p. 2) and say which class your own result fills. A thesis chapter that has only "global convergence" results has, by this test, the weakest class.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: f smooth with gradient available (p. 4); for Zoutendijk's theorem, f bounded below and continuously differentiable near the level set, with Lipschitz gradient (p. 5); strong Wolfe conditions for the conjugate gradient theorems (p. 11); strong convexity (eigenvalues of the Hessian between m and M) for the BFGS and Broyden-class global theory (p. 18). Assumptions he flags as restrictive or for convenience: global efficiency results are "established under very restrictive assumptions and yet provide useful insight" and are commonly restricted to convex or strictly convex quadratic functions (pp. 2, 8); Powell's two-variable analysis assumes unit steps, so "Powell's analysis has some limitations" (p. 24); the SR1 theorem of Conn, Gould and Toint needs uniformly linearly independent steps, which Khalfan, Byrd and Schnabel find often fails in practice (pp. 28–29); positive definite bounded SR1 matrices "appears ... to be a very unrealistic assumption" but are often observed (p. 29). Non-convex BFGS theory is missing (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: after each theorem, check whether its key assumption holds in actual runs (the Khalfan–Byrd–Schnabel move: they tested whether SR1 steps were uniformly linearly independent and found they often were not, pp. 28–29).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (1) Zoutendijk's condition Σ cos²θ_k ‖g_k‖² < ∞, proved in full from the Wolfe conditions and the Lipschitz gradient (p. 5); bounded cos θ gives lim ‖g_k‖ = 0, bounded condition number of B_k gives the same for Newton-type methods, and lim inf results follow by contradiction from a subsequence with cos θ bounded away from zero (p. 6). (2) Dennis–Moré characterization: superlinear iff the step approaches the Newton step, plus unit steplength acceptance (p. 7). (3) Kantorovich inequality for the steepest descent rate on quadratics (p. 8); Nemirovsky–Yudin lower bounds and optimal methods (p. 9). (4) Al-Baali's induction giving sufficient descent for Fletcher–Reeves under strong Wolfe (pp. 11–12). (5) Tightness by counterexample: Gilbert and Nocedal show the |β_k| ≤ β^FR bound is tight, since some c > 1 allows a sequence with gradients bounded away from zero (p. 12); Powell's "algebraic tour de force" shows Polak–Ribière can cycle with exact line searches (p. 13). (6) Property (*), which isolates what makes Polak–Ribière turn toward steepest descent after a tiny step, and a class theorem for any method with β ≥ 0, sufficient descent and Property (*) (p. 14). (7) The trace and determinant relations for BFGS: the trace term proportional to 1/cos θ forces large eigenvalues down and the determinant relation pushes small ones up, the "self correcting mechanism" (pp. 18–19). For the restricted Broyden class the decisive comparison is that the uncertain-sign term scales as 1/cos θ while the trace-decreasing term scales as 1/cos²θ. At φ = 1 (DFP) that term disappears, which explains why DFP corrects large eigenvalues poorly (p. 20). (8) Byrd–Nocedal: if the pairs satisfy curvature bounds, cos θ_j is bounded below for at least ⌈pk⌉ of the first k indices (p. 21); the potential ψ(B) = tr(B) − ln det(B), which bounds ln cond(B) (p. 21). (9) Powell's two-variable quadratic recurrences: when the initial eigenvalue λ is large, DFP may need a number of iterations that grows with λ itself, while BFGS needs only a logarithmic number in the worst case (pp. 23–24; the exact expressions are lost in extraction). (10) Khalfan–Byrd–Schnabel's n-step q-quadratic SR1 rate without the linear-independence assumption (p. 29).
  - Transferable: when two members of a family behave very differently in practice, write the potential (trace, determinant, or ψ = tr − ln det) for the whole family and compare how each term scales in the bad regime (cos θ small). The term that vanishes at the bad parameter value is the explanation (p. 20).
  - Transferable: check whether a counterexample is reachable by practical codes before calling a method unsafe. He notes that Powell's cycling steplength "would probably be accepted by any practical line search algorithm" (p. 13), and that the cycling has "never been observed" in practice (p. 14).
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a for new experiments: the survey runs none. It uses small diagnostic runs, his own or his group's, to confirm or illustrate theory. He has seen Fletcher–Reeves take tiny steps "for hundreds of iterations" on Toint's minimal surface problem until a restart (p. 12). He has seen CONMIN restart at every iteration and use an excessive number of function evaluations (p. 15). The Byrd–Nocedal–Yuan two-variable test (quadratic plus a small quartic term, ill-scaled diagonal start) found BFGS fast and DFP slow, "amazingly close to the estimate given by Powell"; φ moved slightly away from one already changes performance markedly (p. 24). He reports conflicting experimental claims without arbitrating: Conn–Gould–Toint vs Khalfan–Byrd–Schnabel on SR1 Hessian accuracy (p. 28); Lukšan's tests on restricted CG methods are "interesting but inconclusive" (p. 14); column scaling has "not enough data yet" for firm conclusions (p. 27).
  - Transferable: to check a worst-case prediction, build the smallest nearly-quadratic problem the analysis covers (two variables, a small quartic term) and compare the predicted and observed iteration counts (p. 24).
- **D6 Positioning**: He continues Powell (the trace/determinant BFGS analysis, p. 17), Dennis–Moré (local theory, pp. 7, 22) and Zoutendijk/Wolfe (p. 5). His own group's results are placed in that line: Byrd–Nocedal–Yuan (p. 17), Gilbert–Nocedal (pp. 12–14), Byrd–Nocedal (p. 21), Nocedal–Yuan on self-scaling (p. 26), Byrd–Liu–Nocedal on negative φ (p. 25), Lalee–Nocedal on column scaling (p. 26), Liu–Nocedal on L-BFGS (pp. 30–31). The complexity school (Nemirovsky–Yudin, Nesterov) is presented neutrally as one category of theory (p. 9), but Nesterov's optimal CG-type method is judged unlikely to be effective in practice, though "this has not been investigated to the best of my knowledge" (p. 15). Earlier views are revised openly: SR1 was once considered fatally flawed and is "now regarded by many researchers as a serious contender" (p. 27); disappointing self-scaling results are explained, not dismissed (p. 26). He states plainly where theory lags practice: "Ironically" the many studies of variable metric methods "have not resulted in the discovery of new methods" (p. 16).
  - Transferable: give a disputed numerical claim both sides with their stated test conditions (p. 28) rather than choosing one; mark which evidence is still missing.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: First person and openly evaluative: "I asked myself the question" (p. 1), "In my view" (pp. 2, 10, 15), "I agree with this assessment" (p. 15), and a closing personal conjecture on conjugate gradients: "I have always felt that the answer is yes but I could say no more" (p. 16). Section openings are often questions ("How fast is the convergence of conjugate gradient methods", p. 14; "Is BFGS the best variable metric method", p. 24). Open problems are numbered and stated formally (Open Question I on DFP, p. 17; Open Question II on non-convex BFGS, pp. 21–22). Intuition comes before rigour, and he says so: "The discussion that follows is not rigorous but all the statements made below can be established rigorously" (p. 19). Only Zoutendijk's theorem is proved in full, "so that the reader can have a clear idea of how it depends on the properties of the function and line search" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: prove in full only the one lemma everything else rests on (p. 5), and give the rest as heuristic arguments that are labelled non-rigorous (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a survey and synthesis that sets the agenda (theory level), with explicit open questions. Scope limits are stated rather than hidden: no rounding errors or noise (p. 2), no global optimization (p. 2), no nonlinear equations or least squares, Nelder–Mead only mentioned (p. 31), trust regions only contrasted (pp. 31–32). Named future directions: a comprehensive CG theory as "one of the outstanding challenges" (p. 10); DFP convergence on convex problems (p. 17); BFGS on non-convex problems, and a practical line search that bounds ‖y‖²/yᵀs (p. 22); why L-BFGS is as fast as BFGS and how to choose "the most useful corrections pairs not simply the most recent ones" (p. 31); whether SR1 will last (pp. 27, 29); whether line search or trust region will dominate (p. 32). Later events outside this batch (Dai's BFGS example, cited in SKILL.md Method 5) are not part of this card.

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (theory must discriminate) ✅ evidence: the "as implemented in practice" question (p. 1), the question of how well theory can tell efficient from inefficient methods (p. 2), the steepest-descent argument against settling for global convergence (p. 7), the family-wide trace analysis that singles out DFP (pp. 17–20) and the BFGS/DFP efficiency gap (pp. 23–24). Unproved points are kept as numbered open questions (pp. 17, 21–22). This fills in the thin SKILL.md anatomy of this paper.
- Method 2 (received wisdom on trial) ⚠ variant: the received safeguard (angle tests) is rejected by argument and by what the best codes do, not by a new experiment (p. 7). The received verdict on SR1 is reported as reversed by others' studies (p. 27), and the "controversial" restart question gets his own observation (p. 15). The trial is held by review, not by running the dismissed option.
- Method 6 (structure first) ✅ evidence: the partitioned quasi-Newton method for partially separable problems is judged a major advance (pp. 29–30); L-BFGS is competitive only once element functions involve more than a few variables (p. 30); CG is preferred for very large problems with cheap functions (p. 10).
- Also matches Research Taste marks 1 ("deserve to be in a subroutine library", p. 1) and 4 (BFGS self-correction, pp. 16, 19).
- New-pattern candidate: *property abstraction*, i.e. isolate the property that makes the good method good (Property (*)) and prove convergence for the whole class that has it (p. 14).
- New-pattern candidate: *practical-reachability check on counterexamples*, i.e. ask whether practical line searches would produce the counterexample's steps and whether the failure has ever been observed (pp. 13–14, 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before reviewing a field, list the handful of methods "that deserve to be in a subroutine library" with their codes, and review only what is known about each as implemented (pp. 1–3).
- Classify each convergence result as global, local, rate or global efficiency, and say which class is missing (p. 2).
- To explain why one family member fails, compare the scaling of each potential-function term in the bad regime; the term that vanishes at that parameter value is the mechanism (pp. 18–20).
- Test a tightness claim by constructing a counterexample just past the bound (|β_k| ≤ c·β^FR with c > 1, p. 12).
- Before calling a method unsafe because of a counterexample, check whether practical line searches would accept the counterexample's steps (p. 13).
- Confirm a worst-case prediction on the smallest nearly-quadratic test problem the analysis covers (p. 24).

- "Ironically our many theoretical studies of variable metric methods have not resulted in the discovery of new methods but have mainly served to explain phenomena observed in practice" (p. 16)

### S014 · Representations of quasi-Newton matrices and their use in limited memory methods (Mathematical Programming 1994, DOI 10.1007/bf01582063)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 2–29: abstract and introduction; compact representations of BFGS matrices; application to the limited memory method (computations with H_k, operations with H_k and sparse constraints, operations with B_k); alternative formulae (two-loop recursion, a straightforward approach); compact representation of SR1 matrices; Broyden matrices for nonlinear equations; relation to multiple secant updates; acknowledgement. References pp. 30–31 skimmed. Technical-report version (see page mapping). Formula bodies are fragmentary in the text layer; their content is reconstructed from the surrounding prose where described below.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd, Robert B. Schnabel (University of Colorado, Boulder) (p. 1); thanks Peihuang Lu (p. 29) · **Place in trajectory**: the linear-algebra step between L-BFGS for unconstrained problems (Nocedal's limited-storage recursion and Liu–Nocedal, p. 3) and the constrained uses: the bound-constrained algorithm of Byrd, Lu and Nocedal (cited pp. 16–17; S003 in this batch) and the reduced-Hessian SQP of Biegler, Nocedal and Schmid (p. 25).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The application forces the problem. Limited memory methods work well unconstrained, so "it is desirable to use limited memory approximations also for solving problems that include constraints" (p. 2), but constrained algorithms need projections of the Hessian approximation onto the active-constraint subspace and other matrix computations that are expensive for large n "unless special care is taken" (p. 2). Two precise failures of the existing two-loop recursion are named. It ignores sparsity: H_k e_i costs almost as much as H_k g_k. It has no analogue for the direct matrix B_k, which many constrained algorithms need, and a straightforward implementation of B_k is costly (p. 4).
  - Transferable: before proposing a new representation, name the specific operations the old one does badly in the target setting (here products with unit vectors and the direct matrix B_k, p. 4). Those operations then become the benchmark list for the paper.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A lemma writes the product of the BFGS projection matrices V_0⋯V_{k−1} as I − Y_k R_k^{−1} S_k^T with R_k upper triangular, "a useful but little-known result" (p. 5). From it come outer-product ("compact") forms. The inverse BFGS matrix is H_k = H_0 plus [S_k, H_0Y_k] times a small 2k×2k middle matrix built from R_k, D_k and Y_k^T H_0 Y_k, times the transposed block (p. 6). The direct matrix is B_k = B_0 minus [B_0S_k, Y_k] times the inverse of a 2k×2k matrix in S_k^T B_0 S_k, L_k and D_k, times the transposed block (p. 7). What changes is only the representation. The matrices are the same BFGS (and later SR1 and Broyden) matrices, and for unconstrained problems the cost equals the two-loop recursion, and "as far as we know" there is no more efficient way (p. 13). With H_0 = γ_k I or B_0 = σ_k I, the limited memory version stores the n×m matrices S_k, Y_k plus small m×m matrices, and refreshes them by dropping the oldest pair and adding the newest (pp. 9–11). Why this change: products with sparse vectors, AᵀHA for SQP multipliers (pp. 14–15), and reduced Hessians ZᵀBZ for bound constraints then cost work proportional to the number of nonzeros or of free variables t, not to n (pp. 15, 17–18). The same device gives compact forms for SR1 (p. 21) and for Broyden's method for nonlinear equations (pp. 23–25).
  - Transferable: when an iterative update is a product of rank-one projections, look for the closed-form "product of projections" identity (p. 5). It converts a recursion into a low-rank outer product that supports any matrix-vector operation the setting needs.
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: BFGS forms need s_iᵀy_i > 0 for all i and a positive definite starting matrix (pp. 6–7), which makes R_k nonsingular (p. 7). SR1 forms need only that each update is well defined, the denominator (y_j − B_j s_j)ᵀs_j nonzero, with no curvature condition (p. 21). Broyden forms need nonzero s_i and a nonsingular starting matrix (pp. 23, 25). The starting matrix is assumed scalar, H_0 = γ_k I, "because this choice is common in practice"; other initial matrices "would probably result in a more expensive computation" (p. 10). The number of stored pairs m is assumed constant, though adapting to varying m is not difficult (p. 9). No smoothness or convergence assumptions are needed, since the results are algebraic identities.
  - Transferable: state which modelling choice (here a scalar initial matrix, p. 10) the efficiency claims depend on, and say what generalizing it would cost.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: (1) Induction on k for the product-of-projections lemma, with the block inverse of the bordered triangular matrix R_{k+1} (p. 5). (2) Splitting H_k = M_k + N_k into the propagated-H_0 part and the accumulated rank-one part, each proved by induction (pp. 6–7). (3) Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury to pass from the inverse to the direct compact form (pp. 7–8), and later from direct to inverse SR1 (p. 22) and Broyden (p. 25). (4) Reordering the blocks of the indefinite middle matrix so that only the Cholesky factorization of the m×m positive definite matrix S_kᵀB_0S_k + L_k D_k^{−1} L_kᵀ is needed (p. 9). Its positive definiteness is proved by contradiction through the triangular R_k with positive diagonal (p. 9). (5) Itemized operation counts per algorithm step, counting multiplications only (pp. 11–20). (6) Relating the two-loop recursion to the compact form: the stored α_i are R_k^{−1}S_kᵀg_k (p. 19), and the two-loop recursion is the adjoint (reverse-mode differentiation) of g ↦ ½gᵀH_k g (Gilbert–Nocedal, p. 19). (7) Exact equivalence conditions with multiple secant updates: Y_kᵀS_k diagonal for BFGS, i.e. conjugate directions on a quadratic, against the "far milder" symmetry condition sᵢᵀyⱼ = sⱼᵀyᵢ for SR1 (pp. 26–28).
  - Transferable: when an inverse needed in an algorithm is of an indefinite small matrix, reorder blocks so that a Schur complement is positive definite, and prove it with a short contradiction argument (p. 9). A Cholesky factorization then replaces an indefinite solve.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: n/a. The paper has no numerical tests. Operation counts take their place: a leading term proportional to mn plus O(m²) multiplications per unconstrained step, the same as the two-loop recursion (pp. 12–13; the coefficient is lost in extraction); about half the cost of computing H_k v first for vᵀH_k v (p. 14); cost proportional to the number of nonzeros for sparse A (p. 15); work proportional to t rather than n for reduced Hessians (p. 18); compared with the straightforward approach of storing vectors a_i, b_i, which costs more per update (pp. 19–20). The leading coefficients are partly lost in the text layer; the comparisons are stated in prose.
  - Transferable: for a linear-algebra contribution, use per-step multiplication counts against each named alternative (pp. 13, 19–20) as the evaluation protocol, and identify the leading term ("for large problems the term mn predominates", p. 13, the coefficient lost in extraction).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Nocedal's earlier recursion and Matthies–Strang (p. 18) and keeps it: the two methods are equally efficient for unconstrained problems (p. 13), and the two-loop recursion stays preferable when H_0 is more than a scalar multiple of the identity (pp. 18–19). It credits Walker for essentially the same lemma for Householder products (p. 5). It contrasts itself with the straightforward approach used by Mahidhara and Lasdon (pp. 19–20) and with the multiple secant updates of Barnes, Gay–Schnabel, Schnabel and Khalfan (p. 26). The verdict on multiple secant updates is based on structure, not novelty: they lose symmetry and positive definiteness and need perturbations, so "it does not seem advantageous" for optimization (p. 27). For nonlinear equations "the preference between these two formulas does not appear to be clear cut" (p. 28).
  - Transferable: when comparing with an alternative formula, find the exact condition under which the two coincide (pp. 26–28). The comparison then reduces to what happens off that condition.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Theorem–proof with algorithm boxes. Each algorithm step carries its cost (pp. 12–13, 16–17). Claims are modest and hedged: "We believe that these new compact representations ... are of interest in their own right" (p. 3); "as far as we know" (p. 13); "we know of no procedure analogous to the two loop recursion" for the direct update (p. 19). The motivation (the two failures) comes before any formula (pp. 2–4). The closing comparison section is offered "for the sake of completeness" (p. 18).
  - Transferable: put the motivating cost failure of the incumbent formula (p. 4) before the new identity, so the reader knows which operations to watch.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: algorithmic and linear-algebra infrastructure, enabling limited memory matrices inside constrained methods (bounds and SQP, pp. 14–18) and nonlinear equations (pp. 23–25). Limits stated: no numerical tests; detailed limited memory SR1 algorithms are not given "because the ideas are very similar" (p. 23); testing the SR1 skip condition at every stage "can be expensive", and a triangular factorization of M_k is suggested instead (p. 23); for nonlinear equations it "would probably be worthwhile considering either method" (p. 29). The users named are the bound-constrained algorithm of Byrd, Lu and Nocedal (pp. 16–17) and Biegler–Nocedal–Schmid (p. 25).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (keep the classical engine, repair only the component that breaks) ⚠ variant: the BFGS/L-BFGS matrix is kept unchanged and only its representation is replaced, because the recursion is the component that fails in the constrained setting (p. 4). Unconstrained cost is kept equal (p. 13). There is no noise estimate or recovery path, so this is the linear-algebra form of the move.
- Method 6 (structure) ⚠ variant: structure is exploited inside the generic method's linear algebra (sparse constraint gradients, unit-vector bound constraints, cost proportional to nonzeros or free variables, pp. 15, 17–18), not by choosing a structure-exploiting method.
- New-pattern candidate: *representation → algorithm → software staircase*, i.e. a linear-algebra paper that makes a known matrix usable in new settings comes first, then the algorithm paper and code that use it (p. 3: "in this paper we focus on their use in limited memory methods"; users named pp. 16–17, 25; continued in S003 and S005 of this batch).
- New-pattern candidate: *operation counts as the evaluation protocol* for linear-algebra contributions, with equal-cost parity with the incumbent on its home problem (pp. 12–13, 19–20). This also fits Research Heuristic 5 (cheapest adequate option).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a product of rank-one projections into I − Y R^{−1} Sᵀ by induction (p. 5); it gives a compact outer-product form for any quasi-Newton sequence.
- Reorder an indefinite block middle matrix so that only a positive definite m×m Cholesky factorization is needed (p. 9).
- Store S_kᵀg_k and Y_kᵀg_k from the previous iteration so that the small matrices update in O(m²) rather than O(mn) (pp. 11–12).
- Show parity with the incumbent on its home problem (unconstrained, p. 13) before claiming gains elsewhere (sparse and constrained, pp. 15, 18).
- Compare two update formulas by finding the exact condition under which they coincide (pp. 26–28).

**Quotes**:
- "This is true even if limited memory approximations are used unless special care is taken in their representation and manipulation" (p. 2)
- "We believe that these new compact representations of quasiNewton matrices are of interest in their own right but in this paper we focus on their use in limited memory methods" (p. 3)

### S003 · A limited memory algorithm for bound constrained optimization (SIAM Journal on Scientific Computing 1995, DOI 10.1137/0916069)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 2–23 (abstract; introduction and outline; limited memory BFGS matrices; the generalized Cauchy point; methods for subspace minimization: direct primal, primal conjugate gradient, dual; numerical experiments with Tables 1–3 and discussion). References pp. 24–25 skimmed. Technical-report version (see page mapping). The numeric entries of Tables 1–3 (pp. 20–22) are illegible in the text layer; only problem names and F markers remain, so the results are taken from the authors' discussion on pp. 19 and 23.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Peihuang Lu, Ciyou Zhu (Northwestern) (pp. 1–2) · **Place in trajectory**: the algorithm built on S014's compact representation (p. 5). The code is described in the Zhu–Byrd–Lu–Nocedal report (p. 23, reference on p. 25), which is S005 in this batch. It is his most-cited work in the batch list (9,453 cites in the work list).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: min f(x) subject to l ≤ x ≤ u for large n, with only the gradient available and no second derivatives or knowledge of structure, using storage linear in n (p. 2). The entry is an existing framework plus a different engine. Conn–Gould–Toint and Moré–Toraldo already use gradient projection to choose the active set; the new algorithm is distinguished "by our use of line searches (as opposed to trust regions) but mainly by our use of limited memory BFGS matrices" (p. 3). Gradient projection is chosen because recent studies show good theory and practical efficiency (p. 3).
  - Transferable: enter by combining an established active-set framework (gradient projection) with an engine that the framework's authors did not use (L-BFGS), and say in one sentence which of the two differences is the main one (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Each iteration forms the quadratic model with the limited memory matrix B_k (p. 3). It computes the generalized Cauchy point, the first local minimizer of the model along the projected steepest descent path P(x_k − t g_k, l, u) (p. 3). It fixes the variables at bounds there and approximately minimizes the model over the free variables, ignoring their bounds and then truncating the move back into the box (p. 4). Finally a line search along d_k = x̄_{k+1} − x_k enforces sufficient decrease and attempts the curvature condition (p. 4). Kept: the BFGS update, line search, gradient projection. Changed: the compact form B = θI − WMWᵀ (p. 5), which lets the Cauchy search update its one-dimensional derivatives in O(m²) per breakpoint instead of O(n), by carrying two small vectors p = Wᵀd and c = Wᵀz (pp. 8–9). Only the first segment costs O(n) (p. 9). For subspace minimization three options are developed (direct primal via Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury, pp. 11–13; primal conjugate gradient, pp. 14–15; dual with Lagrange multipliers, pp. 15–17), because "Which of these is most appropriate seems problem dependent" (p. 11). Positive definiteness is maintained by discarding a pair when sᵀy ≤ eps‖y‖², without deleting the oldest pair (p. 6), since bounds can prevent the curvature condition (p. 6). Only BFGS is used, "since we have considerable computational experience in the unconstrained case indicating the limited memory BFGS performs well" (p. 6).
  - Transferable: when a step requires a sequential search over n breakpoints, find the small set of auxiliary vectors (here Wᵀd and Wᵀz) whose cheap updates carry the expensive inner products from segment to segment (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: Smooth f with gradient available; simple bounds only; large n; Hessian impractical to compute (p. 2). B_k is positive definite at every iteration, which gives descent: f(x_k) = m_k(x_k) > m_k(x^c) ≥ m_k(x̄_{k+1}), so g_kᵀd_k < 0 (p. 4). No convergence analysis is given: "In this paper we do not present any convergence analysis or study the possibility of zigzagging". The authors believe gradient-projection analyses should carry over and that zigzagging should only be a problem in the degenerate case (p. 4). Near-degeneracy is acknowledged as a weakness of the CG variant (p. 15).
  - Transferable: when you skip the convergence theory, say so in one sentence, name the analysis you expect to transfer, and name the case (degeneracy) where you expect trouble (p. 4).
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No convergence proofs. The analytic devices are cost devices: breakpoints sorted by heapsort (p. 7); the piecewise quadratic on each segment with parameters f, f′, f″ updated through the compact form (pp. 7–9); Algorithm CP with a total cost linear in n plus O(m²) per segment explored, plus n log n for the heapsort (pp. 9–10); the vector c reused to start subspace minimization (p. 10); Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury for the reduced inverse (p. 12); saving and updating YᵀZZᵀY, SᵀZZᵀY, SᵀZZᵀS when few variables change status, and using YᵀZZᵀY = YᵀY − YᵀAAᵀY when t ≫ n − t (p. 13); the identity between the primal matrix (I − θ⁻¹MWᵀZZᵀW) and the dual matrix (I − θMWᵀAAᵀW), which brings the two costs close (pp. 17–18); the descent argument from the Cauchy point (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when two formulations (primal and dual) need different-looking matrices, look for an identity that makes them the same matrix (pp. 17–18). The cost comparison then reduces to bookkeeping.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Codes compared: L-BFGS-B with the three subspace options and the subroutine SBMIN of LANCELOT with its BFGS, SR1 and exact-Hessian options, "In all these cases we used the default settings of LANCELOT" (p. 19). The same stopping test, on the projected gradient, was used for both codes (p. 18). Test set: all bound-constrained problems in CUTE (including some from MINPACK). Problems were discarded if n was small, if algorithms converged to different solutions, or if CUTE had too many instances of essentially one problem (p. 19). Measures: n, nfg (function and gradient together) for L-BFGS-B, nf for LANCELOT, total time; two failure codes (evaluation limit; next iterate too close to be distinct) (p. 19); n_act at the solution (p. 19). Tables 1–3 vary the code options, the memory m, and the subspace method (captions pp. 20–22; entries illegible). Findings as stated on p. 23: primal and dual differ only by rounding; CG is the least effective, and tests with other m gave "three more failures for the cg method than for the primal method"; L-BFGS-B usually needs more evaluations than LANCELOT-SR1, LANCELOT-BFGS is "clearly inferior" to its SR1 option, and in time L-BFGS-B is often the most efficient on the larger problems, the only ones "for which times are meaningful"; "To our surprise" exact-Hessian LANCELOT takes more time on many large problems, though it needs far fewer evaluations. A development episode is reported: the first implementation used a backtracking line search, skipped many BFGS updates and performed very poorly on some problems. That early version was compared with the unconstrained L-BFGS code on unconstrained problems, and L-BFGS was superior on a significant number of problems. Switching to the Moré–Thuente routine, with the maximum step set to the closest bound, made skipped updates rare (pp. 18–19). Unexplained failures to locate the solution accurately are printed: "The reasons for this are not clear to us but are being investigated" (p. 23).
  - Transferable: when an extended code underperforms, run it on its predecessor's home problem class against the predecessor (here the early bound-constrained version on unconstrained problems vs L-BFGS, p. 19). A gap there isolates a regression in a shared component (the line search).
  - Transferable: report CPU time only for problems large enough that time is meaningful, and say so (p. 23); count evaluations everywhere.
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends L-BFGS (Liu–Nocedal; Gilbert–Lemaréchal) to bounds with "similar computational demands" (p. 4). It positions itself against Conn–Gould–Toint and Moré–Toraldo by naming the two differences (p. 3). LANCELOT is used "simply as a benchmark", with a disclaimer that the tests "are not intended to establish the superiority" of either code, since "LANCELOT is tailored for sparse or partially separable problems whereas the limited memory method is well suited for unstructured or dense problems" (p. 23). A finding implicitly revises the 1992 survey: S019 called backtracking "well-suited for constrained problems" (S019 p. 8), while here backtracking was found to degrade BFGS significantly (p. 19).
  - Transferable: when your benchmark rival is designed for a different problem class, say so next to the results and state which class each code is built for (p. 23).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Procedural and cost-annotated: every algorithm step carries its operation count (pp. 9–10, 13, 14, 16–17). Hedging is candid and placed where the evidence is weak: "seems problem dependent" (p. 11), "To our surprise" (p. 23), "The reasons for this are not clear to us" (p. 23). The conclusion separates what the algorithm is good for (low cost per iteration, modest storage, large unstructured dense Hessians) from where it "is less likely to be competitive" (exact Hessian available, exploitable structure) (p. 23).
  - Transferable: close the experiments with a two-sided verdict, "main advantages are ..." and "less likely to be competitive ...", both tied to problem features (p. 23).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: algorithm plus implementation (algorithmic and software levels). It keeps most of L-BFGS's efficiency while adding bounds, at the cost of a considerably more complex code (p. 23). Limits printed: no convergence analysis (p. 4); CG subspace option least effective (p. 23); inaccurate final location and stalls not understood (p. 23); not competitive in evaluations when Hessians or structure are available (p. 23). Code available from the authors (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (keep the classical engine; repair only the component that breaks) ✅ evidence (non-noise case): L-BFGS is kept. The components that break under bounds are named and replaced one at a time: the curvature condition can fail, so pairs are skipped without dropping the oldest (p. 6); the backtracking line search caused skipped updates and was replaced by Moré–Thuente with the step capped at the nearest bound (pp. 18–19). The failure was isolated by comparing with the unconstrained predecessor (p. 19).
- Method 4 (open choices become options) ✅ evidence: the undecided subspace-minimization choice ("seems problem dependent", p. 11) is implemented in three variants and benchmarked (p. 23). S005 then exposes it as the isbmin option (S005 p. 5). Unexplained failures are recorded as under investigation (p. 23).
- Method 6 (structure beats your own brand) ✅ evidence: the paper says where its own method should lose: it is "less likely to be competitive" when an exact Hessian is available or structure can be exploited, and it assigns LANCELOT to partially separable problems (p. 23).
- Method 1 (handicapped, method-level benchmarking) ⚠ variant: the stopping test is aligned on the projected gradient (p. 18), the rival is run in three Hessian options (p. 19), losses in evaluations are printed (p. 23) and ranking is disclaimed (p. 23). But the rival runs only at defaults with no option search (p. 23), while the authors' side is shown in several variants and memory sizes (Tables 2–3, pp. 21–22). This is an early, weaker form of the 2003 protocol in SKILL.md.
- New-pattern candidate: *predecessor as control*, i.e. run the extended code on the predecessor's problem class against the predecessor, to isolate regressions in shared components (p. 19; also S005 pp. 1–2).
- New-pattern candidate: *representation → algorithm → software staircase* (p. 5 builds on S014; code described in the report cited on p. 23 = S005).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Carry the expensive inner products along a piecewise search with a few small auxiliary vectors (Wᵀd, Wᵀz), so each breakpoint after the first costs O(m²) (pp. 8–9).
- Maintain positive definiteness under constraints by skipping a pair that fails sᵀy > eps‖y‖², without deleting the oldest pair (p. 6).
- Cap the line search's maximum step at the nearest bound so a Wolfe line search can still be used inside a box (pp. 18–19).
- Use the unconstrained predecessor as a control on unconstrained problems to locate a regression (p. 19).
- Report CPU time only where problems are large enough for it to mean something, and evaluations everywhere (p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "In contrast when using the new line search the update is very rarely skipped in our tests and the performance of the code was markedly better" (p. 19)
- "The tests described here are not intended to establish the superiority of LANCELOT or of the new limited memory algorithm" (p. 23)

### S005 · Algorithm 778: L-BFGS-B: Fortran subroutines for large-scale bound-constrained optimization (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 1997, DOI 10.1145/279232.279236)
**Read level**: full. All of pp. 1–16: abstract; introduction; how to use the routine (parameter list); the drivers; termination and error messages; implementation (failure devices, machine and scale dependencies, a technical point on the step computation); numerical results with Tables 1–6 and their footnotes; acknowledgements. References pp. 16–17 skimmed. Northwestern report version (see page mapping). Table entries (pp. 10–15) are illegible except problem names and the C/F markers, so results are taken from the discussion on pp. 8–9.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ciyou Zhu, Richard H. Byrd (Colorado), Peihuang Lu (p. 1); thanks Brett Averick and Jorge Moré, whose MINPACK codes the code follows in ideas and style (p. 16) · **Place in trajectory**: the released software for the S003 algorithm ("described in detail in", p. 2), with the S014 compact form as its "novel feature" (p. 2). It is the last step of the staircase in this batch.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Users need a code. The target is a nonlinear f of n variables with optional simple bounds, gradient supplied, no Hessian knowledge, especially when the Hessian is hard to compute or dense (p. 2). The gap in software is named: L-BFGS-B "is at present the only limited memory quasiNewton algorithm capable of handling bounds on the variables"; other published codes are unconstrained only, and nonlinear CG "has not been adequately extended to handle bounds" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: in a software paper, name the precise gap among published codes (here no limited memory code with bounds, p. 2) and the existing code your new one could replace.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Package the S003 algorithm (gradient projection to choose the active set, then subspace minimization of the L-BFGS model, then Moré–Thuente line search, p. 2) behind reverse communication, so that "the user complete control" over function and gradient evaluation is kept (p. 3). The control variable task returns FG, NEW_X, CONV, ABNO, ERROR and can be set to STOP by the user (p. 4). Changes from S003: the primal and dual subspace computations were unified, since the two matrices are identical and can be written as the inverse of one block matrix factorized through Cholesky of submatrices, "and as a result the performance of the two methods is now similar" (p. 8). The undecided subspace method becomes a user parameter, isbmin: primal by default, dual, or conjugate gradient (p. 5). Robustness devices were added (p. 7, see D4). It is claimed to "supersede LBFGS" on unconstrained problems except for its extra storage (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a design choice is still undecided in the algorithm paper, ship it as a documented parameter with a stated default (isbmin, p. 5) and a driver that runs all options on a test set (driver4, p. 6).
- **D3 Assumptions and model**: User supplies f and g in double precision Fortran 77 (p. 3). No Hessian or structure information (p. 2). The memory m is user-chosen within a recommended small range; small values "are not recommended" below a threshold and large ones cost time (p. 3; digits lost). Machine precision is used only twice, in the factr stopping test and in the BFGS skipping criterion, which compares yᵀs with gᵀs scaled by machine precision (exact form lost in extraction) (p. 7). Scale: invariant to scalar multiples of variables and objective except in the first iteration and in the factr test (p. 7); "the limited memory algorithm itself is not invariant to linear transformations in the variables" (p. 7).
  - Transferable: list every place where machine precision or scaling enters the code (here exactly two, p. 7). Users then know which tolerances are machine- or scale-dependent.
- **D4 Analysis and proof devices**: No proofs. The technical devices are robustness and accounting devices. (1) Recovery: if the line search cannot find sufficient decrease after a set number of evaluations, all correction pairs are discarded and the iteration restarts along steepest descent; a second failure terminates with ABNORMAL TERMINATION IN LINE SEARCH (pp. 6–7). (2) The same restart is used if the L-BFGS matrix or a submatrix becomes singular or indefinite, or if the direction is not a descent direction (p. 7). The authors stress that these difficulties "occur only when rounding errors begin to dominate the computation" (p. 7). (3) Skipped updates are counted in isave for the user (p. 7). (4) The primal/dual identity as a single block inverse (p. 8).
  - Transferable: give every recovery a counter or flag the user can read, such as skipped BFGS updates in isave and the line-search exit string (pp. 6–7). Robustness devices then show up in the logs instead of hiding failures.
- **D5 Numerical experiments**: Test set: CUTE bound-constrained problems and unconstrained problems above a size threshold (p. 8; thresholds lost). Benchmark: LANCELOT's SR1 and exact-Hessian options "using all its default options" (p. 8). One stopping test for all runs, the projected-gradient norm below a fixed tolerance (p. 8). Measures: nfg for L-BFGS-B, nf for LANCELOT, CPU time; nbnd taken from LANCELOT-SR1's solution (p. 8). Termination is coded in footnotes: evaluation limit reached; no further progress in f; and two markers, C (gradient test not met but final f at least as good as LANCELOT-SR1's) and F (gradient test not met and f worse) (pp. 10–11). Further markers flag LANCELOT-SR1 converging elsewhere or failing (p. 11). Findings stated in the text: "LBFGSB is a competitive code which is remarkable", since it uses no specific knowledge of the objective function; it uses more evaluations than LANCELOT; CPU times are "comparable though with great variability" (p. 8). C runs are not counted as failures: "We do not interpret these as failures of the algorithm" (p. 9). Increasing m "improves the reliability of the algorithm", reduces evaluations inconsistently, and increases CPU time in most cases (p. 9). With the unified implementation primal and dual take similar time and CG takes more on problems where time matters (p. 9). The test driver driver4 is shipped for testing and profiling the code (p. 6).
  - Transferable: grade every non-converged run against the rival's final objective value with explicit markers (C/F) instead of a bare failure count (pp. 10–11). But see the Method 1 contradiction below: count them as failures too.
- **D6 Positioning**: An extension of L-BFGS (Harwell VA15); "the two codes perform similarly on unconstrained problems" (pp. 1–2), and the only limited memory code for bounds (p. 2). It follows MINPACK in ideas and style (p. 16). Compared with LANCELOT, it is a structure-free method set against a structure-exploiting package (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: User-manual voice with direct recommendations: "We recommend that every user of LBFGSB read this driver" (p. 6); the recommended m range (p. 3); typical factr values for low, moderate and extremely high accuracy (p. 4). The introduction lists numbered advantages, (i) easy to use, (ii) modest controllable storage, (iii) low iteration cost independent of the function's properties, and numbered drawbacks, (i) not rapidly convergent, (ii) may fail to reach high accuracy on highly ill-conditioned problems, (iii) cannot use structure (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: in a software paper, write a numbered "drawbacks" list next to the numbered advantages, before the parameter documentation (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: software (released by anonymous ftp, p. 3) with documented parameters, drivers, error messages and robustness devices. Limits printed: the three drawbacks (p. 3); scale dependence of the factr test (p. 6) and of the first step (p. 7); not invariant to linear transformations (p. 7); difficulty reducing the projected gradient even when f is very good, which "deserves further study" (p. 9).
