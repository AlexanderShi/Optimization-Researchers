### S041 · Price-and-verify: a new algorithm for recursive circle packing using Dantzig–Wolfe decomposition (Annals of Operations Research 2020, DOI 10.1007/s10479-018-3115-5; arXiv 1702.07799)
**Read level**: full.
- Read in full: main text pp. 3-28 (Algorithms 1-2, Tables 1-5, Figures 1-9), acknowledgements and references pp. 29-31.
- Skimmed: appendix Table 6 (pp. 32-48); its 800 rows were recounted by script against the aggregates in the text.
- The file is the preprint ZIB-Report 17-07 / arXiv v2 titled "Exact Methods for Recursive Circle Packing" (pp. 1, 3). Its cover gives the journal title and DOI (p. 1).
- First-page check: title, and Gleixner among the authors (p. 3).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Stephen J. Maher, Benjamin Müller (ZIB), João Pedro Pedroso (Universidade do Porto); alphabetical byline, Gleixner first (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: a decomposition application from the MODAL period (acknowledgements, p. 29), outside the exact-arithmetic line.
- Here "exact" means globally optimal, computed with floating-point solvers. Pedroso et al. had introduced the problem (ref. [27], p. 3) and a local search heuristic for it (p. 4).
- SKILL.md's Heuristic 2 cites this report as [CP17]. This v2 text has "strong dual bounds" in its abstract (p. 3), while the report title still says "Exact Methods" (pp. 1, 3) and the journal title on the cover drops "Exact" (p. 1). v1 was not read.
- Voice: group. There is no contribution statement, so nothing marks a sentence as his.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem comes from an application. Tubes are shipped with smaller tubes nested inside larger ones to cut shipping costs (p. 5). The Recursive Circle Packing Problem (RCPP) asks for the fewest rectangles of the same size that hold all rings (p. 3). The paper enters through a chain of failures, each one measured:
  1. The compact MINLP of Pedroso et al. "is not of practical relevance" (p. 4). SCIP 3.2.1 and BARON 16.8.24 fail on the smallest instance (54 rings) within several hours (p. 7). The symmetry-breaking constraints (8)-(10) "had no impact on the solvability" (p. 8).
  2. Diagnosis: O(kn^2+n^3) nonconvex constraints, big-M binaries with weak LP relaxations, and symmetry left over after (8)-(10) (p. 8).
  3. A first Dantzig-Wolfe (cutting-stock) reformulation (11) fails too: none of its pricing problems "could be solved to optimality or even generate an improving column" in two hours (p. 9).
  - Transferable: open a reformulation paper with the failure of the obvious formulations, each with its measured failure (solvers and versions, time, the strengthening that did not help), before presenting the new model (pp. 7-9).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: The recursion moves from the pricing problems into the master.
  - Circular patterns (one-level packings of circles into a ring) are enumerated up front, and rectangular patterns are generated by pricing.
  - The master (13) counts patterns, so "the placement of rings into other rings—is modeled by counting" (p. 11).
  - The aim is to "balance the complexity of both problems, which is crucial when using a column generation algorithm" (p. 9; again p. 16).

  Kept from the classics: the Gilmore-Gomory cutting-stock structure (p. 4) and the multi-stage cutting-stock form of Muter et al. (p. 12). Changed: the subproblems are nonconvex NLP and MINLP circle-packing problems (pp. 13-14, 17).

  Price-and-verify (Alg. 2, p. 21) adds a verification step for undecided candidates, the circular patterns whose packability the enumeration could not settle. Such a candidate is checked only when it takes a nonzero value in the LP solution, and with larger working limits than in the enumeration (p. 20). Branch-and-price with the Vanderbeck-Wolsey branching rule is "deemed impractical", because most pricing problems cannot be solved even at the root (p. 14). Pricing therefore runs at the root only (price-and-branch, p. 20).
  - Transferable: when every subproblem is hard, choose the decomposition that takes the combinatorial recursion out of the subproblems and leaves the master a counting problem, and say that this balance is the design criterion (pp. 9, 16).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The original model is the nonconvex MINLP (7). It has binaries z, w, u, quadratic non-overlap constraints (4)-(5) and a big-M containment constraint (6), with M_ij set to the largest distance between two rings in the rectangle (pp. 6-7). The reformulations (11) and (13) are pure IPs with exponentially many columns (pp. 8, 12).
  - Assumptions: r_t ≤ R_t ≤ min{W, H}, ring types sorted by external radius (p. 6), and rectangles of the same size (p. 3).
  - Correctness rests on CP* ⊆ CPfeas ∪ CPunknown: every non-dominated circular pattern was either verified or kept as a candidate (p. 18).
  - Data: 9 real tube-industry instances from ref. [27], and 800 synthetic "rand" instances (pp. 22-23).
    - The rand set is a full grid with one instance per 4-tuple: T ∈ {3, 4, 5, 10} ring types, a radius ratio α, a size ratio β (both from 2.0 to 4.7 in steps of 0.3), a demand level γ ∈ {5, 10}, and W = H = 10.
    - All instances are public on GitHub (p. 23).
  - Slips in the definitions (p. 22): α is called "the maximum external radii ratio" but is defined as max_t r_t / min_t R_t, an internal over an external radius; β is introduced once via the "maximum volume" of a ring and then defined by radius.
  - Transferable: to show what drives difficulty, generate a full grid over the parameters you suspect, one instance per combination, and publish it, rather than collecting a convenience sample (pp. 22-23).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: The paper's components and proof devices:
  1. *EnumeratePatterns* (Alg. 1, p. 18). Candidates are skipped by dominance against patterns already shown infeasible or feasible. The rest go to a nonconvex verification NLP (18), and every candidate ends as feasible, infeasible, or unknown (time or memory limit) (pp. 17-18). In (18), the ordering constraints x_i ≤ x_j on equal circles (19) plus an auxiliary objective min x_i make "it easier for the NLP solver to prove infeasibility" (p. 17).
  2. *Valid bounds without complete verification*.
     - CPfeas alone gives a valid primal bound, and CPfeas ∪ CPunknown gives a valid relaxation (p. 18).
     - When pricing hits its limit, Theorem 2 (Farley; Vance et al.) still gives a dual bound, from any valid dual bound z_Pricing of the pricing problem: ν_RMP / (1 − z_Pricing) ≤ OPT (rounding brackets lost in extraction; p. 19).
     - If unverified candidates stay positive in the LP, all are fixed to zero, and the LP then no longer gives a valid dual bound (pp. 20-21).
  3. *Theorem 1*: LP(PDW) = LP(DW) (pp. 14-16).
     - The proof builds an extended LP (17) over "mixed" rectangles that hold both rings and circles. It contains both formulations, so its value Z* is at most either LP bound.
     - The reverse inequalities are shown by contradiction. Take an optimal solution that minimizes µ (rings in rectangles) or ν (use of circular patterns); an exchange that keeps the objective value lowers that count (pp. 15-16).
     - The second part refers to an optimal solution "to (11)" where (17) must be meant (p. 16, as extracted).
     - The theorem shows that the gain from (13) is symmetry breaking, not a stronger bound (p. 16).
  4. *Implementation*:
     - Two SCIP plugins: a pricer, and a constraint handler that does the dynamic verification (p. 21).
     - A greedy heuristic runs before every NLP or pricing solve; it packs each circle at the left-most, then lowest possible position (p. 21).
     - Working limits (p. 22): 10 s per NLP in Alg. 1, and greedy only after 1200 s; 120 s per verification during pricing, stopping after 2400 s; 300 s per pricing problem; 2 h in total.
  5. *Numerical safeguards*: none stated. "Verified" means a global floating-point NLP or MINLP solve. Tolerances are never discussed: there is no occurrence of "tolerance", "numeric", "precision" or "rounding" outside one reference title.
  6. *My reading, not the paper's claim*: Definition 1 defines dominance by "the standard lexicographical order" (p. 17). But the replacement argument ("dominated patterns can be replaced", p. 17) and the pruning in line 4 of Alg. 1 (p. 18) hold for the componentwise order. Under the lexicographic order, (3,(2,0,0)) would dominate (3,(0,1,0)) among the patterns of Fig. 3 (p. 10), although the two hold different circle types. The text does not say which order the code uses.
  - Transferable: when subproblems run under working limits, keep two pattern pools: verified (for valid primal solutions), and verified plus undecided (for a valid relaxation). Spend expensive verification only on undecided items that the current LP actually uses. Give the enumeration small limits and the verification inside the loop larger ones (pp. 18, 20).
  - Transferable: to show that a new decomposition keeps the LP bound, embed both formulations in one extended LP, then take a counterexample that minimizes a counting function and apply an exchange that keeps the objective value (pp. 14-16).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Three experiments, each stated before the results (p. 22):
  - (i) for which instances the enumeration finds all non-dominated circular patterns within 2 h;
  - (ii) whether the method solves instances to global optimality, and how that depends on instance structure;
  - (iii) primal bounds against the GRASP heuristic of ref. [27], with 3 h for both.

  Setup (pp. 22-23):
  - One job per node, "to safeguard against a potential mutual slowdown of parallel processes", on Xeon E5-2660 v3 nodes with 48 GB (p. 23).
  - Software: SCIP 5.0 with CPLEX 12.7.1.0, CppAD and Ipopt 3.12.5 with MUMPS 4.10.0 (p. 23).
  - GRASP runs as the authors' Python code. The paper states the mismatch ("an implementation of GRASP would be much faster" in C) and argues why it does not matter: only solution quality is compared, and GRASP finds its best solutions in the first few minutes (p. 22).

  Metrics:
  - enumeration time as a shifted geometric mean with shift 1.0 (p. 23);
  - the gap (primal − dual)/dual, counted by bracket (pp. 24-25);
  - the primal bound relative to GRASP, as a shifted geometric mean of ratios with the shift not given (p. 27);
  - node counts (p. 26).

  Single runs, with no seeds or permutations.

  Results:
  - The enumeration completes on 392 of 800 instances, and on none with ratio > 3.5 (Table 1, p. 24).
  - 287 instances are solved to optimality; the gap brackets hold 45 / 370 / 97 / 1 instances (Table 2, p. 25).
  - Against GRASP: better on 356 instances, worse on 33, and 2.7% better on average (pp. 27-28).
  - Real set: only the three one-rectangle instances are solved, and the dual bounds are at least 1.6 times the volume bound (pp. 26-27).
  - Of the instances that need more than one node, only 15 are solved (p. 26).

  Failures are reported: 320 instances are never enumerated (p. 24); on s03i3 the method needs three rectangles more than GRASP (p. 26); the mean deterioration on the 33 losses is given (p. 27).

  My recount of Table 6 (pp. 32-48):
  - It reproduces 392, 287, the gap brackets, 356/33 and the 15.
  - It finds 376 instances with more than one node (424 with one), where the text says 367 (p. 26).
  - Two further text slips: "46.3% ... gaps between 25-100%", where Fig. 8 and Table 2 give 46.3% for 25-50% and 12.1% for 50-100% (pp. 24-25); and "only on 33% of the instances worse" for 33 instances (p. 27).
  - Transferable: publish the per-instance table as an appendix, so that every aggregate in the text can be recounted, and report losses with their mean size next to the wins (pp. 27-28, 32-48).
  - Transferable: when your baseline runs in a slower language or setting, say so, and measure what that does not affect: solution quality, and when the baseline finds its best solution (p. 22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Two pools, verified and verified-plus-undecided, give a valid primal bound and a valid relaxation when verification runs under limits (p. 18).

- When pricing cannot be solved to optimality, report the Farley bound from a valid dual bound of the pricing problem instead of no bound (p. 19).
- To prove that two reformulations have the same LP bound, build an extended LP that contains both and apply optimality-preserving exchanges to a minimal counterexample (pp. 14-16).
- Use a full parameter grid with one instance per combination as the test set, publish it, and report results along each parameter (pp. 22-25).
- Publish per-instance results so that every aggregate can be recounted (pp. 32-48).

### S068 · First experiments with structure-aware presolving for a parallel interior-point method (Operations Research Proceedings 2019, 2020, DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-48439-2_13; arXiv 1908.00796)
**Read level**: full.
- Read in full: pp. 1-8 (covers, abstract, §§1-3, Table 1, Figures 1-3, conclusion, acknowledgements, references).
- The file is ZIB-Report 19-39 = arXiv 1908.00796v1, dated July 26, 2019 (pp. 1, 3).
- First-page check: title, and Gleixner first on the byline (p. 3).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Nils-Christian Kempke, Thorsten Koch, Daniel Rehfeldt, Svenja Uslu (all ZIB); alphabetical byline (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: HPC presolve for an LP solver.
- Funding (acknowledgements, p. 8): the energy-systems project BEAM-ME and MODAL, with computing time on JUWELS.
- It extends the group's own extension of PIPS-IPM (ref. [3], p. 3).
- SKILL.md cites this report as [SAP19] for Taste 8 ("Application structure becomes a generic solver component"), and lists a later distributed-presolving paper [DSAP26].
- The 2019 byline is alphabetical, two years after the point where SKILL.md's Common Misreading 4 says bylines "were alphabetical until 2017".
- Voice: group. Nothing marks Gleixner's share.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a conflict between two good things: "beneficial presolving reductions sometimes destroy the special structure of a given problem" (abstract, p. 3), the structure that a structure-exploiting solver needs.
  - The target is block-diagonal LPs from energy-system models, with both linking variables and linking constraints (Fig. 1, pp. 3-4).
  - These LPs are distributed over MPI processes, so "Each process only knows part of the entire problem" (p. 4).
  - The paper frames the engineering, not the mathematics, as the difficulty (abstract, p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a downstream solver depends on structure, state the conflict between the standard reductions and that structure as the problem. "Structure-preserving" then becomes a testable design requirement (pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Four known presolve methods are re-implemented inside the distributed data layout of PIPS-IPM and run in a fixed order: singleton rows, bound tightening, parallel and nearly parallel rows, and model cleanup, all taken from refs. [1, 2, 6] (p. 4). Nothing is new mathematically. What is new:
  - reductions are split into local ones (one block, no communication) and global ones (several blocks, communication);
  - the amount and frequency of communication are kept to a minimum (pp. 4-5).

  Worked example (pp. 5-6): singleton linking rows are detected without asking all processes.
  - Every process keeps nonzero counters for its own block and for block 0, and buffers its changes.
  - Between synchronization points the global counters "provide only an upper bound". Changes are broadcast when a presolver that uses the counters is entered (p. 5).
  - Bound changes on linking variables use a similar synchronization (p. 6).
  - Transferable: in distributed preprocessing, keep stale global counters that remain safe one-sided bounds (upper bounds on nonzeros), and synchronize them only when a routine that needs them starts, not after every change (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pure LP in block-diagonal form, with linking variables x_0 and linking constraints (equalities and ranged inequalities), and bounds in the extended reals (Fig. 1, pp. 3-4).
  - Data model (p. 4): one process stores block i (A_i, B_i, C_i, D_i, F_i, G_i, with its vectors and bounds); block 0 is replicated on every process; blocks may be grouped to reduce N.
  - Allowed reductions (p. 4): delete a row, column or entry; modify bounds, sides and objective coefficients.
  - Correctness: no argument is stated beyond the reductions being standard (p. 4).
  - Instances: 12 real energy-system models, elmod (ref. [7]) and oms and yssp (ref. [4]), with N = 120 to 876 blocks and up to about 717 million nonzeros (Table 1, p. 6).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Presolve only: no branching, no cuts, no tree. The design devices:
  - the split into local and global reductions (p. 4);
  - buffered counters that stay valid as upper bounds (p. 5);
  - a singleton row whose entry lies in a non-linking part is deleted at once, while a change to a linking part notifies all processes (pp. 5-6);
  - model cleanup at the start and again in the sequence, including "elimination of negligibly small entries" (p. 4). This is the only numerical safeguard mentioned, and no threshold is given.

  There are no proofs, and no invariant beyond the counter bound (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Two experiments, each with its goal stated (p. 6).
  1. *Performance and impact against SoPlex*, "the sequential, source-open solver", with SoPlex's presolve routines that PIPS-IPM lacks turned off (p. 6). Table 1 reports the presolve times t_1 (one MPI process), t_N (the maximal number of processes) and t_S (SoPlex), and the nonzeros before and after (p. 6).
  2. *Scalability*, with varying numbers of MPI processes and the speed-up S_n = t_1/t_n (pp. 6-7).

  Hardware (p. 6):
  - PIPS-IPM ran on JUWELS: two Intel Xeon Skylake 8168 processors with 24 cores each, 2.70 GHz, 96 GB.
  - SoPlex "had to" run on a different ZIB machine (Xeon E7-8880 v4, 2.2 GHz, 2 TB), because reading and presolving with SoPlex was too slow on JUWELS.
  - The effect of the different machines is not discussed.

  Single runs, no seeds, and no other parallel presolver as a baseline.

  Results:
  - Sequential structure-aware presolve beats SoPlex except on the two smallest instances (< 3 million nonzeros). SoPlex cannot process the four largest (> 200 million) within 24 h (pp. 6-7).
  - The reduction is "very similar", with "an average deviation of less than 2%", and the nonzeros drop by about 16% on average (p. 7).
  - Parallel presolve is faster on every instance except oms 3 (p. 7).
  - Scalability: elmod 2 scales almost linearly (S_146 ≈ 114), while yssp 2 (S_50 ≈ 36) and oms 4 (S_60 ≈ 31) are sublinear, and runtimes rise again for more processes. The paper explains this by the linking constraints and a "sweet spot" (p. 7).
  - Interior-point time on elmod 1 drops from about 780 to about 380 s (p. 8).

  My recount of Table 1:
  - The 16.2% mean reduction holds.
  - PIPS-IPM leaves fewer nonzeros than SoPlex on all eight instances that both finish.
  - The mean deviation is 2.4% relative to the input nonzeros and 2.7% relative to SoPlex's output, not under 2%. The text does not define the deviation.

  2. SCIP's floating-point heuristics with an exact repair step: fix the integer variables, rounding slightly fractional values, then solve an exact LP over the continuous rest (p. 7).
  3. SoPlex's LP iterative refinement instead of QSopt_ex's precision boosting (p. 7).
  4. Integration details (pp. 7-8): an objective limit for the floating-point LP, padded by "a small amount computed from the statistically observed bounding error so far"; and a safe floating-point feasibility check by running error analysis before any exact check.

  Strategy changes:
  - an exact LP at every 5 depth levels (p. 9);
  - project-and-shift no longer restricted to instances with fewer than 10,000 nonzeros (p. 10);
  - bound-shift switched off when its success rate falls below 20% (p. 10).
  - Transferable: give every heuristic that works on approximate data an exact repair. Fix the discrete part from the approximate solution, then solve the continuous remainder exactly, under a working limit tied to how often the exact solver is called for other purposes (pp. 7, 12).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The MIP is min{c^T x | Ax ≥ b, x ∈ Q^n, x_i ∈ Z for i ∈ I} with rational data, plus a floating-point approximation made of the componentwise closest representable numbers (p. 6).
  - The bounding methods have assumptions. Bound-shift needs finite bounds on all variables (p. 5). Project-and-shift needs an interior point or ray of the dual LP, computed once by an exact auxiliary LP (pp. 5-6).
  - Heuristics must not change the model, for example by adding no-good constraints (p. 7).
  - The rational presolve uses the same acceptance thresholds as the floating-point presolve, but zero comparison and feasibility tolerances (p. 11).
  - Certificates exclude presolve, because PaPILO cannot produce them yet (p. 13).

- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: The proof device is a running error analysis (Higham, Alg. 3.2), adjusted for the roundoff in x̄* − x* and ā − a (p. 8).
  - If s − µ ≥ b_j, the row is guaranteed to be satisfied; if s + µ ≤ b_j, it is certainly violated; otherwise the activity is recomputed exactly.
  - Directed rounding is named as a tighter but slightly costlier alternative. The choice is justified empirically: most constraints are either tight, where an exact check cannot be avoided, or satisfied or violated by more than µ.

  Further devices:
  - objective-limit padding, with a re-solve without the limit when safe bounding fails (pp. 7-8);
  - limits on the repair heuristic: it runs only if it has been called at most half as often as the exact LPs for bounding, and it is disabled above 80% continuous variables (p. 12);

  - certificates for bound-shift, whose dual multipliers must be computed in a postprocessing step (pp. 13-14).

  There are no theorems.
  - Transferable: before an exact check, run a floating-point evaluation with a rigorous error bound µ, and go exact only for the undecided band |s − b| < µ (p. 8).

### D003 · An Algorithm-Independent Measure of Progress for Linear Constraint Propagation (CP 2021, DOI 10.4230/lipics.cp.2021.52; arXiv 2106.07573)
**Read level**: full.
- Read in full: pp. 1-15 (abstract, §§1-6, Algorithm 1, Definitions 1-5, Observations 1-2, Corollary 1, Table 1, Figures 1-2, references).
- The file is arXiv 2106.07573v3 of 24 Aug 2021 (stamp, p. 1), not the LIPIcs typesetting. It is SKILL.md's [CP21], which SKILL.md cites by its LIPIcs DOI.
- First-page check: title, and Gleixner second of three authors (p. 1).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Boro Sofranac (TU Berlin and ZIB), first; Sebastian Pokutta (TU Berlin and ZIB), third; Gleixner (HTW Berlin and ZIB), second (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the follow-up to the authors' GPU-parallel propagation paper (ref. [26], IA3 2020), within SKILL.md's "2020–2026: GPU methods" line ([CP21]).
- SKILL.md uses it, as group work, for Method 1: a progress measure was needed because tolerance-based stopping made propagation codes incomparable.
- Voice: student-first, non-alphabetical byline, supervised work. Nothing marks Gleixner's share.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Bounds propagation in practice is iterative, may converge only in the limit, and is stopped by tolerance-based criteria (pp. 1-2, 4). This makes it hard to compare implementations fairly, "Most significantly" because unbounded variable domains make the relative size of reductions hard to quantify (abstract, p. 1).
  - The entry is a concern that Belotti et al. stated but never quantified: stopping after small improvements "might in principle overlook the possibility of a larger improvement later on". "In their paper, no attempt is made to quantify this statement" (p. 5).
  - Three motivations are listed (p. 5): estimate this "premature stalling"; compare algorithms that take different bound sequences and are stopped early; design stopping criteria.
  - Transferable: turn a plausible caveat in a predecessor's paper ("might in principle ...") into a research question, by building the instrument that can count it (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Fix reference points that do not depend on the algorithm. The start bounds and the fixed point (unique, ref. [9]) are the same for every iterative bounds tightening algorithm (IBTA) (pp. 4, 7).

  Progress splits into two processes, measured separately (pp. 6, 9):
  - reducing infinite bounds to finite ones: P_inf = n_current/n_total, the share of the n_total infinite-to-finite reductions done so far (eq. 9);
  - finite improvements: P_fin, the relative progress of each bound from its "weakest" finite value to its limit value, summed (eqs. 10-11) and normalized by a maximum that can be precomputed (12).

  The first finite values cannot serve as the reference. A sequential algorithm applies changes immediately and a parallel one uses older bounds, so the two reach different first values, which "cannot be used safely to compare finite domain reductions across different implementations" (p. 7). Weakest bounds (Definition 4) are therefore computed by a propagation-like fixed-point procedure that keeps the weakest finite candidate (Alg. 1, pp. 7-8).
  - Transferable: to compare algorithms that take different paths to the same fixed point, measure progress between end points that do not depend on the algorithm. Score qualitatively different phases (infinite to finite, finite improvement) separately, and compare algorithms by the time each needs to reach the same progress (pp. 5, 7, 9, 12).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The MIP (1) has bounds in the extended reals, and linear constraints β_low ≤ a^T x ≤ β_up. Continuous and integer variables and initially unbounded domains are explicitly included (pp. 1, 3). Candidates for integer variables are rounded (eq. 7, p. 4).
  - Assumptions used: the fixed point is unique, and convergence may take infinitely many rounds (refs. [8, 9], p. 4). IBTAs never relax bounds, which makes both scores non-decreasing (p. 9).
  - For the stalling analysis, the progress function P is assumed continuous and twice differentiable. In practice it is sampled once per round and interpolated linearly (pp. 10-11).
  - Test set: MIPLIB 2017's 1065 instances, cut to 540 by counted exclusions (p. 10):
    - 133 that the file reader cannot read;
    - 72 where the two codes reach different fixed points "due to e.g., numerical difficulties";
    - 2 that hit the 100-iteration limit;
    - 310 with no bound change;
    - 8 with inconsistent behaviour.
  - Arithmetic is double precision (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Proof devices:
  - Observation 2: whether an infinite bound can become finite depends only on the signs of the coefficients and on whether the other bounds and sides are finite, not on their values. The proof goes by cases on (6a)/(6b) (p. 6).
  - Corollary 1: infinite-to-finite reductions happen only in the first c rounds, and "By pigeonhole principle" c is at most the number of initially infinite bounds (p. 6).
  - Monotonicity of P_inf and P_fin, because bounds are never relaxed (p. 9).
  - Algorithm 1 (weakest bounds) is described with a constraint-marking speed-up that is "not necessary for the correctness" (p. 7). Its guarantees appear only in the Outlook (finite where some IBTA can produce a finite bound; no IBTA produces a weaker one), and this version does not prove them (pp. 12-13).
  - My check, not the paper's claim. Take x1 ≥ x3, x1 ≥ x2 − 1, x2 ≥ x1 − 1, with x3 = 0 and x1, x2 initially unbounded below. In a scratch simulation, the update rule of lines 18-23 (p. 8) keeps weakening ℓ1 and ℓ2 by 2 per round (0, −2, −4, ...). Every IBTA's first finite values are 0 and −1. So on such cycles the procedure as written neither terminates nor gives the tightest weakest bounds. The paper concedes only the tightness question (pp. 12-13).

  Implementation devices:
  - The limit bounds come from any correct propagation run to the fixed point (p. 10).
  - The measure is computed in one run, and the timing is taken in a second run without it, because measuring "can considerably slow down the execution" (p. 10).
  - Plateau conventions: the score of the last round counts as reached at the last round; other plateaus count from their first recording (p. 10).
  - Derivatives by second-order central differences (p. 11).
  - Pseudocode is given and the code is said to be public (p. 2); this version gives no URL.
  - Transferable: when instrumentation is expensive, take the measurement and the timing from two separate runs of the same deterministic algorithm, and join them per round (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Two questions (abstract, p. 1; §5, p. 10).
  - *(i) Premature stalling.* Definition 5 flags round r when the round has no infinite reduction, the slope P′(t(r)) is below p, and P″ > q somewhere later (p. 11). For the sequential code (Table 1, pp. 11-12):
    - 48 instances ever show P″ > 0;
    - 14 of these also have a slope below 0.1;
    - 1 exceeds q = 0.2;
    - none exceeds q = 0.5, or q = 2.0 at p = 0.5.

    The GPU code is "less susceptible" in all but one parameter combination (p. 12). Verdict: the effect "seems to occur only rarely and on individual instances", so "termination criteria based on local progress are reasonable" (p. 12).
  - *(ii) GPU against sequential under early stopping.* The speed-up t^seq_x / t^gpu_x is taken at each progress level x, as a geometric mean over instances (p. 12). At x = 100 it reproduces the fixed-point speed-up of ref. [26] (footnote 2, p. 12). Results (p. 12; Fig. 2, p. 13):
    - 4.9 (finite) and 5.4 (infinite) at the fixed point;
    - maxima of about 7.8 and 7.0 near 50% progress;
    - a steep drop in the last few percent.

  Setup (p. 10): the GPU code on a Tesla V100 with 32 GB; the CPU code on a 24-core Xeon Gold 6246 at 3.30 GHz with 384 GB. The thread count of the "canonical sequential" code is not stated, and there are no repetitions or variance.

  Exclusions per phase (p. 12): 57 instances with only infinite reductions, and 164 with only finite ones.

  Counts that do not close:
  - After the exclusions the set has 540 instances (p. 10). The stalling test runs on "the remaining 432 instances", after excluding the 310 without bound changes and the 57 with only infinite reductions (p. 11). But 540 − 57 = 483.
  - The 540 itself requires the 2 instances at the iteration limit to have been dropped, which the text does not say (p. 10).
  - Transferable: compare algorithms at equal progress levels across the whole run, not only at termination, and check that the end point of the curve reproduces the earlier fixed-point result (p. 12).
  - Transferable: define a failure mode (premature stalling) by explicit thresholds on the progress curve, and report counts over a grid of thresholds, so that readers can pick their own strictness (pp. 11-12).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper bridges two communities (pp. 2-3): CP (propagators and consistency notions, ref. [23]) and MIP (propagation inside branch and bound, refs. [2, 3]).
  - Belotti et al.'s LP-based fixed-point method is credited as polynomial in theory. The iterative method with stopping criteria is defended as "the most effective method in most cases" (p. 5).
  - The sequential code "closely follows the implementation in the academic solver SCIP" (p. 10).
  - The authors' own GPU paper is re-evaluated, with the claim that the GPU code is "even more competitive in a real-world setting than originally reported" (abstract, p. 1; p. 12).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The two applications are posed as questions in the abstract, and the introduction gives the answers before any analysis ("we find that this situation occurs rarely in practice", p. 2).
  - Definitions, observations and a corollary come before the measure (pp. 3-9).
  - The verdicts are hedged ("seems to occur only rarely", p. 12), and the motivation is framed plainly ("a motivation in itself", p. 5).
  - Slips: "fomalized" (p. 2), and the count arithmetic noted under D5 (pp. 10-11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contributions are a methodology (the measure, with pseudocode) and two empirical answers (pp. 2, 12).

  Limits and future work (pp. 12-13):
  - whether some IBTA actually reaches the weakest bounds is open, with "a stronger version of the framework" as future research;
  - an extension to general constraints, for example factorable programs in global optimization and MINLP.

  The 72 instances where the two codes reach different fixed points are removed, not analyzed (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (group): a belief, Belotti et al.'s unquantified stalling caveat (p. 5), becomes a check computable per round (Definition 5, p. 11). The check is run on a public set and counted (48 → 14 → 1 → 0; Table 1, p. 12). Step 7: the count cuts against the motivating caveat (p. 12).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (step 3, instrument the production solver):
  - both codes are standalone implementations, the sequential one modelled on SCIP's (p. 10);
  - measurement and timing come from separate runs (p. 10).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (step 8: drop instances on which the arms differ): the 72 instances where the two codes reach different fixed points are counted and dropped (p. 10).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant:
  - fairness comes from a common progress scale for algorithms that take different bound sequences (pp. 5, 12), not from stripping features;
  - a GPU is compared against a CPU code whose thread count is not stated (p. 10).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: exclusions are counted per reason (p. 10), but the excluded instances are not named and the counts do not close (pp. 10-11). The 72 numerical disagreements are dropped rather than examined (p. 10); compare step 9 (keep instances where floating-point results disagree out of the statistics).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (step 7): the open question about weakest bounds is printed, with a direction for future work (pp. 12-13).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the measure relies on the fixed point being unique (p. 4), but in double precision the two codes reach different fixed points on 72 instances (p. 10). No exact arithmetic is used to decide which code is right.
- Heuristic 8: ✅ exclusions are counted (p. 10); ⚠ they are not named, and two counts do not close (pp. 10-11).
- New-pattern candidate *algorithm-independent reference points*: define start and end states that every algorithm shares, measure progress between them, and compare algorithms at equal progress (pp. 5, 7, 9, 12).
- New-pattern candidate *text-table count slips*: 432 against 540 − 57 = 483 (pp. 10-11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Split a progress measure into qualitatively different phases (infinite to finite, finite improvement), and normalize each by a maximum that does not depend on the algorithm (pp. 6, 9).
- When algorithms take different paths, use the weakest reachable values, not the first observed values, as the reference point (p. 7).
- When instrumentation would distort time, run measurement and timing separately (p. 10).
- Report speed-up as a function of progress, and check it against the fixed-point speed-up at 100% (p. 12).
- Define the failure mode by thresholds on the derivatives of the progress curve, and report a grid of thresholds (pp. 11-12).
