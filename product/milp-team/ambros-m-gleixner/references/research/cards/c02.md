### S008 · LP-based disaggregation approaches to solving the open pit mining production scheduling problem with block processing selectivity (Computers & Operations Research 2009, DOI 10.1016/j.cor.2007.12.006)
**Read level**: full. All 26 file pages of the journal PDF: abstract and introduction (pp. 1–4), model and structure (pp. 4–8), binning theory with every proposition, lemma and proof (pp. 8–16), Algorithm 4.1 and both splitting rules (pp. 16–19), MIP binnings and Tables 1–6 (pp. 19–24), conclusions, notation and references (pp. 24–26).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Natashia Boland (Melbourne), Irina Dumitrescu (UNSW, corresponding author), Gary Froyland (UNSW); Gleixner is fourth, at the Institut für Mathematik, TU Berlin; the byline is alphabetical and no part is credited to anyone (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: available online 14 December 2007 (p. 1), so written while he was a TU Berlin student, before the ZIB years. It is the journal paper of the mining project whose Diplomarbeit is S025: card c01 reports that the thesis's §3.2 and Chapter 5 also appear in this paper; here they correspond to the split structure of Prop. 2.1 and the dual-value binnings of §5.2 (pp. 7–8, 21; the correspondence is my inference from card c01's description). Data and support come from BHP Billiton and an Australian Research Council Linkage Project (pp. 4, 24).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**:
  - The open pit mining production scheduling problem (OPMPSP): sequence the removal of the blocks of a block model over the mine's life to maximise NPV under mining and processing capacities and precedence (abstract p. 1; pp. 1, 4).
  - Practice aggregates blocks into scheduling units because the block problem is too large (p. 5). The entry is an observation about what aggregation costs: it acts "via precedence and processing selectivity", and since recent aggregation methods align aggregates with precedence, most of the lost profit is lost processing selectivity (p. 5).
  - So mining stays at aggregate level and processing moves to block level, which also brings the cut-off grade into the optimisation: in mine-planning terms, to "simultaneously optimise the cut-off grade" together with the extraction schedule, instead of fixing it beforehand (pp. 2, 5).
  - The gap is named: aggregation-based OPMPSP methods (Smith; Ramazan et al.) never disaggregate, "no general technique for partial or full disaggregation has been developed", and "The present paper fills this gap" (p. 3).
  - MIP is chosen because of recent MIP-solver progress, starting from Fricke's formulation class (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: split the cost of a modelling simplification into its separate channels (here precedence vs. processing selectivity), keep the channel that is cheap to keep coarse, and refine only the other (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Structural insight (Prop. 2.1, pp. 7–8): in the LP relaxation at any branch-and-bound node, the processing variables of each period solve a continuous bounded knapsack, so in some optimum the processing fractions within an aggregate take at most two distinct values per period (three in one aggregate). A partition of each aggregate into "bins" with equal processing fraction therefore loses nothing, if the right partition is known (p. 8).
  - B-MIP replaces the block processing variables by one variable per bin; the aggregate binning is the old coarse model, the block binning is D-MIP (pp. 8–9).
  - Exact LP disaggregation (Algorithm 4.1, GA(B, ε), p. 16): solve B-LP, test with its duals whether the binning is optimal for the block LP D-LP (Prop. 3.1), and split bins that contain blocks with positive "quasi-reduced cost" g − a v* (pp. 11, 16–18). Two rules: single block splitting moves one block per bin and iteration, chosen by the largest quasi-reduced cost with grade-difference and lowest-index tie-breaks (p. 17); set splitting moves all positive blocks at once (p. 18).
  - Relative to adaptive clustering (Litvinchev–Tsurkov; Jörnsten et al.) one thing changes: the number of aggregates may grow instead of being fixed at the number of constraints plus one (pp. 3, 17).
  - MIP stage (Algorithm 5.1, p. 22): run GA once at the root and keep one of four binnings for the whole tree: METHOD 1 the GA output; METHOD 2 a split at the LP duals v* with a band (1 ± θ) around v*, blocks inside the band getting one bin per distinct ratio; METHOD 3 bins by distinct z* values per period ("variable in time"); METHOD 4 bins by z* at the period of maximal extraction ("constant in time") (pp. 21–22). Re-binning at every node is possible in principle but not attempted (pp. 8, 21).
  - Transferable technique: bound the number of distinct values an optimal solution takes within each group before designing the compression, then grow the groups only where a dual test says the compression binds (pp. 7–8, 16).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - MILP with binary variables x per aggregate and period that say whether excavation of the aggregate has begun by period t, continuous mined fractions y and processed fractions z in [0, 1], one processing and one mining knapsack row per period, precedence through cumulative sums and monotone x (constraints (1)–(8), pp. 5–6). No general integers, SOS, indicators or big-M.
  - Modelling assumptions stated: two attributes (rock and one metal) "for simplicity of exposition only" (p. 4); revenue realised when processed, all costs in present value (p. 4); uniform excavation within an aggregate ("not uncommon in models of open pit mining projects") and no stockpiling (p. 5).
  - The structural results hold for D-LP(L, U), the LP at any node with branching fixings L and U (p. 6).
  - Data: block models of two pits from BHP Billiton: "Marvin" (8,513 blocks, from Whittle 4X) with three industrial aggregations of 115, 296 and 1,038 aggregates, and a 96,821-block pit with 125 aggregates; the partner built the aggregations from equipment capacity, rock mechanics, geology and safety; 20 periods for the 8,513-block sets, 25 for the large one (pp. 4, 19–20).
  - B-LP is solved in a reduced form B-LPred without x, with precedence merged into cumulative-y constraints (Lemma 4.2, pp. 18–19).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (devices, with proofs):
  - The closed-form solution of the continuous bounded knapsack, with its split item (13), gives Prop. 2.1 (pp. 7–8); Remark 2.2 reads the dual v_t as a cut-off grade (p. 8).
  - Prop. 3.1 (pp. 10–11): from an optimal B-LP dual (u*, v*, ·) build π = (g − a v*)+ for every block; it completes a D-LP dual optimum iff Σ_k (g − a v*)+ ≤ R_{i,t} for every aggregate and period (21). Proof: primal and dual feasible points with equal objective; converse from dual feasibility. This is the certificate that the compressed LP is exact for the block LP.
  - Prop. 3.2 (pp. 11–12), by contradiction: if (21) fails for (i, t), some bin holds blocks with quasi-reduced costs of both signs, so mixed-sign bins are where to split.
  - δ-MIP (pp. 12–13): deviation variables from the bin value turn the binning into "a model in which some variables are required to be set to zero", so refining bins becomes pricing in revised-simplex style (p. 12). Lemmas 3.3–3.4 give a dual-feasible completion (uniform split u*/|B|) (p. 14); subproblem (36) minimises the worst dual violation per bin, and Lemma 3.5 shows its value is zero iff the positive quasi-reduced costs of the bin sum to at most u* (pp. 15–16), the test of Step 1(i) in Algorithm 4.1 (p. 16).
  - Lemma 4.1 (p. 18): whenever set splitting is called, the bin holds blocks of both signs, so every split is nontrivial. No termination theorem is stated; finite termination for ε = 0 follows because each update splits a bin nontrivially and bins are bounded by blocks (my inference, not in the paper).
  - Exact vs heuristic is stated per parameter: for ε = 0 the methods are exact (B-LP optimum = D-LP optimum); for ε > 0 refinement stops when the relative improvement falls below ε and the methods are heuristics (p. 17). The four MIP binnings are heuristics for D-MIP (pp. 21–22).
  - Small slips: Lemma 4.2 item 2 starts from "a feasible solution of B-LP" and concludes feasibility "of B-LPred", which reads swapped (p. 19); the proof of Lemma 4.1 cites "Remark 3.4" for what is Lemma 3.4 (p. 18). The proof of Lemma 4.2 is "straightforward and omitted" (p. 19).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup: AMD Opteron 252, 2.6 GHz, 1 MB cache, 6 GB RAM, CPLEX 9.1 (p. 4); CPLEX MIP emphasis 0 (default), relative gap tolerance 1%, time limit 60,000 s (pp. 22–23). Single runs; no seeds, permutations or variability measures; no shifted means (Tables 1–6).
  - LP stage: single block splitting on 115-8513 with ε = 0 needs 575 iterations, 34,769 s of B-LP time and 37,613,975 subproblems and ends with 33.72 bins per aggregate (Table 1, p. 19). Set splitting needs at most five iterations on all four sets and is exact (ε = 0) everywhere: B-LP time 21.4 s, 122.08 s, 8,546.28 s and 37.19 s, final 1.18–2.79 bins per aggregate, and the same optimal value as single block splitting on 115-8513 (Tables 1–2, pp. 19–20). ε = 0.1 saves iterations and LP time; its LP values are within 0.006% of the exact ones (my arithmetic, Table 2).
  - The two extreme binnings bracket the MIP: aggregate binning gives NPV/10^8 of 5.497, 5.862, 6.370 and 0.2813; block binning (= D-MIP) gives 7.144, 7.181, 7.245 and no result on 125-96821, where "even the root note LP did not solve within this time limit" (Table 3, p. 22). Reader's arithmetic: block processing adds +30.0%, +22.5% and +13.7% NPV on the three 8,513-block sets.
  - METHOD 1 reaches 7.149, 7.197, 7.242 and 0.4937, the last "an improvement ... of 75.49%" over aggregate binning (Table 4, p. 23); METHODS 2–4 use fewer bins and mostly less time at similar NPV (Tables 5–6, pp. 23–24).
  - Accounting: Tables 4–6 time only the final B-MIP solve, and the text tells the reader to add the GA time of Table 2 (p. 22). With it, METHOD 1 stays below the aggregate-binning time except on 1038-8513, where 15,646 s against 15,380 s is about equal (my arithmetic from Tables 2–4); the paper says "around those for aggregate binning" (p. 22).
  - What is admitted: because CPLEX stops at 1%, a binning can beat the block binning's value ("it can even happen", p. 24; Table 4 has 7.1489 against 7.1440 on 115-8513); one exception to the time trend is named (constant-in-time binning on 1038-8513, p. 24).
  - What is thin: MIP results for ε > 0 are omitted "for brevity" (p. 22), so the claim that "it is not even necessary to completely solve the D-LP" (p. 8) rests on the LP values of Table 2 only; the LP-scale claim that D-LP "can be solved to optimality for much larger data instances" (p. 24) rests on one instance, 125-96821, because direct D-LP times for the 8,513-block sets are not printed (Tables 2–3). No other OPMPSP method or solver is compared (pp. 19–24).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Surveys OPMPSP approaches by family: Lerchs–Grossmann nested pits and floating cones, dynamic programming (Onur–Dowd; Tolwinski–Underwood, quoted on state explosion), and MIP or BIP (Ramazan–Dimitrakopoulos, Caccetta–Hill, Boland et al., Fricke); commercial planners (MineMax, Gemcom Whittle) are named (p. 2).
  - Criticism is by modelling assumption, not by performance: cut-off grade fixed before optimisation in [6, 7] (p. 2); aggregation without disaggregation in [18–20] (p. 3).
  - Bridges two literatures, LP aggregation–disaggregation (Litvinchev–Tsurkov; Rogers et al.) and MIP constraint aggregation (Marchand–Wolsey; Iyer et al.): "In the present paper we link the two worlds" (p. 3).
  - The closest method, adaptive clustering, is credited and distinguished by one property, a fixed vs. a growing number of aggregates (pp. 3, 17).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - The contribution comes as a filled gap ("The present paper fills this gap", p. 3) and as "two crucial improvements" over [6, 7, 10, 11] (p. 2); an outline paragraph previews the main result, binnings that match D-MIP's objective in much less time (p. 4).
  - Each structural result is followed by a paragraph on what it suggests algorithmically (p. 8) and by plain-language images for the duality argument: a "finger of blame" (p. 11), not yet a "smoking gun" (p. 12), blocks that want to "split up out of" their bin (p. 11).
  - Hedges are explicit and then resolved by data: "we cannot guarantee ... suggest this is likely, and our numerical results confirm" (p. 9); single block splitting is expected to be inefficient before it is run (p. 18).
  - Evaluative adjectives sit next to the tables: "far superior" (p. 19), "very significant" (p. 22), "exceedingly well" (p. 22). A notation table closes the paper (pp. 24–25).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution layers: modelling (block processing with aggregate mining, p. 5), an exact LP algorithm with optimality conditions (§§3–4, pp. 9–20), four MIP heuristics (§5, pp. 21–22), evidence on industrial data (pp. 19–24).
  - Limits are printed in prose, without counts: no guarantee that few bins arise (p. 9); binning only at the root, re-binning would give "only marginal benefits, and we do not attempt it" (p. 21); not clear that LP duals give the best split boundaries, hence the band (p. 21); D-MIP not solvable on the largest set (p. 22).
  - Future work: more block models, and extensions with stockpiling and with stochastic geological and financial elements, the method being "general purpose machinery" (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant (structural analogue, inferred): the compressed LP (B-LP) does the solving, a full-model dual condition decides whether its answer is exact for D-LP (Prop. 3.1, optimality_check), and refinement is spent only where the condition fails (pp. 10–11, 16–17). The axis is model resolution, not arithmetic precision; no exact arithmetic is used.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the baselines are built from the same model, solver and settings at both extremes (aggregate binning, and block binning = D-MIP); NPV and time are both printed, and the preprocessing time is added to the method's (pp. 21–23). There is no reduced incumbent and no outside baseline.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: limits are stated (pp. 9, 21–22), but qualitatively and with a generic future-work paragraph (p. 24); there is no counted gap that becomes the next project.
- Heuristics (not counted in the digest): Heuristic 9 ✅ (harvest what the solver already computed): LP duals and the LP solution define the processing bins and decide which bins to split (pp. 11, 21–22).
- New-pattern candidate: *bracket by extremes*: place the new method between the coarsest and the finest version of the same model under identical solver settings (Table 3 against Tables 4–6, pp. 21–23); compare S001's oracle-best and oracle-worst permutations (S001 p. 14) and the virtual best and worst of S002 (card c11).
- New-pattern candidate: *simplest exact rule as a declared base case*: single block splitting is introduced as the simplest refinement, expected to be inefficient, run to optimality and printed next to the efficient rule with the same optimal value (pp. 18–20).
- New-pattern candidate (existing name, card c01): *LP-analogue proxy for an NP-hard structural quantity, with a safety band and a measured loss*: METHOD 2 splits at the LP duals with a (1 ± θ) band and measures NPV against block processing (pp. 21, 23–24); the NP-hardness that motivates the proxy is in the thesis (card c01), not in this paper.
- New-pattern candidate: *exact or heuristic, stated per reduction*: ε = 0 exact, ε > 0 heuristic (p. 17); the MIP binnings called heuristics (p. 21). The same move appears in S017 (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Bound the number of distinct values an optimal solution can take in each group (here at most two, via a continuous knapsack) before choosing a compressed formulation (Prop. 2.1, pp. 7–8).
- [D4] Certify that a compressed LP is exact for the full LP by building a full dual from the compressed dual and checking one inequality per group; split only the failing groups (Prop. 3.1 and Algorithm 4.1, pp. 10–11, 16–17).
- [D4] Split a group by moving its whole positive-score subset at once instead of one member per iteration (5 against 575 iterations, 21.4 s against 34,769 s on 115-8513; Tables 1–2, pp. 19–20).
- [D5] Run the simplest exact rule as a declared base case and print its cost next to the efficient rule (pp. 18–20).
- [D5] Bracket the new method between the coarsest and finest version of the same model, solved with the same solver and settings, and add the preprocessing time to the method's time (pp. 21–22).
- [D5] When the MIP is stopped at a 1% gap, say that differences between arms below 1% can go either way (p. 24).

### S001 · MIPLIB 2010: mixed integer programming library version 5 (Mathematical Programming Computation 2011, DOI 10.1007/s12532-011-0025-9)
**Read level**: full (TR: ZIB-Report 10-31; the MPC typesetting was not seen). Main text pp. 3–22 read completely, with the acknowledgements; references pp. 22–26 read; Table 5 (instance origins, pp. 27–33) read; Table 6 (statistics, pp. 34–41) and the result Tables 7–8 (pp. 42–47) skimmed, headers and footnotes read, because the extraction runs their rows together. Figures 1–6 survive only as captions and axis labels (pp. 4–5, 16–17, 19–20).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Thorsten Koch (first author), Tobias Achterberg (IBM), Erling Andersen (MOSEK), Oliver Bastert (FICO), Timo Berthold, Robert E. Bixby (Gurobi), Emilie Danna (Google), Gerald Gamrath, Stefan Heinz, Andrea Lodi (Bologna), Hans Mittelmann (Arizona State), Ted Ralphs (Lehigh), Domenico Salvagnin (Padova), Daniel E. Steffy, Kati Wolter. Gleixner is one of seven ZIB authors and ninth in a byline that is alphabetical after Koch (pp. 1, 3). No section is credited to anyone; the acknowledgements credit Danna's work on performance variability from 2008 to 2010 (p. 22) · **Position in trajectory**: his first MIPLIB, as a ZIB co-author (MIPLIB 2003 was by Achterberg, Koch and Martin, p. 4); MIPLIB 2017 (S002, card c11) is the first-authored successor. Achterberg and Bixby, later MILP Team voices, are co-authors; Lübbecke and Fischetti are among the thanked contributors (pp. 3, 22). MIPLIB 2010's LP relaxations later enter S020's troublesome pool (S020 p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**:
  - Occasion: the fifth MIPLIB after 1992, 1996 and 2003, motivated by "the continuous progress in the field" (p. 4). Progress is shown three ways: MIPLIB 2003 moved from 22 easy, 3 hard and 35 open instances to 41, 15 and 4 by the end of 2010 (pp. 4–5); CPLEX and Xpress speed-ups on internal test sets (Fig. 2, p. 5); an algorithmic factor of 55,000 times hardware gives "something like 100 million times faster in the last 20 years" (p. 5).
  - The entry is a warning about that progress: practitioners experiment with tractable instances, and "mip codes tend to be tuned to more efficiently solve those models that are already known to be solvable"; the library therefore adds many out-of-scope instances: of 361, 185 are easy, 42 hard and 134 open (p. 5).
  - A second entry: one test set is not enough, since researchers had used subsets of the 60 MIPLIB 2003 instances that became too small for their questions (p. 5).
  - The first MIPLIB "assembled by a large group from academia and from industry" (abstract, p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when progress has "solved" a benchmark, read the progress figure as a selection effect (codes tuned to what is known to be solvable) and add out-of-scope instances on purpose (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and design**:
  - A library of eight test sets, each defined for one purpose: benchmark (87), infeasible (20), primal (40), xxl (11), reoptimize (66), tree (52), unstable (21) and challenge (164) (Table 1, p. 6).
  - Three instruments ship with it for the first time: a solution checker in exact arithmetic (§3, pp. 8–10), a test engine whose scripts run solvers in a defined way and check their answers (§4, pp. 11–12), and a study of performance variability (§5, pp. 12–15).
  - An instance catalogue: origins, statistics, exact LP values (QSopt_ex), exactly feasible MIP solution values and a numerical "attention level" per instance (§6, pp. 15–19).
  - A state-of-the-art snapshot of five solvers on the benchmark set, explicitly not a ranking (§7, pp. 19–21).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - MIP as a linear objective with linear constraints over real and integer variables (p. 4). By variable type: 116 binary programs, 30 pure integer programs (1 with general integers only, 29 with binaries and general integers) and 215 mixed programs (173 binary plus continuous, 12 integer plus continuous, 30 with all three); only 13 instances have no binaries, and 72 of 361 have general integers (Table 3, p. 17).
  - Sources: a call for instances from 26 March to October 2010 brought in over 1,108 instances from 57 contributors; NEOS submissions to SCIP, feaspump and QSopt_ex were translated to MPS; public libraries (Berkeley, COR@L, DEIS) were added (p. 6). The final 361: 2 from MIPLIB, 7 from MIPLIB 3, 17 from MIPLIB 2003, 180 other public, 155 new (Table 2, p. 7).
  - Selection pipeline: about 2,000 candidates; duplicates removed, homogeneous groups cut to fewer than ten, instances solved by SCIP or CBC in under a minute removed (p. 6); 659 left, examined independently by eight teams; two instances added at short notice for missing classes (unitcal_7, cov1075); a first proposal of 100 benchmark instances plus 45 substitutes (p. 7).
  - Benchmark requirements: solvable to optimality within 24 hours by at least one solver (first proposal), and every other set except unstable and challenge represented (p. 7); Table 1 states the final rule as solvable within two hours by at least one solver on a high-end PC (p. 6). Refinement removed over- or under-represented classes, instances too easy or too hard on other machines with developer versions, and numerically difficult ones; ties favoured new contributions, instances with a known application and stable performance, and a reasonable total running time (p. 7).
  - Rule-defined sets: reoptimize (node LPs needing at least 500 simplex iterations on average, root excluded, and at least 100 nodes, p. 7); tree (at least 10^6 nodes); xxl (more than 10^6 variables or constraints or 10^7 nonzeros, not mostly removed by presolve); primal (root LP bound after presolve equals the optimum, finding it appears challenging); challenge (not solved within two hours by any solver); unstable (ill-conditioned bases, coefficients near the solvers' infinity, drastic changes with tolerances, or different results from different solvers) (p. 8).
  - Caveat on all thresholds: they depend on the computational environment and are only "guiding values"; the sets were double-checked with different software on different machines (p. 7).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (devices):
  - What a floating-point solver solves (§3.2, pp. 9–10): MPS writes each number in at most 12 characters, so the file may already approximate the intended problem; reading converts to binary; scaling and tolerances (ε for feasibility, δ for integrality) relax the region; presolve changes it further; "even though an instance is infeasible, it can become feasible by the extension of the feasible area due to the tolerances" (pp. 9–10). Example rocII-4-11: solvers report −6.6556387 or −6.65275574; fixing the rounded integers and re-solving the LP exactly shows some reported solutions infeasible with zero tolerances, which "does not mean that any of the solvers made a mistake" (p. 9).
  - Checker design (§3.3, p. 10): only the feasibility of the returned solution is tested, because polynomial-size optimality certificates for MIP are not expected in general (assuming P ≠ NP). Three questions (arithmetic, data source, tolerances) are answered: GMP arbitrary precision; the original MPS text, with no "nicer" rational inference (0.3333333 stays 3,333,333/10,000,000); the solution read from a simple text file to stay solver-independent; absolute tolerance 10^-4 for linear constraints, bounds and integrality; the objective accepted within absolute 10^-4 or relative 10^-7 (relative to max(|value|, 1)); maximum violations reported.
  - Reliable reference values: the primal bounds for the mismatch test come from rounding the integer variables and solving the remaining LP exactly (p. 11); the catalogue does the same with QSopt_ex, which gives no optimality proof but a truly feasible solution, and prints an inexact, checker-accepted value in italics where QSopt_ex fails (pp. 17–18). LP values are exact QSopt_ex values of the pure LP relaxation without MIP presolve, up to 16 digits (p. 17).
  - Test engine (§4, pp. 11–12): deterministic mode where possible (a footnote defines it: identical runs given the same machine and settings, timing dependencies removed in multi-threaded runs), gap cutoff 0, wall-clock time measured externally, rounded up to the second, parsing included. Gap = (pb − db)/inf{|z| : z in [db, pb]}, undefined ("--") without a solution, for infeasibility claims or when db ≤ 0 ≤ pb; chosen because it is monotone in both bounds and a worst-case bound on the relative gap (p. 11). Solver status ok, stopped or abort; solution status ok, fail, mismatch or error, with mismatch when the solver claims infeasibility or a significantly worse optimum than a known primal bound (p. 11). Solver-specific parts sit in run_xyz.sh and parse_xyz.awk; CBC, CPLEX, Gurobi, MOSEK, SCIP and Xpress are supported (p. 12).
  - Variability instrument (§5, pp. 12–14): the definition ("changes in performance measures for the same problem that are caused by seemingly performance-neutral changes in the environment or the input format", p. 12); causes (imperfect tie-breaking, strong-branching candidate subsets, compiler reordering of floating-point operations, divergence cascades, non-unique root LP bases) and open questions (pp. 12–13); generator: row and column permutations, after Danna [42]; score: the coefficient of variation of solution time over the permutations (pp. 13–14). As extracted, the printed formula is (1/Σt_i)·sqrt(Σ(t_i − Σt_i/n)²), the coefficient of variation divided by √n; the printed values fit a plain coefficient of variation (VS 0.05 for mik-250-1-100-1, whose max/min time ratio is 1.29: possible for a coefficient of variation, at most about 0.13 on that range, but not for one divided by 10), so any slip is in the formula, not in the values (my reasoning; the PDF was not checked) (p. 14).
  - Attention level (p. 18): CPLEX 12.2.0.2's kappa statistics (node limit 1,000, two days) sort basis condition numbers into stable (< 10^7), suspicious, unstable and ill-posed (≥ 10^14), summarised as AL = p_ill-posed + 0.3 p_unstable + 0.01 p_suspicious; with 16 digits and tolerance 10^-6, condition numbers above 10^10 suggest LP errors larger than the tolerance; AL "is not a property inherent to the model" but of solver and settings.

### S017 · Towards globally optimal operation of water supply networks (Numerical Algebra, Control and Optimization 2012, DOI 10.3934/naco.2012.2.695)
**Read level**: full (TR: ZIB-Report 12-25, July 2012, "accepted for publication" in NACO, p. 3; the journal typesetting was not seen). All file pages 3–20: model, solution approach, presolve, experiments, Tables 1–4, references. Figures 1, 2, 4 and 5 survive as captions, Figure 6 as node labels, Figure 3 as axis ticks (pp. 5, 9, 11, 13–14, 16).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Harald Held (Siemens AG, Corporate Technology), Wei Huang (TU Munich, corresponding author), Stefan Vigerske (HU Berlin); Gleixner at ZIB, first in an alphabetical byline (p. 3). The paper names Huang's Master's thesis (FU Berlin 2011) as "the base for our paper" (pp. 4, 19); no part is credited to Gleixner · **Position in trajectory**: an applied MINLP paper from the ZIB years in which, per the skill, he built MINLP components in SCIP. It runs on SCIP's nonlinear extension (Vigerske's thesis and Berthold, Heinz, Vigerske) and lists Undercover (Berthold and Gleixner) among SCIP's MINLP heuristics (pp. 11–12, 18–19). Outside the MILP Team's field (MINLP belongs to minlp-team); read here for evaluation and exactness habits.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**:
  - Cost- and energy-minimal operation of pressurized water supply networks; the full task is a one-day plan with hourly demand forecasts, pump switching and tank filling (pp. 3–4).
  - The entry is a critique of the methods in use: they either simplify the physics (drop or linearise the nonlinearities) or use local or heuristic methods without guaranteed optimality gaps (p. 4). Piecewise-linear MIP approximations can be refined a posteriori to ε-feasibility, but they give no valid dual bound and may cut off optima because a different problem is solved (p. 4).
  - The scope is chosen so that a proof is possible: the stationary problem at one point in time, with given tank levels and constant demands, solved to ε-global optimality (p. 4). It is justified as the one-period subproblem of time-discretised, heuristic or decomposition methods and as a yardstick to evaluate heuristics (pp. 4–5).
  - A second entry is a modelling gap the authors claim: the distinction between "real and imaginary flow", Darcy–Weisbach holding only if water is present at the high-pressure end of a pipe (abstract p. 3; pp. 8–9).
  - Transferable technique: when the full problem cannot be solved with a proof, solve with a proof the subproblem that heuristics for the full problem call repeatedly, and offer it as their yardstick (pp. 4–5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Model: a nonconvex MINLP (17) on a directed graph of junctions and water sources, with pipes, pumps and valves (pp. 5–10).
  - Real and imaginary heads: a binary z_j per junction is forced to 1 if the head exceeds the geodetic height or flow passes; propagation rules along level and sloped pipes; Darcy–Weisbach enforced only between real nodes via big-M (constraints (9)–(15), pp. 8–9). Remark 3: "So far we have not seen this distinction being made in the literature" (p. 8).
  - The solver is used as it is: SCIP's spatial branch and bound with linear outer approximation of sgn(q)q² and of the pump-energy term, branching on flow variables, bound propagation through the pipe equation, MIP heuristics followed by an NLP solve with fixed binaries, and MINLP large-neighbourhood heuristics (pp. 10–12).
  - The algorithmic contribution is problem-specific presolve applied before SCIP (§5, pp. 12–15): fixing z at demand junctions, symmetry breaking in pump stations, contraction of pipe series, contraction of pipe–valve pairs into a convex relaxation.
  - Transferable technique: put the application structure into reductions that make the model "more linear" (fewer nonconvex equations) and leave the generic solver unchanged (pp. 14–15).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - Nonconvex MINLP: binaries for pump status x, valve direction y and real head z; semi-continuous pump flows (Q_min x ≤ q ≤ Q_max x); big-M constraints; nonconvex equalities ∆h = λ sgn(q) q² for every pipe; a separable nonconvex objective (water purchase plus pump energy ∆H_max q − γ1 q^(1+γ2)) (pp. 6–10).
  - Physical simplifications stated: Prandtl–Kármán friction (large Reynolds number, hydraulically rough pipes), which underestimates flow at small pressure differences and so yields conservative solutions (p. 7); pumps at a single fixed speed in the instances (p. 7); pump efficiency taken as constant because flow ranges keep pumps near maximal efficiency (p. 8); water sources assumed real (p. 8).
  - Modelling choices argued from solver behaviour: a valve-direction binary instead of the nonlinear ∆h·q ≥ 0 because it improves the computational behaviour of branch and cut (p. 7; no data shown); big-M chosen per constraint as small as the variable bounds allow (Remark 2, p. 7); no forward/backward arc pairs as in Sherali–Smith, since bidirectional flow is handled algorithmically (Remark 1, p. 7).
  - Data: two Siemens networks, n25p22a18 (25 nodes, 22 pipes, 12 pumps, 6 valves) and n88p64a64 (88 nodes, 64 pipes, 55 pumps, 9 valves), each with hourly demand forecasts for one day (pp. 5, 15–16).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (devices):
  - Presolve exactness is defined: a feasible solution may be cut off only if an essentially identical one remains (p. 12).
  - Pump-station symmetry: identical parallel pumps get x_p1 ≤ … ≤ x_pN, which reduces the choices from 2^N to N + 1 (p. 13).
  - Pipe-series contraction (pp. 13–14): two Darcy–Weisbach equations through a zero-demand junction are equivalent to one with the summed loss coefficient plus an interpolated head h_j = (λ_jk h_i + λ_ij h_k)/(λ_jk + λ_ij); z_j is kept because a high junction can block flow (constraints (15a), (18), (19)); since nonlinear equations disappear, the model becomes "more linear" (p. 14).
  - Pipe–valve contraction (p. 14): the feasible set of (pressure loss, flow) is a union of two convex sets; it is relaxed to the convex quadratic ∆h ≥ λ q² with big-M direction constraints, replacing a nonconvex, nonconcave equation.
  - Remark 5: the reductions cut size and, more importantly, nonconvex equations, and so spatial branching (pp. 14–15).
  - Solver-side devices, described rather than designed here: outer approximation along convex and concave envelopes, tightened by branching on flows (p. 11, Fig. 3); bound propagation from ∆h to q through the square root (p. 12); binaries fixed from a MIP-heuristic point and the NLP solved with Ipopt, so that any NLP-feasible point is MINLP-feasible (p. 12).
  - No theorems; the correctness of each reduction is argued in the text (pp. 12–14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - The goal is stated first: whether and how fast the stationary MINLP solves to ε-global optimality, and what the presolve contributes (p. 15).
  - Scenarios: four demand hours (0–1 am low, 6–7 am first peak, 12–1 pm medium, 6–7 pm second peak), said to be representative for the other hours (not shown), times two tank scenarios: "med" (tanks half full) and "low", built from the med solution by setting to minimum the tanks that would run empty first (the first in n25p22a18, the first four in n88p64a64) (p. 15); 16 runs in all.
  - Software and hardware: only academic software available in source code: SCIP 2.1.1, SoPlex 1.6.0, Ipopt 3.10.1, CppAD 20110101.5, Zimpl 3.2; SCIP defaults; 1 h limit; AMD Opteron 6174, 2.2 GHz, 128 GB (p. 15); optimality tolerance 10^-6 (p. 16). Single runs; no seeds or permutations.
  - Sizes (Table 2, p. 16): binaries cut by 14% and 18% (28 → 24 and 99 → 81), nonlinear constraints only 42 → 40 and 171 → 170, counted before SCIP's own presolve (pp. 15–16).
  - Effect (Table 3, pp. 16–17): on n25p22a18 every scenario takes under one second either way; on n88p64a64 the low scenarios go from 11.2 s to 1.1 s, 595.4 s to 12.8 s, 1,941.4 s to 21.7 s and 399.8 s to 104.0 s, "faster by a factor between 3.8 and 89.5" through smaller trees (p. 16). The slowdowns on n88p64a64 "0–1 am med" and "6–7 am med" (0.4 → 1.1 s and 0.6 → 1.6 s) are explained: SCIP's heuristics no longer find the optimum at the root (p. 16). Reader's check of Table 3: n25p22a18 also slows on "12–1 pm med" (0.5 → 0.6 s) and "6–7 pm low" (0.4 → 0.5 s, nodes 54 → 80), which the text covers only as "can only improve minimally" (pp. 16–17).

### S020 · Improving the accuracy of linear programming solvers with iterative refinement (ISSAC 2012, DOI 10.1145/2442829.2442858)
**Read level**: full (TR: ZIB-Report 12-19 of 8 May 2012, which "has appeared in the Proceedings" of ISSAC 2012, p. 3; the proceedings typesetting was not seen). All file pages 3–12: background, Algorithm 2 with Propositions 3.1–3.2 and their proofs, the computational study with Tables 1–2, conclusion and references. Table 2 was also parsed by a script to recount its aggregates (D5).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel E. Steffy (Oakland University), Kati Wolter (ZIB); alphabetical byline, no part credited (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: the first paper of his exact-LP line, the algorithm that the 2016 journal version S013 (card c04) extends and that the thesis S032 (card b01) contains. The runs use the exact MIP framework of Cook, Koch, Steffy and Wolter [7] (p. 8), and MIPLIB 2010 (S001) LP relaxations are part of its troublesome pool (p. 9).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**:
  - Floating-point LP codes can give unreliable results, and extended-precision or exact solutions are sometimes needed: as subroutines of exact MIP [1, 7] and for computer-assisted results in mathematics [3–5, 9, 14–16] (p. 3).
  - The stated purpose is "to develop and study a technique to build extended precision LP solutions using an approximate LP solver as a subroutine", with faster exact LP solving as a byproduct (p. 3).
  - The starting facts come from the literature: floating-point bases are often optimal for real-world LPs (Dhiflaoui et al.; Koch on NETLIB), and an exactly computed basic solution certifies its own optimality whatever produced the basis (p. 5). QSopt_ex exploits this by incremental precision boosting, which becomes slow when extended-precision pivoting or hard rational systems are needed (Algorithm 1, p. 5).
  - The gap: iterative refinement is classical for linear systems and was tried inside the exact-solution step of QSopt_ex [8], but to the authors' knowledge nobody had described it for linear programming (pp. 5, 7).
  - Transferable technique: lift a classical numerical device (iterative refinement for linear systems) one level up, to the whole optimization problem, instead of applying it inside its linear algebra (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Solve the LP approximately, then repeatedly solve a transformed LP with the same matrix A: the right-hand side replaced by the scaled primal residual ∆P(b − Ax̂), the bounds shifted to −∆P x̂, the objective replaced by the scaled reduced costs ∆D(c − Aᵀŷ); add the scaled corrections and repeat (Algorithm 2, p. 6).
  - Arithmetic split: only the LP solver works in floating point; the transformed data, residuals and corrections are exact rational (abstract p. 3; p. 7).
  - Relative to QSopt_ex's Algorithm 1, the change is to refine the solution by a sequence of double-precision LP solves instead of raising the simplex's working precision (pp. 5–6). Unchanged: a floating-point simplex as subroutine, bases that correspond directly to bases of the original problem, and exact verification of a basis (abstract p. 3; pp. 5–7).
  - Primal and dual are refined together, by changing the feasible region and the objective (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - LP in computational form min{cᵀx : Ax = b, ℓ ≤ x ≤ u}, presented with x ≥ 0 for clarity; A of full rank, n > m; an optimal solution assumed to exist for Algorithm 2; regular bases (pp. 3–4). Infeasible and unbounded LPs are handled through auxiliary LPs (pp. 7–8).
  - The oracle assumption behind convergence: if the floating-point solver always returns solutions accurate to an absolute tolerance, accuracy 1/α^k follows after k rounds (p. 7); the authors state that there is no guarantee of this, that it holds often in practice, and that poorly conditioned LPs can make the floating-point solver return meaningless results (p. 7).
  - The solver must return basic solutions, or a tight-at-bound test replaces the basis (p. 7).
  - Test sets (p. 9): NETLIB (92 LPs, numerically easy in practice though mostly ill-posed per Ordóñez–Freund) and a troublesome set: from over 900 LPs (NETLIB, Mittelmann, Mészáros' sets, LP relaxations of MIPLIB 2010) those on which SoPlex0 found no optimal basis, minus instances running over 24 h, minus iprob (too poorly conditioned for double precision), minus some instances of the over-represented classes delf, large and small; 74 remain. Reader's count of Table 2: these three classes still supply 52 of the 74 (20 delf, 21 large, 11 small) (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (devices, with proofs):
  - Prop. 3.1 (pp. 6–7), by direct algebra on residuals and reduced costs: for any x̂, ŷ and scale factors, primal and dual feasibility within ε of the transformed problem P̂ correspond to feasibility within ε/∆ of P; complementary slackness, optimality and basic solutions for the same basis B correspond one to one. It is proved before the details are explained (p. 6).
  - Scaling: ∆P = min{1/δP, α∆P}, and ∆D likewise, so the largest violation becomes 1; growth is capped at α = 10^12 so that 1/α lies just below the LP solver's tolerance of 10^-9 (pp. 6–7).
  - Correction: x^{k+1} = x^k + x*/∆P and y^{k+1} = y^k + y*/∆D, then nonbasic variables are set exactly to their bounds so that B stays a valid starting basis (line 27, pp. 6–7).
  - Warm start from the previous basis and reuse of the LU factorization, which SoPlex otherwise recomputes every 200 iterations (pp. 7–8).
  - Exact solution by two routes: recompute the basic solution of B exactly, or apply rational reconstruction to the refined solution (p. 7). Suggested speed-ups: update the transformed data instead of recomputing it, round correctors to simple rationals (as Wan does), choose scale factors as powers of 2 (p. 7).
  - Infeasibility and unboundedness (pp. 7–8): a Farkas proof as the dual solution of a homogenised LP (5), an unbounded ray from LP (6); Prop. 3.2 shows that rays and Farkas proofs are valid for P̂ iff valid for P, so a solve that wrongly claims optimality on an infeasible LP can still be continued (proof by direct algebra, p. 8).
  - Precision boosting inside the loop is proposed, not implemented: Algorithm 2 could boost the working precision of its non-exact steps when needed (p. 7).
  - The novelty claim is hedged with a precedent: aspects of the approach may have been used in software packages, and some interior-point solvers resolve with the reduced-cost vector as objective, which amounts to a single dual refinement step (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Implementation: SoPlex 1.6 with GMP 4.3.1; termination tolerances εP = εD = 0, α = 10^12, kmax = 5 (so always five rounds); no LP preprocessing or scaling; the infeasible/unbounded extension is not implemented (p. 8). Hardware: two Xeon X5672 (4 cores, 3.2 GHz), 48 GB (p. 8).
  - Like-for-like arms: SoPlex0 is the same code with kmax = 0; both run with the floating-point tolerance tightened from the default 10^-6 to 10^-9, which found more optimal bases; 10^-12 helped SoPlex0 on some instances but caused numerical trouble and false infeasibility or unboundedness claims on others (pp. 8–9).
  - Quality checks: the returned basis is tested for optimality by exact computation, and it warm-starts QSopt_ex, whose running time measures the gain for exact LP, at the price of discarding SoPlex+'s refined solution (p. 8). Violations of the returned solutions are computed exactly (p. 9).
  - Aggregation: geometric means, times rounded up to 0.1 s (p. 9). Single runs; no seeds or permutations.
  - NETLIB (Table 1, p. 9): LP iterations 773 against 775, LP time 0.21 s against 0.34 s, QSopt_ex time 0.21 s against 0.19 s, optimal bases 89/92 against 92/92 (SoPlex0 fails on dfl001, etamacro and pilot87). The text reads this as no significant overhead (p. 9); in relative terms the geometric-mean LP time rises by 62% (my arithmetic).
  - Troublesome set (Table 2, pp. 9–10): SoPlex+ returns an optimal basis on all 74, within rounds 1–2 except on three (reader's count: mod2 3, unitcal_7 4, world 4); violations range from 5.3·10^-14 to 1.3·10^-7 for SoPlex0 and from 4.3·10^-92 to 4.6·10^-31 for SoPlex+; geometric means: LP iterations 4,680 against 4,863, LP time 1.11 s against 2.00 s (+80%, my arithmetic), QSopt_ex time 17.43 s against 1.40 s. Exact-LP speed-up (SoPlex plus QSopt_ex): an arithmetic mean of per-instance ratios of 9.76, a maximum stated as "239 (mod2)", and 5.45 computed "as a ratio of the geometric means" (p. 9). Named slowdowns: gams30a (tiny) and fome13, the latter because QSopt_ex's first double-precision solve failed to recognise the dual feasibility of the optimal basis and restarted from scratch (pp. 9, 11).
  - Reader's recount of Table 2 by script (74 rows): the 9.76, the column geometric means and the two slowdowns reproduce. The largest ratio, 239.3, belongs to dfl001 (4,143.1 s against 17.3 s), not to mod2 (82.1). And 5.45 reproduces only as (1.11 + 17.43)/(2.00 + 1.40), a ratio of sums of column geometric means; the geometric mean of the per-instance totals gives 19.25 s against 4.52 s, a factor of 4.26 (pp. 9–10). Neither slip changes the direction of the result.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Builds on and against QSopt_ex (Applegate, Cook, Dash, Espinoza): credits its speed when double precision finds the basis, names where it slows, and proposes to take the idea "one step further" (p. 5).
  - Credits the linear-systems lineage (Wilkinson; Golub–van Loan; Ursic–Patarra; Wan; Pan; Saunders et al.) and Cook–Steffy's use of refinement inside QSopt_ex (p. 5); Koch's NETLIB results needed 128-bit arithmetic on some instances, where SoPlex+ found all optimal bases in double precision (p. 9).
  - Places itself against commercial practice: CPLEX's feasibility tolerance cannot be set below 10^-9 (p. 9).
  - Written for a symbolic-computation venue: a textbook-style simplex background for readers outside LP (p. 3) and a pointer to exact and robust computational geometry (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - A purpose sentence in the introduction; correctness proved before details (pp. 3, 6).
  - The novelty claim is hedged twice: "To our knowledge" and a disclosed partial precedent in interior-point codes (p. 7).
  - Main-observation paragraphs follow each table, with adjectives next to numbers: no "significant overhead" (p. 9), "without experiencing a large overhead" (p. 9), "considerably faster" (p. 11); the tables print the numbers that let a reader weigh them.
  - Two aggregations of the same speed-up are printed (9.76 and 5.45) instead of one (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution layers: an algorithm (refinement of whole LPs), theory (Props. 3.1–3.2), an implementation in SoPlex, and computational evidence for extended-precision and exact LP (pp. 6–11).
  - Limits printed: no guarantee for the floating-point oracle, and poorly conditioned LPs may need extended precision (p. 7); the infeasible/unbounded extension is not implemented (p. 8); iprob is excluded as too poorly conditioned (p. 9); a 10^-12 tolerance fails (p. 9); the refined solution is lost when only the basis is passed on (p. 8).
  - Future work: a tighter integration of incremental precision boosting, rational reconstruction and iterative refinement, in particular for instances too poorly conditioned for double precision (p. 11).
