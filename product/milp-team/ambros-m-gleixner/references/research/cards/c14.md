### S015 · Enabling research through the SCIP optimization suite 8.0 (ACM Transactions on Mathematical Software 2023, DOI 10.1145/3585516)
**Read level**: full. arXiv 2303.07101v1, pp. 1–20 read completely (all sections, tables, acknowledgements, affiliations); the references (pp. 21–25) were scanned.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: K. Bestuzheva, M. Besançon, W.-K. Chen, A. Chmiela, T. Donkiewicz, J. van Doornmalen, L. Eifler, O. Gaul, G. Gamrath, L. Gottwald, C. Graczyk, K. Halbig, A. Hoen, C. Hojny, R. van der Hulst, T. Koch, M. Lübbecke, S. J. Maher, F. Matter, E. Mühmer, B. Müller, M. E. Pfetsch, D. Rehfeldt, S. Schlein, F. Schlösser, F. Serrano, Y. Shinano, B. Sofranac, M. Turner, S. Vigerske, F. Wegscheider, P. Wellner, D. Weninger, J. Witzig. Gleixner is 10th of 35; Bestuzheva stands first and the rest is roughly alphabetical (p. 1). Team member Lübbecke is a co-author. · **Position in trajectory**: journal version of the SCIP 8.0 release report of December 2021 (ref. [8], p. 21), written during his ZIB group headship; affiliation ZIB-AIS2T and HTW Berlin (p. 19). There is no contributions statement, so no section can be assigned to him: read it as group practice.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The release is recast as a paper "on the role of the SCIP Optimization Suite in supporting research", with the 8.0 developments presented as examples of SCIP's use as a research tool (abstract, p. 1). Research use is shown by citing works "independent of the authors of this paper" on symmetry, branching, machine learning and applications (p. 3). The main component, the nonlinear framework, is entered from a counted defect. Explicit reformulation could make a solution feasible for the reformulated problem but infeasible for the original one (example exp(ln(1000)+1+xy) ≤ z), and this happened on 7% of MINLPLib (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: open a redesign with the defect and its frequency on a public library, not with the new design (p. 7).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: SCIP is a plugin-based branch-cut-and-price core for constraint integer programs (pp. 2–3). The full list of 8.0 features is on p. 6. Main ideas:
  1. An expression framework with handlers for basic operators only; high-level structures such as quadratics are recognized but no longer made explicit as a new expression type (p. 6).
  2. A rewritten cons_nonlinear that keeps the original constraints and applies "only transformations that, in most situations, do not relax the feasible space when taking tolerances into account". The reformulations needed for the linear relaxation are handled implicitly by annotating expressions; auxiliary variables are known, and branching avoids them (p. 7).
  3. Why a rewrite and not a check: an a-posteriori feasibility check would find the violation, but with the link from original to presolved problem lost it could not say which separation, domain reduction or branching would repair it (p. 7).
  4. Nonlinear handlers (quadratic, bilinear, convex/concave, quotient, semi-continuous, SOC, default) and separators (RLT, principal 2×2 minors, rank-1 intersection cuts off by default) (p. 8).
  5. Symmetry for general variables via SST cuts, suborbitope detection, linear-time symresack cover separation (p. 11); PADM with an option to re-optimize with the original objective, and the new DPS heuristic (pp. 12–13); dual postsolve in PaPILO, which lets SoPlex 6.0 offer PaPILO as an optional presolver (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: when a reformulation produces answers that are feasible only for the reformulated model, do not bolt on a final check. Keep the original model as the object of record, attach the reformulation as annotation, and repair violations where they arise (p. 7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MINLP (1) with g given in algebraic form from known basic expressions; variables may be unbounded but are not fixed to ±∞ (p. 2). CIPs: once all integer variables are fixed, an LP or NLP remains (p. 2). Symmetry handling is restricted to formulation symmetries, since detecting all symmetries is NP-hard (p. 10). Decompositions are bordered block-diagonal partitions with linking rows and columns, user-supplied since 7.0; DPS needs linking constraints only, PADM linking variables only (pp. 11–12). Data:
  - release comparison: MIPLIB 2003, 2010, 2017 and COR@L, 347 MILP instances; MINLPLib, 113 instances (p. 4);
  - component study: the 1678 MINLPLib instances handled by both versions (p. 9);
  - extensions: 194 MISDP instances (p. 16) and the 200 PACE 2018 Steiner tree instances (p. 17).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (no proofs): Extended formulation (MINLPext) in which auxiliary variables replace subexpressions; for example, log(x) → w2 turns log(x)² + 2 log(x) y + y² into a convex bivariate quadratic (pp. 7–8). Nonlinear handlers are managed by cons_nonlinear, not by the SCIP core (p. 8). Symresack minimal-cover separation goes from quadratic to linear time because the components of the auxiliary graph are paths or cycles (p. 11). Orbisacks interacting with set packing or partitioning are upgraded to orbitopes (p. 11). DPS splits the linking right-hand sides into a partition (p_q) and penalizes slacks with weights λ (pp. 12–13). PaPILO checks every reduction for validity before applying it (p. 13). SoPlex has 80-bit extended precision and iterative refinement, and computes exact rational LP solutions by continued fractions or a symbolic LU factorization (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:

Runs with "numerically inconsistent" results are excluded (p. 4). The count is not given, but Tables 1–2 list 1708 and 558 runs, so 27 and 7 runs were dropped (pp. 4–5).

  - MILP (Table 1, p. 5): 1478 vs 1445 solved, time ratio 1.17, [1000, tilim] 1.52, diff-timeouts 84 vs 51. On both-solved the gain is 1.12, and the text reads this as a speed-up that comes more from newly solved instances (pp. 4–5).

  - Component study, classic against new nonlinear handling (pp. 8–10). Classic is the main development branch of 23.08.2021; new is the same branch with only the nonlinear handling replaced, plus symmetry detection extended to nonlinear constraints (p. 9).   - Status counts come before times (Table 3, p. 9). Final solution infeasible for the original problem: 481 → 49. A caution paragraph precedes the table (SDP accuracy, relative tolerances in the SDP solvers against absolute ones in SCIP-SDP, a tighter Mosek feasibility tolerance). "We have relaxed the tolerances" is admitted next to the conclusion "significantly improved" (p. 16).

Solved 199, 198, 176 and 67 for SCIP-Jack with Gurobi as LP solver, SCIP-Jack with SoPlex, SPDP and Gurobi 9.5, which gives "roughly 17 times faster than SPDP, and 96 times faster than Gurobi" (p. 17). "More than six orders of magnitude" against commercial MILP solvers on larger instances is stated without a table (p. 17). 19 Euclidean STPs were solved for the first time; GeoSteiner 5.1 solved none of them in a week (p. 18).

  - Transferable technique: for a rewrite, compare a dated development branch with the same branch plus only the new component. Run each instance also with two row/column permutations, and give status counts (infeasible final incumbent, failed, solved, time limit) before any timing (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: read 'all' against 'both-solved'. If the 'all' gain (1.17) is well above the both-solved gain (1.12), the progress is newly solved instances, not faster solving (pp. 4–5).

### S026 · PaPILO: A Parallel Presolving Library for Integer and Linear Optimization with Multiprecision Support (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2023, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2022.0171)
**Read level**: full. arXiv 2206.10709v3 (titled "...Integer and Linear Optimization..."), pp. 1–20 read completely; the bibliography (pp. 21–22) was scanned; p. 23 is an arXiv stub.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Hoen, L. Gottwald. The order on v3 p. 1 is Gleixner, Hoen, Gottwald; the self-citation of the software on p. 22 lists Gleixner, Gottwald, Hoen. · **Position in trajectory**: Gleixner is first author (ZIB and HTW Berlin, p. 1). PaPILO has been one of SCIP's default presolvers since 7.0 (p. 3), and it supplies exact presolving to exact SCIP (pp. 3, 10). SKILL.md credits the code to Gottwald and Hoen (Attribution; H10); this paper carries the design rationale.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A gap in available software: "the current lack of (a) solver-independent implementations that (b) exploit parallel hardware, and (c) support multiprecision arithmetic" (abstract, p. 1). Presolve matters: disabling it costs 11× in CPLEX and 9× in Gurobi on instances of at least 10 s, by Achterberg–Wunderling 2013 and Achterberg et al. 2019 (p. 3). It is usually kept cheap by strict working limits, and parallelism is meant to reduce the overhead when presolving is more aggressive or the problem is large (abstract, p. 1). Priority claim: PaPILO is "the first project that offers these functionalities" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A transaction-based design (pp. 6–7):
  - presolvers have read-only access to the data;
  - each returns its reductions as transactions together with the conditions their validity depends on;
  - after all presolvers of the round have finished, the core goes through the transactions one by one in a deterministic order, applies those whose conditions still hold, and discards the rest (commented log, Fig. 3, p. 7).

  This avoids both copies of the problem and locking for many tiny "atomic" tasks (p. 6). Two consequences: a presolver written sequentially joins the parallel framework automatically, and the result is the same for every thread count (p. 9). Two levels of parallelism via TBB (pp. 8–10):
  - across presolvers (task parallelism);
  - within presolvers (data parallelism): probing per variable with a per-thread activity vector that is reset by reverting the changes; DomCol and Sparsify; hashing of parallel rows and columns; optional internal loops in seven more presolvers.

  Kept from standard practice: the round-based loop with complexity classes fast O(n log n), medium O(N log N), exhaustive O(N²) and delayed (only Sparsify), and an abort factor (p. 4, footnotes 4–5); the presolvers themselves come from the literature (Table 1, pp. 5–6). The arithmetic type is a template REAL over double, quad and unlimited-precision rational (Boost multiprecision); in rational mode the comparison and feasibility tolerances can be set to zero (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: parallelize a pass of many small interacting modifications by letting workers propose (reduction plus preconditions) and one core commit in a fixed order; determinism then comes for free (pp. 6–9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP with ranged rows L ≤ Ax ≤ U, bounds and integrality (p. 2); LPs are included. Indicator variables and constraints are not supported (p. 10). The validity conditions of a transaction are locks on bounds and rows (p. 7). Test sets (pp. 10–11):
  - the MIPLIB 2017 Collection, 1,065 instances, minus those with indicators and hawaiiv10-130 (out of memory), gives 1,022 instances; two seeds give 2,044 runs;
  - the 240-instance Benchmark Set for the transaction logs.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (no proofs; the invariant is that no conflicting reductions are applied, checked a posteriori per transaction, pp. 6–7, 20): Conflicts are avoided by commit order (SimplifyIneq before Substitution) and by bundling (pairwise parallel rows handled in one transaction) (pp. 7–8). Duplicate reductions from parallel probing are found and the later one is discarded (footnote 8, p. 9). Not every presolver gains from internal parallelism; SimpleProbing's checks are too cheap (p. 10). A substitution is cancelled if it would increase the number of non-zeros (p. 19). When only one thread is available, an optional sequential mode applies reductions immediately (p. 9). Exact presolving with zero tolerances is "more conservative" on numerically difficult instances, that is, it yields fewer reductions (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: PaPILO 2.0 (githash 12cd349), GMP 6.1.2, Boost 1.72, oneTBB 2021.3.0; Xeon E7-8880 v4 at 2.20 GHz, 100,000 MB; reading time excluded; shifted geometric means with shift 1 (p. 11). Seed-instance pairs count as separate observations (p. 10).
  - Scaling from 1 to 32 threads (Table 2, p. 12). All thread counts return the same reductions (p. 11), so time is the only output that varies. 1,617 runs presolve in under 1 s (p. 11). 'All' goes from 0.74 to 0.39 s. On ≥ 10 s (134 runs) 32 threads are 5.4× faster than one; on ≥ 100 s (49 runs) 7.0×, and 1.7× faster than 8 threads; going from 16 to 32 threads gains 1.2× (p. 12).
  - Amdahl reference curves for 5%, 10% and 20% sequential code put ≥ 10 s just above the 20% curve and ≥ 100 s between 10% and 20% (pp. 12–14). The best case, ex10, where probing shrinks 17,860 variables and 69,608 constraints to 19 and 53, reaches 12.4× at 32 threads (pp. 12–13).
  - Sequential modes (Table 3, p. 14), on 990 instances. Excluded: infeasible or unbounded detections, and four proteindesign* instances on which immediate mode passes a threshold that triggers an expensive probing call; the authors say this would distort the results and promise a fix (p. 13). Immediate mode needs 38% fewer rounds but is slower overall (0.78 vs 0.73 s) and faster only on ≥ 10 s and ≥ 100 s; non-zeros are reported next to time (pp. 13–15).
  - Conflicts (Tables 4–7, pp. 15–17). Verbose logs record every transaction. 16 of the 71 presolver pairings ever conflict, and only ColSingleton, DoubleToNEq, Substitution and Sparsify conflict in more than 5% of their rounds (p. 15). Redundant conflicts are detected by a heuristic and are a lower bound (p. 16). Causes are explained per pair, with the instances that generate most conflicts (supportcase12, savsched1, co-100, nw04) (pp. 17–19).
  - Inside solvers: disabling PaPILO in SCIP costs 6%, with no setup given (p. 3); exact presolving cuts the mean solving time of exact SCIP by 35.8% to 84.1%, cited from Eifler and Gleixner 2022 (p. 10).
  - No comparison of reductions or time against another presolver; no significance tests.
  - Transferable technique: design a parallel component so that every thread count produces the identical output; a thread-scaling table is then a like-for-like comparison by construction (pp. 9, 11).
  - Transferable technique: overlay Amdahl curves for 5%, 10% and 20% sequential code on the measured speed-ups to estimate the sequential fraction (pp. 12–14).
- **D6 Positioning**: The presolve lineage runs from Brearley et al. 1975 to Gemander et al. 2020 (pp. 2–3), and most presolvers in Table 1 are referenced to sections of Achterberg et al. 2019 (pp. 5–6). Achterberg's measurements are used as the case for presolving (p. 3). Users named: the testing of PDLP (Applegate et al. 2021) and SCIP's default presolvers (p. 3). Interfaces to Gurobi, HiGHS, SCIP, SoPlex and OR-Tools (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Example first: a two-variable knapsack whose branch-and-bound tree shrinks from 5 nodes to one LP solve after coefficient tightening (Fig. 1, p. 2). A commented transaction log shows the mechanism (Fig. 3, p. 7). The conflict section opens with two questions mapped to tables (p. 15). An unexpected result is reported as such ("Surprisingly", p. 14). The notation of Table 7 is defined before the table (pp. 16–17). Slips: the 38% fewer rounds are referred to Table 7 but are in Table 3 (pp. 13–14), and "(14.72 vs. 4.37)" leaves open which mode has which count (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a software library and a design pattern (p. 20). Claims: conflicts are rare and not a bottleneck; instances of at least 10 s presolve about five times faster; the whole Collection almost 50% faster (p. 20). Limits printed:
  - no indicator constraints (p. 10);
  - little room for parallel gains, since most instances presolve in under 1 s (p. 11);
  - the proteindesign* pathology is still to be fixed (p. 13);
  - DoubleToNEq, Substitution and Sparsify generate non-redundant conflicts, and avoiding them would "probably come at a higher cost" (pp. 18–19).

  The single reductions are not certified: the transaction check guards consistency with the modified problem; it is not a proof (pp. 6–7). Certification comes later with VeriPB logging (S007 p. 17).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence, with Heuristic H10 ✅: the arithmetic type is a template REAL over double, quad and rational (p. 10), and new presolvers implement PresolveMethod<REAL> (p. 20). In exact SCIP presolve changes the problem, so it is a deciding step, and it runs in rational arithmetic with zero tolerances (pp. 3, 10).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: deterministic output means the thread-count arms produce the same presolved problem (pp. 9, 11). Where the arms do differ (immediate vs delayed application), the size of the result (non-zeros) is reported next to time (p. 14). Exclusions are named with reasons: indicators and memory (p. 10), proteindesign* (p. 13).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the belief tested concerns the authors' own design (that conflicts between parallel reductions would eat the gain). It is measured on full transaction logs and answered with counts (16 of 71 pairings) and a verdict (pp. 15, 20).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: limits are printed with a cause and, for proteindesign*, a promise (no indicators, p. 10; proteindesign*, p. 13; substitution-type conflicts, pp. 18–19). The certification gap is closed later by VeriPB proof logging in PaPILO 2.2 (S007 p. 17).
- Heuristic H6 ✅: questions first (p. 15). Heuristic H8 ✅: seed-instance pairs as observations (p. 10); brackets by "at least one of the settings" (p. 11). Inner Tensions (significance testing) ✅: no significance tests.
- Roundtable Card ⚠ variant (the likely disagreement with Achterberg): the paper takes Achterberg's 11× and 9× presolve numbers as its own motivation (p. 3). Any disagreement is about certifiable reductions, not about the value of presolve.
- New-pattern candidate: *deterministic parallel commit*: workers propose, one core validates and commits in a fixed order, so the output does not depend on the thread count (pp. 6–9, 11).
- New-pattern candidate: *pay for exactness with parallelism*: the slowdown of rational arithmetic is said to be partly offset by PaPILO's parallelization (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Transaction pattern for parallel presolve: read-only workers; transactions lock the bounds and rows they rely on; deterministic sequential commit; discard when a lock is violated (pp. 6–7).
- [D2] Organize passes by worst-case complexity per round (fast, medium, exhaustive, delayed). Stop by an abort factor on bound changes and deleted columns, side changes and deleted rows, and coefficient changes, each relative to the problem size (p. 4).

### S060 · A proof system for certifying symmetry and optimality reasoning in integer programming (arXiv preprint 2023, arXiv 2311.03877)
**Read level**: full. arXiv 2311.03877v2: the main text (pp. 1–12) and the whole appendix, A.1 auxiliary results with Prop. 1, A.2 technical proofs, A.3 verification examples (pp. 17–25), were read completely; the references (pp. 13–16) were scanned.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: J. van Doornmalen, L. Eifler, C. Hojny. The byline is alphabetical; Gleixner is third, at ZIB and HTW Berlin (p. 1). · **Position in trajectory**: the proof-logging line (SKILL.md Trajectory, 2020–2025). It carries the pseudo-Boolean proof system of Bogaerts, Gocht, McCreesh and Nordström over to general MIP, and the coverage limits of Gleixner's own VIPR format [14] are its stated motivation (p. 2). Theory only: no code, no experiments.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Proving solver code correct "seems prohibitively difficult", and incorrect results keep being reported [3, 13, 19, 34] (p. 1). The viable alternative is a proof of correctness checked independently; it is standard in SAT and exists for SMT, pseudo-Boolean optimization and exact MIP (p. 1). The entry gap: MIP has no formal proof system, and VIPR [14] covers no reasoning that removes feasible solutions, such as symmetry handling (p. 2). The task: generalize [12] from binary programs to unbounded and continuous variables and to a broad range of MIP techniques (abstract; p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A configuration ⟨C, D, g, z, T, ε⟩ consists of core constraints, derived constraints, objective, objective bound, branching tree and ε; it is (F, f)-valid under (V1)–(V4) (Def. 3, p. 3). A valid configuration with ∅ ∈ D certifies optimality (if z < ∞) or infeasibility (Thm. 1, p. 4); a trivial starting configuration is valid (Thm. 2, p. 4). A proof is a sequence of rule applications, each proven to preserve validity (pp. 4–7).
  - New against [12]: the preorder that keeps symmetry and dominance reductions consistent with each other, mostly abstract in [12], is made concrete by a C-consistent branching tree (T1)–(T7). Its node lists σ_v of signed variable indices extend along tree paths, and two solutions are compared by an ε-strict lexicographic order on σ_v at their deepest common node (Defs. 1–2, pp. 2–3). The ε handles continuous variables, the boundedness condition (T6) keeps chains finite, and the tree captures locality in branch and bound (pp. 2–3).
  - Also new: [12] can encode the preorder on binary vectors as linear inequalities, which is impossible with continuous and unbounded variables, so the tree must be encoded as a graph structure (pp. 11–12).
  - Kept from [12]: the core/derived split and the redundance- and dominance-based rules with witnesses (p. 3, pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The abstract layer treats constraints as arbitrary subsets of R^n (p. 2). The MIP layer is min{cᵀx : Ax ≤ b, x ∈ Z^p × R^(n−p)} with rational data A, b and c (p. 7); constraints are linear inequalities, integrality conditions and implications [A ▷ C] (p. 7). Over unbounded variables, local inequalities cannot be recast as global ones, so a verifier needs native implications (p. 11). Symmetries are formulation symmetries, chosen because a pair (γ, π) is easy to verify (p. 9). The lexicographic linearization (5) needs integer variables with bounded domains [L, U] (p. 10). (T6) needs finite bounds on the variables in σ_v; where they do not follow from the variable bounds, the certifier must derive them before installing the tree (pp. 3, 12).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (with proofs):
  - Rules: implicational derivation (Thm. 3), resolution (Cor. 1), objective bound update (Thm. 4), objective function update (Thm. 5), redundance-based strengthening with a witness ω (Thm. 6), dominance-based strengthening (Thm. 7), ε shrinkage (Thm. 8), transfer (Thm. 9), deletion (Thm. 10), tree exchange (Thm. 11), dimension extension (Thm. 12) (pp. 4–7).
  - Proof devices:
    1. each rule is proved by checking which of (V1)–(V4) it can change (pp. 5–7; App. A.2, pp. 21–24);
    2. the proof of Thm. 7 alternates (V4) and the witness to build a sequence x^i, then shows the sequence is finite: on a finite tree with bounded entries, strict ε-increases at a node cannot repeat forever (argument by the smallest index that decides the lexicographic difference infinitely often) (pp. 5–6);
    3. Lemmas 1–5: the children partition a node's region, the relations are a preorder and a strict order, mixed transitivity, consistency survives added core constraints (pp. 17–19);
    4. Prop. 1 derives the lexicographic inequality (6) inductively by dominance with ω = γ; the proof is written to be machine-verifiable through case-distinction "subproofs" (pp. 19–21).
  - Instantiation (pp. 8–11, 24–25):
    - aggregation with multipliers;
    - disjunctive (split) cuts by aggregation on both sides and resolution, which covers Chvátal–Gomory, GMI, MIR, lift-and-project, knapsack cover and flow cover cuts;
    - RLT cuts via dimension extension;
    - bound propagation by aggregation;
    - reduced cost fixing by implicational derivation once z < ∞;
    - dominated columns by an explicit witness that moves x_k's value onto x_j;
    - symmetry: lexicographic reductions; orbitopal reduction, for which the t − 1 adjacent column swaps suffice instead of all t! permutations; SST cuts, derived as x_i ≥ x_j − ε and tightened by rounding or ε shrinkage; orbital branching.
  - Encoding: witnesses as affine maps x ↦ Qx + q with sparse permutation matrices; the tree as a graph with identifiers; ω(x) ⪰ε,T x is evaluated by diving down the tree (pp. 11–12).
  - Transferable technique: certify a reduction that removes feasible solutions by a witness map that sends each removed solution to a kept, no-worse one that is larger in a fixed order. Prove repair chains finite by ε-strict increases on bounded coordinates (pp. 5–6).
  - Transferable technique: verify a cut family as split cuts: one aggregation proof per side of the disjunction, then resolution (pp. 8, 24–25).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a. There is no implementation and there are no computations; Sec. 4 shows certifiability on paper and discusses how a verifier would work (pp. 7–12).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper builds directly on [12], called "seminal" (p. 2), and on the MIP certificate format [14] (Cheung, Gleixner, Steffy), which serves as a blueprint for encoding (p. 11) while its coverage is called "severely limited" (p. 2). - **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract system comes first (Secs. 2–3), then the instantiation technique by technique (Sec. 4), as the roadmap announces (p. 2).   - "As a∗j ≤ a∗j" (p. 9) and "σv(γ(x))v" where index k is meant (p. 21);
  - the appendix's reduced-cost fixing calls the LP value zMIP and then zLP (p. 25);
  - references [16]/[17] and [40]/[41] are duplicates (pp. 14–15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory: a sound proof system of eleven rules (Thms. 3–12 and Cor. 1). It covers cutting planes, presolve, propagation, reduced cost fixing, dominated columns and four symmetry handling methods (pp. 4–11). Limits:

  - the tree has to be fixed in advance, although it is known only after solving (p. 12);

  - no verifier is built.
