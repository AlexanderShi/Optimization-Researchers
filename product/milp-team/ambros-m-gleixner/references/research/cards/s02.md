### S048 · Branching on multi-aggregated variables (CPAIOR 2015, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-18008-3_10)
**Read level**: full. All 15 pages, including Algorithm 1 (p. 7), Tables 1–2 (pp. 11–12) and the references (pp. 13–15). First-page check: title and Ambros M. Gleixner (ZIB) among the five authors (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Gerald Gamrath (first, ZIB), Anna Melchiori (Padua), Timo Berthold (FICO), Domenico Salvagnin (Padua); Gleixner fourth of five; no roles stated (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: a SCIP branching study of the ZIB group; its outlook points to Gleixner's ZIB-Report 12-36 on factorization updates of a reduced basis matrix as one remedy (ref. [41], pp. 13, 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: Presolve multi-aggregates variables (replaces them by affine sums of others). This usually helps, but branching on such a variable "cannot be realized via branching on active variables", and the authors know of no study of the effect (pp. 1, 6). General disjunctions give smaller trees but usually more time, because the candidate list is huge and each branching adds an LP row (pp. 2, 4). Entry: the multi-aggregations are a candidate list that costs nothing and is linear in the model size (pp. 2–3, 6).
- **D2 Key idea**: add to SCIP's full strong branching the general disjunctions of integer multi-aggregated variables with a fractional LP value; score them with SCIP's product rule (ε = 10^-6); ties go to variables, which are evaluated first (Algorithm 1, pp. 6–8). Unchanged: the strong-branching evaluation, the score and the use of cut-off children (node pruned, or a valid bound or inequality added) (pp. 6–8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP (1) with general integers (p. 1). An integer variable is multi-aggregated only if the aggregation keeps it integral, e.g. as an integer combination of integer variables; fill-in is limited by comparing nonzeros removed and added (p. 5). Sets: 335 resource-allocation and scheduling instances, in whose model the assignment variables are often multi-aggregated, minus those solved in presolve or at the root (263 default, 276 pure) (p. 9); general MIPs from MIPLIB and Cor@l with at least one multi-aggregation, minus never-solved instances and "two numerically unstable instances giving slightly different results with both branching rules" (107 default, 76 pure) (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: two strong-branching loops, over fractional variables and then over fractional multi-aggregated disjunctions (Algorithm 1, p. 7). The time cost has a named mechanism: a general disjunction adds a row, so the basis must be refactorized, and propagation, conflict analysis, heuristics and cuts are built for bound changes (pp. 4, 12). No proofs.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP 3.1.0 with SoPlex 1.7.0.4. The baseline is SCIP's full strong branching (SB) on a Xeon X5672, one job per node, with a 7200 s limit (p. 8). Two settings: "pure" (cuts, heuristics, propagation, restarts and conflict analysis off, the optimal value given as cutoff) and default. Metrics: solved counts, counts of instances at least 10% faster or slower, and shifted geometric means (shift 100 nodes, 10 s) on the instances both rules solve (pp. 9–10). On scheduling, pure solves 142 against 117 with −58% nodes and −49% time, and default solves 135 against 126 with −37% nodes and −17% time (Table 1, pp. 10–11). In-solver statistics: integer multi-aggregations are 5.7% of the integer variables, yet 39% of the branchings choose one (p. 11). On general MIP, pure takes +25% time with −13% nodes, and default takes +26% time with +6% nodes (Table 2, p. 12). Count slip (as extracted): the text says the rule "solved one less instance in both settings" (p. 12), but Table 2 lists 55 (SB-default) against 57 (MA-default); only the pure row (33 against 32) fits. No seeds or permutations are mentioned.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an algorithm and a SCIP plugin, "available in the next release of SCIP" (p. 13); the paper does not say whether it would be on by default. Limits with numbers: no gain on general MIP, called "an important negative result" (p. 13), and node reductions exceed time reductions "in all cases" (p. 11). Future: propagation, conflict analysis and heuristics that profit from added constraints, and factorization updates that survive added rows (refs. [41, 42]) (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (component form, step 6): the baseline is the rule being extended (p. 8). It is measured in a stripped "pure" environment that isolates branching and in the default environment, and both are reported (pp. 9–12).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (step 8): two numerically unstable instances whose results differ between the rules are dropped (p. 10).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the general-MIP failure is printed with numbers and a mechanism, and the bottlenecks become named next steps, one of them Gleixner's factorization report (pp. 12–13).
- Heuristic 9 ✅ evidence (MIP side): what presolve has already computed becomes the branching candidates (pp. 2–3, 6); the skill shows H9 mostly on the MINLP side.
- Common Misreading 4 ⚠ (detail): the skill says bylines "were alphabetical until 2017"; this 2015 byline (Gamrath, Melchiori, Berthold, Gleixner, Salvagnin) is not alphabetical (p. 1). The exceptions row of 01-publications §1.4 (2014–2018) allows such cases.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Measure a branching rule alone in a "pure" setting: switch off cuts, heuristics, propagation, restarts and conflict analysis and give the optimal value as cutoff. Report the default setting next to it (p. 9).
- (D5) Next to shifted geometric means, report how many instances got at least 10% faster or slower, and repeat the means on the instances above 100 s (pp. 10–11).
- (D5) Explain a new rule with in-solver statistics: the share of new candidates, how often they are chosen, how early they are first used, and the gain ratio against the best old candidate (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "This is done in order to measure only the impact of branching without side-effects to and from other solver components." (p. 9)
- "We see our results for general MIPs as an important negative result that confirms previous observations by other authors that it is hard to find a branching rule on general disjunctions which is competitive on standard MIP benchmarks." (p. 13)

### S037 · Towards an accurate solution of wireless network design problems (ISCO 2016, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-45587-7_12; arXiv 1604.00513)
**Read level**: full. arXiv v2 (27 Apr 2017), all 13 pages, including Tables 1–3 (pp. 9–10) and the references (pp. 12–13). First-page check: title and Ambros M. Gleixner (ZIB) as second of two authors (p. 1).

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Fabio D'Andreagiovanni (first; ZIB, Matheon/ECMath, IASI-CNR); no roles stated (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the exact and accurate LP tools (QSopt_ex, Gleixner's iterative refinement, exact SCIP) applied to an engineering model while the INFORMS J. Comput. paper on iterative refinement was "to appear" (ref. [15], p. 13). Its check, fixing the integers and solving the rest exactly, is the one Method 1 step 2 dates to 2014.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: SIR-based "natural" formulations have tiny coefficients of widely varying magnitude and big-M rows, and floating-point solvers return coverage plans with errors (abstract; pp. 2, 5). The literature answers mainly with non-compact reformulations (pp. 2, 5). The entry is a belief: practitioners multiply all fading coefficients by a large power of ten (typically 10^12) "with the belief of eliminating numerical errors" (pp. 2, 6). Three numbered contributions follow: why solvers misclassify solutions, a formal and computational test of scaling, and exact or extended-precision checks of floating-point solutions (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea**: read the solver's feasibility test (9), absolute below 1 and relative above, against the SIR rows (pp. 6–7). With entries below 10^-9, the allowed absolute violation can exceed the right-hand side. A violation of the linearized row (2) equals a violation of the ratio (1) times noise plus interference, which is of order 10^-9 (eq. (10)). So a tolerance near 10^-9 "would at best guarantee violations in the order of 1" for the original rows. Scaling by S tightens the absolute tolerance to ε/S (eq. (11)) (pp. 6–7). The solutions are then checked exactly (p. 10).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: the power assignment LP (3)–(4) and the scheduling and power assignment MILP with binary assignments and big-M SIR rows (5)–(8) (pp. 4–5). Ten single-frequency WiMAX instances defined with a major European telecommunications company: 100–900 receivers and 8–45 transmitters, from 808 variables and 8,000 nonzeros to 32,436 variables and 1,231,200 nonzeros (p. 8, fn. 6). The feasibility test (9) is SCIP's; for closed-source solvers the authors "think" it is similar (fn. 4, p. 6).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: CPLEX produces the binary assignment within an hour. The binaries are fixed, and the remaining power LP (a pure feasibility problem) is solved exactly with QSopt_ex. Exact SCIP (pure branch and bound) is tried for proven optimality, and SoPlex iterative refinement to 10^-25 is tried as an accurate but inexact alternative (pp. 8–11). The proof device is the one-line tolerance algebra of (10)–(11) (pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Xeon E3-1290 v2, jobs run separately "to avoid random noise in the measured running time"; CPLEX 12.5.0.0 deterministic with up to four threads; QSopt_ex 2.5.10 and SoPlex 2.0 single-thread (p. 8). Unscaled models: SIR rows are violated by up to 12.6, few receivers are really served (e.g. 13 of 41, 1 of 73), and every fixed-binary LP is exactly infeasible (Table 1, p. 9; p. 10). Scaled models: every fixed-binary LP is exactly feasible, so the binary part is correct, but the floating-point power vectors still count receivers as served that are not (e.g. 24 of 28) (Table 2, p. 9; p. 10). Exact SCIP gave results only on the smallest instances; on one it needed over 20 hours and 139,097,820 nodes and confirmed CPLEX's value 42 (p. 11; the text says "225 transmitters and 20 receivers", while the tables list 225 receivers and 20 transmitters). SoPlex reached violations of 10^-27 to 10^-40 and was at least twice as fast as QSopt_ex except on two sub-second instances; no firm conclusion is drawn, because the codes differ "in many details" and the set is small (Table 3, p. 10; p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: analysis plus computation, a negative result for scaling, and exact or accurate LP as post-processing (pp. 2–3, 11–12). Limits: exact MIP is too slow, which "should not be taken as a proof" against it, since the code lacks most solver features (p. 11); ten instances of one technology (pp. 8, 12). Future: an accurate LP on the continuous variables inside branch and bound to verify the incumbent, and more instances, e.g. DVB-T (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: a practitioners' belief about scaling becomes a per-solution check with counted outcomes: violation, receivers really served, and exact feasibility of the binary assignment. The answer cuts both ways: scaling repairs the binaries but not the power vectors (pp. 2, 6, 9–10).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (step 3): floating point produces the assignment; the integers are fixed and the rest solved exactly, which decides correctness and repairs the continuous values (pp. 3, 10). Where tolerance-level accuracy suffices, iterative refinement replaces exact solving (p. 11).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (step 4): each floating-point solution is checked by a second, exact program, not by the solver that produced it (p. 10).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (step 2): the exact-MIP failure is read as missing solver features, not as a roadblock, and the next step is named (pp. 11–12).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the SoPlex/QSopt_ex timing is not like-for-like; the paper names the mismatch and withholds the conclusion instead of building a matched baseline (p. 11).
- Research Taste mark 4 ✅: floating-point solvers "yield reliable results for the majority of problems and applications", exactness is for verification, computer proofs and unstable instances (p. 7), and an exact power vector is "more than necessary" in practice (p. 11).
- new-pattern candidate: tolerance back-translation. Compute what the solver's absolute and relative tolerances allow in the user's original constraint, and how scaling changes that (eqs. (9)–(11), pp. 6–7; again in S045, file p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Before trusting a model with tiny coefficients, write the solver's feasibility test next to the row and compute the worst violation it allows in the original ratio or nonlinear constraint (eqs. (9)–(10), pp. 6–7).
- (D5) Check a floating-point MILP solution twice: evaluate the original constraints and count what really holds, then solve the LP with the integers fixed in exact arithmetic (Tables 1–2, pp. 9–10).
- (D5) If exact solving is too slow, test an accurate method (iterative refinement to a stated tolerance) against it, and say what the comparison cannot show (Table 3, pp. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "Although the floating-point numerics used in today's state-of-the-art MIP solvers yield reliable results for the majority of problems and applications, there are cases in which results of higher accuracy are desired or needed, such as verification problems, computer proofs, or simply numerically instable instances." (p. 7)
- "Hence, this should not be taken as a proof that exact MIP solvers are in principle not applicable to this application." (p. 11)

### S052 · Methoden zur Reduktion der Rechenzeit linearer Optimierungsmodelle in der Energiewirtschaft – Eine Performance-Analyse (EnInnov 2016: 14. Symposium Energieinnovation, Graz; ohne DOI)
**Read level**: full (vollständig). Alle 13 Seiten einschließlich Tabellen 1–2 und Literatur; die Abbildungen 1–9 sind im Textlayer nur als Beschriftungen erhalten. Erste Seite geprüft: Titel und Ambros Gleixner (ZIB) als zweiter von drei Autoren (S. 1).

**Rolle**: supplement · **Koautoren**: Karl-Kiên Cao (Erstautor, mit Sternchen; DLR Stuttgart), Matthias Miltenberger (ZIB); keine Rollenangaben (S. 1) · **Stellung im Werdegang**: Beginn der Energiesystem-Linie mit dem DLR; S046 (2017) begründet die Wahl eines Innere-Punkte-Verfahrens mit diesem Beitrag (S046 Lit. [1]).

- **D1 Problem und Einstieg**: Energiesystemmodelle stoßen an die Grenze vertretbarer Rechenzeit; ihre LPs erreichen Größen, die bei der Entwicklung der Lösungsalgorithmen keine Rolle spielten (S. 1). Beschleunigungsstrategien werden nach Implementierungsaufwand statt nach Effektivität gewählt, das Verhältnis von Aufwand zu Gewinn ist meist unbekannt, systematische Benchmarks fehlen (S. 1–2). Einstieg: „ein erster Schritt zu einer systematischen Bewertung“ (S. 2).
- **D2 Kernidee**: eine Einteilung in technische Strategien (Solver-Parametrierung, Lösungsverfahren) und konzeptionelle (Modellverkleinerung durch „Abschneiden“ oder geringere Auflösung, serielles Lösen, Dekomposition) (S. 3–5, Abb. 1–2). Dazu ein Benchmark auf 42 REMix-Instanzen, die fünf Modelleigenschaften in den Größenklassen min, std und max variieren (S. 6–7, Tab. 2).
- **D3 Problemklasse und Datenmodell**: reine LPs mit kostenminimaler Zielfunktion (1) und Leistungsbilanz (2), gekoppelt über Zeit, Raum und Technologie (S. 2–3). Komplizierende Variablen und Nebenbedingungen entstehen z. B. aus der DC-Approximation (S. 5, Abb. 3–4). Instanzgrößen werden nach dem CPLEX-Presolve gezählt (S. 7, Fn. 3). Kein MILP; Benders mit MIP-Master wird nur referiert (S. 6).
- **D4 Algorithmus und Implementierung (kurz)**: kein neuer Algorithmus. Drei Schritte: Dual-Simplex gegen Barrier mit und ohne Crossover; CPLEX 12.6.0, Gurobi 5.6.3, MOSEK 7.0.0.114 und Xpress 26.01.04; 1, 4 und 8 Threads auf einer Teilmenge. Gemessen wird die Zeit bis zur Ausgabe einer optimalen Lösung (S. 7–8). Verkleinerungen werden an Instanzpaaren bewertet, die sich nur in einer Eigenschaft unterscheiden (S. 9).
- **D5 Rechenexperimente**: Xeon X5672 mit 48 GB (S. 7, Fn. 4). Nur 33 der 42 Instanzen sind gelöst, da die größten (26–34) mehr als 48 GB brauchen; der Raumvergleich entfällt (S. 8–9). Simplex ist nie schneller als Barrier, und Crossover kostet das 3,3- bis 8-fache (S. 8). Sequentiell ist CPLEX am stabilsten und schnellsten; Gurobi ist auf einigen Instanzen schneller, verletzt aber öfter leicht die Optimalitätsbedingungen (S. 8). Der sequentielle Vergleich nennt nur CPLEX, Gurobi und Xpress; MOSEK erscheint erst bei 4 Threads (S. 8, 10). Ohne Tuning ist das nur „ein grobes Bild“ (S. 8). Mit 4 bzw. 8 Threads beschleunigt sich CPLEX im Mittel um den Faktor 2–3 bzw. 3–4, ohne höheren Spitzenspeicher (S. 8). Zeitspanne 1 → 30 Tage: Modellgröße etwa ×30, Rechenzeit ×5 bis ×70; Technologien und Ausbauplanung meist ×1 bis ×3; ein CPLEX-Ausreißer ×43 (Instanz 18 gegen 17). MOSEK streut weniger, verletzt aber häufiger Zulässigkeitstoleranzen (S. 9–11). Keine Wiederholungen, keine Mittelwertdefinition.
- **D8 Beitrag und Grenzen**: Empfehlung: Barrier ohne Crossover mit mehreren Threads; kürzere Betrachtungszeiträume haben das größte Potenzial (S. 1, 11). Grenzen: 9 ungelöste Instanzen, kein Raumvergleich, kein Tuning, Solverversionen ändern sich (S. 8–9). Ausblick: Validierung am integrierten Problem, Raumauflösungen mit 8760 Zeitschritten, serielle Verfahren und Dekomposition (Benders, je Knoten oder Zeitschritt unabhängige Teilprobleme, Hochleistungsrechner) (S. 11–12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ Variante: eine Praxis ohne Daten (Strategien werden ohne Benchmark gewählt, S. 1–2) wird gemessen, aber als Benchmark ganzer Instanzen, nicht als Prüfung je Entscheidung.
- Method 2 ⚠ Variante: auf der Instanzseite gleich gegen gleich (Paare, die sich in genau einer Eigenschaft unterscheiden, S. 9); der Solververgleich ist nicht gleichartig (ohne Tuning, MOSEK fehlt sequentiell) und wird ausdrücklich grob genannt (S. 8).
- Method 3 ✅ Beleg: Grenzen mit Zahlen (33 von 42, 48 GB), und der Ausblick auf Dekomposition und Hochleistungsrechner wird in S046 (2017) aufgegriffen (S. 8, 11–12).
- new-pattern candidate (Neues Muster): Genauigkeit als Kriterium im Solververgleich, d. h. schnellere Läufe mit mehr Toleranzverletzungen zählen nicht als Gewinn (Gurobi S. 8, MOSEK S. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Eine Modellvereinfachung an Instanzpaaren messen, die sich nur in diesem Merkmal unterscheiden, und den Faktor für Modellgröße und Rechenzeit getrennt berichten (S. 9–10).
- (D5) Neben der Rechenzeit festhalten, welcher Solver Optimalitäts- oder Zulässigkeitstoleranzen verletzt, und das in die Empfehlung einbeziehen (S. 8, 10).
- (D3) Testinstanzen als Gitter aus Größenklassen je Modelleigenschaft erzeugen, damit jede Strategie einer Dimension zugeordnet werden kann (Tab. 2, S. 7).

**Quotes**:
- „Diese Strategien sind zwar häufig ein Teil der Methodik in der Energiesystemanalyse, systematische Benchmarks zur Bewertung ihrer Effektivität werden jedoch meist nicht durchgeführt.“ (S. 1)
- „Für die Interpretation der Ergebnisse gilt es zu beachten, dass der durchgeführte Vergleich lediglich ein grobes Bild liefert.“ (S. 8)

### D004 · The SCIP Optimization Suite 6.0 (ZIB-Report 18-26, 2018, no DOI)
**Read level**: full for the body: file pp. 1–38 (abstract, introduction, §§2–7 with Algorithms 1–3 and Tables 1–4), the acknowledgements and the "Code Contributions of the Authors" (file pp. 38–39). The references (file pp. 39–42) were not read, and the figures survive only as captions. First-page check: title and Ambros Gleixner as first of 25 authors (file pp. 1, 3).

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: 24 developers of SCIP, SoPlex, GCG and UG from ZIB, RWTH Aachen, TU Darmstadt and Lancaster (file p. 3) · **Stated contributions** (file p. 39): the two conflict-driven diving heuristics "were contributed by JaW and AG" (§2.3); MP maintained the LP interfaces with support from FrS, FeS and AG (§2.7); BM, FW and AG did the work on recursive ring packing (§4.1). Benders (SM), cut separation (RG), lookahead branching (CS, GG), symmetry (MP, CH), SoPlex (MM, GG), GCG and UG are credited to others. Who wrote the performance evaluation (§2.2) is not stated; GH and FrS are credited with the test tools Ipet and Rubberband · **Place in the trajectory**: the 2018 release report; GCG structure information is announced for "The upcoming release of the next MIPLIB" (file p. 34).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: what is new in SCIP 6.0, SoPlex 4.0, GCG 3.0, UG 0.8.5, SCIP-Jack 1.3, SCIP-SDP and Zimpl 3.3.6, with a focus on decomposition (abstract; file pp. 3, 5–6). Benders' decomposition in SCIP used to need a problem-specific constraint handler and cut code (file p. 8).
- **D2 Key idea**: a generic Benders' framework with two new plugin types and two constraint handlers for branch-and-check, reachable from GCG's automatic detection, SMPS files, a default plugin, custom plugins or Python (file pp. 8–13). In the MIP core: Farkas diving (dive toward a proof of local infeasibility), conflict diving on new conflict locks, abbreviated lookahead branching, GCD-based scaling in CMIR and the directed cutoff distance for cut selection (file pp. 15–20). In GCG: a modular detection loop over partial decompositions and pricing jobs in a priority queue (file pp. 30–35).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP (1) and MINLP (2) inside constraint integer programming (file pp. 4–5); Benders' cuts are available for continuous or mixed-integer first stages with continuous second stages, and for binary first stages with mixed-integer second stages (file p. 10). Test sets: 666 MIPs from COR@L and the five MIPLIB versions, without duplicates and without instances marked "numerically unstable"; 143 MINLPLib2 instances, without overrepresented classes and numerically troublesome ones. Both are cut to "solvable" sets of 425 and 113 (file p. 13).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: directed cutoff distance (aᵀx̃ − b)/|aᵀd|, with d the unit direction from the LP point to the incumbent, weighted 0.5 next to efficacy 1.0 (file p. 20). Safeguards: subproblems are merged into the master when no cut can be generated, e.g. after numerical trouble (file p. 12); SCIP now double-checks the Farkas rays returned by the LP solver (on by default) and counts re-solves of LPs marked unstable (file p. 22); SoPlex signals unresolved violations with a new status (file p. 23). Ring packing's price-and-verify verifies circular patterns by nonconvex NLPs only when the master LP uses them; if none can be verified, the dual bound is marked invalid and the last valid one is reported (Algorithm 3, file pp. 26–27).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP 6.0.0 + SoPlex 4.0.0 against SCIP 5.0.0 + SoPlex 3.1.0; five seeds including seed 0, each instance-seed pair counted as one observation; numerically inconsistent results excluded; shifted geometric means (1 s, 100 nodes) on "affected", time brackets, both-solved and diff-timeout subsets; Xeon Gold 5122; 7200 s for MIP, 3600 s for MINLP (file pp. 13–14). MIP: 1925 against 1914 solved, a time factor of 1.08 overall, 1.03 on MIPLIB, 1.11 on COR@L and 1.18 in [100,7200]; the both-solved factor of 1.08 shows that the gain comes from the majority, not from the 11 newly solved instances (Table 1, file pp. 14–15). MINLP: 484 against 453 solved, a factor of 1.23 overall and 1.65 in [100,3600], mostly from the 31 newly solved instances (Table 2, file p. 15). Component figures come from intermediate runs: Farkas diving 2% with two seeds (file p. 17), directed cutoff distance 4% (9% in [100,7200]) (file p. 20), lookahead −40% nodes (−35% by fair node number) (file p. 19). GCG against CPLEX 12.8 on Benders: CPLEX "clearly faster" (file p. 34). UG: five repeated runs, because UG is not deterministic; racing gives most of the gain (Table 4, file pp. 36–37). My arithmetic: the "all" rows hold 2113 of 2125 and 561 of 565 observations, consistent with the stated exclusion.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a suite release and a generic Benders' framework shared by SCIP and GCG (file pp. 3, 38). Printed limits: conflict diving stays off "because a thorough tuning and performance evaluation still needs to be conducted" (file p. 18); two MINLP features are switched off because they seem to hurt on average (file p. 15); GCG is slower than CPLEX on Benders (file p. 34); presolve is not "decomposition aware" (file p. 32); UG is untuned, and its test set is unsuited to parallel gains (file p. 37). Future: Benders enhancements, new cut methods and structure detection (file p. 38).

**Method links**:

### S053 · Mixed-integer programming for cycle detection in nonreversible Markov processes (Multiscale Modeling & Simulation 16(1) 2018, DOI 10.1137/16m1091162; arXiv 1609.02063)
**Read level**: full, in the submitted version: ZIB-Report 16-39 = arXiv 1609.02063v1 ("Date of submission: 25.08.2016", file p. 1; title page dated 8 September 2016, file p. 3). All 23 file pages, including the NP-hardness proof (file pp. 12–14), Table 1 (file p. 17) and the references. The 2018 journal text was not read. First-page check: title (spelled "Non-reversible") and Ambros Gleixner (ZIB) among seven authors. In this version the byline is alphabetical with Witzig last (file pp. 1, 3); the work list gives Witzig first.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Isabel Beckenbach, Leon Eifler and Jakob Witzig (ZIB optimization), Konstantin Fackeldey, Andreas Grever and Marcus Weber (ZIB numerical mathematics) (file p. 3); no roles stated · **Place in the trajectory**: the start of the cycle-clustering line, which continues with Eifler et al.'s branch and cut (S091, batch c15, 2024) and ships as SCIP's CycleClustering application (D004 file p. 24).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: spectral clustering (PCCA+, G-PCCA) and dominant-cycle methods assume reversibility or a dominant cycle (file pp. 4, 6). Catalytic and other biological processes are nearly reversible on the simulation time scale, with a small cyclic tendency, and making the matrix reversible would destroy that characteristic (file pp. 6–7). Entry: a clustering model solved by MIP, with "few assumptions on the input data" and optimality guarantees (file p. 5).
- **D2 Key idea**: cycle clustering partitions the states into m ordered clusters and maximizes the net flow between consecutive clusters plus α times their coherence (Defs. 1–2, objective (3), file pp. 9–10), with the model kept separate from the algorithm (file p. 5). Products of binaries are linearized by a standard reformulation, which gave "significantly lower solution times" than global MINLP solvers (file p. 12).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: input a right-stochastic P with stationary distribution π, or any nonnegative W (file pp. 8, 12, Remark 4); binary assignment variables and continuous flow and coherence variables, (4)–(10) (file p. 12). For m ≤ 2 the net flow is zero, and for m = 3 all pairwise net flows are equal (file p. 10); α = 0.001 (file p. 11). Data: three synthetic potentials sampled by hybrid Monte Carlo with drift (n = 20 bins) and the repressilator (n = 200) (file pp. 15–16).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Theorem 6 proves NP-hardness for every m ≥ 3 and α > 0 by reduction from multiway cut. Big weights between consecutive terminals force each terminal into its own cluster, and maximizing coherence then equals minimizing the multiway-cut weight (file pp. 12–14). In SCIP: greedy construction, LP rounding inspired by Schur vectors, and a Kernighan–Lin-type improvement heuristic (file p. 17). An earlier objective (reversibility within clusters) was dropped because classifying near-zero flows is "inherently instable" (Remark 3, file p. 11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP (the Optimization Suite 3.2 report is cited), sequential runs on identical Xeon machines, 3600 s (file pp. 16–17). The synthetic instances are solved to optimality in 0.1–6.9 s, with metastabilities and drift direction recovered (Table 1, file pp. 17–18). The repressilator is not solved in an hour (best 0.1395, bound 0.2345); its clusters match protein–mRNA pairs, while G-PCCA (under 1 s) finds a net flow an order of magnitude smaller and is "added as a reference" without a bound (file pp. 17–20). Gap slip: the text states the bound as a factor of 1.8 (file p. 18) and later of 2.6 (file p. 20); Table 1 gives 0.2345/0.1395 = 1.68 (my arithmetic).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a model, a complexity result and a demonstration (file pp. 4–5, 20). Limits: hardness, and running time far above G-PCCA's (file p. 20). Future: "improving the performance of MIP solvers for cycle clustering by further dedicated techniques" (file p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the printed running-time gap becomes a later project (S091) and a shipped SCIP application (D004 file p. 24) (file p. 20).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: no like-for-like baseline exists. The body says the G-PCCA comparison "does not provide any kind of benchmark" (file p. 18), yet the abstract has the method "outperforming classical spectral analysis methods" (file p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: objectives robust to tiny differences, i.e. objectives that do not classify near-zero quantities (Remark 3, file p. 11; S037's tolerance algebra, pp. 6–7, shows the same hazard in constraints).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Prove hardness of a new clustering model by reduction from a classical partition problem: big forcing weights pin the terminals to clusters, and the remaining objective equals the classical one up to constants (file pp. 12–14).
- (D2) Before fixing an objective, ask whether it classifies near-zero quantities; if it does, replace it by a robust aggregate such as coherence (Remark 3, file p. 11).
- (D5) Against a method that optimizes something else and gives no bound, compare the structure of both answers (projected matrices, cluster averages), and say that this is not a benchmark (file pp. 18–20).

**Quotes**:
- "Second, it is inherently instable to classify such edges as irreversible or reversible because of the small differences." (p. 11)
- "In this sense we want to emphasize that this comparison does not provide any kind of benchmark." (p. 18)
