- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Floating-point MIP solvers' "theoretical promise of global optimality is compromised by roundoff errors". The errors can accumulate into wrong claims of optimality or infeasibility, and nonconvexity makes even an a posteriori analysis hard (p. 2).
  - Where this binds (p. 2): MIP as a tool in mathematics (refs. [11, 12, 19, 29, 30, 33]). These projects rely on floating-point solvers because "the availability, the flexibility, and most importantly the computational performance" of rigorous MIP solvers is limited. In industry: hardware verification (ref. [1]) and compiler optimization (ref. [36]).
  - Entry: the 2013 milestone framework (ref. [16]) "does not include any of the supplementary techniques that are responsible for the strong performance of floating-point MIP solvers today" (p. 2). The paper makes "a first step to address this research gap" in two directions (p. 2):
    - symbolic presolve, because the literature ranks presolve "one of the components—if not the component—with the largest impact" (refs. [2, 4]);
    - primal heuristics, motivated "less to reduce the total solving time" than by usability.
  - Transferable: enter an engineering line by naming the strongest existing system and the standard components it lacks. Pick the one the literature ranks highest plus one justified by a different metric, and say up front which metric each should move (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Carry hybrid precision into every added component, so that the exact solver gains floating-point machinery without giving up exactness (pp. 2, 4).
  - Kept from ref. [16] (p. 4): the exact rational model next to its floating-point approximation; floating-point LP solves postprocessed into safe dual bounds (bound-shift, project-and-shift, exact LP); reliability branching (ref. [3]); exact feasibility checks of every primal solution.
  - Four extensions (p. 4, §3):
    1. Symbolic presolve with PaPILO in rational arithmetic (p. 5).
    2. SCIP's floating-point heuristics with an exact repair step (pp. 5-6).
    3. SoPlex (LP iterative refinement) instead of QSopt_ex (precision boosting) as the exact LP solver (p. 6).
    4. Tighter integration (pp. 7-8): an objective limit for the floating-point LP, and a safe floating-point feasibility pre-check.
  - Strategy changes:
    - exact LPs at depths 4, 8, 16, ... (p. 10);
    - project-and-shift's restriction to fewer than 10000 nonzeros removed (pp. 10-11);
    - bound-shift switched off below a 20% success rate (p. 12);
    - certificates extended to bound-shift (p. 15).
  - Transferable: when porting a fast code's components into a rigorous one, sort them first. Components that only propose (heuristics, LP solves) stay in floating point behind the cheapest exact step that restores the guarantee (repair, safe bounds). Components whose output changes the model (presolve reductions) run fully in rational arithmetic, made affordable by code templated on the number type (pp. 2, 4-5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The problem is a general MILP, min{c^T x | Ax ≥ b, x ∈ Q^n, x_i ∈ Z for i ∈ I}, with rational A, b, c. The floating-point approximation consists of the componentwise closest representable numbers (p. 4). SOS, indicator and semi-continuous constraints are not discussed anywhere (pp. 1-20).
  - Assumptions of the bounding methods (p. 4): bound-shift needs bounds on all variables; project-and-shift needs an interior point or ray of the dual LP, computed by an exact auxiliary LP once per MIP solve.
  - Heuristics:
    - They stay exact only if they do not change the model, e.g. by creating a no-good constraint (p. 5).
    - Equality constraints make exactly feasible floating-point solutions unlikely (p. 5).
    - Repair cost grows with the share of continuous variables, so repair is off above 80% continuous variables (pp. 5, 14).
    - Instances "that allow solutions within tolerances but are infeasible in exact arithmetic" motivate the 1000-failure limit (p. 14).
  - Rational presolve uses the same acceptance thresholds as floating-point presolve, with zero comparison and feasibility tolerances (p. 13).
  - Test sets:
    - fpeasy (57 instances) and numdiff (50), both curated in ref. [16] (p. 9).
    - The MIPLIB 2017 benchmark instances that SCIP 7.0.2 solves within two hours (p. 9). Its size is not stated; Table 7's 218 instance-seed observations imply at least 73 instances (my arithmetic, p. 15).
    - The complete benchmark set, 239 instances in Table 10 (p. 17).
  - Named structures: chip-verification instances with ill-conditioned LPs (p. 12); the infeasible alu10_1 and alu16_2 (p. 15); ex09 (p. 13); x_4 (p. 16).
  - Certificates cover only runs with presolve disabled (p. 15).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Presolve (p. 5):
    - PaPILO is "fully templatized with respect to the arithmetic type". It runs on a rational copy and iterates 13 named presolvers until no sufficiently large reductions are found.
    - The postsolve information is transferred to SCIP as fixings, aggregations, bound changes and constraint strengthenings or deletions, all in rational arithmetic.
    - PaPILO's parallelization is meant to offset the rational overhead.
    - All other SCIP presolvers are disabled (p. 9).
  - Exact repair (pp. 5-6, Fig. 1): fix the integer variables at the heuristic's values, rounding slightly fractional ones, and solve an exact LP over the continuous remainder. Three filters limit the cost:
    1. repair only solutions whose floating-point objective beats the incumbent;
    2. process directly only those that improve it by more than 20%; buffer the rest and process them in increasing objective order once the buffer is full;
    3. keep a hash table of processed integer assignments and discard repeats.
    Further working limits: off above 80% continuous variables, and at most 1000 consecutive failures (p. 14).
  - Exact LP: SoPlex refines on top of double-precision simplex solves, so it can break down on LPs too ill-conditioned for the floating-point solver. QSopt_ex can boost precision up to a fully rational solve (p. 7). The consequence on chip-verification instances is branching on any unfixed integer variable with the highest priority, an "unsophisticated choice" that grows the trees (p. 12).
  - Objective limit (p. 7): the primal bound passed to the floating-point LP is padded by the average increase observed in safe dual bounding, at least 10^-6. If safe bounding fails, the LP is re-solved without the limit.
  - Safe feasibility pre-check (pp. 7-8):
    - Running error analysis (Higham, ref. [28, Alg. 3.2]) is adjusted for |x̄* − x*| and |ā − a| and computes s and μ with |a^T x* − s| ≤ μ (Algorithm 1, p. 8).
    - If s − μ ≥ b_j, the row is satisfied; if s + μ ≤ b_j, it is violated; otherwise the activity is recomputed exactly (p. 7).
    - Directed rounding is rejected as tighter but costlier. The justification is empirical: rows are either tight, where exact arithmetic cannot be avoided, or have slack larger than μ (pp. 7-8).
  - Certificates: VIPR logging is extended to bound-shift. Its dual multipliers are computed in a postprocessing step, which raises bounding time (pp. 15-16).
  - Proof devices: no theorems. The one invariant is the error bound of Algorithm 1. Its adjustment of Higham's bound is stated without proof (p. 7). As extracted, the listing names its outputs "s, y" while the bound uses μ, and μ has no initialization line (p. 8).
  - Transferable: before an exact check, run a floating-point evaluation with a rigorous error bound and go exact only for the undecided band |s − b_j| < μ (pp. 7-8).
  - Transferable: put an exact repair behind a queue: an improvement filter, a direct-processing threshold with a sorted buffer, and a hash of the discrete part. Add a share-of-continuous-variables switch and a consecutive-failure limit (pp. 5-6, 14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Four numbered questions open §4 (p. 8):
    1. How does the revised framework compare with the original, and which components explain the difference? This covers the bounding methods, then SoPlex against QSopt_ex.
    2. What do presolve and heuristics contribute? This includes rational against floating-point presolve.
    3. What do certificates cost?
    4. What is the overall improvement, and how far is floating-point SCIP, at default and reduced?
  - Setup (p. 9):
    - Intel Xeon Gold 5122, 3.6 GHz, 96 GB.
    - CPLEX 12.3.0 "as floating-point LP solver for all experiments"; the same QSopt_ex as ref. [16], or SoPlex 5.0.2; GMP 6.1.4; PaPILO 1.0.1.
    - Thread counts and jobs per node are not stated. PaPILO runs with 1 and 8 threads in Table 5 (p. 14).
  - Protocol:
    - Three seeds for the new code, each instance-seed pair one observation; one seed in comparisons with the original framework (p. 9).
    - A time limit of 7200 s (p. 9).
    - Shifted geometric means with a shift of 0.001 s or 100 nodes (p. 9), and a shift of 1 for presolve counts (p. 13).
    - Arithmetic means for normalized statistics, geometric means for time per call, each on the subset where both variants ran the bounding method (p. 11).
    - Performance profiles (Fig. 2, p. 10).
  - The ladder, one factor per comparison:
    - Framework, with QSopt_ex in both and presolve and heuristics off, one seed (Table 1, p. 9):
      - 7, 6 and 15 more solved (fpeasy, numdiff, miplib); time −28.5%, +3.2% and −41.6%; nodes −25%, +8.5% and −33.2% (p. 10).
      - The gain is attributed to more aggressive project-and-shift (4 of the 6 numdiff gains and 9 miplib instances were subject to the old restriction) and to interleaved exact LPs, the only change expected to cut nodes. Node-processing time barely moves (0.101 against 0.115 on miplib) (pp. 10-11).
      - Part of the gain comes from "a performance bug in the original framework" (fn. 1, p. 11).
    - Exact LP solver, SoPlex against QSopt_ex (Tables 3-4, pp. 12-13):
      - 3 more solved on fpeasy, 6 fewer on numdiff (all chip verification), 18 more on miplib; time −59.2%, −37.7% and −60.1% (p. 12).
      - Time per exact LP −76.2%, −60.6% and −65.4% (p. 13).
    - Presolve (Tables 5-6, pp. 13-14):
      - Rational and floating-point presolve give "virtually the same results" on fpeasy. Exact presolve is more than ten times slower but takes 0.7%, 1.3% and 0.6% of solving time (p. 13).
      - Enabled, it solves 6, 24 and 13 more, with time −35.8%, −84.1% and −42.4% (p. 14).
    - Heuristics (Tables 7-8, pp. 14-16):
      - Repair success 57.6%, 47.3% and 65.3% (Table 7, p. 15). Repair stays below 1% of solving time, with outliers above 5% (p. 15).
      - Time to the first solution −79.7%, −58.4% and −97.2%; primal integral −27.8%, −35.5% and −62.4% (p. 15).
      - Of the −18.9% on numdiff, part is put down to variability on two infeasible instances. A 10-seed rerun keeps the effect on alu10_1, and without the two the gain is 13% (p. 15, fn. 2).
    - Certificates (Table 9, p. 16): only instances both versions solve, "since timeouts would bias the results in favor of the certificate". Solving time rises by 56.3%, 22% and 46%; checking is cheaper than solving; solvability is unchanged because "the solving tree remains unchanged".
    - Overall, on the MIPLIB 2017 benchmark (Table 10, p. 17): 53 against 18 solved, 170 against 76 with a solution, and −90.6% time on the 54 "onesolved" instances (10.7x).
    - Floating-point SCIP 7.0.1 at default and reduced, without cuts, propagation, conflict analysis and symmetry, and with only the PaPILO presolver (Tables 11-12, pp. 17-18):
      - Instances whose results are inconsistent between exact and inexact runs are discarded (p. 17).
      - On 137 onesolved instances: 133, 62 and 45 solved (default, reduced, exact). On the 42 instances all three solve, exact is 8.1x slower than default and 3.9x slower than reduced (text p. 17; Table 11, p. 18).
      - On numdiff, 5.1x slower than reduced (p. 17); 40.3 s against 3.8 s, about 10.6x, against default (Table 12, p. 18; my arithmetic, not stated).
  - Losses printed: numdiff +3.2% (p. 10); the SoPlex loss of 6 (p. 12); higher bound-shift time per call (p. 12); repair outliers (p. 15); the variability on two instances (p. 15); the remaining factors (p. 17). No significance tests are run (§4, pp. 8-18).

- **D6 Positioning**: The paper revises and extends Cook, Koch, Steffy and Wolter (ref. [16]), "The milestone paper", which "can still be considered the state of the art" (p. 2).
  - The critique of the predecessor is by evidence, not adjectives: a probable performance bug behind its project-and-shift limit (fn. 1, p. 11), and a certificate showing incorrect root reasoning on x_4 (p. 16).
  - Related work is surveyed by how each community avoids roundoff (pp. 3-4):
    - Concorde: safe interval arithmetic, and cuts valid by their combinatorics;
    - SAT and pseudo-Boolean solvers: solver-independent verification;
    - SMT: target applications differ (ref. [21]);
    - polyhedral libraries: purely symbolic and "prohibitively slow";
    - hybrid LP methods: boosting (ref. [6]) and refinement (ref. [25]);
    - safe bounds: Neumaier-Shcherbina (ref. [32]) and Steffy-Wolter (ref. [34]).
  - SAT's proof checking (DRAT-trim, ref. [35]) is the rigor model (p. 3).
  - SoPlex is chosen on his own LP result (refs. [25, 26], p. 6). The price of exactness is set against Wolter's thesis (ref. [37]): a factor of 3, and 9.16 on numdiff (p. 17).
  - The novelty claim: "the first time that the impact of symbolic preprocessing routines for general MIP is analyzed" (p. 2).
  - No other exact MIP code is run; the only exact baseline is ref. [16] (§4, pp. 8-18).
  - 10 of 37 references include Gleixner (pp. 19-20, my count).

  - *text-table count slips*: 61 against 71, 57.5% against 57.6%, a range that omits numdiff, Table 8's caption, 239 against "complete" (pp. 13-18; c10 opened this candidate).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Staircase study: change one factor per table, each on top of the previous configuration. The order here: framework with the exact LP solver fixed, then the exact LP solver, presolve, heuristics, certificates, all-on against the predecessor, and the fast solver at default and reduced (pp. 8-18).
- Attribute a speed-up by decomposition. Compare nodes with time per node, tie a node reduction to the only change that can cause it, and trace the gained instances to the removed restriction (4 of 6 on numdiff, 9 on miplib) (pp. 10-11).
- Negative control for variability: heuristics should not speed up infeasible instances. If they seem to, rerun those instances with 10 seeds, name them, and restate the gain without them (18.9% to 13%) (p. 15, fn. 2).
- Exact repair queue for approximate heuristic solutions. Fix the integers and solve an exact LP, after an improvement filter, a 20% direct threshold with a buffer sorted by objective, and a hash of integer assignments. Switch it off above 80% continuous variables and after 1000 consecutive failures (pp. 5-6, 14).
- Safe floating-point check before exact arithmetic: running error analysis decides most rows, and only the undecided band |s − b_j| < μ is recomputed exactly (pp. 7-8, Algorithm 1).
- Pad an approximate LP's objective cutoff by the average safe-bounding increase observed so far, at least 10^-6, and re-solve without the cutoff when safe bounding fails (p. 7).
- To compare exact and floating-point versions of a component, keep the acceptance thresholds identical and set the tolerances to zero. Report the reductions found and the time; shift count means by 1 (p. 13).
- Before measuring, write why the effect could go either way: dominated-column detection may find less, dual inference more (p. 13).
- When your factor differs from a predecessor's, re-measure on the set both studies used and explain the difference there (3.9 against 3 overall; 5.1 against 9.16 on numdiff, put down to presolve) (pp. 17-18).
