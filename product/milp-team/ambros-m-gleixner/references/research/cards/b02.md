- **D1 Problem and entry point**: From an application wall, not from a literature gap. GAMS-based ESOMs had reached run times of weeks to a month and 100 GB or more of memory on single servers, pushing "CPLEX, Gurobi, XPress, or SCIP" to their limits, and models with high temporal and spatial resolution parallelize only trivially (p. 13); the five project questions follow (pp. 13-14). The guide splits the answer into a modelling route that modellers can apply by themselves (aggregation, heuristic and exact decomposition; pp. 27, 123) and a technical route that needs a specialized algorithm, HPC hardware and an adapted model formulation at once (p. 71). The solver section enters from two facts: commercial LP and MIP solvers usually cannot run efficiently on distributed systems, and the project's largest ESM instances were intractable for them within several hours (p. 71); the open-source PIPS-IPM, built for stochastic LPs, handles linking variables but not linking constraints (p. 72), which ESMs have in large numbers from networks, storage and CO2 limits (p. 124). Extending an existing solver rather than writing a new one was decided after a survey of HPC-capable solvers "with a special focus on open source availability" (p. 14).
  - Transferable: name the one structural feature that the closest open-source code lacks (here linking constraints) and make it the entry point, after a written survey of the candidates (pp. 14, 72).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Keep PIPS-IPM's decomposition and add what ESMs need. Unchanged: each block's data lives on one MPI process, no process stores the whole LP, at most N processes are used (p. 72), and every interior-point iteration solves the block-bordered system (4.2) through a Schur complement (pp. 72-73). Changed: (a) linking constraints F_0 x_0 + ... + F_N x_N = h_{N+1} next to the linking variables x_0 (eq. 4.1, pp. 71-72); (b) the locality of linking: most linking constraints and variables couple only two blocks, so the nonzero pattern of the Schur complement is known in advance, which cuts storage and allows sparse solvers instead of the dense matrix that is intractable with more than 300,000 linking constraints and variables (p. 73); (c) for strong linkage, no explicit Schur complement but a Krylov method with a distributed preconditioner (p. 73); (d) multiple centrality correctors (Colombo and Gondzio) instead of Mehrotra's predictor-corrector for robustness, and separate primal and dual step lengths because only LPs are solved (p. 73); (e) scaling and (f) presolve as preconditioning (p. 74). Why these changes: the prototype failed to find an optimal solution on some ESMs and did not beat the interior-point code of CPLEX, run with its most profitable thread count on a large shared-memory machine (p. 72), so each change targets convergence or run time (pp. 72-74). The modelling route (DLR chapters) instead trades a bounded loss of accuracy for speed by aggregation or temporal decomposition, judged on a few aggregated accuracy indicators (pp. 27, 38, 48-49).
  - Transferable: when extending a parallel code, freeze its decomposition and data distribution and add one component at a time against the reference that the prototype lost to (pp. 72-74).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Continuous LPs only, "the project is only concerned with LPs" (p. 73); integrality appears only as context: simplex is indispensable for MIP because of its warm starts (p. 17), unit-commitment MILPs appear in the literature review (pp. 30, 34), and model reduction is expected to pay even more for mixed-integer ESOMs (pp. 65, 67). Structure: block-diagonal with linking variables and linking constraints (eq. 4.1, p. 71), produced in ESMs by investment variables that span all time steps and by annual fuel and emission limits (eqs. 1.3-1.4, pp. 16-17; eqs. 3.1-3.4, pp. 40-41). The block structure comes from the modeller through GAMS annotation (the .stage attribute; linking variables in block 1, linking constraints in their own block; pp. 85-86), because automatic detection has limits and "there is no unique block structure" (p. 85); time blocks are the default, since regional decomposition "does not appear to be very promising" (p. 98), and the partners found slicing by time steps the easiest way (p. 118). Data: REMix (Germany 2030, 488 nodes, 8,760 hourly steps; after presolve 30.6 M variables, 9.2 M constraints and 69.8 M nonzeros for dispatch, 43.2 M, 32.8 M and 138.0 M for expansion; pp. 39-42), ELMOD (Central Western Europe, and 19 European countries; p. 104), YSSP (named only in Table 5.1, p. 105), the synthetic SIMPLE family (pp. 139-152) and six partner models (p. 20). Correctness assumptions: none stated beyond the equivalence of the scaled and the presolved LP to the original (p. 74).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Devices: (a) Schur-complement elimination: multiply block row i by -B_i^T K_i^{-1}, sum all rows, solve (K_0 - Σ B_i^T K_i^{-1} B_i) Δz_0 = b_0 - Σ B_i^T K_i^{-1} b_i, then insert Δz_0 to get each Δz_i; everything except forming and solving the complement runs locally on the MPI processes (steps 1-4, eq. 4.3, p. 73). (b) The sparsity pattern of the complement predicted from which blocks each linking constraint and variable touches (p. 73; details deferred to ZR 19-41). (c) An iterative solve of the Schur system with a distributed preconditioner, called "quite a novelty" for interior-point methods (p. 73); its measured effect is in §5.3, 415 s to 203 s on ELMOD CWE15 (p. 110). (d) Scaling invariant: Ã = RAC with positive diagonal R and C, and every solution x̃ of the scaled LP gives x = Cx̃ with the same objective (eqs. 4.4-4.5, p. 74); geometric scaling (iterated up to ten times) and equilibrium scaling (one pass) both run in parallel along the arrowhead, so geometric scaling is the default (p. 74). (e) Presolve: four methods (singleton rows, bound tightening, parallel and nearly parallel rows, and a model cleanup that removes redundant rows and negligibly small matrix entries), run in that order with the cleanup also called first, all as equivalence operations undone by postsolve; invariant: no reduction may alter the block structure, or the structure-specific method no longer applies (p. 74). No exact arithmetic, safe rounding or tolerance values are described in §4.1; proofs: n/a. Modelling side (DLR): the CPLEX baseline settings (barrier, crossover off, 16 threads, barrier tolerance 1e-5 or the default 1e-8, predual off, aggressive scaling) are credited with making LPs that took several days solvable in under 24 h and with cutting peak memory to 230 GB where it otherwise exceeded 300 GB (pp. 41-42). Numerics measured, not assumed: slack-generator costs should stay in the range of real costs because the ratio of largest to smallest coefficient should ideally stay below 1e5 (pp. 50-51); rounding inputs to five decimals helped small instances but raised barrier ticks on the 488-region instance, while explicit variable bounds brought the large instances below 50% of the default solver time (pp. 51-52).
  - Transferable: write down the invariant that every preprocessing step must keep for the downstream algorithm (here the block structure) next to the list of reductions (p. 74).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Solver chapter, JUWELS only (p. 103). Scaling study on one SIMPLE instance (5.6 M variables, 5.1 M constraints, 20.4 M nonzeros, 512 diagonal blocks), chosen because a single MPI process solves it in reasonable time and it allows a power-of-2 scaling plot; MPI process counts divide the block count for load balance; 2 OpenMP threads per MPI process; curves for the commercial LP solvers, PIPS-IPM++ and a reduced PIPS-IPM++ that has only the Schur-complement decomposition and none of the §4.1.2 algorithms (Fig. 5.1, pp. 103-104; the values are not in the text). Large instances (Table 5.1, p. 105): eight instances from 85.6 M to 1.15 B variables, run with 250 to 1,024 MPI processes on 19 to 128 nodes; the baseline is the best of CPLEX 12.8, Gurobi 8.1, MOSEK 8.1 and Xpress 8.4.7, each with 16 threads because more threads usually degrade performance (p. 104); times include I/O, and OOM is reported as an outcome (pp. 104-105). PIPS-IPM++ needs 181 s and 216 s on ELMOD CWE15 and CWE16 against 6,201 s and 6,111 s; the commercial solvers ran out of memory on SIMPLE1, both YSSP and both ELMOD EU instances, on which PIPS-IPM++ needs 931 s to 2,311 s, and SIMPLE2 (13,170 s on 128 nodes) could not even be generated sequentially (p. 105), consistent with its 4,174,953,472 nonzeros exceeding the GAMS cap of 2,147,483,647 (p. 90; comparison made here). Some instances intractable on JUWELS were solved by two of the four commercial solvers on a ZIB machine with 2 TB RAM and 88 cores, in more than one day (pp. 104-105). The resource claim: run time per computing resource better than the best commercial result, 19 compute nodes against 1 (p. 105); computed here, 3,439 and 4,104 node-seconds against 6,201 and 6,111 seconds. Single runs: no seeds, repetitions or variability. §5.2: a REMix design over spatial scale (30, 120, 488 regions), investment on or off, DC transport against DC-OPF, and tear-and-wear terms that add linking constraints; results withheld (p. 105). §5.3: Score-P instrumentation with a filter file, then Scalasca and Vampir, on ELMOD CWE15 (19 nodes, 16 tasks per node, 2 threads per task; code of 14 Oct 2019; compiler and library versions named; p. 106): 75% of the run time was spent waiting in n-to-n MPI collectives, 95% of that in one MPI_Allreduce, all ranks waiting for factorize() on rank 0, called 33 times in 34 iterations (pp. 106-109); after the distributed preconditioner the run took 203 s instead of 415 s, with 44% waiting (p. 110); on Hazel Hen 240 MPI processes on 20 nodes were about as fast as 522 on 44 nodes, which used twice the nodes and energy (p. 113). Modelling chapter (DLR): CPLEX 12.8.0 under GAMS 25.1.3 (p. 42); deterministic ticks as the primary solver-time measure because seconds vary with machine load and the software-hardware combination (p. 48); four aggregated accuracy indicators (pp. 48-49); identical exclusive hardware guaranteed only within each strategy's benchmark, the mismatches printed (machines, bug-fixes, precision 1e-8 to 1e-5) with their likely effect, and speed-ups across approaches called not fully comparable (pp. 39, 65-66); results as sufficient speed-up with average and worst accuracy per approach (Table 3.5, p. 68). Failures reported: the prototype's non-convergence (p. 72), OOM (p. 105), and partner models that PIPS-IPM++ could not handle, uncounted (pp. 117-118).
  - Transferable: report a distributed solver against shared-memory baselines by wall clock and by resources used, with I/O included and out-of-memory runs kept in the table (pp. 104-105).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Presolve for a structure-exploiting solver: admit only reductions that keep the block structure, run a cheap cleanup (redundant rows, negligibly small entries) before the other methods, and keep every reduction reversible by postsolve (p. 74). Carrying this to presolve before a Dantzig-Wolfe or Benders decomposition in MILP is an inference, not in the text.
- Predict the Schur complement's sparsity from the locality of the linking constraints and variables and factor it sparsely; switch to a preconditioned Krylov solve when linkage is strong (p. 73).
- Comparing a distributed code with shared-memory solvers: give each commercial solver its best thread count (16 here), take the per-instance best of all four, include I/O, keep OOM as a result, and add a resource-normalized comparison (nodes times seconds) to the wall-clock factor (pp. 104-105).
- Put an ablated version of the new solver (core decomposition only, none of the added algorithms) into the scaling plot, so that the plot shows what the additions buy (p. 103).
- For a strong-scaling study, pick an instance that one process solves in reasonable time, with 2^k blocks, and process counts that divide the block count (p. 103).
- Find the parallel bottleneck with trace tools before tuning: instrument with a filter, read wait states, delay costs and the critical path, and compare timelines before and after the change; here they pointed to one serial factorization on rank 0 (pp. 106-111).
- Without exclusive identical machines, take the solver's deterministic ticks as the time measure and print every remaining mismatch with its likely effect (pp. 48, 65-66).
- Measure numerics hygiene per instance size instead of assuming it: slack and penalty costs in the range of real costs (coefficient range ideally below 1e5), explicit variable bounds, rounding of inputs; the effects can invert with size (pp. 50-52).
