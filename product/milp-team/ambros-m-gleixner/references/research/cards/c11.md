### S002 · MIPLIB 2017: data-driven compilation of the 6th mixed-integer programming library (Mathematical Programming Computation 2021, doi:10.1007/s12532-020-00194-3)
**Read level**: full. All 48 file pages: §§1–7 with every footnote and Tables 1–11 (pp. 1–44) read completely, including the NEOS pre-sampling (pp. 7–9) and the acknowledgements (p. 44); figures only through captions and extracted numbers (Figs. 1, 2, 4 and 5 are images; Figs. 3, 6 and 7 survive as axis values, legends and bar labels); references (pp. 44–46) and affiliations (pp. 47–48) skimmed for self-citations and roles.

[22], pp. 16, 45; S069); it cites QPLIB and the SCIP Optimization Suite 6.0 report with him as author (refs. - **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a measured saturation of the old instrument. On the 87-instance MIPLIB 2010 benchmark set, a virtual best and a virtual worst solver built from CPLEX, Gurobi and Xpress fell from 36.3 s and 113.0 s (2011 versions) to 13.5 s and 31.3 s (December 2018 versions) on the same hardware, as shifted geometric means with a 1 s shift and 12 threads computed from Mittelmann's logs: speed-ups of 2.70 and 3.62, roughly 16% per year "just from improvements in the algorithms" (p. 2, fn. 1). Of the 134 MIPLIB 2010 instances initially open, only 70 remained open (p. 2). The set therefore "was no longer considered to sufficiently reflect the frontier", and a public call in November 2016 was steered by a committee of 21 that included representatives of nine solver teams (p. 2, fn. 2). The research problem is selection: more submissions, a larger target collection and more solver representatives than before posed new challenges to the selection process (p. 3), and the library was to be balanced "in a fair and transparent manner" (p. 3). Two products with different goals are named: a benchmark set for a "meaningful and fair comparison of the average performance" of general-purpose solvers in reasonable time, and a collection for coverage, including unsolved, numerically problematic, indicator and memory-heavy instances (p. 3). The non-purpose is stated in the same place: average performance on the set is not a criterion for choosing a solver for an application, backed by the fact that each of the eight measured solvers was fastest on at least one instance (pp. 3, 20).
  - Transferable: open a benchmark paper by measuring the saturation of the old benchmark with solver-neutral aggregates on unchanged hardware, then state what the new set is for and what it must not be used for, with a data point that supports the disclaimer (pp. 2–3, 20).
- **D2 Core idea**: Selection is posed as "a sequence of optimization problems": diversity preselection MIPs inside large model groups, a collection MIP and a benchmark MIP (p. 24), with the committee's requirements written as linear constraints (p. 30). The collection MIP balances the choice against eleven feature clusterings at once, takes at most five instances per model group and at least one benchmark-suitable instance from each group that has one, at least one instance per submitter, and respects relative caps on MiniZinc, NEOS, small, medium and pure-binary instances (pp. 31–32, Table 9). The benchmark MIP adds 24 performance clusterings, stricter group limits, a total-runtime budget and an objective tilted towards instances that are hard for the virtual best solver (pp. 35–36). Against earlier MIPLIBs, whose instances were "manually selected by the members of the committee" (p. 3), the change is that 105 instance features and eight solvers' performance data drive the choice (pp. 14, 20). The obvious alternative, one clustering over all features with uniform picks, is rejected for the curse of dimensionality and the relative scaling of features (p. 28); the remedy is several clusterings on disjoint feature groups, balanced simultaneously in one MIP (p. 29). What stays: the benchmark/collection split introduced with MIPLIB 2010 (p. 42), the 2010 rule that every benchmark instance is solved by at least one solver (p. 25), the structure of the solution checker (p. 22), distribution of the instances unmodified (p. 12), and the committee as decision-maker, for which the models provide "decision support" (p. 42). The order of work is reversed: the collection is chosen first and the benchmark set as its subset, but benchmark suitability is defined first so that early reductions keep enough benchmark candidates (p. 24).
  - Transferable: encode a curation committee's rules as constraints of a selection MIP; the rules become inspectable, the selection can be recomputed when the data change, and a minimal repair can be computed after release (pp. 30–36, 43–44).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Canonical form (Definition 1, p. 13): minimise c^T x over two-sided rows ℓ^A ≤ Ax ≤ u^A, bounds, and x ∈ {0,1}^nb × Z^ni × Q^nc, with rational data. For the features only, the objective and each row are divided by their largest absolute coefficient; the downloadable files are not altered (p. 14). Format: basic MPS; maximisation negated into minimisation, lazy-constraint markers removed, hexadecimal coefficients converted, and minimal fixes to ambiguous or malformed files (p. 10). Special constructs: indicator constraints (437 instances, many from the MiniZinc submission, which also sent big-M versions, p. 18) are admitted to the collection and excluded from the benchmark set (pp. 17, 25); no SOS, semicontinuous or piecewise-linear constraints were submitted (p. 18). Sources (Table 2, p. 9): MIPLIB 3 65, MIPLIB 2003 28, MIPLIB 2010 335, MiniZinc Challenges 2012–2016 988, new NEOS 785, other new 1897, earlier MIPLIB 2010 submissions 1623, 5721 in all (p. 7), from 128 submissions by 66 submitters at 54 affiliations, 38 academic and 16 industrial (p. 6). For the first time every contribution needed a licence granting redistribution, by default CC BY-SA 4.0 (p. 6). Structures used: model groups (one per homogeneous submission or model type; for the anonymous NEOS instances, synthetic groups from a k-means clustering with k = 110 chosen by hand, pp. 10–11); a 17-type constraint classification from empty to general linear (Table 5, p. 17); and block structure detected by GCG 3.0, because no submitter sent decomposition files although some 20 sent model files (pp. 18–19). Benchmark admission (Definition 2, p. 25): solved by at least one solver within 4 h; at least 10 s for half the solvers; constraint and objective dynamism at most 10^6; matrix coefficients below 10^10; consistent results; no indicator constraints; infeasible or with a finite optimum; solution value below 10^10; at most 10^6 nonzeros. Infeasible instances are kept on purpose; unbounded ones are excluded because a feasible rational MIP has a finite optimum if and only if its LP relaxation has one, which makes the property "more a continuous than a discrete problem", and because there is no consensus on what solvers should output for them (p. 25). An instance counts as solved only with no cross-solver inconsistency and a verified feasible solution (p. 20), and the paper concedes that "correctness was only enforced approximately" with respect to the tolerance (p. 36).
  - Transferable: state the mathematical fact behind an admission rule in one sentence next to the rule (finite optimum of a rational MIP iff of its LP relaxation, p. 25), and keep infeasible instances on purpose when solvers should be tested on proving infeasibility (p. 25).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: the "algorithm" is the selection pipeline; no theorem is proved.
  - NEOS pre-sampling, done before and outside the MIPLIB process (p. 7): of more than a million MIP instances solved on NEOS in 2014–2016, the 14,571 AMPL instances needing more than five minutes were re-run with CPLEX 12.6 (up to 12 h); outliers beyond 3 standard deviations and suspected duplicates were removed (6531 left); three k-means calls with four clusters each (size; shares of binary and integer variables; root gap and root fractionality) gave 64 cells, sampled completely, by half or by a tenth according to cell size: 710 instances, plus 75 screened by Mittelmann (pp. 7–9).
  - Trivial presolving before features: SCIP 5.0 restricted to removing redundant constraints and fixed variables, activity-based bound tightening and coefficient tightening, iterated to a fixed point instead of SCIP's usual stopping rule (p. 13). The reason: modelling artefacts distort features, while full presolving is a solver-specific balancing act (pp. 12–13). Effect: 55 instances become LPs or are solved; variables shrink on 3782 instances (66%), by up to 93% (a2864-99blp); on 445 (8%) more than half the variables are fixed; the average reduction is 15% (p. 13).
  - Feature vector: 105 features in 11 groups (p. 14; Table 4, p. 15), built from five vector statistics with infinite entries dropped and empty vectors set to 0 (p. 14); row dynamism, invariant under the normalisation, flags a numerically difficult row above 10^6 (p. 16); log10 scaling, and siglog(x) = sig(x) log10(|x| + 1) for signed vectors (p. 16). The feature extractor is a SCIP extension modified from the code of ref. [22] and is downloadable (p. 16). Five further matrix features turned out to duplicate others only while the manuscript was prepared; the footnote says the computations included them (fn. 5, p. 16).
  - Decomposition features: GCG 3.0 in a minimal detection configuration groups rows by SCIP type, MIPLIB type and nonzero count, tries such groups as linking rows, and a connected finisher forms the blocks; the decomposition with maximum area score, 1 − (Σ_b |D^r_b||D^c_b| + n|L^r| + m|L^c| − |L^r||L^c|)/(mn), supplies ten features (pp. 18–19).
  - Solution checker (bundled since MIPLIB 2010): each final solution is checked against the original MPS file in GMP arbitrary precision, for feasibility only, since it "cannot check optimality in general" (p. 22). The 2010 absolute test (violation at most 10^-4) is replaced by a_i x* − u_i ≤ ε · max{(a_i x*)^+, (a_i x*)^−, |u_i|, 1} with ε = 10^-5, applied also to bounds and to the reported objective as the row c^T x* = c*; integrality keeps an absolute 10^-4; indicator constraints are now supported (pp. 22–23).
  - Cross-solver consistency, parsed with IPET: an instance is inconsistent if one solver's verified primal value lies below another solver's dual bound at timeout, including a claim of infeasibility (p. 23). Example bc1: seven of eight solvers agree on 3.338 after 3k–20k nodes, the eighth reports 3.418 as optimal after 720 nodes (p. 23). Inconsistent instances are left out of benchmark selection "unless the inconsistency was obviously caused by a bug in one solver" (p. 23).
  - Diversity preselection (1a)–(1e): maximise z subject to z ≤ (d_ij − d̄) x_i x_j + d̄ for all pairs, Σ x_i = κ and at least one benchmark-suitable instance when the group has one; d̄, the largest distance in the group, acts as big-M, and x_i x_j is linearised by w_ij ≤ x_i, w_ij ≤ x_j, w_ij ≥ x_i + x_j − 1 (pp. 26–27). κ = 5 for groups of 6–10 instances and 10 for larger ones; the largest group, cmflsp (360 instances), gives a MIP with 129,242 rows, 64,981 columns and 323,485 nonzeros that took about 800 s, and all others were solved to optimality within 500 s with Gurobi 7.5.1 (p. 27); 5666 instances shrink to 2182 (p. 28).
  - Multiple clusterings: one k-means clustering per feature group, the number of clusters L_k chosen by hand as the smallest reaching 90% quality where possible (quality: the share of pairwise distance that falls between clusters; at least 87%, average 92%; Table 8, pp. 29–30). Small clusters (below half the average size) and large ones (boxplot outliers, more than 1.5 interquartile ranges above the 75% quantile) relax one side of the balance constraint (p. 29). Each cluster is weighted by its total dissimilarity δ, the shifted geometric mean (shift 1) of its pairwise distances in the whole feature space (p. 30).
  - Collection MIP (5): balance (1 − ε) δ_kℓ y_k ≤ Σ_{i∈C_kℓ} x_i ≤ (1 + ε) δ_kℓ y_k with ε = 0.5, the lower side dropped for small clusters and the upper side for large ones ((3), (4a), (4b)); at most one of two feature-identical instances (4c); model-group and submitter rules (4d)–(4f); relative caps (4g): MiniZinc 5%, NEOS 33% (no cap in the benchmark MIP), small (n ≤ 2000) 20%, medium (n ≤ 10,000) 50%, pure binary 20%, the NEOS cap for lack of application information and the binary cap because such instances are often academic puzzles (Table 9, p. 32); objective weight 2 for benchmark-suitable instances and 1 otherwise (p. 32). Result: 1065 instances, 499 of them benchmark-suitable; 48 zero-distance pairs had survived duplicate removal (pp. 32–33).
  - Performance clusterings, three per solver (24 in all): absolute rank (11 equal-size clusters), relative performance (t_w,i + σ)/(min_{w'≠w} t_w',i + σ), and seven exponential time bins T_j = 10^(−3.5+0.5j) · 14400; in each, a solver's timeouts form two further clusters, instances solved by exactly one other solver and by several (pp. 33–34); these clusters are weighted by their size (p. 35). For SCIP, the fastest relative cluster shares only 8 of its 21 instances with the fastest absolute cluster, the other 13 being spread over 8 of 10 absolute clusters, which the paper reads as an almost orthogonal partition (p. 35).
  - Benchmark MIP (8a)–(8e): feature and performance balance, the relative caps, at most one instance per NEOS group and two per other group, and the budget Σ t̄_i x_i ≤ τ with t̄_i the median time capped at 2 h and τ = 32 days (p. 35); objective β_i = 1 + (1/20) √(min_w t_w,i), which favours instances hard for the virtual best solver, the square root being "empirically motivated" (p. 36). Result: 240 instances; the budget did not bind, about 14 days for a median solver run sequentially (p. 36).
  - Proof devices and invariants: one equivalence used without proof or citation (a feasible rational MIP has a finite optimum iff its LP relaxation has one, p. 25). Design rules: the selection MIPs are published but cannot belong to the sets, since their presence would change the feature space and hence their own formulation (p. 42); downloadable solutions are checked with the solution checker, and their exact objective values are computed with SoPlex iterative refinement after fixing all integer variables (p. 42).
  - Transferable: to keep a selected subset representative for every solver, add per-solver clusterings on absolute rank, relative time and fixed time bins, and split each solver's timeouts by whether one or several other solvers finished (pp. 33–35).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Performance campaign (pp. 20–22): every submitted instance with eight solvers (Table 6, p. 20): CBC 2.9.8, IBM CPLEX 12.7.1, Gurobi 7.5.1, MOSEK 8.1.0.30, SAS/OR 14.2 and FICO Xpress 8.2 with 4 threads, MATLAB R2017b (intlinprog) and SCIP 4.0.0 single-threaded (p. 20, fn. 6); 4 h limit; two Linux clusters (32 nodes with two 4-core Xeon X5672 at 3.20 GHz and 48 GB; 16 nodes with two 10-core Xeon E5-2670 v2 at 2.50 GHz and 64 GB), two jobs in parallel per node; almost 40 CPU years (p. 20). Metrics: time to optimality and branch-and-bound nodes, with node counts floored at 1 so that presolving counts as root processing (pp. 20, 22), reported through virtual best, worst and median solvers, never by solver name (p. 20). Results: 1969 of 5721 instances (34%) solved by no solver and 1155 (20%) by all eight (p. 20; Fig. 3a, p. 21); the virtual best solver finishes about 20%, 40% and 60% of the pool within 1 s, 1 min and 1 h, the virtual worst only 20.2% within 4 h, so that the virtual best solves more in 2 s than the virtual worst in 4 h (p. 20); 1507 instances (40% of the solved ones, 26% overall) were solved within one node (p. 22).
  - Evaluation of the instrument: sensitivity of the collection to the balance tolerance ε (Table 10, p. 33: ε = 0.1 infeasible; 0.2 without a feasible selection after 15 minutes; 0.3 gives 740 instances, 0.5 gives 1065, 0.8 and 0.9 give 1140). Validation of the feature space against model groups over all 566,580 pairs of the collection: a same-group pair is among the top-5 similarity pairs with probability 974/1327 ≈ 0.734, against a base rate of about 0.007; without the NEOS instances, whose groups came from the features themselves (p. 11), 0.815 against 0.008; the feature space "recovers the model group data better than we expected" (p. 37). A t-SNE plot shows the benchmark-suitable instances covering most of the collection's feature space except a few regions (Fig. 5, p. 38). Hardness against MIPLIB 2010: the virtual best solver needs more than 1 h on 19.6% of the new benchmark set and on none of the old one, the virtual median more than 4 h on 48.3% against 5.7% (5 of 87) (Table 11, pp. 39–40). One-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests compare, per solver, the benchmark set's CDF with that of the benchmark-suitable pool: virtual median D ≈ 0.10 with p = 0.04, virtual best D = 0.17 with p = 6.513·10^-5, four actual solvers with p < 1%, and four with p > 0.1, reported as not significant (pp. 40–41); the paper concludes that the method "achieved both its conflicting goals, hardness and representability" (p. 41).
  - Not measured, and said so: no per-solver results, because "not all runs could be performed exclusively on the same hardware" (p. 38); performance variability and parallel scalability "were not captured" (p. 36); seeds and permutations are not mentioned anywhere in the text. Not given: the shift σ of the relative performance measure (Eq. (6), p. 34). Asserted without numbers: the benefits of preselection (better k-means results, smaller selection MIPs, larger sets) were "noticed" empirically (p. 28). Failures printed: 328 inconsistent instances removed (5%, p. 23), while Table 7 counts 334 under (B.5) and the difference is not explained (p. 26); the infeasible ε settings (p. 33); the median-solver artefact on blp-ic98 (four solvers within 1700 s, the fifth timing out, median 8050 s, p. 40); and two benchmark instances found numerically flawed after release (fn. 8, p. 43).
  - Transferable: validate a similarity notion against independent labels by the conditional probability over pairs against its base rate, then recompute without the labels that were derived from the same features (p. 37).
  - Transferable: test the design goal that a selected subset is harder than its pool with a one-sided KS test per solver, and print the solvers for which it fails (pp. 40–41).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself in the lineage of reporting standards and presents itself as the operational form of one methodological idea. It opens with Hoffman et al. (1953), the first reported computational comparison of LP codes, whose observation that conjectures could only be settled by actual trial "seems to hold to an even greater extent today" (p. 1). MIPLIB's history runs from Bixby, Boyd and Indovina (1992) through four updates (p. 2). Crowder, Dembo and Mulvey (1978) stand for reproducibility and documented real-world test problems, with the remark that the cost of sharing test problems has since been overcome; the ad hoc committee of Jackson et al. stands for standard test sets (p. 5). Hooker (1993) proposed making problem choice part of the experimental design, and the data-driven selection "can be seen as an extension of this idea" (p. 5). McGeoch's simulation paradigm is cited (p. 5); Smith-Miles' instance-space work (generating instances to fill gaps, finding performance pockets) is called complementary and left aside (p. 5); algorithm selection (Rice; ASlib, whose MIP section is built on MIPLIB 2010) is the neighbouring field (pp. 5–6); MINLPLib, QPLIB, Netlib, COR@L and OR-Library are sibling libraries (p. 6). Earlier MIPLIBs are not criticised: their expert manual selection is described, and the new process is presented as a redesign forced by scale (p. 3). The group's own work supplies the tools: MIPLIB 2010 [32] (pp. 2, 22), the presolve papers [2, 20] (p. 12), feature-based algorithm selection [22] (p. 16), GCG by Gamrath and Lübbecke [21] and the SCIP Optimization Suite 6.0 report [23] (p. 18), IPET by the second author [27] (p. 23), and UG/ParaSCIP for the newly solved instances [46, 47] (p. 44).
  - Transferable: present a test-set method as the operational form of a published methodological principle (here Hooker's proposal to make problem choice part of the experimental design, p. 5) rather than as a correction of earlier test sets.
- **D7 Writing**: Contribution sentences come twice: the selection process is "the main new feature of MIPLIB 2017" (p. 3), and, beyond the library, the data-driven, optimisation-based methodology is "the main contribution of this paper" (p. 42). The arbitrariness is conceded before any method is described: the choice of features, the number of clusters, the easy/hard thresholds and the formalisations of diversity and balance "have a high impact on the final result", and the technical detail is offered to make the process transparent (pp. 3–4); the same concession closes the paper (p. 43). Each admission criterion and cap is justified in prose (Definition 2, p. 25; Table 9 caps, p. 32). Named instances carry the argument at each step (bc1 p. 23, drayage pp. 27–28, blp-ic98 p. 40, fhnw-sq3 p. 44, the "nile instance" p. 10). Admissions go into footnotes (redundant features, fn. 5, p. 16; the two flawed instances, fn. 8, p. 43), and "To avoid misunderstandings" introduces the NEOS pre-sampling as outside the MIPLIB process while describing it "for the sake of completeness and transparency" (p. 7). Self-criticism is local: the distance problems that motivate multiple clusterings also affect the paper's own preselection, mitigated because there all instances share a model group (p. 28). Hedging is light: "Arguably" twice (pp. 28, 31), "we hope" twice (pp. 36, 43), "we believe" once (p. 43); "note that" is the usual caveat marker (on 29 lines of the text). A limits paragraph sits inside the method section, right after the benchmark result, not only in the conclusion (p. 36). The committee voice holds throughout; no section is attributed to an author, and "All responsibility is assumed by the authors" (p. 44).
  - Transferable: list the hand-made choices in the introduction, next to the claim of a data-driven method, and repeat the list as the closing caveat (pp. 3–4, 43).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution levels: infrastructure (the collection of 1065 and the benchmark set of 240 instances; abstract, p. 42); methodology (two related selection MIPs over a feature space "covering more than a hundred different dimensions", with multiple clusterings, pp. 42–43); software and data (feature extractor, the collection and benchmark MIPs, run and validation scripts, versioned files, checked solutions with exact values, and the five most similar instances on each instance page of a new web site, pp. 41–42); and a standing invitation to send any improving solution, not only optimal ones (p. 42). Limits are specific and printed twice. The representation of difficulty "cannot be achieved perfectly and is not even well-defined": the data are a snapshot of current algorithms, taken with a time limit, without variability or parallel scaling, with correctness enforced only up to tolerance, and the performance clusterings may contradict each other (p. 36). The method is "not intended as a general blueprint"; constants, clustering heuristics and the roles of objective and constraints could all change, and the result is "only one of many possible and justified outcomes" (p. 43). The post-release correction is reported as a use of the method: neos-5075914-elvire and neos-3754224-navua, both at the border between feasibility and infeasibility (two solvers agreed on an optimal value for the first although it should mathematically be infeasible; for the second, declared infeasible by one solver only, a solution feasible within tolerances arrived half a year after the benchmark set was published), were exchanged through a minimal update computed with a variant of the benchmark MIP while preserving balancedness, published in June 2019 (pp. 43–44, fn. 8); how many instances that update exchanged is not stated. Future work: a hindsight study comparing speed-ups on whole model groups with those on the selected instances, and diversity-based against nearest-neighbour preselection (p. 43). Reception at the time of writing: 68 originally open instances solved, by UG-based parallel codes, custom cutting planes and one proof of infeasibility without code (fhnw-sq3) (p. 44, fn. 9).
  - Transferable: report a post-release correction as an application of your own method, naming the instances and the failure mode (pp. 43–44).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Max-min diversity selection of κ items from a group: maximise z with z ≤ (d_ij − d̄) x_i x_j + d̄, Σ x_i = κ, a must-cover side constraint, and the product linearised by w_ij ≤ x_i, w_ij ≤ x_j, w_ij ≥ x_i + x_j − 1 (pp. 26–27).
- Balance against several clusterings at once: (1 − ε) δ y_k ≤ Σ_{i∈C} x_i ≤ (1 + ε) δ y_k per cluster, the lower side dropped for small clusters and the upper side for boxplot-outlier clusters, δ the shifted geometric mean of within-cluster distances (pp. 29–31).
- Per-solver performance clusterings (absolute rank, relative time with a shift, exponential time bins; timeouts split by how many other solvers finished), so that no solver looks different on the subset than on the pool (pp. 33–35).
- A hardness tilt in the selection objective, 1 + (1/20) √(virtual-best time), with a knapsack budget on capped median times, checked afterwards for whether the budget binds (pp. 35–36).
- Solution-checker violation test in arbitrary precision: a_i x* − u_i ≤ ε · max{(a_i x*)^+, (a_i x*)^−, |u_i|, 1} with ε = 10^-5; bounds and objective checked the same way, integrality with an absolute 10^-4 (p. 23).
- Inconsistency rule for benchmark admission: a verified primal value below another solver's dual bound or infeasibility claim makes the instance ambiguous; exclude it unless an obvious bug in one solver explains the conflict (p. 23).
- Compute instance features after a trivial presolve iterated to a fixed point, with rows normalised by their largest coefficient and log or siglog scaling, and ship the extractor (pp. 13–16).
- Validate a feature space by P(similar pair | same group) against P(similar pair), then repeat without the groups derived from the features (p. 37).
- One-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov test per solver for whether the subset is harder than the pool, printing the solvers where it is not significant (pp. 40–41).
- A sensitivity table for the key free constant, including the settings where the model becomes infeasible (Table 10, p. 33).
- Versioned download files, and exact objective values of reported solutions by fixing the integers and solving the remaining LP exactly with iterative refinement (p. 42).
- Memorable suffixes for anonymous numeric instance names (neos-xyz-river, skipping ambiguous rivers such as Amazon, p. 10).
