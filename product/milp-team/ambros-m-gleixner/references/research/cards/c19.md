### S078 · Distributed Parallel Structure-Aware Presolving for Arrowhead Linear Programs (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2603.03498)
**Read level**: full. arXiv v2 (20 Oct 2026). Pp. 1–27 read completely, including Appendix A (Schur complement, p. 21), Appendix B (instance table, pp. 22–23), Appendix C (parallel constraint detection, pp. 23–26) and Appendix D (MPI communication, pp. 26–27). The references (pp. 27–30) were scanned.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Nils-Christian Kempke (first author), Stephen J. Maher, Daniel Rehfeldt, Thorsten Koch, Svenja Uslu. The title page gives ORCIDs only; Gleixner's is 0000-0003-0391-5903, as in team.json (p. 1). · **Place in the trajectory**: part of the ZIB energy-system HPC line. Rehfeldt et al. 2022 (S027) built the PIPS-IPM++ solver. Gleixner et al. 2020 (S068), "First Experiments with Structure-Aware Presolving for a Parallel Interior-Point Method", had Gleixner as first author (p. 28). Since S068, "most of our implementation has been rewritten and redesigned" (p. 4). Funding came from the Research Campus MODAL and the BMWi project PEREGRINE (p. 27). The paper is about LP only: no MILP component appears.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: presolve is "indispensable" in LP solvers, as much for numerical robustness as for speed (p. 1). In automatically generated models, redundancies and dependencies arise "as a by-product of modular and expressive model construction", not from modelling errors (p. 2). Yet presolve "has largely remained a serial, structure-agnostic preprocessing step": it can become the bottleneck, and it "may interfere with or even destroy the very structure that specialized solvers rely on" (p. 2). Structure-exploiting IPMs (BlockIp, OOPS, PIPS-IPM, MadNLP) presolve in one monolithic step, "often in a simplified form" (p. 3). General-purpose libraries, "most notably" the group's own PaPILO, do not keep the block assignment, so the structure has to be recovered afterwards, and "these libraries fail to efficiently leverage the arrowhead structure" (p. 3). Entry: a presolve that runs on PIPS-IPM++'s distributed data, communicates only information on the linking parts and preserves the arrowhead form (pp. 3, 7).
- **D2 Key idea and design**:
  - Reductions are classified as local or global (pp. 7–8). A local reduction touches one block and needs no communication. A global reduction touches linking rows or columns, needs MPI communication and acts as a synchronization point.
  - Each process keeps its own postsolve stack (p. 8). Changes to linking quantities are buffered (pp. 10–11).
  - The arrowhead form is an invariant that no presolver may break (p. 7).
  - Added: two arrowhead-specific presolvers, Permutation and LinDependencies (pp. 8–10).
  - Unchanged: the reductions themselves, taken from Andersen and Andersen, Gondzio and Achterberg et al. 2020 (Table 1, p. 6); the two-level hashing for parallel rows of Achterberg et al. 2020 (p. 11); rounds with working limits, where a new round starts only if the previous one made enough reductions relative to the problem size (p. 5).
  - The presolver list is not exhaustive. It holds "all presolvers that seemed general and widely adopted", plus those expected to reduce the energy models of interest (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - Continuous LP: min cᵀx s.t. Ax = b, d ≤ Cx ≤ f, l ≤ x ≤ u, with x ∈ Rⁿ and infinite bounds allowed (p. 4). There are no integer variables.
  - Arrowhead form (3): linking variables x0, linking rows F and G, diagonal blocks B_i and D_i (p. 7). Each block lives on exactly one MPI process and the 0-block on every process, so at most N processes can be used; the presentation assumes as many processes as blocks (p. 7).
  - Input comes only through the GAMS interface, with the modeller's block annotation (p. 12).
  - Test set (pp. 12–13): 82 instances, most from energy-system models. It includes instances that PIPS-IPM++ "cannot solve at all due to an overly large Schur complement" (p. 12). The largest has about 712 million nonzeros (elmod_set5; Table 2, p. 14; Table 7, pp. 22–23). Blocks are nearly equal: on the 62 fully annotated instances, the largest block has at most 8% more variables than the average (p. 13).
  - The only correctness argument is for LinDependencies: if A0 and every B_i have full row rank, the equality matrix without the linking rows has full row rank (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Invariant: no presolver may destroy the arrowhead structure, "even if it could improve size or stability", and none may create linking rows or columns, because coupling between blocks weakens parallel scalability (p. 7).
  - Postsolve targets a primal-dual IPM. It recovers a fully primal-dual feasible solution, including strict complementarity, and reverting a global reduction is a synchronization point (p. 8).
  - Permutation presolver (pp. 8–9):
    - it is called every round and once more at the end, but is not iterated to a fixed point;
    - columns are permuted before rows;
    - all candidates are detected first and then moved at once;
    - local variables that are empty in their blocks become "0-link" variables. These appear as sparse blocks in the Schur complement and need no communication (derivation in Appendix A, Eq. (6), p. 21; the mechanism "has not previously been documented in the literature", p. 9).
  - LinDependencies (pp. 9–10):
    - per block, an elimination on [B_i I] similar to MA50, with pivots restricted to the block's own columns;
    - fully dependent rows are removed or prove infeasibility; rows with a remaining x0 part move to A0, where all processes then eliminate jointly;
    - dependencies through linking rows are not detected and are left to the factorization of the Schur complement: "We do not consider this a limitation in practice" (p. 10).
  - Activities and nonzero counts are tracked throughout. Changes to linking quantities made by local reductions are buffered and communicated when a presolver finishes; postsolve replays synchronization events stored on the stack (pp. 10–11).
  - Parallel rows (pp. 11–12; Algorithms 1–2, pp. 24–26):
    - support hashes are combined by an Allreduce with a custom MPI operator;
    - each process flags which pairs are parallel on its own blocks, and an AND-Allreduce combines these flags; then every process applies the same reductions;
    - pairs of a local and a global row are left to the permutation presolver;
    - communication is balanced against reduction strength, "occasionally weakening presolve to limit overhead".
  - Determinism: the reduction order inside MPI collectives is fixed with Intel MPI's conditional numerical reproducibility (I_MPI_CBWR=2) (p. 13).
  - The numerical tolerances behind "(nearly) equal bounds", nearly parallel rows and TinyEntries are not given (pp. 6, 23).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup:
    - four experiments: scaling, comparison with other presolvers, impact on the IPM, and external presolve (p. 12);
    - hardware: LRZ terrabyte nodes (2 × Xeon Platinum 8380, 80 cores, 1024 GB); jobs took full nodes, "so no concurrent execution was permitted" (p. 12);
    - software: PaPILO 3.0.1, Gurobi 13.0.0, PIPS-IPM++ git hash d34cee38; IPM time limit 1 h, Panua-Pardiso as linear solver (p. 13);
    - rivals: Gurobi's presolve called through Python with a 1 h limit, PaPILO with default settings and a 1.5 h job limit, both on exported MPS files; their times exclude reading the MPS file (p. 13);
    - inside PIPS-IPM++, reading, presolve and postsolve take a median of 0.1%, 0.4% and 0.02% of the total time (p. 13);
    - shifted geometric means (SGM) with a shift of 1 s for times and 1% for sizes (p. 12);
    - data: 69 of the 82 instances, the scripts and the logs are on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.23269066); 13 partner instances cannot be published (p. 13);
    - a "Scope and limitations" paragraph comes before the results (p. 13).
  - Scaling, on eight large instances chosen by a written rule: at least 200 million nonzeros and at least 96 blocks, near-duplicates named and dropped, eustem added for its large linking part (p. 14).
    - Reductions: Gurobi removes 3 to 15 percentage points more nonzeros except on eustem; PaPILO times out on three instances and fails with an error on one (Table 2, p. 14).
    - Rivals: Gurobi's presolve is essentially sequential (26.7 s with one thread, 26.6 s with 64); PaPILO gains only a factor of 1.25 from 1 to 64 threads (p. 14).
    - PIPS-IPM++: with one process, slower than Gurobi, and slower than PaPILO except on elmod_cwe15 and eustem, which the paper attributes to parallel and arrowhead overhead; break-even at 2–4 processes (16 on yssp_exp_488r_1460b) (p. 14).
    - On the six instances with small linking parts, 64 processes are 30–50 times faster than one and the maximum count 36–172 times, which Amdahl's law turns into a sequential fraction of 0.4–1.8% (p. 14).
    - On the two instances with large linking parts the speed-up at 64 processes is only 23 and 7, and on one of them PIPS-IPM++ is slower than Gurobi from 64 processes on (pp. 14–15; Figure 4, p. 16).
  - Comparison of presolvers (Table 3, p. 15):
    - successes: all 82 for PIPS-IPM++ and Gurobi, 70 for PaPILO;
    - SGM time: PIPS-IPM++ 4.39 s with 64 processes and 4.15 s with the maximum count, against 27.42 s for Gurobi and 82.54 s for PaPILO;
    - nonzeros left: 68.43% (PIPS-IPM++), 61.71% (Gurobi), 66.71% (PaPILO).
    - The abstract's factor of 19 against PaPILO divides SGMs over different instance sets, since Table 3 averages over each presolver's own successes. On the 69 instances all three presolve, the factor is 25 against PaPILO and 5.9 against Gurobi (p. 15).
    - On the 18 instances with at least 100 million nonzeros, PIPS-IPM++ is 13 and 21 times faster than Gurobi, measured "on the 17 instances presolved by both" (pp. 15–17).
    - Gurobi's extra 6.7 percentage points of removed nonzeros cost about six times the presolve time. The paper rules out structure preservation as the explanation, because Gurobi's and PaPILO's reductions largely keep the structure too. The gap comes from reductions PIPS-IPM++ lacks: free-column substitution, singleton stuffing, sparsification, dual inference, dominated columns (p. 17).
    - Over the 61 instances with more than 64 blocks, the multi-node speed-up correlates weakly with the nonzero count (Spearman 0.43) and the block count (0.24). It correlates negatively (−0.65) with the share of linking rows coupling more than two consecutive blocks and of linking variables that appear in local rows (p. 17).
  - Determinism and variability (p. 15):
    - runs with the same process count give identical presolved problems; 64 processes and the maximum count differ on 8 of 81 instances, by at most 0.01% of the nonzeros;
    - without the enforced order, the SGM drops from 4.45 s to 3.7 s. The cost is concentrated on five YSSP instances with 1460 blocks ("219 to 494 s vs. 37 to 87 s with 64 processes", read here as with against without the fixed order), and determinism lowers the factor against Gurobi from 7.3 to 6.1;
    - on multiple nodes, the slowest of four runs took more than twice as long as the fastest on 14 of 71 instances. Single deterministic runs are reported, on the ground that Gurobi and PaPILO are deterministic as well.
  - Impact inside the IPM (Table 4, pp. 17–18):
    - PIPS-IPM++ solves 48 instances with presolve and 37 without;
    - on the 35 instances solved both ways, iterations are unchanged (88.4 against 87.2), the time per iteration drops by 20% and the time by 27%;
    - the SGM over all instances, with unsolved runs counted at 3600 s, drops by 40%;
    - of the 13 instances solved only with presolve, the run without presolve wrongly reports infeasibility on three, hits the iteration limit on three and the time limit on four, and crashes on three;
    - two instances are solved only without presolve (p. 18).
  - External presolve (Tables 5–6, pp. 18–20):
    - Automatic re-annotation with pipstools (KaHyPar) lets PIPS-IPM++ solve at most 21 instances. It finds about six times as many linking rows, only half of them coupling two consecutive blocks (99% under the modeller's annotation), and most failed runs end out of memory. Re-annotating the original models gives the same structure, so the loss comes from the re-annotation itself (pp. 18–19).
    - The modeller's annotation can be carried over to the presolved models by matching variable names, verified by nonzero counts. This works on 62 instances only (p. 18).
    - With the inherited annotation, external presolve solves "about as many" instances (Gurobi 40 against 39 for ours; PaPILO 40 against 39). Including the presolve time, ours is the fastest (124 s against 132 s; 141 s against 219 s) (pp. 19–20).
    - Table 6 also shows Gurobi's presolve followed by ours solving 43 instances, a result the text does not discuss (p. 20).
    - On two ELMOD instances Gurobi creates about 680,000 variables that belong to no block. PIPS-IPM++ then runs out of memory without our presolve and takes 3.7 times as long with it as with our presolve alone (p. 19).
    - On the 18 large instances, reading the MPS file alone takes Gurobi 94 s and PaPILO 504 s, against 4.6 s and 2.0 s for PIPS-IPM++'s block files (p. 19).
  - MPI communication (Appendix D, pp. 26–27):
    - communication takes 21% of presolve time on one node and 74% above 256 processes; 98% of it is spent in Allreduce;
    - the number of collectives is 500–600 whatever the process count, while the time per collective rises from 1.7 ms to 2.8 ms and 3.4 ms;
    - the MPI share of the fastest process (10–46%) is a lower bound on the cost of communication itself;
    - about 2 s of collectives bound the presolve time from below on multiple nodes.
  - No significance tests are run; "significant" is used informally (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: the paper places itself between the presolve literature (Andersen and Andersen 1995, Gondzio 1997, Achterberg et al. 2020, Gemander et al. 2020) and the structure-exploiting solvers for arrowhead LPs (specialized simplex, Dantzig-Wolfe, Benders and Lagrangian decomposition, IPMs) (p. 3). The group's own PaPILO is named as the general-purpose library that loses the block assignment (p. 3), and it is then measured as the slowest presolver, with 12 failures (p. 15). For context, PaPILO's own paper reported 1.9 (all instances) to 7.0 (instances of at least 100 s) times faster from 1 to 32 threads on MIPLIB 2017 (S026, p. 12), against the 1.25 measured here on these LPs (p. 14). Gurobi serves as the reference for reduction strength. Beyond the distributed, structure-preserving design and Appendix A, no novelty is claimed (pp. 3, 9).
- **D7 Writing**:
  - The abstract prints the unflattering numbers next to the speed-ups: "removing a few percentage points fewer nonzeros"; external presolve "is slower, especially on large models, and breaks the distributed workflow"; with automatic re-annotation, PIPS-IPM++ "solves at most 21 instances" (p. 1).
  - Three contributions are listed (p. 3). Limitations come before the results (p. 13), with the hedge "We expect our results to carry over to other AHLPs with a good block structure, but we have not tested this" (p. 13).
  - Numbers that are not reconciled:
    - the SGM presolve time with 64 processes appears as 4.39 s (Table 3, p. 15), 4.45 s (p. 15) and 4.49 s (Table 8, 81 instances, p. 26);
    - Gurobi presolves all 82 instances in Table 3 (p. 15), but only 17 of the 18 large ones in the text (pp. 17, 19);
    - Gurobi's extra reductions "do not make the IPM noticeably faster" (p. 17), yet they "do speed-up the IPM (124 s down to 118 s)" (p. 19).
  - The title page is dated "Match 03, 2026" (p. 1).
  - AI use is disclosed: Claude Code wrote the evaluation, job and packaging scripts and helped with language editing, and "The authors checked all scripts, results, and text and take full responsibility for the content" (p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions: software and design (a distributed presolve and postsolve inside PIPS-IPM++); an evaluation; and a comparison with PaPILO and Gurobi, both standalone and as external presolve (p. 3).
  - Limits printed:
    - only energy models annotated by their modellers were tested; the annotation quality is decisive and cannot yet be automated;
    - only two presolvers are arrowhead-specific;
    - block sizes are not balanced across processes;
    - instances with large linking parts gain little from more nodes (p. 13);
    - dependencies through linking rows go undetected (p. 10);
    - there is a reduction gap to Gurobi (p. 17).
  - Future work: make the presolve available to other structure-exploiting methods; add presolvers and tune them, since the paper sees "a gap in algorithmic presolve that we believe is not simply explained by block-structure preservation restrictions" (p. 20); reduce synchronization points by combining collectives or using non-blocking ones (p. 27).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Split reductions into local ones (one block, no communication) and global ones (linking rows or columns, the only synchronization points). Keep one postsolve stack per process, and record deferred global updates as synchronization events on that stack (pp. 7–8, 10–11).
- (D4) Write the structure the downstream solver needs as an invariant no reduction may break (here: no new linking rows or columns). Afterwards, measure downstream whether the reductions you gave up would have mattered (pp. 7, 17, 19).
- (D4) Detect linear dependencies block by block:
  - eliminate on [B_i I] with pivots restricted to the block's own columns;
  - drop fully dependent rows, or prove infeasibility from them;
  - move rows that keep a linking part into the linking block and eliminate there jointly;
  - leave dependencies through linking rows to the downstream factorization (pp. 9–10).
- (D4) Distribute parallel-row detection over two collectives. First combine the support hashes with a custom-operator Allreduce; then combine, with an AND-Allreduce, each process's flags for which row pairs are parallel on its own blocks, before any process applies a reduction (pp. 11–12, 24–26).
- (D5) Make parallel runs deterministic by fixing the order of reductions inside collectives. Check that outputs are identical across repeated runs and process counts, and print what determinism costs and on which instances (pp. 13, 15).
- (D5) Give rivals their best case: time them without file reading, and reconstruct the modeller's annotation for their output where possible, verified by nonzero counts. Report the end-to-end cost separately (pp. 13, 18–20).
- (D5) Explain a scaling result instead of only plotting it:
  - correlate the speed-up (Spearman) with size, block count and the share of the linking part;
  - decompose communication time into the number of collectives × the time per collective, plus local work;
  - use the fastest process's MPI share as a lower bound on the cost of communication (pp. 17, 26–27).
- (D5) Report the SGM over instances both arms solve together with an all-instance SGM that counts unsolved runs at the limit, and classify the weaker arm's failures (wrong infeasibility, iteration limit, time limit, crash) (p. 18).
- (D7) Put a "Scope and limitations" paragraph in the setup section, before the first result (p. 13).

### S085 · A Numerically-safe Branch-Price-and-Cut Algorithm for the Length-Constrained Cycle Partition Problem (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2607.15837)
**Read level**: full. arXiv v1 (17 Jul 2026). Pp. 1–24 read completely, and Appendix A (per-instance Table 5, pp. 25–28) read and recounted. The references (pp. 29–31) were scanned.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Mohammed Ghannam (first author, ZIB), Edward Lam (Monash), Gioni Mexi (ZIB); Gleixner is listed at ZIB and HTW Berlin (p. 1). The byline is alphabetical, while the 2024 version is cited as Ghannam, Mexi, Lam, Gleixner (p. 30). · **Place in the trajectory**:
- the paper extends the IOS 2024 branch and price (S092, arXiv 2401.17937) with cuts and "a framework for numerically-safe dual bounds", plus a larger study (p. 3);
- a side study of the round-up property exists (S081: Runnwerth, Ghannam, Gleixner 2025; pp. 23, 31);
- the baseline is the branch and cut of Hoppmann-Baum et al. 2022, of which Mexi is a co-author (pp. 2, 30);
- funding came from MODAL and MATH+ (p. 23);
- the paper is decomposition research, which SKILL.md's Honest Boundary places outside his field (group work).

- **D1 Problem and entry**:
  - The problem (LCCP): partition the nodes of an undirected graph into the fewest cycles whose travel time does not exceed the smallest critical time of their nodes (p. 1; Eq. (1)). Applications include periodic surveillance or maintenance by a minimum number of agents (p. 2).
  - Prior work: a compact MTZ model solved instances of up to 29 nodes, and a SEC branch and cut on a commercial solver up to 52 nodes. Both models have weak relaxations, auxiliary variables and symmetry (p. 2).
  - First entry: the paper aims "to overcome computational bottlenecks of the previous approaches by considering a set partitioning formulation based on cycle variables" (p. 2), taken from kidney exchange and vehicle routing (p. 3).
  - Second entry (Sec. 5): floating-point duals of the restricted master problem (RMP) are feasible only within a tolerance Δ, "typically between 10−6 and 10−9" (extracted form). Unsafe column generation can therefore report a lower bound that is too large, and it can miss improving columns (p. 13).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: a set partitioning model (SP) over length-feasible cycles, solved by branch-price-and-cut (pp. 2–3). The components:
  - Pricing (LCPCCP) for each start node, by label setting with feasibility and dominance pruning. The visited set is enlarged by neighbours that are already unreachable (pp. 4–6).
  - Bidirectional search. Because the graph is undirected and the travel times are symmetric, labels are extended in one direction only, up to half the critical time. Pairs that share only their end node are merged with three correction terms (pp. 6–7).
  - An ng-route relaxation with neighbourhood expansion and a Lagrangian bound (pp. 8–9).
  - A greedy remove/insert heuristic that runs before labeling (p. 9).
  - Per-node detection of the triangle inequality: such nodes get covering rows, zero-dual nodes are skipped and labels are pruned preemptively (pp. 9–10).
  - Subset-row cuts (SRCs, k = 2, |R| = 3) and clique cuts, each cut lifted to every new column (pp. 11–12).
  - Safe bounds from integer-scaled duals (pp. 12–14).
  - Edge or Ryan–Foster branching, symmetry breaking by critical time, early branching, Farkas pricing, and a heuristic branch and price with relaxed dominance (pp. 14–17).
  - Claimed new: the one-direction bidirectional search, the symmetry breaking by critical time and the exploitation of the triangle inequality. The rest is adapted from vehicle routing and set partitioning (pp. 3, 8, 11–12, 15–16).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - Data: travel times t_ij ≥ 0, possibly non-metric; critical times q_i > 0; duals unrestricted in sign; singleton cycles allowed (pp. 3, 5).
  - The upper bounds λ_C ≤ 1 are dropped as implied by the partitioning rows (p. 4).
  - Pricing is strongly NP-hard even with metric travel times and constant critical times (Theorem 1, p. 4).
  - The objective (number of cycles) is integral; this is used to round bounds and for early branching (pp. 13, 16).
  - Benchmark: the standard set of Hoppmann-Baum et al. 2022, "84 instances with 14 to 100 nodes" (p. 17), although Table 5 lists kro124p with 124 nodes (p. 28).
  - The text does not say how travel and critical times are stored, or whether length feasibility is checked exactly.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Proof devices:
    - Theorem 1: reduction from Hamiltonian cycle with unit data (p. 4);
    - feasibility pruning by induction over label extensions (p. 5);
    - Lemma 1 (dominance: same end node, lower or equal c and t, N(ℓa) ⊆ N(ℓb)), proved by inclusion of the extension sets and the monotone minimum critical time (p. 6);
    - Theorem 2 (bidirectional search with dominance is complete): take the first forward label past the halfway point; the reverse label then lies within the halfway point, and transitivity of dominance supplies a label that can be merged (pp. 7–8);
    - Lemma 2: zero-dual nodes that satisfy the triangle inequality can be skipped, by a shortcut argument (p. 10);
    - post-processing from covering back to partitioning (pp. 9–10);
    - Lagrangian bound z*_MP ≥ z*_RMP + Σ_s min{0, z*_s}, rounded up by integrality (pp. 9, 16).
  - Column limits: bidirectional search can return more, possibly suboptimal, columns, so at most 50 per start node are returned (p. 8). The greedy heuristic adds at most |V| columns per iteration (p. 9).
  - Cut management (p. 12):
    - clique cuts are ignored in pricing (added to the RMP only); SRCs are tested both ways;
    - every cut is lifted to each new column;
    - at most 10 cuts per family per round and 100 in the whole tree;
    - child nodes inherit their parent's cuts.
  - Numerical safeguard (Algorithm 1, p. 14; Algorithm 2, p. 16):
    - set negative covering duals and cut duals to zero (pp. 13–14);
    - set M = 10^15 (extracted as "1015") and π^int = ⌊M·π^inf⌋;
    - check Σ_{i∈C} π^int_i ≤ M in exact integer arithmetic for every column of the RMP;
    - divide M by ten until the check passes; below M < 1 the repair fails;
    - then run pricing and compute the bounds in exact integer arithmetic.
  - Cuts are combinatorial and therefore safe; a floating-point violation check can only affect performance (p. 13).
  - A safe bound changes a decision only when it crosses an integer. It may force a branching where the unsafe bound would already prove optimality, and it prevents an invalidly strong bound, such as 8.0001 rounding up to 9 (pp. 13–14).
  - What the algorithm does when the repair fails is not described (p. 14).
  - Search: best estimate with plunging (Achterberg 2007), with the pricing problems solved in parallel (p. 15); dual smoothing, with no value given for α (p. 16).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup:
    - implementation: the Rust wrapper of SCIP (russcip), with pricing parallelized by Rayon; "The rest of SCIP is sequential." (p. 17). The SCIP version is not stated; the paper cites the 8.0 and 9.0 reports;
    - baseline bnc-sec: the SEC branch and cut on Gurobi 13.0.1 (p. 18);
    - hardware: 2 h time limit, 2 × Xeon Gold 6338, 1 TB, exclusive node, up to 16 threads for both methods (p. 17);
    - four numbered questions open the section (p. 17);
    - SGM with a shift of 1 s, counting timeouts at the limit, over the instances solved by at least one configuration in each table; "consequently, absolute SGM values are not directly comparable across tables" (p. 17);
    - the best known solutions of the heuristic configurations become the initial upper bounds for all later experiments, so that these focus on the dual task (p. 17).
  - Overall results (Table 1, p. 18):
    - solved: bnc-sec 37, bnp-mcv 46, bnp-full 51, bnc-full 40;
    - SGM time: 147.2, 19.8, 10.0 and 109.4 s;
    - root gaps: 30.3% for the SEC model against 0.4% for SP (pp. 18, 20).
    - Like-for-like factors: bnp-mcv against bnc-sec, both started from the MCV solution, "over 7 times" (147.2/19.8 = 7.4); bnp-full against bnc-full, both started from the best known solution, "over 10 times" (109.4/10.0 = 10.9) (p. 19).
    - Primal side: heurbnp-rf improves on 47 instances, with a 0.2% gap to the best known solutions (p. 18).
  - Recount of Table 5 (pp. 25–28):
    - solved: bnp-full 51 and bnc-sec 37; 36 by both, 15 by bnp-full only, and one (t62 hk48) by bnc-sec only;
    - bnc-sec's largest solved instance has 48 nodes. The "52 nodes for the branch-and-cut baseline" (p. 19) is therefore the limit reported in the literature (p. 2), or that of bnc-full, whose per-instance results are not printed;
    - Table 1's SGMs are reproduced (9.95 s and 147.1 s) only if two further instances are counted at the time limit for both arms. By inference, two instances were solved only by bnc-full or bnp-mcv.
  - Ablation (Table 2, p. 20; pp. 19–21):
    - symmetry breaking: 3.6× and 5 instances;
    - bidirectional search: 3.2× and 4 instances;
    - parallel pricing: 2.2×;
    - early branching: negligible, although noearly alone solves t84 brazil58;
    - greedy heuristic: 13%. It succeeds in 93% of pricing calls, supplies 77% of the columns, and cuts ng expansions from 7.5 to 3.6 per instance;
    - best known start solutions: 2.07× and 5 instances;
    - virtual best: 52 instances.
    - The ng neighbourhoods reach at most 67% of full size (38% on average at their maximum). The elementary variant is therefore left out, on the argument that it "would only ever be slower" (p. 21).
    - The root LP converges on 52 instances, and 49 are solved at the root; on all 51 solved instances, the rounded-up root bound is at least z* − 1 (pp. 19–20).
  - Alternatives that improve one isolated metric but not overall performance (p. 21):
    - the triangle inequality halves the root iterations (126 to 64), yet only 49 instances are solved. The two lost instances are exactly the two whose root bound is one unit weaker;
    - Ryan–Foster branching is run without the improved upper bounds, "To expose enough branching to compare the two rules": it solves 45 against 46 and is 1.7× slower (14 nodes against 8 on average on the 15 instances that branch);
    - dual stabilization saves 14% of the root iterations, but the SGM moves only from 6.5 to 6.3 s.
  - Cost of safety (Table 3, p. 21): the unsafe variant takes 4.5 s against 7.6 s and both solve 51, but not the same 51. The unsafe variant closes t62 hk48 and the safe one at62 p43, on which the safe run needs 297.3 s and bnc-sec 0.1 s (p. 26). "No dual bound failures were detected" (p. 21).
  - Cuts (Table 4, p. 22):
    - SRCs are separated on only 3 instances and clique cuts on 2;
    - withsrc solves 3 fewer instances and is 27% slower; withcc is neutral;
    - withsrc+pricing solves 52 instances in 7.2 s;
    - the slowdowns are put down to separation and LP re-optimization, since iteration counts barely change.
    - The extra instance credited to withsrc+pricing, t62 hk48, is not among the three instances where SRCs were added (p. 22), and it also flips with unsafe mode (p. 21).
  - The paper runs no seeds, permutations or significance tests, and does not say whether the parallel pricing is deterministic.
  - The instances and scripts are public in the repository lccp-ejor (p. 24).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - The paper continues Hoppmann et al. 2020 and Hoppmann-Baum et al. 2022 (both with Mexi) through a reformulation, not a better branch and cut (pp. 2–3).
  - It borrows from vehicle routing and kidney exchange: Costa et al. 2019, Lam and Mak-Hau 2020, Irnich 2008, Tilk et al. 2017, Baldacci et al. 2011, Jepsen et al. 2008, Desaulniers 2010, Spliet 2024 (pp. 3–12).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contributions: the formulation, a pricing algorithm with proofs, a framework for safe bounds, and the branch-price-and-cut. Computationally: 52 instances solved with up to 76 nodes, against a previous limit of 52 nodes (pp. 1, 3, 23).
  - Limits printed:
    - instances beyond 76 nodes remain unsolved, and pricing dominates the running time (p. 23);
    - the round-up property fails on t84 eil51 and is undecided on t84 eil76 (p. 23);
    - cuts rarely separate (p. 22);
    - the heuristic "dual bound" comes "with no formal guarantee of exactness" (p. 19).
  - On safety: "While we did not notice any incorrect results when using unsafe floating-point computation", the conclusion calls the overhead of safe computation moderate (p. 23).
  - Future work: faster pricing (parallel labeling, Petersen and Spoorendonk 2025; GPU-accelerated state expansion, Tardivo et al. 2026). Parallelizing the sequential tree search and adding cuts can "only be unlocked once pricing is no longer the bottleneck" (p. 23).

Only what decides the result is exact: the repaired integer duals, the integer-arithmetic pricing and the bound (pp. 12–14, 16). The heuristic variants are best run unsafe (p. 21). Safety costs about 40% of the time and changed no certified bound (pp. 14, 21).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) For an undirected pricing problem with symmetric resources, extend labels in one direction only, up to half the resource bound, and merge pairs that share only their end node. Prove completeness with the first forward label past the halfway point plus transitivity of dominance (pp. 6–8).
- (D4) Add neighbours that are already unreachable to the visited set. This strengthens subset-based dominance without affecting correctness (p. 6).
- (D4) Break symmetry across the per-node pricing problems by generating each cycle only from its node with the smallest critical time (index as tie-break). This also balances the effort across pricing problems. Skip a pricing problem when a fixed edge points to a node that comes earlier in this order (p. 15).
- (D4) Make a floating-point column-generation bound safe:
  - set the cut duals and negative covering duals to zero;
  - scale the duals by M = 10^15 and round down;
  - check every column of the RMP in integer arithmetic, dividing M by ten until the check passes;
  - then price and bound in integer arithmetic (pp. 13–14, 16).
- (D4) With an integer objective, round the safe bound up, and test whether safety ever matters by comparing the rounded safe and unsafe bounds per instance. Run the heuristic variants unsafe (pp. 13–14, 21).
- (D4) When pricing ignores a cut's dual, still lift the cut to every new column (p. 12).
- (D5) Control the shared input across arms: start both methods from the same heuristic solution, and warm-start the rival with the same best known solution as your own method (pp. 18–19).
- (D5) Fix the primal side, using the best known solutions as initial upper bounds, for all component experiments, so that the comparison isolates the dual task (p. 17).
- (D5) When most instances close at the root, compare branching rules in a rerun without the strong initial bounds, so that the trees are large enough to compare, and report it outside the main ablation (p. 21).
- (D5) Attribute a variant's losses instance by instance; here the two lost instances are exactly the two with a root bound one unit weaker (p. 21).
- (D5) State for each table which instances its SGM is taken over, and warn that absolute SGMs are not comparable across tables (p. 17).
