### S036 · Safe and verified Gomory mixed-integer cuts in a rational mixed-integer program framework (SIAM Journal on Optimization 34(1) 2024, DOI 10.1137/23m156046x; arXiv 2303.12365)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v3 (7 Nov 2023), titled "Safe and Verified Gomory Mixed Integer Cuts in a Rational MIP Framework"; pp. 1–29 read, including Appendix A (proof of Lemma 7, pp. 28–29); references (pp. 29–33) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Leon Eifler (first author, ZIB) · **Place in the trajectory**: the first solver component added to exact SCIP after the status update ([13] = Eifler and Gleixner, Math. Program. 2022), which had added presolve and a repair step for heuristics (p. 2); it closes by asking for precision boosting inside exact LP refinement (p. 26).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: roundoff-free MIP is needed where MIP is a tool in mathematics or where correctness is paramount (hardware verification, compiler optimization, infeasibility analysis in hydro unit commitment) (p. 2). The 2013 hybrid framework lacks advanced techniques because their application without roundoff "is often not trivial" (p. 2); after the status update, "One key feature missing to date is a separation routine for cutting planes" (p. 2). Entry: the cut families with the largest measured benefit in Achterberg and Wunderling (MIR 48%, Gomory 28% speed-up), and the obstacle that an exact GMI cut needs an exact tableau row, i.e. a rational LU factorization and an exact LP, while the exact framework has only an approximate floating-point LP solution (pp. 2–3, 6).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: carry the numerically safe GMI cuts of Cook, Dash, Fukasawa and Goycoolea (2009), built for a floating-point solver, into the rational framework: aggregate rows with multipliers from an approximate row of the basis inverse and apply MIR with directed rounding, so every cut is exactly valid but slightly weaker (pp. 3, 6, 8). Changed: rows whose coefficients are not floating-point representable are relaxed first (Lemma 1, p. 7); negative lower bounds are allowed (p. 5); cuts are post-processed by scaling and by bounding their denominators (pp. 10–15); each cut is verified in VIPR (pp. 15–20). Unchanged: aggregation along the tableau row, the MIR formula (p. 8), scaling rules "similar to the ones that are used by the corresponding floating-point algorithm in SCIP" (p. 10) and the floating-point solver's default separation parameters (pp. 26–27).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP with rational data, min{cᵀx : Ax ≤ b, ℓ ≤ x ≤ u, x_j ∈ Z for j ∈ I}, infinite bounds allowed (p. 4). Assumption 1: every variable with a nonzero in a cut has a finite lower or upper bound; splitting free variables always achieves it, after presolving it "usually seems to hold", and no cut had to be discarded in the experiments (p. 4). F is IEEE double precision (p. 6). Correctness rests on Lemma 1, Corollary 2 and Lemma 3 (pp. 7–10) and on Marchand and Wolsey's MIR validity proof (p. 17).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: four steps: approximate rows by F-representable relaxations, aggregate safely, construct the MIR cut, post-process (p. 5). Rounding is defined per arithmetic operation, so the summation order changes the result (Definition 1, pp. 6–7). One device proves Lemma 1, Corollary 2 and Lemma 3: substitute to non-negative variable space through the chosen bound, round coefficients in the safe direction, substitute back, round the right-hand side up (pp. 7–8, 10). The MIR transformation uses the bound closest to the LP value (p. 9); slacks of rows with integer data and integer variables are treated as integer variables in MIR and eliminated safely at the end (pp. 9–10). Post-processing: scale to equilibrium, or to integer coefficients via the Euclidean algorithm and the LCM with an error limit of 10^-6 and an LCM limit of 10^5 (extracted as "10−6" and "105"), falling back to equilibrium (pp. 10–11). Denominator control (Algorithm 1, p. 12) is motivated by the iterative refinement analysis, in which the smallest violation of a basic solution depends on the encoding length ([18, Lemma 5], p. 11): the best approximation with denominator at most M is taken among convergents and the single largest admissible intermediate fraction, the direction fixed by which bound of the variable is finite (Lemma 4, Algorithm 2, pp. 13–15); the needed fact about which intermediate fractions are best approximations had no proof in the literature, so the paper supplies one (footnote, p. 14; Lemma 7, pp. 28–29). Certificates: an MIR cut is a split disjunction plus two aggregations (Lemma 5, Theorem 6, p. 17); safe rounding makes the printed derivations only weakly dominating (Definition 2, p. 18), and a new completion script viprcomp converts them into standard VIPR by adding bound constraints, outside the checker (p. 18); the error from slack elimination cannot be recovered from the certificate, so exact slack coefficients are computed only for cuts that enter the LP and only when certificate printing is on (p. 19); an exact-LP completion option exists but is not the default because it is expensive and may fail (pp. 19–20); verification is printed only for cuts that enter the LP, with aggregation, split, bound and scaling data kept in a hashmap (p. 25).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: three questions open the section (p. 20). Settings: nocuts, safegmi, densmall (denominators limited to 2^17, extracted as "217", "a good tradeoff between accuracy and speed"), fpnocuts and fpgmi, the floating-point ones "with all plugins disabled that are not available in exact solving mode" (pp. 20–21). Xeon Gold 5122 3.6 GHz, 96 GB; SoPlex 6.0.2 for all LPs and exact LPs; GMP 6.1.2; PaPILO 2.0.1 with all other presolvers off (p. 21). Test set: MIPLIB 2017 benchmark minus the instances default floating-point SCIP 8.0 cannot solve in 2 h, 132 instances × 3 seeds = 396; 7200 s; shifted geometric means with shift 1 s and 100 nodes (p. 21). Gap closed (arithmetic means) on 367 instance-seed pairs after removing infeasible instances and those where floating-point and exact solutions disagree: root 0.150 / 0.152 / 0.161 (safegmi / densmall / fpgmi), after the time limit 0.485 / 0.580 / 0.585 (nocuts / safegmi / densmall) and 0.543 / 0.653 (fpnocuts / fpgmi) (Table 1, pp. 21–22). Runtime: solved 130 / 152 / 158; safegmi gains 34 and loses 12 against nocuts; densmall against nocuts −50.5% nodes, −26.8% time, exact-LP time +56.3% (Table 2, pp. 22–24). Floating point: fpgmi +39 solved, −61.5% nodes, −35.1% time (Table 3, pp. 24–25). Pairwise scatter plots because means "do not show the complete picture" (p. 22). Certificates: one seed (argued: overhead "not subject to performance variability", p. 24), densmall only, limit raised to 6 h; on the 49 instances solved in 2 h without certificates, +47.1% solving time and +57.7% including completion (25.73 s) and checking (40.10 s), against 65.8% reported in [13]; presolve uncertified, the optimal solution checked in the original space; outlier mas74 near 8 h (Table 4, pp. 24–26). Failures named: lost instances come from exact-LP time spikes when the refinement LP becomes "numerically too difficult" (p. 23); performance "drastically worsens" when exact LPs are called more often (p. 24). No significance test; "significant" is used informally (pp. 26–27).
- **D6 Positioning**: generalizes [11] (Cook, Dash, Fukasawa, Goycoolea) from a floating-point solver to exact MIP (p. 3) and continues [12] (Cook, Koch, Steffy, Wolter) and [13] (p. 2); certification placed beside SAT proof logging, pseudo-Boolean and MaxSAT certification and the 85,900-city TSP certificate (p. 3); its overhead contrasted with certified symmetry breaking in pseudo-Boolean solving: printing costs more and checking less, attributed to VIPR's more elementary logic (pp. 25–26). Predecessors are restated, not criticised (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: contributions numbered "first … second … third major" (p. 3); three numbered questions (p. 20); hedges: "hopefully a good approximation" (p. 8), "We conjecture" (p. 26), "reasonably confident" (p. 27). Differences of gap-closed fractions are printed as "%" (for example "0.9% more gap than densmall (0.161 vs. 0.152)", p. 22), and one pair is printed in reverse order (p. 21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithm (safe GMI cuts for rational data, encoding-length control), software (exact SCIP; viprcomp with the VIPR code [8], pp. 4, 18) and certification method (pp. 3, 18); 21.5% more instances solved and 26.8% less time (abstract; p. 26), "slightly less than what is achievable in the floating-point setting" (p. 26). Limits printed: the exact LP solver is the bottleneck (pp. 23, 26); presolve is not certified (p. 25). Future: precision boosting inside iterative refinement, tuning separation parameters for exact mode, arithmetically easier cut classes (knapsack cover, flow cover, zero-half) (pp. 26–27).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: floating point produces the multipliers and cuts and directed rounding makes them exactly valid (pp. 5–8); exact LPs only "when strictly necessary" (p. 24); exact slack coefficients only for LP-entering cuts and only with certificates on (p. 19); certificate completion after solving, because completing inside the solver would cancel the gain of safe rounding (p. 18).
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: floating-point SCIP reduced to the exact solver's features (fpnocuts, fpgmi), and the component compared on/off inside each arm (pp. 20–21, 24–25); the floating-point default separation settings kept "to make the results as comparable as possible" (pp. 26–27).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: no default-solver factor is printed; the paper compares the relative effect of the component in the two arms (nocuts → densmall against fpnocuts → fpgmi), not exact against default SCIP (Tables 2–3, pp. 24–25).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: one certificate per solved instance, "checked and verified independently of the solving algorithm" (p. 15); the checker's logic kept unchanged by moving completion into a separate tool (p. 18); coverage and cost printed (presolve uncertified, solution checked in the original space; +47.1% / +57.7%) (pp. 25–26).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: enters through the component the status update lacked (p. 2); prints the exact-LP bottleneck, the lost instances and the smaller-than-floating-point gain (pp. 23–24, 26); names precision boosting and further cut classes as next projects (pp. 26–27).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 9): gap-closed statistics drop the instances whose floating-point and exact solutions disagree (p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: certificate completion outside the checker (pp. 18–20; again in S074, p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate: price-of-exactness attribution, i.e. a smaller gain than in floating point explained by directed-rounding weakening plus zero tolerances (pp. 24, 26; again in S074, pp. 11–12).
- new-pattern candidate: encoding-length control as a numerical safeguard, derived from the convergence analysis of iterative refinement (pp. 11–15, 23–24).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) One safe-rounding lemma for every derived row: shift each variable to non-negative space through the bound you will use, round coefficients in the safe direction, shift back, round the right-hand side up; the same lemma then covers data relaxation, aggregation and scaling (pp. 7–8, 10).
- (D4) Bound the denominators of generated rows by best rational approximation under a denominator limit (convergents plus the largest admissible intermediate fraction), choosing the rounding direction by which bound of the variable is finite (Algorithms 1–2, pp. 12–15).
- (D4) Keep the checker minimal: extend only the certificate language (weak domination) and convert it with a separate completion tool after solving; print verification only for cuts that enter the LP (pp. 18, 25).
- (D5) Measure a new safe component twice, on/off inside the exact solver and on/off inside the floating-point solver stripped to the exact solver's features, and compare the relative effects (pp. 20–21, 24–25).
- (D5) Before computing gap closed, drop infeasible instances and instances where floating-point and exact optima disagree (p. 21).
- (D5) Time a certificate pipeline in three parts (solving with printing, completion, checking) on instances solved without certificates, with a raised time limit so that the same instances finish (pp. 24–26).

**Quotes**:
- "This has the benefit of not complicating the logic used inside the proof checker itself. Thus, confidence in the certification process remains as high as before." (p. 18)
- "We identify exact LP solving as the main difficulty in making safe cutting planes performant, and show ways to overcome this difficulty by post-processing their coefficients." (p. 26)

### S038 · Certifying MIP-Based Presolve Reductions for 0-1 Integer Linear Programs (CPAIOR 2024, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-60597-0_20; arXiv 2401.09277)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (20 Mar 2024); pp. 1–16 read; references (pp. 16–19) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Alexander Hoen (first author, ZIB), Andy Oertel (Lund and Copenhagen), Jakob Nordström (Lund and Copenhagen) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the presolve stage that exact-MIP certificates had left uncertified (p. 2; S036 p. 25), attacked with the pseudo-Boolean proof-logging tool of Nordström's community.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: presolve often decides whether an instance can be solved at all, in floating-point and exact solvers alike (pp. 1–2), yet there is "no established methodology to express and verify the equivalence of two mixed-integer programs" (abstract, p. 1). Solvers err (wrong infeasibility or optimality claims, infeasible "solutions"); SAT answered this with proof logging that covers preprocessing (DRAT, GRIT, LRAT) (p. 2). VIPR certifies only feasibility-based reasoning, so dual presolve is out and an exact-MIP certificate holds only under the assumption that presolve was valid, "as, e.g., in [21]" (the status update) (p. 2); SAT preprocessing proofs reason only about clauses (p. 2). Entry: restrict to 0–1 ILPs, "a large and important class of MIPs" (p. 3), and use VeriPB.
- **D2 Key idea and design**: express each PaPILO reduction on a 0–1 ILP as VeriPB steps: cutting planes or RUP for implied constraints, redundance-based strengthening with a witness substitution for reductions that remove solutions, checked deletion for removed constraints (pp. 3–4, 6–10); add one rule to VeriPB, an objective update that replaces f by f′ once f ≥ f′ and f′ ≥ f are derived from the core set (pp. 4–5). Claimed as the first use of VeriPB to prove reformulations of optimization problems (p. 2). Unchanged: the presolve algorithms themselves; the selection is "motivated by" PaPILO (p. 6).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: 0–1 ILPs (pseudo-Boolean formulas) with integer coefficients, decision and optimization versions (pp. 3, 15); reductions classed as primal (keep all feasible solutions), weak dual (keep all optimal ones) and strong dual (keep at least one optimal) (p. 6). Invariant: the core constraint set has the same optimal value as the input (pp. 3, 5). Data: pseudo-Boolean competitions PB10–PB16 (1398 decision, 532 optimization) and MIPLIB 2017 in OPB translation (295 decision, 145 optimization), minus 10 large instances on which PaPILO runs out of memory, named in a footnote (p. 12).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: general devices: substitution of an equality; aggregation with the rational scale split into integer factors s_E/s_D, which the presolver must store to translate between its own state and the certificate's (p. 6); checked deletion by inverting the derivation that replaced a constraint (pp. 6–7). Primal reductions: bound strengthening by RUP or an explicit derivation; parallel rows with their scalar; probing (three cases, by RUP or two auxiliary constraints); simple probing; sparsify; coefficient tightening via VeriPB's saturation rule; GCD-based simplification as weakening plus divide-and-multiply; substitution of implied free variables with the witnesses x_j ↦ 1 and x_j ↦ 0; singleton variables (pp. 7–9). Dual reductions: duality-based fixing with the witness x_j ↦ v; dominated variables with the swap witness; four advanced dominated-variable fixings (pp. 9–11). A worked certificate for substituting an implied free variable, also in the PaPILO repository (Fig. 1, p. 11). The objective-update rule is stated without a formal proof (p. 5). PaPILO searches reductions in parallel transactions; logging is sequential (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: PaPILO 2.2.0 on 6 threads, VeriPB 2.0; 11th Gen Intel Core i5-1145G7 2.60 GHz, 16 GB (14,000 MB assigned); 3 h limit for presolve, certification and verification together; reading time excluded; shifted geometric means with shift 1 s (p. 12). Proof-logging overhead below 6% on all four sets (Table 2, relative 1.00–1.06, pp. 12–13); objective changes logged as differences, because printing the full objective on normalized-datt256 (135,206 fixings, 262,144 nonzeros) produced a 138 GB certificate and 6625 s instead of 3.3 s, measured on other hardware (Xeon Gold 5122, footnote 6) (p. 12); overhead per applied reduction below the printed bound on 99% of instances (p. 12). Verification: timeouts scored as PAR2; 38 instances only partially verified in 3 h; verifying takes 14.7–22.4 times as long as producing on average, and 25% of instances need a factor of at least 193 (Table 3, Fig. 2, pp. 13–14); read against the overhead of certified CNF translations [31] (p. 13). RUP against explicit cutting-planes proofs (POL) for propagation, on instances with at least 10 propagations and 9 further memory exclusions: POL 0.7–13.1% faster, RUP at most 9.7% slower on 95% of the decision instances; RUP recommended for its constant and smaller certificate cost (Table 4, pp. 14–15). Count slip (my arithmetic): the stated totals of 671 optimization and 1681 decision instances (p. 12) match neither the listed sources minus the ten exclusions (677 and 1693 before exclusion) nor the set sizes of Table 2 (531 + 142 = 673; 1397 + 291 = 1688; p. 13). Single runs, no seeds.
- **D6 Positioning**: sets MIP against SAT's success with proof logging (p. 2); states the limits of VIPR, Gleixner's own earlier format (feasibility-based reasoning only, no dual presolve) (p. 2); credits VeriPB's earlier uses in SAT, MaxSAT, subgraph problems and constraint programming (p. 2); takes presolve methods from Achterberg et al. 2019, Savelsbergh, Gamrath et al., Gemander et al. and PaPILO (pp. 6–10). Results are read against certified CNF translations, not against MIP baselines (pp. 2, 13).
- **D7 Writing**: "first step" framing (abstract; p. 2); the experiment's findings are offered as input for revising the proof format (abstract; p. 15); a surprise is reported and then explained ("it is surprising that the performance difference between the methods is not more pronounced", p. 14); the close is confident ("we believe our results show convincingly", p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a certified presolver for 0–1 ILPs plus one new VeriPB rule (pp. 2, 15); certificate output in PaPILO (p. 11, ref. [38]). Limits: 0–1 ILPs only (p. 3); the new rule's soundness proof omitted (p. 5); verbose certificates and heavy verification on a quarter of the instances (pp. 13, 15). Future: a native substitution rule (estimated up to 90% smaller certificates on substitution-dominated instances), ordered constraint lists for RUP, and extension to bounded and unbounded integer and continuous domains via [34, 42] and the generalized proof system [20] (van Doornmalen, Eifler, Gleixner, Hojny) (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: a stand-alone checker (VeriPB) now covers presolve, the stage exact-MIP certificates had left out (p. 2), with a derivation for every reduction class (pp. 6–11).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the checker's proof system is extended by a rule whose soundness proof is omitted (p. 5), so the method's own limit (a check is only as strong as checker and specification) applies to the new rule.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (step 4): the gap is closed by importing the SAT and pseudo-Boolean community's tool together with its authors (Oertel, Nordström) and publishing at CPAIOR (pp. 1–2); the bottlenecks the experiment exposed are printed as the next revisions of the format (p. 15).
- new-pattern candidate: read certification overhead against a rival community's certified task (pp. 2, 13; S036, pp. 25–26).
- new-pattern candidate: choose a certificate encoding by measurement (RUP against explicit derivations) (pp. 14–15).
- new-pattern candidate (negative): text–table count slips in the computational section (pp. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Classify a reduction before certifying it: primal reductions by implicational steps (cutting planes or RUP), dual reductions by redundance-based strengthening with an explicit witness substitution (pp. 6–10).
- (D4) When the presolver rescales a row internally, store the integer scaling factor so that the solver's state and the certificate's state can be translated (p. 6).
- (D4) Log objective changes as differences, not as full objectives: on one dense instance the full objective gave a 138 GB certificate and 6625 s instead of 3.3 s (p. 12).
- (D5) Report proof-logging cost three ways: relative to the unlogged run, per applied reduction, and verification time relative to generation together with the share of instances above a factor (pp. 12–13).
- (D5) Test two certificate encodings for the same reasoning and choose by verification time and certificate size (pp. 14–15).
- (D5) Score checker timeouts with PAR2 (twice the time limit), the SAT convention (p. 13).
- (D8) Turn bottlenecks found in the experiment into concrete format revisions with an estimated effect (native substitution rule, up to 90%; ordered RUP hints) (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "such a certificate would only establish correctness under the assumption that all the presolving steps were valid" (p. 2)
- "Such a rule needs to be designed with care, so that the optimal value of the problem is preserved. Due to space constraints we cannot provide a formal proof here" (p. 5)

### S055 · The MIP Workshop 2023 Computational Competition on reoptimization (Mathematical Programming Computation 16(2) 2024, DOI 10.1007/s12532-024-00256-w; arXiv 2311.14834)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (29 Nov 2023); pp. 1–8 read; references (pp. 8–9) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Suresh Bolusani (first author, ZIB), Mathieu Besançon, Timo Berthold, Claudia D'Ambrosio, Gonzalo Muñoz, Joseph Paat, Dimitri Thomopulos (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: second edition of the MIP Workshop competition, after a 2022 edition on primal heuristics (p. 1); the competition repository is github.com/ambros-gleixner/MIPcc23 (ref. [1], p. 8). A competition report, not a paper with experiments.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: benchmarks solve each instance from scratch, while in practice and inside algorithms (Benders row generation, column-generation pricing, scenario decomposition, diving and neighbourhood heuristics) solvers face series of similar instances (pp. 1–2); reoptimization research is limited and mostly application-specific or not scalable (p. 2); no dedicated reference benchmark exists (p. 3). Entry: define the task, build the data, design the score.
- **D2 Key idea and design**: series of 50 related MILPs with fixed dimensions and meaning of rows and columns; a submission solves a series in order and may use only information from earlier instances (pp. 2, 6); each instance gets a score on one ordered scale, teams are ranked per instance, later instances weigh more (pp. 6–7). Non-purpose stated: no offline training on applications (p. 2). A jury judges by two criteria, "Novelty and scope" and "Computational excellence" (p. 6).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MILPs whose objective, variable bounds, constraint sides or matrix coefficients vary within a series (p. 2); seven public and five hidden constant-matrix series, plus three matrix-varying series kept out of the evaluation because of "the more challenging nature" of such changes (p. 2; Table 1, p. 3). Bases: MIPLIB instances rococoC10-001000, csched007, ci-s4 and glass4; stochastic multiple binary knapsack; stochastic server location; a synthetic MILP from [18]; decision-tree column-generation subproblems; hydro unit commitment (two series, one an industrial case from a public call); chance-constrained vaccine allocation with big-M; an SAP supply-chain model (pp. 3–5). Time limits per instance 60–600 s by series (Table 1, p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (here the evaluation instrument; no proofs): series built by generating many instances, solving each from scratch and selecting 50 by solve time and by cosine similarity of the varying vector (the formula is a cosine, called "the angle between the vectors"; p. 3); time limits set from the from-scratch times (p. 3). Rules: fixed order, no look-ahead, no change to a returned solution, 1 thread, 16 GB, total time per series (p. 6). Score f = reltime + gap + nofeas, placing solved instances in [0, 1], timeouts with an incumbent in [1, 2] and no solution at 3, continuous at the solved/unsolved boundary (pp. 6–7); loopholes closed: exceeding the limit raises reltime above 1, stopping early without zero gap is charged reltime 1 (p. 7); an infeasible solution or invalid dual bound costs twice the worst possible rank (p. 7); final score C = Σ_s Σ_i (1 + 0.1 i) r(s, i) (p. 7). Instances and generation scripts in two public repositories (p. 3; refs. [1], [2], p. 8).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a as an experiment: no scores, ranks or baseline runs are printed; the paper reports twelve registrations, six final submissions, one winner (Patel, built on SCIP: reuse of primal information and pseudocosts, online parameter tuning) and one honourable mention (influence branching with bandit-based tuning) (p. 7). Not stated: the solver and machine behind the from-scratch times and limits (p. 3), how infeasible solutions or invalid dual bounds were detected (p. 7), the evaluation hardware beyond thanks for infrastructure (p. 7), and how the jury weighed its two criteria (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: claims the first dedicated reoptimization benchmark (p. 3); earlier reoptimization work cited as narrow (shortest paths with time windows, frameworks for specific combinatorial classes, methods for general MILPs only when specific components vary) (p. 2); the score is set against measures such as the confined primal integral, which handle mixed outcomes but not the intended hierarchy (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing**: a design report that gives a reason for each rule and score term (pp. 6–7); one self-assessment without evidence ("In our mind, this scoring framework allows for nuanced evaluation", p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a methodology for generating benchmark series, an evaluation framework and datasets (abstract; p. 7). Limits: matrix-changing series not evaluated (p. 2); nothing in the paper to reproduce the ranking from (p. 7). Future: grow the generation scripts into a benchmark library for reoptimization and add series (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: purpose and non-purpose stated (p. 2); public data and generator scripts (p. 3); hidden series (p. 2); written rules and a score with stated reasons (pp. 6–7); loopholes closed and invalid answers penalised (p. 7).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: the paper does not say how infeasible solutions or invalid dual bounds were detected (p. 7), so the SKILL.md step "verify bounds against exact values" is not supported by this text (it may come from the repository); no result table is printed (p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate: an ordered-band score, continuous at the band boundary and weighted by position in the series (pp. 6–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Put all outcomes on one ordered scale: solved in [0, 1] by time fraction, timed out with an incumbent in [1, 2] by gap, no solution at 3, continuous where solved meets unsolved (pp. 6–7).
- (D4) Close loopholes inside the score: running past the limit raises the time term above 1, stopping early without zero gap is charged the full limit; aggregate by per-instance ranks and give invalid answers twice the worst rank (p. 7).
- (D4) To reward learning across a series, weight later instances more (1 + 0.1 i), and say explicitly that early exploration should not be punished (pp. 6–7).
- (D3) Build series by generating many candidates, solving each from scratch, and selecting by solve time and similarity of the varying vector; publish instances and generators separately and keep hidden series (pp. 2–3).
- (D3) Write the non-purpose and the information rules into the call: no offline training, no look-ahead, no change to submitted answers (pp. 2, 6).

**Quotes**:
- "The intention of this competition was not to perform offline training on different types of applications but to reuse information from current and previous solving processes to accelerate the solution of future instances." (p. 2)
- "we also aimed to avoid unduly restricting approaches that engage in an early exploration phase, which might intentionally deteriorate performance for the first few instances of a series." (p. 6)

### S074 · Certified Constraint Propagation and Dual Proof Analysis in a Numerically Exact MIP Solver (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2403.13567)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v1 (20 Mar 2024); pp. 1–12 read; references (pp. 12–16) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Sander Borst (first author, CWI), Leon Eifler (ZIB) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the next component of exact SCIP after the safe GMI cuts ([EG23] = S036) (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: exact solutions are needed in computational mathematics, in some industry applications and on pathological instances where floating-point solvers err (p. 1); propagation detects infeasible subproblems faster than an LP solve, and conflict analysis "has not yet been adapted to the exact setting" (p. 2). Entry: the lesson of the earlier exact-MIP papers that numerical techniques must run in floating point "in a numerically safe way" (p. 2). Dual proof analysis was chosen over graph-based conflict analysis because the latter is numerically simpler (±1 coefficients) and the paper's focus is numerics (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: keep the floating-point algorithms and make them safe: activities as directed-rounding bounds on the exact activities, dual rays from the floating-point LP, conflict constraints aggregated with safe rounding as for the GMI cuts of [EG23] (pp. 5–7); certify propagated bounds and conflict constraints in VIPR (pp. 7–8). Unchanged: the propagation formulas (1)–(2) and the aging of conflict constraints (pp. 3, 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min cᵀx s.t. Ax ≥ b, ℓ ≤ x ≤ u, x_i ∈ Z for i ∈ S (p. 3); finite bounds assumed ("For the sake of simplicity of presentation"), with propagation on some constraints with unbounded variables skipped (p. 3). Test set: MIPLIB 2017 benchmark minus the instances default floating-point SCIP 8.0.3 cannot solve in 2 h, 132 instances × 3 seeds = 396 (p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: safe activity updates with directed rounding; the maintained maximum activity is never below the exact one, so it is never recomputed, whereas floating-point activities must be recomputed to stop error accumulation (p. 6). Dual proof: ray y from a floating-point LP solve with standard tolerances, slightly negative multipliers set to zero because "any conic combination of the constraints is globally valid" (p. 6); aggregation row by row with the rounding direction chosen by the sign of each variable's bounds (pp. 6–7); the Farkas-proof argument shows why propagation on the conflict constraint cuts the node off (pp. 4–5). Certification: a propagated bound is a VIPR linear combination of the row and the bound constraints with multiplier 1/a_k, plus a rounding step when all coefficients and variables are integral (eqs. (6)–(7), pp. 7–8); conflict constraints are printed as the original multipliers with the current bounds and repaired afterwards by viprcomplete, only for constraints actually used (p. 8). Applying a bound change is still done in exact arithmetic (each floating-point bound converted to a rational), the main extra cost (pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP 8.0.3 with SoPlex 6.0.3 as floating-point and exact LP solver, GMP 6.1.2, PaPILO 2.0.1 with other presolving off, Xeon Gold 5122 3.6 GHz, 2 h, shifted geometric means with shift 1 s and 100 nodes (p. 9). Exact SCIP: propagation −11.6% time, +12 solved, −13.6% nodes; dual proof analysis a further −13.4% time, −17.7% nodes, +3 solved; together −23.4% time, −28.9% nodes, +15 solved (Table 1, pp. 9–10); performance profile (Fig. 1, p. 11). Floating-point SCIP with all cutting planes, graph-based conflict analysis, restarts, presolving other than PaPILO and custom propagation rules disabled: together −44.5% time, +31 solved (Table 2, pp. 9–10). Each table counts only instances solved by one of its own configurations, so the arms use different subsets, which the authors name as one reason for the difference (p. 10). Certificate generation is excluded from all times on the argument that it "does not change the solving path" (p. 8); no certificate is produced, checked or timed in this paper. Without the time spent in propagation the speed-ups are 17.1% and 28.3% (p. 11). Two remedies for large denominators (limit them; no propagation on continuous variables) gave no positive effect (pp. 11–12). Slip (my arithmetic): the text's floating-point node reductions (−67.1% for dual proof analysis, −65.1% overall; pp. 9, 12) do not match Table 2 (12119.4 → 6986.1 is −42.4%; 11415.0 → 6986.1, printed as relative 0.61, is −38.8%; p. 10). No significance test.
- **D6 Positioning**: continues [CKSW13], [EG22] and [EG23] (p. 2); dual proof analysis from Witzig, Berthold and Heinz [WBH16, WBH21] (p. 2); certification framed by the certifying-algorithms literature and by SAT, SMT, pseudo-Boolean and MIP certification (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: the abstract is in the third person ("The authors employ safe rounding methods", "The study also addresses", p. 1); the reasons for weaker exact gains are enumerated ("First … Second …", "There are more reasons", pp. 10–11); a null remedy is reported with its reading (p. 12); one forecast is unhedged: graph-based conflict analysis "is sure to yield positive impact in the exact setting" (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: safe propagation and dual proof analysis in exact SCIP with VIPR certification; code in the exact-rational branch of SCIP (pp. 2, 9 footnote 1). Limits: gains smaller than in floating point, "partially the price to be paid for exactness" (p. 12); exact bound changes cost time (pp. 10–11); no propagation in strong branching "due to technical reasons" (p. 12). Future: floating-point bound changes, propagation in strong branching, conflict strengthening from [WBH21], graph-based conflict analysis, sporadic recomputation of activities (p. 12).

### S091 · Branch and Cut for Partitioning a Graph into a Cycle of Clusters (ISCO 2024, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-60924-4_8; arXiv 2401.08412)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v1 (16 Jan 2024); pp. 1–11 read; references (p. 12) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Leon Eifler (first author, ZIB), Jakob Witzig (ZIB and SAP) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an application line on biology simulation data; the problem was introduced in Witzig et al. 2018 ([16], p. 1). No numerics, exactness or certificates.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: cycle clustering detects cyclic behaviour in nonreversible Markov state models, where spectral methods are not easily applicable (p. 1); solving it to global optimality "has proven to be challenging" (p. 2). Entry: a problem-specific formulation and branch and cut, against the generic compact linearization.
- **D2 Key idea and design**: model only the three relations an edge can be in (same cluster y, consecutive clusters z, further apart) instead of products of assignment variables as in the RLT linearization (pp. 3–4); add valid inequalities with separation and four primal heuristics (pp. 5–9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: complete graph with nonnegative, non-symmetric weights q, m clusters, objective α·(net flow between consecutive clusters) + (1 − α)·(coherence), 0 < α < 1, q_ii = 0 w.l.o.g. (pp. 1–2); assignment (set-partitioning) constraints and non-empty clusters (p. 3); formulations: binary quadratic (2), mixed-binary RLT after Liberti and Mallach, binary CC (3) (pp. 3–4). Data: 65 instances with 20–250 vertices and 3–15 clusters from four simulation types (catalytic cycles, repressilator, Hindmarsh–Rose, Amyloid-β), α = 1/1.001 (p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: both formulations have the same feasible x and the same root LP bound, attained at x_is = 1/m (p. 5); RLT has about 0.5m² times as many variables, CC about twice the constraints, and linearization variables are needed only where q_ij + q_ji > 0 (p. 5); polytope dimension and facet results for bounds and model inequalities (p. 5). Triangle inequalities with facet theorems for m = 4 and m > 4, separated by enumeration in O(n³) (Theorems 1–2, p. 6); partition inequalities, facet-defining for m > 4 and |S| − |T| = ±1, separated heuristically from almost violated triangles with |S| + |T| ≤ 5 (Theorem 3, p. 7); extended subtour and path inequalities, valid because forward transitions around a cycle come in multiples of m, separated exactly by dynamic programming over maximum-weight walks in O(n³m) (Theorem 4, pp. 7–8). Facet proofs "follow standard patterns and are left for an extended version of the paper" (p. 5). Heuristics: greedy, O(n²m), once at the start; sparsify, dropping 97% of the lightest edges for a root-only sub-MIP; rounding, O(nm); Lin–Kernighan-style exchange that accepts negative moves, applied to each new incumbent with at most 5 random perturbations (pp. 8–9).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: both formulations in SCIP (no version given; ref. [7] is the SCIP Optimization Suite 6.0 report), default settings apart from the new plugins; 7200 s; one job per node; Xeon E5-2670 v2 2.7 GHz, 64 GB (p. 9); shifted geometric means with shifts 10 s, 100 nodes and 1000 for primal and dual integrals; final gap (d − p)/min{p + ε, d + ε} as an arithmetic mean, which can exceed 100% (pp. 9–10). Table 1: CC with separation 31 solved in 755.3 s against RLT 29 and RLT with separation 28, plain CC 24; separation cuts CC time by 51.3% and nodes by 81.5% but slows RLT; confirmed on the 34 instances solved by some setting (p. 10). Table 2, one component at a time from CC with all cutting planes and heuristics off: subtour the best separator (−40.4% time, −67.1% nodes), all separators +4 solved and 45% faster; exchange the best heuristic and the only one adding a solved instance (pp. 10–11). No seeds or permutations; no LP solver named (p. 9). Slip: "applying all heuristics together gives the best results" (p. 11) holds in Table 2 only for the primal integral; all heuristics together solve 24 instances, against 27 with none and 28 with exchange alone (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: linearization lineage Padberg, McCormick, Liberti, Mallach and RLT (Adams, Sherali) (p. 3); inequalities borrowed from graph partitioning (Chopra and Rao; Grötschel and Wakabayashi) (pp. 6–7); exchange heuristic after Kernighan and Lin (p. 9); the competitor is credited: the best out-of-the-box performance came from RLT with the improvements of [11, 12] (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing**: a plain contribution list by section (p. 2); hedged explanations ("This may be explained by the fact that (RLT) contains many more variables than constraints", p. 10); deferred proofs flagged (p. 5); one summary sentence not matched by its table (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a formulation, polyhedral results, separation routines and heuristics for one application (p. 2). Limits: only 52.3% of the instances solved by any setting (34 of 65, my arithmetic), and large final gaps because LP solutions alone do not yield good primal solutions on hard instances (pp. 10–11); facet proofs deferred (p. 5). No future work beyond the promised extended version (p. 5).
