### S006 · SCIP: global optimization of mixed-integer nonlinear programs in a branch-and-cut framework (Optimization Methods and Software 2018, DOI 10.1080/10556788.2017.1335312)
**Read level**: full. Abstract and §§1-4 (file pp. 3-27) read completely, including Tables 1-5 (pp. 21-26); references (pp. 27-33) and the supplementary instance table (pp. 34-42) skimmed; Figures 1-5 (pp. 6-11) garbled except captions. Version read: ZIB-Report 16-24, revised May 2017 (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Stefan Vigerske (first author; GAMS Software GmbH, c/o ZIB, p. 3) · **Place in trajectory**: the summary of the MINLP-component line in SCIP that the skill dates to 2006-2015 [LVB13; OBBT17]. It describes the nonlinear extensions available with SCIP 3.1 (released 2014, p. 4) and sends the reader to his own papers for details: Undercover with Berthold [21, 22] (p. 18), Lagrangian variable bounds [47] and the OBBT enhancements [48] (p. 10), and the 2012 MIQCP component study [27] that inspired §3 (p. 18). Written 2016-2017, the years of VIPR and the MIPLIB 2017 call (skill's Sources).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Two aims are announced: describe the extensions that let the constraint integer programming framework SCIP solve factorable nonconvex MINLPs to ε-global optimality, and "provide insights into the impact of single MINLP solver components" by running SCIP with features deactivated (p. 3). The entry is that a solver's performance "crucially depends on its single constituents and their mutual interplay" (p. 3) and that MINLP software is "not competitive with MIP, yet" (p. 4). The predecessor is the authors' 2012 component study on MIQCPs, now repeated for general MINLPs on the much larger MINLPLib2 (p. 18).
  - Transferable: pair a system description with a one-component-off study of the same release, so that every described component gets a measured effect in the same paper (pp. 3-4, 19).
- **D2 Core idea**: Put nonlinear constraints into SCIP's existing CIP solving loop instead of writing a separate MINLP code: presolve reformulation, bound tightening, an LP relaxation updated in a cut loop, integer and then spatial branching, and heuristics all act on them (Figure 1, pp. 5-6), so MIP techniques such as cutting planes and conflict analysis also work on MINLPs (p. 5). Changed against textbook spatial branch and bound (p. 4): one expression graph for all nonlinear functions with common subexpressions shared (p. 6); reformulation into sums of convex, concave, power and quadratic functions whose envelopes are known (p. 15); Lagrangian variable bounds as a cheap in-tree substitute for OBBT (p. 10). Kept: a polyhedral outer approximation, the LP relaxation (pp. 3, 5); NLPs are solved only locally, in the sub-NLP heuristic (p. 17), and as separation points for pure NLPs (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Factorable MINLPs whose nonlinear functions are expression trees over signomials, signed powers, exp, log and absolute value (p. 6), with a linear objective (the reader moves a nonlinear objective into a constraint, p. 34). Finite bounds on variables in nonconvex terms are required for ε-global optimality (p. 4). The CIP definition requires a polyhedral remainder after fixing the integers, which MINLP violates; the authors argue that an NLP solvable to a given precision suffices in practice (p. 5). Structures exploited: second-order cones (p. 14), products of a binary with a linear term (a big-M linearization that uses stored implications, p. 15 and fn. 2), quadratic complementarity (p. 16), pairs of signed-square equations (p. 16), factorable quadratics (p. 14). Test data: MINLPLib2 revision 171 of April 4, 2014, the 789 instances available in OSiL and readable by SCIP (trigonometric functions excluded) (p. 19). Tolerances: gap 10^-4 and primal feasibility 10^-6 in the original space, a modified internal 10^-7 in the presolved space "For increased numerical stability" (p. 19); nonlinear cuts need violation 10^-4 in the initial separation rounds, 10^-6 before branching on an integer-feasible LP solution (p. 11).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - FBBT on the expression graph with rounding-safe interval arithmetic, intervals computed forward and propagated backward (Figures 2-3, pp. 7-8); a relative-tightening threshold prevents endless loops of tiny improvements (p. 7).
  - Tightest interval bounds for univariate and bivariate quadratic forms by case analysis (eqs. (3)-(9), pp. 7-9); Example 1 derives the tight upper bound (√17-1)/4 ≈ 0.780776 where the univariate relaxation improves nothing (p. 9).
  - OBBT at the root over the current LP relaxation with an objective cutoff, a limit on LP iterations and interruptions for cheaper propagation (p. 10).
  - Lagrangian variable bounds: dual multipliers of the OBBT LPs give the valid inequality (12) and the bound (13), which equals the OBBT bound at optimal duals; learned at the root, propagated on local bound changes and globally on each improved incumbent, "a computationally cheap approximation of OBBT" (p. 10; Figure 5, p. 11).
  - Convexity detection by composition rules propagated through the graph (formula (14)) plus a minimum-eigenvalue test for quadratic constraints (p. 11).
  - Underestimators: gradient cuts for convex parts, retried at a perturbed point (16); the convex envelope of concave parts from the LP (17) and its dual (18) over the box vertices; interval-gradient cuts (22) for indefinite parts, computed with CppAD on an interval base type and admitted to be possibly "very weak", which is why presolve reformulates such functions away (pp. 12-13).
  - Powers and quadratics: the envelope tangent point of signed and odd powers found by Newton's method on (24) (p. 13); a secant cut (27) for a square of an integer variable, more violated than the gradient cut (26) (p. 13); term-wise McCormick for nonconvex quadratics, which "avoids the introduction of artificial variables" (p. 14); lifted tangent inequalities taken from Couenne's implementation (p. 14).
  - Branching: fractional integers first by hybrid reliability, then spatial branching by pseudocosts with a distance measure like rb-int-br; the branching point projects the LP value onto an interior sub-interval of the domain with λ = 0.2 (bars lost in extraction), or is -b for violated power constraints (pp. 16-17).
  - Heuristics: all MIP heuristics; NLP local search with the integers fixed and the sub-NLP presolved first (p. 17); Undercover, which fixes a small set-covering selection of variables to obtain a sub-MIP (p. 18); LNS heuristics generalized to the full CIP (RINS, RENS, Crossover, Local Branching, DINS) (p. 18).
  - No proofs; validity rests on the cited envelope and LP-duality facts (pp. 10, 12).
  - Transferable: harvest the duals of bound-tightening LPs as one-row bounds and propagate them in the tree instead of re-running OBBT (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Test set: a preliminary 2-hour default run removed the instances default could not solve ("Arguably, since the alternative settings described below are typically worse than the default, most of these instances would time out also"), leaving 475 instances, listed in the supplement (pp. 19, 34-42). Default solves 455 within one hour; 12 time out (11 with a feasible solution); on 8 SCIP declared a non-optimal solution optimal or aborted on numerical difficulties, without saying how this was detected; no run hit the memory limit (p. 19). Arms: default against 14 settings, each with one feature disabled or switched (pp. 4, 19). Setup: Dell PowerEdge M610 blades, Xeon X5672 at 3.20 GHz, 48 GB, Linux 3.13; SCIP 3.1 with SoPlex 2.0.0 and Ipopt 3.11.8 (MA27); 30 GB SCIP memory limit; one hour per instance and setting (p. 19); threads and job placement not stated. Subsets: all; solved by both and ≥100 s; "diff" (node count or simplex iterations differ); diff + solved; diff + ≥100 s; diff + solved + ≥100 s (pp. 19-20). Instances on which a setting fails, e.g. with a wrong dual bound, are discarded for that setting (p. 20); the full-set sizes in Tables 1-5 run from 456 (domain propagation) to 467 (conflict analysis, inference branching) (pp. 21-26), and 467 = 475 - 8 suggests that the default's eight failures are excluded throughout (inference). Metrics: solved more : less; primal and dual bounds better : worse by at least 10% on unsolved instances; faster : slower by more than 10% and at least one second; shifted geometric means of the primal-dual integral (shift 1 s), nodes (shift 100) and time (shift 1 s) (p. 20). Significance: Wilcoxon signed-rank test from SciPy with correction=False and the "pratt" zero method, marked +, ++, +++ for p below 5%, 0.5%, 0.05%, independent of the size of the mean change (p. 20). Variability is the stated reason for the test ("looms even larger on mixed-integer nonlinear programs", p. 20), but no seeds or permutations are run (pp. 19-20). Losses are reported: MIP cuts do not pay off on 77 instances (p. 23), outer approximation on 39 (p. 24); clay0205h runs 36% faster without expression reformulation despite 440% more nodes (p. 22); LNS heuristics are "mostly a waste of computing time" on easy instances and never improve a final primal bound by more than 10% (p. 26); aggressive heuristics lose three instances (pp. 26-27). Headline counts: without expression reformulation 66 fewer solved and none more (p. 22); without outer approximation 99 fewer (p. 23; Table 3: 1 : 100, p. 24); without OBBT 24 fewer (p. 23; Table 2: 1 : 25, p. 22).
  - Transferable: a "diff" subset (instances whose node count or LP iterations changed) keeps a component's mean from being diluted by instances it never touches (p. 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits BARON, "One of the first of this kind and still actively maintained", Lindo API, Couenne and ANTIGONE without comparing against them (p. 4). Builds on Achterberg's thesis for CIP and hybrid reliability branching [1, 4] (pp. 5, 16), on Belotti et al. [11] for the branching hierarchy and the rb-int-br measure (pp. 16-17), on Berthold [18, 19] for the primal-dual integral, on Hendel's master thesis [52] for the Wilcoxon procedure in MIP comparisons and on Danna [35] for performance variability (p. 20). Its own predecessor [27] is the study it extends (p. 18). The conclusion reads the branching result as parallel to MIP experience: improving the dual bound matters more than reducing constraint violation (p. 27).
- **D7 Writing**: The introduction announces "first ... Second" aims (p. 3). Each result subsection opens by naming the setting and what it disables ("With setting quadratic reformulation, we evaluate disabling the recognition of second-order cone constraints", p. 21), then gives the share of instances affected (94% for domain propagation, p. 22; 55% for conflict analysis, p. 23), the counts, and an explaining instance (portfol*, edgecross*, clay0205h, pp. 21-22); there are no numbered questions. Hedges are local: "Arguably" (p. 19), "probably due to" (p. 21), "maybe because" (p. 25). Verdicts are phrased as default decisions: conflict analysis solves four more and none fewer, "making it a good default setting" (p. 23).
  - Transferable: explain an average by its split (the node reduction is an increase on 62 and a decrease on 74 instances, p. 21) and by one counterexample instance with its mechanism (clay0205h: a smaller LP and cheaper nodes, p. 22).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Software plus a computational study (p. 27). Limits are printed with causes: conflict analysis is undervalued because most nonlinear constraints do not take part in it (p. 23); an automatic OBBT default is "nontrivial" to design (p. 23); the interval-gradient estimator is weak in many situations (p. 22); a compromise between a static presolve big-M and a tighter local one for binary products is open, perhaps through indicator constraints (p. 21); the insights are "valid specifically for SCIP's current implementation", and similar studies for other MINLP solvers would be desirable (p. 27). The improvements after SCIP 3.1 are listed: edge-concave cuts, extended SOC recognition, better separation for convex quadratics, OBBT ordering [43] (p. 27).

- Accept nonlinear cuts at violation 10^-4 in the first separation rounds and down to 10^-6 only before branching on an integer-feasible LP solution (p. 11).

### S009 · QPLIB: a library of quadratic programming instances (Mathematical Programming Computation 2019, DOI 10.1007/s12532-018-0147-4)
**Read level**: full. Main text (§§1-4 and acknowledgments, pp. 1-22, with Tables 1-6) and Appendix B, the file format (pp. 38-42), read completely; references (pp. 22-33) and Appendix A, the per-instance Table 7 (pp. 33-38), skimmed; Figures 1-5 (pp. 19-21) garbled except captions. Version read: authors' manuscript without journal layout.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Fabio Furini and Emiliano Traversi (first two), then alphabetically Pietro Belotti, Antonio Frangioni, Gleixner, Nick Gould, Leo Liberti, Andrea Lodi, Ruth Misener, Hans Mittelmann, Nick Sahinidis, Stefan Vigerske, Angelika Wiegele (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the call for instances went out in January 2014 (p. 13), with support from COST Action TD1207 (p. 22), the Action of his 2014 visit report [STSM14]; Gleixner and Vigerske, the fifth and twelfth authors, were funded by the Research Campus MODAL (p. 22), and the website at qplib.zib.de extends MINLPLib 2 tools (p. 20). He is a co-author of MIPLIB 2010, which QPLIB cites as the MILP library (ref. [84], pp. 2, 28). The 2014 call predates the MIPLIB 2017 call of November 2016 (skill's [Call16]).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: No library covers all kinds of QP; existing ones are tied to an application or to structural properties suited to one class of algorithms (p. 2). QP comprises sub-classes "ranging from trivial to undecidable" (p. 1), so the set must be "not overwhelmingly onerous but sufficiently challenging" for several communities (p. 2). "Interesting" is defined as "challenging for a significant set of solution methods": an instance that many solvers solve quickly is out, while one that only a structure-exploiting solver solves fast stays in (p. 2). Motivation: QPs are the "least-nonlinear nonlinear problems" and a "sweet spot" between MILP and MINLP (pp. 2-3), illustrated by an application table (Table 1, pp. 3-4).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three design moves. (1) A three-field taxonomy, objective-variables-constraints ("OVC"), whose values are ordered so that a method for one code applies to every simpler code: a total order for O and C, partial orders C ⊂ M ⊂ G, B ⊂ M ⊂ G and B ⊂ I ⊂ G for V (pp. 6-7). (2) Selection relative to the solver population: two filters in cascade, the first discarding instances solved by at least 30% of the applicable complete solvers within 30 s, the second a manual removal of near-duplicates followed by a 120 s run of a complete solver for QGQ (pp. 15-16). (3) No reformulation: instances stay in the contributor's "natural" form, because reformulations make an instance easier for one class of solvers (p. 8). Around these: a new stand-alone .qplib format and a metadata website (pp. 20-21, 38-42).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Mixed-integer quadratically constrained QPs with two-sided quadratic constraints, variable bounds and integrality (p. 5). Convexity is classified by the sufficient eigenvalue condition with thresholds ±10^-12 (p. 15), so conic constraints written as quadratics count as nonconvex (**Q), on the ground that not all solvers detect them (pp. 8, 15). Hardness facts frame the classes: LIQ is undecidable (Jeroslow), QCQ is NP-hard even for QCB with a single negative eigenvalue (pp. 7, 9). Method classes after Neumaier: incomplete, asymptotically complete, complete, rigorous; none of the solvers used is rigorous, and "exact" is used for complete (pp. 10-13). Sources: a public call (January 2014, open ten months) and seven libraries (BARON, CUTEst, GAMS Performance, MacMINLP, Maros-Mészáros, MINLPLib, POLIP), more than eight thousand instances in all, translated to GAMS (pp. 13-14); no licence or redistribution terms are stated.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (library, taxonomy and format; no solver algorithm):
  - Reformulation facts used to draw the class lines: x_j ∈ {0,1} ⇔ x_j^2 = x_j; a linear objective without loss of generality once quadratic constraints are allowed; a PSD Hessian factored by Cholesky becomes diagonal at the price of extra linear constraints, hence D*L is kept apart from C*B (pp. 6-8).
  - "Exact" qualified by the computational model: a complete method is exact on a Real RAM, not on a Turing machine, so calling a floating-point solver exact is "stretching the meaning of the word"; the paper then loosens the distinction on purpose (pp. 12-13).
  - A solver-by-class capability table (complete, asymptotically complete, incomplete) chooses the solvers for each instance category (Table 2, p. 11).
  - File format: free format with comment lines, one-based indices, a value for infinity, a default value plus a list of non-default entries for every vector, binary bounds overriding stated bounds, sections omitted by problem type; worked example MIPBAND (pp. 38-42).
  - Website: per-instance metadata, sparsity plots of the Jacobian and of the Lagrangian Hessian, feasible solution points "for most instances", a searchable table and a csv of features from which users can compile their own subsets (pp. 20-21).
  - Transferable: give every library instance a short class code whose fields are ordered by containment, so a reader sees at once which methods apply (pp. 6-7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Runs serve only the selection: the complete GAMS solvers applicable to each category (Table 2, p. 11; p. 15), time limits of 30 s and 120 s (pp. 15-16). Counts per stage, approximate: ≈8500 gathered, ≈6000 discrete and ≈2500 continuous, ≈3000 and ≈1000 after the first filter, 319 and 134 after the second (Table 3, p. 16); the 453 instances fall into 32 continuous convex, 102 continuous nonconvex, 31 discrete convex and 288 discrete nonconvex (Table 4, p. 16). Solver versions, hardware and thread counts are not stated, nor which complete solver made the 120 s run (pp. 15-16). There is no solver comparison by design (pp. 2, 21); performance is left to Mittelmann's benchmark pages (pp. 4-5). No check of the provided feasible points is described (p. 21). Diversity is shown by size, discrete share, quadratic-constraint share, "problematic" eigenvalues (about 25% of the quadratic objectives convex, most others near 50%) and Hessian density (about 30% below 5%, about 30% above 50%) (pp. 17-20); the largest instances, QPLIB 8547 and QPLIB 9008, have about 10^6 variables and constraints (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: MILP has its library (MIPLIB 2010, ref. [84]) while QP has none spanning all classes (p. 2); specialized libraries are absorbed as sources (p. 14); performance is delegated to plato.asu.edu (pp. 4-5); the method taxonomy is Neumaier's [110] (p. 10); the website reuses MINLPLib 2 tools [139] (p. 20). The authors offer their process as "useful guidelines for other researchers interested in constructing benchmarks" (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: The non-purpose is stated at the start and repeated at the end (pp. 2, 21). Each "nontrivial" choice is announced and justified where it is made (no reformulation, p. 8; sufficient-condition convexity, pp. 8, 15). Wording is guarded where it carries weight ("exact", pp. 12-13), while the stage counts are approximations (≈, pp. 14, 16).
  - Transferable: say what a benchmark is not for in the introduction and repeat it in the conclusion (pp. 2, 21).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Infrastructure: the first comprehensive QP library, a taxonomy, a format and a website (p. 21). Limits: the collection "can only be a snapshot" of past interests (p. 21); convex conic instances may be classed as nonconvex (pp. 8, 15); the second filter rests on manual judgment (p. 16). Future: literature references on the website and dynamic extension with new instances (p. 21).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 evidence ✅: purpose and non-purpose first (pp. 2, 21; step 1); a broad public collection (pp. 13-14; step 2, though without a licence statement); admission by written hardness rules with counts per stage, tilted to "challenging" instances (pp. 15-16, Table 3; step 4); the manual step and the no-reformulation choice printed as decisions (pp. 8, 16; step 6); per-instance metadata for user-built subsets (p. 21).
- Method 6 variant ⚠: two steps of the MIPLIB 2017 instrument are missing: no exact or other check of contributed solutions or solver answers is described (step 3; p. 21 says only that feasible points are provided), and the similarity filter is a one-by-one manual judgment, not an optimization model over feature clusterings (step 5; p. 16); the counts are approximate (pp. 14, 16). With its 2014 call (p. 13), QPLIB reads as a hand-made predecessor of the MIPLIB 2017 instrument (inference).
- Method 4 variant ⚠: the skill's labelling discipline (no "exact" or "verified" beyond what is covered) appears here as terminology: complete against rigorous methods, no rigorous solver used, "exact" qualified by the computational model (pp. 10-13), then deliberately loosened (p. 13); no checker is involved.
- New-pattern candidate: keep library instances in the contributor's form, without reformulation, so the library does not favour one solver class (p. 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Admission cascade relative to the solver population: drop instances solved by at least 30% of the applicable complete solvers within 30 s; keep a few representatives per donor and size, preferring the larger and harder ones; drop what a complete solver proves optimal within 120 s (pp. 15-16).
- Classify convexity by a stated sufficient condition with a numeric eigenvalue threshold (10^-12) and say which convex cases it misses (pp. 8, 15).
- Publish the instance features as a csv so users build their own subsets instead of relying on one fixed test set (p. 21).
- Write the instance format as default values plus lists of non-default entries for every vector, so sparse data stays small and two-sided constraints need no repetition (pp. 20, 38-40).
