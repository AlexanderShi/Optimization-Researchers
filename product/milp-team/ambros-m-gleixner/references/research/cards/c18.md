### S082 · Sorting criteria for line-based periodic timetabling heuristics (Operations Research Proceedings 2024, Lecture Notes in Operations Research, pp. 348–354, 2025, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-92575-7_50)
**Read level**: full. Text read: ZIB-Report 24-07 (October 2024), all of file pp. 1–8 (cover and imprint pp. 1–2; the paper pp. 3–8 with Algorithm 1, Figs. 1–2 as labels only, Table 1 and the references).

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Patricia Ebert (first author; ZIB and HTW Berlin), Berenike Masing and Niels Lindner (ZIB); Gleixner last of four (ZIB and HTW) (p. 3); no contribution statement (p. 7) · **Place in the trajectory**: an applied project of the HTW years (periodic timetabling at ZIB), outside MILP solver engineering; a short proceedings paper (six pages in the report version, pp. 3–8) that varies one design choice of a ZIB colleague's heuristic (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: PESP, the standard model for periodic timetables, is NP-hard, and none of the PESPlib instances (published 2012) has been solved to proven optimality (p. 3). Lindner and Liebchen's incremental heuristic improved PESPlib primal bounds, its line-based variant beating the station-based one (pp. 3–4), but there "the order in which the lines are processed has been fixed" (p. 4). Entry: vary that one fixed choice and keep everything else, including the computational setup of [3] (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: sort lines by an importance measure, in descending order: number (n), weight (w), weighted span (ws) or lower bound (l) of arcs, counted over driving (-d), driving and dwelling (-dd), outgoing (-o) or transfer (-t) arcs, nine measures in all (pp. 5–6); combine each with no connectivity rule, min-connectivity or max-connectivity, 27 sortings (abstract; pp. 4, 6). Heavy lines go first because only the early, small subinstances can be solved to optimality (p. 5); weights stand for passenger numbers, and transfer arcs carry the large spans, i.e. the optimization potential (p. 6). Unchanged: Algorithm 1 (spanning forest, extend, fix_opt, full_opt per added line) is "an incarnation of the algorithm in [3]", run inside ConcurrentPESP (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: a PESP instance (N, T, l, u, w) with periodic span constraints, minimizing the weighted periodic tension; an activity is free if u − l ≥ T − 1 (p. 4). The construction's feasibility rests on free stratifications and the extension theorem [3, Thm. 1] (p. 4). The structure exploited comes from TimPassLib: a line map under which every transfer activity is free and each line's own activities form two disjoint paths (p. 5). Test data: the 16 PESPlib railway instances (p. 6). With no distances, speeds or station positions available, the sum of lower bounds (l-dd) stands in for line length (p. 6). MILP is only the background formulation (p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: each iteration extends the previous timetable along a spanning forest whose non-forest arcs are old or free, then optimizes with the old part fixed (fix_opt) and released (full_opt) (p. 5). Min-/max-connectivity: start from the most important line; then, among the remaining lines with the fewest (most) transfer connections to the current subinstance, add the one with the highest measure (p. 6). Limits: 60 s wall time per full_opt call, none on fix_opt (pp. 6–7). No new proof: every extension is feasible by Theorem 1 of [3] (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: one machine (Intel Xeon E3-1270, 32 GB) (p. 6). Metrics: per instance, the gap of each sorting's final primal value to the best sorting (Fig. 1, p. 7); a ranking by accumulated gap over the 16 instances and an average per instance group (Fig. 2, p. 8); new incumbents against the PESPlib best known (Table 1, p. 8). Findings: the min-runs are never best; there is no consistent winner; the best sorting always involves weights; l-dd and n-d are "significantly worse", with no test behind the adverb (p. 7). Five new incumbents (R2L2, R2L4, R3L3, R3L4, R4L3), 0.06% to 1.89% (Table 1, p. 8); by my arithmetic the percentages are relative to the new value (R3L3: 750,031 / 39,674,349 = 1.89%). Not reported: which of the 27 arms reproduces the order of [3], repeated runs or seeds, total running times (pp. 6–8).
- **D6 Positioning**: a direct continuation of Lindner and Liebchen [3] (same algorithm, setup and solver [1]) (pp. 3–4); the weighted span is credited to [3] (p. 6); the dual-bound work [4] (Masing, Lindner, Ebert) is named as the other line of attack (p. 3). The predecessor is not criticised: the gap is a choice that was never varied (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing**: the abstract's result is a count ("five new incumbent solutions, resulting in improvements of up to 2%", abstract); conclusions are read off figures ("This plot suggests", p. 7). Slips: "(n-dt)" is not one of the defined measures (p. 7, against pp. 5–6), and "the best performing sorting always involves the arc weights" is followed two sentences later by an exception for R4L1 (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an empirical comparison of construction orders and five new PESPlib incumbents (pp. 7–8). No limitation or future-work paragraph (pp. 7–8); the recommendation of weighted transfer-arc sortings rests on 16 instances with one reported run per sorting (pp. 6–7).

**Method links**:

- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the 27 arms are compared with each other and with the library's incumbents (pp. 7–8); the predecessor's own order is not identified as an arm, so the gain from re-sorting over [3] is not isolated (pp. 4, 6–8).

### S087 · Branch and price for nonlinear production-maintenance scheduling in complex machinery (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2511.07143)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v1 (10 Nov 2025), pp. 1–31 (main text pp. 1–30, Appendix A with Example 7 and Fig. 8 pp. 30–31, funding statement p. 31); bibliography (pp. 32–35) scanned for positioning.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: João Dionísio (first and corresponding author; Universidade do Porto, CMUP and ZIB; funded by an FCT scholarship, 2023.00422.BD, p. 31), João Pedro Pedroso (Porto), Ksenia Bestuzheva (ZIB); Gleixner second of four (ZIB and HTW) (p. 1); no contribution statement · **Place in the trajectory**: decomposition with a nonlinear pricing problem, continuing two branch-and-price works in his co-authored record that the paper cites: price-and-verify for recursive circle packing with Pedroso (Gleixner et al. 2018, p. 10; reference p. 33) and the length-constrained cycle partition (Ghannam, Mexi, Lam, Gleixner 2024, p. 25; reference p. 33).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: industrial maintenance costs (54 billion USD in 2024, projected 73 billion by 2029) and old, costly critical equipment (p. 1); production drives degradation, so production and maintenance should be planned together (p. 2); Example 1 shows a separated plan becoming infeasible (pp. 2–3). Practice uses time- and condition-based heuristics, shown inefficient for a single machine's profit against a MIP in Dionísio's 2021 master's thesis (p. 3; reference p. 33); just-in-time maintenance is called possibly "arbitrarily bad" (p. 3); machine-learning approaches need historical data, while MIP gives feasibility and optimality guarantees and a distance to the optimum (p. 4). The literature is mostly linear, treats machines as inseparable units or models failure stochastically (p. 4). Claimed novelty: machines as structures of interacting, separately maintainable components, with nonlinear degradation (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: the compact model is block diagonal over machines with the demand constraints linking them (p. 8), so a Dantzig–Wolfe reformulation delegates the nonlinearities to machine-wise MINLP pricing problems (pp. 10, 12–13); identical machines are aggregated to remove symmetry (pp. 13–14); branching is on sets of bounds on original variables after Vanderbeck (2000) and Desrosiers et al. (2024, p. 470) (pp. 14–15), with the branching duals carried into pricing by indicator variables (pp. 18–19). Changed against the Vanderbeck–Savelsbergh discretization: fractional master solutions are allowed "as long as we can translate them into an integer solution in the original variable space" by a post-solve repair (pp. 10, 20–22). Unchanged: the generic branch-and-price toolkit (Farkas pricing, Lagrangian and Farley bounds, early branching, Price-and-Branch) (pp. 23–26).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: an MINLP with continuous production y and condition r, binary maintenance x; demand [1.1], a production cap g(r) with g increasing [1.2], maintenance durations [1.3], implications [1.4], downtime [1.5] and degradation f with a big-M reset [1.6]; all components start in optimal condition (pp. 5–7); the big-M is given for concave f (p. 7); a valid inequality [1.9] (p. 7) and a demand cap in pricing [4.3] (p. 12). The repair step relies on "the convexity assumptions made in this work" (p. 20) and on the compact formulation being convex (p. 21); λ* is assumed unique (p. 14) and a single aggregated subproblem is assumed for exposition (p. 15). Test data: generated instances in which f and g are randomly linear, polynomial (at most cubic) or exponential with coefficients uniform in [0, 3] (p. 26); whether these meet the convexity that the repair needs is not stated (pp. 20–21, 26).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: branching refines a set β of variable bounds until the master mass vβ on the matching columns is fractional, using a priority queue over subsets (p. 15); for patterns without continuous variables this terminates after at most log(|F|) refinements [Vanderbeck 2000, Prop. 3] (p. 15); the direction keeps the larger set, for a more balanced tree, with ties broken by the sum of squared master values (pp. 23–24). Pricing sees each branching decision through an indicator δ_b, linearized by [5.6]–[5.7] or [5.8] according to the sign of its dual (pp. 18–19). Repair device: where no hyperplane on integer variables separates fractional columns (Example 5, p. 20), columns with the same maintenance pattern are merged into their convex combination normalized by λ1* + λ2*; argued on Example 6 with the claim that the explanation generalizes (pp. 20–21); integrality is replaced by integrality per pattern class [5.9] (pp. 21–22); with several subproblems it holds only if λ-branching applies in none (p. 21). Acceleration: Farkas pricing, or a feasibility-emphasis run of the compact model where there are many subproblems with few identical machines (p. 23); subproblem order from a just-in-time heuristic, and pricing first under gap and solution limits (p. 23); a Lagrangian bound valid for any valid pricing bounds [Lübbecke 2011, Prop. 2.1], early termination of the last pricing round and a valid cut [5.11] (p. 24); the Farley bound, valid for any dual vector and so also under dual smoothing (pp. 24–25); rounding bounds up to multiples of a common objective factor (p. 25); early branching that loses nothing when ⌈c*_RMP⌉ ≤ lb, taken from Ghannam et al. 2024 (p. 25); Price-and-Branch under time, solution and objective limits (p. 26). Implementation: SCIP through PySCIPOpt, everything else in Python (pp. 22, 27, 29). Nonlinear branching thresholds are proposed and "will not be explored in this work" (p. 19).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: 400 generated instances (10 or 20 periods × 20 identical or 2 × 10 identical machines × high or low complexity × 50) (pp. 26–27); two machine families because "it is easy to create instances that favor each of the formulations" (pp. 26–27). Exclusions are counted per rule: 72 solved by both in under 5 s, 107 with no incumbent from either, leaving 221 (118 feasible, 103 infeasible); the infeasible ones are kept as the expected use (an expert tweaking parameters such as demand) (p. 27). Arms: Compact (SCIP on the compact model) against DW with exact pricing (p. 27). Metrics: arithmetic means of time and nodes; gap = (primal − dual)/max(1, primal), averaged over feasible instances; easy, medium and hard subsets (p. 27). Setup: a 6-core Intel Core i7, 64 GB, 3.20 GHz; 300 s; SCIP 9.2.1, SoPlex 7.1.3, IPOPT 3.14.17, PySCIPOpt 5.6.0, Python 3.13.3 (p. 27); thread count and seeds not stated. Table 7 (p. 28): easy 57 (9 feasible), Compact solves 23 (9) in 189.1 s mean against DW's 57 (9) in 6.1 s; medium 73 (18), Compact 12 (12) against DW 62 (7); hard 91, gap 126.8% against 11.3%, nodes about 6.4k against 47. Compact proves optimality more often (21 against 16) (p. 28). Time split: exact pricing 72.82%, branching 11.35%, Python 7.66%, Price-and-Branch 6.96%, subproblem reordering 1.18%, RMP reoptimization 0.03% (pp. 28–29). A failure mode is printed: on some instances DW makes little progress after the root, creating many nodes with the same local dual bound (p. 28). Code and results are on GitHub (p. 26, fn. 5). By my arithmetic, under the stated definitions Compact solved all 9 feasible easy instances, so its easy mean gap should be 0, yet the table prints 5.6; for a non-negative cost the stated gap formula cannot exceed 100% when the dual bound is non-negative (my inference), yet the hard mean is 126.8%; how runs without a primal solution enter the mean is not stated (pp. 27–28). Not reported: results per machine family, although the families were built because the generator favors one arm (pp. 26–28); a primal metric behind "able to find good solutions much more quickly" (p. 28); an ablation of the acceleration techniques (pp. 22–29).
- **D6 Positioning**: production-maintenance scheduling after the review of Geurtsen et al. (2023); linear variants, stochastic failure models with Markov chains or Benders, Pries's thesis (Benders and Dantzig–Wolfe for identical single-structure machines), the heuristic decoupling of Abiri-Jahromi et al. (pp. 3–4). Decomposition: Dantzig–Wolfe (1960), Vanderbeck–Savelsbergh (2006), Degraeve–Jans (2007) (p. 10); nonlinear pricing in Aganagic–Mokhtari (1997), Allman–Zhang (2021) and Gleixner et al. (2018) (p. 10); Vanderbeck (2000) and Desrosiers et al. (2024) (pp. 14–15); Lübbecke (2011), Farley (1990), Wentges (1997), Gamrath (2010), Ghannam et al. (2024) for the accelerations (pp. 23–25). Novelty claims: "To the best of our knowledge" for the component model (p. 4), and variable-bound branching "has always been achieved by finding hyperplanes" in the works seen (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing**: seven worked examples (pp. 2–3, 8–9, 16–21, 30), a figure that "sacrifices some rigor in favor of visual clarity" (Fig. 3, p. 16), a central step stated as a claim and argued on an example (p. 20), hedges such as "we feel like" (p. 29); the template running head "Title Suppressed Due to Excessive Length" is left in (pp. 3, 5, 7, …). The conclusion prints the counter-result next to the recommendation ("except for proving optimality", p. 29). Slips: the "respectively" in the complexity definition pairs the higher number of components with 1–3 and the lower with 3–7 (p. 26); the introduction's "arbitrarily bad" (p. 3) is stronger than what Appendix A's Example 7 shows, namely that just-in-time maintenance "does not always produce the optimal solution" (p. 30).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a model (the MINLP), an algorithm (Dantzig–Wolfe with original-variable-bound branching and the repair step), code and a comparison (pp. 4, 26, 29). Limits printed with numbers: Compact proves optimality more often (21 against 16); exact pricing takes 72.82%, so a pricing heuristic is "a priority"; branching (11.35%) and Python (7.66%) call for a faster language (pp. 28–29). ### H002 · Boolean Algebra–Driven Sepsis Diagnosis (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2605.29686)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v1 (28 May 2026; pages dated May 29, 2026), pp. 1–14 including the limitations, the acknowledgments with the contribution statement, the ethics statement and the supporting-material note (pp. 13–14); references (pp. 14–16) scanned.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Marcus Weber (ZIB) and Konstantin Fackeldey (ZIB and TU Berlin), corresponding authors; Kai Kappert and Wolfgang Bauer (Charité); Marco Reidelbach (ZIB); Gleixner fourth of six, with an HTW Berlin affiliation (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: outside MILP and solver work (arXiv class math.RA, p. 1): an interdisciplinary ZIB–Charité project of 2026 in which, by the contribution statement, Weber and Fackeldey built the method, Kappert and Bauer supplied data and clinical expertise, Reidelbach did data management and the workflow description, and "AG discussed limitations, the role of exceptions, and possible extensions of the method regarding thresholds" (p. 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: sepsis has an incidence of 677.5 per 100,000 person-years, about 48.9 million cases and 11 million deaths in 2017 (p. 1), and no diagnostic test reliably identifies it (p. 2). Question: can logical statements derived from serum protein concentrations support the diagnosis (p. 2)? The AI and probabilistic models cited all concern patients already in intensive care (p. 2). Entry: carry the logical data analysis used for the archaeology of Tell el-Amarna [18] over to a clinical dataset [19], and with it the humanities' use of context knowledge that is not in the data (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: a true logical statement is a selection criterion that selects no observed patient; these criteria form an ideal I in the Boolean polynomial ring, generated by the complement of the sum of the observed patterns' selectors (pp. 4–6). A Gröbner basis of I (Buchberger) yields the least restrictive generators, since restricting a rule multiplies it and the basis meets the divisibility requirement on leading monomials (p. 7). Rules are read off polynomials of the form S1·s + S2 as positive (sepsis) or negative (non-sepsis) rules (p. 7). Experts mark relevant polynomials, which generate a subideal J; remainders modulo J simplify the rest (inner loop) (pp. 7–8); declaring a rare observation an exception turns it into a non-observation and enlarges I (outer loop) (p. 8). Algorithm: Steps 1–4 (p. 9), plus a generalization step, "Step 5" (p. 10).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: 390 emergency-department measurements (137 sepsis, 253 non-sepsis by a retrospective definition), recruited February 2019 to August 2022 (p. 3); nine proteins pre-selected by expert knowledge (p. 3); each threshold is set so that exactly 137 cases count as "high" (p. 3; Table 1, p. 4); ten Boolean variables with 194 of 1,024 possible patterns observed, so 830 are unobserved and 2^830 empty criteria exist (p. 5). The MILP form: n/a. A stated prerequisite: the thresholds must be meaningful (p. 13).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: proof devices: the two closure properties, restriction (multiplication) and disjunction (sum), make the empty criteria an ideal (p. 6); the fact that finitely generated ideals of a Boolean ring are principal is cited as a textbook exercise [25] (p. 6); remainders modulo J stay in I (p. 8). Selection rules, re-chosen per cycle: cycle 1 keeps rules covering more than 20 patients with fewer than 5 variables, cycle 2 more than 30 patients with 4 variables, cycle 3 more than 40 with 5 (pp. 9–10); an exception is a pattern whose remainder has 2 monomials and fewer than 4 variables plus s (p. 10), and patient No. 2237 is declared one on a biological argument (p. 10). Implementation: a Julia/OSCAR notebook (p. 14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: three outer cycles, three exceptions (Nos. 2237, 127, 545) (pp. 9–10; Table 3, p. 10); final rules (Table 4, p. 11), e.g. a negative rule covering 45 patients with one sepsis case and a positive rule covering 22, all septic; generalized rules (Table 5, p. 11): the negative rule T(y + 1) (high TRAIL, low MyD88) selects 108 with 6 septic, the positive rule Gy (high GLP-1 and MyD88) 67 with 62 septic (p. 10). Evaluation design: the rules are counted on the same 390 measurements they were derived from; there is no held-out data, cross-validation or comparison with the classifiers of [19] on the same data, and agreement with [19] is argued qualitatively (pp. 10–13). By my arithmetic, MyD88 alone errs 39 times each way (137 − 98 = 39, Table 5) because every threshold fixes 137 "high" values (pp. 3, 11–12). Code and data are available on request only (p. 14). Baselines, seeds and timings: n/a.
- **D6 Positioning**: machine-learning, probabilistic and real-time sepsis models [5–9] (p. 2); Boolean logic in medicine and biology [10–14] (p. 2); data-internal statistical reasoning is contrasted with the humanities' external context knowledge (p. 2); the method comes from the authors' humanities projects [18, 23, 24] (pp. 3, 6); the clinical findings of [19] serve as a consistency check (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing**: rules stated in words with patient counts (pp. 5–6, 9–10); two set-off maxims, "Not every true logical statement about sepsis is an insight." and "Not every rare observation is an exception." (p. 8); a digression on Cicero's "exceptio probat regulam" (p. 8); hedged clinical readings ("may suggest", abstract and p. 12). The limitations take three short paragraphs (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a method (algebraic rule extraction with an expert filter) and candidate biomarker rules (pp. 11–13). Limits: the thresholds are a prerequisite and "This approach only works if this choice is meaningful"; other experts could select other rules and exceptions; simple rules may appear only in the course of the algorithm (p. 13). Future work: optimize the thresholds (p. 13); the contribution statement credits Gleixner with discussing extensions regarding thresholds (p. 13).

### S065 · MIP-DD: Delta debugging for mixed-integer programming solvers (INFORMS Journal on Computing 38(2), 414–423, 2026, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2024.0844; arXiv 2405.19770)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v5 (16 Apr 2025), pp. 1–14 (including Table 1, the five case studies and the acknowledgments); references (pp. 15–17) scanned.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Alexander Hoen (first author; ZIB), Dominik Kamp (University of Bayreuth); Gleixner last of three (ZIB and HTW) (p. 1); no contribution statement; the acknowledgments name Leon Eifler for the interface to exact SCIP and SoPlex, Mark Turner and Felipe Serrano for proofreading, and Armin Biere for "his experience and perspective on delta debugging in the SAT solver world" (p. 13) · **Place in the trajectory**: debugging infrastructure for SCIP, with code at github.com/INFORMSJoC/2024.0844 [7] (p. 15); the outlook ties it to exact SCIP and VIPR (p. 13). SKILL.md lists it under Latest as "MIP-DD in print".

- **D1 Problem and entry**: solver code grows with performance: SCIP went from 703,965 lines (3.1) to 1,017,718 (9.1), +44.6% (p. 1); bugs cause false infeasibility or unboundedness and infeasible or suboptimal "optimal solutions" (p. 1). The usual tools have three limits: assertions and debug-solution checks are too costly to run as often as needed; numerical bugs can pass tolerance-based assertions; and strong dual reductions and symmetry handling may legitimately cut off optimal solutions, so a failing debug-solution check is no proof of a bug (p. 2). Bug reports come with instances too large and slow to trace (p. 2). Entry: import delta debugging (Zeller 1999), established in SAT and SMT solving, described as "the first application of delta debugging to mixed-integer programming and mathematical optimization in general" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: a greedy hypothesis–trial–result loop: a modification (or a batch of them) is kept if the solver still fails and reverted otherwise (p. 3). Nine modifiers in a fixed priority order, substantial reductions first: delete a constraint, fix a variable to the reference value, delete a coefficient of a fixed variable, remove a fixed variable, set a parameter to its target value, turn an inequality into an equation at the reference activity, zero an objective coefficient, round a variable's bounds and objective, round a constraint's coefficients (p. 3). Invariant: "each modification must preserve the feasibility of a provided reference solution", optimality not required; the rounding modifiers relax bounds and sides to keep it (p. 4). Consecutive batches bounded by `nbatches` (pp. 4–5) and a stage-round controller modelled on presolving (Algorithm 1, p. 6); each round writes its settings-problem pair to a file as a test instance (p. 6). The procedure resembles Chinneck's IIS deletion filter, and MIP-DD 2.1 adds an IIS option (p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: any MIP plus a settings file and a reference solution (pp. 3–4). Tolerance model: a zero tolerance ε and a feasibility tolerance δ, with SCIP's relative violation measure (p. 8). The reference should be as close to exactly feasible as possible: a toy example shows an infeasible reference turning a correct answer into a false dual fail (p. 7), so it is to be polished or computed by exact SCIP (p. 8). Instances: user reports, a backlog case over three years old (p. 9), and MIPLIB's rococoC10-001000 (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: solver-independent checks in the abstract interface: the dual bound cuts off the reference objective (suboptimality), a returned solution violates the problem (infeasibility), a primal ray is verified, the primal bound beats the evaluated optimum, or an unexpected error occurs (p. 5); return codes 0 (no bug), negative (solver errors), positive (MIP-DD checks) (p. 5); the checks stay valid under strong dual reductions and symmetry handling (p. 6). No proofs; the reference-solution invariant (p. 4) is what makes the dual-bound check sound. Usage rules: fewer batches when a reproduction is slow, time or node limits on both settings, `passcodes` to suppress known failure types, an exact reference (pp. 6–8). Bugs isolated, each reproducible at a stated git hash: an index shift in Dual-Infer's residual found by reducing the settings (p. 9, fix cf362110); the intobj separator applied to interior points from closecuts (p. 10); PaPILO probing that floors a tiny quotient into infeasibility, fixed by ceiling against δ and checking (p. 11, fix 019915e0); a normalization that pushes a right-hand side below δ, fixed by skipping it for vanishing sides before coefficient tightening (pp. 11–12, fix 6b2dd64c); the counting mode on a fully presolved problem (p. 12, fix 434945be).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: no benchmark: the time savings are "difficult to quantify directly" (p. 8). Instead, (a) a count from the release record: preliminary versions reduced the reproducing instances of "24 among all the 51 MIP-related and finally fixed bugs listed in the release reports" of SCIP 8.0.1–8.1.1, with statistics in the repository (p. 8; abstract); (b) five case studies (Table 1, p. 9): 3.1 from 6/6/36 (variables/constraints/nonzeros) to 5/3/15 in 0.2 s; 3.2 from 3117/1293/11751 to 7/4/11 in 2869 s with 69,430 solves; 3.3 and 3.4 from 653/745/1819 to 7/2/8 and 4/4/11 in about 4 s; 3.5 from 204/104/940 to the empty problem in 3.4 s. Setup: MIP-DD 1.0 (hash 455b7913) on a MacBook Air M2 with 16 GB (p. 8). A worse setting is printed: with default target settings, case 3.2 stopped at 1528 variables and 301 constraints (p. 10). Reduced instances join SCIP's regular tests (check/instances/Issue) (p. 8). Variability of MIP-DD itself is not measured; seeds appear only as a proposed extension (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: delta debugging and fuzzing in SAT, SMT, AIG and MaxSAT (Brummayer and Biere; Niemetz and Biere's ddSMT; Kaufmann and Biere; Paxian and Biere) (p. 2); IIS computation (Chinneck and others) (p. 4); the presolve workflow (Achterberg et al. 2019; PaPILO) for the controller (p. 6); exact SCIP [21–23] for reference solutions (p. 8) and VIPR [33] in the outlook (p. 13). Assertions and debug solutions are restated with their limits, not criticised (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: both "first" claims carry "to the best of our knowledge" (abstract; p. 2); the headline is a count ("24 out of all 51 documented MIP-related bugfixes", abstract); each case study gives the setup, the reduced instance in full, the mechanism and the fixing commit (pp. 9–12); the unmeasured benefit is said plainly (p. 8), and an observation is explained with a hedge ("An explanation for this behaviour might be given", pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a tool (open source, solver-independent), a process (reduced instances as regression tests) and an imported method (pp. 2, 8, 13). Limits: benefit not quantified (p. 8); batching can hide valid modifications (p. 6); default target settings give larger instances (p. 10). **Transferable techniques**:

### S076 · CHAP: A Hybrid GPU-CPU Heuristic for MIP (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv 2605.05086)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v1 (6 May 2026), pp. 1–12 (main text pp. 1–11; Appendix A, Table 3 with per-instance results, p. 12); references (pp. 13–16) scanned.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Gennesaret Kharistio Tjusila (first author), Alexander Hoen, Nils-Christian Kempke, Gioni Mexi, Timo Berthold (also FICO), Thorsten Koch, Sebastian Pokutta; Gleixner sixth of eight (ZIB and HTW) (p. 1); no contribution statement · **Place in the trajectory**: the GPU line, a framework "developed for" the 2026 Land–Doig MIP Competition on GPU-accelerated primal heuristics (p. 2). The text reports the authors' own runs on the 50 competition instances and no competition ranking or result (pp. 1–11).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: GPU first-order LP methods now carry substantial parts of the optimization pipeline, and compute nodes are heterogeneous, so heuristic design also means deciding "which work should be carried out where" and how to exchange information without synchronization overhead (pp. 1–2). Entry: "not merely to port an existing MIP heuristic to the GPU, but to design cross-platform heuristic frameworks" in which the two sides reinforce each other, made timely by recent GPU heuristics and the competition (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: a portfolio of CPU and GPU workers coordinated through a shared pool of LP solutions, feasible incumbents and infeasible ("partial") candidates (pp. 2–4, 8). The GPU tabu search uses LocalMIP's scoring but a single move type, the best shift, computed exactly for every variable by sort, scan and reduce (pp. 4–6), where Feasibility Jump recomputes only the last moved variable, LocalMIP samples constraints and cuOpt evaluates violated constraints plus a sample (p. 5). cuPDLPx iterates are streamed to the pool at checkpoints instead of waiting for convergence, following Rothberg's concurrent-crossover idea (p. 8); FeasPump and fix-propagate-repair repair the tabu search's infeasible candidates (p. 9); every heuristic imposes a cutoff from the best known objective (p. 8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{cᵀx : Ax ≤ b, l ≤ x ≤ u, x_i ∈ Z for i ∈ I} (p. 4), with all constraints normalized to the ≤ form in preprocessing (p. 7); binaries are the majority of variables in the competition's public test set (p. 6). The correctness of the exact best shift rests on the move score being a step function with steps only at the constraint breakpoints, the bounds and the incumbent value, "Following a similar argument to [33]" (p. 5). Test set: the 50 instances provided by the organizers, "presumably chosen" to be hard for current CPU and GPU solvers (p. 9), wording suggesting the authors did not set the selection (my inference).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Algorithm 1 (pp. 5–6): breakpoint triplets with two accumulators, β (the score as x̂_j → −∞) and α (the offset for the asymmetric reward at the incumbent); two entries per ≤-type breakpoint, so that the score at t_ij is read before the penalty; a lexicographic sort, an inclusive scan and an argmax by parallel reduction. Engineering, each with its reason: store residuals (one global read per nonzero instead of two), normalize constraints against warp divergence, row-wise (CSR) rather than column-wise evaluation, a bitset incumbent pinned to L2 (about 50 MB on the H100) (pp. 6–7); bitonic sorting networks on short columns packed into one warp, dispatch by vector length, CUDA Graphs against kernel-submission overhead (pp. 7–8); independent parallel tabu instances (p. 8); locking only for writing solutions (p. 8). LP checkpoints after 10^2, 10^3, 10^4 and 10^5 PDHG iterations, each warm-started from the last (p. 8). Xpress solves the final LP once FPR has fixed the integers (p. 9). The layout and packing choices are backed by "Our experiments confirm" and "preliminary testing", with no numbers shown (pp. 7–8).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: 50 competition instances, 5 minutes; an Intel Xeon Platinum 8481C and an NVIDIA H100; Xpress 9.8.0, Gurobi 12.0.1, cuOpt 25.10 (p. 9). Ablation ladder (Table 1, p. 10): pure CPU 47 found, 3 wins, gap 19.57%, PI 72.59; with the GPU LP 46, 13, 12.17%, 51.01; with the GPU tabu search as well 47, 22, 8.84%, 41.84; wins counted over the 47 instances solved by some configuration, 9 ties; the gap as a shifted geometric mean with shift 1.0, the primal integral after Berthold (2013), both against the best Gurobi or Xpress solution in 8 hours (pp. 9–10). Solver comparison (Table 2, p. 10): CHAP 47 found, 11 wins, 1 only-solver instance; Gurobi in default mode 44, 25, 1; cuOpt heuristics-only 43, 6, 0; 6 ties, 2 instances unsolved by all; head to head with cuOpt 24 to 19, 5 ties (pp. 10–11). Not reported: thread counts, Gurobi's hardware, seeds or repeated runs, although the one-instance difference in the ladder "generally proved unstable" (p. 10). Text and tables disagree: the text compares with "the virtual best commercial solver (45)" and credits "the virtual best" with 25 wins, while Table 2 and the abstract list Gurobi with 44 (abstract; p. 10); the caption of Table 3 calls PI "the primal gap", measured against the best of the VBS or CHAP, while Table 1 defines PI as the primal integral against the 8-hour best (pp. 10, 12).
