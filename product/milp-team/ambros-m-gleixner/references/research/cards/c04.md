### S013 · Iterative Refinement for Linear Programming (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2016, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2016.0692)
**Read level**: full (TR, ZIB-Report 15-15). Main text pp. 3–23 and the reference list pp. 23–25 read; Appendix A (proofs, pp. 26–27) and Appendix B (general-form LPs, pp. 28–32) read in full; Appendix C.1 (test-set construction, p. 33) read; the instance tables (Table 4 statistics pp. 34–55, Table 5 pp. 56–78, Table 6 pp. 79–100) skimmed by eye and parsed by a script to recount the aggregates of Tables 2–3 (see D5).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel E. Steffy (Oakland University), Kati Wolter (MOSEK ApS) (p. 3); alphabetical byline, the paper does not say who did what · **Position in trajectory**: Gleixner at ZIB, supported by the Research Campus MODAL (pp. 3, 23). A journal extension of the ISSAC 2012 paper (p. 3); the rational extension of SoPlex dates from version 1.7 of July 2012 (p. 16). It is the LP piece of the skill's 2012–2016 exact-LP line, and it prints the limitation (no recovery when the floating-point LP solver fails on an ill-conditioned LP; remedy: raise the working precision, p. 23) that the skill's Methods 3 and 5 trace to the 2025 precision-boosting paper [PB25].

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Two motivations open the paper (p. 3): fast LP codes work in floating point and can give unreliable results while some uses need extended-precision or exact solutions, and fast exact LP is a subroutine of exact MIP (Applegate et al. 2007a; Cook et al. 2011). Ten uses of exact LP or IP for theorems or numerically demanding models are cited, and the tools are said to have become available only recently (p. 3). The state of the art it enters is QSopt_ex: rely on the observation that floating-point bases are often optimal (Dhiflaoui et al. 2003; Koch 2004), verify the basic solution in rational arithmetic and repeat the simplex in higher precision if it is not optimal (Algorithm 1, p. 5); this becomes slow when extended-precision pivots are needed or the rational systems are hard (p. 5). The entry point is a device already inside floating-point LP codes: iterative refinement of linear systems (Wilkinson 1963, p. 5; used in LP solvers per a Gurobi personal communication, Maes 2013, p. 6). The paper takes it "one step further" from the linear system to the whole LP (p. 6). The problem is stated as building extended-precision LP solutions with an approximate LP solver as a subroutine, faster exact LP being a byproduct (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: look for a numerical device that the solver already applies at a lower level (here refinement of the basis systems) and ask what applying it to the whole optimization problem would mean (p. 6).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Theorem 3.1 (p. 6): given an approximate primal–dual pair x*, y*, replace b, ℓ and c by the residuals b̂ = b − Ax*, ℓ̂ = ℓ − x*, ĉ = c − Aᵀy* and scale them, giving min{Δ_D ĉᵀx | Ax = Δ_P b̂, x ≥ Δ_P ℓ̂}. The constraint matrix stays the same. An approximate solution x̂, ŷ of this LP yields x* + x̂/Δ_P, y* + ŷ/Δ_D, whose primal and dual violations are divided by Δ_P and Δ_D and whose complementary-slackness violation is divided by Δ_PΔ_D; optimal solutions and bases correspond one-to-one (points 1–5, p. 6). The theorem gets two readings (p. 7): numerically, refinement with magnified residuals; geometrically, a zoom on x* (shift, then scale the primal space) and a tilt of the objective to the reduced costs. Algorithm 2 (p. 9) iterates this: compute the violations exactly, stop at the tolerances, set Δ_{P,k+1} = 1/max{δ_{P,k}, (αΔ_{P,k})⁻¹} and likewise Δ_D, round the transformed data to the solver's precision, solve, correct (pp. 8–9). What changed against classical refinement is the scaling (Remark 3.4, p. 8): LP solvers use fixed absolute tolerances, so without scaling the zero solution would be optimal within tolerance and the corrector would be zero; scaling is also needed to exceed the precision the floating-point solver can represent (Wan 2006). What did not change is the LP solver, treated as a black-box oracle reached through an interface so that it can be swapped (p. 9), and the matrix A (p. 3). The simplex method is chosen because all refinement LPs share A and have affinely related solution spaces, so the previous basis is a warm start (pp. 15–16). Novelty is claimed with care ("To our knowledge", p. 8), naming two partial precedents: the zoom strategy for warm-starting interior-point QP methods (Saunders and Tenenblat 2006) and a single dual-refinement step reportedly tried in interior-point codes (Ladányi 2011, personal communication) (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: when the subproblem solver works with absolute tolerances, rescale the correction problem so that its largest violation becomes one, and cap the growth of the scale factor (α) so it stays finite when a violation reaches zero (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: LPs with rational data in standard form min{cᵀx | Ax = b, x ≥ ℓ}, with A of full row rank and n > m without loss of generality (p. 4); upper bounds and ranged rows L ≤ Ax ≤ U are treated in Appendix B (pp. 28–32); the status need not be known in advance (§4.4, p. 15). The convergence result rests on one assumption, Assumption 3.5 (p. 9): for each matrix A there are ε < 1 and σ such that, for every feasible right-hand side, bound vector and objective rounded to working precision, the solver returns x̄, ȳ within absolute tolerance ε for the rows, the bounds and the reduced costs, and with duality gap at most σ. The authors concede at once that this is not reasonable as stated (p. 10): floating-point precision is relative, LP codes such as CPLEX use absolute tolerances (Klotz 2014), and a floating-point solver will in general not meet absolute tolerances. Capping the scale factors by the inverse violations makes the violated entries at most one in absolute value, where relative accuracy implies the assumption, but entries that are already satisfied can still become large (p. 10). The computational section repeats that the assumption "will not hold in general" and hopes it holds for the LPs refinement builds (p. 15). Data: 1,202 primal and dual feasible LPs from Netlib (with the kennington folder), Mittelmann's and Mészáros's LP sets, and the LP relaxations of the COR@L MIP set and of all five MIPLIB versions (p. 18; App. C.1, p. 33). Of 1,242 candidates left after removing duplicates, 7 with 4,366,648 to 183,263,061 nonzeros were excluded because parsing them in rational arithmetic exceeded 48 GB, and 33 were removed because floating-point SoPlex could not solve them within two hours; all 40 are named in footnotes 15–16 (p. 33). Sizes run from 3 to 2,277,736 columns, 1 to 656,900 rows and 6 to 27,678,735 nonzeros (p. 33); Table 4 lists every instance's coefficient range (pp. 34–55), e.g. de063155 from 2.1e-07 to 8.4e+11 (p. 36). Not discussed by the authors (my reading): the set contains no infeasible or unbounded LP, and it is conditioned on the floating-point oracle being able to solve the LP (p. 33).
  - Transferable technique: state the accuracy contract of the floating-point component as a numbered assumption, and follow the theorem with a paragraph on why the contract fails in general and which part of the algorithm protects against that (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Devices and design decisions:
  - **Correspondence and convergence** (pp. 6, 10, 26–27): Theorem 3.1 is proved by direct algebra on the violations, the duality gap and the basis conditions (App. A.1, p. 26). Corollary 3.6 (p. 10) gives linear convergence: with ε̃ = max{ε, 1/α}, the scale factors are at least ε̃^{−(k−1)}, the violations at most ε̃^k and the duality gap at most σε̃^{2(k−1)}, so at most max{log ε_P, log ε_D, log(ε_S/σ)/2}/log ε̃ LP solves are needed; the proof is an induction over k (App. A.2, pp. 26–27). A reductio explains why the scaling must be tied to the violations: if a floating-point solver met absolute tolerances, one solve with Δ_P = Δ_D = N arbitrarily large would give any precision in one step (p. 10).
  - **Which arithmetic does what** (§3.3, p. 11): LP solves run in floating point; per round only two matrix-vector products and a constant multiple of n + m further operations run in rational arithmetic, so the rational work grows linearly with the nonzeros. Because rational cost grows with the encoding length of the iterate, the scale factors are rounded to powers of two, which does not affect convergence since only their order of magnitude matters. For a fixed accuracy target the rational arithmetic could be replaced by high extended precision (p. 11).
  - **Infeasibility** (§4.1, pp. 11–12): the feasibility LP max{τ | Aξ − (b − Aℓ)τ = 0, ξ ≥ 0, τ ≤ 1} (4) is primal and dual feasible by construction, has value one exactly when the LP is feasible, and its dual gives a Farkas proof (Lemma 4.1, pp. 11–12; proof p. 27); the primal termination test is divided by τ (p. 12).
  - **Infeasibility box** (§4.2, pp. 12–14): a Farkas proof is scale-invariant, so absolute tolerances mean nothing for it, and refining it does not by itself give a reliable certificate (p. 12). The aggregated Farkas cut (yᵀA)x ≥ bᵀy (after Neumaier and Shcherbina 2004) is turned into the largest R such that no feasible x has ‖x‖∞ < R, R = ρ(d, d₀) = d₀/‖d‖₁ (8) (p. 12). Algorithm 3 adds bound constraints in order of decreasing lower bound as long as −ℓᵢ < ρ, criterion (9) (p. 13). It runs in rational arithmetic, or in interval arithmetic for speed at the price of a possibly smaller box (Remark 4.2, p. 14), and is interleaved with the refinement of the feasibility LP. τ is compared with 1 − δ_{S,k+1}, never with a fixed threshold, because the feasibility LP of an infeasible LP can have value arbitrarily close to one (p. 14; an example in App. B.3, p. 31).
  - **Unboundedness** (§4.3, pp. 14–15): the feasibility test applied to Av = 0, v ≥ 0, cᵀv = −1, with the primal violation normalized by |cᵀv*|.
  - **Wrong answers of the oracle** (§4.4, Figure 3, pp. 15–16): a floating-point claim of infeasibility or unboundedness interrupts the loop and is tested by refining the auxiliary LP; if a feasible point is found the claim is rejected and the original LP is refined again with modified solver settings ("try harder" in Figure 3, p. 16). The authors call this ability to cope with incorrect answers of the floating-point solver a crucial property (p. 15). For ill-posed LPs the scheme is biased toward approximately feasible solutions (Remark 4.3, p. 15).
  - **SoPlex implementation** (§5.1, pp. 16–17): rational data via GMP, with EGlib for faster memory allocation (p. 16); the dual violation is taken from the basis status, and complementary slackness is not tested because basic solutions are complementary slack (p. 16); each refinement LP is hot-started from the previous basis and its factorization, using Wunderling's shifting technique for the infeasible starting basis (p. 16); in the correction, nonbasic variables are set exactly to their bounds, which removes a rounding error and saves a rational multiply-add (pp. 16–17); failed floating-point solves are retried with a series of parameter settings before giving up (p. 17); residuals are updated from the sparse correctors, which the authors call a heuristic minimization of the rational operations (p. 17).
  - **General form** (App. B, pp. 28–32): with two-sided bounds a reduced cost is charged to the bound nearest the primal value instead of being split by sign, since a sign split can add U·10⁻⁹ to the duality gap (p. 28); ranged rows are handled with slack variables only inside the floating-point solver, whose objective coefficients are set to the scaled duals, (16) (p. 30). A failed alternative is documented: fixing ranged rows with nonzero duals to one side worked for almost optimal starts, but on harder cases increased the number of LP solves, sometimes drastically, because of repeated unfixing (Remark B.2, p. 30). The general-form box certifies bounds on the variables only, because that is easier for the user to interpret (Remark B.3, p. 32).
  - Transferable technique: round the scale factors of a refinement loop to powers of two so the rational iterate keeps a short encoding; convergence is unaffected, and on momentum3 the last round took 0.5 s instead of almost 2 s without it (pp. 11, 18).
  - Transferable technique: turn an approximate infeasibility proof into an exact statement of what it does prove: aggregate the Farkas cut, compute the box radius d₀/‖d‖₁ exactly, and strengthen it with the bounds in sorted order (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: compare a status indicator that can lie arbitrarily close to its threshold (τ against one) with the current accuracy of the iterate, never with a fixed constant (p. 14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **Questions first** (§5.2, p. 17): (1) does plain refinement always converge, how fast in rounds and time, how much time goes to refinement against the first solve and to rational arithmetic, and do easy and difficult instances differ; (2) does refinement always reach an optimal basis, checked with Koch's basis verifier perPlex; (3) does refinement speed up exact LP.
  - **Arms** (pp. 17–18): SoPlex9 (floating point, primal and dual tolerance 10⁻⁹) against SoPlex50 and SoPlex250 (refinement to 10⁻⁵⁰ and 10⁻²⁵⁰); in all arms the LP files are parsed exactly and the violations computed in rational arithmetic (p. 17). The floating-point tolerance was tightened from SoPlex's default 10⁻⁶ to 10⁻⁹, but not to 10⁻¹², which risks numerical breakdown; presolving and scaling are applied only when solving from scratch (p. 18). For exact LP, comparing with plain QSopt_ex is rejected as biased because the floating-point simplex codes of SoPlex and QSopt_ex differ (QSopt_ex has no presolving); the reference is QSopt_ex warm-started from SoPlex9's basis, against QSopt_ex warm-started from SoPlex50's basis (p. 17).
  - **Platform** (p. 18): Intel Xeon X5672 at 3.2 GHz with 12 MB cache and 48 GB, one job per node; a two-hour limit for each SoPlex, perPlex and QSopt_ex run; SoPlex developer version 2.0.0.2 built with GCC 4.8.2 and linked with GMP 5.1.3, EGlib 2.6.20 and zlib 1.2.8. The QSopt_ex and perPlex versions are not given; thread counts are not stated (SoPlex is a sequential code, p. 16 fn. 3); there are no seeds or permutations.
  - **Metrics** (pp. 18, 20, 22): violations as rounded negative base-10 logarithms (p. 18); shifted geometric means with shifts of 2 s and 100 simplex iterations, arithmetic means for refinement rounds (p. 20); instances classed by R0, the number of refinement rounds until the final basis (p. 20); QSopt_ex precision boosts B (p. 22). Exclusions with reasons: the five instances where the infeasibility test fired, since their run amounts to refining two LPs, and instances solved in under 2 s by every algorithm (p. 20).
  - **Single instances** (Table 1, pp. 18–19): momentum3 gains 7 to 16 orders of magnitude per round, reaches 10⁻²⁵⁰ in 18 rounds and pays 4.2% overhead, dominated by rational arithmetic (p. 18). world pivots up to round 12 with setbacks in the dual violation, which the authors explain as a violation of Assumption 3.5 (nonbasic variables with large transformed objective coefficients entering the basis), and needs 29 rounds (pp. 18–19).
  - **Whole set** (pp. 19–20): 1,195 of 1,202 converge. The seven failures are named: three time out in the first refinement LP (fn. 6); on four the first floating-point solve wrongly claimed unboundedness (three) or infeasibility (one), the claims were rejected, but SoPlex then failed to return an approximately optimal solution even with other settings (fn. 7, pp. 19–20). On five more instances an intermediate infeasibility claim was rejected and the run converged (fn. 8, p. 20). SoPlex9 alone returned a wrong status on 4 instances, violations above 10⁻⁹ on 99 (most only slightly), and a meaningless solution on de063155 (p. 20). perPlex verified 1,024 SoPlex9 bases as optimal and found 95 primal infeasible, 30 dual infeasible and 6 primal-and-dual infeasible ones, so the Dhiflaoui/Koch observation is qualified for more than 14% of the instances (p. 20). After refinement every final basis perPlex could handle was primal and dual feasible; 61 instances ended with zero violation; only de063155, mod2 and world needed 3, 11 and 12 rounds to reach the final basis (p. 20).
  - **Averages** (Table 2, pp. 20–21): on 356 instances SoPlex50 costs +3% (R0 = 0, 326 instances) and +7% (R0 ≥ 1, 30 instances); SoPlex250 costs +14% and +19%. SoPlex250 needs 18.5 rounds on R0 ≥ 1 where 250/9 − 1 ≈ 26.8 would be expected from 10⁻⁹ solves, so the floating-point solves often return more accuracy than their tolerance (p. 21).
  - **Exact LP** (pp. 21–22, Table 3): of 1,195 instances, 1,166 are solved by both arms, 9 only from SoPlex50's basis (named in fn. 9, 108 to 2,026 s) and 20 by neither (p. 21). On 307 nontrivial instances, R0 = 0 (284) costs +1% with identical QSopt_ex runs (only maros-r7 is boosted, to 192 bit, in both arms); R0 ≥ 1 (23) needs 18% of the time, a factor 5.5, without counting the nine, and the precision boosts drop from 14 to 0 (p. 22). The authors note that the reduction in simplex iterations alone does not explain the gain (p. 22).
  - **Failures and slowdowns reported**: yes, with names and counts (pp. 19–22); every instance's numbers are in Tables 5–6 (pp. 56–100).
  - **Reader's recount** (mine, not in the paper; a script parse of Tables 5–6 of the extracted text, 1,202 and 1,195 rows): (a) Table 2's R0 ≥ 1 classes (26, 2, 1, 1) are reproduced exactly by "at least 2 s in one of the three runs"; R0 = 0 gives 327 against 326. (b) Table 3's R0 ≥ 1 row (23 instances, shifted geometric means 171.9 s and 30.6 s) is reproduced to 0.1 s only when the stated criterion (at least one version took more than 2 s, p. 21) is applied to the SoPlex run times, not to the total times. Applied to total times, 70 further R0 ≥ 1 instances qualify (64 of them in the delf and large families); from SoPlex9's basis QSopt_ex needed 128-bit pivots on 69 of them, from SoPlex50's basis on none, and their shifted geometric mean total time is 14.1 s against 1.9 s (shift 2 s, ratio 0.13). The published factor 5.5 is therefore conservative. (c) Table 5 gives 91 and 30 instances with R0 = 1 and R0 = 2 among the 1,190 converged instances outside the five feasibility-test cases, against 93 and 30 in the text (p. 20); the difference of two is unexplained. (d) Arithmetic on p. 20: 131 of 1,202 SoPlex9 bases (10.9%) were shown non-optimal; the "more than 14%" (178 bases, 14.8%) also counts the 47 instances perPlex could not decide.
  - Transferable technique: when the new method can feed the incumbent, compare the incumbent warm-started from the new method's output with the incumbent warm-started from the plain floating-point output, so the difference between the two codes cancels out (p. 17).
  - Transferable technique: class instances by an internal difficulty indicator (rounds to the final basis) and report the cost per class: the easy class shows the overhead, the hard class the gain (pp. 20–22).
  - Transferable technique: compute what the tolerances predict (26.8 rounds) and print it beside what was observed (18.5) (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: run an independent verifier (perPlex) on the baseline's own output before relying on a folklore claim about it (p. 20).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper presents itself as the LP analogue of Wilkinson's refinement for linear systems (pp. 5–6, 22) and surveys exact LP in three lines (pp. 4–6): the rational simplex and its improvements (Espinoza 2006 on encoding length, Edmonds' Q-pivoting, Gärtner's mixed-precision simplex, p. 4); basis verification with precision boosting (QSopt_ex, Applegate et al. 2007b, p. 5); and rational reconstruction for linear systems and Diophantine approximation (Ursic and Patarra 1983; Wan 2006; Pan 2011; Saunders et al. 2011; Cook and Steffy 2011; Grötschel, Lovász and Schrijver 1988, pp. 5–6). Safe bounds by interval arithmetic (Jansson 2004; Neumaier and Shcherbina 2004; Althaus and Dumitriu 2009; Steffy and Wolter 2013) are named as a different line (p. 6). QSopt_ex, called the state of the art in general-purpose exact LP (p. 17), is not attacked but used: the exact-LP claim is that refinement makes it faster (pp. 17, 22). Critique comes as extension: the interval test of Neumaier and Shcherbina may fail even for a very accurate Farkas proof, so their Farkas-cut idea is extended to the infeasibility box (p. 12); the observation that floating-point bases are often optimal (Dhiflaoui et al. 2003; Koch 2004) is confirmed for most instances and qualified for the rest (p. 20). The priority claim is hedged and partial precedents are named, including two personal communications from solver developers (Maes 2013, Gurobi, p. 6; Ladányi 2011, IBM, p. 8). Own prior work: ISSAC 2012 (p. 3), Cook, Koch, Steffy and Wolter 2011 on exact MIP (p. 3), Steffy and Wolter 2013 on safe bounds (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The contribution sentence comes on the first page: a new refinement-based algorithm that builds extended-precision LP solutions with an approximate LP solver as subroutine, with faster exact LP as a byproduct (p. 3). The theorem is given a numerical and a geometric reading, and two two-variable examples with figures, each with a 10⁻⁶ error that becomes visible only after shifting and scaling, come before the algorithm (pp. 7–8). The key assumption is followed at once by its critique (p. 10). Hedging is moderate and attributed: "To our knowledge", "we believe", "we have heard" (p. 8), "We hope" (p. 15). The main text has one theorem, one corollary and one lemma, with all proofs in Appendix A (pp. 26–27), so the body carries algorithms, examples and remarks. The computational section opens with its questions (p. 17) and justifies the exact-LP baseline with a one-sentence bias argument (p. 17). Footnotes name every failing or excluded instance (pp. 19–21, 33). A failed variant is kept in an appendix remark (Remark B.2, p. 30). Two summary sentences are looser than the results they summarise: the conclusion says the refined basis was always optimal (p. 22), where §5.3 says so for the instances perPlex could handle (p. 20); and it says nine more instances were solved than with QSopt_ex alone (p. 23), where the reference was QSopt_ex warm-started from SoPlex9's basis (pp. 17, 21).
  - Transferable technique: before the algorithm, show a two-variable LP in which a 10⁻⁶ violation is invisible at the original scale and obvious after the shift and scale (Examples 3.2–3.3, pp. 7–8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution layers: an algorithm (refinement for LP, auxiliary LPs for infeasibility and unboundedness, the infeasibility box; §§3–4, pp. 6–16), a simple convergence theory under an oracle assumption (p. 10), public software (an extension of SoPlex since version 1.7, pp. 3, 16) and a computational study on 1,202 LPs (§5, pp. 15–22). Stated limits: refinement breaks down when the LP is too ill-conditioned for the floating-point routine, which could be overcome by raising the working precision as QSopt_ex does (p. 23; already suggested as a minor modification on p. 10); Assumption 3.5 does not hold in general (pp. 10, 15); seven failures (pp. 19–20). Stated directions: faster refinement to 10⁻²⁵⁰ with the techniques of Wan, Pan or Saunders et al. (p. 21); extended instead of rational precision for fixed accuracy targets (p. 11); interval arithmetic for the box (p. 14); and an application niche, extended-precision solutions where exact ones are not needed, at less than the time of an exact LP solver (p. 23). Not stated by the authors (my reading): the test set has no infeasible or unbounded LP, so the certificate machinery of §4, and the box in particular, is exercised only through the rejection of 4 + 5 false floating-point claims (pp. 18–20, 33); the 33 LPs that floating-point SoPlex could not solve were removed (p. 33), so the study does not show what refinement does when the oracle fails from the start.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Refine a whole LP, not only its linear systems: replace right-hand side, bounds and objective by residuals scaled with the inverse of the largest violation, capped by a growth limit α, and keep the matrix (Theorem 3.1 and Algorithm 2, pp. 6–9).
- [D4] Round refinement scale factors to powers of two to keep the rational encoding short (pp. 11, 18).
- [D4] In the correction step set nonbasic variables exactly to their bounds, and update residuals from the sparse correctors (pp. 16–17).
- [D4] With two-sided bounds, charge each reduced cost to the bound nearest the primal value, not by its sign, when measuring dual violation and duality gap (p. 28).
- [D4] Use the feasibility LP with a homogenizing column τ to obtain either a feasible point or a Farkas proof from one primal and dual feasible LP, and divide the primal termination test by τ (pp. 11–12).
- [D4] Convert an approximate Farkas proof into an exact infeasibility box by aggregation, ρ = d₀/‖d‖₁ and bound strengthening in sorted order (Algorithm 3, p. 13).
- [D4] Treat every infeasible or unbounded verdict of the floating-point solver as a claim to be tested, and retry with other settings when it is rejected (Figure 3, pp. 15–16).
- [D5] Measure the effect of a better starting point for the incumbent exact solver by warm-starting it from both the plain and the refined basis (p. 17).
- [D5] Class instances by rounds to the final basis and report overhead per class with stated shifts (pp. 20–21).
