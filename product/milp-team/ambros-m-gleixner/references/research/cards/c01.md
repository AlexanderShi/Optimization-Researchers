- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem is the open pit mining production scheduling problem (OPMPSP): given a block model, decide when each block is removed over the mine's life so that net present value is maximised (abstract p. 5; p. 15).
  - The entry is a decision that the literature fixes before optimising. The IP and MIP formulations of Caccetta and Hill, Fricke and Menabde et al. all need a priori cutoff grades and treat each scheduling unit as homogeneous in ore (pp. 25–29).
  - Realistic models, however, can be solved only at aggregate level, and optimal cutoff grades vary over time; they typically decrease, because early profit counts more (pp. 29, 115).
  - The model D-MIP moves the processing decision into the optimisation at block level, while mining stays at aggregate level (pp. 29–30).
  - The thesis then makes the cost of that flexibility its research question: the "large number of additional variables" (abstract p. 5; p. 32). The plan follows from it: structure (Ch. 3), dual bounds (Ch. 4), aggregation of the processing variables (Ch. 5) and primal heuristics (Ch. 6) (pp. 16–18).
  - The IP route is justified by recent MIP-solver progress (pp. 25, 115) and by Fricke's remark that methods for realistic sizes remain an open question (p. 16).
  - Dual bounds are motivated as what lets engineers trust a plan: they serve to "evaluate the quality of solutions and provide engineers with confidence in the mine plans developed" (pp. 17, 69).
  - The topic came from outside: the model, the data and the honours year, with the Berlin supervisor agreeing when he wanted to "adjust the topic" (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: enter an application through a decision that the published models fix in advance (here cutoff grades), move it into the model, and make the computational price of the larger model the research question (pp. 29, 32).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Three ideas, each resting on a structural result.
  1. Split ratios. In any optimum of D-MIP or D-LP, the processing variables of each period solve a continuous bounded knapsack. So each block is either not processed or processed as far as its aggregate is mined, with one threshold per period, the "split ratio" (Prop. 3.4, pp. 45–46), which plays the role of a cutoff grade (p. 116). Exact split ratios are NP-hard to compute (Prop. 3.9, pp. 48–50). The optimal LP duals of the processing rows, however, are split ratios of D-LP for every LP optimum (Prop. 3.15, p. 63), so they serve as a heuristic proxy. Blocks with similar profit-to-tonnage ratios are aggregated into "bins"; blocks inside a band (1 ± ε)π_t keep a bin per distinct ratio, and bins may change from period to period (pp. 87–92; Remark 5.4, p. 89).
  2. A Lagrangean bound. Relaxing the 2T resource rows leaves an integral polytope (Prop. 3.10), so the Lagrangean dual gives only the LP bound (Prop. 3.11, p. 55). But every subproblem is a minimum cut in the network of Möhring et al. [41] (pp. 55–61), and a bundle method reaches that bound faster than any LP code tried (Ch. 4).
  3. Greedy sub-MIP heuristics. Start heuristics follow one generic greedy sub-MIP scheme over nested precedence-feasible index sets, instantiated two ways: by time windows (look-ahead L, fix F) and by the start sets of a Lagrangean solution. A variant keeps the not-yet-added columns integer-relaxed, which gives each step a global view (PIR-MIP). An LNS lets each aggregate's start move by δ periods (pp. 98–107).

  Kept: the standard precedence and resource structure, and the minimum-cut method for project scheduling from his supervisor's paper, applied "in a straightforward way" (p. 116). Changed:
  - processing is decided per block;
  - aggregation is applied to columns (model aggregation) rather than to blocks (aggregate modelling) (pp. 83–84);
  - in §6.3 the LP bound inside branch-and-bound is replaced by the Lagrangean bound (pp. 109–111).
  - Transferable technique: when a structural parameter of the optimum is NP-hard to compute, prove that its LP counterpart exists and can be read off the duals. Then use it with a safety band to define the reduction, and measure the loss in the full model (pp. 63, 91–95).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - MILP form (pp. 29–30). Binary variables x_{k,t}, equal to 1 if aggregate K_k may be mined during periods t, …, T, hence monotone in t. Continuous variables: the fractions mined, y_{k,t} per aggregate, and processed, z_{i,t} per block. Precedence between aggregates is expressed through cumulative sums (2.5b)–(2.5c), processing is bounded by z_{i,t} ≤ y_{k,t} (2.5d), and each period has one mining and one processing knapsack row (2.5e)–(2.5f). There are no general integers, SOS, indicators or big-M terms.
  - Structures exploited: the precedence-constrained knapsack and resource-constrained project scheduling (pp. 33–36), a continuous bounded knapsack per period (pp. 45–46), and a precedence system that becomes totally unimodular after a substitution (pp. 51–52).
  - Stated modelling assumptions (p. 15): a deterministic block model; known costs, prices and discount rates; fixed infrastructure; perfect grade control; slope requirements as precedence cones.
  - A hidden assumption, exposed by the author himself: (2.5d) assumes that an aggregate mined by a fraction has all its blocks mined by that fraction. This violates block precedences unless the aggregates are panels (p. 31). The strict variant D-MIP' (each aggregate mined within one period) avoids this, and D-MIP is its relaxation with the same LP bound (Cor. 3.2, pp. 32, 44).
  - Assumptions behind the correctness arguments:
    - integer PCKP weights and profits for the hardness proofs (p. 33 fn. 4; pp. 48–49);
    - b¹ ≥ 0 for moving a cut into the reduced LPs and the Lagrangean scheme (Lemma 3.17, p. 64), and b³ = 0 to keep the split-ratio structure (p. 66);
    - m ∈ Ω(K) for the complexity bound, i.e. a precedence graph "not unrealistically sparse" (Cor. 3.14, p. 61);
    - feasibility of the aggregated LP (Assumption 5.1, p. 85).
  - Data: 29 instances from three mines, supplied by BHP Billiton (pp. 18–19). Periods are one year with a 10% discount factor.
    - "marvin" is an artificial Whittle 4X model with 8513 blocks, aggregated to 115, 296 and 1038 aggregates;
    - "wa" is a real Western Australian mine with 96821 blocks in 125 panels and a 25-year life;
    - "ca" is a real Canadian mine with 29266 blocks, 121 aggregates and 25 scenarios for rock and ore tonnage.

    Each subset has a stated purpose (p. 19): marvin varies the aggregation resolution on the same data, ca gives easier and harder scenarios on one precedence model, and wa has many blocks per aggregate for Chapter 5. The mining rows never bind in any LP or Lagrangean solution (pp. 81–82).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (devices, with proofs):
  - Formulation strength and reduction. The cumulative form (2.5b) gives a stronger LP than y ≤ x (p. 31). Either x or y can be eliminated from the LP without loss (Prop. 3.1, pp. 43–44). The reduction is called "basically a presolving technique", with the caution that presolving can change the dual space; here the resource multipliers are shown to be preserved (Remark 3.3, p. 44). He also warns that an out-of-the-box MIP solver may not remove the redundancy completely (p. 44).
  - NP-hardness of D-MIP and D-MIP' by reduction from PCKP. For D-MIP, a node-splitting construction puts the knapsack row on the "+" copies and the profits on the "−" copies (Prop. 2.1, pp. 37–38).
  - Split ratios:
    - the split item of a continuous bounded knapsack gives the threshold (Prop. 3.4, pp. 45–46);
    - existence (Cor. 3.6) and the interval of valid values, computed from an optimal solution (Prop. 3.7, p. 47);
    - NP-hardness of computing them: a PCKP containment test is encoded in a one-period D-MIP' with an auxiliary aggregate and processing profits 1/2 and 1/3 (Prop. 3.9, pp. 48–50). Removing predecessor or successor sets turns the test into a full PCKP algorithm (p. 50).
  - Integrality of the precedence polytope. A bijective substitution to cumulative variables gives a matrix whose rows contain one ±1 or a pair 1, −1, hence a totally unimodular matrix. Extreme points of the full polytope project to extreme points without z, and a fractional z splits into a convex combination (Prop. 3.10, pp. 51–52).
  - Lagrangean bound:
    - LD = LP through Geoffrion's integrality property (Prop. 3.11, p. 55);
    - the subproblem becomes unconstrained project scheduling, with a slack period T+1 and shifted non-negative costs, solved as a minimum a-b-cut in a digraph with K(T+2)+2 nodes (Lemma 3.12, Prop. 3.13, pp. 57–61);
    - a minimum cut is converted to a K-cut with one assignment arc per aggregate in O(KT) (Lemma 3.12, p. 59);
    - the total cost is O(NT + mKT² log(K²T/m)) with push-relabel (Cor. 3.14, p. 61);
    - the multipliers read as time-dependent cutoff grades (p. 62), and by complementary slackness the LP duals are split ratios (Prop. 3.15, p. 63).
  - Cuts and fixings without breaking the network. A valid inequality with b¹ ≥ 0 is rewritten in y only (Lemma 3.17, pp. 64–65) and then dualised with its own multiplier, so the subproblem stays a min-cut (pp. 65–66). Fixings derived from predecessor-cone tonnage against cumulative mining capacity (Prop. 3.18, p. 67) are imposed in the digraph, by shortening chains or by infinite capacities (p. 68).
  - Dual-bound code:
    - ConicBundle, with its scaling heuristic and "active bounds fixing" (p. 77);
    - Hochbaum's pseudoflow algorithm, running phase one only (p. 77);
    - a residual-graph warmstart, dropped because each cut took at least twice as long (pp. 77–78);
    - infinite capacities replaced by Σ_k c(e_{k,T+1}) (p. 78).

    A bundle variant with aggregated subgradients stalled at 10⁻³ relative precision, with running times ten-fold or more (p. 80).
  - Aggregation:
    - B-MIP(B) uses 0/1 weights, which violate Zipkin's normalisation, and bins that vary over time (pp. 87–89);
    - primal LP-binnings are either variable or constant over time (p. 90);
    - the dual LP-binnings B^ε refine the variable primal binning for every ε > 0 (pp. 91–92);
    - disaggregation fixes x at the aggregated solution and solves the remaining LP of D-MIP (p. 94).
  - Heuristics:
    - Lemma 6.2 shows that every sub-MIP of the greedy scheme is feasible, by extending the previous solution with zero activity (pp. 99–100), and the scheme is given as pseudocode with a gap limit ε (p. 100);
    - the instantiations are the time-based (L, F) rule (p. 101), the Lagrangean rule (p. 102) and PIR-MIP (p. 103);
    - δ-LNS leaves at most (2δ+1)K integers free (p. 107).
  - Lagrangean branch-and-bound in SCIP 1.05, with the LP turned off (pp. 110–111):
    - the bundle method is warmstarted from the parent's or the root's multipliers;
    - down-branches become fixings, and up-branches fix all predecessors to 1 (a claim that "can be shown" but is not proved here);
    - heuristics run only at a certain frequency;
    - strong branching prunes and fixes against the primal bound from the root heuristics.
  - Transferable technique: before dualising a cut in a Lagrangean scheme whose subproblem is a min-cut, rewrite the cut in variables that keep the network structure (here b¹ ≥ 0 lets x be replaced by cumulative y). Impose fixings in the graph instead of dualising them (pp. 64–68).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup: C++ on one 32-bit Pentium 4 at 3 GHz with 2 GB RAM, single-threaded; CPLEX 11.0, ConicBundle 0.2d, Pseudoflow 3.21 and SCIP 1.05 (pp. 19–20, 124–125).
  - Chapter 4: the LP baseline was searched for, not assumed. Primal simplex, dual simplex and barrier were run on D-LP, x-D-LP and y-D-LP; barrier without crossover on x-D-LP was the fastest (p. 76).
    - marvin: 11, 27 and 260 s, against 34, 97 and 506 s for the primal simplex; the dual simplex was "even slower by a factor of 2 to 10";
    - wa: unsolved by either simplex in 1 h, solved by barrier in 325 s;
    - y-D-LP was numerically harder: barrier gave only near-optimal solutions on 4 of the 25 ca scenarios.

    Bundle times are reported as min-max values: for each window of 10 consecutive maximum bundle sizes (sizes from 10 to 100), take the worst time, then report the best window. Objective values are reported the same way, and multiplier deviations as the worst over all sizes (pp. 78–80, Figs. 4.1–4.3). Result: the Lagrangean dual matches the LP bound within a relative 10⁻⁶, in 3.6% to 17.5% of the LP time (Table 4.1, p. 81). The bundle method always stopped before its 10⁻⁹ target "due to numerical limits" in the interplay of subproblem and QP solver (p. 81).
  - Chapter 5. Runs used CPLEX MIP with emphasis 0, traditional branch-and-cut, one thread and a 1 h limit; unsolved instances were rerun with two threads for 5 h. Metrics (Table 5.1, p. 93):
    - bins per period;
    - time and gap when the gap first fell below 1% and below 0.5%;
    - final gap and nodes;
    - the best value relative to B^block.

    The dual LP-binnings lose less than 0.06% (p. 94). wa is solvable only after binning, because B^block runs out of memory (pp. 93–94). Disaggregation LPs take under 38 s (p. 94), and Table 5.2 gives their gains (p. 95).
  - Chapter 6. Sub-MIPs were solved to 0.01% with emphasis 1 and the primal simplex at the root. PIR-MIP and LNS had 30 min limits. Metrics: time, an optimality gap against the proven optimum of the aggregated model B-MIP(B^0.01) from Table 5.1, and an LP gap; the captions give each gap's formula and denominator (pp. 104–105, 107–108). Failures are reported: PIR-MIP did not finish on marvin-1038 within 30 min (pp. 105–106), and 2-LNS hit its limit once (p. 107).
  - §6.3, the SCIP experiments, has no table and no count. A control run of SCIP with default settings and the separators off confirms that branching alone leaves the dual bound at the root value (pp. 110–111).
  - Absent: seeds or permutations, any variability analysis, significance tests and shifted geometric means. The ca rows are arithmetic means over 25 scenarios, with the hardest scenario shown separately (pp. 81, 93). No solver other than CPLEX is used as a comparison, and no code from the literature.
  - Transferable technique: search the baseline space (algorithm × equivalent formulation) and compare against the fastest route found; give the conventional route's numbers in the text (p. 76).
  - Transferable technique: report a tuning parameter of your own method by min-max over windows of consecutive values. The reported time is then one you could have obtained without tuning on the test set (pp. 78–80).

The analogy is my inference.
- Method 1 variant ⚠ (p. 91): a heuristic premise, that LP split ratios approximate MIP split ratios, is checked against the exact intervals computed from an optimal MIP solution, on one instance. The maximum relative deviation is 8.3% (Fig. 5.1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Search the baseline before comparing. Run every LP algorithm (primal, dual, barrier with and without crossover) on every equivalent formulation, compare the new method against the fastest route found, and give the conventional route in a sentence (p. 76; Table 4.1, p. 81).
- Report a tuning parameter of your own method pessimistically. Over windows of 10 consecutive values, take each window's worst time and report the best window; report accuracy deviations as the worst over all values (pp. 78–80).
- Replace an NP-hard structural parameter by its LP counterpart from the duals, and keep a (1 ± ε) band unaggregated. Check the proxy against exact values on one solvable instance (Fig. 5.1, p. 91), and measure the loss in the full model (Tables 5.1–5.2, pp. 93, 95).
- Disaggregate an aggregated MIP solution by fixing its integer variables in the full model and solving the remaining LP; tabulate the gain over fixed-weight disaggregation (pp. 94–95).
- Keep a Lagrangean min-cut subproblem intact when adding cuts. Rewrite each cut without the variables that break the network (b¹ ≥ 0, Lemma 3.17), dualise it, and impose variable fixings in the graph (pp. 64–68).
- Build start heuristics as a nested sequence of precedence-feasible index sets, solved as sub-MIPs. Prove once, by zero extension (Lemma 6.2), that every step is feasible.
