### S058 · Solving quadratic programs to high precision using scaled iterative refinement (Mathematical Programming Computation 11(3) 2019, DOI 10.1007/s12532-019-00154-6; arXiv 1803.07178)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 of ZIB-Report 18-04 (inner title page dated March 19, 2018); pp. 3–17 read (Sections 1–6, references), the proof of Lemma 8 (pp. 9–10) skimmed; appendix Tables 5–8 (pp. 18–30) seen only for headers and first rows.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Tobias Weber, Sebastian Sager (Otto von Guericke University Magdeburg, first and second authors; Gleixner the ZIB author, p. 3) · **Place in the trajectory**: carries the LP iterative refinement of [9] (Gleixner, Steffy, Wolter, ISSAC 2012) to QP (p. 4); its basic theorem was "formulated and proven" in Gleixner's thesis [8, Theorem 5.2] (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: QP solvers "calculate and check the solution of a QP in floating-point arithmetic", so the claimed precision may be violated and in extreme cases optima missed, a problem when solutions are used for rigorous reasoning (p. 3). Entry: interval arithmetic rules out standard solvers and is conservative; the one exact rational QP code [6] targets computational geometry with few constraints or variables (p. 4); the LP refinement of [9] and the remark in [8] that it carries over to QP (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: a floating-point QP solver used as a black-box oracle on a sequence of refined QPs: rational residuals, one scaling factor Δ_k = min{δ_P⁻¹, δ_D⁻¹, αΔ_{k−1}}, oracle solve of the rounded refined QP, scaled correction added in rationals (Algorithm 1, pp. 6–7). Changed from LP: the quadratic term, and one common factor for primal and dual scaling so that Q and A stay fixed and the oracle can hotstart (p. 7). Unchanged: refinement of KKT residuals with scaling as in [9] (p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: convex QP min ½xᵀQx + cᵀx s.t. Ax = b, x ≥ l with rational data, feasible and bounded, Q positive semidefinite on the feasible set (Definition 1, pp. 4–5); general QPs by one slack per inequality, upper bounds handled (p. 11). Assumption 5: oracle solutions of relative accuracy ε ∈ [0,1) and complementarity violation σ (p. 7); strict convexity only for Lemma 8 and Theorem 9 (pp. 9–10). No detection of infeasible or unbounded QPs (p. 12). Test set: the 138 convex QPs of Maros and Mészáros (p. 13).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Theorem 3 (tolerances of the refined QP map back by 1/Δ_P, 1/Δ_D, 1/(Δ_PΔ_D)) (p. 5); Lemma 6 by induction: residuals at most ε̃^k, complementarity at most σε̃^{2(k−1)}, an iteration bound (pp. 7–8); Remark 7: local KKT pairs of nonconvex QPs (p. 8); Theorem 9: linear rate ε̃^{1/2} of primal iterates for strictly convex QPs (pp. 10–11). SoPlex 2.2.1.2, GMP 6.1.0, qpOASES 3.2 and a 4.0 pre-release without hotstart (p. 11). Safeguards: rational LU solve of the basis system only after the same basis is returned for several iterations ("ratfac minstalls"); on oracle failure, reliable settings, then the scaling factor reduced by two orders of magnitude ("backstepping") (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: exact solves with three settings on all 138 QPs: 91 exact, 39 within 1e-100, 8 without high precision (virtual best), 8.84 refinements on average, six library objective values off by more than 1e-7 (pp. 13–14); at tolerance 1e-10 against qpOASES MPC/Default/Reliable on the 73 QPs with at most 1,000 variables and constraints, only QPs solved by all settings, subsets "> t" by at least one algorithm: never slower than Reliable in mean time, more accurate, rational overhead below 2% (pp. 14–15); hardware and time limit not stated in the text read.
- **D6 Positioning**: extends [9] and [8] (p. 4); set against interval methods and the exact QP simplex code of [6] (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing**: contributions "on three levels" (p. 3); a two-variable worked example with a figure before the algorithm (pp. 5–6); a closing wish that exact checking becomes a "future trend" (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithm with convergence proofs, open code (github.com/TobiasWeber/QPrefinement, p. 11), exact reference solutions for Maros–Mészáros (p. 15). Limits printed: no infeasibility or unboundedness detection (p. 12), eight QPs without high precision and 32 memory failures of the dense setting (p. 13), a bias toward qpOASES from excluded failures (p. 15). Future: that detection, one scaling factor per variable, refinement counts as a measure of a floating-point QP solver's accuracy (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: all work except the oracle solves is rational (p. 6); the oracle runs with loose fast settings because its inaccuracies "are detected anyway" by the rational residuals and corrected in the next iteration (p. 12); the exact basis solve is triggered by a stalled basis (step 6, check the basis early) (p. 12).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: the QP code has an oracle-failure recovery (reliable settings, then backstepping by 10²) (p. 12), against the limitation stated for LP refinement under Method 5.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the baseline is the oracle itself in its three standard settings, restricted to its design range (73 QPs) and to QPs all settings solve; the claim is stated against Reliable and against Default separately (pp. 14–15).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 3): a public test library solved in exact arithmetic, six literature optimal values corrected, precise values published online (p. 14).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a remark in the thesis becomes the paper (p. 4); limits printed with counts and carried into future work (pp. 12–16).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: proposes refinement counts, with violations computed in rational precision, as a measure of floating-point solver accuracy (p. 16); proposed, not carried out.
- (Heuristic 8 ✅: subsets defined by at least one algorithm "avoids a biased definition", and the bias from excluded failures is named (pp. 14–15).)

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Use one scaling factor for primal and dual residuals so the oracle's matrices stay fixed and it can hotstart from its last basis (p. 7).
- (D4) Trigger the expensive exact step (rational LU of the basis system) only after the oracle returns the same basis several times, and stop early when the exact basic solution verifies (p. 12).
- (D4) Run the floating-point oracle with fast, loose settings; on failure retry with reliable settings, then backstep the scaling factor (p. 12).
- (D5) Publish exact reference values for a standard test library and name the instances whose literature values are wrong (p. 14).
- (D5) Define each difficulty subset as the instances "for which at least one algorithm took more than t seconds", so the subsets do not favour one method (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "Note that all calculations except the expensive solves of the QPs are done in rational precision." (p. 6)
- "As a concluding remark, we hope that the idea of checking numerical results of floating-point algorithms in exact or safe arithmetic will become a future trend when applying or analyzing numerical algorithms." (p. 16)

### S064 · A massively parallel interior-point solver for linear energy system models with block structure (ZIB-Report 19-41 2019, no DOI or arXiv id in the work list)
**Read level**: partial. Text read: ZIB-Report 19-41 (revised, October 2019); pp. 3–7, 11–13 and 15–30 read; the ELMOD equations (pp. 7–11), the block elimination of §3.1 (pp. 13–15) and Algorithms 1–2 (pp. 21–22) skimmed; references not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Daniel Rehfeldt (first), Thorsten Koch (ZIB); Hannes Hobbie, David Schönheit, Dominik Möst (TU Dresden, energy economics) (p. 3) · **Place in the trajectory**: an application project (BMWi project BEAM-ME, p. 30); per the work-list note the EJOR 2022 paper S027 has the same six authors.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: high-resolution linear energy system models give LPs with hundreds of millions of variables and constraints that commercial solvers take prohibitively long on or cannot hold in a desktop's memory, and general LP solvers cannot run on distributed memory (p. 4). Entry: the arrowhead (doubly bordered block-diagonal) form that a discrete time horizon produces (pp. 4, 11), with often more than 100,000 linking constraints and variables, most of which link only two consecutive blocks ("local"), which rules out a plain extension of the two-stage stochastic approaches (p. 5).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: extend PIPS-IPM's Schur complement decomposition to arrowhead LPs: precompute the block-tridiagonal pattern the local links give the Schur complement (p. 17), whose nonzeros grow linearly in the number of blocks (Observation 1, p. 18), and build a distributed sparsified Schur complement C̃ (after a circuit-simulation preconditioner [5]) (pp. 18–19) that replaces C and acts as an implicit preconditioner of the whole augmented system (pp. 20–21). Unchanged: PIPS-IPM's framework and its BiCGSTAB (pp. 21–23).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: LPs, not MIPs, in arrowhead form (1)–(6), linking constraints and variables split into local and global (pp. 4–5); few global linking constraints and few linking variables assumed (p. 15); full-rank diagonal blocks for the elimination (p. 14). Data: ELMOD, a European nodal-pricing electricity market model with DC load flow over 8,760 hours, where storage and ramping give local links and the reservoir aggregate a global one (pp. 6–9); SIMPLE, a generator of arrowhead instances of adjustable size, not the authors' work (pp. 11–12); blocks annotated by the user through a GAMS callback (p. 24).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: PIPS-IPM++, more than 50,000 lines added or modified (p. 22): Colombo–Gondzio multiple centrality correctors instead of Mehrotra's predictor-corrector and separate primal and dual step lengths, for "robustness, even at the cost of some performance" (pp. 22–23); BiCGSTAB with divergence and stagnation checks and a limit of 75 iterations, drop threshold t from 1e-3 lowered to at most 1e-6 when convergence is slow, more than 95% of Schur nonzeros dropped (p. 23); distributed scaling and presolve (bound strengthening, singleton rows, parallel and nearly parallel rows) (pp. 23–24); Observation 2 (the Schur residual equals the full residual) proved because it is used (pp. 19–20).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: JUWELS (2×24-core Xeon Platinum 8168 nodes, 96 or 192 GB) and a 2 TB, 88-core ZIB machine for commercial runs that ran out of memory; CPLEX 12.8, Gurobi 8.1, MOSEK 8.1, Xpress 8.4.7 barrier, crossover off, 16 threads, anonymised, one solver dropped after crashes (pp. 26, 28); SIMPLE scaling: commercial solvers on one thread at least 5 times faster than one MPI process, but PIPS++ 23 s on 512 processes against no commercial run below 400 s, the decomposition-only version not competitive (pp. 26–27); Table 1: SIMPLE2 546 s against 38,800 s on different machines, ELMOD EU about 20 min with a gap below 1% against 18–24 h for the one commercial solver that finishes (pp. 28–29); the text's "factor of up to 67" (p. 29) does not match Table 1 as extracted (my arithmetic: 79,094/1,119 ≈ 70.7 on EU16, 38,800/546 ≈ 71.1 on SIMPLE2); no seeds or repeated runs reported.
- **D6 Positioning**: extends PIPS-IPM and OOPS from two-stage stochastic programs (p. 5); the preconditioner is borrowed from circuit simulation [5] and claimed new for interior-point methods (p. 19).
- **D7 Writing**: speedups tied to node counts (19 and 38 compute nodes against one commercial node, p. 29); an earlier version's wrongly generated instances admitted in a footnote (pp. 27–28).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithm and software for distributed IPM on arrowhead LPs (pp. 29–30). Limits printed: sequentially far slower than commercial codes (pp. 26, 29); EU instances only to a gap (p. 29); comparisons on different machines (p. 28); SIMPLE3 too large for GAMS to write as one LP, so no comparison (p. 29). Future: substitution and dependent-row presolve, more aggressive scaling, the homogeneous self-dual IPM, a hierarchical Schur decomposition, distributed model generation, a public release (pp. 25, 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (steps 1–2, 5): the incumbents are stripped of what the new code lacks (crossover off, p. 26), run at their best thread count (p. 26) and, on EU, with more aggressive numerics including vendor advice (p. 29); the machine mismatch for SIMPLE2 is printed (p. 28).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the second baseline is the new code reduced to the bare decomposition, not the default incumbent (pp. 26–27).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: limits printed with numbers (factor 5 sequentially, EU gap below 1%, one solver's crashes) and turned into next steps (pp. 26–30).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant (step 7): instances found wrongly generated in the earlier report version were exchanged and the change admitted in a footnote, with sizes and run times changed (pp. 27–28); applied to a report's own test instances, not to a benchmark library.
- new-pattern candidate: attribution check by switching the new component off: the remaining EU gap stays almost the same with the preconditioning deactivated (p. 29).
- (Research Taste 8 ✅: the arrowhead form of time-discretised energy models becomes a generic solver capability, also for REMix, Balmorel and SIMPLE (pp. 11–12).)

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Before choosing a dense or sparse Schur strategy, count the nonzeros the structure implies (linear in the number of blocks when global links stay fixed) and precompute the pattern to cut memory and communication (pp. 17–18).
- (D4) Use a cheap sparsified operator (entries dropped relative to the diagonal, threshold adapted to Krylov convergence) as an implicit preconditioner of the whole system instead of solving the reduced system iteratively (pp. 18–23).
- (D5) Put a decomposition-only version of the new code ("New solver reduced") on the scaling plot to show that the supplementary techniques make it competitive (pp. 26–27).
- (D5) Switch off in the incumbent what your code lacks (here crossover) and give it its best thread count before comparing (p. 26).
- (D5) When a residual gap remains, rerun with the new component deactivated to show the gap is not its fault (p. 29).

**Quotes**:
- "Also, crossover (which can considerably increase the run time) is turned off, as this feature is not implemented in our solver." (p. 26)
- "Interestingly, tests on the large-memory machine at ZIB revealed that this gap is not a result of our Schur complement preconditioning: The gap stays almost the same if the preconditioning approach is deactivated." (p. 29)

### D006 · Learn to Relax: Integrating 0-1 Integer Linear Programming with Pseudo-Boolean Conflict-Driven Search (CPAIOR 2020, LNCS 12296, pp. xxiv–xxvi; no DOI or arXiv id in the work list)
**Read level**: full. Text read: the extended abstract in the CPAIOR 2020 front matter, pp. 1–3 (text pp. 1–2, references pp. 2–3).

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Jo Devriendt (first; Lund, Copenhagen), Jakob Nordström (Copenhagen, Lund) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the conference abstract of the work the skill cites as [L2R21] (Constraints 26, 2021; S024 in the work list, same title and authors).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: conflict-driven PB solvers that reason natively with linear constraints could run exponentially faster than CDCL (cutting planes against resolution), yet they "can sometimes get hopelessly stuck" where the LP relaxation of the residual problem is already infeasible (p. 1). Entry: a known weakness of the rival paradigm that MIP's relaxation addresses (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: interleave incremental LP solving with conflict-driven PB search, the two solvers communicating throughout (p. 1): an infeasible LP yields, by Farkas' lemma, one linear constraint that starts PB conflict analysis; a rational LP solution yields Gomory cuts for both sides and can guide variable assignment; learned PB constraints may be passed to the LP (p. 1). Claimed first combination of LP relaxations and cut generation with full PB conflict analysis on linear inequalities (p. 1). Implementation: SoPlex with RoundingSat (p. 2).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: 0–1 integer linear programs, solved by PB solvers (p. 1); benchmarks not named in the abstract (p. 2).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: LP calls under a strict time budget, terminated when it is exceeded (p. 1); no proofs (n/a).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: "extensive experiments" claimed, with significantly improved performance on a wide range of benchmarks (p. 2); no test sets, numbers or baselines in the abstract.
- **D6 Positioning**: against PB solvers that re-encode to CNF, and against MIP and CP solvers that run resolution on clauses derived from linear constraints (Achterberg; Downing) (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing**: a hedged close ("we believe that there is ample room for further improvements") with a "best of two worlds" framing (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a solver integration (p. 2); the only limit stated is that room for improvement remains (p. 2); details are left to other publications.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (step 4): the import runs the other way: MIP's LP relaxation and Gomory cuts are carried into the pseudo-Boolean community's solver, with Nordström's group, at CPAIOR (pp. 1–2); Method 3 step 4 describes importing a rival's mechanism into his own solver.
- (Heuristic 7 ✅: the supplementary LP runs on a strict budget so that it cannot starve the host search (p. 1). Heuristic 9 ✅: the LP's infeasibility proof feeds conflict analysis (p. 1).)

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Give an auxiliary solver embedded in another a strict time budget per call and abort it when exceeded, so it cannot starve the host search (p. 1).
- (D4) Turn an infeasible LP's Farkas multipliers into one linear constraint that starts conflict analysis, and a fractional LP solution into Gomory cuts shared by both solvers (p. 1).

**Quotes**:
- "Inspired by mixed integer programming (MIP), we address this problem by interleaving incremental LP solving with the conflict-driven pseudo-Boolean search." (p. 1)
- "In order to balance resources and avoid that the LP solver starves the PB solver, LP calls are made with a strict time budget and are terminated as soon as this budget is exceeded." (p. 1)

### S031 · Two-row and two-column mixed-integer presolve using hashing-based pairing methods (EURO Journal on Computational Optimization 8(3) 2020, DOI 10.1007/s13675-020-00129-6)
**Read level**: full. Text read: the published open-access article (journal pp. 205–240 = file pp. 1–36); pp. 1–33 read, the case analysis of §3.4.2 with Examples 3.6, 3.7 and 3.10 (pp. 15–20) skimmed; references (pp. 33–36) not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Patrick Gemander (first), Dieter Weninger, Alexander Martin (FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg), Wei-Kun Chen (Beijing Institute of Technology), Leona Gottwald (ZIB) (p. 36) · **Place in the trajectory**: presolve research, which the skill lists as group work (Honest Boundary), in SCIP 6.0.2 (p. 26).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: single-row or single-column presolve is fast but local and weak, global structures are slow; pairs of rows or columns are the compromise (p. 2). Entry: published two-row and two-column methods leave open how to find promising pairs, since enumerating all pairs is too expensive in presolve (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: two hashing-based pairing mechanisms: a hashtable keyed by (j, k, a_ij/a_ik) that finds row pairs with two cancellable coefficients (§4.1), and four hashlists by sign pattern of variable pairs that find row pairs sharing two variables with opposite signs (§4.2), motivated by Lemma 3.1 (pp. 9, 23–26). Five methods use them: two-row and two-column nonzero cancellation, LP-based bound tightening on two constraints, Belotti's bound tightening on convex combinations of two rows extended by disjoint set-packing constraints from the clique table, and a two-column version of the complementary-slackness dual presolve of Achterberg et al. (pp. 6, 14–23, 29).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: general MIP min cᵀx, Ax ∘ b, possibly infinite bounds, integer subset I (p. 3); primal reductions keep all feasible solutions, dual ones at least one optimum (p. 6); two-column cancellation on integer columns needs an integral scalar and an integer partner (p. 8); the dual method assumes ≥ rows and zero lower bounds and uses only continuous columns (p. 21). Correctness: Lemma 3.1 (p. 9), Proposition 3.5 after Belotti (p. 12), Lemma 3.8 and Theorem 3.9 for the extended breakpoint set (pp. 17–18). Test set: MIPLIB 2017 benchmark v2 (240 instances), plus TSCCP and supply-chain sets (pp. 26, 28, 30).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Belotti's breakpoint sweep in O(|N|²) (Algorithm 1, pp. 12–14); the set-packing breakpoints cost O(|N| log |N|) and leave the total complexity unchanged (pp. 18–19). Working limits: at most 4,900 pairs per row and a failure budget of 100·|M|, refunded on success but never negative (p. 24); for the hashlists at most 10,000 pairs per row and 10·|M| in total, stop after 1,000 fruitless or 1,000 repeated pairs, at most |M| processed pairs (pp. 25–26). Numerics and structure: |λ| ≤ 1000, and no cancellation that destroys integrality, ±1 coefficients, set packing/covering/partitioning or locks (p. 24). Both mechanisms can miss reductions (pp. 24, 26).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP 6.0.2/SoPlex 4.0.2, Xeon E3-1240 v5, 32 GB, 7,200 s, defaults; baseline with all five methods off; five seeds per instance, each instance–seed pair one observation, pairs with numerical trouble or exposed bugs discarded; brackets [t, 7200] over instances solved by at least one run; shifted geometric means with shifts 1 s and 100 nodes (pp. 26–27); results range from three fewer solved (two-row cancellation, p. 27) to 4% and 6% faster on [1000, 7200] (pp. 29–30), 147 against 98 solved and 0.26 relative time on 150 TSCCP runs (p. 28), reductions on 35 of 40 supply-chain instances (p. 30), all five together slightly slower (p. 31); without working limits presolve time ×5 on average and 31 more memory failures for 14 models at least 1% smaller (p. 32); Table 8 as extracted prints 568 solved for "All" in the All row but 563 in the [0,7200] row (p. 31; my reading).
- **D6 Positioning**: builds on Achterberg et al. (2019), Belotti (2013), Bixby–Wagner, Chang–McCormick and Gondzio (pp. 2, 6, 11, 21); presolve's weight taken from Achterberg–Wunderling (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing**: neutral and negative results stated plainly, with two candidate causes (overhead; unfavourable substructure) (p. 27); a closing paragraph argues for keeping such methods (p. 32).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithms (pairings, clique extension) in SCIP (p. 33). Limits printed: cancellation neutral or negative, few continuous variables in MIPLIB limit the dual method, more nodes on ten instances with the clique extension (pp. 29–33). Future: redundancy detection with equal signs (Remark 3.2, pp. 9–10), tuning, new pairings, three or more rows or columns (p. 33).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the baseline is default SCIP minus exactly the five methods under study, one of which was already default; each method alone, all five together, and the clique extension against itself without it (pp. 26–31); a component comparison inside one solver, so no default factor arises.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: losses printed (three fewer solved; the slightly negative combination) with suspected causes, and open directions listed (pp. 27, 31–33).
- new-pattern candidate: keep neutral components in the development line, for interactions, for other instance classes and against overtuning (p. 32); possible tension with Heuristic 3 (a default has to earn its place), since the paper does not say whether the methods are on by default (my reading).
- new-pattern candidate: ablate the working limits themselves, to show they cost almost no reductions and save time and memory (p. 32).
- (Heuristic 8 ✅: five seeds, each instance–seed pair one observation, brackets over instances solved by at least one run, exclusions named by reason (pp. 26–27).)

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Prove first which pairs can help (Lemma 3.1: only opposite-sign shared coefficients can beat single-row FBBT), then hash rows by the sign pattern of variable pairs so that only such pairs are examined (pp. 9, 25).
- (D4) Give an expensive presolver a failure budget linear in the problem size, refunded on success but never negative, so it continues into useful regions and quits early otherwise (p. 24).
- (D4) Refuse reductions that destroy structure other components exploit (integral or ±1 coefficients, set-packing types, locks) and cap scale factors (p. 24).
- (D5) Measure each new presolver alone against a baseline with all new ones off, then all together, and count instances with reductions in both settings (Tables 1–9, pp. 26–32).
- (D5) When a method is neutral on MIPLIB, show the instance class where it matters with the same settings and seeds (pp. 28, 30–31).

**Quotes**:
- "In most publications there is no answer to this question." (p. 3)
- "Finally, it is also important to actively involve new presolve methods in the development process of a solver to avoid overtuning of established methods." (p. 32)

### S043 · Using two-dimensional projections for stronger separation and propagation of bilinear terms (SIAM Journal on Optimization 30(2) 2020, DOI 10.1137/19m1249825; arXiv 1903.05521)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (10 Jan 2020) of ZIB-Report 19-15; pp. 3–26 read, the proof of Theorem 1 (pp. 12–14) skimmed; references (pp. 26–30) consulted only for [21], [22], [34], [36]; electronic-supplement Tables 3–5 (pp. 31–72) not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Benjamin Müller (first), Felipe Serrano · **Place in the trajectory**: MINLP components in SCIP; uses Gleixner et al. [21] (OBBT enhancements, J. Global Optim. 67; the skill's [OBBT17]) and [22] (Lagrangian variable bounds, CPAIOR 2013; [LVB13]) (pp. 8, 18, 21, 28).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: McCormick inequalities are the envelopes of xy only over the box; over a strict non-rectangular subset the convex hull of the graph is much tighter and bounds improve too (abstract; pp. 3–5). Entry: Locatelli's algorithm gives envelopes over an explicit polytope, but such two-variable inequalities are rarely in the model; they may be implied, as in crudeoil_lee1_05 (pp. 5–6).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: compute a relaxation P_ij of the projection of the LP relaxation onto (x_i, x_j) with at most four LPs akin to OBBT, run right after OBBT, each shooting a ray from the box center to a box vertex; read the separating inequality off the LP duals (pp. 9–11); use P_ij for Locatelli's envelopes in separation (p. 17) and for best-possible bounds of x_i x_j, x_i, x_j over P_ij in propagation (pp. 17–19).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: nonconvex MIQCP (1) with bounded variables, reformulated with X_ij = x_i x_j (pp. 3–4), extending to bilinear products in factorable MINLP reformulations (p. 4); X a polyhedral relaxation available in LP-based spatial branch and bound (p. 9); Lemma 1 and Theorem 1 need bounds just computed by OBBT over X (pp. 11–12); terms only in convex constraints or with binaries ignored (p. 21). Test set: MINLPLib, 1,671 of 1,682 instances usable (p. 21).

### S047 · On the relation between the extended supporting hyperplane algorithm and Kelley's cutting plane algorithm (Journal of Global Optimization 78(1) 2020, DOI 10.1007/s10898-020-00906-y; arXiv 1905.08157)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v1 (20 May 2019) of ZIB-Report 19-18, pp. 1–18, all sections and proofs; the journal version not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Felipe Serrano (first), Robert Schwarz (all three at ZIB, p. 3) · **Place in the trajectory**: convex MINLP theory from the MINLP line; ideas developed at Dagstuhl Seminar 18081 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: Kronqvist et al. rediscovered Veinott's supporting hyperplane algorithm (ESH) and showed its benefits for convex MINLP (abstract, p. 3; p. 5); Kelley's gradient cuts need not support the feasible region (Example 1, Figure 1, pp. 5–6). Entry question: when is a gradient cut of a convex g supporting to {g ≤ 0}? (p. 5).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: characterise supporting linearisations (Theorem 1, pp. 8–9); note that sublinear functions always qualify (Example 2, p. 10); represent C by its gauge φ_C, whose evaluation is exactly ESH's line search (pp. 11, 13); conclude that ESH is Kelley's algorithm applied to min{cᵀx : φ_C(x) ≤ 1}, so its convergence follows from Kelley's (pp. 6, 14–15).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MICP min{cᵀx : x ∈ C ∩ (Z^p × R^{n−p})}, C closed convex given by convex differentiable g_j (p. 3); an interior point, taken as 0 (p. 14); condition (8), nonvanishing gradients of active constraints on ∂C, implied by Slater (p. 12); §6: C convex but described by nonconvex differentiable g_j under (8) (pp. 15–16); empty interior handled by the ε-relaxation of [9] (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: no implementation (n/a). Proof devices: an epigraph supporting-hyperplane argument for Theorem 1 (pp. 9–10); Examples 2–4 (sublinear; linear in one variable; a convex quadratic has supporting gradient cuts iff b ∉ R(A)) (pp. 10–11); gauge properties and the boundary point x̄/φ_C(x̄) (Propositions 4, 6; Lemma 5) (pp. 11–12); Lemma 8 and Theorem 9 (normalised supporting cuts are subgradients of the gauge) (pp. 14–15); Proposition 10 (validity for nonconvex representations by a directional-derivative contradiction) (p. 16). Numerics: reformulating with the gauge can break ε-feasibility, so the gauge is used only for separation (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: none; practical relevance argued from the experiments of [9] (p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Veinott [15] and Kronqvist et al. [9] reread through Kelley [8]; OA, ECP and projection-based cuts placed in the Horst–Tuy frame of which relaxation and where to cut (pp. 7–8); §6 related to Lasserre and Dutta–Lalitha (p. 6).
- **D7 Writing**: a circle and its gauge open the argument (p. 5); the characterisation is offered modestly (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: theory: a characterisation, a unifying reinterpretation, and convergence for convex sets with nonconvex differentiable representations (pp. 16–17). Limits: no closed form of the gauge in general, and subgradients matter more than evaluations (pp. 12, 16); the general algorithm of Remark 11 need not reach a KKT point (p. 16). No future work stated (pp. 16–17).

**Method links**:
- None of Methods 1–6 applies: a theory paper without experiments, certificates or benchmarks (pp. 3–17).
- new-pattern candidate: re-derive a rival's successful algorithm as a classic one on a reformulation, so that its convergence is inherited and its domain widened (pp. 6, 14–16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) To test whether a cutting-plane rule is new, look for a reformulation under which it becomes a classic method; the classic's convergence proof then applies (pp. 14–15).
- (D4) Characterise when a linearisation supports a sublevel set by an affinity condition along a segment, then sort function families by it (pp. 8–11).
- (D4) Before reformulating a constraint for theory, check what the reformulation does to tolerance-based feasibility; use it only implicitly if it breaks ε-feasibility (p. 12).

### S067 · Accelerating domain propagation: An efficient gpu-parallel algorithm over sparse matrices (2020 IEEE/ACM 10th Workshop on Irregular Applications: Architectures and Algorithms (IA3) 2020, DOI 10.1109/ia351965.2020.00007; arXiv 2009.07785)
**Read level**: full, of a later version. Text read: arXiv v5 (25 Aug 2021), the extended version, pp. 1–22 including Appendices A–D; the IA3 paper itself is not in the file. Appendix D lists the differences to the conference version (code extended, bug fixes, a missing compiler flag that slowed the conference version's baseline, improved numerics) (p. 22), so all pages and numbers below are v5's; cite as [S067 arXiv p. **Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Boro Sofranac (first; TU Berlin and ZIB), Sebastian Pokutta (TU Berlin and ZIB); Gleixner listed with HTW Berlin and ZIB (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an early paper (2020) of the GPU line the skill lists (Research Trajectory, 2020–2026, [CP21]); the work list notes that OPUS links the same arXiv id to the Parallel Computing 2022 article S057.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: domain propagation of linear constraints is single-threaded in state-of-the-art solvers because dynamic behaviour, dependency structure and irregular sparsity defeat parallel implementations (abstract, p. 1), while GPUs sit idle in MIP solving (p. 2). Entry: two named irregularities, the sequential algorithm's early termination and constraint marking, and a very sparse matrix with a few dense rows (pp. 4, 7).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: drop early termination and marking for a static round, all activities first and then bound candidates for every nonzero, a "breadth-first" instead of "depth-first" order (Algorithm 2, p. 6); compute activities like a sparse matrix–vector product with the CSR-adaptive scheme of Greathouse and Daga, adapted to two bound vectors and very long rows (pp. 7–8). - **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP with linear constraints β ≤ aᵀx ≤ β̄ and possibly infinite bounds, integer bounds rounded (pp. 1–2); iterated propagation converges to a unique limit point, not necessarily finitely (p. 3), so rounds are capped at 100 (p. 12). Data: MIPLIB 2017 collection, 1,065 instances; 987 readable; 893 converging to the same limit point; 786 with at least 1,000 variables or constraints, split into eight size sets (pp. 11–12).

- **D7 Writing**: the PaPILO comparison is called "not on equal grounds" and its check "not a rigorous way" (p. 17); the conclusion states why the result does not yet pay inside a solver (p. 18).
