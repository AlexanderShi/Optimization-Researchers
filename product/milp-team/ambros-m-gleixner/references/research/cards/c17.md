### S070 · Efficient separation of RLT cuts for implicit and explicit bilinear terms (Mathematical Programming 210(1) 2025, DOI 10.1007/s10107-024-02104-0; arXiv 2211.13545)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v3 (19 Jul 2024), which calls itself "the extended version" of the IPCO 2023 paper S056 (p. 1); pp. 1–22 read; references (pp. 23–25) and the per-instance tables of Appendix A (pp. 26–28) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Ksenia Bestuzheva (first and corresponding author, ZIB), Tobias Achterberg (Gurobi GmbH) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an MINLP-side cutting-plane component in SCIP, later than the "MINLP components to 2017" of the SKILL's field boundary, applied here to MILP as well and replicated in Gurobi 10.0 beta (pp. 13, 21); Achterberg appears as a co-author on a component, not only as a MIPLIB partner (p. 1). Funded by MODAL (p. 22). What the extension adds over the IPCO version is not stated in this text (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: RLT yields strong relaxations but very many cuts; even for bilinear terms the number of factors and cuts slows solvers, through separation cost and through LP size (p. 2). Two entries. (i) MILPs already contain bilinear product relations implicitly: a product of a binary and a bounded variable can be written as two linear constraints, so RLT becomes applicable to "a new class of problems" (abstract; pp. 2, 5). (ii) The cost of separating all RLT cuts is named the major practical bottleneck (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: three devices. (i) Detection: Theorem 1 derives a relation Axi + Bwij + Cxj + D ⋚ xixj from two linear constraints on the same three variables with xi binary, if b1b2 > 0 and γ = c2b1 − b2c1 ≠ 0 (pp. 5–6). Candidates come from implied relations hashed by variable triple, implied bounds, the clique table, two-variable constraints and global bounds (p. 7). A relation is kept only if a non-redundancy interval for xj meets its domain (pp. 8–9). (ii) Row marking: for each violated product, the sign of x*_i x*_j − w*_ij and the sign of the row coefficient decide which bound factor can raise the violation. Rows are marked LT, GT or BOTH, and only marked (row, factor) pairs are multiplied (pp. 9–11, Algorithm 1). (iii) Projection filtering: when a variable sits at a bound, McCormick is tight, so rows are projected onto the variables strictly inside their bounds and the projected cut's violation is checked first (pp. 11–13). Unchanged: the RLT linearization rules (McCormick, secants and tangents for squares, clique-implied products) (p. 4); one bound factor at a time; constraint factors not used (pp. 4–5). The method "detects and enforces bilinear relations that are already implicitly present in the model", where earlier approaches introduce multilinear relations by multiplication (p. 2).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: mixed-binary nonlinear programs in extended form with auxiliary variables wij for bilinear terms, (1a)–(1f) (p. 3). General integers are allowed but handled like continuous variables (p. 3). Implicit products need a binary multiplier and finite bounds on the other variable (pp. 2, 5). Beyond Theorem 1, two conditions: a nonzero coefficient of the binary (always enforced) and opposite signs of the binary's coefficients in the two rows (a parameter, on by default) (p. 7). Test sets: MINLPLib (1846 instances) and 666 MILPs from MIPLIB 3, 2003, 2010, 2017 and Cor@l; kept only if they contain or yield bilinear terms: 1357 MINLP and 195 MILP (p. 13). The equivalence behind projection filtering holds only when all McCormick inequalities are in the LP; SCIP separates them dynamically, so in SCIP it does not hold (pp. 11–12).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Theorem 1 is proved by writing the unknown relation and both constraints as implications for xi = 1 and xi = 0 and matching coefficients with scaling factors α, β of equal sign (p. 6). Redundancy is settled by algebra on the rewritten constraints, case by case for b1, b2 > 0 and b1, b2 < 0 (pp. 8–9). Products are stored with uplocks and downlocks, after Achterberg's thesis (p. 8). Row marks are bit flags in sorted sparse arrays (p. 10). Projection filtering is argued in two cases: if McCormick holds, testing the projected cut is equivalent to testing the full cut; if not, violations of products with a variable at a bound are ignored, which in effect prioritises McCormick cuts (pp. 12–13). Settings: at most 20 unknown bilinear terms per row-factor product; separation every 10 nodes; one round in non-root nodes and 10 at the root; an RLT-only cut selection by efficacy and parallelism before RLT cuts compete with other cuts (pp. 13–14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP development branch (githash dd6c54a9d7) with SoPlex 5.0.2.4, CppAD, PaPILO 1.0.0.1, bliss 0.73p and Ipopt 3.12.11; Xeon E5-2670 v2 at 2.50 GHz, 64 GB; one hour; gap tolerance 10^-4 for MINLP and 10^-6 for MILP (p. 13). Shifted geometric means with shifts 1 s and 100 nodes, on "clean" instances only (all settings successful), with affected (LP iterations differ), [x,timelim] and all-optimal subsets (p. 14). Permutations: failures are counted "including permuted instances" (p. 14) and Table A1 uses "4 permutations" (p. 26), but the setup section does not say how many versions per instance enter Tables 1–10 (p. 13). The MILP counts fit the original plus four permutations: 195 × 5 = 975 = 971 clean + 4 failures (my arithmetic, Table 1, p. 15). Failures are printed per setting (MILP 3 for Off, 1 for IERLT; MINLP 155, 161, 158) and put down to numerical errors and memory limits (p. 14). Results (time ratios new/old):
  - MILP, Off against IERLT: 1.03 on clean, 0.98 on [100,timelim], 0.79 on [1000,timelim] (Table 1, p. 15).
  - MINLP, Off against ERLT: 0.85 on clean, 0.13 on [1000,timelim]; 123 more instances solved (Table 2, p. 15).
  - Implicit products on MINLP: 1.01 on clean, 1.09 on [100,timelim], called "slightly detrimental" (Table 3, pp. 15–16). Root dual bounds still favour IERLT over ERLT (Table 4, p. 16).
  - Row marking against standard separation: 0.37 on clean MILP, with 780 → 890 solved, and 0.88 on MINLP (Table 5, p. 17). The share of time spent in RLT separation falls from 54.6% to 2.8% (MILP) and from 16.7% to 2.6% (MINLP); failures fall from 16 to 0 and from 77 to 16 (Table 6, p. 18).
  - Redundancy filtering: 1–7% faster on every subset except the 50 hardest MINLPs, where it is 10% slower; 3 more MILPs and 4 fewer MINLPs solved (Tables 7–8, pp. 18–19). Projection filtering: time ratios within 3% on every subset (Tables 9–10, pp. 19–20). A control run on a reduced instance set with all McCormick inequalities forced leaves projection neutral (+2% time) (p. 20). Effects by instance size are not monotonic (Table 11, p. 21).
  - Gurobi 10.0 beta on its internal test set (customer and public models, built to avoid over-representing any one problem class), 10,000 s: MILP 0.99 on all and 0.89 on [1000,timelim]; MINLP 0.73 and 0.12; instances solved by only one setting, off +41 against on +37 (MILP) and off +2 against on +35 (MINLP) (Table 12, pp. 21–22).
  - No significance test. Some always-time-limit instances were dropped for the redundancy experiment, without a count (p. 18).
  - Slips: the text gives separation shares of 54.2% and 15.1% → 2.4%, where Table 6 prints 54.6, 16.7 and 2.6 (pp. 17–18). Table 8 lists 288 affected instances with 586 solved (p. 19). Tables 7–8 have 1638 MILP and 708 MINLP clean instances, not reconciled with the 195- and 1357-instance sets beyond a rerun of detection and dropped time-limit instances (p. 18). On the hardest MINLPs, projection causes a "slowdown of 7%" in the text (p. 20), where Table 10 prints time 1.03 and nodes 1.07 and the previous page says "2 and 3%" (p. 19). For Gurobi MILP the solved-only counts favour off (41 against 37), while the text says "no slowdown is observed with Gurobi on any subset of MILP instances" (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: continues Adams and Sherali's RLT and its hierarchies (p. 2). Earlier ways to cut the number of RLT cuts are restated, not criticised: Sherali and Tuncbilek's sign-based subsets, reduced RLT (Liberti; Sherali, Dalkiran and Liberti), compact and inductive linearization (Liberti; Mallach) (p. 2). The novelty is placed against approaches that create products by multiplication [4, 5] (p. 2). MIPLIB 2017 appears only as an instance source (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: two contributions announced as "first" and "second" (pp. 2–3). Each table gets a mechanism sentence; for example, more nodes under row marking are put down to faster separation and checked on the all-optimal subset (p. 17). Results are written as per-subset percentages, negative findings plainly (p. 15). Instance groups that gain or lose from implicit-product cuts are named (csched*, lectsched*, tr12-30*, smallinvSNPr*) (p. 16). - **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic (detection, filtering, separation) and computational (two independent implementations) (p. 22). Printed limits: implicit-product cuts help only challenging MILPs and slightly hurt MINLP on average, so their use "depends on the problem" (p. 16); projection filtering is nearly neutral and may keep useful cuts out of the pool (p. 20). No future work is stated (p. 22). Whether any feature is on by default in SCIP or Gurobi is not stated.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2/D4) Find hidden structure in pairs of model rows by writing both rows and the target relation as implications on a binary variable and matching coefficients; then drop derived relations that are redundant over the variable's domain (pp. 5–9).
- (D4) Before separating a large cut family, mark the (row, factor) pairs that can raise the violation, using only the sign of the current violation and the signs of row coefficients, and separate only those (pp. 9–11).
- (D5) Report component effects on clean instances, with difficulty brackets, the affected subset and an all-optimal subset for nodes, and print each setting's failure count with its cause (p. 14).
- (D5) When a mechanism is suspected behind a neutral result, run a control that removes it (here: force all McCormick inequalities) and print the outcome even when it is null (p. 20).
- (D5) Replicate a component's effect with an independent implementation in a second solver and report the agreement of tendencies without pooling numbers across setups (pp. 21–22).
- (D5) Judge a separation-speed technique by the share of solving time it takes and how that share is distributed over instances, not only by mean time (Table 6, pp. 17–18).

### S072 · Analyzing the numerical correctness of branch-and-bound decisions for mixed-integer programming (CPAIOR 2025, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-95976-9_3; arXiv 2412.14710)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v3 (3 Apr 2025); pp. 1–15 read; references (pp. 15–17) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Alexander Hoen (first author, ZIB) (p. 1); Gleixner second of two (ZIB and HTW Berlin) · **Place in the trajectory**: the SKILL's [NC25], the main practice evidence for Method 1. It reuses FPEasy and NumDiff from Cook, Koch, Steffy and Wolter's exact MIP solver [6] (p. 8), runs the checks with SoPlex's exact LP [13, 14] (p. 7) and compares results with exact SCIP [6, 8, 9] (p. 9). Code at github.com/alexhoen/bnbanalyzer (pp. 2, 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: floating-point solvers decide with tolerances, and commercial ones stop at relative gaps of 0.01% to 0.0001% (p. 2). Wrong answers are documented, and MIPLIB 2017 tags 193 instances "numerics" (p. 2). Yet "it is widely accepted" that mature solvers handle numerics robustly (p. 2). The question: to what extent are the individual decisions of floating-point LP-based branch and bound correct in exact arithmetic, and when the final answer is right, is that because every decision was right or because the wrong ones did not matter (p. 2)? The authors know of no systematic study; existing work compares final results only, for example [7] (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: check every leaf a posteriori, whether it was closed as (a) LP infeasible, (b) pruned by its bound or (c) integer feasible (p. 2), with a ladder of increasingly expensive checks: safe dual bounding, rational reconstruction by continued fractions, exact rational LU factorization of the floating-point solver's basis, and exact LP with Exact-SoPlex last (pp. 6–7). Only pruning decisions are critical. Branching-variable and node-order choices are heuristic, and "any sequence of branching decisions yields a correct branch-and-bound process" (p. 4). The solver is not rewritten: SCIP is configured to mimic the pure LP-based branch and bound of [6] and observed through events (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIPs with rational data, min cᵀx s.t. Ax ≥ b, bounds, integrality (p. 3). Pure LP-based branch and bound, configured to mimic [6] (p. 7), with only "simple presolving steps": model cleanup, singleton rows turned into bounds, and limited constraint propagation in rational arithmetic to reduce infinite bounds (p. 8). Error classes (pp. 4–5):
  - solution errors: weak if the node's LP is exactly infeasible, strong otherwise;
  - bound errors: weak if a later exactly feasible solution justifies the pruning, strong otherwise;
  - gap errors, weak and strong;
  - infeasibility errors.
  Bound checks treat the primal bound registered in the floating-point solver at that moment as valid (p. 6). Test sets: FPEasy (57) and NumDiff (50) from [6] (p. 8). Safe bounding needs finite variable bounds; with infinite ones the bound becomes minus infinity (p. 6).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: the safe bound computes the dual residual ε̂ = c − Aᵀŷ − r̂⁺ + r̂⁻ exactly and shifts it into the bound duals (eqs. (1)–(2)). This gives an exactly feasible dual solution whose bound is weakened by the bound-weighted shift; the same works for Farkas proofs (p. 6). Factorization is skipped at LP-infeasible nodes, which have no basis (p. 7). Primal conversion fixes the integers to their rounded values and solves the remaining LP exactly with Exact-SoPlex (p. 7). Instrumentation: the events BESTSOLUTION, NODEINFEASIBLE, NODEDELETE and NODEFEASIBLE; at NODEDELETE the dual LP is re-solved in floating point (p. 8). The minimum ẑ of the safe bounds at all error leaves certifies that the optimum lies in [ẑ, z*] (p. 6). No proofs beyond the validity of the dual correction (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP 9.3 (hash b7906251d5); SoPlex 8.0 (hash 7b9bd461) as both the floating-point and the exact LP solver (p. 7). Xeon E7-8880 v4 at 2.20 GHz; 10,800 s per instance, including the evaluation of the leaves (p. 8). Each instance is run original and permuted (p. 8).
  - Table 1 (p. 9). FPEasy, both versions: 49 correct, 6 correct time-outs, 2 dual fails (vpm2 still correct, dano3_4 slightly suboptimal). NumDiff original: 11 correct, 20 fails (12 primal, 9 dual), 14 correct time-outs, 5 failed time-outs. Two MIPLIB instances that timed out before reaching any leaf are "labeled as correct" (p. 9).
  - Per-instance counts of weak and strong errors (Tables 2–4, pp. 10–12). For example, vpm2 has 39 and 178 wrong leaves among about 690,000 and 650,000 (p. 10), and alu16_5 has 875 infeasibility errors in a time-out (p. 12). Exact SCIP (hash c7bd4be7b9, 18,800 s) is printed beside the floating-point results: the alu instances are infeasible or have large gaps (Table 5, p. 13).
  - The obvious remedy was tested: with the feasibility tolerance tightened from 10^-6 to 10^-9, SCIP finds exactly feasible or convertible solutions, or times out, on six listed instances, but still produces slightly infeasible solutions on alu16_7 and ns1859355; dano3_4's gap errors disappear; node counts rise, and blend2 can no longer be verified (p. 12). This run is reported in prose only, without a table (p. 12).
  - Which check decided each leaf (Table 6, p. 14, seed 0 only). Floating-point techniques settle 93.76% (FPEasy) and 99.69% (NumDiff) of leaves; exact LP 2.85% and 0.27%. On instances with unbounded variables, exact LP rises to 29.89% and 3.86%. Error rates are 0.00020% and 0.0054% of leaves.
  - Not reported: aggregated times or node counts, and the cost of the analysis. Since the time limit includes the analysis (p. 8), time-outs mix solving and checking.
  - Slips:
    - Table 1's NumDiff permuted row sums to 51 instances (13 + 18 + 13 + 7). The text's "25 (original) and 27 (permuted)" fully correct, with 14 time-outs "On each seed" (p. 9), does not match that row's 13 correct time-outs.
    - The text says "alu10 9 produces 46 infeasibility errors" (p. 10), where Table 3 lists the 46 for alu16_1 (p. 11).
    - Exact LP calls rise "from 0.06% to 3.86% on NumDiff" (p. 14), where Table 6 prints 0.27%.
    - The text says only ns1859355 and vpm2 have a correct result despite wrong decisions (p. 10). Table 5 shows equal floating-point and exact values also for tkat3K, tkat3T, tkat3TV and tkatTV5 (p. 13), which have bound errors in Table 3 (p. 11); which notion of correct result is meant is not said.
- **D6 Positioning**: builds on safe bounds (Neumaier and Shcherbina [17]; Steffy and Wolter [21]), rational reconstruction [18, 20, 23, 25] and SoPlex's exact LP [13, 14] (pp. 6–7). For documented wrong answers it cites Cook et al. [6], Klotz [15], Paxian and Biere's fuzzing of MaxSAT solvers [19] and Steffy's thesis [22] (p. 2). The study is set against comparisons of final results [7] (p. 2), and the outlook connects it to VIPR [5] (p. 15). The belief is restated, not attacked (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: guiding questions open the computational section, in prose (p. 7). Error classes are defined before any data (pp. 4–5). The conclusion reads the result two ways, "On the one hand" robustness quantified, "On the other hand" a route to exact solving with the floating-point solver as a grey box (pp. 14–15). Hedges: "typically single-digit" (abstract; p. 14); "Surprisingly" for the 93.76–99.69% (p. 14); "significant" without a test (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: methodological and empirical, presented as a first a posteriori analysis of all critical decisions, with public code (pp. 2, 8). Printed limits: pure LP-based branch and bound only; exactly infeasible subproblems where slightly violated solutions trigger pruning are the troubling case, and tighter tolerances help only partly (pp. 12, 14); unbounded variables drive the share of exact LP calls (p. 14). Future work: partial certificates verifiable in VIPR, completed by re-exploring wrongly discarded subproblems with higher precision or an exact MIP solver; extend the analysis to presolving and cutting planes before it can compete with exact MIP solving (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (the SKILL's [NC25]), step by step:
  - the belief is stated (p. 2);
  - a per-decision check is defined (pp. 4–7);
  - the production solver is observed through events, not rewritten (pp. 7–8);
  - one set where the belief should hold and one built to break it, each also permuted (p. 8);
  - counts per error class (Tables 1–4, pp. 9–12);
  - the mechanism is named and the obvious remedy tested (pp. 12, 14);
  - the conclusion is read in favour of robustness (p. 14).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (numbers): the SKILL's "25 (original) and 27 (permuted) of 50" (Method 1 step 5) follows the text of p. 9. Table 1's permuted row gives 13 + 13 = 26 and sums to 51 instances (p. 9).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: cheapest check first, exact LP only when everything else fails (pp. 6–7). The floating-point techniques decide 93.76–99.69% of leaves (p. 14). Rational propagation is used only to shrink infinite bounds so that floating-point safe bounding succeeds (p. 8).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: no certificate is produced yet, but the analysis audits the reasoning: weak errors are recorded although "justified in hindsight" (p. 5). Partial VIPR certificates are future work (p. 15).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the printed limits become the next steps (partial certificates, completion, presolving and cutting planes) (pp. 14–15).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (step 7): the predecessor's FPEasy and NumDiff are reused (p. 8), but no harder public set is added.
- Taste 4 ✅ (card only): for most industrial applications slightly suboptimal or slightly infeasible solutions may be acceptable, while chip-design verification, combinatorial auctions and computational proofs need certainty (p. 2).
- Common Misreading 1 ✅: the overwhelming majority of decisions are exactly correct (abstract; p. 14).
- Inner Tensions (significance testing) ✅: "significant" without a test (p. 12).
- new-pattern candidate: verify only the decisions that decide correctness (heuristic against critical decisions) (p. 4).
- new-pattern candidate: test the suspected mechanism's remedy and print the null (p. 12; name as in c15).
- new-pattern candidate: text-table slips in computational sections (negative) (pp. 9–11, 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Split an algorithm's decisions into heuristic ones (any choice keeps it correct: branching variable, node order) and critical ones (pruning, accepting a solution, declaring infeasibility), and verify only the critical ones (p. 4).
- (D3) Define error classes before measuring, each split into weak and strong by whether a later event justifies the decision in hindsight, so that a correct final answer can still be scored for wrong reasoning (pp. 4–5).
- (D4) Check each decision with a cost ladder: a floating-point safe bound from an exact dual-residual correction, then rational reconstruction, then exact factorization of the solver's own basis, then exact LP; report which rung decided each leaf (pp. 6–7; Table 6, p. 14).
- (D4) Judge each pruning decision from the solver's own state at the time (its registered primal bound), so that one error type does not contaminate the count of another (p. 6).
- (D5) Run one set where the belief should hold and one built to break it, each original and permuted, and report per-instance leaf counts per error class (pp. 8–12).
- (D5) Test the obvious remedy (a tighter feasibility tolerance) and report its side effects too (more nodes, a newly unverifiable instance) (p. 12).

### S073 · A diving heuristic for mixed-integer problems with unbounded semi-continuous variables (EURO Journal on Computational Optimization 13, 100107, 2025, DOI 10.1016/j.ejco.2025.100107; arXiv 2403.19411)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (16 Oct 2024; the title page says October 2024); pp. 1–15 read (body, acknowledgments); references (pp. 15–19) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Katrin Halbig (first and corresponding author, FAU Erlangen-Nürnberg), Alexander Hoen (ZIB), Jakob Witzig (SAP SE), Dieter Weninger (FAU) (p. 1); Gleixner third of five · **Place in the trajectory**: the SAP supply-chain line. Reference [25] (Gamrath, Gleixner, Koch et al. 2019) describes very similar instances (pp. 10, 17), and SAP is thanked for "its long-term support" (pp. 14–15). For non-indicator candidates the heuristic uses Farkas diving from Witzig and Gleixner's conflict-driven heuristics [46] (p. 7; DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2020.0973, p. 19), which the SKILL lists as [CDH20], "not read".

- **D1 Problem and entry**: semi-continuous variables y ∈ {0} ∪ [s, u], with u possibly infinite (p. 2). For u = ∞ the convex hull is not a bounded MIP-representable set, so no linear mixed-binary formulation exists (p. 4). Practice leaves lot sizes unbounded: no feasible finite bound is known or of interest, and finite maximum lot sizes cause numerical instability in strategic supply-chain planning (p. 4). Big-M needs a bound; a "huge" M can cut off optimal solutions, and 1/M can fall to machine accuracy or below the solver's tolerances (pp. 4–5). Most of the literature treats only the bounded case (p. 3). NP-hardness, by reduction from 0-1 integer programming, motivates a heuristic (p. 3). The paper keeps indicator constraints although their LP relaxation is "even worse than the LP relaxation of the big-M-formulation" (p. 6), because of big-M's numerical issues and possible cutoff of optimal solutions (p. 6).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: Indicator Diving (ID).
  - Candidates are the fractional integer variables plus each integral, unfixed indicator variable whose indicator constraint the LP solution violates (p. 6; Algorithm 1, p. 7).
  - An indicator candidate scores 100·(s − ŷ)/s when ŷ ∈ (0, s), and −1 otherwise. Other candidates are scored by an existing strategy (Farkas diving in the experiments), mapped by an order-preserving sigmoid into [−300, −100], so indicators always come first (pp. 7–8).
  - The indicator is fixed to 1 if ŷ ≥ 0.5 s and to 0 otherwise. The bound on one variable thus depends on the LP value of another, which is where ID differs from known diving heuristics (p. 8).
  - Unchanged: SCIP's generic diving framework with optional propagation, LP re-solves, rounding and backtracking (pp. 6–8).
  - ID is presented as a transfer of the semi-continuous branching of Beale and of de Farias [8, 21] (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MILPs with semi-continuous (and semi-integer) variables, unit and set-up costs, in the indicator formulation (5)–(6) (pp. 2–5); the LP relaxation drops the indicator constraints (pp. 5–6). Three test sets (p. 10):
  - SCM: 588 real-world SAP supply-chain instances, not public;
  - Cellphone: 192 artificial instances built by SAP from real-world models, public [38];
  - Miplib: all 42 MIPLIB 2017 collection instances with indicator constraints, not filtered further.
  Table 1 counts the motivating structure before and after presolve (pp. 10–11). Presolve bounds almost all unbounded semi-continuous variables: on SCM, 315,083 indicator constraints of unbounded variables become 276; on Cellphone, all of them become bounded. Many big bounds (10^4 < u < ∞) remain. The Miplib instances have no big or infinite bounds at all. The line at 10^4 is drawn because SCIP adds y ≤ uz for smaller bounds (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: a SCIP plugin heur_indicatordiving.c on SCIP's generic diving framework (p. 8). A random factor among the equal −1 scores removes dependence on the input order (p. 10). ID is called only at the root, after the LP (p. 9). Proof devices: n/a, apart from the NP-hardness reduction (p. 3).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: setup and metrics.
  - Pre-release SCIP 9.0 (7cc9c068bc) with SoPlex 6.0.3 (555f5d54), single-threaded; Xeon Gold 6326 at 2.9 GHz, 32 GB (pp. 8–9).
  - Metrics: primal gap and primal integral, after Berthold [11], averaged as shifted geometric means with shift 1 (p. 9).
  - Three seeds, each instance-seed pair one observation: 1764, 576 and 126 runs (p. 10).

  Root-node experiment (stop after the root or at 5 h):
  - Calls: ID ran on 1300, 463 and 99 runs and found a solution on 1052, 365 and 16; its solution was still best at the end of the root on 287, 55 and 7 (Table 2, pp. 11–12).
  - Primal gap, with ID against without: ratio 0.79, 0.92 and 0.95 at the end of the root, and 0.30, 0.46 and 0.50 at the moment ID's solution is passed to SCIP (Table 2, pp. 11–12).
  - Premise check: in the first iteration, 13% (SCM) and 14% (Cellphone) of indicator variables are candidates, with 60% and 99% of their semi-continuous variables in the forbidden range (0, s) (p. 12).
  - Wins and losses at a 10^-4 threshold: 432/154, 106/20 and 17/8 (Fig. 1, pp. 12–13).

  Tree search (20 min, no node limit):
  - Primal integral ratios: 0.86 and 0.83 (SCM, called and found), 0.97 and 0.98 (Cellphone), 0.98 and 0.92 (Miplib). Mean time in ID is at most 4.456 s (Table 3, p. 13).
  - Solving-time ratios: 1.01 (SCM), 0.91 (Cellphone), 0.99 (Miplib). Time-outs on SCM: 277 with ID against 262 without (Table 3, p. 13). The variation in time-outs is put down to performance variability "of the subsequent processes of SCIP", without a test (pp. 13–14).

  Instances where ID was not called are left out and counted (pp. 11–12). No significance tests.
  - Slip: the summary "As the total solving time is neutral or increases" (p. 14) conflicts with Table 3's 9–10% decrease on Cellphone (p. 13).
- **D6 Positioning**: places itself in the literature on semi-continuous variables (bound strengthening, branching, cuts, convex hulls, heuristics), which is mostly limited to the bounded case except [5, 21, 22] (p. 3). Indicator-constraint handling follows Belotti et al. and Bonami et al. (p. 5). ID is set against the diving heuristics of [10, 46, 47] and claims a difference only in the cross-variable bound rule (pp. 6, 8). Earlier work is restated, not criticised (pp. 3, 5–6).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: contributions are listed by section (p. 3), and a complexity argument justifies a heuristic (p. 3). The paper is candid about its data: the Miplib subset is used unfiltered (p. 10), and those instances lack the property that motivated ID (p. 11). ### S079 · Clique Probing for Mixed-Integer Programs (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2512.17551)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (1 Jan 2026); pp. 1–10 read, plus the per-instance Table 2 (p. 12); references (pp. 10–11) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Jacob von Holly-Ponientzietz (first author, ZIB), Alexander Hoen (ZIB and HTW Berlin), Mark Turner (ZIB) (p. 1); Gleixner last of four · **Place in the trajectory**: a new routine in PaPILO, the presolve library of Gleixner, Gottwald and Hoen [7] (the SKILL's [PaPILO]) (pp. 5, 11), measured inside a SCIP 10.0 development version (p. 7). The work list gives it as an arXiv preprint; the text names no venue.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: probing matters. In Achterberg et al. [2], disabling it slowed instances over 1000 s by 7% to 33% and made 11 of 3155 models unsolvable (p. 1). Probing every variable is too slow, so solvers rank variables by a score and stop early, yet no published work describes the scores or the termination criteria (p. 2). Solvers exploit structure elsewhere (clique tables, SOS constraints, knapsack covers), "to the best of our knowledge" not yet in probing (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: probe a clique's assignments, not its variables (p. 3).
  - An at-most-one clique of n binaries admits n + 1 assignments (n for exactly-one), against 2n propagations for single-variable probing.
  - Advantages claimed: (a) fewer probing and propagation calls; (b) faster propagation, since several variables are fixed at once; (c) tighter bounds for cliques of more than two variables; (d) the infeasible all-zero assignment of exactly-one cliques is exploited.
  - Definition 2 adapts the four probing rules (infeasibility, bounds, substitution, implications). If the all-zero assignment is infeasible, the clique is upgraded to exactly-one.
  - Unchanged: PaPILO's probing score, and standard probing on the variables clique probing did not touch (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: binary variables in explicit cliques of the presolved model. PaPILO keeps no clique table and SCIP does not share its own, so only cliques that appear explicitly are used (p. 10). PaPILO runs on a problem that SCIP has already reduced, possibly several times (pp. 6–7). Test set: MIPLIB 2017 benchmark (240) with four seeds each, minus the instances solved by neither setting on any of the four seeds within 2 h and the instances where PaPILO finds no clique: 93 instances, 372 runs (p. 7).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Data structure: instead of storing all bounds after each assignment, six values per variable: minimum lower and maximum upper bound, the second-lowest and second-highest values, and the argmin and argmax (p. 4). They also give an upper bound on the reductions still possible, used to abort early (p. 4).
  - Algorithms 1–2: sort cliques by average variable score; skip cliques with more than 50% overlap; stop after two consecutive unsuccessful cliques; disable clique probing for later presolve rounds if it found nothing; then continue standard probing (pp. 4–6).
  - Implementation: PaPILO's transaction-based architecture; an optional parallel loop with thread-local copies of the bound data, synchronised in batches without locks (pp. 5–6).
  - Parameters: clique size at most 150; at most 3000 variables per run; initial batch of 2; abort when fewer than three reductions per propagation are found twice in a row; a work limit (p. 7).
  - Proof devices: n/a; the rules are stated, not proved (p. 3).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Builds: PaPILO 3.0 (0e1165d6) against default PaPILO (32328a5c, "a verbose variant of d808e1f3"); SCIP 10.0 development version (80c1fbfe1b); Xeon E7-8880 v4 at 2.20 GHz (p. 7). Runs are sequential "to determine the pure algorithmic advantage of clique probing" (p. 6). Whether the clique-probing build branches from the baseline commit is not stated (p. 7).
  - First probing call: Figure 1 plots reductions with and without clique probing on log scales, omitting instances with identical counts whose runtime differs by less than 5% or 0.2 s (p. 8). Two outliers are explained by mechanism: on square47, two large cliques take long and yield 41 bound changes; on net12, an unfavourable clique choice ends clique probing early, while default probing is "lucky" and probes 1600 variables instead of 250 (p. 8). Gains are named per instance, for example neos8 fixings 1223 → 21,340 (pp. 8–9; Table 2, p. 12). Overall, "5% more reductions per second of presolving" (p. 9).
  - Solver: shifted geometric means with shifts 1 for time and 100 for nodes (p. 9). Table 1 (p. 10): 324 against 321 solved. Time ratio (default/clique) 1.03 on all, 1.06 on affected (340 of 372), 1.06 at ≥ 100 s and 1.08 at ≥ 1000 s; node ratios 1.01–1.06. The per-instance table is in the repository (p. 7). No significance test; no failure count.
  - Measured or argued: Table 2 shows more propagations with clique probing on most listed instances (for example air05 20 → 392, square47 20 → 377), and reductions per propagation fall on some (s250r10 4.3 → 1.6) (p. 12). Its caption names a Props/s column that the extracted table lacks (p. 12). So advantage (a), fewer propagation calls (p. 3), is argued, not measured.
  - Slips: square47's probing time is 68.5 s in the text (p. 8) and 69.5 s in Table 2 (p. 12); neos8's fixings start "from 1233" in the text (p. 8) and from 1223 in Table 2 (p. 12); "The first two questions" introduces a list of two (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: builds on Savelsbergh's probing [11] and Achterberg's thesis [1] (p. 1). Probing in SCIP and HiGHS is summarised: scores, absolute or relative limits, a stall criterion (p. 2). The sources cited there are the SCIP 9.0 release report and Huangfu and Hall's paper on the parallel dual simplex method (references, p. 11). Together with the claim that no published work describes scores or termination criteria (p. 2), this suggests the summary comes from the codes (inferred). Novelty is claimed "to the best of our knowledge" (p. 2). No criticism of predecessors (pp. 1–2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: a short paper with two questions before the study (p. 6). Claims are hedged with "Generally" and "slightly" (p. 9). Outliers are explained by mechanism (p. 8). The paper explains why small reduction gains can matter: presolvers run in rounds and interact, and presolve runs again in sub-MIPs and after restarts (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithmic and software (PaPILO). Printed limits: explicit cliques only; implications cannot be passed to the solver (pp. 6, 10); an abort discards a clique's progress (p. 9). Future work: similar probing on other structures, such as SOS2 constraints (p. 10). Whether clique probing is on by default is not stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: two printed limits with their causes (no clique table in PaPILO, no implication interface) and a named next structure (SOS2) (p. 10). The paper also starts from a gap in the documentation of practice: unpublished probing scores and termination rules (p. 2).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: like-for-like by build control, not by stripping features. The baseline is the same library at a named commit, built verbose so reductions can be counted, and runs are sequential so parallelism does not confound the comparison (pp. 6–7). No reduced-and-default pair applies.
- Heuristic H6 ✅ (card only): questions before the study (p. 6).
- Heuristic H8 ✅ (card only): seed combinations as observations, difficulty brackets, the affected subset, and exclusion rules with counts (93 instances, 372 runs) (pp. 7, 9); the excluded instances are not named.
- Heuristic H9 ✅ (card only): clique structure that the presolved model already carries cuts probing work (pp. 2–3, 9).
- Workflow D step 1 and Workflow E step 1 ✅ (card only): surprising outliers are explained (p. 8), and per-instance results are archived in the repository (p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate: new component as a front end to the incumbent, not a rival (clique probing runs first, standard probing continues on the rest) (p. 5; name as in c04).
- new-pattern candidate: success-gated execution (switched off for later rounds when it finds nothing; abort after two unsuccessful cliques) (pp. 5–7; name as in c13).
- new-pattern candidate: text-table slips in computational sections (negative) (pp. 6, 8, 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2) Count the assignments a structure allows (n + 1 for an at-most-one clique, n for exactly-one) and probe assignments instead of single variables (p. 3).
- (D4) Per variable, keep the extreme and second-extreme bound values with their indices instead of every propagated bound vector. They yield the final reductions and an upper bound on the remaining ones, usable for early termination (p. 4).
- (D4) Run a new presolver first, let the incumbent routine continue on what it did not touch, and switch the new one off for later rounds when it finds nothing (pp. 5–6).
- (D5) Compare against the same library at a named commit, built verbose and run single-threaded, so that only the algorithm differs (pp. 6–7).
- (D5) In a scatter plot of reduction counts, omit only instances with identical counts and runtime within a stated tolerance, and explain each outlier by mechanism (p. 8).

**Quotes**:

- "Since PaPILO does not maintain a clique table, and SCIP does not share its clique table with PaPILO, we are currently limited to detecting only those cliques that appear explicitly in the model" (p. 10)

### S080 · Cut-based conflict analysis in mixed integer programming (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2025, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2024.0999; arXiv 2410.15110)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (19 Oct 2025); pp. 1–23 read; references (pp. 23–25) skimmed. The "Appendix D" that p. 17 refers to is not in this file.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Gioni Mexi (first author, ZIB), Felipe Serrano (COPT GmbH), Timo Berthold (FICO and TU Berlin), Jakob Nordström (University of Copenhagen and Lund University) (p. 1); Gleixner fourth of five · **Place in the trajectory**: the journal extension of the CP 2023 paper on pure binary programs by Mexi, Berthold, Gleixner and Nordström (pp. 3–4), in the SKILL's line of pseudo-Boolean methods brought into MIP with Nordström; "Learn to relax" (the SKILL's [L2R21]) is cited for combining dual-proof and pseudo-Boolean conflict analysis (pp. 3, 24). Code and results are in the INFORMS JoC GitHub repository (pp. 18, 25).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: MIP solvers learn from local infeasibilities with graph-based conflict analysis, which corresponds to resolution, a weak proof system with exponential lower bounds for simple principles, and works on clauses extracted from the linear constraints (p. 2). Pseudo-Boolean solvers (SAT4J, RoundingSat) do conflict analysis in the cutting-planes proof system, "exponentially more powerful" as a method of reasoning (p. 2). Entry: restate pseudo-Boolean conflict analysis in MIP terms, use MIP tools to improve it, and extend it beyond pure binary programs (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Key idea and design**:
  - The pseudo-Boolean "reduction" of the reason constraint (division or saturation) is a cut that separates the fractional vertex of the one-row LP (9) defined by the reason constraint (pp. 3, 9).
  - New cMIR-based reduction: complement the relaxable variables instead of weakening them, then apply MIR (Prop. 2, p. 12). It equals the Gomory mixed-integer cut from the optimal tableau of the one-row LP (Remark 2, p. 12). It dominates the earlier wMIR reduction and hence the Chvátal–Gomory reduction (Prop. 3, pp. 13–14).
  - Mixed binary programs: before reducing, resolve the non-relaxable continuous variables out of the reason constraint with their own reason constraints from the propagation history (Algorithm 3, Prop. 4, pp. 15–17).
  - General integers: no guarantee; heuristic aggregation plus general cMIR (pp. 17–18), with a fallback to graph-based analysis (p. 19).
  - Unchanged: the conflict-analysis loop to the first unique implication point (Algorithm 1, p. 10), and SCIP's length and age limits for conflicts (p. 21).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP (1) with real data (pp. 4–5), in three classes: pure binary programs, mixed binary programs (binary and continuous variables) and general MIP (p. 5). The mixed-binary guarantee needs branching decisions on binary variables only (p. 4). Assumption 1 normalises the reason constraint (complement negative coefficients, scale), "without loss of generality" (p. 11). Test set: MIPLIB 2017 benchmark with five permutations, minus the models no setting solves on any of the five: 795 measurements (pp. 18–19).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: proof devices, in order of use.
  - Remark 1: resolution read as Fourier–Motzkin elimination (p. 8).
  - Example 1: a counterexample to naive generalized resolution (p. 8).
  - Prop. 1, by maximal activity: a tightly propagating reason keeps the resolvent infeasible (p. 9).
  - A geometric characterization: propagation is non-tight exactly when the one-row LP attains its optimum at a non-integer vertex (p. 9).
  - Prop. 2 (tightness of the cMIR-reduced constraint) and Prop. 3 (dominance via equal fractional parts) (pp. 12–14).
  - Example 2: an impossibility shown with two integer-feasible points whose midpoint lies in the local domain (pp. 14–15).
  - Prop. 4, by comparing the bounds before and after eliminating the continuous variable; Remark 3, where a sign change proves infeasibility at an earlier state (pp. 15–16).
  - Termination of Algorithm 3 via the lexicographic order of states (p. 17).
  - For general integers, Figure 3 shows that no linear cut separates the vertex when an integer variable contributes a non-global bound (pp. 17–18).

  Implementation counts (preliminary experiments): where integer variables do not contribute a global bound, resolution still succeeds without any reduction in 72% of cases; general cMIR rescues about 3% of the remaining 28% (p. 18). Weakening for the coefficient-tightening reduction uses a single sweep (p. 19). The fallback to graph-based conflict analysis occurs in about 19% of calls (p. 19).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup: SCIP 9.0; Xeon Gold 5122 at 3.60 GHz, 96 GB; single thread; 2 h; MIPLIB 2017 benchmark with five permutations (p. 18).
  - Isolation experiment, design: a conflict-generation run collects conflicts without letting them act, so every algorithm sees the same tree and the same infeasibilities; an exploitation run then adds the conflicts from the start (pp. 19–20).   - Isolation experiment, results (Table 1, p. 20), for GRAPH, COEFT and CMIR: solved to optimality 553, 562, 562; time 433.0, 437.8, 436.8; conflict length 27.5, 59.6, 59.7; conflicts used in propagation 31.2%, 44.9%, 45.4%; bound changes per node 4.9, 24.4, 24.2. - (D4) Before generalizing an algorithm, build the smallest counterexample on which the naive extension fails, then design the fix against that example (Examples 1–3, pp. 8, 14–17).
- (D4) Show that no valid inequality can exist with two feasible points whose convex combination lies in the local domain (Example 2, pp. 14–15).
- (D5) To compare learning components fairly, split the work into a generation run (collect, but do not use) and an exploitation run (add from the start), so that every variant sees identical inputs (pp. 19–20).
- (D5) In paired runs, replace wall-clock limits by deterministic work limits such as nodes and calls (p. 20).
- (D5) For instances only one setting solves, rerun with a longer limit and report how many swaps survive (p. 22).
