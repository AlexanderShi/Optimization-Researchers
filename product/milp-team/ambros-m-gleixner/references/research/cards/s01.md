### S035 · Undercover – a primal heuristic for MINLP based on sub-MIPs generated by set covering (EWMINLP 2010, pp. 103–112, no DOI or arXiv id)
**Read level**: full. Text read: the proceedings chapter (chapter 22), pp. 1–8; references (pp. 9–10) skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Timo Berthold (first author, ZIB) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the first Undercover paper. Its journal version is ref. [10] of S054 (p. 14), and S054 reuses its cover computation for branching; S075 (p. 4) and H001 (p. 5) list Undercover among SCIP's MINLP heuristics.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: MIP solvers rely on general-purpose primal heuristics, but for MINLP "there have only been a few publications on general-purpose primal heuristics" (p. 1). Entry: in MIP an easy subproblem has few integer variables, in MINLP it can be one with few nonlinear constraints, and for several practical MIQCPs a small set of fixed variables already linearizes the problem (p. 2); the idea came from the authors' MIQCP work (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: fix an x*-cover, a set of variables whose fixing makes every constraint affine (Definitions 22.1–22.2, p. 2), to values from a relaxation (rounded for integers), and solve the resulting sub-MIP with a MIP solver; every sub-MIP solution is feasible for the MINLP (p. 1; Figure 1, p. 3). Changed against the MIP large-neighbourhood heuristics (RINS, DINS, RENS, Local Branching): the fixing targets nonlinearity, not integrality, so the heuristic fixes as few variables as possible (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MINLP (22.1) with a linear objective w.l.o.g. (p. 2); MIQCPs with symmetric, possibly indefinite A_i (p. 4). A cover of x'Qx must contain u or v for every nonzero Q_uv, so a minimum cover is a binary covering program (22.3)–(22.5), Lemma 22.6 (pp. 4–5). Remark 22.3 concedes that a minimum cover only approximates a largest sub-MIP, because it looks at the graph of each function and ignores bounds and interactions between constraints (p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: the covering problem is an optimisation version of 2-SAT and hence polynomial, but a general MIP solver solved it in a fraction of a second, so no special algorithm was coded (Remark 22.7, p. 5). Variants: sequential fix-and-propagate (a value outside the propagated domain moves to the closest bound), one-level backtracking with listed alternative values, recomputing the cover, other impact measures, NLP postprocessing, convexification, domain reduction instead of fixing (pp. 5–6, fn. 3). The SCIP implementation has fix-and-propagate, backtracking and NLP postprocessing (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: 33 MINLPLib MIQCPs (six that are linear after presolve and all but two nuclear instances excluded, with reasons), every solver stopped after the root (UC alone with cuts off; SCIP 1.2.0.4 with its ten root heuristics; BARON; Couenne): UC finds solutions on 21 instances (SCIP 14, BARON 15, Couenne 9; 7 found by UC alone), is better/equal/worse than SCIP on 1/1/8 common instances, than BARON on 4/4/3 and than Couenne on 1/2/3, and its own running time stays under 0.2 s except on one instance (1.1 s); no hardware is stated (pp. 6–8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a general-purpose start heuristic, implemented in SCIP. Printed limits: no fixing values when the root LP is unbounded (p. 7); solution quality mostly below SCIP's root heuristics (p. 8). Future work: implement and test the §4 variants and take fixing values from NLP relaxations (p. 8). The conclusion is hedged: Undercover "seems to be a fast start heuristic" (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: matched by stage, not by features. All solvers stop after the root so that the heuristic meets root heuristics, not complete solvers, which the authors call "a comparison rather insignificant" (pp. 6–7); UC runs alone while SCIP's default runs ten heuristics, and the unflattering pairwise counts are printed (pp. 6, 8).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (weak): the paper prints where the heuristic cannot run (nuclear instances, p. 7), its quality losses (p. 8) and its unimplemented variants (p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate: build MINLP on mature MIP machinery. The nonlinear part is reduced to a sub-MIP so that "the full strength of state-of-the-art MIP solving" applies (p. 2); again in S075 (p. 1), S030 (p. 20) and S054 (pp. 7–8, 13). Group-level.
- Not counted in the digest: Heuristic 7 ✅ (a heuristic judged by solutions found, their quality and time; infeasible sub-MIPs are mostly caught in propagation, so "no time was invested in vain", p. 8); Heuristic 8 ✅ (excluded instances named with reasons, p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D3/D4) To find the fewest variables whose fixing linearizes a quadratic system, solve a vertex-cover program over the nonzero quadratic terms (squares must be fixed, one endpoint of each bilinear term); if the MIP solver handles it in a fraction of a second, skip the special-case algorithm (pp. 4–5).
- (D4) Fix one variable at a time and propagate after each fixing; move a value that left its domain to the closest bound; on infeasibility undo the last fixing and try a short list of alternatives (finite bounds, the midpoints to them, zero, twice the value) (p. 5, fn. 3).
- (D5) Evaluate a start heuristic against the other solvers' root-node heuristics only (node limit 1 everywhere), and report solutions found, pairwise better/equal/worse counts on the common instances, and time (pp. 6–8).

**Quotes**:
- "Thus our heuristic will aim at fixing as few variables as possible to obtain as large a linear subproblem as possible." (p. 3)
- "We note that in 10 out of the 14 cases where the resulting sub-MIP was infeasible, the infeasibility was already detected during the fix-and-propagate stage." (p. 8)

### S075 · Extending SCIP for solving MIQCPs (EWMINLP 2010, pp. 181–196, no DOI or arXiv id)
**Read level**: full. Text read: the proceedings chapter (chapter 32), a poster abstract: the text (pp. 1–4) and the appendix text (pp. 7–8) in full; the performance profiles (p. 5), the eight appendix tables (pp. 8–15) and the references (pp. 5–6) skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz (ZIB), Stefan Vigerske (HU Berlin). The file's byline has these four authors (p. 1); the work list also names T. Koch, who is not on it · **Place in the trajectory**: a poster on the group's SCIP extension for MIQCPs. The group's full paper without Gleixner (Berthold, Heinz, Vigerske, ZIB-Report 09-23) is cited in S035 (p. 9) and H001 (p. 7). The poster lists Undercover (S035) among three MINLP heuristics (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: show "how a framework for CIPs can be extended towards a competitive solver" for MIQCPs, so that existing MIP and CP technology handles the linear and discrete parts (p. 1).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: new SCIP constraint handlers for quadratic, second-order cone and (experimentally) convex nonlinear constraints, and three large-neighbourhood heuristics (Fix-and-NLP; a RENS extension as Fix-and-MINLP; Undercover as Fix-and-MIP). SCIP's LP-based branch and bound and all default MIP heuristics are kept (pp. 2–4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIQCPs including nonconvex ones, MIQPs, mixed-integer conic (SOC) portfolio problems and convex MINLPs. Four test sets: 25 Mittelmann MIQPs, 22 portfolio MICPs, 60 general MIQCPs (42 MINLPLib, 6 clay, 12 truss) and 47 convex MINLPs reproduced from Bonami, Kılınç and Linderoth (pp. 4, 7–8; Tables 1, 3, 5, 7). Presolve turns 15 of the MIQPs and six of the MINLPLib instances into MILPs (p. 7).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: presolve linearizes binary×linear products, detects SOCs, reformulates ℓ ≤ xy ≤ u with sign-definite y, and checks convexity by the minimum eigenvalue; separation linearizes convex constraints and underestimates nonconvex terms one by one (linearization, secant, McCormick); propagation uses interval arithmetic; quadratic constraints are enforced after integrality, so spatial branching (by pseudocosts) happens only at integer-feasible LP solutions (pp. 2–3). A null result is reported: an SOC outer approximation in presolve gave no benefit (p. 3).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP 1.2.0.7 against BARON, Couenne, CPLEX, LINDOglobal (limited licence) and Mosek, and for convex MINLPs against AlphaECP, three BONMIN variants, DICOPT and SBB; 1 h, gap 10^-4, feasibility tolerance 10^-6, one Core2 Duo, the non-default options listed; profiles on the 80 MIQCPs where at least two solvers needed more than 10 s and the licence allowed (p. 4); per-instance times or final bounds in the appendix (pp. 9–15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: software (constraint handlers and heuristics in SCIP) with a broad comparison. There is no conclusion or outlook, and the text draws no claim from the profiles (pp. 4–5); the convex nonlinear support is "experimental" (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: a written inclusion rule for the profile (at least two solvers above 10 s, within the licence limit) and a column marking which instances entered it (pp. 4, 13), an early form of Method 6 step 4; but it filters an assembled collection for one figure, and the exclusions are not counted.
- new-pattern candidate: build MINLP on mature MIP machinery (p. 1; see S035).
- Not counted in the digest: Heuristic 7 ✅ (a component that gave nothing is said plainly: the SOC outer approximation, p. 3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Enforce nonlinear constraints after integrality, so that spatial branching only meets LP solutions whose integer variables are integral (p. 3).
- (D5) Before drawing a performance profile, filter trivial instances by a written rule (at least two solvers need more than 10 s) and mark in the per-instance table which instances entered the profile (pp. 4, 13).

**Quotes**:
- "This allows to utilize the complete power of already existing MIP and CP technologies for handling the linear and the discrete parts of the problem." (p. 1)
- "We also experimented with adding a linear outer-approximation of the SOC as suggested in [5] during presolve, but did not observe a computational benefit for the instances in our testset." (p. 3)

### H001 · On the computational impact of MIQCP solver components (ZIB-Report 11-01 2011, no DOI or arXiv id; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-11998)
**Read level**: full. Text read: ZIB-Report 11-01 (January 2011), pp. 3–7 in full; the appendix table (pp. 8–9) and references (p. 7) skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz (ZIB), Stefan Vigerske (HU Berlin) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: the January 2011 report of the component study that returns, revised, as S028 (this batch).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: the performance of an MINLP solver "crucially depends on its single constituents and the mutual interplay" of its features (p. 3). Aim: measure the impact of single components on MIQCPs in SCIP, whose plug-in architecture suits such an analysis (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: run the default against 11 settings, each disabling one feature or switching it to a simpler rule, including the MILP components, since many MIQCPs have a large linear and discrete part (p. 5). For features that act only on some instances, split the set into a "relevant" and a "control" group, the control group being expected to show no change (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIQCPs with possibly indefinite A_j (p. 3); 86 public instances (clay and SLay layout problems, Mittelmann MIQPs, portfolio, truss, uflquad, MINLPLib), with statistics before and after presolve (pp. 5, 8–9); the relevant group of the convexity check is the instances with convex constraints that contain bilinear terms (p. 8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: the components ablated (pp. 4–5): MILP presolve, binary-product reformulation, SOC detection, eigenvalue convexity check, MILP cuts at the root, linearizations and termwise underestimators, interval propagation, conflict analysis on the linear part only (quadratic constraints take no part), hybrid branching for integers and pseudocost spatial branching, MILP heuristics plus three MIQCP heuristics.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Xeon 5150, SCIP 1.2.1.1 with CPLEX 12.1.0 and Ipopt 3.8.1, optimality tolerance zero, 1 h, 4 GB; the default solves 55 of 86; per setting: solved difference, instances more than 10% faster or slower (bounds compared at termination when unsolved), and shifted geometric means of time, nodes and time to first and to optimal solution, with no shift stated. Nonlinear separation off loses 19 instances (+140% time), SOC upgrades off lose 8 of the 12 relevant (+1098%), heuristics off cost +347% time to the first solution (pp. 5–6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: "All 11 features proved to be beneficial", the nonlinear ones most, though often on a small subset (p. 6). MILP and CP techniques "do not unfold their full potential for MINLP, yet", for conflict analysis "probably due to a theoretical gap" (p. 7); only one solver was tested, and the same experiment for others "would be interesting" (p. 7). Domain propagation "surprised us": fewer nodes and better dual bounds without a time gain (p. 6), left unexplained.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: gaps printed with a suspected cause: quadratic constraints outside conflict analysis (p. 5), MILP and CP techniques below their MILP effect and a "theoretical gap" for conflict analysis (p. 7), a single solver (p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate: relevant/control split in an ablation (p. 5; again in S028, p. 7).
- new-pattern candidate (negative): text–table slips. The text lists ten settings for "11 alternative settings" (conflict analysis appears only in Table 1) (pp. 5–6), and one citation is unresolved ("[?]", p. 4).
- Not counted in the digest: Heuristic 7 ✅ (heuristics judged by time to the first solution, p. 6) and ⚠ (shifted means without their shift, p. 5); Heuristic 8 ✅ (a 10% threshold for faster and slower; bounds at termination for timeouts, p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Ablate a nonlinear solver one component at a time against its default, including the classical MILP components (presolve, cuts, conflict analysis), and keep all rows in one table (pp. 5–6).
- (D5) For a feature that acts only on some instances, report the affected group and a control group; a change in the control group exposes noise or side effects (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "The overall computational performance of a solver crucially depends on its single constituents and the mutual interplay of these features." (p. 3)
- "The results for domain propagation surprised us." (p. 6)

### S028 · Analyzing the computational impact of MIQCP solver components (Numerical Algebra, Control and Optimization 2(4) 2012, DOI 10.3934/naco.2012.2.739)
**Read level**: full. Text read: the report version, ZIB-Report 13-08 (February 2013), pp. 3–10 in full; the appendix table (pp. 11–12) and references (pp. 12–13) skimmed. The journal typesetting (pp. 739–748) was not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz (ZIB), Stefan Vigerske (HU Berlin) (p. 3) · **Place in the trajectory**: the revised H001: SCIP 2.1.1 instead of 1.2.1.1, a new cluster, twelve settings instead of eleven, the same 86 instances (p. 7).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: as in H001 (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: twelve settings: a QCP local-search setting is added, MILP cuts and QCP cuts become separate rows, and conflict analysis is now listed in the text (p. 7); the relevant/control split is kept (p. 7); the table marks each measure as worse (bold blue) or better (red italics) after disabling (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: the same sources, now with selection rules and their reasons: trivial and extremely hard instances are removed because switching single components "will not produce a significant difference" on them and they "would skew average-based performance measures"; instances on which SCIP 2.1.1 failed with a setting are removed; families of similar instances are thinned out against over-representation (p. 7). ilaser0, "erroneously reported as infeasible", is excluded, with the fixing release in a footnote (p. 7); one instance per setting is excluded where a tree-depth limit was hit (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: components as in H001, plus the big-M linearization of binary products (p. 5) and the remark that conflict analysis on the linear constraints alone "is often sufficient to generate short conflict constraints" (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Xeon X5672 cluster with Hyperthreading and Turboboost off and one job per node, SCIP 2.1.1 with CPLEX 12.4.0.0, 1 h, 8 GB (p. 7); the default solves 55 of 86 (p. 7); measures: solved difference ("a rough indicator"), primal and dual bounds better or worse by at least 2% for timeouts, time more than 10% faster or slower, and shifted geometric means of time, time to first and to optimal solution and nodes, no shift stated (pp. 8–9). QCP cuts off: −12 solved (+100% time); SOC upgrades off: −7 of 12 relevant (+1182%); hybrid branching off: −7 (+97%); heuristics off: +413% time to the first solution; linear presolve off: +1 solved (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: "Eleven out of twelve features proved to be beneficial" (p. 9). Binary reformulation helps mean time but degrades five of eight measures, so it "should not be used by default" (p. 9). MILP and CP components stay below their potential, and only one solver was tested (p. 10). Against H001: domain propagation now "gives a clear benefit w.r.t. the computation time" (p. 9), where H001 had been surprised by it (H001 p. 6), and H001's sentence on a theoretical gap for conflict analysis is gone (p. 10); neither change is commented on.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the test set is filtered by written rules, each with a reason (trivial and hopeless instances skew means; failed runs; over-represented classes), and a wrongly answered instance is named and excluded (p. 7). These are early forms of Method 6 steps 3–4, though the exclusions are not counted per rule.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the wrong answer is printed with the release that fixes it (p. 7, fn. 1), and the single-solver limit is kept (p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: relevant/control split in an ablation (p. 7; H001 p. 5).
- new-pattern candidate (negative): text–table slips. The text says "disabling a certain feature always leads to an increase in overall computation time" (p. 9), while Table 1 shows 0% mean time for QCP local search (p. 8).
- Not counted in the digest: Heuristic 3 ✅ (a mean-time gain does not earn default status when five of eight measures degrade, p. 9); Honest Boundary: the one-job-per-node rule, listed there as unverified, is practised and justified in this 2013 report (p. 7); Heuristic 7 ⚠ (no shifts stated, pp. 8–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) State the test-set filter with its reason: drop instances that are trivial or hopeless for the solver, because switching one component cannot change them and they skew means; thin out families of near-identical instances (p. 7).
- (D5) Time with one job per node and Hyperthreading and Turboboost off, and say why (cache misses when processes share resources) (p. 7).
- (D5) Mark in the table, per measure, whether disabling the component made it worse or better, and decide the default on the count of measures, not on mean time alone (pp. 8–9).

**Quotes**:
- "In all experiments, we ran only one job per node to avoid random noise in the measured running time that might be caused by cache-misses if multiple processes share common resources." (p. 7)
- "We come to the conclusion that, despite of the benefits in mean running time, binary variable reformulation should not be used by default." (p. 9)

### S030 · Comparing MIQCP solvers to a specialised algorithm for mine production scheduling (Modeling, Simulation and Optimization of Complex Processes 2012, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-25707-0_3)
**Read level**: full. Text read: the report version, ZIB-Report 09-32 (October 2009), pp. 3–20 in full; references (pp. 20–22) skimmed. The 2012 chapter was not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Andreas Bley (TU Berlin), Thorsten Koch (ZIB), Stefan Vigerske (HU Berlin) (p. 3) · **Place in the trajectory**: the mining line. His Diplom thesis (S025) is ref. [13] and a paper by Boland, Dumitrescu, Froyland and Gleixner is ref. [8] (p. 21); the benchmark is the algorithm of Bley, Boland, Froyland and Zuckerberg [7] (pp. 4, 10, 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: general MIP solvers are now "often very hard to outperform", while many nonconvex problems still need specialised algorithms (p. 3). Question: how close do general-purpose MIQCP solvers come to a hand-crafted branch and bound on open-pit mine production scheduling with a stockpile; the case was chosen because the authors had worked on it [7] (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: run BARON, Couenne, SBB and SCIP on the two MIQCP formulations of the benchmark algorithm (the aggregated basic formulation BF and the extended warehouse formulation WF), and SCIP also on WF with an a priori discretisation of the out-fractions at 1, 2 and 4 levels (pp. 6–10, 14).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: one attribute and one stockpile of infinite capacity (p. 5); Assumption S on when stockpile material moves (p. 7); nonconvex quadratic mixing constraints (14) and (19), written to avoid zero denominators (pp. 8–9); BF aggregates WF, so WF's LP relaxation is tighter (p. 10). Data: two industry instances, Marvin (85 aggregates, 15 years) and Dent (125 aggregates, 25 years); public instances are "typically not available" (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: the benchmark branches on violated mixing constraints by adding linear inequalities for all aggregates at once, which is invalid for a general MINLP solver but valid here because the out-fractions must be equal (pp. 10–11); it runs on a tuned CPLEX 11.2.1 with precedence-knapsack fixings and cuts (p. 12). SBB's dual bounds on these nonconvex problems "cannot be trusted" and are printed in parentheses (pp. 13, 15).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: single thread, 10,000 s, bounds and nodes after 1 h and at the end, default and written-out tuned settings for BARON, Couenne and SBB, one SCIP setting (pp. 14–15). On WF the benchmark ends with gaps of 0.02% and 0.33%; the best general-purpose primal bounds are 0.4% and 0.2% below its solutions, the best dual bounds 1.4% and 0.2% away (p. 16); SCIP has the best primal bounds among the global solvers (p. 17); 40-hour SCIP runs move its gaps from 1.81% to 1.75% and from 1.08% to 0.47% (p. 19); more discretisation levels hurt because the LP grows (p. 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: claim: general-purpose solvers "nearly match" the hand-crafted algorithm (p. 3; p. 20). The remaining gap is traced partly to structure the general solvers do not exploit (p. 16) and to SCIP's heuristics (one of 25 extended to nonlinearities) (p. 20); SBB's NLP relaxation is more robust to the formulation but only a heuristic as long as relaxations are not solved globally (p. 20). Future work: an SLP/SQP-based QCP solver in SCIP (p. 20).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (step 5): the general-purpose solvers run on "the very formulations on which the benchmark algorithm" rests (p. 14), and the remaining difference is attributed, beyond the nonlinear handling, to the knapsack structure the benchmark exploits (p. 16).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: no arm is reduced to match another: BARON, Couenne and SBB get tuned settings, SCIP one setting, the benchmark a tuned CPLEX (pp. 12, 14–15).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the gap to the hand-crafted code is printed as numbers with suspected causes, and the next component is named (pp. 16, 20).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: a belief of the field (nonconvex problems still need specialised algorithms, p. 3) is tested by a head-to-head on one application with two instances, not by checking decisions; the answer cuts against the belief (p. 20).
- new-pattern candidate: build MINLP on mature MIP machinery: "extending a MIP framework compares favourably" (p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) Compare general-purpose solvers with a specialised code on the specialised code's own formulations, then say which part of the remaining gap comes from structure the specialised code exploits (pp. 14, 16).
- (D5) Report bounds at two time points and rerun the best solver much longer (here 40 h) to see whether the verdict depends on the time limit (pp. 14, 19).
- (D5) Keep a solver whose dual bounds are not valid on the class (NLP-based branch and bound on nonconvex models) for its primal solutions, but print its dual bounds and gaps in parentheses (pp. 13, 15).

**Quotes**:
- "The computational results presented show that general-purpose solvers with no particular knowledge of problem structure are able to nearly match the performance of a hand-crafted algorithm." (p. 3)
- "Note that this difference is not only due to the handling of the nonlinear constraints." (p. 16)

### S066 · Factorization and update of a reduced basis matrix for the revised simplex method (ZIB-Report 12-36 2012, no DOI or arXiv id)
**Read level**: full. Text read: ZIB-Report 12-36 (October 2012), pp. 3–12 in full; the algorithm figures (pp. 7–8) read for their structure only, since the extraction scrambles their layout; references (p. 13) skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: none (sole author); Steffy, Berthold, Koch and Miltenberger are thanked for comments (p. 13) · **Place in the trajectory**: simplex linear algebra in his SoPlex years, the year of the iterative refinement report (cited as [20] in S071, p. 22); the only sole-authored text in this batch.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: FTRAN and BTRAN account for "a major part of the computational effort in each simplex iteration" (p. 9). Entry: an old idea, a basis matrix made of the basic columns and nonbasic rows, noted by Zoutendijk and tested by Powell on three instances, whose results have then "seemingly been disregarded" (p. 3).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: factorize only the reduced basis matrix A_{R̄,C}, of dimension at most min{m, n}, which changes from iteration to iteration; FTRAN and BTRAN become one triangular solve of that size plus a matrix–vector product (pp. 3, 6). New: Forrest–Tomlin-type LU updates for this matrix, including the cases where its dimension grows or shrinks (pp. 9–11); the approach unifies the column and row forms (p. 3). Kept: the rest of the primal and dual simplex (Figs. 1–2, pp. 7–8), so that it can be "integrated seamlessly into existing simplex codes", unlike Wunderling's 2012 redesign of the algorithm (p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: LPs min{c'x : Ax ≥ b, x ≥ 0}, chosen "For clarity of presentation" (p. 4); bases (C, R) with |C| + |R| = m and regular bases (Definition 1, p. 4); regularity in column form is equivalent to regularity in row form (Lemma 1, p. 5). The method pays most when bases are dominated by tight column bounds: slack starting bases, LPs with more rows than columns, relaxations in branch and cut (p. 12).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: product-form updates for the four basis changes (column exchange, row exchange, column in and row out, column out and row in), with Lemma 2 for the shrinking case via deletion matrices (pp. 9–10). LU updates: the Forrest–Tomlin spike and permutation for a column exchange, its transposed analogue for a row exchange, bordering of the factors when the matrix grows, and, when it shrinks, replacing the leaving column and entering row by unit vectors, updating as for exchanges and deleting the last row and column (pp. 10–11). The Suhl–Suhl improvements are left out "merely for clarity" (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a. No implementation or experiment is reported; the advantages are argued from operation counts and from fill-in "observed by Powell" and "confirmed by Wunderling" (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: update formulas (an algorithmic contribution, no software). The argued benefit for MIP: in branch-cut-and-price a new column enters nonbasic and a new row basic, so A_{R̄,C} and its factorization survive reoptimization (p. 12). No limitation or open question is stated, and the introduction says the method "shows various computational advantages" (p. 3) without measuring one.

### S071 · SCIP Optimization Suite を利用した混合整数 (線形/非線形) 計画問題の解法 [Solving mixed integer linear and nonlinear problems using the SCIP Optimization Suite] (24th RAMP Symposium 2012, pp. 165–192, no DOI or arXiv id)
**Read level**: full. Text read: ZIB-Report 12-24 (July 2012), in Japanese, the version announced for the RAMP 2012 proceedings (p. 3, footnote): pp. 3–21 in full, with the shell transcripts and the code listing (pp. 12–16) skimmed; references (pp. 22–24) skimmed.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch, Yuji Shinano (ZIB) (p. 3); a reader of the Japanese manuscript is thanked (p. 21) · **Place in the trajectory**: a tutorial on the suite as of SCIP 3.0. - **D1 Problem and entry**: introduce the SCIP Optimization Suite (Zimpl, SoPlex, SCIP) and show how to model and solve MIPs and MINLPs with it (p. 3). Motivation: the practical advantages of MIP formulations, and a pure algorithmic speed-up of commercial solvers by a factor of 55,000 over 20 years, cited from MIPLIB 2010 (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: SCIP is a plug-in framework in which constraint handlers decide feasibility for their constraint class and may add presolving, propagation and separation (126 plug-ins and 26 constraint handlers in SCIP 3.0) (p. 5); branch and price needs only a pricer plug-in, with the tree managed by SCIP (p. 6). Zimpl computes in exact rationals (GMP), so translating a model into solver input adds no rounding error (p. 7). SoPlex switches automatically between primal and dual simplex, shifts bounds instead of running a phase I, updates its LU factors by Forrest–Tomlin or eta updates, offers a row form for LPs with more constraints than variables (p. 8) and, since version 1.7, iterative refinement to arbitrary precision (p. 9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: classes covered: LP; MIP (knapsack, set packing, partitioning and covering, variable bounds; indicator and SOS1/2 constraints); MINLP (expression trees, quadratic, SOC, bivariate and absolute-power constraints); pseudo-Boolean; SAT; scheduling with cumulative constraints (pp. 5–6); more than ten input formats (p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: walkthroughs of a Zimpl TSP model with all subtour constraints (13 cities: 78 variables, 8,021 constraints) (pp. 10–11) and of the shell on stein27, whose run-to-run differences in time and nodes are explained as performance variability caused by its more than 2000 optimal solutions (pp. 11–13); the callable library and the plug-in templates (pp. 15–17). Debugging: a debug build with asserts and consistency checks, debug messages, and a debug solution that shows where in the solving process a known feasible solution is removed; testing: one command runs a test set with a settings file and limits and writes per-instance results with totals and geometric means (p. 17).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a. There is no evaluation; the speed claims rest on third-party benchmarks and Vigerske's thesis (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: documentation and tutorial. Stated belief: research needs source access, to know exactly what the algorithm does and to be able to change any part of it (p. 20). Future work: parallel solving; exact integer programming, because floating-point solvers suffice for most commercial use, but some problem classes, feasibility questions above all, need safety against numerical error and research wants exact optimality proofs, with many open points [13]; combining MINLP and CP techniques; faster solving of larger instances (p. 21). A footnote says that 証明 (proof) in the text means a proof up to numerical error, i.e. a gap of zero rather than of a small epsilon (p. 21, fn. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence (group, stated): SoPlex reaches arbitrary precision by repeating floating-point solves together with the GNU Multiple Precision library (p. 9); Zimpl computes the model in exact rationals, so no rounding error enters when it is translated into solver input (p. 7).

### S054 · Undercover Branching (Experimental Algorithms: 12th International Symposium SEA 2013, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-38527-8_20)
**Read level**: full. Text read: ZIB-Report 13-14 (April 2013), announced as the SEA 2013 paper (p. 1): pp. 3–13 in full; references (pp. 13–15) skimmed. The proceedings version was not read.

**Role**: supplement · **Co-authors**: Timo Berthold (first author, ZIB) (p. 3) · **Place in the trajectory**: turns the cover of the Undercover heuristic (S035 and its journal version, refs. [9]–[10], p. 14) into a branching rule.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: branching is among the highest-impact components of MIP solvers (p. 3), and for MINLP "research has mainly focused on adopting MIP branching rules" (p. 4). Entry: no rule uses the nonlinearity when branching on fractional integer variables; a minimum cover, a measure of the "grade of nonlinearity", can drive the subproblems towards linearity (pp. 3, 5, 8).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: when the fractional (or spatial) candidates meet the cover, branch only among those in the cover, otherwise keep the solver's candidates; any variable-based rule decides underneath (Fig. 2, p. 8). Against information-based and backdoor branching: the structure is computed exactly, once, at negligible cost, with no sampling phase or restarts (pp. 4–5, 9), and no strict branching priorities are imposed (p. 9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MINLP (1) with constraint functions twice continuously differentiable on the interior of the box (pp. 5–6). Covers are exactly the vertex covers of the co-occurrence graph of the Hessian sparsity (Theorem 1, proof in [10]), so finding a minimum cover is NP-hard (Corollary 1, p. 6); an integer cover with finite bounds reduces P to at most ∏(u_i − ℓ_i + 1) MIPs (Lemma 1, p. 8). On 255 MINLPLib instances one third have minimum covers below 14% of the variables and another third below 36% (p. 7). Test set: 195 of 273 MINLPLib instances after named removal steps (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: covers come from a binary vertex-cover program solved by SCIP 3.0, with CppAD for the Hessian sparsity, in at most 0.2 s each (p. 6); the Undercover heuristic is switched off "To avoid interactions" and restarts "To measure tree sizes accurately" (p. 9). With a strong-branching base rule, restricting the candidates also changes which strong-branching LPs and fixings occur (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Xeon X5672 cluster, 1 h, Hyperthreading and Turboboost off, one job per node for "the latter experiment" (p. 9). "How often?": the rule changes decisions on 23 of 195 instances (p. 10). "How good?": on those 23, two more instances are solved with either base rule; shifted geometric means (shifts 100 nodes, 100 strong branchings, 10 s) fall by 79% in time and 52% in nodes with reliability/pseudocost and by 76% and 45% with pseudocost/pseudocost; losses are counted (nodes up on four instances, time on four); the gain survives removing two positive outliers and one negative outlier (pp. 11–13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a branching strategy and its evaluation. Limit: "the fraction of affected instances is relatively small (23 out of 195 instances)" (p. 13). Future work: non-minimum covers and the trade-off between cover size and affected instances; detecting linear subproblems with large subtrees and giving them to a pure MIP solver (p. 13). Slips: the text gives 45% fewer nodes for the reliability rule (p. 11, repeated in the conclusion, p. 13) where Table 1 shows −52% (−45% is the strong-branch column), and says strong branches "only increased once" where Table 1 shows two increases (pump +3%, tltr +54%) (pp. 11–12).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: like-for-like inside one solver: each base rule with and without the pre-selection, the interacting heuristic and restarts off (p. 9), and a second base rule without strong branching to separate the strong-branching side effect (p. 11).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the limit is printed with a count (23 of 195) and turned into the next projects (p. 13).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (stated): MINLP test sets "often decompose into very easy and extremely difficult instances" and are "not as heterogeneous as, e.g., the MIPLIBs" (p. 10), the qualities Method 6 later designs into a benchmark.
- new-pattern candidate: build MINLP on mature MIP machinery: a cover turns the MINLP into a MIP for any assignment of its variables, and linear subtrees are to go to a MIP solver (pp. 7–8, 13).
- new-pattern candidate: drop-in over redesign: a filter that wraps any variable-based rule (p. 8; S066 pp. 4, 12).
- new-pattern candidate (negative): text–table slips (pp. 11–13; also H001 and S028).
- Not counted in the digest: Heuristic 6 ✅ (the two questions are the subsection titles, p. 10); Heuristic 7 ✅ (shifts stated, p. 11, fn. 6); Heuristic 8 ✅ (headline on the affected subset, outliers removed as a check, removal steps named, pp. 6, 9–11); Research Taste 3 ✅ (newly solved instances counted, p. 11); Honest Boundary: one job per node practised in 2013 (p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
