### S092 · Branch and price for the length-constrained cycle partition problem (arXiv preprint 2024, arXiv 2401.17937)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (2 Feb 2024), titled "Branch and Price for the Length-Constrained Cycle Partition Problem"; pp. 1–10 read; references (pp. 11–12) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Mohammed Ghannam (first author; HTW Berlin, ZIB, felmo GmbH), Gioni Mexi (ZIB), Edward Lam (Monash University) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an application line at HTW; the predecessor approach is by ZIB colleagues (Hoppmann-Baum, Mexi, Koch et al. [9, 11], p. 2). The SKILL.md ledger links this paper to a later numerically safe branch-price-and-cut ([LCCP26]); nothing in this text anticipates that step (p. 10). No numerics, exactness or certificates in this paper.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: LCCP: partition the nodes of an undirected graph into the minimum number of disjoint cycles, each no longer than the smallest critical time of a node on it (eq. (1), pp. 1–2); applications in periodic surveillance or maintenance, related length constraints in kidney exchange (p. 2). Prior work: NP-hard without a polynomial-time approximation algorithm; a compact MTZ-based MIP solved up to 29 nodes; a SEC branch and cut on a commercial solver up to 52 nodes; yet "the proposed models suffer from symmetry, leading to a weak linear relaxation and ineffective branching" (p. 2). Entry: a set-partitioning model over cycle variables (2), inspired by kidney-exchange cycle formulations [14] (co-author Lam) and vehicle routing [4] (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: column generation for the LP relaxation of (2) inside exact branch and price; the pricing problem (LCPCCP: a length-feasible cycle of maximum dual weight) is solved per fixed start node by labeling, as a variant of the resource-constrained shortest path problem (pp. 2–4). New against RCSPP labeling: because the graph is undirected and travel times are symmetric, bidirectional search needs extensions in one direction only, extending labels while t(ℓ) < q(ℓ)/2 and merging two half-paths (p. 5). Added: symmetry breaking by start-node index, heuristic before exact pricing, parallel pricing, early branching, and a set-covering variant under the triangle inequality (pp. 3, 7–8). Unchanged: standard branching on implicit edge variables [4] and best-estimate node selection with plunging [1] (p. 7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: binary set partitioning with exponentially many columns (p. 2); critical times q_i > 0 and travel times t_ij ≥ 0, possibly non-metric; duals unrestricted in sign; LCPCCP called strongly NP-hard "by reduction from the Hamiltonian cycle problem" without a proof (p. 3); singleton cycles allowed (p. 4). Lemma 3 assumes the triangle inequality (pp. 7–8). Data: the standard benchmark set of [11], 84 instances with 14 to 100 nodes (p. 8), plus a derived set in which each travel time is replaced by the shortest-path distance (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: labels (N, v, c, t, q) with an explicit extension rule (p. 4); pruning when t(ℓ) > q(ℓ), shown by induction (p. 4); dominance Lemma 1 (same end node, reduced cost and time no larger, node set a subset), with proof (p. 5). Lemma 2 proves that bidirectional search with dominance still finds a minimum reduced-cost cycle: take the first forward label past the halfway point, show that the reverse remainder lies within half the cycle length, and replace a dominated label by its dominator in the merge (p. 6). At most 50 cycles per call, sorted by reduced cost, because extra columns help convergence but can make re-optimization harder (p. 6). Branching on the most-used edge not in a singleton cycle (p. 7); symmetry breaking by ignoring nodes of smaller index, with nodes sorted by critical time (p. 7); heuristic pricing that drops the subset condition (4c), then exact pricing, which always yields the Lagrangian bound, rounded up by integrality of the objective (p. 7); early branching when ⌈z*_RMP⌉ = ⌈LB_lg⌉ at a child's first RMP, after Mehrotra and Trick [17] (p. 7); Farkas pricing for infeasible RMPs and a root RMP built from singleton cycles and the MCV heuristic's cycles (p. 7). Under the triangle inequality: set covering with nonnegative duals, and Lemma 3 (nodes with zero dual can be ignored in pricing), stated "By the same arguments" with no proof (p. 8). Code: PySCIPOpt with a Rust pricer parallelized by Rayon; "The rest of SCIP is sequential" (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: three numbered questions, then a fourth one asked out of curiosity, on the triangle inequality (p. 8). Arms: bnc-sec (the branch and cut of [11] on Gurobi 10.0.3), bnp-basic, bnp-full and four ablations of bnp-full (no bidirectional labeling, no parallelization, no symmetry breaking, no early branching) (pp. 8–9); all arms start from the MCV solution (p. 8). Xeon Gold 5122 at 3.6 GHz with 96 GB, 2 h limit; code and per-instance results on GitHub (p. 8). Shifted geometric mean of time with shift 1 s over instances solved by at least one arm, timeouts counted at the limit (p. 9). Table 1: bnc-sec 142.8 s and 39 solved; bnp-basic 189.1 s and 30; bnp-full 9.8 s and 52; the ablations are 1.3 to 4.4 times slower and solve 40 to 50 (p. 10); all 39 bnc-sec solves are also bnp-full solves (p. 9). Root LP: column generation reached the optimum on only 56 of 84 instances (memory or time limit otherwise); where both arms have a root bound, the average gap to the best known solution is 1.1% against 23.6%; with a known optimum the rounded-up root bound is at least z* − 1, read as a possible round-up property (p. 9). Primal side: the MCV heuristic already has the optimum on 21 of the 39 jointly solved instances but on only 2 of the 13 newly solved ones (p. 9). Metric-closed set: both arms get slower (15.0 s and 187.1 s); exploiting the triangle inequality gives 55 solved and 7.5 s, "around 25.2 times faster" than bnc-sec (p. 10), which the printed means reproduce as 24.9 (my arithmetic, within rounding). Not stated: the thread count of the Gurobi baseline, while bnp-full prices in parallel (p. 8). The same Xeon Gold 5122 cluster is described as "each chip had 8 cores, and allowed for 16 threads to run in parallel" here (p. 8) and as "each chip has 4 cores" in S049 (p. 5). One run per instance, no seeds, no significance test although "significantly" is used (pp. 9–10).
- **D6 Positioning**: continues and supersedes Hoppmann-Baum et al. [9, 11], whose models are criticised for symmetry (p. 2); borrows from vehicle-routing branch-price-and-cut [4], bidirectional labeling [21, 22], kidney-exchange cycle models [14] and Mehrotra–Trick early branching [17] (pp. 2, 5, 7); dates column generation to Kantorovich and Zalgaller (1951) and Gilmore and Gomory (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing**: contributions listed by section, with the headline "on average 14.6 times faster" and the 13 closed instances up front (p. 3); numbered questions (p. 8); a counter-intuitive result reported as such ("Curiously", p. 10); the conclusion concedes that the gain depends on engineering techniques (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: algorithm (bidirectional labeling for LCPCCP with a correctness proof; branch-and-price enhancements) and computation (13 open instances closed, the largest with 76 nodes against 52 before) (abstract; pp. 3, 9–10); code public (p. 8). Limits printed: root column generation fails on 28 of 84 instances (p. 9); when both methods time out, the MCV primal bounds are rarely improved (2 instances, by 1) (p. 9); bnp-basic without the techniques loses to bnc-sec (p. 10). Future: cutting planes inside branch and price, and transfer to kidney exchange (p. 10). Numerical safety is not named as a gap.

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (step 1): limits printed with counts where they arise (28 of 84 root LPs not solved; 2 of 13 new optima found by the start heuristic) (p. 9).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the printed future work is cutting planes and kidney exchange (p. 10); the ledger edge to a numerically safe version ([LCCP26] in SKILL.md) is not printed here as a gap.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the headline factor 14.6 compares against the published predecessor re-run on the same machines with Gurobi 10.0.3 (pp. 8–9), not against a baseline stripped to the same features; thread counts of the arms are neither matched nor stated (p. 8). The component questions are answered inside the new code, by switching one technique off at a time (step 6 pattern), on the predecessor's set (step 7) (pp. 8–10).
- Also Heuristic 6 (questions first) ✅ (p. 8) and Research Taste mark 3 (headline counts instances newly solved) ✅ (abstract; p. 3).
- new-pattern candidate: assumption-ablation companion set: test whether a structural assumption matters by transforming the benchmark so that it holds (shortest-path closure) and running all arms on both versions (p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Prove bidirectional labeling correct by taking the first forward label past the halfway point, bounding the reverse remainder by half the cycle length, and letting a dominating label stand in for a dominated one in the merge (Lemma 2, p. 6).
- (D4) Use integrality of the objective twice: round the Lagrangian bound up, and skip pricing at a child whose first RMP value rounds up to the parent's bound (p. 7).
- (D4) Return only a fixed number (50) of the lowest reduced-cost columns per pricing call, to keep RMP re-optimization cheap (p. 6).
- (D5) Report an ablation table with one row per disabled technique (time ratio to the full method, solved count) together with a solved-over-time plot (Table 1, Fig. 2, p. 10).
- (D5) Check which side the new method helps by counting how often the warm-start heuristic already had the optimum on old and on newly solved instances (p. 9).
- (D5) Build a companion benchmark by one transformation that enforces an assumption (metric closure of travel times) and run every arm on both sets (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Curiously, this change makes the instances harder to solve for both approaches." (p. 10)
- "the results also show that this is only possible with the help of some nontrivial improvement techniques" (p. 10)

### S021 · The SCIP optimization suite 10.0 (arXiv preprint 2025, arXiv 2511.18580)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v1 (23 Nov 2025; title page dated November 25, 2025); pp. 1–40 read (main text, acknowledgements, contributions of the authors); references (pp. 40–46) skimmed; author affiliations (pp. 46–49) read.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: 34 others, Christopher Hojny first and the rest alphabetical, Gleixner tenth (p. 1), affiliated with HTW Berlin and ZIB (p. 47) · **Place in the trajectory**: the release that ships exact rational MILP solving in SCIP, closing the line status update [36] → safe GMI cuts [37] → certified propagation and dual proof analysis [18], with presolve certification [54] and MIP-DD [55] alongside (pp. 7, 37–38). Attribution: "Leon Eifler was the main author of SCIP’s new exact solving mode, with contributions and revisions by Ambros Gleixner and Dominik Kamp, and a concerted code review effort of the whole team" (p. 39); Gleixner and Eifler thank Kati Jarck and Daniel Steffy for the prototype of the exact mode and its safe dual bounding (p. 39). The other sections are other people's work (pp. 39–40); the card reads them all but links them to the skill only where they bear on its methods (release protocol, defaults, PaPILO, MIP-DD).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: a report "to highlight the features becoming available in the latest release" (p. 2). Exact mode: floating-point solvers "by design" cannot guarantee results unaffected by accumulated roundoff, "nor can they produce independently verifiable certificates of correctness" (pp. 6–7). Other entries: implied integrality detected one variable at a time (pp. 11–12); conflict analysis restricted to conflict graphs (p. 17); debugging needs the failing instance, which users may not be allowed to share (pp. 37–38).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: exact mode "follows a hybrid-precision approach", replacing symbolic computation "at many places" by safe floating-point computation with directed rounding (p. 7), switched on by one parameter (exact/enabled) and certified through another (certificate/filename) (p. 7); every critical plugin type carries a flag, and the core runs only callbacks of plugins marked safe, so an unsafe user plugin cannot compromise exactness (pp. 7–8). Elsewhere: implied-integer detection through network submatrices (pp. 12–13), reflection symmetries (pp. 14–16), cut-based conflict analysis taken from pseudo-Boolean solvers (p. 17), flower inequalities (p. 18), kernel search with user decompositions (pp. 19–20), ancestral pseudocosts and probabilistic lookahead (pp. 21–22), Benders updates, an IIS finder, a CONOPT interface and a bound check for aggregated variables (pp. 22–26); GCG 4.0, PaPILO 3.0, MIP-DD 2.0 and a pseudo-Boolean application (pp. 26–39).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: SCIP solves CIPs; MILP (1) allows free and one-sided bounds (p. 3). Exact mode "is restricted to mixed-integer linear programs" with rational data, read exactly from MPS, LP, CIP, OPB/WBO and ZIMPL files (pp. 7–8). Release test sets: 349 MILPs from MIPLIB 2003, 2010, 2017 and COR@L, namely "all instances that could be solved by previous releases", × 3 seeds = 1047; 169 MINLPLib instances × 5 seeds = 845; an MINLP counts as solved at a relative gap of 10^-4 or an absolute gap of 10^-6 (p. 4). Exact mode: MIPLIB 2017 benchmark set, 3 seeds (p. 11). Implied integers: 1035 MIPLIB 2017 collection instances, after 5 memory and 25 presolve-solved exclusions (pp. 13–14); correctness by Hoffman–Kruskal integrality of the y-subproblem for every integer fixing of x (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (exact mode): SCIP_RATIONAL wraps Boost, GMP and MPFR (p. 8); exact variable data, with the floating-point bounds kept "as a safe outward rounding" of the exact ones (p. 8); an exact linear constraint handler replaces the linear one and uses a running error analysis for faster feasibility checks (p. 8); PaPILO, templated in the number type, presolves in rational arithmetic with zero tolerances, optionally multithreaded (p. 8); safe dual bounds by bound shift and project-and-shift, with the exact LP (SoPlex by default, QSopt_ex possible) as fallback, plus exact LPs at the end of the root, at depth levels 2^k (extracted as "2k"), for unboundedness, for nearly integral LP solutions, for LP bounds near the cutoff and when all integers are fixed (p. 9). Reliability pseudocost branching is the only exact branching rule; strong branching uses the floating-point LP and its results are not used for fixings or bound changes (p. 9). Only Gomory mixed-integer cuts, aggregated and rounded in double precision with directed rounding and post-processed (p. 9). Safe dual proof analysis whose learned constraints are only propagated, and safe propagation for all exact linear constraints (pp. 9–10). Default heuristics are marked safe after their unsafe reductions were switched off; their candidates are repaired by exactsol (round and fix the integers, solve the remaining LP exactly), buffered and processed by best approximate objective (p. 10). Certificates in VIPR with three rules (conic combination, Chvátal–Gomory rounding, disjunction with "unsplitting") (pp. 10–11); presolve is outside: the certificate covers the transformed problem, and the primal bound is checked by feasibility of the final solution in the original space (p. 11); a C++ checker in the VIPR repository and a checker formally verified in HOL4 through CakeML (p. 11). Other designs that bear on the skill: PaPILO compresses column dominations into a directed forest so that transactions never conflict (p. 34); MIP-DD 2.0 adapts modification batches and solving limits (p. 38); cons_fixedvar adds the aggregation as a cut when a solution violates bounds of aggregated variables (pp. 25–26).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: release protocol: SCIP 10.0 against the previous major 9.0 (new features) and the last bugfix release 9.2.4 (bugfixes), with SoPlex, PaPILO, Ipopt, nauty or bliss and sassy versions listed per arm (p. 4); Xeon Gold 5122, one single-threaded run per machine, 5400 s for MILP and 3600 s for MINLP (p. 4); shifted geometric means with shifts of 100 nodes and 1 s; subsets all, affected (arms differ in dual simplex iterations), brackets [t, timelim] and both-solved (p. 5). "all" excludes instances "with numerically inconsistent results" (p. 5), whose number is not stated; the tables imply 1 of 1047 MILP runs and 36 and 29 of 845 MINLP runs (Tables 1–4, pp. 6–7; my arithmetic). MILP: 888 against 886 solved and time 0.98 against 9.0; 0.96 against 9.2.4, down to 0.89 on [1000, timelim] (Tables 1–2, p. 6); nodes rise against 9.0 (relative 1.02 on all; the text says 3%, p. 5), although the abstract claims an improvement in node counts (p. 1); "more reliable" is argued from the bugfixes of 9.2.4, not measured (p. 5). MINLP runs use CPLEX 12.10.0.0 as LP solver (Tables 3–4, p. 7), which §2.1 does not say (p. 4); time 0.94 against 9.0 and 0.91 against 9.2.4 (pp. 6–7). Exact mode (Table 5, "taken from [35]", Eifler's 2025 dissertation, p. 12; the measured version is not stated): fp-reduced (features absent from exact mode disabled) and fp-default, 2 h, 3 seeds; one-solved 350 runs and both-solved 153 (the "count" column presumably counts instance-seed runs, my inference); solved 161 / 235 / 342; slowdown of exact against fp-reduced 2.6 (one-solved) and 3.6 (both-solved), against fp-default 10.8 and 6.8 (p. 11). Table 5 gives 2731.2/255.0 = 10.7 for the printed 10.8, and the "+155%" nodes against fp-reduced hold on both-solved (8462.8 against 3322.1), while on one-solved the increase is 49% (p. 12; my arithmetic). The certificate overhead "does not exceed the time needed for solving" is asserted without data in this report (p. 10). Implied integers: detected for 18.8% of the variables against 3.3%, in 13 ms, but the performance impact is "still only relatively neutral", so detection is off by default (pp. 13–14). Flower separator: neutral on all, gains on 14, 21 and 11 affected instances (Table 7, p. 19). CONOPT against Ipopt: the text reports mean time +7% and nodes +4%, while Table 8 shows time 1.04 and nodes 1.07, i.e. the two are swapped (p. 25; my arithmetic); −40% time on [1000, timelim] and 3 more solved (p. 25). MIP-DD helped fix "24 out of all 51" MIP-related bugs of SCIP 8.0.1 to 8.1.1 (p. 38). Pseudo-Boolean Competition 2024: SCIP solved 759 and FiberSCIP 776 of 1,207 instances (p. 39). No significance tests.
- **D6 Positioning**: exact mode is set against "virtually all available MIP solvers" (p. 6) and built on the prototype of Cook, Koch, Steffy and Wolter and on Jarck's thesis [25, 61] (pp. 9, 39); SCIP's implied-integer result is reported as a "stark contrast" to Gurobi's published gain [6] (p. 13); conflict analysis and delta debugging are imported from pseudo-Boolean, SAT and SMT practice (pp. 17, 38).
- **D7 Writing**: feature by feature, each with exact parameter names (exact/enabled, certificate/filename, presolving/milp/nthreads, conflict/usegenres, …) (pp. 7–8, 17–18); every feature shipped off by default gets its reason (pp. 13, 19, 20, 21, 32); a "Contributions of the Authors" section (pp. 39–40); hedge: "Note that this can be vastly greater or smaller depending on the problem instance" (p. 11). The exact-mode summary "between 3 and 4" and "around 10" rounds the measured pairs 2.6 and 3.6, and 10.8 and 6.8 (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: software release of six packages with licences (p. 2). Exact-mode limits printed: MILP only (p. 7); presolve certified only for binary programs with integer data, through [54] (p. 7); strong branching not safe (p. 9); only GMI cuts (p. 9); a slowdown of about 10 against default (p. 11); certificates do not guarantee a correct implementation (p. 10). Other limits: implied integrality not yet exploited (p. 13); kernel search, ancestral pseudocosts, flower cuts on continuous products and IPColGen off by default, GCG parallel pricing single-threaded by default for determinism (pp. 19–21, 32); GCG separators experimental (p. 28); clique merging discards reductions (p. 35); SoPlex 8.0 without algorithmic changes (p. 35). Future: exploit implied integrality (p. 13); deterministic parallel pricing (p. 32); for the pseudo-Boolean application a problem-type detection, a feasibility definition for numerically difficult instances and an exact mode for the verified categories (p. 39).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) Guard an exact or safe mode by a per-plugin safety flag that the core checks, so that third-party plugins cannot silently break correctness (pp. 7–8).
- (D4) Keep a floating-point copy of every exact bound as a safe outward rounding, so that fast floating-point propagation and dual bounding stay valid (p. 8).

- (D5) Compare a release with the previous major release and with its latest bugfix release, so that the effect of bugfixes and of new features can be told apart (p. 4).
- (D5) Define the "affected" subset mechanically, as the instances on which the arms differ in dual simplex iterations (p. 5).
- (D5) Report an exact solver against a floating-point solver stripped to the same features and against its default, on one-solved and on both-solved subsets (p. 11).
- (D4) Reduce each bug-triggering instance with a delta debugger and add the reduced instance to the regression pipeline (p. 38).

### S049 · Hybrid genetic search for dynamic vehicle routing with time windows (International Conference on Operations Research, Operations Research Proceedings 2023, LNOR 2025, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-58405-3_35; arXiv 2307.11800)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (26 Jul 2023); pp. 1–6 read; references (p. 7) skimmed.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Mohammed Ghannam (first author; HTW Berlin and ZIB) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an applied HTW line on the EURO meets NeurIPS 2022 Vehicle Routing Competition (pp. 2, 5); no MIP solver, numerics or exactness. It is framed as a method that "does not require any pre-training" against learned entries (pp. 2, 6), in the period when the trajectory records the group's ML-for-MIP work.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: DVRPTW: customers arrive in epochs; after each epoch, without knowledge of later epochs, choose which customers to dispatch and their routes; must-go customers and every customer of the last epoch must be dispatched; minimize total drive time (p. 2). Entry: any VRPTW method can be lifted trivially by fixing the dispatched subset per epoch, as the competition's baselines do; the paper instead extends HGS to decide dispatch and routes "in an integrated fashion" (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: adapt the components of HGS (Vidal et al. [12], in the implementation of Kool et al. [5]) to variable-size solutions: giant tours of variable length (p. 4); an initial population from random orderings with optional customers removed with probabilities {0, 0.1, 0.5, 1} (p. 4); ordered crossover on variable-size tours (p. 4); a fitness normalized by the number of dispatched nodes and by epoch progress, with a lateness term (p. 4); three new local-search operators run before the original ones (p. 5). Unchanged: HGS's adaptive penalty weights and its route-improvement heuristics (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: VRPTW on a directed graph with arc costs, time windows, demands and a capacity (p. 1); DVRPTW over epochs t = 1, …, T with must-go customers (p. 2). Data: the full set of 250 competition instances (p. 5). No assumption is stated for the fitness design.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (no proofs): fitness = (p_dist S_dist + p_cap S_cap + p_time S_time + p_late S_late) / (((t − 1)/T + 1)(n + 1)), where S_late is the sum of the time-window ends of the dispatched customers and p_late = 100 (pp. 4–5); crossover never shrinks a solution, a diversity risk "hopefully mitigated" by the delete operator (p. 4); delete removes an optional customer if this improves feasibility, add inserts one if the cost does not rise significantly, swap-out exchanges a dispatched and an undispatched customer if the HGS score improves (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: baselines provided by the organizers, all using HGS as a subroutine and differing only in the dispatch strategy: greedy, lazy, random, oracle (hindsight, itself heuristic, read as an approximate bound), supervised (graph-neural imitation of the oracle) and dqn (reinforcement learning) (p. 5). Xeon Gold 5122 at 3.6 GHz with 96 GB, single-threaded; 5 min per epoch, the oracle 10 min after a greedy start; every solver starts from single-customer routes (p. 5). ### S050 · Combining precision boosting with LP iterative refinement for exact linear optimization (INFORMS Journal on Computing 37(4) 2025, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.2023.0409; arXiv 2311.08037)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v1 (14 Nov 2023; title page dated November 15, 2023); pp. 1–14 read; references (pp. 14–15) skimmed. The published IJOC version was not read; the slips noted below refer to arXiv v1.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Leon Eifler (first author, ZIB), Jules Nicolas-Thouvenin (no affiliation in the extracted text) (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: closes the gap that LP iterative refinement (Gleixner, Steffy and Wolter [14]; Gleixner and Steffy [13]) left open and that the safe-GMI paper [9] (S036) met as failing exact LPs (pp. 2, 9); the mechanism is imported from QSopt_ex (Applegate, Cook, Dash and Espinoza [1]; Espinoza [11]) (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: exact LP over the rationals is needed inside exact MIP [6, 8] and for numerically challenging LPs and theoretical results (p. 1); exact simplex arithmetic is too slow and a fixed high precision is impractical (pp. 1–2). Two state-of-the-art methods: incremental precision boosting (QSopt_ex) and LP iterative refinement (SoPlex) (p. 2). Entry: refinement wins in most cases, boosting is more robust, and refinement "has no reliable way of recovering" when the floating-point LP fails, which was observed on pure LPs and in exact branch and cut (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: keep iterative refinement as the main loop, boost the precision only when refinement fails, then restart refinement in the higher precision (pp. 5–6). Changed against refinement: the old recovery (new settings for presolve, scaling, ratio test, pricing and tolerances, then a restart from scratch) is disabled as too time-consuming (pp. 5–6); new are failure triggers, re-approximation of the rational data at each precision, scaled zero tolerances and stored warm-start bases (pp. 6–8). Changed against boosting: feasibility and optimality tolerances stay fixed because refinement handles them (p. 7); there is no 128-bit step "Due to implementation details", and precision is capped at 1000 bits because some SoPlex tolerances expressed in double precision would round to zero (p. 6).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: LP min{cᵀx | Ax = b, x ≥ ℓ} with rational A of full row rank and all tolerances zero (p. 2); simplex-based methods only, for their warm starts inside branch and bound (p. 2). The final answer is decided by a rational factorization of the final basis or by reconstruction (pp. 3, 10). Convergence rests on the limited-precision LP oracle (4a)–(4d) of [13] (p. 8). Test sets: lplib (Netlib including kennington, Mittelmann's benchmark, Mészáros's collection, LP relaxations of COR@L and of MIPLIB instances up to MIPLIB 2010) and cutlib (100 LPs from exact SCIP runs on MIPLIB 2017 benchmark instances that were difficult for the exact LP solver in [9]); MIP: MIPLIB 2017 benchmark instances solved by default floating-point SCIP 8.0 within 2 h, 132 × 3 seeds = 396 (p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: failure triggers: (i) numerical trouble: a non-optimal status from a numerically singular basis or cycling, or an "optimal" basis whose residuals are close to or above one, for which refinement is not guaranteed to converge (pp. 5–6); (ii) stalling: two consecutive iterations in which the maximum violation falls by less than a factor of 2^4 (extracted as "24") (p. 6); (iii) incorrect status: infeasibility or unboundedness claimed on the auxiliary LPs (2) and (3), which are feasible and bounded by construction, or on the original LP after feasibility or boundedness has been established (p. 6). Boosting: precision 64 → 192 → 288 … bits, at most 1000 (p. 6); the rational A, b, ℓ, c re-approximated at each level, since roundoff in the data can itself produce a wrong basis (p. 6); zero tolerances 10^(−⌊p′c⌋) with p′ = p log 2/log 10, c = 1 for the zero tolerance of the solve and c = 0.625 for pivot selection, chosen to reproduce the double-precision values (p. 7); restart from the last optimal basis after stalling, and from bases stored "if the number of iterations is a power of two, and at least all 10000 iterations" after numerical trouble (p. 7); separate stored bases for the original, the feasibility and the unboundedness problem (p. 8). Convergence: with arbitrary precision a limited-precision oracle "becomes possible to construct", giving an "unconditional convergence guarantee" (p. 8); the detailed proof is "beyond the scope of this paper" and the argument is intuitive, conditioned on the LU update (Bartels–Golub, backward stable, would suffice; Forrest–Tomlin is not proven backward stable) (p. 8). Pure boosting lacks a proof because its tolerances shrink at the rate at which precision grows; the combination can keep η < 1 fixed (pp. 8–9).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: three questions, with the expectation from the literature stated before the test (p. 9). All settings in SoPlex 6.0.3 with GMP 6.1.2; the exact-rational branch of SCIP with PaPILO 2.0.1; Xeon Gold 5122, 7200 s; LP shifts 0.1 s and 10 iterations, MIP shifts 1 s and 100 nodes; one run per LP because variability is "not as pronounced" there, three seeds for MIP (p. 9). Settings ir-double, boosting-pure and ir-boosting, all ending with a rational factorization of the final basis, which "clearly outperformed" reconstruction on average in preliminary tests (p. 10). Only LPs whose initial double-precision basis is not optimal are reported (84 lplib, 91 cutlib), timed after the shared initial solve (p. 10). Solved: lplib 79 / 44 / 79 and cutlib 91 / 26 / 91 (ir-boosting / ir-double / boosting-pure) (Table 2, pp. 10–11). On the all-solved subset (44 and 26 LPs) ir-boosting is 3.5% faster (lplib) and 16.2% slower (cutlib) than ir-double, and boosting-pure is 54.8% and 100% slower than ir-boosting; the authors state that this subset "is biased in favor of ir-double", because it keeps refinement's successful restarts and drops the failed ones (Table 1, pp. 10–11). Slip (my arithmetic): the next comparison is introduced as one between boosting-pure and ir-boosting, which solve the same set, but reads "ir-boosting is 17.3% faster than ir-double" on lplib and "5.5% slower than ir-double" on cutlib (p. 10); Table 2 gives 4.12 against 5.98 for boosting-pure on lplib (31.1% faster) and 0.96 against 0.91 on cutlib (5.5% slower), so the cutlib figure matches boosting-pure and the lplib figure matches no column (p. 11). Refinement fails on 71% of cutlib (pp. 10–11; 65 of 91 in Table 2). Split by boosting: ir-boosting 20.9% faster where no boost occurred and 2.1% faster where one did, against an unnamed partner (p. 11). MIP: four settings (cut post-processing densmall or safegmi from [9], each with ir-double or ir-boosting); "To reduce the amount of performance variability" the analysis is restricted to the 146 runs in which some setting boosted (p. 12; 146 exceeds the 132 instances, so these are instance-seed pairs, my inference). Two definitions of speed-up in one section: 1 − t_new/t_old for the boosting comparisons, t_slow/t_fast − 1 for densmall against safegmi (31.2% and 34.1%) (pp. 12–13; my arithmetic). No significance tests.
- **D6 Positioning**: a "natural combination" of the two state-of-the-art methods (p. 2), credited to Dhiflaoui et al. [7] and to Applegate et al. and Espinoza [1, 11] for boosting and to the authors' own [13, 14] for refinement (p. 2); the literature lacks a convergence proof for pure precision boosting (p. 8); first implementation of both methods in one LP solver (p. 9); future LU work from Lourenco et al. - **D7 Writing**: the literature's expectation is restated and the experiment framed as verifying it in one solver (p. 9); a hope stated before the MIP test (boosting "may help to remove the need" for cut weakening, p. 12) is answered against itself: boosting "is rather a complementary technique" (p. 13); a theoretical contribution is claimed on an intuitive argument (p. 8); typos remain in the preprint (Algorithm 2 referred to as "Algorithm 1", p. 4; "bybrid", p. 12).

### S061 · Scylla: a matrix-free fix-propagate-and-project heuristic for mixed-integer optimization (International Conference on Operations Research, Operations Research Proceedings 2023, LNOR 2025, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-58405-3_9; arXiv 2307.03466)
**Read level**: full. Text read: arXiv v2 (26 Jul 2023); pp. 1–6 read; references (pp. 6–7) skimmed. In Algorithm 1.1 (p. 3) the extraction maps symbols to other glyphs (for example the glyph "ě" stands for ≥, "pkq" for (k), and a left curly quotation mark for =); the formulas below are read through that mapping.

**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Gioni Mexi (first and corresponding author), Mathieu Besançon, Suresh Bolusani, Antonia Chmiela, Alexander Hoen, all ZIB, Interactive Optimization and Learning (p. 1); Gleixner last, ZIB and HTW (p. 1) · **Place in the trajectory**: an early matrix-free MIP heuristic built on the first-order LP solver PDLP, ahead of the GPU methods of the trajectory; the feasibility-pump baseline is Mexi's own open-source collection [16] (p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: LP-based heuristics assume an optimal LP solution and cheap re-optimization; this breaks during presolving, where no LP solution exists yet, in applications where simplex re-optimization is expensive [14], and for stand-alone heuristics on large instances, where factorization-based LP is costly (pp. 1–2). Entry: first-order methods trade LP accuracy against time, a trade-off "rarely reasoned about" in LP and MIP (p. 2).
- **D2 Key idea and design**: a feasibility pump whose projection is replaced by a limited number of PDHG iterations (PDLP in OR-Tools) under relaxed stopping criteria and whose rounding is fix-and-propagate (p. 2), with the objective moved toward an ℓ1 distance as in the objective feasibility pump (p. 4). Changed in PDLP: warm start from the previous primal-dual pair and custom stopping criteria (p. 3). Matrix-free: only sparse matrix-vector products (pp. 2–3). SCIP presolve runs first (p. 2).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{cᵀx : Ax ≥ b, ℓ ≤ x ≤ u, x_j ∈ Z for j ∈ I} (Algorithm 1.1, p. 3); no structure exploited. Test set: MIPLIB 2017 and the instances of FP 2.0 [10], minus those solved in SCIP presolve, 283 instances (p. 5); which part of MIPLIB 2017 is meant is not stated ("the benchmark library MIPLIB 2017", p. 5; "a large set of instances from the MIPLIB 2017 collection", p. 5).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (no proofs): outer loop K: PDLP iterations until approximately optimal, stalling bounds or an iteration limit; return if MIP-feasible; fix-and-propagate; perturb on cycling; objective update ĉᵀx = α^K (√|I| / ‖c‖) cᵀx + (1 − α^K) Δ(x, x̂^(K)) with α = 0.9 (Algorithm 1.1, p. 3; p. 4). Stopping tolerance ε^(1) = 0.01, reduced geometrically, ε^(K+1) = max{β ε^(K), 10^-8} with β = 0.98 (p. 4). Fix-and-propagate fixes fractional integers and propagates; at an empty domain it continues, ignoring the constraints that cause it, and fixes the remaining variables to their reference values, so the result is integer-feasible "but not necessarily LP-feasible" (p. 4). Cycling is broken by random perturbation as in [9] (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: baseline: the open-source feasibility pump of [16] with SoPlex 6.0.2, which the authors extended with PDLP (p. 5); Xeon Gold 6338 at 2.0 GHz with 1024 GB, 3600 s; both methods with ten seeds "To mitigate the impact of performance variability" (p. 5); 2830 instance-seed runs, which the text calls instances (p. 5). Metrics: solutions found, found by one method only, wins (10% better in gap or time), shifted geometric means of the gap to the best known solution and of time, and model-building overhead; the shifts are not stated (Table 1, p. 6). All runs: FP better in gap (27.46 against 40.92) and time (10.06 s against 17.30 s), with 2268 against 2270 solutions found (p. 6); the loss is explained by easy LPs and effective simplex warm starts (p. 5). Root LP 1 to 60 s: FP finds more solutions (660 against 635) of better quality, Scylla is faster (36.75 s against 43.16 s) despite 1.99 s of overhead (pp. 5–6). Root LP above 60 s: Scylla finds 205 against 163 (42 more) in 234.80 s against 974.66 s, "nearly four times faster" (p. 5); the table ratio is 4.15 (my arithmetic). Which solver's LP time defines the brackets is not stated (p. 6); "significantly" is used without a test (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: continues the feasibility pump of Fischetti, Glover and Lodi and its refinements [9, 4, 2, 10] and fix-and-propagate heuristics [6, 11] (pp. 2, 4); builds on PDLP by Applegate et al. [3] (p. 3); no novelty claim beyond the combination.
- **D7 Writing**: the overall loss is reported first and explained ("This is an expected result", p. 5); an implementation overhead is separated from the method ("tied to the current implementation and not to the heuristic itself", p. 5); the conclusion names the subset where the method is preferred (pp. 5–6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: a heuristic and its evaluation (pp. 5–6). Limits: it loses on the whole test set (p. 5); the OR-Tools interface rebuilds the model in every pump iteration (p. 5); the parallelism of sparse matrix-vector products is not used (p. 5). Future: a multithreaded sparse BLAS, MIQPs with diagonal Hessian, and an ℓ2 distance that avoids auxiliary variables for general integers (pp. 5–6).
