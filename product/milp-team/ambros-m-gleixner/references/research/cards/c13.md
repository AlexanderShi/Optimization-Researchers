### S012 · Learning to schedule heuristics in branch and bound (Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 34, 2021, arXiv 2103.10294)
**Read level**: full. I read the whole arXiv v1 text (stamp 18 Mar 2021, p. 1): pp. 1–18, including the references (pp. 14–15), Appendix A on the MIQP (pp. 16–17) and Appendix B on implementation details (pp. 17–18). Figures were read through their captions and the prose. Not read: the NeurIPS 2021 camera-ready version and the code repository (p. 10, fn. 2).
**Role**: core (student-led group work) · **Coauthors**: Antonia Chmiela (first and corresponding author, ZIB), Elias B. Khalil (Toronto), Andrea Lodi (Polytechnique Montréal), Sebastian Pokutta (ZIB, TU Berlin) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the NeurIPS paper of his ML-for-MIP line (06 T8). Funding is acknowledged from MATH+ (EXC-2046/1) and the BMBF Research Campus MODAL (p. 1). The MATH+ project EF1-9 (heads Gleixner and Pokutta, 2021–2022; 06-trajectory) is probably its frame, but the text does not name it (inferred). Gleixner is third of five authors. Chmiela's PhD (2025) was supervised by Pokutta (06-trajectory, 2 Jun 2025 row).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters through a gap in solver practice. Much MIP research designs new heuristics, but the question of how to manage several heuristics in one solver "has not received equal attention". Solvers follow hard-coded rules tuned on broad test sets, while heuristic performance depends on the instance (abstract; p. 2). The gap is shown by a measurement first: the success rates of ten heuristics, sorted for GISP, are "far from being sorted" for FCMNF (Fig. 1, p. 2). The practical framing is that applications need good solutions early (abstract). In Berthold 2013a, primal heuristics improved the primal bound by about 80% and cut solving time by up to 30% (p. 2). The problem statement: learn from data on "the duration and success of every heuristic call" a schedule that decides when and for how long each heuristic runs (p. 2), given a homogeneous training set from one problem class (p. 4).
  - Transferable: T1.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A schedule S = ⟨(h1, τ1), …, (hk, τk)⟩ controls two things inside the solver's heuristic loop (pp. 4–5):
  - the order, through priorities; the loop stops at the first new incumbent;
  - the duration, through iteration limits.

  The primal integral depends on the incumbent sequence, on pruning and on the tree, so the paper optimizes a surrogate over logged nodes instead. Problem (PS) minimizes the total iterations subject to finding a feasible solution at ≥ α of the nodes (p. 5). Feasible solutions replace incumbents to get more data, and incumbents gave "no benefit" in preliminary experiments (p. 5). (PS) is solved by a greedy algorithm adapted from Streeter 2007 (pp. 6–8). Changed against Streeter: nodes rather than instances are the units; there is no suspend-and-resume, so each heuristic appears at most once; and iteration proxies replace time (p. 6). The paper also adds an extension move and a cost normalization (p. 7). Changed against SCIP's default: only the 16 controlled heuristics are scheduled, and all others run after the schedule with their default settings (p. 10). Kept: the heuristic loop itself.
  - Transferable: T1, T4.
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The MIP is min cᵀx s.t. Ax ≤ b, x_i ∈ Z for i ∈ I (p. 3). The model assumes that node indices are unique even across trees of different instances (p. 3), and that instances come from one class (p. 4). The test class is GISP, chosen as primally hard: revenue 100 per vertex, cost 1 per edge, removable-edge probability 0.75 (p. 10). Two instance groups:
  - DIMACS graphs: the twelve dense graphs and the train/test split of Khalil et al. 2017, with 20 instances per graph, giving 120 training and 120 test instances (pp. 10–11);
  - random graphs [L,U] with edge probability 0.1: nine size buckets from 150–160 to 550–560 nodes, with 25 training and 10 test instances each (p. 11).

  FCMNF appears only in Fig. 1 (p. 2). There is no correctness argument, since the controlled components are heuristics.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Components: 16 heuristics, ten diving and six LNS, covering "the majority of the more complex heuristics implemented in SCIP" (p. 10).
  - Duration proxies: the maximal diving depth for diving and the sub-MIP node limit for LNS (p. 10). Time is rejected because it "can lead to nondeterministic behavior" and would need exclusive machines (pp. 4, 6, 18). Costs are normalized across heuristic classes by the average time per iteration t^h_avg (p. 7; Fig. 4).
  - Model devices: (PS) as an MIQP with O(|H||N_X|) variables, written out with its linearizations omitted (App. A, pp. 16–17; p. 6). (PS) is NP-hard as a generalization of the pipelined set cover problem (p. 6).
  - Counterexample: Streeter's greedy, a 4-approximation in his setting (p. 7), "can perform arbitrarily bad" here. With 100 nodes and one heuristic that solves one node in one iteration and 99 nodes in 100 iterations, the greedy returns ⟨(h, 1)⟩, which is infeasible for any α > 0.01 (p. 7).
  - Fix: allow the last heuristic to be extended, charging only the increment τ − τ_{j−1} (p. 7). This gives Algorithm 1 (p. 8), with a worked three-heuristic example (Fig. 5, pp. 8–9).
  - No guarantee: the modified greedy does not enforce α. The observed schedules reach "α = 98% or above" (p. 8).
  - Data collection: a modified SCIP runs heuristics "in a sandbox environment" in a shadow mode. New incumbents are recorded but not passed back, so a single B&B run per training instance yields the data set D (p. 9). This is an improved version of Khalil et al.'s 2017 shadow mode (p. 9). Its stated leak: SCIP cannot hide all heuristic actions, and switching off propagation while diving would distort the data (p. 18).
  - Transferable: T2, T3, T4.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup: SCIP 7.0 with CPLEX 12.10.0.0, on Xeon E5-2660 v3 nodes with 128 GB. The time limit is 2 h, and 4 h for data collection. One process runs per machine because the primal integral depends on time (p. 11).
  - Variability: "extensive cross-validation in addition to multiple random seeds" (p. 11). The number of seeds is not stated.
  - Baselines:
    - default SCIP;
    - "scip tuned": frequency offset 0 for all diving heuristics (fn. 4), built to emulate an expert as "an even stronger baseline" (p. 11);
    - SMAC schedules, given the same training compute of 100 h per training set (p. 12).
  - Metrics: relative primal integral against default (mean ± std), the fraction of instances with a strictly better primal integral or bound than scip tuned, and on DIMACS the incumbent times, heuristic calls and time, gap and primal-dual integral (Tables 1–5, pp. 11–13). Table 5 uses geometric means with no shift stated (p. 13).
  - Table 1 (random graphs, pp. 11): the schedules reach 0.58–0.99, against 0.76–1.08 for scip tuned. scip tuned is worse than default on three of the nine test sets (1.08, 1.05, 1.03). Rows [400,410] and [450,460] agree in eight of nine columns, which the text does not explain.
  - Table 2 (p. 12): by the text, schedules beat scip tuned on 69–76% of instances in primal integral and on 59–70% in primal bound. The table prints 0.68–0.76 and 0.55–0.70.
  - Table 3: diving plus LNS gives only a "marginal" further gain (p. 12).
  - SMAC: worse on average than the greedy schedules. It took over four days per schedule, against four hours for the greedy "with enough machines". The best of ten SMAC schedules was picked on the same-size test set (p. 12). As read, this selection favours SMAC, and the text does not remark on it.
  - DIMACS (Table 5, p. 13): primal integral 934.48 (default), 555.75 (tuned) and 470.73 (schedule), the 49% reduction of the abstract. Against tuned the reduction is about 15% (my arithmetic from Table 5).
  - Losses and tests: no per-instance losses are reported. "Significant" and "significantly" are used without a test (pp. 11, 12).
  - Transferable: T5, T6, T7.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper sets itself apart from three lines (p. 3):
  - Khalil et al. 2017 learn at which nodes to run heuristics and then follow the default rules;
  - Hendel 2018 and Hendel et al. 2018 use bandits that "only adapt the order";
  - hyperparameter tuning (ParamILS, SMAC) is called extremely costly and does not exploit problem structure.

  The scheduling model starts from Streeter 2007, with three stated differences (pp. 6–7). The ML-for-MIP context is Hutter, He, Khalil 2016, Nair 2020, Baltean-Lugojan 2019 and Kruber 2017 (p. 1). The metric comes from Berthold 2013a (p. 3) and performance variability from Lodi & Tramontani 2013 (p. 11). Criticism is by contrast ("In contrast to the method proposed in this paper", p. 3), never direct.
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Claims: a priority claim in the abstract ("the first data-driven framework") and a numbered contribution list mapped to sections (pp. 2–3).
  - Exposition: one exemplary instance in Fig. 2 (p. 2) and a worked example of Algorithm 1 (pp. 8–9). The implementation appendix opens by conceding that theory does not always translate into practice (p. 17).
  - Hedging is thin: "significant improvement" with no test (p. 11).
  - Slips: the Table 2 ranges in the text do not match the table (p. 12). Typos: "Even through" (p. 2), "alloted" (p. 4), "heurisitcs" (p. 8), "Furtheremore" (p. 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is algorithmic: a formulation, a greedy solver, a data-collection scheme and a computational study (pp. 2–3, 13). Stated limits (p. 13):
  - one "one-size-fits-all" schedule per instance class, with contextual schedules called "not trivial";
  - no learning-theoretic analysis or generalization guarantee, with Balcan et al. 2019 named as a possible framework;
  - the shadow mode's leak (p. 18).

  Where it went (by title only; not read): Chmiela et al., *Online Learning for Scheduling MIP Heuristics*, CPAIOR 2023 (06-trajectory, 2023 row), addresses the one-schedule limit. No ML-titled paper with Gleixner appears after 2023 (06 T8).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (variant ⚠, group): the paper constructs a stronger baseline rather than a reduced one: the hand-tuned "scip tuned" (p. 11) and SMAC with equal training compute (p. 12). Table 5 prints both the default and the tuned figures (p. 13), but the abstract's headline 49% is against default only. Against tuned the reduction is about 15% (my arithmetic). The "even stronger baseline" is worse than default on three of nine test sets (Table 1, p. 11).
- Method 1 (variant ⚠, group): the solver is instrumented, not rewritten (step 3): the shadow mode records per-node heuristic outcomes in one run (p. 9), and its leaks are listed (p. 18). Fig. 1 turns the claim that static rules underperform on specific families into data (p. 2). Here the measurement motivates a method; no belief of the field is tested and counted.
- Method 3 (variant ⚠, group): the limits are printed without counts (p. 13, p. 18). The one-schedule limit matches the 2023 online-learning paper by title (06-trajectory; not read).
- Heuristics (card only):
  - Heuristic 7, evidence: the primal integral is the main metric for a heuristic component (p. 3), with time to first and best incumbent also reported (p. 13).
  - Heuristic 8, variant ⚠: the seed count is not given (p. 11), "significant" carries no test (pp. 11–12), and geometric means come without a shift (p. 13). This also supports the SKILL's Inner Tension on significance testing (no test in a co-authored text).
- 06 T8: consistent with "follower". The first author is a student, and the ML expertise sits with Khalil, Lodi and Pokutta (p. 1).
- New-pattern candidates:
  - a deterministic effort proxy instead of wall-clock time (pp. 4, 6, 18);
  - shadow-mode instrumentation for unbiased per-node data (pp. 9, 18);
  - a counterexample before reusing an approximation algorithm from a neighbouring setting (p. 7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- T1. When the target metric (primal integral) depends on the tree, optimize a surrogate over logged nodes: minimize total heuristic iterations subject to finding a solution at ≥ α of the nodes. Count feasible solutions rather than incumbents to get more data (p. 5).
- T2. Shadow-mode data collection: run every heuristic at each node in a sandbox that records incumbents but does not return them, so one B&B run per training instance gives unbiased per-node data. List what the sandbox cannot hide (pp. 9, 18).
- T3. Control effort by deterministic proxies (maximal diving depth, sub-MIP node limit) and make heuristic classes comparable by dividing by the average time per iteration (pp. 4, 7, 10, 18).
- T4. Before reusing an approximation algorithm from a neighbouring setting, build a minimal counterexample under your own constraints (100 nodes, one heuristic). Then repair the move set: allow extending the last action and charge only the increment (p. 7).
- T5. Emulate the expert: add a hand-tuned setting for the instance class as a second baseline, and give a black-box tuner the same training budget (pp. 11–12).
- T6. Size-transfer grid: train on each size bucket and test on all buckets, so that generalization from small to large instances is visible in one table (Table 1, p. 11).
- T7. Run one process per machine when the metric integrates over time (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "Unfortunately, we can show that the resulting schedule can perform arbitrarily bad in our setting." (p. 7)
- "Since the primal integral depends on time, we ran one process at a time on every machine, allowing for accurate on time measurements." (p. 11)

### S024 · Learn to relax: Integrating 0-1 integer linear programming with pseudo-Boolean conflict-driven search (Constraints 26(1), 2021, DOI 10.1007/s10601-020-09318-x)
**Read level**: full. I read the whole 34-page manuscript, including the acknowledgements (pp. 30–31) and the references (pp. 31–34). The plots of Figs. 3, 4 and 6–12 (pp. 17–27) are garbled glyphs in the text layer, so their content comes from captions, prose and Table 5. Not read: the journal's typeset version (pp. 26–55), the CPAIOR 2020 extended abstract (INDEX D006), and the repository and Zenodo deposits [26] (pp. 15, 17, 18, 26).
**Role**: core (group work) · **Coauthors**: Jo Devriendt (first author; Lund and Copenhagen) and Jakob Nordström (Copenhagen and Lund) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: his first paper with Nordström (06-trajectory, Jan 2021 row). The starting point was Jan Elffers' RoundingSat–SoPlex experiments (p. 30). Gleixner was funded by the BMBF Research Campus MODAL; the first and third authors by the Swedish Research Council (p. 30). Who wrote what is not stated. The MIP side (SoPlex, SCIP, Gomory cuts) matches Gleixner's expertise and the proof-complexity side matches Nordström's (inferred).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters through a failure of the rival paradigm. Conflict-driven pseudo-Boolean (PB) solvers use cutting planes, which are exponentially stronger than the resolution behind CDCL (p. 2). Yet they "can sometimes get hopelessly stuck even when the linear programming (LP) relaxation is infeasible over the reals" (abstract). Rationally infeasible formulas need only LP, and Farkas' lemma guarantees an easy cutting-planes proof, yet small such examples are beyond PB solvers [31] (p. 2). MIP solvers detect such formulas at once, but their conflict analysis works on clauses extracted from the constraints (p. 2). The question: "Is it possible to combine pseudo-Boolean solving and mixed integer linear programming to get the best of both worlds?" (p. 2). The challenges are listed before the method (p. 3):
  - LP as a preprocessing step does not help, because formulas turn infeasible after a few assignments;
  - an LP before every decision is orders of magnitude too slow;
  - backtracking after an infeasible LP needs a violated constraint;
  - a floating-point LP must feed a solver that must reason soundly;
  - should LP cuts go to the PB solver, and learned constraints to the LP?
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Interleave incremental LP solving with conflict-driven PB search (pp. 3–4; Algorithms 1–2, pp. 10–11):
  - LP infeasibility yields a Farkas constraint that starts PB conflict analysis;
  - an LP solution yields Gomory (MIR) cuts and phase values.

  Changed against PB search:
  - an LP check after a conflict-free propagation phase and before the next decision (p. 9; Algorithm 1, lines 14–16, p. 10);
  - phases initialized from the rounded root LP solution (p. 12).

  Changed against MIP (SCIP): conflict analysis runs on the linear constraints themselves rather than on clauses. Clause extraction is said to incur "an exponential loss in reasoning power", which is not proved here ("It is not hard to prove formally", p. 6).

  Changed against LCG-glucose (p. 5):
  - the LP stops early when its budget runs out;
  - the Farkas constraint enters conflict analysis as a full linear constraint;
  - the LP covers the whole formula.

  Kept: RoundingSat's search loop, restarts coordinated with database cleaning, and linear bounding for optimization (pp. 7–8, 15).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - Model: 0-1 ILPs written as normalized PB constraints with nonnegative integer coefficients and positive degree (pp. 1, 6). Optimization works by adding an objective-bound constraint after each solution (p. 8).
  - Integer limit: coefficients must stay below RoundingSat's 10^9. Constraints are computed in 128-bit precision and then scaled down, and literals with coefficients "a million times smaller than the average coefficient" are eliminated (p. 15). Some MIPLIB instances had to be rescaled (p. 18). As read, the rounding rule of the scale-down step is not spelled out (p. 15).
  - Soundness assumption: every reasoning step uses exact integer arithmetic (p. 6).
  - LP use: clausal inputs without objective run with all LP features off (p. 15).
  - Test material:
    - rationally infeasible dominating-set formulas [31] (p. 16);
    - 783 knapsack instances from Pisinger [54] (p. 17);
    - PB16 DEC-SMALLINT-LIN (1783 instances) and OPT-SMALLINT-LIN (1600) (pp. 18–19);
    - MIPLIB 2017 0-1 ILPs: 291 optimization instances and 556 decision versions, built by replacing the objective with "at least the best known value" and "strictly better". The caveat is stated: rounding, or a best-known value that is not optimal, can flip a decision version's status (p. 18);
    - composed pebbling–PHP formulas (pp. 25–26).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Farkas constraint: the floating-point multipliers λ_k from SoPlex are scaled and rounded to integers, and the combination is computed in exact integer arithmetic, so it "is guaranteed to be implied by ϕ". It is used only if it is still falsified. Otherwise the search resumes and the LP basis is reset, so the same multipliers are not returned again (p. 10).
  - When the LP is called: only at three moments: the start, a restart before database cleanup, and the first decision after backtracking (p. 11).
  - Total LP effort: the ratio of LP pivots to conflicts must stay at p/c ≤ r, with separate counters for root and deep calls (p. 11).
  - Per-call budget: b = b_init · 2^t, where t counts the forcibly terminated calls, so b never decreases (p. 12). Pivots serve as the time proxy, "which also allows us to obtain reproducible results" (p. 11). In preliminary runs a single LP call took more than the 5000 s limit (p. 11).
  - Cuts: the MIR formula (2), with a worked example (p. 13). Gomory cuts are computed in exact arithmetic and discarded if no longer violated after rounding, with at most γ = 100 candidates (p. 14). Candidates are normalized, sorted by rational slack and filtered by parallelism (cosine ≤ 0.1, from [66]) (pp. 14–15). Cuts with zero dual multiplier are removed. Gomory cuts are erased by the LBD heuristic, scored by their number of variables (fns. 8–9, p. 14).
  - Parameters: b_init = 1000; r = 1; for decision problems the LP minimizes the sum of all variables (p. 15).
  - Proof devices: Farkas' lemma (p. 8) and the pebbling–PHP construction. Pebbling on pyramid graphs makes the formulas hard for tree-like reasoning, and pigeonhole formulas make them hard for resolution, while cutting planes "should in principle" refute them easily (pp. 25–26; fn. 17). No theorem is proved.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup: AMD Opteron 6238 nodes, one single-thread run per 6-core node, 5000 s (pp. 15–16).
  - Solvers (p. 16): SCIP 7.0.0 with SoPlex 5.0.0 and PaPILO 1.0, symmetry off, single thread; RoundingSat at commit 69846924; the hybrids RS+SPX, RS+SPX+GC and RS+SPX+GC+LC, all with SoPlex 5.0.0; Sat4j at commit 94b8f653 in its "Both" mode, which runs two threads and "was in effect given twice the time" (fn. 13); NaPS at commit 7aaa54f4. Gurobi 9.0.2 and CPLEX 12.10.0 were run only on pebbling–PHP, on an Intel Core i7-5600U because of licence restrictions (p. 26).
  - Correctness: outputs on 5536 decision and 2839 optimization instances were "consistent with those from other solvers" (p. 15).
  - Results:
    - Rationally infeasible formulas: SCIP and the hybrid refute all of them at the initial LP (pp. 16–17).
    - KNAP: SCIP solves almost all 783. RoundingSat solves about two thirds, Gomory cuts add 70 solved instances and learned-constraint cuts 10 more. SCIP's dynamic program for pure knapsacks is named as a reason (p. 17).
    - Table 5 (p. 19): MIPLIBopt SCIP 125, RS 78, RS+SPX 101; PB16opt SCIP 1057, RS 862, RS+SPX+GC+LC 993, NaPS 896; PB16dec RS 1472 (best), RS+SPX 1453; MIPLIBdec SCIP 264, RS+SPX 263.
    - Cut options change solved counts by at most 2 (p. 22).
    - Pebbling–PHP: the RoundingSat-based solvers are fast. Gurobi and SCIP "perform very poorly", and CPLEX is better but "nowhere close". The LP brings no gain here (p. 27).
  - Analysis tools:
    - the solver ranking is the same under PAR-2 as under solved counts (p. 19);
    - conflict counts serve as a proxy for search quality (p. 19);
    - a usage analysis of constraint types (Figs. 10–11, pp. 22–25).
  - Losses are reported: RS+SPX loses on satisfiable PB16dec (p. 19), and SCIP wins the MIPLIB sets and PB16opt (pp. 19, 25). There are no seeds or variability runs.
  - Transferable: T2, T4, T5, T6.
- **D6 Literature positioning**: The paper places itself in four communities:
  - PB solving: Hooker's generalized resolution, bsolo, and [17] (pp. 4–5);
  - CP with LP: Eplex, B-Prolog, and LCG-glucose, with three stated differences (pp. 4–5);
  - SMT: there the LP runs to completion and conflict analysis does not use derivations over linear constraints (p. 5);
  - MIP: SCIP with clause-based conflict analysis [1, 2] (pp. 5–6).

  The primer on PB conflict analysis is written "in MIP language" for MIP readers (p. 5). On cuts from learned constraints the paper agrees with [1, 60], which found clause cuts not worthwhile, and extends the finding to non-clausal learned constraints (p. 22). It credits Jan Elffers' experiments as its starting point and Nikolaj Bjørner for suggesting knapsack problems (p. 30).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**:
  - Framing: the aim is stated as a question (p. 2), and the challenges are posed as questions before the method (p. 3).
  - Limits conceded up front: an exhaustive evaluation is impossible, "if nothing else because the parameter space is infinite" (p. 9).
  - Assumptions labelled: "this is an assumption, and not an iron-clad fact" (p. 23). Speculation is flagged as such ("If we allow ourselves to speculate", p. 29).
  - Negative results in plain words: "does not seem to pay off" (p. 22), "We do not know whether" (p. 22), "We find it similarly disappointing" (p. 29).
  - Tension: the abstract speaks of "approaching a 'best-of-both-worlds' scenario", while §4.5 concedes that SCIP wins even on the PB competition's "home turf" (p. 25). The conclusion again claims results "close to a kind of 'best-of-both-worlds'" (p. 28).
  - Slip: the counts of clausal-only instances differ between fn. 11 (54 MIPLIBdec, 305 PB16dec; p. 15) and p. 19 (35 and 298). The difference is unexplained.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution claimed is the first combination of LP relaxations and cut generation with PB conflict analysis over linear inequalities (pp. 4, 28). The solver is a proof of concept, "mainly built for evaluation purposes" (p. 28). Stated limits (pp. 22, 24–25, 27–29):
  - Gomory cuts bring no overall gain;
  - passing learned constraints to the LP brings no gain;
  - "learned Farkas constraints" are less useful than ordinary learned constraints;
  - the LP is useless on pebbling–PHP;
  - the LP budget is static;
  - cut erasure is "fairly aggressive".

  Future work (pp. 29–30): a dynamic LP budget set by the measured benefit, LP-guided variable selection, MIP presolving for PB formulas, and continuous variables handled through the LP and Farkas constraints. Where it went (by title only, from 06-trajectory; not read):
  - The claim that "no MIP solvers currently have this kind of conflict analysis implemented" (p. 27) matches Mexi, Serrano, Berthold, Gleixner, Nordström, *Cut-Based Conflict Analysis in Mixed Integer Programming* (IJOC, online 2025).
  - The MIP-presolving item (p. 30) matches Hoen, Oertel, Gleixner, Nordström, *Certifying MIP-Based Presolve Reductions for 0–1 Integer Linear Programs* (CPAIOR 2024).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (evidence ✅, group): the floating-point LP produces the Farkas and Gomory multipliers, and exact integer arithmetic decides validity. Results no longer violated are discarded, or the basis is reset (pp. 3, 10, 14); the computation uses 128-bit precision (p. 15). This is Method 5 outside MIP, inside a pseudo-Boolean solver. The authors note that this makes the approach usable for "verification or theorem proving applications" (p. 6).

**Role**: core (student-led, two authors) · **Coauthor**: Jakob Witzig (first author, ZIB) (pp. 1, 3) · **Position in trajectory**: part of Witzig's PhD project. Gleixner mentored it; Koch was the formal advisor (06-trajectory, "Mentored, not formally supervised"). - **D1 Problem and entry point**: The paper enters through a separation in the literature. Diving heuristics and conflict analysis "have mostly been studied independently": heuristics for early good solutions, conflict analysis for pruning and the dual bound (abstract, p. 3). Both components matter. Disabling all primal heuristics costs about 11%–32% (Berthold 2014a) or 5%–15% (Achterberg & Wunderling 2013) in solving time. Conflict analysis speeds up the proof of optimality by as much as 28% on affected instances (p. 4). The paper combines the two "in two different ways" (p. 3):
  - a diving heuristic that aims at producing conflict constraints;
  - a diving heuristic guided by conflict information.
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**:
  - Farkas diving: drive the dual LP toward a valid Farkas proof. The rounding that reduces the violation of the Farkas condition (4) turns out to be rounding toward the best bound with respect to the objective, the optimistic "pseudo solution" (pp. 7–8). Fractionality breaks ties when c_j = 0. The score is |c_j|·d_j·f_j^rel (eqs. (8)–(10), pp. 7–8).
  - Conflict diving: apply variable locks to conflict constraints (conflict locks) and mix them with model locks, weighted by κ (p. 10). It reverses coefficient diving's rounding to the "risky" direction (eq. (11)) and keeps coefficient diving's ordering (eq. (12)), an aggressive fail-fast strategy (p. 10).
  - Kept: SCIP's generic diving scheme with propagation and conflict analysis on infeasibility (Algorithm 1, p. 6).
  - The claim: "the first LP-based heuristics for MIP which explicitly produce and exploit conflict constraints" (p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - Model: general MIPs min{cᵀx : Ax ≥ b, ℓ ≤ x ≤ u, x_j ∈ Z for j ∈ I}, with bounds in R ∪ {±∞} (p. 5).
  - Assumptions for Farkas diving: local bounds of integer variables are integral without loss of generality, and fractional integer variables have zero reduced cost by complementary slackness (p. 7). The heuristic runs only on instances with a nonzero objective (p. 12).
  - Conflict locks count only the conflict constraints currently in the pool, without VSIDS-style decay (p. 10).
  - Test set "MMMC": MIPLIB 3, 2003 and 2010 plus COR@L, after removing duplicates and problems "known to be numerically unstable", which leaves 488 problems. With four seeds this gives 1952 instances (p. 11). The removed problems are not named in this text.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Derivation device: write the dual LP (6), note that (y*, r* − c) satisfies (3), and compute how tightening a bound reduces the violation of (4) by |c_j|·d_j (pp. 7–8). A primal rule is read off a dual target.
  - Lemma 2 (p. 9): if a Farkas proof contributes a conflict up-lock (down-lock) to x_j, then some model constraint contributes a variable up-lock (down-lock). The proof is a one-line sign argument from y ≥ 0. Conflict locks therefore refine model locks toward the constraints "actively involved in pruning" (p. 9).
  - Contrast with VSIDS: conflict locks are not periodically scaled and cover only the current pool, so they describe the current conflict set rather than past conflicts (p. 10).
  - Farkas diving solves the LP at every diving node (p. 12). The other SCIP divers solve it when bound changes since the last LP exceed 0.15 times the number of variables (p. 18).
  - Call rule: Farkas diving continues in the tree only if it found a feasible solution at the root (p. 12; restated in the conclusion, p. 25).
  - Conflict diving uses κ = 0.75, so "conflict locks dominate by a factor of 3" (p. 19).
  - Transferable: T1, T2, T8.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup: SCIP git hash bf6a486 (based on 6.0) with SoPlex 4.0, evaluated with IPET, on Xeon E5-2690 nodes with 128 GB, 7200 s (p. 11).
  - Variability: four global seeds, and every problem-seed pair counts as one observation (p. 11).
  - Exclusions: 5 runs (Table 1) and 11 runs (Table 3) are excluded "because at least one setting finished with numerical violations" (p. 11). They are counted but not named.
  - Subsets: all, MIPLIB 2010, "affected" (the solving path differs), and nested brackets [k,tilim]. These are defined after Achterberg & Wunderling 2013 as excluding instances easy for all settings "in an unbiased manner" (p. 11).
  - Aggregation: how T and N are averaged (for example shifted geometric means) is not stated in this version (not found). Bold marks relative changes of at least 5% (Table 1 caption, p. 12).
  - Farkas diving, overall (Table 1, p. 12): 706 of 1947 instances are affected; time −5.4% and nodes −14.8% on them; on [100,tilim], −14.8% time and more than 25% fewer nodes. The performance profiles cross once, at a time factor of 1.021. On [10,tilim], 248 instances are solved best by default and 281 by farkdiving (p. 12). The reading is "expensive on easy instances ..., but superior on harder" (p. 13).
  - Farkas diving, mechanisms: it is compared with the per-instance "virtual best" of SCIP's diving heuristics, which overestimates each single heuristic, so the comparison is conservative (pp. 13, 15). It finds 7.3% more conflicts on the "afterroot" subset (273 instances) (p. 13). It finds 39.7% more feasible and 3.7% more improving solutions on affected instances (p. 13), and 57.2% more feasible and more than twice as many improving solutions on afterroot (p. 15). - **D1 Problem and entry point**: IP is "faced with two main computational difficulties" when used for rigorous proofs (pp. 1–2):
  - roundoff, since virtually all solvers use floating point, so "their results are compromised by roundoff errors";
  - missing output that would allow the result to be checked.

  SAT has proof logging and solver-independent verification [33], while "exact IP software with verifiable results is still in its infancy". Exact SCIP is the only general exact IP solver the authors know (p. 2). The paper enters by demonstrating a safe framework on one mathematical problem: Chvátal's conjecture (1974), where research "yielded limited progress" and |U(D)| ≤ 5 follows from Sterboul (pp. 2, 4, 8).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Couple three components (pp. 2–3; Fig. 1):
  1. exact rational SCIP;
  2. VIPR certificates, checked independently of the solve;
  3. a Coq input checker.

  ZIMPL's exact rational model instantiation comes first. The element claimed as new: a proof assistant ensures "the correctness of the certificate's input data" (p. 2). The model is refined in three stages (pp. 5–9):
  - Pinf(n), where feasibility means a counterexample;
  - Popt(n), where an optimum of zero means the conjecture holds;
  - Pred(n), which adds inequalities and fixings derived from the literature.
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: A binary IP over variables x_S and y_S indexed by the power set, plus one integer z. Its size grows exponentially with n (pp. 4–6). Correctness rests on three theorems (pp. 5–9):
  - Theorem 2: Pinf(n) is infeasible iff the conjecture holds for |U| ≤ n;
  - Theorem 3: the optimum of Popt(n) is zero iff the conjecture holds;
  - Theorem 6: the same for Pred(n), under the hypothesis for n − 1.

  Pred(7) has 208 variables and 4050 inequalities, Pred(8) 455 and 12424 (Table 1, p. 10). These sizes are small by industrial standards, which "underlines the difficulty of the underlying combinatorial question" (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Exact SCIP: a hybrid branch and bound. It obtains safe dual bounds by correcting floating-point LP solutions, and uses QSopt_ex "as sparingly as possible". It still lacks presolving, cutting planes and symmetry handling (p. 3).
  - VIPR: the problem definition plus the branch-and-bound proof as a list of valid inequalities. Three inference steps: aggregation, rounding of right-hand sides and resolution of a binary disjunction. It is checkable by hand "in theory" and has a standalone checker; the simple format admits alternative checkers (p. 3).
  - Coq input checker: builds its own constraint matrix for Pred(n) and compares it with the certificate's problem section. It is problem-specific but adaptable (p. 3).
  - Proof devices:
    - incidence vectors map a counterexample to a feasible point and back (Theorem 2, p. 5);
    - Observation 1: some star inequality is tight at an optimum (p. 6);
    - without loss of generality x_S = max_{T⊇S} y_T (Theorem 3, p. 6);
    - partition inequalities dominate the intersecting inequalities, and the solver adds them dynamically from conflicting assignments (Props. 1–2, p. 7);
    - Berge's and Anderson's bound 2|Y| ≤ |D| (Corollary 2, pp. 7–8);
    - Kleitman–Magnanti fixes y_S = 0 for |S| ≤ 2 (Corollary 3, p. 8);
    - a counting proof for n ≤ 5 (Prop. 3, p. 8);
    - induction: fix the singletons at n using the result for n − 1 (Prop. 4, pp. 8–9);
    - symmetry: fix all subsets of [4] by permuting the ground set. This "may exclude counterexamples, but only as long as at least one symmetric counterexample remains feasible" (Prop. 5 and the proof of Theorem 6, p. 9).
  - Transferable: T1–T5.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Questions first: three questions precede Table 1: solve times and the effect of the formulation; certificate size and checking cost; exact against floating-point performance (p. 10).
  - Setup: Xeon Gold 5122 at 3.6 GHz with 96 GB. Exact SCIP uses CPLEX 12.6.3 and QSopt_ex 2.5.10; floating-point SCIP 6.0.0 uses CPLEX 12.8.0. The time limit is 12 h, with one job per node (p. 10).
  - Table 1 (p. 10):

    | Model | Floating-point SCIP | Exact SCIP | Certificate | Check |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Popt(7) | 91.0 s | 4024.2 s | 21000 MB | 1258.3 s |
    | Pred(7) | 13.5 s | 124.5 s | 163 MB | 28.9 s |
    | Pred(8) | 7278.9 s | not solved | – | – |

    Popt(8) is solved by neither. The Coq input check takes under 5 s.
  - The exact/floating ratio is not printed. It is about 9 for Pred(7) and about 44 for Popt(7) (my arithmetic).
  - "Several floating-point MIP solvers" solved Pred(8), but only SCIP 6.0.0 is reported (p. 10).
  - The two SCIP builds use different CPLEX versions, which the text does not remark on (p. 10).
  - Each case was run once; no variability runs are reported.
  - Transferable: T6, T7.

**Transferable techniques**:
- T1. A trust chain stage by stage: exact model instantiation (ZIMPL rationals), an exact solver, an independent certificate (VIPR), and an input checker in a proof assistant that rebuilds the constraint matrix and compares it with the certificate's problem section (pp. 2–3).
- T2. Write the conjecture for fixed n as an IP with a proved equivalence (infeasible ⟺ conjecture; optimum 0 ⟺ conjecture), and prefer the optimality form, which then carries the reductions (pp. 5–7, 9).
- T3. Turn known theorems into valid inequalities and fixings, each proved to keep the equivalence, before they enter a computer proof (Corollaries 2–3, Props. 4–5, Theorem 6; pp. 7–9).
- T4. Break symmetry by fixing a canonical set ({1,2,3,4}), justified by permuting the ground set, and state that one symmetric counterexample stays feasible (p. 9).
- T5. Incremental induction over n: having solved n − 1, fix the singletons at n (Prop. 4, pp. 8–9).
- T6. Report certificate size and checking time beside solve time, and the input-check time (Table 1, p. 10).
- T7. Separate proof from evidence in the text: the floating-point solve of n = 8 makes the conjecture "highly likely" for n = 8 but "does not constitute a safe proof" (p. 10).
