### S002 · Modeling and solving the train timetabling problem (Operations Research 2002, DOI 10.1287/opre.50.5.851.362)
**Read level**: full. The whole 24-page author preprint (OCR text) and its reference list were read. Prose and formulas are readable despite OCR noise; the figures in Tables 1 and 5–7 are partly illegible, so the card uses only results stated in the prose (pp. 18, 20–21). The journal version (Oper. Res. 50(5), 851–861) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Caprara, P. Toth · **Position in trajectory**: railway codes for Ferrovie dello Stato (FS) inside the EU Esprit project EUROPE-TRIS (pp. 1, 21), the skill's 1989–2006 strand. The heuristic machinery (a heuristic at every subgradient iteration, iterative fixing) is carried over from the authors' own set-covering heuristic (Caprara, Fischetti, Toth 1999; pp. 14–15). No general-purpose MIP solver appears anywhere in the method or the tests.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem came from joint research with FS (p. 1). The authors restrict it to the corridor case, one one-way track between two major stations with intermediate stations, arguing that the rest of the network is easy to time once the corridors are fixed (p. 2). Each train has an ideal timetable that may be shifted, stretched (longer stops, slower runs) or cancelled; the profit is the ideal profit minus a nondecreasing shift penalty minus a linear stretch penalty (pp. 2–3, eq. (1)). Because nearly every paper treats a different variant, they prove NP-hardness for their own, together with the impossibility of any polynomial approximation guarantee unless P = NP (p. 4). The closest prior model (Brännlund et al.: binaries per train, track block and one-minute slot) is set aside as unsuited to instances of their size (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when the literature's versions of a problem differ, prove hardness for the exact variant you solve (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: A time-expanded acyclic multigraph whose nodes are departures and arrivals at a station at a time instant; every source–sink path in a train's arc set is a feasible timetable for that train, so all single-train constraints live in the graph (pp. 5–6). Track capacity becomes clique constraints over incompatible pairs of arcs (Figure 3; ILP (2)–(7), pp. 8–9), a multicommodity flow model with the commodity index hidden in the multigraph (p. 9). The key move: an arc-based Lagrangian relaxation is impractical because every arc variable carries its own Lagrangian profit (p. 10), so the capacity constraints are rewritten over node variables y_v and train–node variables z_jv (eqs. (10)–(15), pp. 10–11). After relaxation the arc profits are unchanged, the penalties sit on nodes, and each train's subproblem is a longest path in an acyclic graph (pp. 10, 12). Changed with respect to the classical Lagrangian approach: where the multipliers live. Unchanged: subgradient optimisation with the Held–Karp step (p. 13).
  - Transferable technique: if a Lagrangian relaxation forces you to track a multiplier-adjusted cost for every arc, introduce node (or commodity–node) variables linked by flow equalities, so that the relaxed constraints and their penalties live on the much smaller node set (pp. 10–12).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: A 0-1 ILP with arc variables, flow conservation per train and an exponential family of clique constraints (pp. 8–9). Time is discretised to minutes over a cyclic day, q = 1440 (pp. 2, 17); speed is constant on each segment, so segment headways follow from station headways (p. 3). Data: minimum headways a_i = 4 and d_i = 2 minutes from FS operating rules, a linear shift penalty, profit coefficients identical within a train type (a Eurostar earns 200, minus 7 per minute of shift and 10 per minute of stretch), and no explicit time windows in the tested instances (pp. 17–18). Overtaking constraints are lifted from a weak four-variable form (14) to a strong form (15) that sums each train's visit variables over the whole crossing window (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: lift a pairwise "no crossing" constraint by summing visit variables over the time window instead of the two crossing nodes only (eqs. (14)–(15), p. 11).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Relax-and-cut, credited to Fisher and to Escudero, Guignard and Malik (p. 12): all multipliers start at zero with an empty pool; at each subgradient iteration the violated headway or overtaking constraint whose window is centred as closely as possible on the conflict is added, and a constraint leaves the pool when its multiplier reaches zero (pp. 12–13). The Held–Karp step size starts at 1 and is halved after 10 iterations without bound improvement (p. 13). At every iteration a constructive heuristic ranks the trains by Lagrangian profit and routes each by its best compatible path (pp. 13–14), followed by a refining pass on actual profits that reroutes scheduled trains and inserts unscheduled ones until nothing improves (pp. 14–15). When the step size falls below 0.1, the incumbent's multipliers are restored and ⌈t/20⌉ trains are fixed or removed, with a heuristic multiplier update based on how often each train visited a constraint's nodes; unfixing and full restarts at step size 0.25 follow (pp. 15–16). Implementation: arcs are never stored; each per-train graph keeps, for each node, only the first and last compatible successor plus an interval of forbidden successors (pp. 16–17). An earlier version that rebuilt the train graphs implicitly needed less memory but computed paths much more slowly (pp. 16–17). The only formal result is the reduction of Proposition 1: one train per node of the independent-set instance, one station segment per edge, and ideal timetables that collide exactly on that edge's segment (Figure 1, pp. 4–5). Separation of the clique constraints for the LP relaxation is noted to reduce to a maximum-weight stable set in a comparability graph, hence a max-flow problem, and is left out of scope (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: in a hardness gadget, give each edge of the source graph its own track segment, so that each segment encodes exactly one incompatibility (pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable technique: store a successor list as the [first, last] interval of compatible nodes plus a forbidden interval, which suffices when compatibility is monotone in time (pp. 16–17).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Code in C; 17 instances: 5 from FS (EUROPE-TRIS simulations of operators' requests on a cross-border line, 16–68 trains), 6 from Ansaldo (the actual timetables requested by FS operators, 40–221 trains) and 6 highly congested instances supplied at FS's request, with 50–500 trains (pp. 17–19, Tables 2–4). Digital Ultimate Workstation 533 MHz, a limit of 1,000 subgradient iterations (pp. 18–19). Reported per instance: the ideal profit (a trivial upper bound), the best upper bound, a greedy value, the best heuristic value with its improvement over greedy, the gap, the trains scheduled, the average shift and stretch, and the time to the best solution (pp. 19–20). The upper bound comes from a separate, ad hoc parameter tuning, different from the one used for the heuristic, and the paper says so (p. 19). The only baseline is a greedy rule that schedules trains by decreasing ideal profit, which the authors say reproduces the planners' manual method (p. 19; see Quotes). Stated outcome: almost all trains are scheduled except on MU-VR, PC-BO-1 and PC-BO-4; the final gap is below 2% on most instances; the congested set shows large improvements over greedy (p. 20). The running time is called possibly unacceptable for real-time adjustment or a timetabling teleconference, so Figure 4 plots the incumbent against log CPU time under 10,000 iterations to show that good solutions come early (pp. 20–22). No other algorithm, no stock solver, single runs.
  - Transferable technique: when no published method solves the practitioners' variant, make the baseline an explicit reconstruction of their manual rule and say how close it is to their actual output (p. 19).
  - Transferable technique: for an application with a real-time use, add an anytime plot (incumbent against log time) next to the final values (pp. 20–22).
- **D6 Positioning**: A list of a dozen timetabling papers, of which only Brännlund et al. is discussed and set aside on size grounds (p. 3). Relax-and-cut is credited to Fisher and to Escudero, Guignard and Malik (p. 12), the step formula to Held and Karp (p. 13), and the heuristic-at-every-iteration and fixing devices to the authors' set-covering work (pp. 14–15). Cutting-plane work on similar clique structures (Reinert et al.; Carr, Lancia and Istrail) is cited but not used (p. 9).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The novelty is stated in one sentence of the abstract (p. 1; see Quotes) and justified again where the model is introduced (p. 10). The exact route is closed with a flat sentence, that at the present state of the art an exact algorithm based on this model cannot find an optimum in reasonable time (p. 9); no experiment backs it. Implementation details get their own subsection (§4.4, pp. 16–17). There is no conclusions section: the paper ends with results and acknowledgements to FS and Ansaldo staff (pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable technique: put the one-sentence novelty in the abstract and repeat its justification at the point where the model is introduced (pp. 1, 10).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Three levels of contribution: a model (node-based capacity constraints), an algorithm (a Lagrangian heuristic with relax-and-cut and fixing), and an application on real FS and Ansaldo data (pp. 1, 10–17). Limits stated: the running time may be too long for real-time rescheduling (p. 20); an exact method is judged impossible (p. 9); separation for the LP relaxation is out of scope (p. 9). No future-work section.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the model is chosen to fit the solution method, as in step 2, but by adding variables (y, z) rather than removing any, so that the multipliers live on nodes and no arc is ever stored (pp. 10–12, 16).
- Method 6 ✗ contradiction (dated, before the 2002–2009 general-purpose period): no general-purpose solver is run on the model, not even as a baseline; the exact route is ruled out by a size argument (p. 9) and a problem-specific code is built (pp. 13–17). The ILP model still drives the bound and the multipliers, so the paper is not the "model kept only to describe the problem" of the skill's warning sign 1.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (partial), step 2: the baseline is the planners' manual rule reconstructed as a greedy, and the separate tuning of the bound procedure is disclosed (p. 19). Single runs and no rival algorithm.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (a precursor of step 4): heuristic solutions are computed at every subgradient iteration because slightly different multipliers give solutions of very different quality, and Lagrangian rather than actual profits are used partly because they diversify the solutions (p. 14). Diversity is exploited, but there are no seeds and no placebo arm.
- New-pattern candidate: *put the multipliers on the small index set* (pp. 10, 12, 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *prove hardness for your exact variant when the literature's versions differ* (pp. 4–5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] State your exact variant and prove its hardness, inapproximability included, when published variants differ (pp. 4–5).
- [D2] Rewrite the relaxed constraints over node or commodity–node variables so that Lagrangian penalties attach to nodes and arc costs stay fixed (pp. 10–12).
- [D3] Lift a pairwise crossing constraint to a window sum (eqs. (14)–(15), p. 11).
- [D4] Relax-and-cut pool: start from zero multipliers, add the violated constraint centred on the conflict, drop it when its multiplier returns to zero (pp. 12–13).
- [D4] Run the Lagrangian heuristic at every iteration, ranked by Lagrangian profit, then refine on actual profits (pp. 13–15).
- [D4] When the step size falls below a threshold, restore the incumbent's multipliers and fix or remove a fixed fraction (⌈t/20⌉) of the items; unfix and restart when the restricted bound falls to the incumbent (pp. 15–16).
- [D4] Interval-coded successor lists instead of stored arcs (pp. 16–17).
- [D5] The practitioners' manual rule as the baseline, plus an anytime plot for the real-time question (pp. 19–22).

**Quotes**:
- "A novel feature of our model is that the variables in the relaxed constraints are associated only with nodes (as opposed to arcs) of the aforementioned graph." (p. 1)
- "The manual methods proceed in a similar way, therefore the quality of the solution provided by the FS practitioners is typically close to the value given in this entry." (p. 19)

### S001 · Local branching (Mathematical Programming 2003, DOI 10.1007/s10107-003-0395-5)
**Read level**: full. The author preprint ("May 2002, Revised December 2002", 24 pp.) was read whole: the main text (pp. 1–17) closely; the appendix tables 6–11 (pp. 18–24, gaps of the three codes at checkpoints from 6 minutes to 5 hours for every instance) skimmed and spot-checked against Table 2; the reference list read. The journal version (Math. Program. 98, 23–47) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Lodi · **Position in trajectory**: opens the skill's 2002–2009 strand of general-purpose MIP heuristics around a black-box solver; an early version was presented at Aussois in January 2002 (p. 2). The seed is the soft-fixing refiner of the crew-scheduling code TURNI (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is the heuristic behaviour of exact MIP solvers: in practice what matters is finding reasonable solutions early in the run (pp. 1–2). The failure named: in hard cases even a cleverly tuned general-purpose solver is not enough, so users leave the MIP framework for ad hoc heuristics and lose its generality (p. 2). The concrete target is hard variable fixing (diving): which variables to fix is unclear, a wrong early fixing is hard to detect because the bound stays almost unchanged and then jumps after an innocent-looking late fixing, and backtracking is difficult (p. 3). The application seed: soft fixing on top of TURNI for an NS Reizigers instance (800+ drivers, 8000+ trips), where TURNI reached 833 duties after 41 CPU minutes and soft fixing then converged to 809 duties in 55 more minutes (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: take a refinement trick that works inside one application code and restate it as a constraint on a generic model (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Fix how many variables, not which ones: one linear soft-fixing constraint (1) lets the solver choose (p. 3; see Quotes). This becomes the local branching constraint Δ(x, x̄) ≤ k, the Hamming distance to the incumbent on the binaries (eq. (7)), with an asymmetric form for supports of constant cardinality (eq. (8)) and the disjunction Δ ≤ k or Δ ≥ k+1 as a branching rule (p. 4). Left branches are solved by the black-box solver at the "tactical" level, the external enumeration is the "strategic" level, and the fractional LP point is not necessarily cut off by a local branching (p. 5). Values of k in [10, 20] proved effective (p. 4). Enhancements: a node time limit with two cases, an improved solution (new left branch, no right-branch condition) or none (k reduced by ⌈k/2⌉) (p. 7); soft diversification (k enlarged by ⌈k/2⌉), then strong diversification in the spirit of variable neighbourhood search (the first feasible solution within distance k + 2⌈k/2⌉, with no objective cutoff, becomes the new reference) (p. 8); full pseudo-code LocBra (Figure 5, p. 9) with four cases (pp. 10–11). Unchanged: the MIP solver itself, used with its own branching (pp. 2, 5).
  - Transferable technique: write a neighbourhood as one linear constraint on the binaries and let the black-box solver explore it (pp. 3–5).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: MIPs with a nonempty set of binaries plus possibly general integers and continuous variables (eqs. (2)–(6), p. 4); a feasible reference solution is required (pp. 4, 16). The binaries are assumed to carry the difficulty, being "often associated with big-M terms" (p. 16). With general integers or continuous variables, exactness needs the REFINE step (p. 10); REFINE can be too slow with many general integers and was switched off on arki001 (pp. 10, 12). Distances over general integers (Δ1–Δ3) are proposed but not tested (pp. 16–17). Test set: 29 instances that none of the three codes solves in 5 hours: 7 from MIPLIB 3.0 and 22 from other authors (network and telecom design, crew and railway-crew scheduling with "core problem" versions, nesting, lot sizing, rolling stock, line planning), made available online (pp. 2, 11–13).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Correctness bookkeeping is the main device. The complement Δ ≥ k+1 is imposed only after the left neighbourhood has been solved to optimality or proved infeasible; after a time-limited, incompletely explored node it "would be mathematically incorrect" to impose it (p. 7). When a node stops with an improved but unproven solution, the reference solution is cut off by the tabu constraint Δ(x, x̄) ≥ 1, which is safe only if x̄ is optimal with all binaries fixed; REFINE certifies this by solving the problem with all binaries fixed, and when REFINE is deactivated the tabu constraint may not be used (p. 10). With dv_max finite (and no total time limit) the scheme is exact; with dv_max = ∞ it is a local search heuristic (p. 11). The solver is reached through one routine, MIP_SOLVE(TL, UB, first), that can stop at a time limit, at a cutoff or at the first feasible solution (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: keep a heuristic wrapper exact by imposing a branch's complement only after the branch is closed, and by certifying any solution you cut off with a tabu constraint (pp. 7, 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Three codes: CPLEX 7.0 emphasising optimality (cpx-O), CPLEX 7.0 emphasising feasibility (cpx-F), and LocBra with cpx-O at the tactical level; 5 CPU hours (10 for NSR8K) on a Digital Alpha Ultimate 533 MHz workstation (p. 12). LocBra settings: k = 20, dv_max = ∞, a node time limit by instance size (300–2,400 s, Table 3; pp. 12, 15). Metric: percentage or absolute gap to the best value found by any of the three codes, after 1, 3 and 5 hours (Table 2, pp. 12, 14), with the full time series in the appendix (pp. 18–24). Headline: LocBra's final solution ranked first on 23 of 29 instances (p. 12). In the extracted Table 2, two of these 23 are ties (danoint, all three codes at gap 0; van, cpx-F also at 0), and the six instances LocBra loses split 5 for cpx-F and 1 for cpx-O, while the text says 4 and 2 (pp. 12, 14; my count). The losses are visible in the table (markshare1, markshare2, net12, UMTS and B2C1S1 to cpx-F; tr12-30 to cpx-O; p. 14). The headline run is untuned (p. 13; see Quotes); two tuned variants are reported separately with improvements starred: the asymmetric constraint with k′ = 10 on the set-covering instances (Table 4), and feasibility emphasis at the tactical level on the six instances LocBra had not won, which produced a new best value for UMTS (Table 5) (pp. 13–15). A single-instance trace (tr24-15, k = 18, all codes with the same parameters apart from the emphasis) shows LocBra also proving optimality sooner (1,878 s against 3,827 s for cpx-O), with the caveat that this cannot be guaranteed and needs a different study (p. 6). Single runs per instance; no seeds or permutations.
  - Transferable technique: compare against the same solver in both of its emphasis settings, measure the gap to the best value any code found, and report checkpoints plus full traces (pp. 12, 14, 18–24).
  - Transferable technique: keep the headline run untuned and report tuned variants in separate tables that name the changed setting (pp. 13–15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The paper places itself among general-purpose MIP heuristics (ten references, p. 1) and in the spirit of local search metaheuristics (p. 1). Diving is the named predecessor (pp. 2–3), the TSP k′-OPT neighbourhood the analogy for a fixed small k (p. 4), and variable neighbourhood search the source of strong diversification (p. 8). Instance providers are cited by personal communication (pp. 11–12, 17–18); TURNI is credited as the origin (p. 3), and a Padova master's thesis (Polo) for preliminary results of a branch-and-cut tabu or VNS framework (p. 16).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The method is introduced as the simple answer to a question (how should one choose the variables to fix?, p. 3). The abstract frames it as two-level branching, exact in nature but designed for heuristic behaviour, and states the 23-of-29 result (p. 1). Claims are hedged where they are not shown: faster optimality proofs cannot be guaranteed (p. 6). The conclusion calls the idea very simple yet effective and lists four extensions it explicitly leaves untested (pp. 14, 16). Two traces (Figures 2 and 6) come before any table (pp. 6, 11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: a general framework (local branching constraints and LocBra) and a computational study on hard instances that are made public (pp. 1–2, 11). Limits: a feasible start and relevant binaries are required (p. 16); REFINE can be too expensive with many general integers (p. 10); the optimality speed-up is not established (p. 6); effort is wasted on time-limited nodes that find nothing (p. 16). Future work, all left untested: tighter integration inside the solver, local search by branch-and-cut (tabu search or VNS written as cuts), infeasible reference solutions through penalised slacks, and distances over general integers (pp. 16–17).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence, steps 2–3 (with Lodi): the critical sub-decision (which variables to fix) becomes one linear constraint that the black-box CPLEX 7.0 handles; the code is a thin external loop around MIP_SOLVE (pp. 3–5, 9–10). This is the skill's own example of local branching as one constraint around a black box.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (partial), steps 2, 5 and 6: both CPLEX emphasis settings serve as baselines, with the same parameters otherwise (pp. 6, 12); the headline run is untuned and the tuned variants are labelled (pp. 13–15); the rivals' wins are in the table (p. 14). Lapses: the node time limit is chosen per instance size (Table 3, p. 15); text and table disagree on the cpx-O/cpx-F split, and the abstract's "significantly better" incumbents on 23 of 29 include two ties (pp. 1, 12, 14).
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction (dated, consistent with the skill dating seeds to FP 2.0): one run per instance, and differences of a few units of absolute gap are read without any spread (p. 14).
- Also confirmed: Common Misreading 5 (an exact strategic branching scheme, pp. 1, 11); Research Heuristic 4 (TURNI soft fixing became a general method, pp. 3–4); Research Heuristic 8 (Aussois premiere, p. 2; traces before tables, pp. 6, 11). The skill's anatomy figures (29 instances unsolved in 5 hours, k = 20, no fine tuning, first on 23 of 29; TURNI 833 → 809 duties) match pp. 3, 12–13. Its "Aussois 2002 traces on seymour and arki001" are not in this text, whose traces are tr24-15 and B1C1S1 (pp. 6, 11).
- New-pattern candidate: *exactness bookkeeping around a heuristic wrapper* (pp. 7, 10–11).
- New-pattern candidate: *name the cost of wrapping the black box*: effort wasted on time-limited nodes is named and tighter integration left as future work (p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *turn a structural failure case into an extension*: REFINE switched off on general-integer-heavy arki001, then general-integer distances proposed (pp. 12, 16–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Restate a refinement trick from an application code (soft fixing in TURNI) as a constraint on a generic MIP (pp. 3–4).
- [D2] Neighbourhood as one linear Hamming-distance constraint on the binaries, with the asymmetric form for constant-cardinality supports (eqs. (7)–(8), p. 4).
- [D2] Adapt the neighbourhood at run time: halve after a fruitless node time limit, enlarge then jump (first feasible solution within a larger radius, no cutoff) when a neighbourhood is exhausted (pp. 7–8).
- [D4] Exactness bookkeeping: impose Δ ≥ k+1 only after the left node is closed; use Δ ≥ 1 only with a REFINE certificate (pp. 7, 10).
- [D4] Wrap the solver in one call with time limit, cutoff and stop-at-first-feasible flags (p. 10).
- [D5] Baselines = the same solver in both emphasis settings; gap to the best value any code found; checkpoints at 1, 3, 5 hours plus full time series (pp. 12, 14, 18–24).
- [D5] Untuned headline run; tuned variants in separate tables with the changed setting named (pp. 13–15).
- [D5] Publish the test instances (pp. 2, 11).

**Quotes**:
- "Put in these terms, the question lends itself to a simple answer: just add to the MIP model a linear soft fixing constraint" (p. 3)
- "In the previous experiments we deliberately avoided any fine tuning of the LocBra parameters." (p. 13)

### D003 · Optimizing over the First Chvàtal Closure (IPCO 2005, DOI 10.1007/11496915_2)
**Read level**: full. The whole 11-page conference paper, footnotes and references included. Per its work-list note this is the IPCO 2005 conference version of S033 (Math. Program. 110, 2007), which is not read in this batch; the title follows the paper's own spelling.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: A. Lodi (DEIS Bologna and IBM T. J. Watson, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the first use in this batch of a general-purpose MIP solver as a separation oracle; it links the black-box heuristics (S001, S007) to the skill's 2008–2012 strand on Gomory's cuts.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The abstract is a list of questions: how hard it is in practice to optimise exactly over the first Chvátal closure, which fraction of the integrality gap that closes on hard MIPLIB problems, whether insisting on rank-1 Chvátal–Gomory (CG) cuts pays against the usual Gomory cuts of any rank, how the approach fares on matching problems where the closure equals the integer hull, and whether it can serve as an off-line tool to guess facets (p. 1). The stated aim is to evaluate the practical strength of the closure P1 in approximating P_I (p. 3), given that CG separation is NP-hard (Eisenbrand, p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when a closure is theoretically central but practically unmeasured, make "how much gap does the exact closure close?" the paper (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Model rank-1 CG separation as a MIP and hand it to the general-purpose MIP solver (p. 3; see Quotes). Reductions keep the separation MIP small: variables at zero in x* are dropped and their cut coefficients recomputed afterwards, variables at their upper bound are complemented, and multipliers satisfy u_i < 1 on integer rows (p. 3). The model (6)–(11) maximises violation minus a penalty Σ w_i u_i with w_i = 10⁻⁴ to favour minimal multiplier vectors, a term that matters only on tight rows; fractionality slacks f_j lie in [0, 1−δ] with δ = 0.01 (p. 4). The loop around it is a pure cutting-plane method with rank-1 cuts only: Gomory fractional cuts read from the first optimal tableau, a minimum violation of 0.01, every improving incumbent of the separation MIP stored as a cut, cuts of equal violation reduced to the sparsest, and a node-count stop (τ = 1000 or 100 nodes after the last incumbent update, by violation) (pp. 4–5).
  - Transferable technique: write the separation problem of a cut family as a MIP over its multipliers, restricted to the support of x*, and harvest every incumbent the separation MIP finds, not only its optimum (pp. 3–5).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: Pure ILPs min{cᵀx : Ax ≤ b, x ≥ 0 integer} with integer (A, b) and P_I strictly inside P (pp. 1–2). Free variables, equations and continuous variables can be accommodated (for a continuous variable, α_j = 0 and uᵀA_j ≥ 0), although the mixed-integer case is said to belong to Gomory mixed-integer cuts (p. 4, footnote 5). Test sets: 2-matching instances from TSPLIB, an Edmonds–Johnson matching class where P1 = P_I (pp. 5–7); all pure-integer MIPLIB 3.0 problems (pp. 6–8); the MIPLIB 2003 instances timtab1 and nsrand-ipx treated as pure ILPs (p. 9); the ATSP instance ry48p over the asymmetric assignment relaxation (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Numerical safeguards are explicit: some bounds on f_j are redundant but kept because they improve numerical behaviour, and δ = 0.01 improves stability at the price of possibly losing exactness in pathological cases (p. 4). A rank-1 invariant: later Gomory cuts would be admissible only with a zero multiplier on every non-original constraint (p. 5). The prototype deliberately omits ad hoc heuristics for the separation MIP and cut sequences with disjoint supports (p. 5). Beyond rank 1, a cut-preprocessing loop alternates CPLEX root cuts (emphasis "move best bound") with 600 s of CG separation and saves the model with the cuts active in the last LP (cpx-cg), against CPLEX root cuts alone (cpx) (p. 8). Proof device for the ATSP result: a separated cut is normalised to h-canonical form by adding 1 to its negative multipliers, clones are removed, the resulting 8-node inequality is shown facet-defining "by computational methods", and clique lifting extends it to a class (p. 10).
  - Transferable technique: to read a polyhedral result off a computer-found cut, normalise it to a canonical form, strip clones to a small core, verify the facet computationally, then lift (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: CPLEX 9.0.2 as LP solver and as separation MIP solver ("mip emphasis 4"), on a Pentium M 1.7 GHz notebook with 512 MB (p. 5). 2-matching: on all 15 TSPLIB instances the closure optimum is reached and is integer, as expected (Table 1, pp. 6–7); the paper notes that plain CPLEX on the original rank-0 model is much faster on some of them (p. 6; see Quotes), then shows a cut-and-branch use (100 separation calls, then CPLEX) beating CPLEX alone on the hard ones, such as ts225, pr226 and lin318, which CPLEX alone did not finish in one hour (Table 2, pp. 6–7). ### S007 · The feasibility pump (Mathematical Programming 2005, DOI 10.1007/s10107-004-0570-3)
**Read level**: full. The whole "Revised version" manuscript (15 pp.), all five tables and the references. The journal version (Math. Program. 104, 91–104) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: F. Glover (Colorado), A. Lodi (Bologna) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the second general-purpose heuristic of the 2002–2009 strand, motivated by the fact that local branching and RINS need an incumbent (p. 2); a preliminary version was presented at Aussois in January 2004 (p. 2). It is the paper whose objective cutoff the 2026 pump study restores (skill, Method 5).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Finding a feasible solution of a MIP is NP-hard and can be very hard in practice: state-of-the-art solvers may spend a very large effort before their first solution (p. 1), and the successful local-search methods, local branching and RINS or guided dives, can only start from one (p. 2). The entry is a trivial characterisation: a feasible point is one equal to its rounding; replace "equal" by "as close as possible" under a distance minimised by LP (abstract, p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: start from a trivial characterisation of the target (feasible means equal to its rounding) and relax the equality to a distance you can minimise with an LP (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Alternate an L1 projection (the LP min{Δ(x, x̃) : Ax ≥ b}) with rounding, producing two trajectories, one satisfying the linear constraints and the other integrality (pp. 2–3). The infeasibility measure is the distance to P rather than the weighted constraint violation customary in MIP heuristics, explained as a "difference of pressure" pumped from x̃ into x* (p. 3). Relation to local branching: the same small-distance subproblem, but modelled as an LP with the distance in the objective instead of a MIP with a distance constraint (p. 3; see Quotes). The basic 0-1 version (Figure 1, p. 4) starts from the LP optimum to favour cheap solutions; when rounding repeats, it flips TT = rand(T/2, 3T/2) entries with the largest |x*_j − x̃_j| (p. 4); against cycles, detected over the last three iterations and in any case every R iterations, it applies a random perturbation, flipping x̃_j when |x*_j − x̃_j| + max{ρ_j, 0} > 0.5 with ρ_j uniform in [−0.3, 0.7] (p. 5). Variants: FP1 solves the first LP by barrier to a gap γ without crossover (using the near-optimal duals for a valid Lagrangian bound) and the step-5 LPs by primal simplex with a pivot limit SIL; FP2 starts from a random point with no initial LP (p. 10). The quality extension (§3.2, pp. 10–11) adds a cutoff cᵀx ≤ UB to the step-5 LPs, with UB = α z*_LP + (1−α) z^H after each new solution (FP2: UB = z^H − β|z^H|), allows IL extra iterations, and after each rounding solves the recovery LP min{cᵀx : Ax ≥ b, x_j = x̃_j for j ∈ I}, because the rounded point may be feasible for Ax ≥ b but not for the cut-off system (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: move a requirement from a constraint into the objective to turn a MIP subproblem into an LP (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: Generic MIPs, with auxiliary variables x⁺, x⁻ for the distance on general integers (eq. (4), p. 2), but the paper treats 0-1 MIPs (p. 4). Test bed: 83 0-1 MIPs, the 44 of MIPLIB 2003 (stp3d excluded because its first LP takes over an hour) plus 39 hard instances from several sources, available on request (pp. 5–6). Structure that defeats the pump: big-M rows αᵀy ≤ β + Mz with costly z (p2756), whose LP values of z are tiny and always rounded down, so the pump looks for a y satisfying every constraint (pp. 9–10). General integers seem to make stalling more likely (preliminary experiments, p. 14).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: The safeguards are the stalling flips and the anti-cycling perturbation (pp. 4–5); there is no convergence argument, and failure within the time limit is called unsurprising for an NP-hard task (p. 4). The observation used: Δ(x*, x̃) = 0 makes x* (not necessarily x̃) feasible (p. 3). FP1 keeps a "mathematically-correct" lower bound although its LP is solved approximately (p. 10). LPs are solved through CPXoptimize with default settings, leaving the choice of LP algorithm to CPLEX (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: when an LP is solved approximately (barrier without crossover), use the near-optimal duals as Lagrangian multipliers to keep a valid bound (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Both codes stop at the first feasible solution; Pentium M 1.6 GHz notebook with 512 MB; T = 20, TL = 1,800 s, R = 100 (p. 6). The CPLEX 8.1 baseline is configured after extensive experiments and contacts with ILOG staff: presolve off and the default balance strategy; the root failures under the three other settings tried are reported (25, 41 and 33, against 19 for the chosen one) (p. 6; see Quotes). Metrics: value and time of the first solution, pump iterations, CPLEX nodes, N/A for a failure (pp. 6–8). Results: the pump fails 3 times; CPLEX fails 19 times at the root and 10 times even with 1,800 s of branching (p. 7). CPLEX's first solution is strictly better in 33 cases and the pump's in 46; excluding instances under 1 s for both, CPLEX is faster in 26 cases and the pump in 31 (p. 9). Failures are analysed: rail4284c takes 3 iterations but 1,095.8 s against 273.1 s for CPLEX's root, explained by two LPs with different objectives, and forcing the dual simplex cuts it to 311.1 s (p. 9); on the manpower instances an ad hoc CPLEX tuning suggested by Bixby lets CPLEX find solutions (p. 9); p2756 is analysed structurally (pp. 9–10). No problem-dependent tuning of the LP parameters for either code (p. 9). The variants run on a 26-instance subset chosen by a rule in which both codes play the same role (both found a solution within 1,800 s, and at least one needed 10 s or more), plus the manpower instances with Bixby's tuning for CPLEX (p. 11), with α = 0.50, β = 0.25, γ = 0.20, SIL = 1,000, IL = 250 (p. 11). FP2's first solution often comes 1–2 orders of magnitude faster (NSR8K: 1.5 s, against 1,478.6 s for CPLEX and 1,751.4 s for the pump), with three failures; at the end FP2 is faster than CPLEX in 12 of the 26 cases and better or equal in 11; FP1 after 250 more iterations is equal to CPLEX in 6 cases, better in 12 and worse in 8 (p. 12). Single runs although the method randomises; no seeds. The conclusion counts 4 cases where CPLEX finds nothing even with branching, against 10 in §2 (pp. 7, 12); the difference matches the six manpower instances solved under Bixby's tuning (p. 9), which is my inference.
  - Transferable technique: search the rival's settings and report its failure count under each one before choosing the setting you compare against (p. 6).
  - Transferable technique: choose follow-up subsets by a rule in which both methods play the same role (p. 11).
  - Transferable technique: count wins separately for quality and for time, and drop from the time count the instances both codes solve in under a second (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: A list of general MIP heuristics (p. 2); local branching and RINS as the methods that need a first solution (p. 2); the pump as a modified local branching (p. 3); its infeasibility measure contrasted with the customary weighted violation, and a similar measure for nonlinear problems credited to Chinneck (p. 3, footnote 3). Solver developers appear as sources: personal communications from Rothberg and Bixby for the CPLEX settings (pp. 6, 9, 14–15); Bertsimas is thanked for discussions on randomness in rounding (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract opens with "Trivially," and states the whole method in two sentences (p. 1). A physical metaphor names the method (p. 3). The drawbacks are named before they are fixed: stalling and cycling (pp. 3, 5). Surprises are flagged ("Quite surprisingly", p. 9) and then explained. The conclusion repeats the headline success rates, 96.3% for the pump against 77.1% for CPLEX in its best-tuned version (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: a new general-purpose heuristic and its evaluation on 83 instances (pp. 1, 12). Limits: the basic version pays no attention to quality, using the objective only to start (p. 10); LP re-solves waste time and need better integration with the LP solver (p. 9); big-M structures defeat the rounding (pp. 9–10); general integers (p. 14). Future work: general integers; using the minimum-distance infeasible point as the start of a "feasibility recovery" by local branching or RINS (p. 14); a mechanism to round some variables up whatever their LP value (p. 10).

### S006 · Combinatorial Benders' cuts for mixed-integer linear programming (Operations Research 2006, DOI 10.1287/opre.1060.0286)
**Read level**: full. The whole preprint ("May 24, 2004; Revised, February 16, 2005", 22 pp.), all three tables, both figures and the references. The journal version (Oper. Res. 54(4), 756–766) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: G. Codato (Padova); the paper is based on his master's thesis, which won AIRO's 2003 Camerini–Carraresi prize (p. 4) · **Position in trajectory**: Fischetti's Benders paper of the general-purpose period, a decade before the facility-location redesign; presented at IPCO X in June 2004 (p. 4). Combinatorial cuts are added inside CPLEX's own branch-and-cut.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: MIPs whose logical implications are modelled with big-M coefficients are among the hardest to solve (abstract, p. 1). The continuous variables are "just artificial variables" that certify a property of x (p. 2). The big-M LP relaxation is poor, its x part barely reacts to the y constraints, and the solver carries the conditional constraints at every node although they matter only when the binary is at 1 (p. 3). Classical Benders removes y, but its cuts are weak and still depend on M: it speeds up the LP without improving it (p. 3; see Quotes). Origin: the map-labelling work of Mützel and Klau, whose purely combinatorial model corresponds to the big-M MIP; generalising that correspondence started the project (pp. 12–13).
  - Transferable technique: find an application where someone replaced a big-M model by a combinatorial one by hand, and ask whether the replacement can be derived automatically (pp. 12–13).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Work in the x space only and replace the conditional constraints by combinatorial Benders (CB) cuts Σ_{i∈C} x_j(i) ≤ |C| − 1, one for each minimal infeasible subsystem (MIS) C of the slave (p. 3), in the general form (21) (p. 6). Exact separation weighs each conditional row by 1 − x*_j(i) and asks for a minimum-weight MIS (NP-hard); the heuristic starts from the rows with x*_j(i) = 1, checks infeasibility by LP and shrinks the set greedily, and it is exact for integer x* (p. 3). The master is an ILP with no continuous variables; the slave LP validates master solutions (pp. 5–6). With the objective on y only, the bound dᵀy ≤ UB − ε moves into the slave, so the cuts express optimality as well as feasibility (p. 6). Any MIP with binary and continuous variables fits through a continuous copy x^c = x placed in the slave, with Fx ≤ g optionally moved there too (p. 7). {0,1/2}-cuts become separable on the pure master and are reported to contribute substantially (pp. 4, 11). Changed with respect to classical Benders: the cut (combinatorial, independent of M). Unchanged: the master–slave split, and cut generation on the fly within one branch-and-cut tree ("branch and check", p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: when the oracle's infeasibility certificate is a set of rows, cut with the set (a no-good on the binaries that switch those rows on), not with the dual multipliers (pp. 3, 6).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: MIPs with integer x and continuous y linked only through rows with exactly one nonzero, in a binary column (eq. (14), p. 5); the main case has an objective depending only on x (d = 0) (p. 5); the case with c ≠ 0 and d ≠ 0 is declared not handled effectively (p. 6). The implementation checks the required form automatically (p. 11). Classes: map labelling (18 four-slider and four-position instances from Klau) and two-group statistical classification (38 instances generated from UCI data with Rubin's program, a maximum feasible subsystem problem) (pp. 12–14). Excluded with a reason: the ATSP with time windows, where CB cuts only forbid single paths and are dominated by tournament inequalities (p. 12).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Correctness argument: any separator that is exact for integer x* suffices, as long as a violated cut is generated before each update of the master's incumbent (p. 6). MIS search: the Farkas dual of the infeasible system, with the objective replaced by the normalisation uᵀb̃ = 1; vertices of this polyhedron have MIS supports (Gleeson and Ryan); weights w_i steer the LP towards small support on the linking rows, and zeroing some u variables yields alternative MISs (pp. 10–11); this beats CPLEX's IloCplex::getIIS() for their use (p. 11). Implementation in C++ on ILOG Concert Technology 1.2 and CPLEX 8.1 (p. 11). CB and {0,1/2} separation run before each incumbent update, at nodes of depth at most 10 and after each backtracking step (p. 11); cuts go to a pool purged from time to time. Because CPLEX 8.1 has no internal cut pool and cannot remove global cuts, the cuts are declared local so that they are dropped automatically, at some memory cost (pp. 11–12). The scheme is related to Chvátal's resolution search (the master is a 0-1 model of the nogood pool, the slave the oracle; unlike there, the master is an NP-hard ILP) and to branching resequencing in the spirit of Glover and Tangedahl's dynamic branch and bound (pp. 8–10).
  - Transferable technique: compute an MIS with a Farkas LP plus a normalisation row and a weighted objective, and enumerate alternatives by zeroing multipliers (pp. 10–11).
  - Transferable technique: when the solver cannot delete global cuts, declare them local to get automatic purging (pp. 11–12).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: AMD Athlon 2100+, 1 GB, Linux (p. 14). Each instance is run twice: CPLEX 8.1 with default settings on the original MIP, and CBC, their code (p. 14); 3 CPU hours each; node files written to disk (p. 14). Classes by both outcomes: solved by both, by CBC only, by neither; no instance is solved by CPLEX only (p. 14). Subset 1: CBC is up to three orders of magnitude faster, 24 minutes in total against almost 8.5 hours (21 times); node counts are flagged as not comparable across formulations (pp. 14–15). Subset 2: CPLEX's gap at the moment CBC finished and at its own time limit, 71% and 33% on average for classification, 3.6% and 2.0% for map labelling; 28 more hours would only halve the classification gap, and more than 31 hours do not halve the map-labelling gap (pp. 15–17). Subset 3: CBC ends with a smaller gap on all 10 instances (pp. 17–18), although the table shows CBC using several times more memory on the classification instances (13,363 against 1,621 MB in total), which the text does not discuss (p. 18). Bound and incumbent traces are given for Chorales-116 and Bridges-132 (pp. 14, 17–18). Two claims have no table behind them: the reduction of up to 50% of computing time from {0,1/2}-cuts (p. 11) and the statement that the map-labelling results are comparable with Mützel and Klau's (p. 13). One slowdown appears (Balloons76, 14 s against 10 s), though its ratio cell reads 71.4, and the Breast-Cancer-400 cell reads 1062 where the times give about 10.6, probably lost decimal points (Table 1, p. 15; my reading of the extraction). Single runs; no seeds.
  - Transferable technique: when the new code finishes first, report the baseline's gap at that moment and at the baseline's own limit (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable technique: classify instances by both codes' outcomes and state that the class solved only by the baseline is empty (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: The technique "is based on Hooker's idea" of Benders cuts from minimal sets of inconsistencies, and the contributions are stated as (a) a separation heuristic for minimal Benders cuts in the special case of conditional constraints with linear implications and (b) a computational test (pp. 4, 19). Related work (§3, pp. 7–10): logic-based Benders (Hooker; Hooker and Yan; Jain and Grossmann; Cambazard et al.), Kim and Hooker as "very similar", with nogoods found in a network-flow relaxation (p. 8), branch and check (Thorsteinsson, p. 8), Chvátal's resolution search (pp. 8–9), and Glover and Tangedahl (p. 10). The acknowledgement says the referees helped set the contribution in its Constraint Programming context (pp. 19–20).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The introduction shows the idea on the simplest form first and generalises in §2 (pp. 2–7). Metaphors carry the diagnosis: the solver "carrying on its shoulders" the conditional constraints (p. 3), and the cut generator as "an automatic device to distill" combinatorial information from the model (p. 4). An explicit scope sentence precedes the test bed (p. 12; see Quotes). The contribution paragraph of the introduction is repeated almost word for word in the conclusion (pp. 4, 19).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: an automatic reformulation that removes the dependence on big-M, a separation heuristic, and evidence on two classes (pp. 17, 19). Limits: CB cuts can be weak (ATSP with time windows, p. 12); mixed objectives are not handled (p. 6); leaving y in the master and using CB cuts as plain cutting planes yields "wild fractional points" the simple separator cannot handle (p. 19); the local-cut workaround costs memory (p. 12). Future work: mixed objectives, with encouraging preliminary results, and a two-step approach that runs the decomposition first and then restarts the original model with the CB cuts in its cut pool (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (with Codato), step 3: the artificial continuous variables, and with them the big-M constants, are projected out, and the master is a pure ILP (pp. 1–4, 7). This dates the projection habit to 2004–2006, before the 2011–2017 range the skill gives for Method 1's practice.
- Method 6 ✅ evidence, step 3: a simple implementation inside CPLEX's branch-and-cut via Concert callbacks, tested to see whether it "can already" beat a sophisticated solver (pp. 4, 11); the oracle is an LP (pp. 10–11).
