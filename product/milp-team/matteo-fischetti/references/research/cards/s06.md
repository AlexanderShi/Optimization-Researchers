### S096 · Just MIP it! (Matheuristics: Hybridizing Metaheuristics and Mathematical Programming 2009, DOI 10.1007/978-1-4419-1306-7_2)
**Read level**: full (all 28 file pages of the author preprint, reference list skimmed; the text has no conclusions section and ends with the dominance experiments, p. 25). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Lodi, Salvagnin match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Andrea Lodi, Domenico Salvagnin · **Position in trajectory**: the survey that names the MIPping principle behind the 2002–2009 heuristics and MIP-separation work; Sections 2, 3 and 4 rest on Fischetti–Lodi 2007 (MIPping closures), Fischetti–Lodi 2008 (repairing MIP infeasibility) and Salvagnin's 2005 master's thesis (p. 2; references, pp. 27–28).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Solvers already spend auxiliary LPs on strong branching and lift-and-project cuts, and good heuristic MIP solutions often cost about as much as the LP relaxation; so the crucial decisions inside a MIP algorithm (how to cut, how to improve the incumbent, whether a node is dominated) can be modelled as MIPs and solved heuristically on the fly (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three surveyed applications. (i) Separation over rank-1 closures as a MIP: Chvátal–Gomory cuts with integer coefficient variables (p. 4), projected CG cuts (p. 5), split/MIR cuts through a parametric MIP over a grid of u0 values (pp. 5–6) or a linearised MINLP (pp. 6–7). (ii) Local branching started from an infeasible feasibility-pump point: each violated row gets a continuous slack bounded by a binary indicator, the objective counts violated rows, as in a simplex phase 1, and the original objective returns once a feasible point is found (pp. 10, 13–14). (iii) The Fischetti–Toth local dominance test as an auxiliary MIP over the fixed variables, with a nogood pool borrowed from constraint programming and local-branching restrictions on the auxiliary problem (pp. 19–22).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pure ILPs and general MIPs with rational data for the cuts (pp. 2–3); 0-1 MIPs with at least one binary for the heuristic, since local branching needs binaries (p. 10); for dominance, a MIP whose branching fixes integer variables (p. 19). Test sets: MIPLIB 3.0 (25 pure, 33 mixed) for the closures (p. 9); 33 of the 45 0-1 MIPLIB 2003 instances plus 39 other hard 0-1 MIPs, with stp3d and 11 trivially solved instances removed (pp. 14–15); generated single and multiple knapsack instances (p. 23).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Any feasible solution of a separation MIP yields a violated cut, so heuristic solves suffice; separation restricted to the support of x*, variables at their upper bound complemented, a penalty on multipliers to sparsify cuts (p. 8); MIP separation run for a fixed time as preprocessing (p. 9). Local branching: the asymmetric constraint (p. 14); neighbourhood size set to halve the number of violated rows, 10 once fewer than 20 remain, 30 s per neighbourhood (p. 15). Dominance: correctness from an inheritance proposition (proof in the thesis) plus a lexicographic tie-break, chosen because a MIP solver's output depends on the order of its input (pp. 20–21); minimal nogoods kept in a pool (p. 21); test skipped before the first incumbent and outside a depth window (p. 23).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Closures as aggregated percentage of gap closed (split 71.7% vs CG 62.6% on pure ILPs, split 84.3% vs projected CG 36.4% on MILPs), lower approximations because of time limits (p. 9). LB0/LB10/LB100 against FP and ILOG-Cplex 9.0.3 on a Pentium M 1.6 GHz notebook, 1,800 s, stop at the first feasible solution, no parameter tuning (pp. 15–16): LB100 failed 3 times vs 5 for Cplex, was faster on 41 of 66 instances vs 21, mean 65.0 vs 146.7 s, but found worse first solutions (geometric-mean ratio to best 2.11 vs 1.13) (p. 16). Dominance with Cplex 9.0 default on an Athlon64, 2,000 s (one hour for multiple knapsacks): on single knapsacks total nodes fell by a factor 18.95 and time by 1.61; on multiple knapsacks total time rose (ratio 0.84) (pp. 23–25). One run per instance and method, no seeds.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Survey level. The closure studies targeted bound strength, not practical use (p. 9); LB's first solutions are worse than Cplex's and the good dc1c case does not carry over to other instances (pp. 16, 19); dominance helped single but not multiple knapsacks, possibly hidden by the time limit (pp. 24–25); open topic: using a first feasible solution well inside enumeration (p. 19). The text reports LB0 failing 8 times and then LB0 again failing 3 times; the second must be LB100 (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the survey states the principle behind step 2 (decisions as auxiliary MIPs solved by a general-purpose solver, even heuristically, pp. 1–2, 8, 20) and practises step 5 (slow MIP separation moved to a time-limited preprocessing phase, which solved nsrand-ipx and arki001 for the first time, p. 9) and step 4 (closure strength measured before practical use, pp. 9–10).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence, with a caveat: the LB study avoids parameter tuning and prints Cplex's better solution quality (pp. 15–16); the dominance loss on multiple knapsacks is printed (p. 25). Caveat: the dominance testbed is chosen as classes whose structure can trigger dominance, and a per-instance tuning table is shown beside the main one (pp. 23–24).
- Method 5 ✅ evidence: Section 4 revives the 1988 Fischetti–Toth procedure through Salvagnin's 2005 master's thesis (pp. 2, 19; references, pp. 27–28).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence (early): solver nondeterminism is treated as a correctness issue in 2009, before the erraticism papers; the tie-break no longer relies on a deterministic solver (p. 21).
- Also Heuristic 2 ✅ (feasibility moved into the objective so that local branching can start from an infeasible point, pp. 13–14) and Heuristic 3 ✅ (nogoods borrowed from constraint programming, p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: Lexicographic rule as stabiliser and tie-breaker (p. 21; also D011).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Model a separation problem as a MIP and accept any feasible solution as a cut; restrict it to the support of x*, complement variables at their upper bound and penalise the multipliers lightly to get sparser cuts (p. 8).
- To start an improvement heuristic from an infeasible point, relax each violated row with a continuous slack bounded by a binary indicator and minimise the number of indicators at one; switch back to the true objective once feasible (pp. 13–14).
- When a correctness argument breaks ties between equal-cost solutions, rank them lexicographically rather than by which one a solver returns, since solver output depends on input order (p. 21).
- Run an expensive node test only between a minimum and a maximum depth, at fixed depth intervals and after the first incumbent (p. 23).

**Quotes**:
- "with the aim of bringing the MIP technology well inside the MIP solver" (p. 1)

### S112 · Minimal infeasible subsystems and Benders cuts (Mathematical Programming manuscript, to appear per Scholar 2009, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (all 17 file pages of the undated manuscript, with Tables 1–3 and Figures 1–2). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Salvagnin, Zanette match (p. 1). S024 (same three authors) opens with a different manuscript, *On the selection of Benders' cuts*, revised 23 February 2010 and 8 pages long in the index; how the two relate was not checked beyond the two first pages.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin, Arrigo Zanette · **Position in trajectory**: the long cut-selection study on Benders, before the in-out loop (S174) and the 2017 facility-location redesign.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Benders cuts often disappoint when implemented as in the textbook (abstract, p. 1). The paper names three design questions (when to cut, what to cut, how to choose the cut) and takes only the third (p. 2). Textbook Benders gives no rule for choosing among unbounded rays, so feasibility-cut selection is random, and implementations called standard rarely give details (pp. 5–7).
- **D2 Core idea**: Read the cut generating LP as a feasibility problem whose minimal infeasible subsystems are the supports of the vertices of the alternative polyhedron (Gleeson–Ryan); swap the normalization (multipliers summing to one) with the violation objective, so the CGLP returns a small-support violated cut (pp. 8–9). Variants: mis (one cut per call) and mis2 (an extra feasibility cut with π0 = 0 whenever the first is an optimality cut) (p. 10). Magnanti–Wong is set aside with four listed drawbacks: a bounded slave, a relative-interior point of the integer hull, two LPs per cut, and ties that are rare at fractional points (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{cx + dy : Ax ≥ b, Tx + Qy ≥ r, x ≥ 0 integer, y ≥ 0}, integer variables in the master and continuous ones in the slave (pp. 3, 10); the study works on the root LP (p. 5). Testbed: 11 MIPLIB 2003 mixed-integer instances with meaningful numbers of both variable types; numerically unstable and too-easy instances dropped, unnamed (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Optimality cuts move the bound more, but are denser and have higher dynamism than feasibility cuts; the textbook method is biased towards feasibility cuts, and an optimality cut can be read off the vertex at which the primal simplex finds a ray (p. 6). The normalized CGLP is never empty or unbounded, is numerically safer, and only its objective changes between calls, so primal-simplex re-optimisation is fast (p. 9). Trivial bound on η from the bounds on y, equations kept with free multipliers, bound shifting (pp. 6, 10–11). ATSP argument: in the flow model the Benders cuts are exactly the facet-defining subtour elimination constraints, so the trouble lies in how cuts are handled and chosen (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: tb, tb_noopt, mis and mis2 with ILOG Cplex 11.0 as LP solver (presolve off, primal simplex on the dual slave), Core2 Quad Q6600, 2,000 s; time and iterations to reach 80/90/95/99% of the root bound, cut density and a regression slope of master time per iteration (pp. 10–12, 17). tb is best on 1 of 11 instances, mis or mis2 lead on the other 10 with 1–2 orders of magnitude speed-up; tb_noopt was never better than tb and is not tabulated; numerical failures are marked (pp. 11–13). Single runs.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: One or two cuts per call and the root only; rounds of cuts and the stabilisation of the point to cut are left aside (pp. 2–3). mis is the default, but mis2 wins where feasibility cuts matter (timtab1, timtab2, tr12-30) (p. 13). The conclusion only restates the speed-up (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the diagnosis is Method 2's (in the ATSP case the cuts are excellent and the textbook handling is not, p. 7), but the cure works on the oracle, a normalization that picks a better CGLP vertex, while the question of which point to cut and how to stabilise it is named and set aside (pp. 2, 8–9). This is the cut-selection stage that Method 2 and Common Misreading 3 place before the stabilised-loop work.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the paper first asks what a modern yet classical Benders baseline should be, defines two textbook variants and keeps the stronger (pp. 5, 10–11); it prints where the textbook method wins (aflow40b) and where mis loses to mis2 (p. 13). Exclusions are given with reasons but without names (p. 10).
- new-pattern candidate: Diagnose with an example whose answer is known (the ATSP flow model, whose Benders cuts are known facets, p. 7; also S068).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a cut generating LP, fix the multiplier sum to one and maximise violation instead of fixing the violation: the LP is never empty or unbounded, the dense row is numerically safer, and only the objective changes between calls (p. 9).
- Compare cut-selection rules by the time and iterations needed to reach 80, 90, 95 and 99% of the root bound, with cut density and the growth rate of master solve time per iteration (pp. 12, 17).
- Before claiming a speed-up over textbook Benders, define the textbook variants explicitly (one cut per call, or a feasibility plus an optimality cut from the same simplex vertex) and compare against the stronger one (pp. 6, 10–11).
- To get usable rays from an unbounded dual slave, solve it with the primal simplex and switch LP presolve off (pp. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "Benders cuts make a wonderful job" (p. 7)

### S174 · Yoyo search: a bisection cutting-plane method (author manuscript 2009, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (all 13 file pages of the manuscript dated 20 November 2009, with Tables 1–4). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Salvagnin match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin · **Position in trajectory**: the first write-up of the in-out cut loop, unpublished under this title; the skill reports that the 2010 paper on the loop credits Ben-Ameur and Neto, who are not cited here (references, pp. 12–13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A cutting-plane method has two equally important parts, the separation oracle and the search framework that picks the next point to cut; the MIP community studied the first and almost always cuts an optimal LP vertex (Kelley), which is inefficient when cuts are shallow (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Interior-point schemes (ellipsoid, analytic centre) depend less on cut quality, but recomputing the centre is costly and their objective cuts are awkward in branch-and-cut (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep an internal point q of the cut closure and the LP optimum x*; ask the oracle to cut their midpoint; if it cuts, add the cut and re-optimise x*, otherwise move q to the midpoint, which halves the interval [cx*, cq] holding the closure bound (pp. 3–4, Figure 1). In one dimension it is binary search (p. 3). Against the ellipsoid and analytic-centre methods, q is updated at no cost, may lie on a face of the LP polyhedron and needs no neutral cuts (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{cx : Ax ≤ b, x_j integer for j in J} and an oracle that defines a cut closure (p. 3); q from any feasible integer solution, a problem-specific formula, or a phase 1 (barrier on the zero objective) (p. 4). Test data: root LPs of 19 MIPLIB 2003 instances and 14 OR-Library set-covering instances, picked for their number of inequalities (p. 5); random grid and random multicommodity network design instances, with zero routing costs (feasibility cuts only) or unit costs (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Lower and upper bounds at every iteration allow early stops, including pruning in branch-and-cut (p. 4); after more than 3 consecutive updates of q the method separates x* directly, so that it can stop once x* lies in the closure (p. 3). Stated risks: the midpoint can be denser than x*, and a heuristic oracle that misses a cut moves q wrongly, giving wrong upper bounds, weaker cuts or cycling (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Controlled oracle: the LP's own inequalities stored inside the separator, which returns (A) the deepest violated row, (B) its average with up to 10 other violated or tight rows, or (C) cut B with half its violation; at most 10,000 calls. Yoyo matches the standard loop under A, is about 10 times faster on set covering under B, and closes about 68% vs 35% of the gap on MIPLIB under C, though more slowly there (pp. 5–6, Table 1). Benders: the same separator in both arms, geometric means 105 vs 314 iterations and 22.86 vs 50.74 s on 40 instances; yoyo is slower on two of them (pp. 8, 11). Phase-1 time is not reported (p. 7); hardware is not stated in the text read.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Gains come mainly when cuts are shallow (p. 10); a comparison with the analytic-centre method and dedicated phase-1 code are planned (pp. 4, 6); the heuristic-oracle risk above (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the earliest text found for step 3 (the oracle stays, the query point moves to the midpoint between a core point and the LP optimum) and for step 4 (a controlled oracle with artificial cut-quality rules first, then real Benders separation with exactly the same separator in both arms) (pp. 1–8).
- Common Misreading 4 ✅ (primary evidence; also the Workflow F checkpoint): the scheme is presented as new (pp. 1–2); internal points in Benders are credited only to Magnanti–Wong and Naoum-Sawaya–Elhedhli, as quite different from this method (pp. 7–8); Ben-Ameur and Neto are absent from the references (pp. 12–13).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the same separator runs in both arms, but phase-1 cuts and time are left out of the yoyo figures (disclosed, p. 7); for set covering and network design q comes from a formula (pp. 7–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To test a new cut loop without a real separator, hide an LP's own inequalities inside the oracle and set cut quality by rule (deepest, averaged, weakened); the loop's target is then the known LP optimum (p. 5).
- Track an internal point and the LP optimum together and cut their midpoint: every failed separation halves the bound interval at no extra LP cost (pp. 3–5).
- Seed the internal point with a cheap feasible point (all components 1/2 when every row is covered by at least two columns, a large capacity vector in network design) to avoid a phase 1 (pp. 7–8).
- Report per instance the iterations needed to close 90, 95 and 99% of the gap, beside totals (pp. 7–9).

**Quotes**:
- "the search component was much less studied—at least by the MIP community" (p. 1)

### S134 · On the knapsack closure of 0-1 Integer Linear Programs (Electronic Notes in Discrete Mathematics 36 2010, DOI 10.1016/j.endm.2010.05.101)
**Read level**: full (all 6 file pages; equation numbers are displaced in the extraction, the text is readable). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Lodi match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Andrea Lodi · **Position in trajectory**: a concept note in the closures line, after MIP separation over the first Chvátal closure (named on p. 4) and the corner-relaxation study with Monaci (reference [2], p. 5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A measured finding is the entry: Gomory's corner relaxation approximates the integer hull well with general integers but poorly for 0-1 problems (Fischetti–Monaci; observed by Balas in 1973); the stated explanation is that even nonbinding bounds 0 ≤ x_j ≤ 1 matter in 0-1 programs, so the question is how to keep them when generating Gomory-like cuts (abstract, p. 1; p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The knapsack closure: for every inequality wx ≤ w0 valid for the LP relaxation, add all valid inequalities of conv{x in {0,1}^n : wx ≤ w0} (pp. 1–3); the conclusion calls it a strengthening of the Chvátal–Gomory closure (p. 5). With the source row given, separation is an LP over the points of the knapsack set solved by row generation with a knapsack oracle (pp. 3–4); with the source row free, a MILP (p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: 0-1 ILP min{cx : x in P ∩ X}, P = {Ax ≤ b, x ≥ 0}, X a set over which linear optimisation is practically tractable, such as the 0-1 points (p. 3); normalization −1 ≤ α_j ≤ 1 (pp. 3–4); a large M and a small ε in the MILP (p. 4).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: In the MILP the source inequality's coefficients are variables certified valid by Farkas multipliers u (w ≤ uA, w0 ≥ ub), and a binary per point of X lets that point violate the cut only if the source inequality cuts it off (pp. 4–5); solved by the same point generation with the knapsack oracle (p. 5). The note gives no proof of the strengthening claim (my reading: a CG cut with multipliers u is valid for the knapsack set of (uA, ub), so the knapsack closure lies inside the CG closure).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a (no experiments).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Concept and separation models only; their practical viability is stated to be under investigation (p. 5).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: separation over a new closure is again written as a MILP for a general-purpose solver (step 2), here with the source inequality itself as variables (pp. 4–5); step 4, measuring the potential, is still pending (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When cut strength depends on which inequality the cut is derived from, put that inequality's coefficients into the separation model as variables and certify their validity with Farkas multipliers (p. 4).
- For a separation LP with one row per point of a discrete set, generate the rows with an optimisation oracle over the set and stop when the oracle's best point satisfies the cut (pp. 3–4).

**Quotes**:
- "whose practical viability is under investigation" (p. 5)

### S068 · On the separation of disjunctive cuts (Mathematical Programming 128(1) 2011, DOI 10.1007/s10107-009-0300-y)
**Read level**: full (all 24 file pages of the manuscript, reference list skimmed; figure axes are extraction debris; the text ends with the set-covering section, without a conclusions section, p. 23). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Lodi, Tramontani match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Andrea Lodi, Andrea Tramontani · **Position in trajectory**: the CGLP-normalization line on which S112 builds its Benders cut selection; S112 cites a Fischetti–Lodi–Tramontani report in preparation, *Experiments with disjunctive cuts* (S112 pp. 7, 16), presumably this work under an earlier title (not verified).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The ingredients of a disjunctive-cut separation, above all the normalization of the cut generating LP, are not well understood and often misread; the paper isolates their effect on the root bound (abstract, p. 1; p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Compare normalizations of the CGLP cone. The trivial one (u0 + v0 = 1) gives the GMI cut in closed form and uses only tight rows (Theorem 2, pp. 4–5). The standard one (SNC: all multipliers sum to one) works far better because small multipliers penalise rank implicitly and keep cuts sparse (pp. 6–7), but it depends on row scaling and is fooled by rows redundant for one side of the disjunction (pp. 7–8, 13–15). Remedies: drop redundant rows, keep variables with redundant tight bounds in an extended support, and a Euclidean normalization that weights each multiplier by the norm of its row (pp. 17–21).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{cx : Ax ≥ b, x_j integer for j in J} with bounds inside Ax ≥ b and the trivial inequality 0x ≥ −1 implied (p. 1); split disjunctions on one fractional variable, cuts separated from x* (pp. 2–3). Testbed: 14 MIPLIB 3.0 instances, markshare1 and stein27 excluded from the averages (pp. 5–6); 10 OR-Library set-covering instances (p. 22).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Lemma 1 bounds the violation (p. 4); Theorem 5: a CGLP extreme ray yields a strictly dominated cut iff some multiplier vector for the same cut uses a row on both sides (p. 13); Proposition 7: a strictly redundant row with a positive multiplier makes the cut nonsupporting (p. 14); Theorem 8: redundant rows create scaled copies of dominated cuts with larger violation, which the SNC prefers (pp. 14–15); Lemma 9: the Euclidean normalization is scaling-invariant (p. 20). Toy ILPs whose CGLP vertices are enumerated with PORTA: 117 extended-space vertices against 4 facets of the disjunctive hull, and the facet cut is never chosen (pp. 9–12).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Ten rounds, one cut per fractional variable, no a posteriori strengthening; percentage of gap closed and dual-support size after 10 rounds, per-round plots on p0201 (pp. 3, 5–6, 9, 19). Average gap closed: trivial 37.6% vs SNC 56.9% (p. 6); SNC with each cut multiplied by 1,000, 46.8% (p. 8); without projection, 55.9% rising to 58.3% once redundant rows are removed (pp. 17–18); on the extended support 58.8% (pp. 18–19); Euclidean 57.1%, and 60.0% after redundancy removal, with rout falling from 24.3% to 7.9% in the first comparison (pp. 21–22); set covering 11.9% to 15.0% (pp. 22–23). No times, solver version or hardware reported.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Projection on the support can hide redundancy (pp. 17–19); no known normalization makes every optimal extended-space vertex extremal in the cut space (p. 16); an on-the-fly redundancy check is left to future work (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: step 1 is there (one instance traced round by round: bound, density, dual support, rank, pp. 5–6, 9, 19) and the SNC's success is explained by loop dynamics (earlier cuts have small norms and are expensive to reuse, so rank stays low, p. 7), but the remedies change the oracle, not the point it is asked to cut.
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: a change with no mathematical content, multiplying every generated cut by 1,000, lowers the average gap closed from 56.9% to 46.8%; the authors use it to show how implementation details move results (p. 8).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: strengthening switched off to limit side effects (step 4, p. 3); the instance where the new normalization loses is printed and analysed (step 6, p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: Diagnose with an example whose answer is known (toy ILPs with enumerated CGLP vertices, pp. 9–12, 15–17; also S112).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before trusting a CGLP normalization, re-run with every generated cut multiplied by a constant; a normalization whose results change is scaling-dependent (p. 8).
- Remove rows that are redundant for either side of the disjunction (or fix their multipliers to zero), and do not project away variables whose tight bound is redundant (pp. 15, 17–18).
- Weight each multiplier in the normalization by the Euclidean norm of its row to make separation scaling-invariant (pp. 20–21).
- Enumerate the vertices of a small CGLP (PORTA) to see which cuts a normalization can return (pp. 11–12).

**Quotes**:
- "the importance of “small implementation details” when evaluating the performance of a method" (p. 8)

### S095 · A relax-and-cut framework for Gomory mixed-integer cuts (Mathematical Programming Computation 3(2) 2011, DOI 10.1007/s12532-011-0024-x)
**Read level**: full (file pp. 1–19, the whole text; appendix Tables 8–11 on pp. 21–26 skimmed). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Salvagnin match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin · **Position in trajectory**: the journal version of the CPAIOR 2010 paper (D008, not read here), in the Gomory-revisited line beside the lexicographic papers with Zanette and Balas (reference [30], p. 21).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Solvers use GMICs conservatively because rounds of cuts make the LP basis determinant grow; the entry claim is that the instability comes from the closed loop between LP solver and cut generator, not from the cuts, so the loop needs a decoupling filter (p. 2; Figure 1, p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Relax-and-cut: GMICs read from bases of the original LP go to a pool and are dualised in a Lagrangian fashion instead of being added to the LP (pp. 2, 5–6). Variants subg, hybr, fast and faster share one scheme (Algorithm 1 and Table 1, p. 10); in the fast variants the subgradient method, with a tiny step, only samples near-optimal Lagrangian bases, while the multipliers come from solving the LP over the pool ten times (pp. 8–9). The authors read this as a Dantzig–Wolfe decomposition of x* into vertices of P, as Ralphs et al. did for CVRP capacity cuts (p. 9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{cx : Ax = b, x ≥ 0, x_j integer for j in J} with P assumed bounded and nonempty; when an instance breaks the assumption the subgradient loop is interrupted (pp. 4, 11). Cuts come only from primal-feasible bases, so the bound may stay below the first GMIC closure (p. 11). Testbed: 54 MIPLIB 3.0 and 20 MIPLIB 2003 instances after the rival's own filter (p. 10); pseudo-branching assumes binary variables (p. 14).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Pooled cuts stay globally valid even when the pool holds invalid rows, since they come from bases of the original system (p. 6); Polyak step with a heuristic upper bound and rules for the step parameter, the aggressive start justified because convergence is not expected (p. 7); new cuts every 10 iterations, new multipliers every 50 (p. 8); rows with fractionality at least 0.001, cuts with dynamism above 1e10 dropped, a 1 GB pool purged by efficacy and age (p. 11); higher-rank cuts only as a diversification step when sampling finds no cut (pp. 12–13); pseudo-branching dives by large cost changes on one of the five most fractional binaries (p. 14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: C++, Cplex 11.2 as LP solver, one core of an Intel Q6600 (pp. 10–11); average gap closed and geometric-mean time against one GMIC round, Dash–Goycoolea (times copied from their paper, other machines, speeds stated) and 10 rounds of CglLandP lift-and-project with Clp (pp. 11–14): faster closes 57.1% in 1.53 s on MIPLIB 3.0, and 43.3% vs 39.7% for Dash–Goycoolea on MIPLIB 2003 (p. 11). On top of Cplex root cuts (p. 16) and in cut-and-branch on 18 hard instances with 10,000 s: 3 solved vs 1 and 2 for the Cplex root variants and 2 for Cplex default, whose final gap is lower, 15.7% vs 16.2% (p. 17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Room for improvement is claimed (p. 18); open: a better Lagrangian dual optimizer, primal heuristics from Lagrangian vertices, pseudo-branching restarts, rank-1 GMICs at non-root nodes, and a meta-scheme for cut generation (pp. 18–19); whether higher rank, or fast over faster, is worth its time is left open (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: a direct statement of the loop diagnosis (instability in the framework, not in the cuts) and a remedy that changes what the generator is fed while the generator stays the same (pp. 2–6).
- Method 3 ✅ evidence: the erratic behaviour of MIP solvers on near-identical input is described as useful diversification in MIP-based cut separation (p. 4).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the potential is measured first (the first GMIC closure approximated, p. 11), then the method runs on top of Cplex's root cuts and inside cut-and-branch with black-box Cplex (pp. 16–17); a quick shot, one FP 2.0 rounding per Lagrangian vertex, gives 40 of 72 successes vs 69 for objective FP 2.0 but better solutions (gap 2.54% vs 6.73%) (pp. 18–19).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the rival's instance filter is adopted and cut-and-branch exclusions are given with reasons (pp. 10, 17), but the Dash–Goycoolea times come from other machines (p. 12), and the text's claim of more gap closed compares only with the two Cplex root variants, not with Cplex default (p. 17).
- Also Heuristic 3 ✅ (a meta-scheme for cut generation in the spirit of tabu search or VNS, p. 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Keep generated cuts out of the LP: pool them, dualise them, and read new cuts only from bases of the original system, so every cut stays rank 1 and the tableau does not degrade (pp. 2, 5–6).
- Use a cheap inexact method only to sample (small subgradient steps that visit near-optimal bases) and take the multipliers from a periodic exact LP over the pool (pp. 8–9).
- Warm-start a cut pool with short randomised dives that branch by changing costs, so the cuts stay globally valid (p. 14).
- To see whether root cuts pay off, run cut-and-branch on instances the default solver cannot solve within an hour, with the black-box solver's cut generation off below the root (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "stability issues are largely due to the overall framework where GMICs are used" (p. 2)

### D011 · A hard integer program made easy by lexicography (Mathematical Programming 135(1–2) 2012, DOI 10.1007/s10107-011-0450-6)
**Read level**: full (all 5 file pages of the manuscript dated 16 February 2011, with Tables 1–2 and Figures 1–2). First-page check: title and authors Balas, Fischetti, Zanette match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Egon Balas, Arrigo Zanette · **Position in trajectory**: an addendum to Zanette, Fischetti and Balas 2009 on lexicographic Gomory cuts (p. 1), in the Gomory-revisited line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A historical hard instance: Knuth's 1960 drum-latency integer program (51 integer variables, 43 inequalities), which his own Gomory implementation could not solve and which waited 35 years until Alevras solved it with CPLEX in 1995 (8,880 nodes to find the optimum, 732,200 to prove it) (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Run the lexicographic version of Gomory's fractional-cut method, which restores lexicographic optimality of the tableau after each cut, in single-cut, multi-cut and binary-search-on-the-objective forms (pp. 2–3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: A pure integer program in nonnegative integer variables (p. 1), plus Knuth's corrected formulation (one inequality replaced by two and a new variable) as a second instance (p. 5); both posted for download (pp. 2, 5).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: The explanation: under dual degeneracy the plain dual simplex picks the optimum closest to the previous one, which needs huge basis determinants, while the lexicographic dual simplex goes on to a lexicographically optimal basis, usually far from the old point, so determinants need not grow; here they even fall, which the authors put down to numerically clean cuts making the original rows redundant (pp. 3–5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: One instance and its corrected version; textbook multi-cut Gomory, three lexicographic variants and CPLEX 12.2 in default, strong-branching and aggressive-cut settings; time, LPs, pivots, cuts, nodes, gap closed; single runs, hardware not stated (pp. 2–5). Original: binary-search variant 0.15 s vs 4.32 s for the best CPLEX setting, textbook Gomory over 8 hours with 69% of the gap closed (p. 3); corrected: 0.6 s vs 5.46 s (p. 5).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The scope is one instance; the authors set their research code against an industrial product (p. 2). The abstract's factor of at least 10 holds on the original instance; on the corrected one the best-setting ratio is about 9 (my arithmetic from Table 2, p. 5), which the text rounds to 10.

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the stall is read off the loop (basis determinants and LP trajectories against LPs solved) and cured by changing how the LP is re-optimised after each cut, not the cuts (pp. 3–5).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: the commercial baseline runs in three settings and the comparison is made against its best one; the harder corrected instance, where the margin shrinks, is reported too (pp. 3, 5).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: one run per setting and no seeds, although CPLEX's own settings spread from 4.32 to 9.01 s on the original and from 5.46 to 31.17 s on the corrected instance; the claim rests on the size of the gap (pp. 3, 5).
- Also Marks of good research 1 ✅: a 50-year-old hard instance falls to a simple device in under a second (pp. 1–3).
- new-pattern candidate: Lexicographic rule as stabiliser and tie-breaker (pp. 2–5; also S096 p. 21).
- new-pattern candidate: Post the showcase instance file (pp. 2, 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a pure cutting-plane run stalls numerically, plot the basis determinant against LPs solved and try lexicographic dual-simplex re-optimisation after each cut (pp. 3–4).
- Report pivots and LPs solved beside time when a research code meets a commercial solver, since times also measure engineering effort (p. 2).
- Run the commercial solver in several settings and quote the ratio to its best setting (p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "the “handicraft” work of a single student helped by his advisor" (p. 2)

### D013 · Orbital Shrinking (ISCO 2012, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-32147-4_6)
**Read level**: full (all 11 file pages of the preprint dated 12 July 2011, with Tables 1–2 and Figure 1). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Liberti match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Leo Liberti · **Position in trajectory**: the conference version of orbital shrinking; the four-author journal paper is S097 (not read here).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Discrete optimisers break symmetry, by symmetry-breaking constraints or by isomorphism pruning and orbital branching; the paper turns this around, treating symmetry as a feature to keep and to break only as a last resort (abstract, p. 1; p. 4). The question came from Mauro Diligenti, who asked why discrete optimisers want to destroy symmetry (acknowledgements, p. 10).
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace integrality of the variables by integrality of their sums over each orbit and rewrite the problem with one variable per orbit: the orbital-shrinking relaxation (pp. 4–5). Any subgroup also gives a relaxation; ranking the generators by the number of indices they fix gives a chain of subgroups that trades bound against solve time (pp. 5–6), and an orbit–constraint incidence measure picks a point on the chain automatically (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: min{f(x) : x in X, x_j integer for j in J} with f and X convex, a permutation group leaving f and the feasible set invariant, problems feasible and bounded (pp. 2–3); MILP and convex MINLP are covered (p. 3). Testbed: 39 symmetric MILPs from Margot's site, groups detected automatically with nauty (pp. 5–6).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Theorem 3.1: the shrunken problem has the value of the surrogate relaxation and bounds the original, proved by averaging an optimal solution over the group and using convexity (pp. 4–5); Corollary 3.2: exact when there are no integer variables (p. 5). Orbits are merged from the generators (p. 2); the relaxation is built with bash, GAP, AMPL and ROSE (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Cplex 12.2 on a 1.4 GHz Core 2 Duo, 1,800 s per relaxation (pp. 5–6); OSR bound and time against the LP bound and the time CPLEX needs to reach the same bound (pp. 9–10). With the whole group the bound is no better than the LP bound on 31 of 39 instances and no better than CPLEX's root bound on the other 8 (p. 6). For the 23 instances whose bound depends on the subgroup, the subgroup was hand-picked after solving every relaxation, the automatic rule's target (mean relative incidence 0.82, standard deviation 0.17) is the mean of those picks, and the rule is evaluated on the same 23 (pp. 6–9). CPLEX reaches the same bound faster on cod83 (p. 10).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Results called encouraging (p. 9); open: choosing the generators, MINLP extensions, use inside branch-and-bound with the relaxation changed along the tree, heuristics from the relaxed integer solution (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: symmetric variables are aggregated along orbits instead of breaking the symmetry (step 3), with a validity theorem (pp. 1, 4–5); this paper is the primary source of the limitation the skill takes from its peer-critique note (whole-group bound no better than the LP bound on 31 of 39 instances, p. 6).
- Method 4 ✗ contradiction: the subgroup choice is tuned on the instances it is evaluated on, and 16 of 39 instances are dropped because their bound does not depend on the choice (pp. 6–10); this is the 2011 lapse the skill lists under Method 4.
- Also Heuristic 1 ✅ (invert the reflex: keep the symmetry, pp. 1, 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a symmetric MILP, add one integer variable per orbit for the sum of its variables, solve the shrunken model as a relaxation, and check its bound against both the LP bound and the solver's root bound (pp. 4–6, 9–10).
- Rank symmetry generators by how many indices they fix to get a chain of subgroups, and plot bound and time along the chain before choosing a point (pp. 6–7).
- Score an aggregation by its incidence with constraint supports: an orbit that covers a whole constraint turns it into a mere bound on the new variable (pp. 8–9).

**Quotes**:
- "not better than the LP bound 31 times out of 39" (p. 6)
