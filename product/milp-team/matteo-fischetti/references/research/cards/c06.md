### S124 · Pruning moves (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2010, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1090.0329)
**Read level**: full. Abstract and §§1-7 (pp. 1-23) read in full, including Tables 1-6 (pp. 16-22) and the worked example of Figure 1 (pp. 6-8, read from the extracted tree labels and the text); acknowledgments and references (pp. 23-25) skimmed. The file is the author preprint dated May 4, 2008 (p. 1), not the journal text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin (DMPA, Padova, p. 1; his doctoral student of 2009 per SKILL.md's lineage) · **Place in trajectory**: a 2008 return to his 1988 dominance procedure with Toth (ref. [6], p. 24), funded by a Padova project "Integrating Integer Programming and Constraint Programming" (p. 23); the authors place it beside local branching [4] and MIP separation over the first Chvátal closure [5] as another case of modelling a sub-decision as a MILP (p. 5). The text does not mention a master's thesis.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Dominance between nodes is old (Kohler and Steiglitz [14], Ibaraki [11]) but unused in general-purpose MILP codes, because the classical relation "is too vague and of difficult application in a generic MILP context—in principle, every node not leading to an optimal solution could be declared as being dominated" (p. 2). The entry is his own 1988 procedure, which needs no information about other nodes (pp. 2, 5), together with the reason nobody had used it: "the approach, as described, tends to be excessively time consuming—the reduction in the number of nodes does not compensate for the large overhead introduced" (p. 2). The problem is thus stated as making a known test cheap enough to embed in a commercial solver (pp. 2-3).
  - Transferable: when an old method of your own was never adopted, write down the one obstacle (here overhead against node savings) and make the paper the removal of that obstacle (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep the Fischetti-Toth test, the auxiliary problem XP^α (2)-(4), a MILP over the fixed variables only, with right-hand side b^α = Σ_{j∈J^α} A_j x^α_j (p. 5), and harvest its by-product: a successful test yields a nogood and, combined with the dominating assignment, a "pruning move" Δ = [J^α, x^β − x^α] (p. 9). Moves go into a pool that "is checked in a rather inexpensive way at each node of the branching tree" (p. 3), by a search "akin to separation for cutting-plane methods" (p. 10). For binaries a move equals a nogood; for general integers one move covers a whole family, e.g. (−1, 1) for all pairs ((a, b), (a − 1, b + 1)) (pp. 9-10). The auxiliary problem is changed (§4): a random second-level objective fixed for the whole search replaces the lexicographic order, which "cannot be expressed as a linear objective without resorting to huge coefficients" (pp. 10-11); an optimality constraint (p. 11); a local branching constraint of radius k, so that only short moves are sought (pp. 11-12); and a local search around each new incumbent with every constraint kept at its incumbent activity (5), so that any two solutions of the solver's multiple-solution list give a move (p. 13). Considered and dropped: relaxing right-hand sides, which "can interfere and become incompatible with the post-processing procedure" for moves (p. 12). The use of test-set moves is inverted: "These moves are not used to improve the incumbent, as in the classical test set environment, but rather to fathom nodes" (p. 23).
  - Transferable: when an expensive oracle succeeds, store what it certified in a form that can be checked cheaply elsewhere (a pool), and call the oracle itself only at a few gated nodes (pp. 10, 14).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MILP min{c^T x : Ax ≤ b, l ≤ x ≤ u, x_j integer for j ∈ J} (p. 4); the auxiliary problem relies on the ≤ form of the rows (2) (p. 5). Dominance (Definition 1) requires the same fixed set, a partial cost not worse, and every completion of α to be a completion of β (p. 4). Correctness assumes the projection of F(P) on the integer variables bounded (Proposition 1, p. 8). The test-bed is drawn from "classes of practically relevant problems whose structure can trigger the dominance relationship" (p. 15): hard single knapsacks, Pisinger's spanner instances with almost strongly correlated profits, 60-90 items, natural for dominance (pp. 15-16), and network loading with single-path commodities and integer capacity units (8)-(10), where dominance is "hidden well inside the solution structure" (pp. 15, 17-18). The authors liken this to isomorphic pruning, of no use on problems without symmetry (p. 15). Right-hand-side relaxation would need knowledge of the constraint structure (knapsack and set covering examples, p. 12).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: (a) A counterexample comes first: a five-variable binary problem and a "deterministic" greedy heuristic whose fixing order depends on the size of the auxiliary problem fathom both optimal solutions, so "the LD tests produced a wrong answer" (Figure 1, pp. 6-8); the tie-break criterion so broken is one "proposed in [6]", his own 1988 paper (p. 6), and "it is not easy to define a “deterministic” algorithm for MILP", since solver output depends, e.g., on the input order of the variables (p. 6). (b) Proposition 1 (with the lexicographic tie-break the method returns the classical optimal value) by an exchange argument on the lexicographically minimal optimum x*: z takes x^β on the fixed set and x* elsewhere and would be cheaper or lexicographically smaller (pp. 8-9). (c) The proof uses only two properties of the order, well order and inheritance, so any order with both may replace it; a random objective fixed for the whole search qualifies (pp. 9, 11). (d) Pool hygiene: store minimal moves (drop components with x^α_j = x^β_j) and drop moves absorbed by others; a polynomial pool by relevance or length-bounded nogood recording is mentioned, not implemented (p. 10). (e) Placement and budgets (§5): skip the test until a first incumbent exists, since it can postpone finding one; run the auxiliary MILP only for depths in [depthmin, depthmax] and at multiples of a depth interval, after the node LP and just before branching ("applying the LD test before the LP relaxation is solved turned out to be less effective"); node limits N1 (auxiliary MILP) and N2 (incumbent search); a cap M on the number of tests, after which "the pruning effect is left to the move pool only"; the pool checked at every node (p. 14). C++ on Linux inside a commercial solver (p. 14).
  - Transferable: before claiming a pruning rule correct, build the smallest instance on which a plausible variant of it cuts off every optimum (pp. 6-8).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: ILOG Cplex 11.0 with default options; Intel Q6600 2.4 GHz, 4 GB, Linux (p. 15). Arms: the same B&C with and without the LD tests; "For a fair comparison, the same seed was used to initialize the random generator in all runs" (p. 16); one run per instance; seven parameters set by hand (p. 15), with k, N1 and N2 changed for network loading "due to the greatly increased size of the instances" (p. 18). Knapsack (16 instances; standard runs stopped at 1,200 s shown as lower bounds): geometric means >1,761,164 nodes and >98.78 s against 11,625 and 6.00 s, time ratio 16.47 geometric and 54.45 arithmetic (Table 1, p. 16). The text reports "an overall speedup of 23 times" with a node ratio of about 1:33, which are the ratios of the arithmetic means, and on the same page "an average speed-up of about 2-3 orders of magnitude", which matches the node ratios (398.89 average, 151.50 geometric) rather than the time ratios (my reading, p. 16). Internal statistics: pool checks and LD tests each fathom about a third of the times they are applied, about two thirds of the nodes in all; LD tests take 24.47% of the time on average (Table 2, pp. 16-17). Network loading (16 random grid instances, 1-hour limit): time ratio 4.78 and node ratio 7.68 (geometric means; Table 3, p. 19); LD success only 1.51% (Table 4, p. 19). Ablation by removal (§6.3): no pool with the same cap M (LD1) is "disastrous"; no pool and no cap (LD2) is about 5 times slower on knapsack and reaches the 1-hour limit on 11 of 16 network instances; no incumbent local search (LD3) always gives more nodes, yet less time on knapsack (geometric time ratio 0.74), and is kept because it pays on hard instances such as kp70 1 and kp90 1 (pp. 18, 20-21). The caption of Table 6 warns that LD1 and LD2 means "are seriously underestimated" because of time limits (p. 22). No MIPLIB instance is run; classes without dominance structure are excluded by design, which is said in advance (p. 15).
  - Transferable: print each component's overhead and success rate (pool time, pool success, test time, test success) next to the node and time ratios (Tables 2 and 4, pp. 17, 19).
- **D6 Positioning**: Credits the classical theory (Papadimitriou and Steiglitz [18], Kohler and Steiglitz [14], Ibaraki [11]) and says why it fails in MILP (pp. 2-4); puts the procedure near Hooker's logic cuts [10] and Margot's isomorphic pruning [15, 16] (p. 4); borrows nogoods from CP (Hooker and Yan [9], Kim and Hooker [13], his own Codato-Fischetti [3]) and improving moves from test-set theory (Graver [8], Scarf [21], Thomas and Weismantel [23]), adding that computing test sets for NP-hard problems "is however by far too expensive in practice" (pp. 2-3); his local branching [4] and Chvátal-closure MIP [5] are named as the same vein of "MIPping" (p. 5). The pool is distinguished from state-based dominance: "this is really not the case since the amount of information stored is much smaller" (p. 10).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The abstract names his own 1988 procedure as the starting point and renames a borrowed concept for its new use, "that we rename “pruning moves” so as to stress their use in a node fathoming context" (p. 1). Claims are hedged in the abstract ("can lead to a considerable speedup", p. 1) and in the conclusions ("speedups of up to 2 orders of magnitude on hard MILPs whose structure is amenable to dominance", p. 23); the contribution sentence opens "In our view, a main contribution of our work is the innovative use of improving moves" (p. 23). A worked counterexample (pp. 6-8) and a proof (pp. 8-9) precede the engineering sections. One mismatch sits inside p. 16 (23 times against 2-3 orders of magnitude; D5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic and computational: a dominance procedure usable inside a commercial MILP solver, with a corrected tie-break (pp. 8-9, 23). Limits written by the authors: dominance pays only on structures that trigger it (p. 15); the test can postpone incumbents and is skipped until one exists (p. 14); fathoming is "very unlikely at the very beginning of the search" (p. 13); the relative overhead on knapsack is significant because node relaxations are cheap (p. 16); the right-hand-side improvement is left out (p. 12); seven parameters are set by hand per class (pp. 15, 18). Future: adaptive parameter tuning and a tighter CP integration (p. 23).

**Method links**: Method 6 evidence ✅: the node-dominance sub-decision is written as an auxiliary MILP and solved "possibly in a heuristic way" by a general-purpose solver, which the authors call MIPping the dominance test (step 2, p. 5); the solver's multiple-solution mode feeds the pool (p. 13); the auxiliary MILP gets node limits and a call cap, after which the cheap pool takes over, a mild form of step 5 (drop the auxiliary MIP, or move it, when it is too slow) (p. 14). Method 5 evidence ✅: his own 1988 procedure is reopened (step 1, a procedure general-purpose codes never took up), the obstacle to adoption is conceded on the second page (step 3, p. 2), and a tie-break criterion of the 1988 paper is shown by counterexample to give wrong answers (pp. 6-8); SKILL.md's Method 5 practice line adds "through a master's thesis", which this text does not mention. Method 1 variant ⚠: ablation by removal is done (pool, cap M, incumbent search; pp. 18-22), but the incumbent search is kept although removing it lowers knapsack time (geometric ratio 0.74), because it pays on the hard instances (pp. 20-21): step 5's "keep one only if removing it hurts" is applied per instance, not on average. Method 3 variant ⚠ (dated, 2008): one fixed seed in all runs "For a fair comparison" (p. 16), so spread is controlled away rather than measured; the effect sizes (geometric time ratios 16.47 and 4.78, pp. 16, 19) are far above the seed effect M3 cites. Method 4 variant ⚠: same solver, seed and code, with components switched off for the ablation (pp. 16, 18), but the test-bed is declared structure-friendly instead of being built by a neutral rule, and no class where the method does not pay is run (p. 15). Heuristic 3 evidence ✅: nogoods from CP and improving moves from test sets (pp. 2-3, 9). Heuristic 2 evidence ✅: the test-set move is kept and its use is moved from improving the incumbent to fathoming (p. 23). New-pattern candidate: *harvest the by-products of expensive oracle calls* (a successful auxiliary MILP becomes a stored move checked at every node, pp. 9-10, 14; the same move appears in D012 and S063 of this batch). New-pattern candidate: *strip a correctness proof to the properties it uses, then substitute any object that has them* (lexicographic order replaced by a fixed random objective, pp. 9-11).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Model a node-level yes/no test as a small MILP over the fixed variables, solve it under a node limit, and store each success as a reusable move or nogood (pp. 5, 9-10, 14).
- Keep stored moves minimal (drop unchanged components) and drop moves absorbed by others, so the pool stays short without losing fathoming power (p. 10).
- Seed the pool early with a short local search around each new incumbent that keeps every constraint at its incumbent activity (equalities (5)), so that every pair of solutions found yields a move (p. 13).
- Gate an expensive node test by a depth window, a depth interval, a call cap and the existence of a first incumbent, and place it after the node LP, just before branching (p. 14).
- List what a correctness proof needs from a tie-break order (well order, inheritance) and use the cheapest order that has those properties (pp. 9, 11).
**Quotes**:
- "In a sense, we are using here the approach of “MIPping the dominance test” (i.e., of modeling it as a MILP)" (p. 5)
- "These moves are not used to improve the incumbent, as in the classical test set environment, but rather to fathom nodes in the enumeration tree—hence the name “pruning moves”." (p. 23)

### S074 · Lexicography and degeneracy: can a pure cutting plane algorithm work? (Mathematical Programming 2011, DOI 10.1007/s10107-009-0335-0)
**Read level**: full. Abstract and §§1-8 (pp. 1-25) read in full; Tables 1-4 (pp. 11, 13, 18, 19) read, with exit codes and gap columns counted row by row; Figures 1-9 (pp. 3, 8, 15-16, 20-21, 23-24) read from captions and text, since the plotted data are not in the extraction; references (p. 26) skimmed. The file is an author version with running heads and its own page numbers 1-26.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Arrigo Zanette (first author) and Egon Balas · **Place in trajectory**: the journal version of the IPCO 2008 paper [19] (p. 4; D006 per the work list), in the 2008-2012 "Gomory's cuts revisited" period of SKILL.md's trajectory table; the decisive idea is credited to "our youngest coauthor" (p. 2), Zanette, his doctoral student per SKILL.md's lineage; the project's "scope extends well beyond the present paper" (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Pure cutting-plane methods with Gomory cuts "are typically not used in practice, due to their poor convergence properties" (p. 1). The entry is a reframing of branching: "In a sense, branching can be viewed as just a “symptomatic cure” to the well-known drawbacks of Gomory cuts", a cure that "hides the real source of the problems" (p. 1); the benefit of rounds of cuts was found only in 1996, "about 40 years after their introduction" (pp. 1-2). The goal is a viable pure cutting-plane method, one "not knocked out by numerical difficulties", "even if on most problems it will not be competitive with the branch-and-bound based methods" (p. 2). Four questions frame the study, the last whether the paradigm "the stronger the cut, the better" holds for cuts read from the tableau (p. 2). The decisive idea came while testing ways to keep the basis determinant and condition number down: the lexicographic dual simplex of one of Gomory's finiteness proofs, a method Gomory himself did not advocate for practice and that, to the authors' knowledge, nobody had tried extensively (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when a component is always used inside a larger scheme that masks its failure, study it alone, where the failure is visible (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Cutting-plane LPs are massively dual degenerate; use that degeneracy to pick, among the equivalent optima, the lexicographically minimal vertex, so that the tableau stays "clean" and so do the next cuts (pp. 5-6). The Gomory fractional cut is unchanged; what changes is which optimal vertex the loop hands to the cut generator, and the rounding direction. The lexicographic simplex is implemented without touching the black-box LP solver: fix the nonbasic variables with nonzero reduced cost, reoptimize with x1 as objective, fix again, continue over smaller degenerate subspaces, then unfix and restore the objective (pp. 6-7); the scheme is akin to the Balinski-Tucker tableau and to slack fixing in preemptive goal programming (p. 6). Two cheaper heuristics mimic it: Heur1 adds the most lex-significant fractional variable to the objective (min x0 + x_h), Heur2 gives a small negative cost to the slacks of earlier cuts so that they stay basic; both run as two LPs to avoid perturbation numerics (p. 9). Rounding must agree with the lexicographic objective (coefficients rounded up for minimization, pp. 7-8); the right and the wrong sign differ hugely on bm23 in gap and coefficient size (Figure 3, p. 8). For mixed-integer cuts, discretized GMI cuts (d-GMI) sit between GFC and GMI (pp. 22-23).
  - Transferable: when an LP has many optimal vertices, choose on purpose which one to hand to the separator (a second-level objective over the optimal face), instead of whichever vertex the solver happens to return (pp. 5-7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pure ILP min c^T x, Ax = b, x ≥ 0 integer, with A, b, c integer and P bounded (p. 4); input integer "possibly after scaling", an integer variable x0 carrying the objective as Gomory's proof prescribes, cuts derived in integer form and slacks substituted back (p. 11). Test bed of 25 instances, mainly MIPLIB 3 and 2003 plus two hard single knapsacks and two 2-matching instances (Table 1, p. 11); "even very small instances of these libraries (such as stein15, bm23, etc.) have never been solved by a pure cutting plane method based on GFC or GMI cuts read from the LP tableau" (p. 11). Correctness assumptions: finite convergence needs the lexicographic simplex and a rounding consistent with it, details deferred to Gomory [14] (pp. 7-8); cut validity is defined relative to the LP solver's optimality threshold (Definition 1, p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: (a) Convergence sketch: under the lexicographic sign pattern every j ∈ J+ has a row t < i with a_tj < 0, so after each cut either the cut-generating variable rises to ⌈x*_h⌉ or a higher-ranked variable increases (pp. 7-8). (b) Validity device: a GFC has Chvátal rank 1 for the current formulation, so an LP certifies it; subtracting the tableau row gives s = f_i0 − Σ f_ij x_j, so the current basis maximizes the cut's slack function at value f_i0 < 1 (p. 10). (c) ε-validity: a cut is ε-valid if the basis ε-maximizes its left-hand side under the solver's reduced-cost test, so that "cut invalidity would come into play only when the optimality test itself is invalidated by numerical problems" (Definition 1, p. 10); rounding with floor(a) = ⌊a + ε⌋ preserves ε-validity (pp. 10-11), ε "set in a conservative way with respect to the optimality tolerance of the LP solver" (p. 11), ε = 1e−8 (p. 11). (d) Cut purging: Lex may drop cuts as soon as they are slack; the other methods only after a nonzero change of objective value, because otherwise "the entire algorithm can loop—a situation that we actually encountered during our experiments", at the price of out-of-memory stops after long stalls (p. 12). (e) Two GFCs per selected row (the row and its negative); TB chooses rows at random with a bias towards fractional parts near 0.5, and "alternative rules produced comparable results"; cuts with coefficients above 10^10 are discarded (exit status N) (p. 12). (f) Diagnostics: maximum cut coefficient and maximum condition number κ beside the closed gap (p. 12); LP-vertex trajectories projected by multidimensional scaling (Figure 4, pp. 14-15); "fractional spectrography", a pseudo-colour plot of variable values over iterations in lexicographic order (pp. 15-16); average cut depth and distance between consecutive optima (Figures 6-7, pp. 17, 20-21). (g) d-GMI: δ_j replaced by k_j/α, a weakening of GMI that dominates GFC; slacks are multiples of 1/α and kept rational for the substitution (pp. 22-23).
  - Transferable: tie a cut's validity tolerance to the LP solver's own optimality tolerance, and derive the cut in integer arithmetic (pp. 10-11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Intel Core 2 Q6600 2.40 GHz, 1 hour and 2 GB per instance (p. 11); one run per instance and method. Single cut (Table 2, p. 13): TB stopped for lack of acceptable cuts in 60% of the cases, Lex solved 12 of 25 to optimality (p. 14); the text says Lex met the no-acceptable-cut situation "in a single case" (p. 14), while the table shows status N for Lex on cap6000 and p0201 (my count, p. 13). Multi-cut (Table 3, p. 18): Lex reaches the optimum on 13 instances, "13 out of the 26 instances" in the text although Table 1 lists 25 (pp. 11, 16); the difference between single- and multi-cut is "not as striking as one would expect", explained by convergence resting on a single cut (pp. 16-17). Losses are in the tables, not in the text: in single-cut mode TB closes more gap than Lex on lin318 (100% in 8.53 s against 63.64% at the limit), mitre, p0548z, seymour and timtab1-int (p. 13); in multi-cut mode on mzzv11, mzzv42z and p0548 (p. 18) (my counts). Heuristics (Table 4, p. 19): "both heuristics are effective in controlling the coefficient size, determinant, and condition number", with closed gap better than TB and worse than Lex (p. 17); Heur2 ends out of memory on 18 of 25 instances (my count, p. 19). In the extracted Table 4 the Heur1 columns Itrs, Cuts, Time, ClGap and Coeff repeat the TB multi-cut entries of Table 3 on all 25 rows (e.g. air04 29, 9277, 711.79, 9.07, 8.8e+02), only κ and the exit code differing (pp. 18-19, my check), so Heur1's coefficient control cannot be read from the table as printed in this version. A control is mentioned but not reported: restarting the LP solver from scratch after adding the cuts "had a significant impact on the performance of the textbook method (though not comparable to that derived from the use of the lexicographic method)", read as "showing the importance of breaking the correlation of the optimal LP bases" (p. 16). d-GMI on bm23: 714, 663 and 651 iterations for approximation errors 1, 0.25 and 0.0625, at most 9% fewer, with coefficient sizes growing exponentially in α; "These results were confirmed on other instances" (p. 25).
  - Transferable: report numerical-health indicators (maximum coefficient, condition number, determinant) as results next to the bound, and give each run an exit code that says why it stopped (pp. 12-13).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Gomory [12-14] and on Balas, Ceria, Cornuéjols and Natraj [3] (pp. 1-2). Separates the tableau method from methods that work on the original polyhedron, with separation "decoupled from the LP reoptimization", among them Fischetti and Lodi [11] and Balas and Saxena [10]; the Gomory method is "intrinsically different" (p. 5). Takes Margot's finding that invalid cuts "are generated more frequently than expected" [16] as motivation (p. 9). Criticizes Cook et al.'s numerically safe GMI cuts [8] for a pure cutting-plane context, since they "can produce slightly-modified cuts that do not preserve the nice properties of the original cuts" (p. 10). Distinguishes d-GMI from Letchford and Lodi's strengthening [15] (p. 22).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The title is a question; the introduction is told as two surprises ("The implementation of this method produced a huge surprise", p. 2; "Here came our second great surprise", p. 3), with plots before any table (Figures 1-2, p. 3). A medical metaphor carries the thesis (p. 1). Explanations are offered and hedged: "this property does not guarantee that there will be no numerical problems, but the method seems to work pretty well in practice" (p. 14). The negative d-GMI result is stated plainly ("This is quite unsatisfactory", p. 25). The idea is credited to the youngest coauthor (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Computational and algorithmic: an implementation of lexicographic Gomory with the right rounding and ε-valid floors, solving stein15, stein27 and bm23 and others by pure cutting planes (pp. 1, 14, 25). Limits written: not competitive with branch-and-bound on most problems (p. 2); no guarantee against numerical trouble (p. 14); GMI cuts, even discretized, appear "intrinsically more difficult to handle than GFCs, at least within a cutting plane method for pure ILPs" (p. 25). Future: the lexicographic simplex with other cuts, GMI included (p. 25). Not discussed: the instances where TB beats Lex, the heuristics' memory stops, the unreported restart control.

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅ (already in SKILL.md's Method 2 practice): the failure blamed on Gomory cuts is traced to the loop, which hands the separator whatever vertex the dual simplex reaches first, so that "the textbook method enters a feedback loop that is responsible for the exponential growth of the determinant" (p. 14); the trajectory and the determinant are plotted on one small instance, stein15 (step 1, Figure 4, pp. 14-15); the remedy moves the query point by lexicographic reoptimization and leaves the cut alone (step 3, pp. 5-7); the not-competitive caveat matches step 6 (p. 2). Method 3 variant ⚠: a cheap intervention that should partly reproduce the effect if the explanation is right (an LP restart) is run and helps TB partly (p. 16), and a deliberately wrong variant (wrong rounding sign) serves as a contrast arm (Figure 3, p. 8); but the restart results are not reported, TB's random row choice gets one run (p. 12), and no spread is measured. Method 6 evidence ✅ (step 3, do not duplicate the solver): the lexicographic simplex is mimicked by fixings and reoptimizations of the black-box LP solver, since "a clever approach should not interfere too much with the black-box LP solver used" (p. 6). Method 4 variant ⚠: all methods share the LP solver, the cut derivation and ε (pp. 11-12), but the purge rule differs by necessity (p. 12) and the losses in Tables 2-3 go undiscussed. Heuristic 1 evidence ✅: dual degeneracy, usually fought, is exploited to choose the cleanest LP solution among the equivalent optima (pp. 5-6); "the stronger the cut, the better" is put in question (p. 2), and GMI is found harder to handle than GFC (p. 25). Heuristic 8 evidence ✅: bound plots on air04 and stein27 open the paper (Figures 1-2, p. 3), and trajectories on stein15 come before the aggregates are discussed (pp. 14-16). Heuristic 10 evidence ✅: cut validity is certified, with a conservative ε, after Margot (pp. 9-11). Research Taste mark 3 variant ⚠: the d-GMI failure is printed (p. 25), the Lex losses only in the tables (pp. 13, 18). New-pattern candidate: *take the proof device seriously as an algorithm* (the lexicographic simplex that Gomory used to simplify a finiteness proof becomes the practical remedy, p. 2). ### D012 · Branching on nonchimerical fractionalities (Operations Research Letters 2012, DOI 10.1016/j.orl.2012.01.008)
**Read level**: full. Abstract, §§1-5 (pp. 1-10), Table 4 (p. 11) and the performance profiles of Figure 1 (p. 12, read from the caption and the text of p. 10) read in full; references (pp. 10, 13) read. The file is the manuscript "Submitted: 6 April 2011; Revised: 26 October 2011" (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michele Monaci · **Place in trajectory**: one of the 2011 branching papers with Monaci, citing their IPCO 2011 backdoor branching [7] (p. 2; S063 in this batch); not on the Scholar profile (work-list note). The search path is said to change "in an erratic way" with numerics (p. 7), before the 2014 erraticism paper (my observation).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Full strong branching (FSB) gives the smallest trees, "though its associated overhead may be substantial in most cases" (p. 1). The entry is a number taken from the literature: in Achterberg's SCIP comparison FSB reduces nodes by 75% while doubling time, so a 2x faster FSB "would suffice to make it be the fastest option" in that environment (p. 2). The question becomes how to gather the same information as FSB with fewer LPs (p. 2). A self-imposed constraint shapes the study: the authors try to escape "the “local optimal” common practice" of mixing strong branching with pseudocosts and use no pseudocost information, "so as to be forced to find valid alternatives" (p. 2). The motivation is a qualitative picture: with side constraints, many small fractional knapsack items can trick a rule away from the one large item, and in big-M models an "important" binary often sits near zero (pp. 1-2).
  - Transferable: compute the break-even from a published comparison (node reduction against time increase) and set it as the target before designing anything (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Name the target: chimerical fractionalities, fixable by "only a small deterioration of the objective function", against nonchimerical ones, where branching "has a large impact on the LP solution" (p. 3); "FSB can be viewed as a computationally-expensive way to detect nonchimerical fractionalities" (p. 3). Three changes to FSB, each in one piece: (1) parametrized FSB (PFSB): LB0_j and LB1_j are upper bounds with exactness flags; every LP solved for one candidate updates the bounds of all candidates whose value it already rounds; the loop stops when the best-scoring candidate is exact on both sides (Algorithm 1, pp. 3-4), giving "the same information as the standard implementation of FSB" with fewer LPs (p. 3); (2) perseverant branching (PPFSB): only variables already branched on at earlier nodes stay candidates, the full list being restored when it is empty, also read as a heuristic for a backdoor (p. 5); (3) asymmetric branching (APPFSB): for 0-1 problems where the down branch is the critical one, all up-flags start as exact, so up-branch LPs are never solved (p. 5). Unchanged: the score functions (min, prod; pp. 5-6), the LP solver and the tree search.
  - Transferable: when a selection rule queries an expensive oracle candidate by candidate, keep bounds plus exactness flags for all candidates and let every answer update each candidate it informs (Algorithm 1, p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{c^T x : Ax ≤ b, x_j integer ∀j ∈ I}, branching on a single variable (p. 1). Assumptions: the score is monotone (p. 3); with a dual-pivot limit some LB values "may not represent valid upper bounds", which the experiments are said not to affect (p. 4); the asymmetric rule depends on structure (set covering, partitioning or packing; big-M network design) and is not invariant under complementing the binaries: "flipping the binary variables of a 0-1 MIP model does not change the problem at hand, but likely would have a bad impact on the performance of asymmetric branching" (p. 5). Test set: all instances of Achterberg, Koch and Martin [3], those of Karzan, Nemhauser and Savelsbergh [8] not classified "easy", and MIPLIB 2010 "benchmark", "hard" and "tree", each taken after root preprocessing; instances not solved by standard FSB within 10,000 s and those solved by preprocessing removed, leaving 60 (p. 6).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Algorithm 1 (p. 4): an LP with x_k ≤ ⌊x*_k⌋ or x_k ≥ ⌈x*_k⌉ gives δ; candidates whose value the new solution already rounds get min{LB, δ}, and δ = 0 makes their flag exact (p. 4). Invariant used as a check: FSB and PFSB use "exactly the same lexicographic rule to break ties", so they produce the same tree, and node counts differ only through "very rare numerical issues" (p. 7). Implementation in C inside IBM ILOG Cplex 12.2 through callbacks, single thread (p. 6). FSB is coded by the authors rather than through CPXstrongbranch, which "has direct access to the internal data-structure and does not need, e.g., to refactor the basis" (p. 6); RB, the proxy for solver defaults, uses pseudocosts once a variable is reliable (η_rel = 8) and CPXstrongbranch otherwise (pp. 6-7). Hybrid (§4): strong branching at the first 10 levels and for the first θ nodes, then pseudocosts (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Intel Core i5 2.67 GHz, single thread, 10,000 s, at most 20 dual pivots per strong-branching LP unless varied (p. 6). Fairness, following [8]: no further preprocessing, no heuristics, no cuts, and the optimal value as upper cutoff (p. 6); default node selection is kept because basis refactorization may change "the whole search path in an erratic way" (p. 7). Metrics: geometric mean of time (no shift stated) with its ratio to FSB, number unsolved, geometric mean of nodes (pp. 6-7). Table 1 (prod score): FSB 471.00 s, PFSB 355.68 (0.76), PPFSB 214.08 (0.45), APPFSB 199.41 (0.42), RB 226.19 (0.48); speedups about 1.3, 2.2 and 2.3; nodes almost unchanged for PFSB, slightly up for PPFSB, doubled for APPFSB (p. 7). Table 2 repeats the comparison with 10, 50 and unlimited pivots (p. 8). Hybrid (Table 3, p. 9): PFSB+pcosts about 10% faster than FSB+pcosts, PPFSB+pcosts a further 30%; Cplex 12.2 default at 48.00 s is "significantly faster than all other methods", with the caveat that it has native access and uses strong-branching information for fixing and propagation, so "a direct comparison of IBM ILOG Cplex’s performance with that of the other methods would be not completely fair" (p. 9). The appendix reports each hybrid "with its best-tuned parameter θ" (θ = 500 and 1,000), chosen on the same 60 instances (pp. 9, 11). Per-instance results show time limits (APPFSB on neos13, FSB+pcosts on neos-1396125, RB on ns1688347; Table 4, p. 11); in the performance profiles Cplex is "the clear winner in terms of speed" and FSB best in nodes (p. 10; Figure 1, p. 12). - **D6 Positioning**: Surveys the rules in two paragraphs: most-fractional "not significantly better then just choosing xb at random" [3] (p. 1); FSB of Applegate, Bixby, Chvátal and Cook [4], pseudocosts [5], reliability branching [3], Patel and Chinneck [12], Pryor and Chinneck [13], inference branching [1, 2], Karzan et al. ### S067 · Three ideas for the quadratic assignment problem (Operations Research 2012, DOI 10.1287/opre.1120.1073)
**Read level**: full. Abstract, §§1-5 (pp. 1-16), Tables 1-3 (pp. 10, 12, 15) and footnotes 1-3 (pp. 2, 12, 15) read in full; Figures 1-3 (pp. 3, 14, 15) read from captions and the extracted node labels; references (pp. 16-17) skimmed. The file is manuscript OPRE-2011-06-292, "Submitted: 11 June 2011; rev. 23 September 2011" (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michele Monaci and Domenico Salvagnin · **Place in trajectory**: first presented at CPAIOR 2011 as a late-breaking abstract (pp. 2, 16); dedicated "In memory of Jens Clausen" (p. 1); the work behind SKILL.md's Research Taste mark 1 (esc instances "in minutes on a notebook", a deck phrase) and the slim QAP models in its Method 1 practice line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A public challenge: a collection of "(apparently very small) test-cases" in QAPLIB "that cannot be solved by the current state of the art even by allowing for tremendous computing power"; tho30 alone took the equivalent of 8,997 days on a grid (p. 2). Seven esc instances were open at the start of 2011, and four were announced solved in January 2011 by Nyberg and Westerlund, with no description of the method (p. 2). The pointer taken is Anstreicher's remark that the structure of the larger esc instances "is likely to be important" (p. 2). The largest instance sets the requirement: "As our ultimate goal was to solve esc128, we decided to address more scalable models" (p. 5).
  - Transferable: pick an open benchmark instance whose difficulty you suspect is structural, and let the largest open instance set the scalability requirement for every modelling choice (pp. 2, 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three ideas (pp. 2, 16). (1) Clone shrinking: study "the symmetry of the QAP instance at hand—rather than that of its formulation" (p. 2); two facilities are clones when their mutual flows are equal, their flows to and from every other facility coincide and their assignment costs coincide; the clone relation partitions the facilities into types, and the QAP becomes a rectangular one in which type u places μ_u identical copies (Definition 1, (5)-(10), pp. 3-4); the question asked is "can one actually take advantage of symmetry?", not how to break it (p. 3); one variable is fixed through the single b-orbit, the pair chosen by expected branching impact rather than at random (p. 5). (2) A compact model: not the Adams-Johnson linearization with Θ(n^4) variables but an O(n^2) Kaufman-Broeckx-type model (p. 5), improved along Xia and Yuan and Zhang et al. into (32)-(35), with reduced-cost coefficients made sparse and lifted (Theorem 3, pp. 8-9); solved first by black-box Cplex with a branching-priority file, then by an ad-hoc B&C with local cuts, its own branching rule and orbital branching on the Hamming-distance symmetries of the esc distance matrix (pp. 9-12). (3) Facility-flow splitting: for a = a1 + a2 with nonnegative parts, opt(a1) + opt(a2) ≤ opt(a) (Theorem 4, p. 13), with the split chosen to create clones in both parts (criterion (i), the support of a; criterion (ii), a facility subset with little flow across the cut; p. 13). The third idea came from an anomaly: with some flow entries of esc128 set to zero a bound of 48 was proved in about 2 seconds, while the full instance reached about 40 after minutes; "reducing the costs can in fact improve the bound obtained after a fixed amount of enumeration", because the reduced matrix induced symmetries (pp. 12-13).
  - Transferable: when a deliberately weakened instance gives a better bound after a fixed effort than the original, look for the structure the weakening created, and turn the observation into a valid decomposition bound (pp. 12-13).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Koopmans-Beckmann QAP (1)-(4) with nonnegative flows a, distances b and costs c, and a_ff = b_ii = 0 after moving the linear terms into c (p. 1). Structure exploited: esc instances have zero (dummy) facilities, the setting of Kaibel, which clone shrinking generalizes (p. 4); tai64c has two clone classes of sizes 13 and 51 (p. 4), so its 64 facilities become m = 2 types (p. 9); "almost no clone exists" in the b matrices of QAPLIB (p. 4); every esc instance has a single b-orbit, checked with nauty (p. 5); b_ij is the Hamming distance between the binary representations of i − 1 and j − 1, minus 1 (p. 11). The evenness trick requires symmetric integer a and b and an even integer c (p. 9); Theorem 4 assumes c = 0, and criterion (i) an integer a (p. 13). The disjunctive hull P_iu is complex: for a toy with n = m = 5, PORTA gave 1693 facets with coefficients up to 84 in absolute value (p. 6), which is why one constraint per pair is sought instead (p. 6).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: (a) Proofs: Theorem 1 (the shrunken model is valid) by cost-preserving maps in both directions, the inverse built by scanning nodes and marking used clones (p. 4); Theorem 3 by the reduced-cost property on the x_iu = 1 side and, for x_iu = 0, by the last incompatible pair in the lifting sequence (p. 8); Theorem 4 by splitting the cost of any feasible y (p. 13). (b) Lessons from "a number of preliminary (sometimes quite disappointing) tests" (p. 7): a single big-M constraint per pair stays weak however it is lifted; the root LP value "is not a discriminating factor (all forms producing essentially the same LP bound)", so prefer the form that fits the solver, sparse with small coefficients; do not hide the instance symmetry, since over-sophisticated formulations can stop a general-purpose solver from recognizing it (p. 7). (c) Black-box run: priority score 10^6 (MX_iu − MN_iu) + 10^3 u + i (p. 9); Cplex cuts and heuristics off because cuts "seem to be counterproductive", the most aggressive symmetry level (5), and a cutoff of 10^8 only to help preprocessing (p. 9). (d) B&C: local cuts σ_iu ≥ (MN+_iu − MN_iu) x_iu from an O(n) Gilmore-Lawler-like computation, generated only at nodes with an even number of variables fixed to 1 in [2..16] and added only if at least 5 are violated, because adding cuts to the LP costs more than separating them (pp. 10-11); a branching rule that strengthens the weak zero branch (p. 11); specialized orbital branching, (x_iu = 1) ∨ (x_ju = 0 for all j in the orbit of i under the pointwise stabilizer), with all Cplex symmetry features and dual reductions off "To ensure correctness of the resulting code" (p. 11); with i_fix = n the bit flips drop out, leaving bit permutations, all 7! = 5,040 of them precomputed for esc128 (pp. 11-12).
  - Transferable: before writing a compact formulation, enumerate the facets of the exact disjunctive hull on a toy instance (PORTA) to see whether a complete description is even plausible (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Quad-core Intel Xeon 3.2 GHz, 16 GB, Mac OS 10.6, Cplex 12.2 with 8 threads in interactive mode (pp. 9-10). Black box (Table 1, p. 10): the esc16 instances, esc32e and esc32g within 131 s; esc32c 9,643.82 s, esc32d 2,973.26 s, esc64a 509.87 s, tai64c 18,250.40 s; all optima equal the QAPLIB best known values; the .lp and .ord files and the logs are "available, on request, from the authors" (p. 10). Rival: Nyberg and Westerlund's times "on a similar PC" (24,365, 36,256, 16,370 and 182,983 s) are quoted, not re-run; their method solved esc32a, "whereas our method was not successful on this instance" (p. 10). Splitting (Table 3, p. 15): esc32h proved optimal at 438 (7,799.90 s), esc128 at 64 (9.63 s), esc32a bounded at 128 of 130, esc32b at 136, above the 132 (out of 168) in QAPLIB (pp. 13-15); times exclude writing the model files, about one minute for esc128 (p. 15). Variability is measured once, in a footnote: "we tried 200 random perturbations of our branching priority order: the bound available after 50,000 enumeration nodes using IBM ILOG Cplex 12.2 in interactive mode ranged from just 4 to 46" (p. 12, fn. 2). - Detect interchangeable objects in the data (clones) and aggregate them into types with multiplicities before modelling; solutions map back by any assignment of the copies (pp. 3-4).

- When you implement your own symmetry handling inside a commercial solver, switch off the solver's symmetry features and dual reductions, which can conflict with yours (p. 11).
- Bound a hard instance by splitting its cost data into two nonnegative parts chosen to make both subproblems more symmetric; the two optima add up to a valid bound (pp. 13-15).

### S063 · Backdoor branching (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2013, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1120.0531)

The hypothesis is stated: even hard MIPs could be solved by a compact tree "if an appropriate set of branching variables is chosen" (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: (1) MIP compactness as set covering: every LP vertex cheaper than v(P) is an obstacle whose fractional support the chosen branching set must cover, (5)-(7), solved by constraint generation in a "Benders-like" loop (Figure 1, p. 4); the approach is said to recall Chvátal's resolution search (p. 3). (2) Backdoor branching: a master-slave multi-restart; the set covering model (master) proposes a small priority set, and the black-box MIP solver (slave), with those priorities and best-bound-first search, collects new low-cost fractional solutions; after Kmax = 10 uncovered ones it returns to the master, for at most R = 10 rounds; when the cover needs more than Γ = 5 variables the cost threshold is lowered by (8) and the backdoor becomes approximate (pp. 5-6); a final run in default mode takes priority 1 for the last cover and 0 for the rest, "thus avoiding any other interference with its highly-optimized internal mechanisms" (p. 1). The cover's costs γ_j = M − p_j reward variables with large pseudocosts read from the interrupted slaves, and never-branched variables are treated as continuous, a guard against covers made of variables "whose integrality requirement is in fact redundant" (pp. 6-7).
  - Transferable: use restarts to collect evidence (here fractional solutions that block the bound) and let a small combinatorial model turn the evidence into one decision handed to the unmodified solver (pp. 5-7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP (P) with integer set I and continuous set C, bounds inside Ax ≥ b (pp. 1-2). The certificate tree assumes pure branch-and-bound (no cuts, variable fixing or node preprocessing) and a known v(P), relaxed later (p. 2). The compactness model is restricted to 0-1 MIPs (p. 3); general integers are treated heuristically by the set covering model (p. 3) and would have to be counted, e.g., in proportion to the logarithm of their range (p. 2). Without v(P) the method can over-estimate the backdoor in the degenerate case c^T x̃ = v(P) (p. 5). Instances from MIPLIB 2003, COR@L and MIPLIB 2010 (p. 7).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: (a) Device: branching on 0-1 variables adds no new vertices, shown by moving each branching into the objective with a large constant M, so every node vertex is a vertex of P and the obstacles can be taken over the root polyhedron (p. 4). (b) Conceptual exact loop: solve SCP(F), compute L(S) by enumeration, add the optimal x̃ if it is cheaper than v(P), repeat (Figure 1, p. 4). (c) The heuristic loop of Figure 2 with Kmax, R, Γ and the threshold rule (8) (pp. 6-7); the set covering is solved with no time limit, "as its optimal solution always required a very short computing time" (p. 5). (d) Pseudocost weights p_j = ⌊100 (Ψ−_j + Ψ+_j)/Ψmax⌋ with M = 1000, which "proved useful to deal with degenerate cases with a lot of ties" (pp. 6-7). (e) Accounting: times and nodes sum all sampling and final runs, the root counted once because all runs start from the same root information (p. 8). (f) Integration limit: static priorities, "our very rigid scheme" (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Intel i5 750 2.67 GHz, single thread, 10,000 s and 8 GB per run (p. 7). Test-bed rule, "intended to reduce biasing in favor of one of two codes under comparison" (p. 7): keep the instances Cplex 12.2 default (single thread, no cutoff) solves within the limit, minus those solved within 1,000 nodes or 100 s (pp. 7-8); then, for both codes, the optimal value as cutoff and heuristics off following [11], cuts off, "resulting into a significant slowdown or even a time-limit condition for some instances", and variable aggregation off because it interferes with priorities (p. 8); then drop instances solved at the root and instances solved by neither code, leaving 70 (p. 8). Arms: CPX default (no cutoff), Cplex (the restricted settings) and Backdoor (same settings) (p. 8). Results (Table 1, pp. 9-10): solved 70, 63 and 66; arithmetic means 2,172.45, 2,637.60 and 2,274.25 s; geometric means 1,108.64, 711.33 and 712.78 s. Against Cplex, Backdoor solves three more and cuts arithmetic-mean time and nodes by about 15% and 25% (pp. 8-9); CPX default leads in arithmetic mean, "largely expected" with cuts and aggregation off in the other two (p. 8). On geometric means the authors argue that time limits "penalize the more clever methods that are able to solve more instances" and apply Hutter et al.'s tenfold penalty, giving 1108, 985 and 813 s (p. 9). Losses are printed: Backdoor reaches the time limit on four instances Cplex solves (neos-1324574, neos-1337307, neos-1451294, ns1324654) and loses clearly on, e.g., neos-1439395 (140.32 against 21.40 s) and glass4 (23.54 against 5.11 s) (pp. 9-10); two of those rows carry identical node counts for Cplex (52,413) and for CPX default (21,777), which suggests one model under two names (my observation, pp. 9-10). The final-run column Tlast (geometric mean 679.27 against 712.78 for the total) is offered as the saving a tighter integration could approach (p. 10). A variant with strong branching in the sampling runs shortened the final run but did not repay its sampling time (p. 11). One run per instance; no seeds. The exact compactness model of §2 is not run: no backdoor size is reported in this text.
  - Transferable: sum all runs of a restart scheme, count the shared root once, and report the final run separately (pp. 8, 10).
  - Transferable: report arithmetic and geometric means and, where arms hit time limits differently, a penalized mean (tenfold time for unsolved runs) (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: Bridges AI and MIP: backdoors (Williams, Gomes and Selman [16]; Dilkina et al. [9]) (pp. 2-3) and Chvátal's resolution search [7] (p. 3); the restart idea is "in the spirit of" Karzan, Nemhauser and Savelsbergh [11], whose fairness protocol is adopted too (pp. 3, 8). The branching literature is named briefly (strong branching [4], reliability branching [2], Patel and Chinneck [14], Pryor and Chinneck [15]; p. 2). Hutter, Hoos and Leyton-Brown [10] supply the penalized mean (p. 9). The baseline, named in the abstract, is the state-of-the-art commercial solver (p. 1).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A modest claim in the abstract: "some speedup can be achieved even with respect to a state-of-the-art solver such as IBM ILOG Cplex 12.2" (p. 1); "proof-of-concept" recurs (pp. 3, 6, 11). Steps announced as a "first order of business" and a "second step" (p. 3). The authors name the risk of their own definition ("can be quite risky in practice", p. 6). A surprise is reported and then explained ("Surprisingly enough, Backdoor turns out to be even faster than CPX default", p. 9). The conclusion puts the implementation first: "The actual implementation of the above scheme is of course instrumental for its practical success." (p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Conceptual (MIP compactness as set covering), algorithmic (backdoor branching) and a computational proof of concept (pp. 3, 11). Limits stated: static priorities and no access to branching decisions (p. 10); full integration "would require a complete access to source codes" (p. 11); the strong-branching variant does not pay, "at least in our current implementation" (p. 11); CPX default solves more (p. 8). Not stated: the gain over Cplex rests on arithmetic means and three instances, with nearly equal geometric means (711.33 against 712.78, p. 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build a test-bed by a stated rule: solved by the default baseline within the limit, not within 1,000 nodes or 100 s, not at the root under the experimental settings, and solved by at least one arm (pp. 7-8).
- When a new branching idea must live beside a commercial solver, pass it only as priorities, and switch off for every arm the features that interfere with priorities (variable aggregation) (p. 8).
- Weight a covering or selection model by pseudocosts collected from interrupted runs, and treat never-branched variables as continuous, to avoid choosing redundant variables (pp. 6-7).
- Show that 0-1 branching creates no new vertices by moving the branching into the objective with a large constant; obstacles can then be collected at the root polyhedron (p. 4).
