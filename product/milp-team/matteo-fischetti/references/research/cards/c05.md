### S018 · Fast approaches to improve the robustness of a railway timetable (Transportation Science 2009, DOI 10.1287/trsc.1090.0264)
**Read level**: full. All 29 file pages: text, formulas, Tables 1-4, figure captions and references. Figures 1-9 survive only as axis ticks and legends, so their content is taken from the captions and the prose (pp. 9, 15-16, 21-26). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Salvagnin, Zanette match (p. 1). The journal version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin, Arrigo Zanette (both DMPA Padova, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: written inside the EU FP6 project ARRIVAL and the PRIN 2006 project on robust network optimization (p. 27); "An early version of the present work was presented at ATMOS 2007" (p. 2; ref. [8], p. 28). It continues the 1989-2006 railway strand: the nominal timetables come from the Caprara-Fischetti-Toth Lagrangian heuristic [3], supplied by Toth, Caprara and Cacchiani (pp. 13-14, 27), and it applies Light Robustness from the Fischetti-Monaci ARRIVAL report [7] (pp. 12-13, 28). Among the works it cites is recoverable robustness by Liebchen, Lübbecke, Möhring and Stiller [13] (pp. 3, 28), a link to the MILP team's Lübbecke. The c04 card on S010 describes a similar fat/slim/LR timetabling experiment; I did not re-read S010, so the overlap is not checked here.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry from practice: maximizing efficiency "is not enough", optimal timetables are not robust enough and "easy-to-compute robust solutions tend to be too conservative" (p. 1); practitioners add buffer times by simple rules (p. 2). The task is narrowed to improving the robustness of a given solution of the aperiodic TTP of Caprara et al. [3] (p. 2), placed in a three-stage framework: nominal solution, "robustness training" (a term borrowed from AI), and "robustness validation" by a separate tool, "thus allowing for a fair comparison of different training methods" (p. 2). Only stage 2 is the research object; nominal solutions are inputs and validation is a simple LP model (p. 2).
  - Transferable: cut the problem into stages and hold the neighbouring stages fixed (given input, common validator), so that methods for the middle stage become comparable (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace the standard scenario-based stochastic program ("fat": one copy of the timetable per scenario, (18)-(22), p. 11) by cheaper surrogates judged against it: a "slim" recourse model with one timetable and slacks s^ω_ij for the unabsorbed extra time, from which every row with zero extra time is dropped, "a situation that occurs very frequently in practice" (p. 12); its weights matter, and slim2 gives an event weight k + 1 when k events follow it, because early disturbances propagate (p. 14); and Light Robustness, a scenario-free LP with protection Δ_ij on each trip row, t_i − t_j + γ_ij ≥ d_ij + Δ_ij (p. 13), with Δ = −µ ln(1/2), the buffer that absorbs an exponential delay with probability 1/2 (p. 14). In all three the nominal objective moves into a constraint, Σ_h ρ_h ≥ (1 − α)z* in (18) and (24), and c^T x ≤ (1 + α)z* in the generic LR model (31), and robustness becomes the objective (pp. 11-13). Unchanged: the nominal solution's trains and event precedences, frozen to obtain LPs (p. 11).
  - Transferable: when the full scenario model is too slow, keep it as the reference on a small sample and look for a surrogate whose weights encode the propagation mechanism (pp. 12, 14).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The nominal TTP is an event-based MIP in the PESP spirit, (1)-(6): continuous event times, binary precedence variables x^{h,k}_{i,j} with a big-M for the headway rows (2)-(3), no overtaking as x^{h,k}_{i,j} = x^{h,k}_{i+1,j+1} (pp. 5-6). Integrality is not imposed on the times, so "after fixing the event precedence variables x, the model becomes a plain linear model"; fractional times are rounded before publication (arrivals to nearest, departures down) (p. 6). Two simplifying hypotheses, all trains scheduled and all precedences fixed from the nominal solution: "These strong assumptions were made to obtain tractable (LP) models" (p. 11). Uncertainty: a per-train cumulative delay spread over line sections and time intervals by (14) (p. 8); in the tests exponential extra times with mean µ = 5%, section probabilities proportional to segment length plus Gaussian noise (σ = 0.01) and interval probabilities set to 1, assumed "In lack of more detailed data" from the operator (p. 14). Data: four unidirectional single-line Trenitalia corridors, BZVR (27 stations, 127 trains), BrBO (48, 68), MUVR (48, 48), PDBO (17, 33) (p. 13). Validation assumptions: no cancellations, precedences fixed, unlimited stretch (no upper bounds), non-anticipativity t^ω_i ≥ t_i (pp. 10-11). SAA convergence and the bound estimators are taken from the literature, [25] and [17] (pp. 7-8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: (a) External validation LP (15)-(17), one scenario at a time: minimize the weighted cumulative delay subject to the trip and headway rows shifted by the scenario's extra times and to t^ω ≥ t; repeated over many scenarios, it returns the average cumulative delay (pp. 10-11). (b) Fat SP with non-anticipativity linking (p. 11), the "perfect model" kept "for benchmark purposes" (p. 12). (c) Slim SP (23)-(28) (p. 12). (d) LR (29)-(33) and its TTP rows (p. 13). (e) Exterior sampling (SAA) chosen over interior sampling for implementation, convergence, inference and parallelism (p. 7), with upper and lower bound estimators (9)-(13) that give confidence intervals (p. 8). (f) Latin Hypercube sampling against sampling variance: split [0, 1] into N subintervals, draw one uniform sample in each, invert the CDF, permute (p. 9); σ falls from 0.24352 to 0.19907 on an exponential example (Fig. 1, p. 9), and "an even larger variance reduction (one order of magnitude or more)" is reported for the tests without numbers (p. 9). (g) Two experiments that price the frozen precedences: robustness from the 10 best incumbents of a 5-minute MIP run against a 1-hour reference z_ref with gap below 4% (pp. 18-19), and the MIP version of LR with free precedences, given z_ref as first incumbent and solved to a gap below 1% within an hour, "only viable for LR, since the other models are too large" (p. 20). C++, CPLEX 10.1, AMD Athlon64 X2 4200+ with 4 GB (p. 14). Proof devices: n/a (no theorem of the authors' own; SAA facts cited).
  - Transferable: store every incumbent of one time-limited MIP run and use them as a graded family of inputs of increasing quality (p. 19).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Arms: fat (50 scenarios), slim1 (equal weights, 400 scenarios), slim2 (400), LR with the slim2 objective, for α = 1%, 5%, 10%, 20% (p. 14); Table 2 also has α = 0% and 40% blocks that this list omits (pp. 14, 17). Validation on 500 scenarios with equal event weights, plotted with confidence intervals (pp. 14-15, Figs. 2-3). Metrics: cumulative delay, WAD and time (Table 2, p. 17). Results stated: slim2 "always yields a very tight approximation of fat", slim1 "is often poorer", LR only slightly worse than slim2 "mainly in the most-realistic cases where the tradeoff parameter α is small", and LR more than two orders of magnitude faster than fat (p. 15). From the Table 2 totals (my computation, p. 17): slim2 is within 0.5-5.3% of fat's total delay over α = 0-40%, LR within 1.4-27.1% (the gap grows with α), slim1 within 1.9-34.8%; total times about 10,000-11,500 s (fat), 1,300 s (slim2), 3.2-3.6 s (LR). A λ-family of weights gains "always less than 5%", with many negative entries (p. 18; Table 3, p. 19). Worse nominal inputs lose robustness (BrBO, α = 10%: a 4.5% worse start loses 13.9%), and freeing the precedences in LR-MIP gains at most 4.3% over linear LR; PDBO and BZVR found no better solution and are not reported (pp. 19-21, Table 4). A practitioners' proportional buffer rule behaves like slim1 (p. 20; Fig. 5, p. 21). SAA bounds show "very narrow estimation gaps", fat a little worse with fewer scenarios (p. 23; Fig. 9, p. 26). Losses are printed (slim1; LR at large α; negative λ entries). Lapses: four corridors with one nominal solution each in the main test (pp. 13-14); no repetition of the training sample is reported, and the text does not say whether the 500 validation scenarios are drawn independently of the training ones (my reading of pp. 11, 14-15).
  - Transferable: report a surrogate's distance to the full model over the whole trade-off range, since a fast surrogate can be good at one end only (LR, Table 2, p. 17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Periodic timetabling (PESP, Serafini-Ukovich [23]; cycle-based formulations [20, 14, 21]), its stochastic variant by Kroon et al. [12] and recoverable robustness [13] are reviewed (pp. 2-3); the paper builds on the aperiodic model of Caprara et al. [3] (p. 3) and takes buffer insights from Kroon et al.: the λ variant is "motivated by observations in [12]" and the early-allocation finding "confirms the findings reported in [12]" (pp. 18, 22). Prior work is restated, not criticized; the authors' own LR [7] enters as one of four methods (pp. 12-14).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Conclusion first: "two of the proposed techniques are very fast" (abstract, p. 1), repeated in the outline (p. 2). Hedges are specific: "the gain is quite small (always less than 5%)" (p. 18); "this may be no longer the case for more complex network topologies" (p. 20). Names do work: "robustness training" (p. 2), and the fat model is named for its cost, "hence we called it 'fat'" (p. 12). Two small inconsistencies: the abstract counts four methods, Section 6 "three different approaches" (pp. 1, 11); a sentence on p. 14 refers to λ, which is defined only on p. 18 (my reading: left over from an earlier draft).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A testing framework with training separated from validation, and evidence that slim2 and LR approximate the full SP at a fraction of the time, LR qualifying "as the method of choice for addressing large-scale real cases" (pp. 15, 27). Limits named: the fat model is "very hard (if not impossible)" on practical instances (p. 27); LR degrades when the efficiency constraint is loose, because its fixed buffers become too small and the LP spreads the excess "in a somehow unpredictable way" (p. 22); LR-MIP ignores unscheduled trains (p. 20); single corridors only (pp. 20, 27). Future work: the precedence analysis for slim2, complex networks, and measuring "the actual price required to recover a delayed timetable" with real delay-management strategies instead of the simplified validator (p. 27).

**Method links**: Method 1 evidence ✅: the fat full-scenario model is kept only as the benchmark while thin surrogates are solved: one timetable copy plus recourse slacks with the zero-extra-time rows dropped (pp. 11-12), and LR with no scenarios at all (p. 13); event-time integrality is dropped and precedences frozen so that every model is an LP (pp. 6, 11); and the price of this thinning is then measured (pp. 18-20). The fat/slim words are in this manuscript, dated December 2007 and revised October 2008 (pp. 1, 12); which version introduced them cannot be told from the text. Method 4 evidence ✅: every method is judged by the same external LP validator, independent of all training models (pp. 9-11), with validation separated from training "for a fair comparison" (p. 2); a practitioner's rule (proportional buffers) is a baseline (p. 20); the fat model's handicap is stated, 50 against 400 scenarios (pp. 14-15); lapse: four corridors, one nominal solution each in the main test (pp. 13-14). Method 6 evidence ✅: the general-purpose MIP solver is the measuring instrument for the frozen-precedence hypothesis (a 5-minute run's incumbents, a 1-hour reference, LR-MIP to a gap below 1%; pp. 18-20). Method 3 variant ⚠: the variance handled is the sampling noise of the scenario generator; it is reduced by Latin Hypercube sampling (p. 9) and its remainder shown as confidence intervals and SAA bounds (pp. 8, 15, 23); no seed or placebo arm for solver behaviour. Heuristic 2 evidence ✅: the nominal objective moves into a constraint and robustness into the objective (pp. 11-13). Research Taste mark 3 evidence ✅: losses printed and small gains called small (pp. 14, 18, 20). New-pattern candidates: *an independent validation simulator as the common yardstick* (the label c04 uses for S010 and S015; here pp. 2, 9-11); *price the simplifying assumption*: the precedence freezing that makes the models LPs is relaxed on the one model where a MIP is still tractable, and the gain is reported (pp. 11, 18-20).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Build an external validator (an LP simulation over many scenarios) that no compared method uses for training, and judge every method by it (pp. 2, 9-11).
- Keep the full ("fat") model as the quality reference on a reduced sample, and report each surrogate's quality and time against it across the trade-off parameter (pp. 12, 14-15, 17).
- Price your own simplification: after freezing a decision to get a tractable model, solve the unfrozen version where it is still tractable and publish the difference (pp. 11, 18-20).
- Include the practitioners' rule of thumb (proportional buffers) as a baseline and show which method it resembles (p. 20; Fig. 5, p. 21).
- Explain differences between methods by a solution-structure statistic (here WAD, the weighted average position of the buffers) before claiming a mechanism (p. 22).
- Use Latin Hypercube sampling for scenario generation and report SAA lower and upper bounds with confidence intervals (pp. 8-9, 23).
**Quotes**:
- "However, we decided to implement an external simulation-based validation module that is independent from the optimization model itself" (p. 9)
- "These strong assumptions were made to obtain tractable (LP) models." (p. 11)

### S041 · Feasibility pump 2.0 (Mathematical Programming Computation 2009, DOI 10.1007/s12532-009-0007-3)
**Read level**: full. All 24 file pages: text, Figures 2-4 (pseudocode), Tables 1-5 and references. Figures 1 and 5 survive only as axis ticks; they are read through their captions and the prose (pp. 2, 14-15). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Salvagnin match (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin (DMPA Padova, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the third step of the pump line after Fischetti-Glover-Lodi 2005 [11] and Bertacco-Fischetti-Lodi 2007 [8] (p. 1), in the skill's 2002-2009 period of general-purpose heuristics; supported by a Padova project "Integrating Integer Programming and Constraint Programming" and by ARRIVAL (p. 21). The 2026 pump study (card c13, S164) returns to this line.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A reinterpretation opens the problem: the pump "can be interpreted as a clever way of rounding a sequence of fractional solutions of the LP relaxation", so the rounding, "fast and simple, but somehow blind", is the piece to replace (p. 2; abstract, p. 1). The failure is shown on trivial instances: in a binary knapsack the critical item above 0.5 is rounded up into infeasibility, and set covering may need several pumping cycles though feasibility is trivial (p. 9); Fig. 1 contrasts a pump that converges without restarts with one that oscillates through restarts and perturbations (p. 2).
  - Transferable: before redesigning a heuristic, re-describe it as a sequence of one elementary operation and look for the operation that ignores information the rest of the loop has (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: "rounding a variable means temporarily fixing it to a given value", so each rounding is propagated before the next variable is rounded, "very similar to what is done in modern MIP solvers during diving phases, but without the overhead of solving the linear relaxations" (p. 9; Fig. 4, p. 10). Kept: LinearProj, the perturbation and the three stages of [8] (pp. 4-5, 13); in §6.1 the new rounding is placed in both the original pump (α = 0) and Achterberg and Berthold's objective pump (α = 1) (p. 16). Properties: on one iteration the new rounding "strictly dominates" simple rounding (a feasible simple rounding cannot be ruled out by propagation), but there is no dominance in the feasibility degree of the rounded point nor at the pump level, and the result depends on the variable order (p. 10). Failed propagators are ignored and propagation runs to the end, since every integer variable needs a value (pp. 10-11).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{c^T x : Ax ≤ b, x_j integer ∀j ∈ I} with finite bounds on the integer variables; "integer" ignores the continuous components, and the distance Δ is on integer components, linearized for general integers with auxiliary variables d_j (pp. 3-4). Propagation terminates on finite domains (exponential in domain size, polynomial for pure binaries with polynomial propagators) but may not converge on continuous variables (Hooker's two-variable example) (pp. 8-9), hence a cap on the tightenings per variable, e.g. 10, within one rounding (p. 13). Testbed: "78 instances from MIPLIB 2003 [3] and [9, 28], which are the same instances used in [8]", with stp3d left out (first LP too demanding) and momentum* left out (numerical problems) (p. 13); Tables 2-3 list 78 binary and general-integer instances (pp. 18-19).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: (a) Bound strengthening of one row from its minimum activity L_min, new bounds (1)-(2), floors and ceilings for integers, and the test max_j |a_j(u_j − l_j)| ≤ b − L_min that skips useless propagator calls (pp. 6-7). (b) A propagation engine after Schulte and Stuckey: propagators as decreasing, monotonic, correct and checking functions, a queue of non-fixpoint propagators (Figure 3, pp. 7-8). (c) Specialized propagators for knapsack, cardinality and binary-logic rows with an automatic classifier, "a speedup of up to one order of magnitude in the propagation phase" (p. 12); advisors after Lagerkvist and Schulte, as in Gecode, for incremental propagation (pp. 12-13). (d) Restarts for general integers ported from the earlier code: "A careful analysis of the original source code [7]" documents a per-variable perturbation by domain size and a count RP of perturbed variables that shrinks by 0.85 per restart-free iteration, grows linearly by 40 otherwise and is capped at 10% of the general-integer variables (p. 11); simply adapting the binary restart "results in a seriously worsening" (p. 11). (e) Order: increasing fractionality, binaries first ("smallest domain first"), with 10% of the permutation randomly swapped after the 10th iteration against cycling (p. 13). (f) C++; CPLEX 11.2 for the LPs and preprocessing; Intel Core2 Q6600 2.40 GHz, 4 GB (p. 13). Proof devices: the dominance remark with its two non-dominance counter-remarks (p. 10); the termination and complexity remark with Hooker's non-convergent example (pp. 8-9).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Two scenarios: alone (standard parameters of [8], Stage 3 capped at the time of Stages 1-2; rococo* reoptimized with CPLEX defaults instead of the primal simplex) and embed (iteration limit 20, Stage 3 skipped, as in SCIP) (pp. 13-14). Difficulty ladder: each instance also with c^T x ≤ UB := z*(1 + β), z* the best known value, β ∈ {0.1, 1.0, +∞} (p. 14). Metrics: success, iterations, time, share of runs reaching Stage 3 (p. 14). Ten seeds per instance and variant, arithmetic means per instance, "an algorithm is considered successful if the number of solutions found is greater than 6 (out of 10)" (p. 14); Fig. 5 plots per-seed iterations of std against prop on air04: 4 to 49 against 3 to 25, prop better in 8 of 10 runs (pp. 14-15). Table 1: geometric means, relative improvements with 95% confidence intervals, boldface for significance (pp. 15, 17). Success in embed rises by 39%, 72% and 23% for no cutoff, UB = 100% and UB = 10% (the last not significant) and in alone by 3%, 5% and 18%, the alone gains masked by the large iteration limit and Stage 3 (pp. 15-16). Time is comparable except at UB = 10%, where prop is about 30% (alone) and 20% (embed) slower (p. 16; Table 1, p. 17). Quality without cutoff (§6.1): average gap 77.2% → 57.6% (α = 0) and 52.4% → 35.5% (α = 1); prop better on 49 of 78 instances, worse on 20 (pp. 16-17; Tables 2-3, pp. 18-19). Orders (§6.2): frac, rev, lr and rnd against std and prop; "a standard Cochran Q-test followed by post hoc McNemar comparisons" finds no difference in success except for lr in embed; quality differs, lr, rnd and rev being "about the same as std" (p. 20; Tables 4-5, p. 21). Losses printed: the UB = 10% slow-down, the 20 worse instances.
  - Transferable: grade each instance into harder variants with an objective cutoff ladder z*(1 + β) instead of hunting for harder instances (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: The pump "is currently implemented in several optimization solvers, both commercial and open-source" (p. 1; a self-report, no solver named). The new rounding is compared inside both earlier schemes [8] and [2] (p. 3), and "the state-of-the-art FP scheme for producing good-quality solutions is the objective FP of Achterberg and Berthold [2]" (p. 16). FGL's objective cutoff appears only in the review (§2.2), as a UB updated with each incumbent, a convex combination of the LP and incumbent values (p. 5); the tests use the static z*(1 + β) bound instead, as "a quick method for generating difficult instances" (pp. 14, 16). The 2026 study describes FP 2.0 as having mentioned the cutoff in passing (card c13, S164 p. 2), which matches p. 5. Borrowings from CP are credited: Schulte and Stuckey, Hooker, Lagerkvist and Schulte, Gecode (pp. 6-8, 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Version-number title; a reinterpretation (p. 2) and two tiny examples (p. 9) carry the motivation; claims come with confidence intervals and tests (pp. 15-17, 20). The abstract says the approach works "without slowing-down the method" (p. 1), which holds except at UB = 10%, where the slow-down is significant in both scenarios (Table 1, p. 17) and called "acceptable" in the text (p. 16). One-instance figures come before the tables (Fig. 1, p. 2; Fig. 5, p. 15). Table 5 repeats "gap%(α = 0)" in both column heads, the second being α = 1 by its caption (p. 21). Four-sentence conclusions without a lesson line (pp. 20-21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic (propagation rounding) and implementation contribution (specialized propagators, advisors, restart policy), measured on 78 instances (pp. 9-13, 20-21). Limits conceded: no dominance beyond a single iteration (p. 10); slower under tight cutoffs (p. 16); the order matters for quality (p. 20). Not addressed: whether embed predicts behaviour inside a real branch-and-cut, since embed is defined by parameter changes and no branch-and-cut run is reported (my reading of p. 14). No future work is stated (pp. 20-21).

**Method links**: Method 3 evidence ✅: seeds are in the design from the start, since "it is not completely fair to compare different variants on a single run" (p. 14): 10 seeds, a more-than-6-of-10 success rule and a per-seed scatter on one instance (pp. 14-15). The rnd order is a control arm for the ordering rule: success hardly depends on the order, but the quality gain needs the fractionality order (lr, rnd and rev as poor as std, p. 20); this is a control for attribution, not a placebo of the "change that cannot help" kind. Method 4 evidence ✅: the testbed of [8] with each exclusion and its reason (p. 13); the new rounding inside both pump versions (p. 16); "all FP parameters are kept unchanged, but the variable order" (p. 20); losses printed (pp. 16-17). Method 4 contradiction ✗: the skill's say-do line "No significance test before 2026" (and the Roundtable Card blind spot "no statistical tests before 2026") does not hold for this 2009 paper: Table 1 gives 95% confidence intervals with significant entries marked (pp. 15, 17), and §6.2 runs a Cochran Q-test with post hoc McNemar comparisons (p. 20). Method 5 variant ⚠: step 2 (read the implementation, not papers about it) is practised in 2009, when the restart rule is reconstructed from "the original source code [7]" (p. 11), but to port a mechanism, not to re-test a claim; and the limitation "FP 2.0 itself used the static cutoff" holds with a nuance: the static bound is an evaluation device that grades difficulty, while FGL's dynamic cutoff is described only in the review (pp. 5, 14). Method 6 variant ⚠: Stage 3 hands the last resort to a MIP solver as a black box (p. 5), capped in time (pp. 13-14), but the contribution itself is a propagation engine written from scratch with specialized propagators and advisors (pp. 11-13), not a one-callback test around the stock solver. Heuristic 3 evidence ✅ (constraint propagation borrowed from CP, pp. 2, 9, 20); Heuristic 9 evidence ✅ (the objective pump of Achterberg and Berthold as the state of the art and second base, p. 16); Heuristic 2 evidence ✅ (one piece moved, the rounding; the rest of the pump kept, pp. 9, 20). New-pattern candidates: *difficulty ladder by objective cutoff* (pp. 14, 16); *random control arm for attribution* (p. 20; compare the label "matched-budget attribution with a random control" in another batch); *test standalone and at the embedded budget* (pp. 13-14).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Run every variant with 10 seeds, call an instance solved only if more than 6 of the 10 runs succeed, and plot per-seed results of the two variants against each other on one instance (pp. 14-15).
- Report relative improvements with 95% confidence intervals, and compare more than two variants on success with a Cochran Q-test and post hoc McNemar comparisons (pp. 15, 17, 20).
- When a gain may depend on an ordering rule, add arbitrary orders (left-to-right, reverse, random) as control arms (p. 20).
- Test a primal heuristic both standalone and with the budget it would have inside branch-and-cut (iteration limit 20, last-resort stage off) (pp. 13-14).
- Add an objective cutoff z*(1 + β), β ∈ {0.1, 1, ∞}, to produce harder variants of each test instance (p. 14).
- Bound propagation cost inside a rounding pass by capping the tightenings per variable (e.g. 10) (p. 13); skip a row when max_j |a_j(u_j − l_j)| ≤ b − L_min (p. 7).
- Before porting a mechanism from an earlier code, read that code and write down the rule it actually implements (p. 11).
**Quotes**:
- "As already discussed, random perturbations are a fundamental ingredient of the FP scheme, so it is not completely fair to compare different variants on a single run." (p. 14)
- "To assess the statistical significance of the performance difference for the two rounding schemes, we also report the 95% confidence intervals for the entries of columns impr% (columns L and U)." (p. 15)

### D009 · On the enumerative nature of Gomory's dual cutting plane method (Mathematical Programming 2010, DOI 10.1007/s10107-010-0392-4)
**Read level**: full. All 25 file pages: text, Examples 1-4, Tables 1-4 and references. Figures 2-11 survive only as axis ticks, tree labels and node numbers; they are read through their captions and the prose (pp. 8-18). First-page check: title and authors Balas, Fischetti, Zanette match (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Egon Balas (Carnegie Mellon, listed first), Arrigo Zanette (Padova) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: "the second in a sequence" (abstract, p. 1) after Zanette, Fischetti and Balas, "Lexicography and degeneracy: can a pure cutting plane algorithm work?" [17] (pp. 3, 25), in the skill's 2008-2012 "Gomory's cuts revisited" strand. Per the work-list note it is on DBLP and the homepage list, not on the Scholar profile.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: An anomaly in the field's history: cutting planes became practical only inside branch and bound, and "pure cutting plane methods alone have poor convergence properties and are typically not used in practice" (abstract, p. 1); the authors study them in pure form to understand the failure (p. 1). The specific entry is the success of the lexicographic version in [17] (exact solution of stein15, stein27 and bm23, where the textbook method closes "a tiny fraction" of the gap) and the question why (p. 3). Gomory "never advocated the practical use of this method", and "we have no information on anybody ever having tried extensively this method before" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: when a method works in an untried variant, the follow-up paper asks why it works and turns the answer into new variants (pp. 3, 14-21).
- **D2 Core idea**: The lexicographic Gomory method is an implicit enumeration: the lex order defines a tree (level 0 the integer values of x0 = c^T x, then x1, x2, ...), visited depth-first, and each GFC either branches (moves the first fractional variable to its next integer value) or backtracks (p. 11, after Nourie and Venta [15]). Its performance therefore "can only be improved if one gets rid of the rigidity of the enumerative shell" (p. 24), and the cures come from branch and bound: (i) treat the objective implicitly, replacing it by the cutoff 2c^T x ≤ 2U − 1 and using lex-Gomory as a feasibility subroutine (L-CP, p. 15), or by a binary search on the cutoff value with the cutoff's slack last in the lex order (bin-GFC, pp. 18-19); (ii) redefine the lex order at run time, taking as next "branching" variable a fractional one (fractional part closest to 0.5) of the LP with the true objective and then lex-minimizing it (L-CP.dyn, lex-GFC.dyn, pp. 19-21), since "Gomory-like cutting plane methods do need a dynamic rearrangement of the lexicographic sequence, just as branch-and-bound algorithms need to avoid branching on integer-valued variables" (p. 19). Kept: GFCs read from the lex-optimal tableau with the "right" (≥, rounded-up) form (pp. 6-7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pure ILP min c^T x, Ax = b, x ≥ 0 integer, integer data, P bounded (p. 2); L-CP and L-B&B are stated for 0-1 ILPs (pp. 15-16); the dynamic GFCs stay globally valid for general integers as well (p. 21). GFC against GMI: GMI dominates polyhedrally, but loses integral coefficients and adds continuous slacks that weaken later cuts, so "it is unclear whether GFCs or GMI cuts are better suited" for a pure method (pp. 2-3). Dual degeneracy is "an intrinsic property" of these LPs, because the cuts become nearly parallel to the objective (pp. 3-4). Testbed (Table 1, p. 22): 25 pure ILPs from MIPLIB 3 and 2003 plus two single knapsacks and two 2-matching instances, the testbed of [17]; difficult instances "likely not to be solvable by any pure cutting plane method" are left out (p. 21).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: (a) Degeneracy argument: if every LP were dual nondegenerate, a naive method adding c^T x ≥ ⌈c^T x*⌉, or any cut that moves x*, would reach the ILP optimum in a pseudo-polynomial number of steps (p. 4), so degeneracy is the issue to manage. (b) The lex dual simplex on a black-box LP solver without a perturbed objective ("numerically unacceptable"): a sequence of reoptimizations that fixes the nonbasic variables with nonzero reduced cost and minimizes the next variable, akin to slack fixing in preemptive goal programming (pp. 4-5). (c) Lex-optimality from the tableau sign pattern (Example 1, Fig. 1, pp. 5-6) and the convergence step: GFCs rounded upward give "a significant lexicographic step" (x_h reaches ⌈x*_h⌉ or a higher-ranked variable increases), after Gomory [13] (pp. 6-7); with the wrong (≤) rounding the lex steps are too small to carry the argument (Fig. 7, p. 13), and without lex reoptimization the sequence is not even lex-increasing, with a risk of loops (Fig. 8, pp. 13-14). (d) Example 3, the 0-1 version of the Cook-Kannan-Schrijver pathological case with y = z/M: about M GFCs for lex-Gomory, "just a pair of GFCs" once the objective variable is removed (pp. 14-15). (e) L-B&B on the same lex tree with a correctness argument (p. 16); "It is not difficult to show that L-CP always visits a smaller number of nodes than L-B&B", stated without proof (p. 16); Example 4, 13 LP nodes against 6 (pp. 17-18). (f) Numerical safety by construction: the cutoff written 2c^T x ≤ 2U − 1 "so as to avoid to have it tight when an integer solution is found" (footnote, p. 15); bin-GFC leaves out the valid c^T x ≥ L', which slowed the computation "due to numerical issues" (p. 19). (g) Shortcuts: the lex reoptimizations stop at the first variable that turns fractional, and all GFCs of the final tableau are added as a round (p. 21). C++, CPLEX 11 with defaults as LP black box, Intel Core 2 Q6600 2.40 GHz, 2 hours CPU and 2 GB per instance (p. 21).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Mechanism plots on one small instance come first, all on stein15: bound and average cut coefficient, textbook against lex (Fig. 2, pp. 7-8); trajectories of the separated points by multidimensional scaling and basis-determinant growth (Fig. 3, "taken from [17]", pp. 8-9); cut depth falling to one millionth of its start in the textbook method against stable under lex, and the distance between consecutive separated points (Fig. 4, pp. 9-10); right against wrong rounding (Fig. 5, pp. 10-11). Tables: lex-GFC, L-CP and bin-GFC by rounds, time and LB/UB (Table 2, p. 23); bin-GFC "qualifies as the fastest method" (first on 6 of the 14 instances solved, 9-5 against lex-GFC), and p0201 is solved for the first time by a pure cutting-plane method (p. 22). Tree sizes, L-CP against L-B&B, the latter given 4 hours instead of 2 (Table 3, pp. 22-23): reductions of "several orders of magnitude in some cases" (p. 22). Time is in the same table but not discussed: L-B&B is faster on 7 of the 12 instances, and L-B&B.dyn on 9 of 12 (my count from Table 3, p. 23). Erratic behaviour of the dynamic versions is reported and put down to run-time branching choices, "a known feature also afflicting standard branch-and-bound or branch-and-cut methods" (p. 22). Table 4: lex-GFC against lex-GFC.dyn with termination codes O, T, E and C; dyn best on 18 of 25, losses named (l152lav and pipex lost, mitre gained) (pp. 23-24). By my count from Table 4 one of the 18 (p2756) is a tie on closed gap decided on time by a run that stopped with a numerical error (code E), and one dyn time (p0548, 110781 under code T) exceeds the 2-hour limit without comment (pp. 21, 24). One run per instance, no seeds.
- **D6 Positioning**: A classical method restored with a modern implementation, framed against the polyhedral reading: the link between GFCs and CG cuts was not recognized at once, "not surprising since ... the specific GFCs actually used in Gomory's method are based on enumerative (rather than purely polyhedral) considerations" (p. 2). The method is set apart from approaches in which separation is decoupled from LP reoptimization (Fischetti-Lodi on CG cuts [10], Balas-Saxena [3], Dash-Günlük-Lodi [9]) (p. 3). Credits: the enumerative interpretation to Nourie and Venta [15] (p. 11), the convergence proof to Gomory [13] (pp. 5-7), slack fixing to goal programming [2, 16] and Balinski-Tucker [4] (p. 4), the pathological case to Cook, Kannan and Schrijver [8] (p. 14).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Built on worked examples (Examples 1-4, Figs. 1 and 6-11) that precede the tables, announced as "with the help of a number of illustrative examples" (p. 3). A metaphor in the conclusion: "GFCs and the lexicographic method are the two blades of a pair of scissors" (p. 24). Claims are hedged: "Preliminary computational results seem to indicate that the new methods have some potential" (p. 25). The node-dominance claim rests on "It is not difficult to show" (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: An interpretation (pp. 11-14) turned into algorithm variants (L-CP, bin-GFC, dynamic order; pp. 14-21) with preliminary tests (pp. 21-24). Limits: results called preliminary (pp. 1, 25); dyn loses on some instances (pp. 22, 24); numerical errors remain (code E, p. 24); the testbed excludes instances out of reach of pure cutting planes (p. 21). Future work: "put even more flexibility in pure cutting plane methods", expecting "a positive follow-up for the branch-and-cut side too" (p. 25).

**Method links**: Method 2 evidence ✅: the stall of textbook Gomory is blamed on the loop and the LP solver, not on the cut family: the textbook method "enters a feedback loop that is responsible for the exponential growth of the determinant", "a consequence of the efficiency of the underlying LP solver" that stops at the first optimal vertex (p. 8); the remedy keeps the GFCs and changes which vertex is separated, by lex reoptimization (pp. 4-5, 8-10); the diagnosis is step 1 on one small instance (Figs. 2-5 on stein15, pp. 7-11); and the cuts are judged by their side effects over "a long sequence of cuts" (p. 3). Step 6 evidence ✅: the one pure cutting-plane loop that works is shown to be an enumeration in disguise (pp. 11-14), and its improved versions get better by borrowing branching rules (pp. 19-21). Method 1 variant ⚠ (my reading): the removal move applied inside an algorithm: the objective is taken out of the lex order and becomes a cutoff, which turns about M GFCs into two on Example 3 (pp. 14-15). Method 4 variant ⚠: the rival L-B&B gets twice the time limit (p. 22) and losses appear in the tables and partly in the text (pp. 22, 24), but the time column of Table 3, where L-B&B mostly wins, is not discussed (my count, p. 23). Method 3 variant ⚠: run-to-run erraticism is named in 2009 as intrinsic to run-time branching (p. 22) but not measured (one run per instance). Research Taste mark 4 evidence ✅: Gomory's 1958 method, "typically not used in practice", redone with a black-box LP solver (pp. 1, 3). Heuristic 3 evidence ✅: the pattern is named (enumeration) and the cure borrowed from branch and bound (implicit objective, dynamic branching order, no branching on integer-valued variables) (pp. 14, 19). Heuristic 8 evidence ✅ (traces half): one-instance plots and worked examples precede all tables (pp. 5-18 against pp. 22-24). Inner Tension 3 evidence ✅: the design must "not interfere too much with the black-box LP solver used" (p. 4), yet the textbook failure is put down to that solver's efficiency (p. 8). New-pattern candidates: *study the component in its pure form* (abstract, p. 1; pp. 3, 25; also S106); *numerically safe by construction* (the label of another batch: no perturbed objective, the 2U − 1 cutoff, the omitted valid lower bound, GFC kept over GMI; pp. 2-4, 15, 19); *name the cost of wrapping the black box* (the label of another batch: the LP solver's efficiency as the cause of the textbook failure, p. 8); *a pathological instance as the design argument* (Example 3, pp. 14-15).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Instrument a stalling cut loop on one small instance with five curves against iterations: bound, average cut coefficient, basis determinant, cut depth and distance between consecutive separated points (Figs. 2-5, pp. 7-11).
- Project the trajectory of separated points to 2-D by multidimensional scaling to see whether the loop keeps cutting the same region (p. 8).
- Implement a lexicographic reoptimization on a black-box LP solver as a sequence of LPs, fixing the nonbasic variables with nonzero reduced cost before minimizing the next variable (pp. 4-5).
- Turn an optimization method into a feasibility subroutine by replacing the objective with an invalid cutoff 2c^T x ≤ 2U − 1 (tight at no integer point), and wrap it in a binary search on U (pp. 15, 18-19).
- To make provable statements about a cutting-plane method, build a purpose-made enumerative twin on the same tree and compare node counts (pp. 14-16; Table 3, p. 23).
- Print a termination code (optimal, time, numerical error, cut limit) for every run in the table (pp. 23-24).

### S024 · A note on the selection of Benders' cuts (Mathematical Programming 2010, DOI 10.1007/s10107-010-0365-7)
**Read level**: full. All 8 file pages of the author preprint, titled "On the selection of Benders' cuts" and "Revised 23 February 2010": text, Tables 1-4 and references. First-page check: authors Fischetti, Salvagnin, Zanette match; the title differs from the journal's only by "A note on" (p. 1). The journal version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin, Arrigo Zanette (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a 2010 Benders note within ARRIVAL and the PRIN 2006 project on robust network optimization (p. 7); the same year and first two authors as the in-out abstract S106, and seven years before the Benders redesign with Ljubić and Sinnl in SKILL.md's anatomy. The skill records critiques of this rule as unanswered (Method 2 limitations).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry from the general theory of cut selection: in disjunctive programming the CGLP's normalization decides which violated cut is returned, and Benders' dual slave (3) is "akin" to a CGLP (p. 2); the Benders literature (Benders [5], Magnanti and Wong [12], Wentges [15]) chose cuts otherwise (p. 2). The hard case is when both feasibility and optimality cuts exist: finding a most-violated optimality cut "is equivalent to finding an optimal vertex of a polyhedron with unbounded rays, which is a strongly NP-hard problem [10]" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Benders separation "can always be rephrased as a pure feasibility problem": a violated cut exists if and only if the extended slave (4), d^T y ≤ η*, Qy ≥ r − Tx*, y ≥ 0, is infeasible, i.e. the modified dual (5) is unbounded, and a ray (π, π0) gives the cut π^T(r − Tx) − π0 η ≤ 0 for both cut types at once (p. 2). The rows of a minimal infeasible subsystem (MIS) of (4) index the vertices of the alternative polyhedron (6) (Gleeson and Ryan [11]); swapping objective and normalization gives CGLP (7) with normalization Σ w_i π_i + w0 π0 = 1 (p. 3). Classical Benders is w0 = 1 with all other weights 0; all weights 1 heuristically seek a minimum-cardinality MIS; the proposal (8) weights only the rows i ∈ I(T) where T is nonzero, because rows with null T are "static" conditions: "For these rows, there is no reason to penalize the corresponding multiplier" (p. 3); w0 is "just set to 1" (p. 3). Changed: the separator's normalization. Kept: the loop and the master (p. 4).
  - Transferable: in a CGLP normalization, put zero weight on the multipliers of rows that do not depend on the master variables (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{c^T x + d^T y : Ax ≥ b, Tx + Qy ≥ r, x ∈ Z^n_+, y ∈ R^t_+} (1), with the master relaxation (2) assumed bounded (p. 1). Instances chosen as "known to be well suited for Benders' methods [9]" (p. 4): random multicommodity-flow network design on grid and random topologies, in a feas scenario (zero routing cost, feasibility cuts only) and an opt scenario (unit flow costs, both cut types), 8 classes with 5 instances each, about 1,150-4,300 rows, 5,000-11,600 variables and 34-56 integer variables (pp. 4-5; Table 1, p. 5); and 30 hard network-expansion instances from Atamtürk et al. [2], "the easiest instances have been removed from the testbed", feasibility cuts only (p. 6). "All instances and detailed tables of results are available, on request, from the authors" (p. 4). Correctness rests on the iff characterization (p. 2) and the MIS correspondence (p. 3); no theorem of the authors' own.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: C++, CPLEX 11.2 at default parameters as LP and MIP solver (p. 4). Master loop: every new incumbent of the master's branch-and-bound is separated on the fly, but its cut is added only at the next main iteration; the master continues until its incumbent violates a Benders cut and has not changed for K = 1,000 nodes, so no time is spent proving optimality of a point known to be infeasible and the master is not restarted at every incumbent; described as a "light" version of Rei et al.'s local-branching scheme [14] (p. 4). Arms: cpx (CPLEX branch-and-cut on the full model); std (CGLP (3), the "blind" π0 = 1); std2 (Benders' own truncation Σ π_i ≤ M with a dynamic big-M, equivalent to Σ π_i + π0 = 1 when feasibility cuts are generated); mis (normalization (8)) (p. 4). Same code otherwise, with three stated asymmetries: std and std2 can return two cuts per CGLP call (vertex and ray), mis at most one; "Both std and std2 (but not mis) implement the Magnanti-Wong [12] cut selection rule"; the McDaniel-Devine warm start [13] is off for all, since it degraded performance (p. 4). Proof devices: the feasibility rephrasing (4)-(5) (p. 2); the Gleeson-Ryan MIS correspondence and the swap of objective and normalization after Cornuéjols and Lemaréchal [8] (p. 3); hardness pointers [10] and [1] (pp. 2-3).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Intel Core2 Q6600 2.40 GHz, 4 GB, 10 hours per run (p. 4). Geometric means over the 5 instances of a class; time, share of time in the CGLP, main iterations, feasibility and optimality cuts; time-limit runs enter with their figures at abort, their number in parentheses (p. 5). cpx is not tabulated "because this method exceeded the 10-hour time limit in most runs" (pp. 5-6). Network design (Table 2, p. 6): std2 is "much worse" than std, about 3 times the iterations and cuts and about 25 times the time in feas (p. 5); mis beats std by a factor of about 2 (feas) and 3.5 (opt), with far fewer cuts and iterations (p. 5). Hard cases (Table 3, p. 7): mis hits the limit only on r 25 5 4 opt, where all methods do and mis closes more gap (p. 6). Network expansion (Table 4, p. 8): the order of std and std2 reverses, std2 "by far faster", and mis "very similar" to std2 (pp. 6-7). Summary: mis "almost always the best (possibly with ties)", std and std2 "much more erratic" across structures (p. 7). One run per instance, although std picks its unbounded ray at random (p. 5); the removed easy expansion instances are not listed (p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: Cut selection is "of fundamental importance for any cutting plane method" (p. 2), and the framework is borrowed from disjunctive programming (Balas, Ceria and Cornuéjols [4]; Cornuéjols [7]; Cornuéjols and Lemaréchal [8]; Cadoux [6]) (pp. 2-3). Earlier Benders enhancements become arms or are switched off (Benders [5], Magnanti-Wong [12], McDaniel-Devine [13], Rei et al. [14]) (pp. 2, 4). The poor showing of the "more elaborated" std2 is reported as "A surprising outcome", not as a criticism (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A two-sentence abstract (p. 1); the key move is a one-line normalization backed by "computational experience" ("this simple change leads to a substantial improvement of the generated cuts in many cases", p. 3); robustness is defined operationally at the end, as "almost always the best (possibly with ties)" (p. 7). Light hedges ("often", "in many cases", pp. 1, 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A cut-selection rule that treats feasibility and optimality cuts "in a sound unified framework" and is robust across two network classes (pp. 2, 7); "As expected, mis obtains its best speedup when both optimality and feasibility cuts are be separated" (p. 7). Limits: w0 untuned (p. 3); no explanation of why std2 is the better baseline on expansion beyond "equally effective for this class" (p. 7); two network-design families only (pp. 4-6). No future work is stated (p. 7).

**Method links**: Method 2 contradiction ✗ (dated 2010): this note is the cure Method 2 sets itself against: the separator's normalization changes, while the separated point does not (the master's integer incumbents, separated as they are found, with no core point or stabilization), and no loop is instrumented on a small instance (pp. 2-4). With S106 of the same year (in-out loop, same separator), it shows that in 2010 the component cure and the loop cure were pursued side by side, for different cut families; this supports Common Misreading 3's dating (the loop became the Benders story later) and means that Method 2's say-do span "(2008-2026)" is not exclusive. Method 4 evidence ✅ with a variant ⚠: one code in which the arms differ only in cut selection (p. 4), the stated asymmetries running in the rivals' favour (Magnanti-Wong and two cuts per call for std and std2 only, p. 4); the stock solver is run and its failure reported (pp. 5-6); lapses: the easiest expansion instances removed without a list (p. 6) and per-instance data only on request (p. 4). Method 6 evidence ✅ (step 1): the generic branch-and-cut on the full model is run first and fails within 10 hours on most instances (pp. 5-6); every subproblem uses CPLEX at defaults (p. 4). Method 3 variant ⚠: one run per instance although std's ray choice is random (p. 5); no spread measured. Heuristic 3 evidence ✅: the normalization machinery of disjunctive CGLPs is carried over to Benders (pp. 2-3). New-pattern candidate: *one separation problem for both cut types* (the feasibility rephrasing (4)-(5) and CGLP (7)-(8), pp. 2-3, 7).
**Transferable techniques**:
- Rephrase a separation problem as the infeasibility of an extended LP so that two cut types come out of one CGLP and one normalization (pp. 2-3).
- Choose the CGLP weights to favour small infeasible subsystems, and give zero weight to rows whose right-hand side does not depend on the master variables (p. 3).
- Keep all arms in one code that differs only in the component under test, and list every asymmetry that remains (extra rules given to rivals, cuts per call, a warm start switched off for all) (p. 4).
- In a Benders master solved by branch-and-bound, separate each new incumbent on the fly, add the cuts at the next main iteration, and stop the master once its incumbent is infeasible and unchanged for K = 1,000 nodes (p. 4).
- Let time-limit runs enter the means with their figures at abort, and print their number in parentheses (p. 5).
**Quotes**:
- "For these rows, there is no reason to penalize the corresponding multiplier" (p. 3)
- "The three Benders’ implementations above are completely general purpose, and only differ in the way the cuts are selected—all other features are identical." (p. 4)

### S106 · An in-out approach to disjunctive optimization (CPAIOR 2010, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-13520-0_17)
**Read level**: full. All 5 file pages of the extended abstract: abstract, §§1-4, Table 1 and references. First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Salvagnin match (p. 1).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin (DMPA Padova, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: 2010, the year and authors of the Benders cut-selection note S024; the in-out loop is credited to Ben-Ameur and Neto [1] (abstract, p. 1; p. 2); seven years before in-out stabilization became the key ingredient of the Benders redesign in SKILL.md's anatomy.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is the neglected half of a cutting-plane method: it has "two equally important components", the oracle and "the overall search framework that actually uses the generated cuts" (p. 1); decades of work went into cut families, separation and selection, "However, the search component was much less studied" in MIP, where one cuts a vertex and reoptimizes, the Kelley method, inefficient when the cuts are shallow (pp. 1-2). The framing comes from CP: "The importance of a sound search scheme is well known in the Constraint Programming (CP) community" (abstract, p. 1). The research programme is named: move the focus from the separation module to the search scheme (p. 2).
  - Transferable: list the components of your method and ask which one the literature has neglected; make that one the object of study (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Keep the separator, a CGLP for the elementary disjunctions x_j ≤ 0 ∨ x_j ≥ 1, and change the point it is asked to cut. In-out search keeps an internal point q ∈ P1 and an optimal vertex x* of the current LP, so that z1 lies in [c^T x*, c^T q]; it separates y = (x* + q)/2 (α = 1/2 in the general y = αx* + (1 − α)q), adds a returned cut and reoptimizes, or else sets q := y, halving the interval (p. 3). Their change for the endgame, when x* is already in P1 but q is still far: count the consecutive q updates k and separate x* itself when k > 3, terminating if that fails (p. 3). q starts at a heuristic integer solution (p. 3), here CPLEX's (p. 4). Interior-point schemes (ellipsoid, analytic center) are less sensitive to cut quality but must recompute a core point; in-out is a hybrid that needs only LP reoptimizations (p. 2).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP min{c^T x : Ax = b, l ≤ x ≤ u, x_j ∈ Z ∀j ∈ I}, LP polyhedron P, and an oracle that defines a closure P1 (pp. 2-3); in the tests, 0-1 MIPs and the Lift-and-Project closure of the elementary disjunctions (pp. 2-3). Testbed: 50 0-1 MIPs from MIPLIB 3.0 and 2003 and 15 ORLIB set-covering instances (p. 4), without an instance list. Correctness: q ∈ P1 and x* bound z1 from above and below "By construction" (p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: CGLP (1)-(4), of a size comparable with the original LP rather than doubled (p. 4), the most-violated cut γx ≥ γ0 read from its dual multipliers (p. 4). Both loops generate one cut per iteration, from the disjunction of the most fractional variable, within one hour, with CPLEX 11.2 as black-box LP solver and as the source of the first heuristic solution (p. 4). Proof devices: the uncertainty-interval argument (p. 3); n/a otherwise.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Intel Q6600 2.4 GHz (p. 4). Aggregates only (Table 1, p. 5): geometric means of time and iterations, the average gap closed with respect to the best known integer solution (cl.gap) and with respect to the best known upper bound on z1, the minimum of that solution and in-out's last upper bound (L&P cl.gap) (p. 4). MIPLIB: kelley 38.18 s, 1,501 cuts, 40.8% and 63.7%; in-out 28.42 s, 592 cuts, 41.2% and 64.1%. Set covering: kelley 2,281.60 s, 16,993 cuts, 35.2% and 71.8%; in-out 757.29 s, 1,575 cuts, 38.7% and 85.8% (p. 5). The gain is large on set covering and modest on MIPLIB: "The results clearly show the effectiveness of in-out search, in particular for set covering instances" (p. 4). No per-instance data, no seeds, and no instance where Kelley wins can be seen in the aggregates (pp. 4-5).
- **D6 Positioning**: The scheme is credited: "a general meta-scheme for cutting plane algorithms, called in-out search, that was recently proposed by Ben-Ameur and Neto [1]" (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Kelley [5]; interior and analytic-center methods [6-12] with Boyd and Vandenberghe's notes [13] (p. 2); Naoum-Sawaya and Elhedhli [4] as the "notable exception" on the search side, for Benders (p. 2); disjunctive programming (Balas [14]) and cut selection (Cornuéjols [2]; Cornuéjols and Lemaréchal [3]) (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A short programmatic text: the result is stated in the introduction, "though they use exactly the same separation module" (p. 2); the results section is titled "(sketch)" (p. 4); the abstract hedges ("may lead to a significant improvement", p. 1), and the work is "A first step in this direction" (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: A computational demonstration on the L&P closure plus one endgame fix (pp. 3-5).
