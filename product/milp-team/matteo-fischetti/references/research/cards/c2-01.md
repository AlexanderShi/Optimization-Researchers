### S012 · Algorithms for railway crew management (Mathematical Programming 1997, DOI 10.1007/bf02614314)
**Read level**: full. All 20 preprint pages ("Preprint submitted to Elsevier Science 4 June 1997", p. 1): §§1–4, Tables 1–2 (pp. 12, 18) and the references (pp. 18–20). Figures 1–2 (pp. 2–3) were read from their captions and the text, since their trip labels are garbled. The three weekly limits listed on p. 13 and the exponents of two complexity bounds on p. 17 are missing from the text layer and were read from PDF pages 13 and 17; the symbols of the relaxation's formulas (pp. 15–17) were checked on the PDF pages. This reading replaces the partial c01 card, whose text layer had no digits. The journal version (pp. 125–141) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Alberto Caprara, Paolo Toth, Daniele Vigo (DEIS, Bologna) and Pier Luigi Guida (Ferrovie dello Stato SpA); Fischetti's affiliation is DMI, University of Udine (p. 1; marker b, checked on the PDF page because the OCR garbles the markers) · **Position in trajectory**: the railway-codes strand of 1989–2006: an overview, co-signed by an FS author, of the two codes the Bologna group built for FS competitions, the CFT set-covering heuristic (FASTER) and the crew-rostering bound and heuristic of S017 (FARO), its refs [11] and [12] (pp. 9–11, 14–18; refs p. 19). No general-purpose MIP solver appears.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is industrial: deregulation and privatisation push European railways towards optimisation (p. 1), and FS runs about 8,000 trains a day with 25,000 drivers, its largest planning problems having about 2,000 trains split into 5,000 trips a day (p. 3). The problem is taken in the two phases of practice, crew scheduling (duties covering the trips) and then crew rostering (duties sequenced into rosters) (p. 3), and the decomposition is justified by three reasons: crews return to their home depot within L days, the two levels obey rules of different nature (minutes between trips, 18–22 hours of home rest between duties), and a global approach is unlikely to be implemented, given its difficulty and the planners' attachment to current practice (pp. 3–4). Railways are set against urban transit: there, scheduling fixes the crew count and rostering only balances workload, whereas in railways clever sequencing saves crews, so scheduling costs should anticipate rostering and the two phases call for feedback (p. 4). The crew-scheduling project itself was promoted by FS in 1994 (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: justify a decomposition by its operational reasons, including what the users will accept, and name the coupling it gives up (here the feedback from rostering to scheduling costs) (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Both subproblems are min-cost covers of the nodes of a directed graph by circuits, the items being trips or duties (pp. 4–5); in railways the graph is not acyclic, because times are taken modulo 24 hours, and dummy depot nodes close each duty into a circuit (p. 5). Two ILP families follow: an arc model with one binary per transition and exponentially many constraints forbidding inclusion-minimal infeasible arc sets, (1)–(5), with a crew-type-indexed variant (6)–(10) (pp. 6–7); and set partitioning over feasible circuits with crew-base side constraints, (11)–(14) (pp. 7–8). The choice rule, stated as experience: set partitioning when feasible circuits cover few nodes and their feasibility is cumbersome and depends on the whole sequence (railway crew scheduling); the arc model when the main rules concern two consecutive items and can be pushed into the arc set and costs (railway crew rostering) (p. 8; see Quotes). Scheduling: all feasible duties are generated beforehand (pairing generation); free deadheading relaxes partitioning to covering, so only inclusion-maximal duties among those of equal cost are generated (pp. 8–9). CFT: Lagrangian costs from near-optimal multipliers become greedy scores, and the greedy is run for several near-optimal multiplier vectors, because nearly equivalent multipliers give solutions of very different quality and the bound does not predict which (p. 10); three phases, subgradient with an aggressive step-size policy, heuristic, and fixing of high-probability duties to 1 on a shrinking instance, are iterated and followed by refining (p. 11); a core of lowest-Lagrangian-cost duties is re-priced inside the Lagrangian optimisation instead of being frozen (p. 11). Rostering: S017's relaxation RP on a multigraph of direct, simple-rest and double-rest arcs whose costs are minimum start-to-start times, with integer rest counts r and z (pp. 14–16); relaxing two counting constraints splits it into one assignment problem (AP) plus scalar choices, and two fixed multiplier pairs give the bound (pp. 16–17). A constructive heuristic builds one roster at a time, choosing each next duty by the bound increase of its arc and by the duty's characteristics, updating the bound parametrically and stopping at 10 weeks (p. 17). The paper does not spell out what CFT changes relative to the Lagrangian SCP heuristics it follows (Balas and Ho onwards, p. 10); the ingredients it names are the sequence of multiplier vectors in the heuristic phase, the fix-and-iterate loop and the priced core, the last called one of the main ingredients of the scheme's success (pp. 10–11); the subgradient-plus-greedy core is the common one.
  - Transferable technique: pick the formulation by where the feasibility rules live: set partitioning when columns are short and their feasibility depends on the whole sequence, an arc model when the binding rules concern consecutive pairs and fit into the arc costs (pp. 6–8).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: Binary covering of graph nodes by circuits. The arc model needs costs additive over arcs, cannot price a circuit by its whole sequence or by crew type, and its LP can be very weak when the infeasibility constraints are tight (p. 6); the crew-type variant allows type-dependent costs and possibly tighter LPs at the price of size (p. 7); set partitioning allows any circuit cost and a typically much tighter LP, its side constraints reduce to compact crew-base rows, but it has very many columns and may need column generation with an effective pricing procedure (p. 8). Scheduling at FS is pure SCP (15)–(17) (p. 9): up to 5,000 trips and 1,000,000 duties, one to two orders of magnitude larger than typical airline instances, while the exact SCP codes of the literature reach a few hundred trips and a few thousand duties (p. 9). FASTER instances range from 507 × 63,009 to 4,872 × 968,672 with densities of 0.2–1.3% (Table 1, p. 12). Rostering data: per duty a start and an end time in [0, 1440), a spread time, a working time and a paid time, and the attributes external rest, long (more than 8 hours 5 minutes of work, no external rest), overnight (work between midnight and 5 am) and heavy overnight (more than 1 hour 30 minutes of such work, no external rest) (pp. 12–13); weeks of 6 days, rosters typically 30 and at most 60 days, these two limits not imposed as constraints; minimising weeks minimises crews, 6 per week (p. 13). Operational constraints: per week at most 2 external-rest duties, 1 long and 2 overnight (list from PDF p. 13); a weekly rest spanning the sixth day; simple rests of at least 48 hours, double rests of at least two complete days; at least 40% double rests and an average rest of at least 58 hours; per cyclic 30 days at most 7 external-rest duties and 170 paid hours; per cyclic 7 days at most 36 working hours (p. 13). Sequencing rules: breaks of at least 18 hours, 22 hours between two overnight duties of which at most one is heavy, a complete day between two heavy ones, and rest rules around overnight duties and early starts (p. 14). FARO instances: 21–525 duties (Table 2, p. 18). The bound argument assumes arc costs that differ by multiples of 1440, so that v(RP) is a whole number of days (pp. 15–16).
  - Transferable technique: when over-covering is allowed (here deadheading), relax partitioning to covering and generate only inclusion-maximal columns among those of equal cost (p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Devices: the SCP Lagrangian subproblem is solved by the sign of each Lagrangian cost, and the subgradient s_i(u) = 1 − Σ_{j∈J_i} y_j(u) drives the multiplier sequence (p. 10). RP's value is in minutes but always a whole number of days, so r bounds the number of weeks from below (p. 16). With multipliers λ1, λ2 ≥ 0 on the two counting constraints and a relaxed weeks constraint (29), LRP decomposes into an AP on d_ij, the minimum of the three Lagrangian arc costs, a scalar search for the smallest integer r with αr ≥ λ1 r + λ2⌈0.4r⌉ + v(AP), and z = ⌈0.4r⌉, in O(n³) overall (pp. 16–17; exponent from PDF p. 17). Experience shows that two APs, at (λ1, λ2) = (1440, 1440) and (1440, 0), give a tight bound v* (p. 17). The roster heuristic updates the bound parametrically in O(n²) per step (p. 17; exponent from the PDF). CFT saves time through the priced core and the aggressive step size (p. 11). No other proof is given, and no solver is named for the APs.
  - Transferable technique: relax the counting side constraints of a sequencing relaxation so that it collapses to one assignment problem plus scalar searches, then try the few multiplier values the cost lattice suggests (two APs) before any subgradient (pp. 16–17).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: FASTER (Table 1, p. 12): seven FS instances with, per instance, the subgradient lower bound, CFT's value and the best value found by other methods; time limits of 3,000 CPU s on a PC 486/33 for the first three instances and 10,000 CPU s on an HP 9000 735/125 for the others (p. 11). By the table CFT ties the others' best on four instances and beats it on three (691 vs 692, 947 vs 951, 1,065 vs 1,070), never losing (my reading, p. 12); the stated 0.9% average gap between bound and heuristic (p. 11) agrees with the table (my recomputation of (CFT − LB)/LB: 0.86%). The other methods are not named and no per-instance times are printed (pp. 11–12). On the SCP instances of the literature CFT is said to reach the optimal or best known value on 92 of 94 and to improve on 6 of the 22 open ones, with no table here (p. 10). FARO (Table 2, p. 18): eight instances of 21–525 duties with the best simple bound, the Lagrangian bound, the heuristic's weeks and its time in Pentium 90 CPU seconds; the Lagrangian bound exceeds the simple one on seven of eight and equals it (108) on FARO360 (my reading, p. 18). The text concludes that 6 out of 7 instances were solved to proven optimality within 20 minutes (p. 18; see Quotes), but the printed table has eight rows, seven of which reach the Lagrangian bound (all but FARO360, 111 weeks against 108) in at most 1,185 s (my reading, p. 18). The eight rows are identical to the first eight rows of S017's Table 3 (S017 p. 25), which S017 attributes to the post-competition final version (S017 p. 23); here they are presented only as tests on the FARO instances (p. 18). Single runs; the rostering refinement perturbs parameters at random (p. 17), yet no seeds or spread are reported, and no failure is reported.
  - Transferable technique: print a lower bound beside every heuristic value, so that each row certifies its optimality or shows its gap (Tables 1–2, pp. 12, 18).
- **D6 Positioning**: Crew and vehicle management surveys are cited in one paragraph (p. 2). For SCP, the exact codes (Beasley; Beasley and Jörnsten; Balas and Carrera) are ruled out by size, classical greedy algorithms are said to be very fast but of low solution quality, and the Lagrangian line (Balas and Ho; Beasley; Fisher and Kedia; Balas and Carrera; Ceria, Nobili and Sassano; Wedelin), Lorena and Lopes's surrogate variant and the genetic and local-search methods of Beasley and Chu and of Jacobs and Brusco are listed (pp. 9–10). Rostering work is grouped as urban transit (Jachnik; Bodin et al.; Carraresi and Gallo; Hagberg; Bianco et al.), airline set partitioning (Ryan; Gamache and Soumis; Gamache et al.; Jarrah and Diamond) and cyclic scheduling (Tien and Kamiyama; Balakrishnan and Wong) (p. 12). Railways are positioned against airlines by size (p. 9) and against transit by objective (p. 4). The authors' own method is said to outperform previously published methods (p. 10); details are deferred to their reports (pp. 9, 14, 17).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: An outline (abstract, p. 1) whose details are deferred to the authors' reports (pp. 9, 14, 17), presenting the techniques as those currently adopted at FS (pp. 1, 4). Modelling advice is framed as experience (p. 8). A two-figure toy example shows why a 12-day roster with every sixth day idle needs 12 crews (pp. 2–3). There is no conclusions section; the paper ends with the rostering results (p. 18). Paragraphs are shared with the group's other papers: the CFT paragraphs recur nearly word for word in S003 (S012 pp. 10–11; S003 pp. 4–7), and the rostering opening and heuristic follow S017 (S012 pp. 12, 17; S017 pp. 3, 13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: a modelling guide for crew management (model choice by structure, p. 8) and the description of the two FS codes with their competition data (pp. 9–18). Limits stated: decomposition is a compromise (p. 4); feedback between the phases is suggested, not implemented (p. 4); the core definition is critical (p. 11); exact SCP codes are out of reach at FS size (p. 9); the arc model's LP can be very weak (p. 6). Not stated: the Table 1 rivals are anonymous, and the FARO count does not match its table (pp. 11, 18). ### S017 · Modeling and solving the crew rostering problem (Operations Research 1998, DOI 10.1287/opre.46.6.820)
**Read level**: full. The whole technical report DEIS-OR-95-6(R) (26 file pages): cover (p. 1), abstract and introduction (pp. 2–4), the general model (pp. 4–11), bounds (pp. 11–13), the heuristic (pp. 13–16), the FARO problem (pp. 16–21), results with Tables 1–3 (pp. 21–25), acknowledgments and references (pp. 24–26). The prose is clean in the text layer, but most formulas, the values of Tables 1 and 3 and several of Table 2 are lost, so those values, the formulas and the complexity exponents (pp. 12–16) were read from the PDF pages of the same number. Figures 1–2 (pp. 5, 7) were read from captions and text. The journal version (Oper. Res. 46(6), 820–830) was not read; the work list notes the homepage title "Models and Algorithms for the Crew Rostering".
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Alberto Caprara, Paolo Toth, Daniele Vigo (DEIS, Bologna); Fischetti's affiliation is DMI, University of Udine (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the code that won FARO, the second of the two FS/AIRO competitions announced in 1994, after FASTER (set covering; the group's FASTER report is ref. [7]) (pp. 2–3, 24). The report is the revised version: referees are thanked (p. 24). S012 summarises it (S012 pp. 14–18).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The rostering phase of crew management, sequencing given duties into rosters, is taken alone (pp. 2–3). The entry is a difference of objective: earlier rostering work addresses urban mass transit, where the minimum number of crews is easily determined and the aim is to balance workload, whereas railway rostering has more involved sequencing rules and minimises the number of crews (abstract, p. 2; p. 3). The problem came as a competition: in 1994 FS and AIRO, the Italian OR society, organised FASTER (SCP with up to 5,000 rows and 1,000,000 columns) and FARO (rostering with a hierarchical objective led by the crew count) among Italian university departments; FARO codes were sent to FS and judged on three instances of up to 1,000 duties within 90 minutes on a PC 486/33 with 8 MB, for prizes of about US$60,000 and US$30,000 (p. 3). The Bologna unit won the first prize with the best solutions on all competition instances (pp. 3–4). The paper aims beyond the competition, at a general model for airline and railway rostering (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: when the literature optimises another objective than your application (workload balance versus crew count), make that difference the entry point (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: A roster is a looped cycle in a complete directed multigraph G = (V, A ∪ L): three arcs between every pair of duties (direct, simple-rest, double-rest), whose lengths c¹, c², c³ are minimum start-to-start times encoding the sequencing rules, and loops for idle days (pp. 6–7); not every looped cycle is a feasible roster, so infeasible looped paths are forbidden (pp. 7–8). The ILP (2)–(8) has binary arc and loop variables, degree constraints, one loop per vertex and one constraint per minimal infeasible looped path (pp. 8–9); sequencing rules are implicit in the lengths, operational constraints sit in (6) (p. 8). Integer counts w (rest arcs) and z (double-rest arcs) with inequalities (9)–(13) are added, redundant while (6) holds but useful once it is dropped (p. 10). The relaxation drops (6), which makes every loop variable trivial, relaxes the two counting inequalities (10) and (12) with multipliers λ1, λ2, and replaces (9) by a valid inequality (15) (p. 11); Proposition 1 reduces it to one assignment problem plus a scalar search (p. 12). Subgradient optimisation is replaced by experience: the best multipliers are usually λ1 = λ2 = 1440, in a few cases λ1 = 1440 and λ2 = 0, because the three lengths of a pair differ by multiples of 1440, so LB is the larger of two AP values (pp. 12–13); the global bound LB* also takes the trivial bounds (p. 13). The heuristic builds one roster at a time and is steered by the AP dual: reduced costs are said to estimate an arc's likelihood of being optimal far better than its length, so rosters of zero reduced-cost arcs are sought (p. 13); the initial duty is scored by its zero reduced-cost in- and out-arcs (p. 14); each next duty is chosen among three moves (direct, simple rest, double rest) by the increase of LB*, counting the idle days a move needs and penalising deviation from the target share β of double rests (p. 14); tie-break terms put critical duties first (few zero reduced-cost arcs, long working or paid time, attributes), with weights that follow each constraint's tightness as the ratio of its trivial bound to LB*, and spread the workload (p. 15); a look-ahead is added when fewer than about 100 duties remain (p. 15); closing the roster is scored by the bound difference (pp. 15–16); no idle day is left unless strictly necessary (p. 14). A refining loop computes, for each roster, the global bound with and without imposing it, removes every roster with a positive difference, changes the construction parameters randomly or adaptively, rebuilds, and repeats until the time limit (p. 16). Changed relative to standard Lagrangian practice: no subgradient, two fixed multiplier pairs; kept: the AP as the bounding engine.
  - Transferable technique: when the relaxation's costs live on a lattice (multiples of 1440 minutes), try the few structural multiplier values before running subgradient optimisation (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: Periodic rostering, every duty every day; integer times in minutes; per duty a start time, a duration, a working time w_i ≤ p_i and a paid time a_i ≥ w_i, allowed to exceed the duration because some paid intervals are fictitious (p. 4); attributes given on input (p. 4). Weeks of k days, rosters of a multiple of k days up to q, every k-th day idle; simple and double weekly rests, the double ones at least a fraction β of the weeks; a threshold on the average weekly rest; caps per week and per cyclic group of m_j days on attribute counts and on working and paid time (pp. 4–6); minimising weeks minimises crews, k per week (p. 6). The model is a 0-1 ILP with exponentially many path constraints (6), whose LP strengthening is declined in favour of combinatorial relaxations (p. 9; see Quotes); it absorbs q sequencing types (q parallel arcs per pair) and, unchanged, weeks of different lengths (pp. 9–10). FARO: k = 6, q = 60; external-rest, long, overnight and heavy overnight duties; technical intervals (idle days inside a week) to be avoided; long duties never sequenced directly (p. 17); per week at most 2 external-rest, 1 long and 2 overnight duties; β = 0.4 and an average weekly rest of at least 58 hours; per cyclic 30 days at most 7 external-rest duties and 170 paid hours; per cyclic 7 days at most 36 working hours (p. 18); one optional special one-week roster with its last three days idle (p. 18); a hierarchical objective of weeks, then technical intervals, then available days (p. 19). Data: eight FS instances of 21–525 duties, five distributed before the competition and three used in it, with attribute counts and average duration, working and paid time (Table 2, p. 23). The average paid time exceeds the average duration on five of the eight, as the model allows (my reading, p. 23; p. 4); more than one third of the duties are said to be overnight (p. 23), true in aggregate (695 of 1,692, my count) though not on FARO069 (18 of 69). The test bed is enlarged with all 28 merged pairs of distinct FARO instances (p. 23). Correctness rests on lengths that differ by multiples of 1440 (pp. 12–13); the lower-level bounds of the hierarchical objective are valid only when every higher-level bound equals its optimum (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: add counting variables and inequalities that are redundant in the full ILP but bind once the hard constraints are relaxed (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Proof devices: validity of (15) by comparing its right-hand side with that of (9) on feasible solutions (p. 11); Proposition 1: with (6) gone, keep for each pair the arc of minimum Lagrangian cost, solve one AP, take the smallest integer w satisfying (16) and set z := ⌈βw⌉, the AP costing O(n³) (p. 12); trivial bounds in O(n) from each cap, e.g. the paid-time bound (1440 · 30) Σ a_i / (170 · 60) (p. 11), and for FARO five such bounds and their versions with the special roster (p. 19). For the hierarchical objective: a small ε added to every direct arc that needs a technical interval turns the AP value into a lower bound on technical intervals, set to 0 when the day bound is not a multiple of 6 (p. 20); a dummy duty of three idle days forces the special roster and gives LB′, the overall bound min{LB, LB′} and rules (18)–(19) for the technical-interval bound and the available-day upper bound (pp. 20–21); with FARO's parameters LB′ is not less than LB (p. 21). A vertex-splitting transformation would replace the loops by arcs (p. 8). Complexity: each construction step, the look-ahead and the closing test cost O(n²) through parametric AP re-solves; a roster costs O(n²) because its length is capped at q, and the algorithm O(n³) (pp. 13–16; exponents from the PDF). Imposed arcs are realised by setting the competing lengths to +∞ (p. 15). FORTRAN code (p. 21); two people are thanked for help in programming (p. 24). Carpaneto and Toth's primal-dual AP algorithms are in the reference list (p. 26) but not cited in the text.
  - Transferable technique: for a lexicographic objective, bound a lower level by adding ε to the costs of the arcs that create it, and declare that bound valid only where every higher-level bound is attained (p. 20).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Two layers. (a) The competition (Table 1, p. 22): seven teams entered, and the table gives the three whose codes produced valid solutions on all three test instances, BO (the authors), NA (Naples) and TO (Turin), whose reports are a greedy and a genetic algorithm by their titles (pp. 22, 24, 26), with weeks, technical intervals and available days within 90 minutes on a PC 486/33 and the authors' lower bound. Weeks: bounds 48, 108, 164 on FARO164, FARO360, FARO525; BO 48, 112, 165; NA 48, 113, 168; TO 49, 113, 168; on FARO164 BO and NA tie on weeks and BO wins on technical intervals, 0 against 5 (Table 1, p. 22). The times to the reported solutions are given as 18, 76 and 25 minutes, without saying whose; read as the authors' (p. 22). The FARO360 gap is diagnosed: the paid-time constraint binds more than elsewhere, the Lagrangian bound and the paid-time trivial bound are both 108, several candidate rosters fail on paid time, and with that constraint relaxed the heuristic reaches 108 (p. 22; see Quotes). ### S003 · Algorithms for the set covering problem (Annals of Operations Research 2000, DOI 10.1023/a:1019225027893)
**Read level**: full for this copy, which has 12 pages: the text (pp. 1–9), the references (pp. 10–11) and Table 1 (p. 12). Tables 2–4, discussed on pp. 7–9, are not in this copy, so for them only the prose summaries are used. Table 1's values are lost in the text layer (only the sizes survive) and were read from PDF page 12; the relation signs of the reduction and fixing rules were checked on PDF pages 2 and 4. The journal version (Ann. Oper. Res. 98, 353–371) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Alberto Caprara, Paolo Toth (DEIS, Bologna); Fischetti's affiliation is DEI, University of Padova (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a survey by the authors of the leading SCP heuristic CFT (ref. [9], S004, then to appear in Operations Research) and of an exact LP code for SCP (ref. [10], a 1998 working paper), citing S012 as ref. [11] (pp. 1, 4–5, 10). It closes the railway set-covering strand, and it is the first of these three works in which a general-purpose solver is measured.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: SCP is introduced as the model behind crew scheduling in railway and mass-transit companies (abstract and p. 1, citing S012). The scope is fixed by a test bed: the most recent and effective heuristic and exact algorithms, compared on Beasley's OR Library; methods never tried on those instances are not described (p. 1). The state of the art is stated in sizes: a few hundred rows and a few thousand columns are solved to proven optimality, a few thousand rows and a few million columns to within about 1% in reasonable time (p. 2). Classes 4, 5, 6 and e are dropped as easy for both heuristics and exact codes, leaving the 40 instances of classes A–H (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: scope a computational survey by one public test bed, and drop, with the reason, the classes on which every method succeeds (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The organising claim is that the most effective heuristic and exact methods rest on the LP relaxation, solved exactly or, through Lagrangian or surrogate relaxation with subgradient optimisation, heuristically in its dual (pp. 2–3): any multiplier vector gives a bound and can be repaired greedily into a dual-feasible one without lowering it, so near-optimal multipliers are in some sense a heuristic dual solution (p. 3). Devices surveyed: the Held-Karp subgradient step, its parameter halved after p iterations without improvement (p. 3); Lagrangian cost fixing (p. 4); Balas and Carrera's partial relaxation that keeps a set of disjoint rows, and their dynamic subgradient that keeps the multipliers dual feasible (p. 4); the authors' priced core and their step-size and direction changes (p. 4); Ceria, Nobili and Sassano's primal-dual subgradient, which bounds the LP value from both sides (p. 4); Lorena and Lopes's surrogate relaxation, solved as a continuous knapsack in O(n) (p. 4); Wedelin's row-by-row update to the mean of the two smallest modified Lagrangian costs, with two values per row (pp. 4–5); the authors' new exact LP code, a SPRINT-like sequence of core LPs with pricing as the optimality test, a Lagrangian start for the core and the duals, and multiplier adjustment that keeps the bounds increasing (p. 5). Heuristics: a Lagrangian-cost greedy, run at several near-optimal multiplier vectors, is the common core (pp. 5–6), in Beasley's, Balas and Carrera's, Lorena and Lopes's, Ceria, Nobili and Sassano's (a core chosen carefully once and then frozen, against the authors' dynamically updated core) and Haddadi's codes, and in the authors' three-phase scheme with fixing of the first k greedy columns and refinement (pp. 6–7); the non-Lagrangian exceptions are Beasley and Chu's genetic algorithm and the simulated annealing of Jacobs and Brusco and of Brusco, Jacobs and Thompson with morphs (p. 7). Exact: LP-based branch-and-bound by Beasley (1987), Beasley and Jörnsten (1992; constraints excluding the incumbent and Gomory fractional cuts) and Balas and Carrera (1996; dynamic subgradient at every node, two dual-based branching schemes) (pp. 8–9). From this follows a comparison rule: relaxations used inside heuristics are compared by the heuristics they drive, not by the bounds they give (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: judge a relaxation used inside a heuristic by the solutions it leads to, not by its bound (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: SCP (1)–(3) with a 0-1 matrix and integer costs, positive without loss of generality (p. 1); q nonzeros, generally far fewer than mn (p. 2); strongly NP-hard and hard to approximate (p. 2). Reduction rules recalled: drop a column j when a column k ≠ j covers a superset of its rows with c_j ≥ c_k, drop a column whose cost is at least the sum, over its rows, of the cheapest cost covering each, drop a row whose cover set contains another row's, and fix a column that alone covers a row (relation signs from PDF p. 2); they need careful implementation on large instances, and each algorithm's preprocessing is deliberately left out to show its essential structure (p. 2). Test bed (Table 1, p. 12): classes A and B 300 × 3,000, C and D 400 × 4,000, E and F 500 × 5,000, G and H 1,000 × 10,000, five instances each, densities 2%, 5%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 2% and 5%, costs 1–100; optima known for A–D and for few of E–H (pp. 7–8). The devices rely on the integrality property of the Lagrangian subproblem, which makes the Lagrangian dual equal the LP dual (p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Devices with their arguments: the integrality property (p. 3); the greedy dual repair that never lowers the bound (p. 3); Lagrangian cost fixing in both directions against an upper bound UB, x_j fixed to 0 when L(u) + c_j(u) ≥ UB and to 1 when L(u) − c_j(u) ≥ UB (p. 4; the OCR prints > where PDF p. 4 has ≥); the partial relaxation, solvable in O(q), whose best bound equals the LP value (p. 4); the optimal removal of redundant columns as an SCP of its own (p. 6). An explanatory argument for exact methods: LP-based branch-and-bound wins because markedly stronger bounds seem very hard to obtain by costlier methods, which also explains why general-purpose ILP packages, with their effort on parametric LP re-optimisation, are competitive (p. 8). Implementation facts: the authors' exact LP code calls the CPLEX simplex internally, and its time inside CPLEX is reported (p. 5; Table 1, p. 12); for the exact comparison, CPLEX and MINTO are also given an instance preprocessed by the authors' heuristic (without post-optimisation), their LP code and LP reduced-cost fixing: a much smaller instance with a near-optimal solution and the optimal LP solution (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: fix variables by Lagrangian costs in both directions against the incumbent (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Table 1 (p. 12): the authors' LP code against CPLEX 4.0.8's primal, dual, network and barrier-with-crossover algorithms, each tuned for the best overall performance on the 40 instances (p. 5). Stated averages: about 420 s (primal), 370 (barrier), 46 (dual and network) and 10 for the new code, about 7 of them inside CPLEX, a factor of almost 5 over the best CPLEX algorithms (p. 5). My recomputation from Table 1: 418.8, 369.9, 45.6, 46.0 and 10.25 s, with 6.54 s inside CPLEX; the ratio of averages is 4.4–4.5; the new code is faster than both the dual and the network simplex on all 40 instances, by factors from 1.9 to 7.6, and on classes G and H most of its time is spent inside CPLEX (G.1: 21.54 of 24.65 s) (p. 12). Table 2 (classes A–D, optima known; not in this copy): Beasley and Chu's and the authors' heuristics find every optimum; average times about 4 s (Lorena and Lopes), 30 (Haddadi), 40 (Beasley), 45 (Balas and Carrera), 50 (the authors') and 180 (Beasley and Chu); Lorena and Lopes's code misses the optimum in 15 of 20 cases, and its published times exclude the initial reduction (pp. 7–8). Table 3 (classes E–H; not in this copy): the authors' heuristic gives the best known value on all; Beasley and Chu and Brusco, Jacobs and Thompson match it with three and two exceptions; Ceria, Nobili and Sassano and Jacobs and Brusco are slightly worse; average times about 200 s (Beasley; Balas and Carrera), 250 (Brusco, Jacobs and Thompson), 400 (Haddadi; the authors') and 7,500 (Beasley and Chu); no time comparison for the two codes that report only a time limit (p. 8). Table 4 (classes A–D; not in this copy): about 3,000 s (Beasley), 2,000 (Beasley and Jörnsten) and 1,500 (Balas and Carrera) against about 700 for CPLEX 4.0.8 and 750 for MINTO 2.3, and about 400 and 500 when the two solvers get the preprocessed instance (p. 9; see Quotes). Nobili and Sassano's cutting-plane code is left out because only node counts were published (p. 8). Times are in DECstation 5000/240 CPU seconds, measured by the authors for their own codes, CPLEX and MINTO, and converted with Dongarra's figures for the others, a conversion the text calls a very crude approximation that still gives clear indications in most cases (p. 2). - **D6 Positioning**: The history is left to Balas's survey and Ceria, Nobili and Sassano's annotated bibliography (p. 1), and the inclusion rule is the test bed (p. 1). - **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: a comparative map of SCP methods on one test bed; a new exact LP code that beats CPLEX's LP algorithms on it; and the finding that general-purpose ILP solvers compete with special-purpose exact codes and gain from external preprocessing (pp. 1, 5, 9).
