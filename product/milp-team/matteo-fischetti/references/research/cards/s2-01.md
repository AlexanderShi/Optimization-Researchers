### H003 · Experiments with Controlled Rounding for Statistical Disclosure Control in Tabular Data with Linear Constraints (Journal of Official Statistics 1998, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (all 13 file pages: §§1–5, Tables 1–6 and the references; the article has no conclusions section). The font layer drops or garbles mathematical symbols ("=" is missing in "Ma  b", "∈" prints as "[", "≤" as "#", pp. 2, 4) and the fi/fl ligatures (printed "®", "¯"), but the prose and all six tables are readable. First-page check: title, Matteo Fischetti and Juan-José Salazar-González, Journal of Official Statistics Vol 14, No. 4, 1998, pp. 553–565 (p. 1); this is the article itself, not the CPR interim report that round 1 found under this ID (s01).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Juan-José Salazar-González · **Position in trajectory**: the statistical-disclosure-control strand of ESPRIT project 20462-SDS, next to the authors' cell-suppression papers (Fischetti and Salazar 1996, 1998), with the algorithms embedded in τ-ARGUS (printed "t-ARGUS") at Statistics Netherlands (pp. 1, 3); Alberto Caprara implemented the Gomory and {0, 1/2}-cut separators (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A statistical agency must publish as much as it can without revealing respondents, a trade-off "of primary importance in practice" (p. 1). Controlled rounding moves each cell to an adjacent multiple of a base b and keeps the marginal totals; cells already multiples of b stay fixed (zero-restricted) so that the rounded table is unbiased (pp. 1–2). Earlier exact work covers 2- and 3-D tables with marginals: Cox and Ernst (1982) show 2-D is always feasible, Causey, Cox and Ernst (1985) give an infeasible 2×2×2 table, Kelly, Golden, Assad and Baker (1990) an LP-based branch-and-bound (p. 3). The entry here is generality and feasibility: cells under any linear system Ma = b, which covers k-dimensional, hierarchical and linked tables (pp. 1, 3), and finding any feasible rounding is "the main issue" in practice when no objective is given (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: CRP asks for an integer point of P_CRP = {ã : Mã = b, ⌊a_i⌋ ≤ ã_i ≤ ⌈a_i⌉}, which is never empty because it contains the nominal table (p. 4). Its vertices are the lever: a vertex has no more fractional components than M has rows, so it is a good start for rounding, and when M is totally unimodular (2-D tables) every vertex is a feasible rounding (p. 4). Then one method per structure: min-cost flow, or without an objective a circulation rounding with no LP solver, for 2-D (pp. 5–6); LP-based branch-and-bound for 3-D (p. 7); for instances with no zero-restricted rounding, an ILP with enlarged rounding windows solved by branch-and-cut (pp. 8, 10).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: General-integer rounding variables bounded by their rounding windows, with equality links Ma = b ("can easily be extended" to inequalities, p. 2); base b scaled to 1 without loss of generality and weights w⁻, w⁺ for rounding down or up (p. 2). Polynomial for k ≤ 2, strongly NP-hard for k ≥ 3 (p. 2). The vertex procedure assumes M of full row rank (p. 4). Test data: random tables generated as in Kelly, Golden, Assad and Baker (1990), entries 0 with probability d (0–90% zeros) and otherwise 1 or 2, base 3 (pp. 5, 7, 12): 2-D from 100×100 to 300×300 (p. 6), 3-D with 60 entries, up to 8×8×8 and, for relaxed windows, up to 10×18×18 (pp. 7–10), 4-D from 4×4×4×4 to 4×4×8×8 and 4×6×6×6 with all 1-, 2- and 3-way marginals (pp. 11–12). No real tables.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Vertex existence by construction: move the point along a nonzero direction λ supported on the fractional set F with Σ λ_i M_i = 0 until a bound is hit, which makes at least one more component integral; repeat until the columns indexed by F are independent (p. 4). In 2-D, consistent roundings correspond one-to-one to integer circulations in a row-and-column network with capacities ⌊a_ij⌋, ⌈a_ij⌉ (CPLEX 3.0 network simplex, p. 5); without an objective, each circuit of the incremental network removes one fractional flow, coded in C with no LP solver (p. 6). 3-D: LP-based branch-and-bound "in the vein of" Kelly et al. (p. 7). Relaxed windows: rule 1 zero-restricted, rule 2 lets an integer cell rise to a_i + 1 at a large weight; rule 2 was applied only after rule 1 failed at every node, though it can be used directly since the weights keep such moves to the minimum, none if a zero-restricted rounding exists (p. 10). Branch-and-cut after Padberg and Rinaldi with Gomory cuts and {0, 1/2}-cuts (Caprara and Fischetti 1996) at every node (p. 10), in C with CPLEX 3.0 as LP solver (p. 12).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: PC Pentium/75 notebook, CPLEX 3.0, averages (and maxima) per size and density cell (pp. 5–12): optimal 2-D roundings in at most 25.28 s on average against 10.06 s for the feasibility-only rounding (Tables 1–2, p. 6); 3-D tables with 60 entries never above 0.5 s, at most 156 of 1,000 needing branching (Table 3, p. 7); relaxed-window branch-and-cut on 10 instances per cell, up to 1,602.96 s average on 3-D (p. 10) and 3,357.11 s on 4-D tables (p. 11). All 600 relaxed-window 3-D instances have a zero-restricted rounding, while 193 of the 450 4-D instances have none (none at 0% zeros, 82 of 90 at 75%; my count from Table 6, p. 11), and rule 2 always restored feasibility (p. 12). No rival code and no time limit; the r1/r2 column definitions on p. 12 look swapped (r1 + r2 = 10 in every row of Tables 5–6).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Models for CRP under any linear system, LP-based enumerative algorithms embedded in τ-ARGUS, the relaxed-window model, and the finding that 4-D tables often admit no zero-restricted rounding while slightly enlarged windows always did in the test bed (p. 1). No limits or future work are stated (the text ends with the §5 results, p. 12); the generality claimed for hierarchical and linked tables (pp. 1, 3) is not tested, since every experiment uses random k-dimensional tables with marginals (pp. 5–12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the method is fitted to the structure by table dimension. Total unimodularity makes 2-D a flow problem, and when no objective is given even the LP solver is dropped for a circulation rounding with "a considerable computing time saving" (p. 6). The thinning is of the algorithm and of the question asked (feasibility before optimality, p. 3), not of the model's variables.
- Method 6 ⚠ variant (era): in 1998 the general-purpose component is the LP engine (CPLEX 3.0 network simplex, p. 5; CPLEX 3.0 under the authors' own branch-and-cut, p. 12); the text reports no run of a stock MIP solver on the ILP, unlike the black-box stance of his work from 2003 on.
- new-pattern candidate: "Big-penalty slack so every solve returns a point" (rule 2 prices window enlargements with large weights, so one model returns the zero-restricted rounding when it exists and the least enlargement otherwise, p. 10; also H006 pp. 10–11 and S086 pp. 2–3, 12).
- new-pattern candidate: "Near-integral LP points as the heuristic's lever" (a vertex has at most rank(M) fractional components and is a good start for rounding, p. 4; branch-and-bound is cheap because 3-D vertices are almost integral, p. 8; also S086 p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To make a fractional feasible point near-integral without an LP solve, move it along null-space directions of the constraint matrix supported on its fractional components until a bound is hit; each step fixes one more component (p. 4). On network matrices the directions are circuits of the incremental network (p. 6).
- When a hard requirement (here zero-restrictedness) may make the model infeasible, widen only the windows that the requirement fixes and price the widening with a large weight: one model then returns the strict solution when it exists and the least relaxation otherwise (p. 10).
- Generate random instances exactly as the closest predecessor did (here Kelly, Golden, Assad and Baker 1990) and report, per size and density cell, how many instances needed branching and the nodes of those that did (Tables 3–4, pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "An interesting outcome is that 4-dimensional tables often admit no zero-restricted rounding, whereas slightly enlarged rounding windows produced feasible instances in all the cases in our test bed." (p. 1)
- "a vertex of the polytope PCRP associated with 3-dimensional tables very likely has (almost) all integer components" (p. 8)

### H006 · Robustness by cutting planes and the Uncertain Set Covering Problem (preliminary draft, author's papers directory 2009, no DOI)
**Read level**: full (all 19 file pages: abstract, §§1–4, Tables 1–6 and the references; Figures 1 and 2 are images whose data are not in the text, pp. 14, 17). First-page check: title, Matteo Fischetti and Michele Monaci (DEI, Padova), "Preliminary draft: May 25, 2009" (p. 1). Relation to S045 (Mathematical Programming Computation 2012): the first part has the same topic, but nothing in this file names a later version and S045 itself was not read here, so whether this is its early version is not verified.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michele Monaci · **Position in trajectory**: the Padova robustness work with Monaci, after their "light robustness" report (ref. [14], 2008; p. 2), supported by the EC FP6 project ARRIVAL (FP6-021235-2) and a PRIN 2006 grant on robust optimization of networks (p. 18).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The Bertsimas–Sim (BS) robust counterpart is compact, adding polynomially many variables and constraints (p. 2), but the same robustness can be enforced in the original variables by exponentially many "robustness cuts", each row's separation being the LP relaxation of a cardinality-constrained knapsack (p. 2); "As far as we know" the two had never been compared computationally (p. 2). Second entry: uncertainty made of yes-no events (a set-covering column that disappears) cannot be dualized as BS requires, so cuts become "the only available option" (pp. 1–2, 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Project BS onto the x-space: constraint (4) becomes the cuts (12) for all S with |S| ≤ Γ_i, separated per row by picking the Γ_i largest profits â_ij x*_j in O(n) time with a partial-sorting technique (pp. 4–5). For the Uncertain Set Covering Problem (USCP: column j disappears independently with probability p_j, row i must be covered with probability above P_i, p. 7), take logarithms, w_j = −ln p_j and W_i = −ln(1 − P_i), so that the coverage condition becomes a knapsack cover over the selected columns (p. 8). Model M1 adds the exponential cuts (23) with a 0-1 knapsack separation (pp. 8–9); model M2 is the compact knapsack-cover ILP (29) with the same integer feasible set (Theorem 1, p. 9); M2′ adds the rounded inequalities (31) (pp. 10–11). The same reasoning yields a cut model (36) for uncertain graph connectivity, separated by minimum cut (pp. 15–16).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: LPs with x ≥ 0 and BS interval uncertainty in the inequality rows, at most Γ_i coefficients per row at their worst (integer Γ_i assumed for simplicity, p. 3), equality rows certain (p. 5); the results are said to carry over to MILPs because enumeration solves sequences of LPs (p. 3). USCP and connectivity: binary x, independent disappearance probabilities (pp. 7–8, 15), one probability condition per row (17) or per cut (36) (pp. 7, 15); M2's validity relies on the 0-1 coefficients and right-hand sides of SCP (p. 9). Test sets: 12 NETLIB LPs with at least 1,000 variables and 1,000 constraints, coefficient deviations up to 1%, Γ ∈ {1, 10, 50} (p. 5); 59 ORLIB set-covering instances, those CPLEX 11.0 solved to optimality within 600 s, with p_j uniform in [0, 0.2] and log-weights multiplied by 1,000 and rounded (p. 11); railway crew instance rail2536c (2,536 rows, 15,284 columns, a core problem; p. 14); 14 TSPLIB graphs with at most 50 nodes, sparsified to the union of 5 disjoint minimum spanning trees (pp. 16–17).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Theorem 1 (M1 and M2 have the same integer solutions) by contradiction in both directions, using the separation problem's optimal set S_i = J*_i (pp. 9–10); Example 1, with three columns, shows that neither LP bound dominates the other (p. 10); (31) comes from "a simple rounding argument similar to that used to derive Gomory’s fractional cuts" and is called numerically more stable than (29) (p. 10); Theorem 2 (strong NP-hardness of uncertain connectivity) by reduction from the min-cost 2-edge-connected subgraph problem with p = 1/2 and P = 3/4 (p. 15). A greedy initial heuristic from three starts, one of them the nominal optimum over high-weight columns with high-cost slacks "so as to ensure feasibility" (pp. 10–11). C code embedded in CPLEX 11.0 at default settings, the most violated robustness cut per row added each round, LPs re-optimized by dual simplex (pp. 5–6); (31) for k = 2, 3 as lazy constraints for USCP (p. 11) and separated before (36) by extra min-cuts for connectivity (p. 16); AMD Athlon 4200+, 4 GB (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Robust LPs (Table 2, p. 6): arithmetic means over the 12 instances (BS 62.74 s against 2.73 s for the cuts at Γ = 1), 93–98% of the BS total coming from the three instances without a robust solution (my arithmetic); the cuts lose in 7 of the 36 instance-Γ cases (SCTAP2, SCTAP3 and STOCFOR3 at Γ = 1 and 10, GANGES at 10), covered by "for most instances", and the "1-2 orders of magnitude" on the infeasible instances holds for DEGEN3 (40–164×) and roughly PILOT (9.5–51×), not D2Q06C (4–8×) (my arithmetic, p. 6). USCP, 1,800 s per model: of 59 instances at P_min = 0.85/0.90/0.95/0.99, M1 solves 54/50/35/5, M2 59/54/58/47, M2′ 59/52/54/46 (pp. 12–13); the run with CPLEX preprocessing and cuts off is shown only on scpe2 (p. 14). Connectivity, 10,000 s: M2′ solves 6/7/13/8 of 14 against M2's 5/3/4/4 (p. 17), but the text's "all the instances with n ≤ 26" (p. 16) fails for gr24 at P_min = 0.85 (Table 5, p. 17). No repeated random draws or seeds are mentioned.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: The first computational comparison of compact and cutting-plane BS (p. 2), the USCP with three models and an equivalence theorem, and an NP-hard uncertain connectivity problem (pp. 6–17). The conclusions only restate this, with performance "comparable or better" than BS (p. 18), more modest than the abstract's "considerable speedup" (p. 1); no limits or future work are given. §3 is titled "Cutting planes as the only option" (p. 6), yet for the USCP the compact M2 wins (p. 11), and cuts are indispensable only in the connectivity model (pp. 15–16). Connectivity is required cut by cut in (36); my reading is that this is necessary but not sufficient for the graph to stay connected with probability P, which the draft does not discuss (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (step 3, project out; steps 2 and 4, choose the model by measurement): the BS auxiliary variables are projected out and replaced by cuts in the original space (p. 2), where the compact model needs up to 44,330 extra variables and 40,911 extra constraints (PILOT, Table 1, p. 5); for the USCP, three models with the same integer solutions are measured on the same instances and the compact one in the original variables wins (p. 9; p. 11).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 3, do not duplicate the solver): the authors expected M2's log-weight knapsacks to be "very challenging for MIP solvers", found CPLEX's preprocessing and cut generation "quite effective" (p. 10), and saw their own cuts (31) slow M2 down because the solver had already squeezed out their information, which they checked by switching those features off (pp. 11, 14).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (dated lapse): both robust-LP arms pass through the same CPLEX with its own choice of algorithm for the first LP (p. 6), but the headline rests on arithmetic means dominated by the infeasible instances, and the cases where the cuts lose are printed without comment (Table 2, p. 6). This is the averaging that SKILL.md's Method 4 lapse reports for the 2012 robust study, via Bertsimas, Dunning and Lubin; whether S045 kept this table is not verified.
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction (dated lapse, 2009): a randomized ILP study with one set of random probabilities per instance and no seeds or repeated runs mentioned (pp. 11, 16).
- new-pattern candidate: "Strengthening cuts pay only where presolve is blind" (the same inequalities (31) slow down the compact USCP model, whose constraints the solver preprocesses, p. 11, and pay off in the connectivity model, whose constraints are generated at run time, p. 16).
- new-pattern candidate (existing): "Chvátal–Gomory derivation as validity proof and separation handle" ((31) is proved valid by a Gomory-fractional rounding, p. 10, and separated by min-cut with modified capacities, p. 16).
- new-pattern candidate: "Big-penalty slack so every solve returns a point" (the third heuristic start adds high-cost slacks to each row to ensure feasibility, pp. 10–11; also H003 p. 10, S086 pp. 2–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn an independent-failure probability requirement into a linear knapsack-cover constraint with w_j = −ln p_j and W = −ln(1 − P); for a MIP solver, multiply by 1,000 and round to integers (pp. 8, 11).
- Prove that a compact model and a cut model have the same integer solutions, show with a three-variable example that their LP bounds do not dominate each other, then decide between them by measurement on the same instances (pp. 9–11).
- When a user cut does not help a compact model, rerun with the solver's preprocessing and cut generation off; if the cut now helps, the solver was already deriving its information (pp. 11, 14).
- Separate interval-robustness cuts for one row by selecting the Γ largest values â_ij x*_j with a linear-time selection instead of a sort (p. 5).

### S086 · Proximity Benders: a decomposition heuristic for stochastic programs (Journal of Heuristics 2016, DOI 10.1007/s10732-015-9306-1)
**Read level**: partial (all 13 file pages of the author preprint dated December 7, 2015: abstract, §§1–5 and the references). This text is an OCR layer: the three result tables, printed on rotated pages (pp. 7, 9, 11), come out as unreadable character strings, so no table number is reported, and Figure 1 (p. 10) is legible only in its axes and legend. Words and phrases are missing throughout the prose: the opening of the abstract and of the Benders paragraph (p. 1), a recurring noun after "K" and "each", apparently "subproblems" (pp. 3–5, 12), the second baseline's bullet (p. 5) and parts of result sentences (pp. 6, 8, 10, 12). The PDF's own text layer (checked with pdftotext) has the same gaps, so they are in the document, not in the extraction. First-page check: title and authors Boland, Fischetti, Monaci and Savelsbergh (p. 1). A round-1 card on this work is in s07.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Natashia Boland, Michele Monaci, Martin Savelsbergh · **Position in trajectory**: proximity search (Fischetti and Monaci 2014, ref. [10]) put on top of the Benders cut recipe of Fischetti, Salvagnin and Zanette (2010, ref. [11]) (pp. 1, 3); Fischetti and Monaci were funded by the Padova project "Exploiting randomness in Mixed Integer Linear Programming" (p. 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Stochastic programs "tend to be huge" and need decomposition, Benders being one of the most used (p. 1); its convergence can take so long that the method is unsatisfactory as a heuristic (abstract, p. 1, where a word is missing). Proximity search improves a given feasible solution step by step, so the two "naturally complement each other" when good rather than optimal solutions are wanted (p. 2). Target: instances that MILP solvers cannot handle because they do not fit in memory or the root relaxation is already prohibitive (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Run proximity search on the Benders master: add a cutoff on the total cost at U − θ (U the incumbent's cost), replace the objective with the Hamming distance to the incumbent ȳ, impose the cutoff softly with a continuous variable z₀ of large cost M₀ ("proximity search with incumbent"), and solve the master with the current cuts by "any black-box MILP solver" (p. 3). Each second-stage LP gets two nonnegative slacks z_k, v_k with a large penalty M_k, following [11]: z_k > 0 gives a feasibility cut, z_k = 0 and v_k > 0 an optimality cut, and the incumbent moves when all z_k are zero and the cost is below U (pp. 3–4; Algorithm 2, p. 4). Changing the master objective changes the master points and therefore the cuts (p. 4). Rei et al. use local branching inside Benders; here a black-box Benders runs inside an outer local search (p. 4). A repair heuristic raises components of an infeasible y through auxiliary problems when only a few scenarios are infeasible (p. 5; Algorithm 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Binary first-stage y in a polyhedron, K continuous recourse variables, and K LPs once y is fixed (block structure); a feasible solution is assumed easy to find (p. 3); the repair assumes that y′ ≥ y is feasible whenever y is, so y = (1, …, 1) is always feasible (p. 5). Instances: stochastic capacitated facility location from Bodur et al., 4 classes of 4 instances each with 250 scenarios and 4 more classes with 500, optima known (p. 6); stochastic network interdiction, only the harder classes 3 and 4, budgets 30 to 90 (p. 8); stochastic fixed-charge multi-commodity network design on 35 networks of Crainic et al., with 2,000 scenarios drawn per commodity uniformly between the minimum and maximum demand of the original 64 scenarios (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: C with IBM ILOG Cplex 12.6.1 as the MILP solver (p. 5); a minimum improvement θ proportional to the incumbent value z₀ (the factor is garbled in this text, p. 5) under "a non-aggressive policy" in which θ decreases during the run (p. 5); large M_k push the second stages towards feasibility and the repair heuristic covers the rest (p. 12). No proofs (n/a).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Single thread, 1 h per instance, Intel Xeon E3-1220V2 3.10 GHz, 16 GB (p. 5); arms: Cplex default on the full MILP, a second arm whose bullet is empty (p. 5) but which Figure 1's legend names "Benders" among "the three heuristics" (p. 10), and ProxyBenders; primal integral after Achterberg et al. and Berthold, wins, instances improved and improving solutions at 100, 600 and 3,600 s (pp. 5–6), against the optimum on facility location (p. 6) and the best known value on network design (p. 10). Prose results only: on facility location ProxyBenders leads Cplex at short limits (the verb is missing; "loses its edge" implies it) and loses its edge with more time, Cplex solving all but two instances within the hour (p. 6); Cplex cannot solve the root LP of twenty network-design instances in an hour, and ProxyBenders is "reasonably effective, especially for the largest instance classes" 8, 9 and 10 (pp. 10, 12). No seeds or repeated runs are mentioned.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A proof-of-concept (p. 12) put forward as "the method of choice" when high-quality solutions to very large stochastic programs are needed quickly, in particular when the LP relaxation is prohibitive (p. 12). The facility-location set is conceded to be "inadequate to fully reveal the power" of the method (p. 6) and the later sets are called "(somewhat) more appropriate to show the benefits" (p. 8). The implementation targets no specific problem (p. 12); parallelization is asserted ("naturally and easily parallelizes", p. 12), not tested. Future: sensitivity to the initial solution, more aggressive θ schemes, cut management, not solving every subproblem at each iteration, randomization as in the feasibility pump, tailored implementations (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (step 3, change the master objective): the cut recipe of [11] stays and the master objective becomes a proximity function, which changes the sequence of master points and their Benders cuts (p. 4).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the modified master "can be solved by any black-box MILP solver" (p. 3), and proximity search applies the MILP solver as a black box (p. 2).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the facility-location set, on which Cplex catches up within the hour, is kept and its weakness conceded (p. 6), but the next sets are chosen as "(somewhat) more appropriate to show the benefits" and, for interdiction, restricted to "the more difficult instances" (p. 8).
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction (dated lapse, 2015): heuristics compared by primal integral with no seeds or repeated runs in the text, although the work was funded by a project on exploiting randomness in MILP; randomization appears only as future work, credited to the feasibility pump (pp. 5, 12).
- new-pattern candidate: "Role reversal of nested methods" (an outer proximity search drives an inner black-box Benders, the reverse of Rei et al., p. 4; the same work as the s07 card, so no new paper for the count).
- new-pattern candidate: "Big-penalty slack so every solve returns a point" (the soft cutoff with z₀ and a large M₀, pp. 2–3; slack-augmented second stages whose large M_k "enforces feasibility in many cases", pp. 3, 12; also H003 p. 10, H006 pp. 10–11).
- new-pattern candidate: "Near-integral LP points as the heuristic's lever" (the proximity objective improves the "relaxation grip": LP solutions have many integer components, which raises the success rate of the solver's heuristics, in particular where feasibility is enforced by cut generation, p. 2; also H003 pp. 4, 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn an exact Benders code into a heuristic by changing only the master: Hamming objective to the incumbent, a soft cutoff with a heavily priced slack, all cuts kept, and the incumbent moved whenever every second-stage slack is zero and the cost improves (pp. 3–4).
- Add two penalized slacks to each second-stage LP so that it is always solvable and one solve tells whether to add a feasibility cut (z_k > 0) or an optimality cut (v_k > 0) (pp. 3–4).
- When raising first-stage variables cannot destroy feasibility, repair an infeasible first-stage point by raising components through one auxiliary problem per infeasible scenario (p. 5).
