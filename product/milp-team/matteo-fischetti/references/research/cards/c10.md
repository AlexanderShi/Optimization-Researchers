### S145 · Integer (Linear) Optimization (Advances and Trends in Optimization with Engineering Applications, MOS-SIAM Series on Optimization, chapter 5, 2017, DOI 10.1137/1.9781611974683.ch5)
**Read level**: full. Whole text pp. 1-19 (author preprint): body pp. 1-16, reference list pp. 16-19 skimmed; Fig. 1 survives only as labels (p. 7) and the displayed formulas are partly broken in the text layer (pp. 2-5, 10), but the prose around each is intact.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Egon Balas (first author; Carnegie Mellon, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a 2017 handbook chapter for engineers, written in the thinning-out period of SKILL.md's trajectory (2014-2020). It reads as two voices joined: the cutting-plane history follows Balas's own line (refs [2], [4]-[12], pp. 5-9, 16-17) and the heuristics and how-to sections rest on Fischetti's papers (refs [13], [16], [17], [24]-[27], [34], cited pp. 9-11, 15, listed pp. 17-18). Who wrote which section is not stated, so this split is an inference.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry by modelling, not by a solver failure: linear and convex models cannot represent nonconvexities and discontinuities, and integer programming "is a universal tool" for them (p. 1); the running example is a production plan whose LP optimum violates thresholds, implications, mutual exclusivity and precedences (p. 1). The task is a survey for an engineering readership (venue line, p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: The argument is historical. Branch-and-bound was for about three decades the only practical tool, limited to 30-50 variables, while cutting-plane codes failed on numerical problems (p. 7); a "true revolution" between 1990 and 2005 came from embedding cutting planes in branch-and-bound (p. 8). Heuristics are a second line: special-structure heuristics, then metaheuristics, then the feasibility pump, then "a new generation of MIP heuristics" that call a black-box MIP solver on a neighbourhood defined by invalid constraints (local branching, RINS) (pp. 9-11). The chapter closes with a practical guide to running a MIP code: command mode, parameters, heuristic mode, seeds, parallel modes, callbacks (pp. 14-16).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP in the form min{c^T x : Ax ≥ b, x ≥ 0, x_j integer for j ∈ N1} (p. 1); 0-1 programming is "by far the most important special case" because thresholds, implications and the like are written with 0-1 variables, e.g. qδ ≤ x ≤ Qδ (p. 2). Model families shown: fixed charge, facility location, knapsack, set covering and partitioning (crew scheduling, medical diagnostics), matching, the TSP with subtour elimination (pp. 2-5). Formulation criterion: not the number of variables and constraints as in LP but the tightness of the LP relaxation, even against twice as many variables or constraints (pp. 3-4). Theory recalled without proof: Edmonds' odd-set description of the matching polytope (p. 4); Chvátal closure and rank, intersection cuts, and the Gomory cut as the intersection cut of a split (p. 6); the Farkas characterization of valid inequalities for a union of polyhedra and the sequential convexifiability of 0-1 programs, disjunctive rank n (p. 7). No model library is used; MIPLIB appears as the measure of progress (p. 8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Branch-and-cut generates cuts "as long as they 'work'" and branches when cut generation "stalls"; it became possible for general MIP once cuts generated at a node could be lifted to be valid at any node (p. 8). Implementation lessons recalled: rounds of cuts, one per fractional basic row, before reoptimizing curbed the numerical trouble of the one-cut-then-reoptimize loop, and Gomory cuts generated in rounds inside branch-and-bound became tractable [11] (p. 9); lift-and-project cuts reached commercial codes once the CGLP could be solved by pivoting in the LP tableau [12] (p. 9). The feasibility pump is described for pure 0-1 programs as alternating an L1 projection and a rounding through Δ(x*, x~) (p. 10); local branching as the invalid constraint Δ(x, x~) ≤ k, typically k = 10 or 20, explored "often heuristically" within a node limit (p. 10); RINS as fixing the variables on which LP solution and incumbent agree (pp. 10-11). Usage design: limits on time, nodes, solutions and gap (p. 14); defaults adapt the aggressiveness of heuristics and cuts automatically, but they aim at a proof of optimality, so a heuristic mode raises the heuristic frequency to every 1 or 10 nodes, tries the final polishing step with a short limit, and may turn cuts off, "note however that cuts can be beneficial for heuristics too" (pp. 14-15); a changed random seed gives a different search path (p. 15); parallel deterministic, opportunistic and distributed modes (p. 15); informative, lazy-constraint, heuristic, user-cut, solve, branch and node callbacks (pp. 15-16). Proof devices: n/a (results are recalled, not proved).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a: no experiment of the authors. Progress figures are quoted from solver developers' talks ("According to the developers of the commercial code CPLEX [15]", p. 9): CPLEX 1.2 (1991) against CPLEX 11.0 (2007) on 1,852 instances, a 29,530-fold speed-up with the solved share rising from 15% to 69%, and Gurobi 5.6 (2013) 21 times faster than Gurobi 1.0 (2009) (p. 9), sourced to an INFORMS meeting talk [15] and a Dagstuhl seminar [14] (p. 17). The seed advice is a "sometimes" with no data: ten 5-minute runs with different seeds against one 50-minute run (p. 15). Applications are quoted from Edelman entries: Netherlands Railways 2008, with $60 million of additional annual profit in 2007 (p. 11) and TURNI solving crew-scheduling instances in "a few hours" on a personal computer (p. 12), and TNT Express 2012, $207 million over seven years (p. 12); 53% of all Edelman finalists used MIP according to Nemhauser [38] (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: Self-cited on both sides. Balas's intersection cuts, disjunctive programming and lift-and-project carry the cutting-plane history and the revolution's "key impetus" (pp. 6-9); Fischetti's pump papers, local branching, the erraticism paper and the Netherlands Railways paper carry the heuristics and usage sections (pp. 10-11, 15; refs pp. 17-18). Local branching is claimed as "the first method embedding a MIP solver within a general MIP heuristic framework" (p. 10); RINS and Achterberg and Berthold's pump improvement are credited (pp. 10-11). Absent from the whole text (checked by search): Benders decomposition, symmetry, presolve and proximity search; column generation appears only inside TURNI (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Handbook register with strong adjectives ("An amazing variety", p. 1; "a miraculous tool", p. 7; "a true revolution", p. 8); numbers carry the progress claim (p. 9). One objection is answered in place: the exact solver inside a heuristic "may appear naive" but works when the invalid constraints simplify the MIP (p. 10). The how-to section is written as advice to a user, with each parameter's purpose (pp. 14-16), unusual in a survey. No conclusion section: the text ends on the list of callbacks (p. 16).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: exposition of history, heuristics, applications and usage for engineers (pp. 1-16). Limits are not discussed and no open problems or future directions are named (pp. 1-16). The formulation criterion (pp. 3-4) and the seed advice (p. 15) are given without the conditions under which they hold or fail.

**Method links**: Method 6 evidence ✅: the stock solver in command mode as a black box "is just what is needed" in many practical cases (p. 14); the MIP solver inside a heuristic is justified by the structural simplification that the invalid constraints bring, stronger preprocessing and node pruning (p. 10); re-aiming the stock solver by parameters (heuristic mode) is presented as the first resort, before programs and callbacks (pp. 14-15), which is also Heuristic 5's practitioner setting. Method 3 evidence ✅, for the resource half only: seeds are offered as a way to diversify the solver for heuristic search, several short seeded runs, in parallel where possible, credited to the erraticism paper [26] (p. 15); there is no bet on one run, no placebo and no data. Method 2 variant ⚠ (historical, joint voice, Balas's line): the same diagnosis, that cutting planes looked unworkable for three decades because of the one-cut-then-reoptimize loop rather than the cut family (pp. 7-9), but a different cure from Method 2's moved query point: rounds of cuts before reoptimizing, and branching when cuts stall (pp. 8-9). Method 1 contradiction ✗ (joint textbook voice): the leading formulation criterion is LP tightness, and the formulation with the higher LP optimum is preferred even with twice the variables or constraints (pp. 3-4), the opposite of Method 1 step 2; it agrees with Method 1's own limitation that the root LP bound is critical for exact work, and the lazy-constraint callback, presented as the way to keep a huge constraint family out of the initial model (pp. 15-16), is the device a Benders projection (Method 1 step 3) runs on (inference).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before writing a heuristic, run the stock solver in its heuristic mode: heuristic frequency every 1 or 10 nodes, the final polishing step with a short time limit, cuts off as one arm but not as a rule (pp. 14-15).
- When the target is a good incumbent rather than a proof, compare k short runs with different seeds against one run of the same total time (the chapter's example: ten 5-minute runs against one 50-minute run), in parallel when cores allow (p. 15).
- Keep a huge or complex constraint family out of the initial model and enforce it in the lazy-constraint callback, which must certify each rejected solution by a violated cut (pp. 15-16).
- Pick the parallel mode by purpose: deterministic for reproducible experiments, opportunistic for speed (p. 15).
- When a design looks naive (an exact solver inside a heuristic), name in the text the mechanism that makes it work, here the structural simplification that enables preprocessing and pruning (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "the formulation with the higher LP optimum is usually preferable" (p. 4)
- "may appear naive at first glance, but it turns out to be effective" (p. 10)

### S008 · Deep neural networks and mixed integer linear optimization (Constraints 23(3) 2018, DOI 10.1007/s10601-018-9285-6)
**Read level**: full. Whole text pp. 1-15 (author manuscript); Tables 1-3 read (pp. 12-13); Figures 1-4 survive only as captions (pp. 7-10); reference list read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Jason Jo (MILA and IVADO, Montreal, p. 1); Fischetti is the corresponding author (p. 1); Bengio and Lodi are thanked for discussions (p. 14) · **Place in trajectory**: the "machine learning from the MILP side" period of SKILL.md's trajectory (2015-2022), whose representative work it is there; the homepage lists S054 (the arXiv feasibility study) as its preliminary version (work-list note), and the text says an early version went to CPAIOR 2018 in November 2017 (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry by a structural observation and a feasibility question: for fixed parameters a ReLU (and max-pooling) network is a 0-1 MILP (abstract, p. 1), and indicator constraints of this kind "tend to produce very hard mixed-integer instances", so "the evaluation of the practical feasibility" of the model was one of the main motivations (pp. 4-5). Priority is conceded at once: a similar model was proposed independently, almost at the same time, in [2, 13, 15], so the claim moves to the applications and discussions (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Write each ReLU as w^T y + b = x − s with x, s ≥ 0 and one binary z with the indicator implications z = 1 → x ≤ 0 and z = 0 → s ≤ 0, rather than the bilinear complementarity xs ≤ 0, and let the solver turn the indicators into big-M rows from bounds M+ and M− (pp. 3-4); max pooling takes one binary per input with Σ z_i = 1 (p. 4). Then make the model solvable by bound tightening: scan the units by increasing layer; for unit (j, k) delete all units of the same and later layers and solve two MILPs, maximizing x and maximizing s; use the optimum, or the solver's optimistic estimate after a short limit, as the bound (p. 5). Uses: inputs of maximum activation for feature visualization, and optimal adversarial examples (pp. 6-7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: 0-1 MILP with indicator constraints (big-M internally) over continuous unit outputs; all weights, biases and objective costs fixed; input bounds lb^0 and ub^0 set by the inputs' physical meaning (p. 4). Structural facts used: fixing the input fixes every x and z except a z whose ReLU input is zero, so the model can never be infeasible and any input within its bounds extends in a unique way to a feasible solution (p. 5); with the weights as variables the products become bilinear, so the model is "not suited for training" (p. 6). Data: MNIST, 784 pixel inputs in [0, 1] and ten outputs; DNN1-DNN5 with 3-6 hidden layers of 8-20 ReLUs, trained for 50 epochs by SGD to 93-96% top-1 test accuracy (pp. 6, 11).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Bound tightening by auxiliary MILPs solved with the same solver (p. 5). The invariants that make it pay: the network is acyclic, so bounds tightened in one layer are used in all later ones; the bounds depend only on the a priori input bounds, not on the input, so they are computed once, saved in a file and reused for any later optimization of the same network; each layer's computations can be distributed without communication (p. 5). The cheap variant keeps validity: a short limit per bound computation, which the paper calls "mathematically correct" but possibly weaker, or stopping after the root node (p. 12). Adversarial model: the target digit d = (d~ + 5) mod 10 imposed by x^K_{d+1} ≥ 1.2 x^K_{j+1} (12), the L1 distance to the original figure by auxiliary variables d_j (13), optionally d_j ≤ 0.2 (pp. 7, 9). Proof devices: n/a beyond the uniqueness and acyclicity arguments above (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: CPLEX 12.7 on a 4-core notebook (Intel i7 2.3 GHz, 16 GB, GPU unused), 300 s per run, 100 runs per network and model, each on a different MNIST training point; metrics %solved, %gap, average nodes and wall-clock time, time-outs counted at 300 s; preprocessing time excluded from Table 1 (pp. 11-12). Basic against tightened ("improved") model, %solved: 100/100, 97/100, 64/100, 24/98, 7/67 for DNN1-DNN5 (Table 1, p. 12). The preprocessing cost is reported separately: negligible for the three smaller networks, 1,112 s and 4,913 s in total for DNN4 and DNN5 (p. 12); with a 1-second limit per bound it falls to 69.4 s and 72.6 s, while DNN5 drops from 67% to 57% solved (Table 2, p. 13). A run to a 1% gap with a 3,600 s limit: DNN5 hits the limit 81 times with the basic model and 11 times with the improved one (Table 3, p. 13). The feature-visualization models solve within 1 s with tightening and more than an order of magnitude slower without (p. 6). One run per instance, no seeds; arithmetic averages (pp. 11-13). Gradient-based visualizers and attacks are not run; their weakness is reported from the authors' own "preliminary implementation" (p. 6). Larger networks such as (30, 20, 10, 10, 10, 8, 8, 8) take an hour or more (p. 14).
- **D6 Positioning**: Concedes the model to [2, 13, 15] (p. 2); takes the bound tightening "very much in the spirit of" the indicator-constraint paper of Belotti et al. [1], his own among its co-authors (p. 5); cites his SVM-training paper [5] for the view that insisting on proven optimality in training is "not at all a clever policy" because of overfitting (p. 6); names local branching, Rothberg's polishing and proximity search as the refinement heuristics of a hybrid scheme (pp. 5, 14). Against the learning literature: greedy ascent for maximum activations can be trapped in local optima (p. 6), and other adversarial methods impose a perturbation pattern such as blurring [15] and produce changes that look like random noise (pp. 7, 11).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: The priority concession comes in the introduction, before the model (p. 2). The contribution is framed as "a very first step" (p. 13), the claims carry first-person hedges ("In our view", p. 6), and results are "striking" and "quite encouraging" (p. 12), while the loss with weaker bounds on DNN5 is described as "without deteriorating too much" (Table 2 caption, p. 13). Wordplay marks the scope: the model is "(un)fortunately not suited for training" (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a model discussion, a bound-tightening preprocessing, two applications and a computational study on small networks (pp. 13-14). Limits stated: small networks only, larger ones take an hour or more (p. 14); unusable for training (p. 6); a hybrid of gradient descent and MILP refinement is proposed but "not investigated" (p. 5). Future work: less computational effort for the exact model, heuristics for adversarial examples on large and convolutional networks, new deep-learning uses of the model (p. 14).

**Method links**: Method 6 evidence ✅: the sub-decision that makes the model solvable, how large each unit's input can be, is written as auxiliary MILPs and solved with the same solver, even heuristically (a short limit or the root node only); it is moved to a one-off preprocessing step reused for every query (p. 5) and cut to a 1-second version where it is slow (p. 12): steps 2 and 5 of the method; the first question put to the solver is whether the plain model is practical at all (pp. 4-5, 11). Method 1 variant ⚠: each bound is computed on a thinned model with the later units deleted (p. 5), but the main lever is strengthening the big-M links, not removing variables (Table 1, p. 12). Method 4 variant ⚠: the add-on's cost is excluded from Table 1 but reported, then cut to a cheap valid version whose loss is printed (pp. 12-13), close to tying an add-on's budget to its benefit; yet the only arms are his own two models, and the learning baselines are not run (p. 6). Method 3 is not tested here: one run per instance and no seeds (pp. 11-13), with effects far larger than seed noise would plausibly explain (DNN4 24% against 98% solved, p. 12; inference). New-pattern candidate: the defect turned into the mechanism; overfitting the given network is "a desired property" where optimality is wanted, as for adversarial examples, while it is a defect in training (p. 6; compare S133 p. 2 in this batch).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Tighten the big-M bounds of a layered model by auxiliary MILPs on the truncated model (the current unit and all earlier layers), one maximization per bounded variable, accepting the solver's optimistic bound after a short limit as a valid bound (pp. 5, 12).
- When a preprocessing step depends only on a priori data, compute it once per model, store it in a file and reuse it for every query; report its cost separately, together with a cheap variant (exact against 1 s per bound) in its own table (pp. 5, 12-13).
- Model ReLU and max pooling with an x − s split and indicator constraints rather than bilinear complementarity, and let the solver derive the big-M values from the bounds (pp. 3-4).
- Exploit a model that cannot be infeasible: any random or gradient-found input extends to a feasible solution, so refinement heuristics (local branching, polishing, proximity search) can start from it (p. 5).
- Report %solved, %gap, nodes and time with time-outs counted at the limit, plus a second run to a 1% gap under a longer limit with the number of time-outs (pp. 11-13).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore we cannot claim the model is new." (p. 2)
- "In those cases, indeed, overfitting the given DNN is actually a desired property." (p. 6)

### S133 · Faster SGD training by minibatch persistency (arXiv preprint 2018, arXiv 1806.07353)
**Read level**: full. Whole text pp. 1-7 (arXiv v1, 19 Jun 2018, marked "Preprint. Work in progress.", p. 1); Figures 1-5 survive only as captions and axis labels (pp. 3-6), so only the numbers stated in the prose were read; references read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Iacopo Mandatelli (Padova, with a student e-mail address, p. 1) and Domenico Salvagnin (Padova, p. 1); Fischetti is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the machine-learning period (2015-2022); SKILL.md Method 5 names this preprint as the claim he re-tested in 2026 under pre-registration (arXiv 2609.13922); this card reads only the 2018 text.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry by a hardware pressure and an unexamined default: large minibatches exploit GPUs but converge slowly and generalize poorly (abstract, p. 1); the literature adjusts the learning rate for them (pp. 1-2) but takes for granted a "disposable minibatch", changed at every SGD iteration (p. 2). The question: does reusing the same minibatch for K consecutive iterations speed up training without hurting generalization (p. 2)?
- **D2 Core idea**: One new parameter, the persistency K, with K = 1 "the standard rule" (p. 2). The stated status is a "computational conjecture": a large minibatch is a significant sample of the training set, so one can afford "slightly overfitting" it (abstract, p. 1; p. 2); a side gain is less data loading into GPU memory (p. 2). A follow-up idea, the learning rate k·µ at the k-th use of a minibatch, is tried in preliminary runs only (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: n/a for MILP. The learning setting: CIFAR-10 split into 50,000 training and 10,000 test examples, and a reduced AlexNet (five convolutional layers with stated kernel sizes and channels, 2x2 max pooling, 256 hidden units in the fully connected layer) (p. 2); minibatch sizes m = 32, 256 and 512 (p. 3). The claim's scope is stated as "at least in the setting we considered" (p. 2) and "the specific setting" of AlexNet on CIFAR-10 (p. 5).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: SGD with momentum and cross-entropy loss; the minibatch gradient is the sum, not the mean, of the m example gradients (p. 2), so at a fixed µ the step grows with m (my inference; the paper does not discuss it). 100 epochs in PyTorch with µ = 0.001 and momentum γ = 0.5, fixed a priori for every m and K and "not viewed as hyper-parameters to tune"; batch normalization, dropout and data augmentation off, to isolate K (pp. 2-3). Proof devices: n/a.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: One GPU (Nvidia GTX 1080 Ti), wall-clock seconds (p. 2); test accuracy and test loss plotted against both time and epochs, because each epoch evaluates every example K times and epochs alone would flatter K > 1 (p. 3). For m = 32, K = 5 took about 2,000 s against 700 s for K = 1 and overfit earlier, while its best accuracy, 0.64450, came after 30 epochs and about 600 s against 0.63640 at epoch 80 for K = 1 (p. 3). For m = 256 the time hardly grew with K; for m = 512, "K = 5 is the clear winner" (p. 4). Final accuracies of about 60% are "definitely not competitive with the state of the art" (p. 3). The same seed for all runs, which the paper calls deterministic, one run per configuration, no spread, one dataset and one network (pp. 2, 5). The adaptive learning-rate runs are preliminary, and those for m = 256 are not reported (p. 5).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Masters and Luschi's small-batch study, "very much in the spirit of" [9], which finds the best results for this setting at m ≤ 8 (pp. 2-3); sits next to learning-rate linear scaling and warm-up [2] and other large-batch work [5, 8, 13] (pp. 1-2), and cites [13] on the drawbacks of a learning rate independent of the minibatch size, which is its own setting (p. 3). The adaptive rule is called "reminiscent of" cyclical learning rates [11] (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Status labels in the abstract: "computational conjecture" and "The lesson learned is" (p. 1), repeated with a scope clause in the introduction (p. 2). Each limit is stated where it bites: absolute accuracy not competitive (p. 3), possibly worse behaviour in other settings (p. 5). The verdicts are mild ("rather encouraging", p. 4; "promising", p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a one-parameter training device and a small experiment (pp. 2-5). Limits stated: one dataset and network (p. 5), learning rate and momentum not tuned (pp. 2-3), low absolute accuracy (p. 3). Not stated as a limit: a single seed and no repeated runs (p. 2). Future work: other datasets and networks, adaptive learning-rate strategies (p. 5).

**Method links**: Method 5 evidence ✅: this is the 2018 side of the re-test that SKILL.md describes; the text already names its untuned learning rate (pp. 2-3) and its single setting (p. 5), the weaknesses a later re-test has to answer. Method 3 contradiction ✗: one seed for all runs and one run per configuration (p. 2); differences such as a best accuracy of 0.64450 against 0.63640 (p. 3) are of a size a seed change could plausibly produce, but no spread was measured (inference). Method 4 variant ⚠: the measure is chosen so as not to favour the new arm (time reported next to epochs, p. 3) and every K runs with the same fixed settings (p. 2); but the baseline is not what a practitioner would run with large minibatches (learning-rate scaling and warm-up [2], pp. 1-2), which the paper itself points out (p. 3). Also Heuristic 1 (the field's tacit default, the disposable minibatch, made a parameter, p. 2) and Heuristic 7 (conjecture and lesson labels, p. 1). New-pattern candidate: the defect turned into the mechanism, "slightly overfitting" a large minibatch as the source of the speed-up (pp. 1-2; compare S008 p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn an assumption every published method takes for granted into a named parameter whose standard value is one arm (K = 1) (p. 2).
- When one arm does more work per epoch or iteration, plot the results against wall-clock time as well as against epochs or iterations (p. 3).
- Strip a pipeline to its bare algorithm to isolate one parameter (no batch normalization, dropout or augmentation; fixed learning rate and momentum) and say what this costs in absolute performance (pp. 2-3); the same choice makes the baseline unrepresentative, which a later re-test has to address (SKILL.md Method 5).
- Put the claim's status ("computational conjecture") and its scope ("at least in the setting we considered") in the abstract and the introduction (pp. 1-2).

**Quotes**:
- "The computational conjecture here is that a large minibatch contains a significant sample" (p. 1)
- "initial random seed (affecting, in particular, the random weight initialization) was kept the same" (p. 2)

### S079 · A branch-and-cut algorithm for mixed-integer bilinear programming (European Journal of Operational Research 282(2) 2020, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2019.09.043)
**Read level**: full. Whole text pp. 1-21 (author preprint, "February 22, 2019; Revised, 20 September 2019", p. 1); Algorithms 1-2, Tables 1-4 and the captions of Figures 1-4 read (the curves survive only as axis ticks); reference list read.
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Michele Monaci (Bologna, p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the 2014-2020 period, carrying his MILP callback practice into MINLP; the acknowledgements credit Dagstuhl Seminar 18081 on MINLP (February 2018) "for inspiring discussions" and Salvagnin for help with SCIP (p. 19). SKILL.md cites this paper under Method 6 (a vanilla code with intersection cuts implemented but unused) and under Research Taste (BIL20 p. 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry by reformulation and by tool: every bilinear or quadratic term is isolated in an equation x_r = x_p x_q (5), so that dropping (5) leaves a MILP, the "bilinear relaxation", and any MIQP can be written this way (pp. 1-2). The paper names no failure of existing global solvers (SCIP, BARON, ANTIGONE, COUENNE and others are listed, p. 3); its premise is that an effective MILP branch-and-cut solver can carry the search on the bilinear relaxation, with (5) enforced by cuts and branching (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Solve the bilinear relaxation with a stock branch-and-cut MILP solver; enforce (5) by locally valid McCormick and gradient cuts generated on the fly; branch on integer variables by the solver's own rule whenever possible, and use spatial branching "only as a last resort", when the current solution is integral but bilinear infeasible (p. 3). Two new pieces: a branching threshold θ = x* ± δ chosen so that the bound change in one child and a McCormick inequality in the other are violated by the same δ, which gives δ = ρ*/(1 + u_y − y*) with ρ* = z* − x*y*, the variable of maximum δ being chosen (pp. 5-6, Algorithms 1-2); and intersection cuts whose convex set is "bilinear-free" instead of lattice-free, built by simulating a 2-way (S1) or 4-way (S2) branching and taking in each branch the most violated McCormick inequality (pp. 7-8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIBLP (1)-(5) with general integers allowed, squares x_p = x_q allowed, and finite bounds on every factor variable, possibly implied by other constraints (p. 2); general MIQP is strongly NP-hard (p. 2). The method needs a bounded root bilinear relaxation, so of the 768 MINLPlib instances in .lp format those with an unbounded one are removed, leaving 620 (p. 11). Correctness rests on recalled results: the McCormick inequalities describe the convex hull of z = xy over a box (p. 4); intersection cuts need the separated point to be a vertex of the current relaxation and are only locally valid (p. 7). Many instances are small: of the 408 solved by at least one method, 144 (188) have at most 50 (100) variables after reformulation (p. 16).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: CPLEX 12.8 with callbacks (p. 9). The lazy-constraint callback checks bilinear feasibility, tries a McCormick or gradient cut first and then an intersection cut from S1 and then S2, at most one per violated equation, and discards numerically unreliable cuts; bilinear-infeasible solutions from CPLEX's heuristics, which carry no LP basis, are simply rejected (p. 9). The user-cut callback is not installed by default, so fractional points get only CPLEX's own cuts (p. 10). The problem type is set to MILP and a branching callback applies the bilinear rule only when CPLEX's own branching condition is undefined (p. 10). The price of callbacks: dual and nonlinear presolve reductions off, and all preprocessing off (CPX_PARAM_PREIND = 0) when intersection cuts need the node basis (p. 9). Numerical device: intersection-cut coefficients come from the original data through the disjunctive 3-step procedure, so inaccurate multipliers u_i can reduce the violation but not the validity (pp. 7-8). Tolerances: ε = 10^-6, and θ moved to the midpoint when within 10ε of a bound (p. 6). Pieces tried and removed: more elaborate bilinear-free sets, removed "to keep our implementation as clean as possible" (p. 9); McCormick inequalities added as local constraints when child nodes are created, not significantly better (pp. 12-13); fractional separation at the root ("root-only"), 64 time-outs against 36 (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: C code, Intel Xeon E3-1220V2 at 3.10 GHz, 16 GB, single thread, 3,600 s (pp. 10-11). Arms: SCIP 5.0.1 on the quadratic model (CPLEX 12.8 as LP solver, IPOPT 3.12.9 as NLP solver), basic (new branching plus McCormick cuts) and with-IC (p. 10). Common settings: SCIP's gap tolerances set to CPLEX's defaults, CPLEX's integrality and feasibility tolerances set to SCIP's 10^-6, CPLEX on one thread with aggressive cuts and strong branching (pp. 10-11); every arm receives the optimal or best-known solution "to reduce performance variability" (p. 10). Preliminary: the new branching rule against Belotti et al.'s rule (α = 0.25) wins 222 to 93 on 339 instances, "not striking for the hardest cases" (p. 11). Final, 620 instances: solved 378 (SCIP), 328 (basic) and 323 (with-IC); shifted geometric means (shift 1 s) of 34.45, 53.04 and 67.93 s (Table 1, p. 13). On the 248 instances all arms solve, classes C1-C5 are set by the slowest arm's time (p. 14); the text reads basic as better than SCIP in every class but C1 and C4 (Table 2, p. 15), and nested classes G1-G5 show the trend on harder instances (Table 3, p. 15). Intersection cuts help on nine selected instances, e.g. turkey in 0.11 s against 3,600 s for basic, a behaviour confirmed with 5 random seeds (Table 4, p. 16). Small instances stay in every statistic; the subsets with n > 50 and n > 100 are shown in Figure 3 (pp. 16-17). Parallel: 4 opportunistic threads against 1 on 349 instances, time-outs 19 against 2, and speed-ups above 4 "mainly due to performance variability" (pp. 16-17).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on McCormick, RLT, Audet et al., Tawarmalani et al., Adams and Sherali, Gupte et al., Bienstock, and intersection cuts for polynomial optimization [8] (p. 2); intersection cuts are credited to Balas [6] and Conforti, Cornuéjols and Zambelli [14], their disjunctive interpretation to Glover [22, 23], with details in his own bilevel paper [18] (p. 7). The rival is SCIP alone among the eight solvers listed (pp. 3, 10), and the text says that a direct comparison is not possible because the technologies differ: SCIP was only meant to show that basic "is not far from the state of the art" (p. 13).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: "Our order of business" opens a derivation and the experiments (pp. 5, 11). The abstract says the method without intersection cuts "is competitive" with a state-of-the-art solver (p. 1), a claim that the full test set qualifies (SCIP solves 50 more instances, p. 13) and the all-solved subset supports, a subset the text itself calls biased against SCIP (p. 14). The negative result is put as field experience: such cut classes are "not beneficial when a large and varied testbed is considered", a "(somehow frustrating) experience" (p. 16). SKILL.md's Taste 3 quotes a 2018 deck calling SCIP the more robust solver; the paper's own wording is the milder one above (pp. 1, 13).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithm and implementation: a callback-based exact code for MIBLP, a branching rule and a family of intersection cuts (p. 18). Limits stated: intersection cuts rarely pay on average and force preprocessing off (p. 16); a bounded root relaxation is required (p. 11); SCIP is better on very easy instances thanks to bilinear presolve and an NLP solver (p. 15); a better tailing-off strategy might change the root-only result (p. 13). Future work: other bilinear-free sets, and instance features that decide per instance whether to separate intersection cuts (p. 18).

**Method links**: Method 6 evidence ✅: the stock MILP solver runs the whole search on the relaxation; the code steps in only where the solver is blind (lazy cuts for (5); spatial branching only when CPLEX's branching is undefined) and leaves the user-cut callback off (pp. 3, 9-10); the vanilla arm is the recommended one (pp. 13-16). Method 1 evidence ✅ (step 5, in add-then-drop form): three pieces were built, measured and removed because they did not pay (pp. 9, 12-13). Method 4 evidence ✅: tolerances and threads aligned across solvers (pp. 10-11); classes by a rule in which the arms play an indistinguishable role (p. 14), the same device and nearly the same sentence as the erraticism paper quoted under Method 4; the subset's bias against SCIP disclosed (p. 14); instances easy for SCIP kept because removing them would be unfair (p. 16); SCIP's wins printed (pp. 13, 15). The lapses are a rival of a different technology and a single rival (pp. 3, 13). Method 3 variant ⚠: variability is reduced by giving every arm the optimal value rather than measured; seeds are used only to confirm the nine selected wins (pp. 10, 16) and variability is invoked to read the parallel speed-ups (p. 17); no placebo arm. Method 2 variant ⚠: the one loop variant tried at the root was dropped on its time-outs, with "a more clever tailing-off strategy" named and not tried (p. 13), and the intersection cuts can only separate LP vertices (p. 7), so the closing verdict on the cut family (p. 16) rests on vertex separation alone. Also Heuristic 10 in a variant form: validity built to survive numerical error rather than re-checked afterwards (pp. 7-9). New-pattern candidate: make cut validity independent of tableau accuracy (pp. 7-8, with [18]).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Solve the relaxation that is a MILP with the stock solver and enforce the remaining constraints on integral candidates in the lazy-constraint callback; let the solver branch, and take over in a branching callback only when its own branching condition is undefined (pp. 3, 9-10).
- Choose a spatial-branching threshold by balancing the children: x ≤ x* − δ in one, a McCormick inequality violated by the same δ in the other, so δ = ρ*/(1 + u_y − y*); branch on the largest δ and fall back to the midpoint near a bound (pp. 5-6).
- Build the convex set of an intersection cut by simulating a k-way branching and reversing the most violated valid inequality of each branch (p. 8); compute the cut by the disjunctive 3-step procedure from the original data, so that numerical error weakens the cut but never invalidates it (pp. 7-8).
- In a cut study across solvers, give every arm the optimal value, align tolerances, gap limits and threads, define difficulty classes by the slowest arm's time, and add nested classes G_k = C_k ∪ ... ∪ C_5 to read the trend on harder instances (pp. 10-11, 14-15).
- Keep instances that favour the rival in the main statistics and show the subsets without them (n > 50, n > 100) separately (p. 16, Fig. 3).
- Confirm a table of selected wins with five seeds and label it as selected (Table 4, p. 16).
- Read parallel speed-ups beyond the thread count as performance variability, not as parallel efficiency (p. 17).

**Quotes**:
- "the competing methods play an indistinguishable role in the class definition" (p. 14)
- "would have been unfair to SCIP as it would have produced a biased dataset" (p. 16)

### S076 · Learning to search in local branching (Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence 36(4) 2022, DOI 10.1609/aaai.v36i4.20294; arXiv 2112.02195)
**Read level**: full, of the extended version arXiv v2. The file is arXiv 2112.02195v2 (3 Aug 2022), retitled "Revisiting local branching with a machine learning lens" and stated to be "an extension of [37]", the AAAI paper (pp. 1, 24); whole text pp. 1-25 read, Tables 1-10 read, Figures 1-5 surviving only as captions (pp. 3, 10, 12, 21, 22), reference list skimmed. By the paper's own account the conference version "did not consider" the time limit per neighbourhood, so §4.3, §5.3.2 and the in-solver §6 are new in v2 (p. 3); whether the rest equals the AAAI text was not checked. Cite as [S076 arXiv v2 p. N].
**Role**: core · **Co-authors**: Defeng Liu (first author, CERC, Polytechnique Montréal) and Andrea Lodi (CERC and Cornell Tech) (p. 1); "All authors contributed to the study and development" (p. 25), so Fischetti's share is unknown; SKILL.md's lineage records that Liu was not his doctoral student · **Place in trajectory**: the end of the machine-learning period (2015-2022); his 2003 local branching, co-authored with Lodi, revisited with learning; SKILL.md Method 5 lists it as practice (the fixed neighbourhood size learned per instance).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry by a parameter of his own method: in the original local branching (LB) the neighbourhood size is "mostly initialized by a small value", a conservative setting with "a lot of space for improvement" (p. 2), and proximity search [19] had already suggested that ad-hoc tuning of the size can pay (p. 2); the best size is "strongly dependent on the particular MILP instance" (p. 2; Fig. 1 on a set-covering and an independent-set instance, p. 3). Formal statement: choose k to minimize an unknown cost C(p, x̄; A_k) that trades speed against quality (pp. 5-6); black-box search over k is too slow for online use, which is why the original LB fixed a small k and adapted it by a deterministic rule (p. 6). The data argument: instances of the same problem are solved repeatedly (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Learn the knob, not the solution: predict an instance-wise scalar (the neighbourhood size, and in v2 also the time limit per LB subproblem) instead of variable values, as other learned neighbourhood-search methods do (p. 4). Phase 1, scaled regression for the first iteration: k0 = φ0 · k^0 with k^0 = Δ(x^0, x̄), the LB distance of the LP optimum from the incumbent, since any larger k is "likely to be useless" (following [19]); a graph neural network on the bipartite variable-constraint graph predicts φ0 ∈ (0, 1) (pp. 8-9, Algorithm 1). Phase 2, reinforcement learning for the later iterations: actions {+kstep, 0, −kstep; reset} on the last k, reward r_k = o_imp · (t_max − t_elaps), REINFORCE policy gradient (pp. 10-11, Algorithm 2). v2 adds an outer loop on the time limit with actions {∗tstep, 0, /tstep; reset} and a penalty when a subproblem is unsolved without improvement, trained with the k-policy held fixed (pp. 12-13, Algorithm 3). What stays as in 2003: each neighbourhood is explored by "a generic black-box MILP solver" and the scheme is exact in nature (p. 6), with the branching Δ(x, x̄) ≤ k on the left and Δ(x, x̄) ≥ k + 1 on the right (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MILP with binary, general-integer and continuous variables, but binaries required by LB (an extension to general integers is pointed to [7]); the asymmetric constraint (6) for solutions with small binary support (p. 5); an initial incumbent required, either SCIP's first solution or its best solution at the end of the root node (p. 14). Benchmarks: set covering (5,000 rows, 2,000 columns), maximum independent set on Barabási-Albert graphs with 1,000 nodes, combinatorial auctions (4,000 items, 2,000 bids), 200 instances each, split 70/10/20; 40 instances each of doubled size, for evaluation only; 35 GISP instances; 29 binary MIPLIB 2017 instances "also evaluated by the original LB heuristic" (pp. 13-14). Training: the regression on SC, MIS and CA, separately or mixed, and the RL policies on SC only (pp. 15-16); the text names the regression's training sets once as "SC, MIS and LCS" (p. 16) and once as SC, MIS and CA (p. 18).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Labels come from a grid search over φ0 at resolution 0.01 under the cost α·t_scaled + (1 − α)·o_scaled with α = 0.5; the best label is typically the largest k whose subproblem still solves to optimality within the time limit (pp. 7, 14, 17). The network has 64-neuron hidden layers, two half-convolutions and a sigmoid output (pp. 14-15) on twelve variable, constraint and edge features (Table 1, p. 15); the RL policies are linear, with seven instance-independent solve-status inputs and four outputs (Table 2, pp. 15-16). Settings: learning rates picked from 10^-5 to 10^-1 (10^-4 for the regression, 10^-2 and 10^-1 for the two policies), 300 epochs, kstep = 0.5, tstep = 2, β1 = β2 = 1, and 10 s per LB iteration for all compared algorithms (p. 17); Python 3.7 with PyTorch, PyTorch Geometric, PySCIPOpt 3.1.1 and SCIP "7.01" (p. 17); code public (pp. 17, 25). Inside SCIP (v2, §6): LB at the root only, or where the first incumbent appears, against LB every f nodes, with f = 100 chosen from {1, 10, 100, 1000} (p. 21). Proof devices: n/a; the bound k^0 is a heuristic argument borrowed from [19] (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: The baseline lb-base is "the original local branching algorithm" on SCIP, with a 60 s limit per instance (p. 17); neither its initial k nor the default k of lb-rl is stated (pp. 9, 17). Metrics: the primal gap against the optimal or best-known solution and the primal integral [9], both reported as geometric means with no shift stated (pp. 16-20). On SC, MIS and CA the learned variants beat the baseline in most cells, and lb-srmrl is best in most cases (Tables 3-4, p. 18); larger instances, GISP and MIPLIB follow (Tables 5-8, p. 19). With a 600 s limit on GISP and MIPLIB the time-limit adaptation lowers the primal integral in all four columns (Table 9, p. 20), but in final primal gap the baseline still wins on MIPLIB from the root start, 3.389 against 5.273 and 4.396 (Table 10, p. 20). Other losses in the tables: on MIS from the root start the baseline has the lowest final gap of all five arms (Table 4, p. 18), and lb-rl's primal integral on MIPLIB from the first solution is worse than the baseline's (Table 7, p. 19), while the text says that all the learning-based algorithms outperform the baseline (p. 19). The in-solver results appear only as a curve, with the root-only placement best (pp. 21-22). Not reported anywhere: hardware, threads, seeds or any spread; no competing heuristic (RINS, ALNS or SCIP's own neighbourhood search) in §5 (pp. 13-21).
- **D6 Positioning**: Two lines of learning for MILP are surveyed, decisions inside the solver and learned primal heuristics (p. 4); learned neighbourhood-search methods are criticized for variable-wise predictions that meet the problem's combinatorial difficulty and limit generalization, for static features only, and in one case for relying on parallel computation (p. 4). LB is placed as "one of the first methods using a generic MILP solver as a black-box tool inside a heuristic framework" (p. 2); proximity search [19] supplies both the motivation for tuning k and the bound k^0 (pp. 2, 8).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: Generalization is the headline, "remarkably, across instances" (abstract, p. 1; p. 20), explained by the instance-wise design (p. 18). Claims are firm ("significantly outperform", p. 18) and the losses are visible only in the tables (pp. 18-20). Exactness is stated and set aside in a parenthesis: turning the scheme into a heuristic is "trivial (and generally preferrable)" (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: a learning framework for two LB parameters, with public code (pp. 21-22, 25). Limits stated: an initial solution is needed (p. 22), binaries are needed (p. 5). Not stated as limits: the absence of seeds, the baseline's settings, the final-gap losses (pp. 17-20). Future work: hybrid frameworks that learn to coordinate several refinement heuristics (p. 22).

**Method links**: Method 5 evidence ✅: one of his own 2003 design choices, a small fixed neighbourhood adapted conservatively, is reopened (pp. 2, 6), the original LB is kept as the baseline arm (p. 17), and the MIPLIB arm uses binary instances also evaluated in the original LB paper (p. 14); a variant in form, since the revisit is a learning study whose first author is at the CERC in Montréal with Lodi (p. 1), not a re-test of a published claim with the implementers' code read. Method 6 evidence ✅: the learned parts set two scalars and leave the black-box solver to explore every neighbourhood (pp. 6, 17); inside SCIP the cheapest placement, the root only, works best (p. 22). Method 3 contradiction ✗: no seeds, no spread and no hardware for a comparison of learned heuristics across five benchmarks (pp. 13-21); joint work, Fischetti's share unknown. Method 4 variant ⚠: every arm uses the same solver and the same 10 s per iteration (p. 17), but the baseline's settings are not given (p. 17), the in-solver frequency f is tuned without saying on which instances (p. 21), and the losses in Tables 4, 7 and 10 go unmentioned in the prose (pp. 18-20). New-pattern candidate: learn the knob, not the variables (pp. 4, 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Scale an integer control parameter by an instance-derived bound so that the learning target lies in (0, 1) and transfers across sizes: k = φ·Δ(x^LP, x̄), with Δ(x^LP, x̄) the size from which on the LB constraint no longer cuts off the LP optimum (p. 8).
- Generate labels for a parameter predictor by a one-dimensional grid search (resolution 0.01) under a cost that mixes scaled time and scaled objective (α = 0.5) (pp. 7, 14, 17).
- Give an RL controller relative actions on the last value plus a reset ({+kstep, 0, −kstep; reset}, kstep = 0.5) and only instance-independent solve-status features, so that a linear policy trained on one class can run on others (pp. 10, 15-16).
- Train nested controllers one at a time, holding the inner policy fixed while the outer one learns (p. 13).
- Report every heuristic from two starting incumbents (the solver's first solution and its root-end best) and with both the primal integral and the final primal gap, which can rank the arms differently (Tables 9-10, p. 20).
- Test a stand-alone neighbourhood scheme also as an in-solver primal heuristic, at the root only and every f nodes (p. 21).
