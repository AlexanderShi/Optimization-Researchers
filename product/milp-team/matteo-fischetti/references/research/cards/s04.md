- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The 2001 'Church Circles' production model (crews on fewer routes, train changes in principle only at the meal break, aimed at punctuality) met strikes (p. 5); the complaints were monotony and an unfair split of aggression-prone lines among depots (p. 6). No proof device.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: One real case (14,678 trips, all driver work of a Tuesday): the first pass takes 4:06 h on a Pentium 4 at 3.0 GHz and gives 971 duties, the best solution (965 duties) comes in pass 3 after 14:47 h, and the Lagrangian bound stabilizes after about 45 minutes (plotted; no bound value or gap printed) (pp. 15–16); impact for 2004: work +3.2%, duties +1.2%, an initial saving of about 2% (over $7 million a year), of which $4.8 million was realized because part was given back for acceptance (p. 17).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: An application and practice contribution, claimed as the first time a large European railway used OR actively in crew scheduling (p. 18); punctuality rose from about 80% to about 85%, which the authors do not attribute to the model alone (p. 18); traffic control finds real-time repair of the more complex duties after disruptions harder (p. 18); the savings estimates are called "quite conservative" (p. 17); next: a 3.5% cut for 2005 through 1% intrinsic gains and 2.5% from relaxed rules, such as 20 instead of 25 minutes for a train change (p. 17).

- **D3 Problem class and data model**: DCVRP on a complete undirected graph with a fixed number k of routes, capacity C and route-duration limit D (pp. 1–2); coordinates assumed for the sweep start (pp. 9, 16); with triangular costs constraint (3) may become ≥, a set-covering ILP (p. 12). - **D8 Contributions and limits**: Algorithmic; 12 improved best-known solutions (p. 1). - Keep the exact but slow model as a "perfect model" arm and judge each fast approximation by its distance to it at equal efficiency loss (pp. 8, 12–13).
- Freeze the precedences of a good nominal solution so that robustness is trained by an LP over the times (pp. 4, 7).
- Weight recourse slacks by the number of downstream events (k + 1) so that a slim model mimics delay propagation (p. 10).
- Add a static structural diagnostic (WAD of the buffers) to explain why one method is more robust than another (pp. 13–14).

**Quotes**:
- "independent from the optimization model itself, so that it can be of general applicability and allows one to compare solutions coming from different methods." (p. 6)
- "For realistic instances and number of scenarios this model becomes very time consuming (if not impossible) to solve–hence we called it “fat”." (p. 8)

### S070 · Embedding {0, ½}-Cuts in a Branch-and-Cut Framework: A Computational Study (INFORMS Journal on Computing 19(2) 2007, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1050.0162)
**Read level**: full (all 16 file pages of the "Preliminary Draft, October 28, 2003", titled "Embedding Cuts in a Branch&Cut Framework: a Computational Study with {0, 1/2}-Cuts"; the IJOC article was not read, so its testbed and numbers may differ). First-page check: Andreello, Caprara, Fischetti match (p. 1); the title wording differs as given.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Giuseppe Andreello, Alberto Caprara · **Position in trajectory**: builds on Caprara and Fischetti's {0, ½}-cuts (1996) and on the mod-k separation paper [4, 5] (pp. 15–16); S118 later cites it as the direction to follow for using easy-to-compute cuts effectively (S118 p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: "Paradoxically", a separator that finds many violated cuts can make branch-and-cut slower, so designers skip cuts or cut only at the root (p. 1); the first embedding of {0, ½}-cuts into CPLEX 8.1 was not significantly faster than CPLEX, although the cuts looked better than those of other general-purpose methods (p. 1; Table 1, p. 11).
- **D2 Core idea**: Leave the separator untouched as a black box and put an own cut pool and a selection policy between it and the LP: efficacy (distance of x* from the cut hyperplane) above an adaptive threshold, a cap on pairwise parallelism among the cuts added at the node, a global cap of cut_factor times the initial rows, separation in 5 root rounds and then at every fourth backtrack, and recombination of earlier cuts (pp. 6–8, Algorithm 1). The separator: relax the rows to at most two odd coefficients by LU-weakenings and find a minimum-weight odd cycle (pp. 3–5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: ILPs with integer data, rows scaled to relatively prime integers (pp. 2, 4); a testbed with strong combinatorial structure: DIMACS SAT instances (given a random objective, the sum of a random half of the constraint left-hand sides, stopped at the first integer solution), their MAXSAT versions, and random linear ordering instances with n = 30 generated as Reinelt suggested so that the root gap is significant, since LOLIB instances have a negligible one (pp. 9–10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Theorem 1: {0, ½} multipliers correspond one to one to odd Eulerian cycles, so separation becomes a shortest odd cycle in a doubled graph (Dijkstra), O(mn³) with reductions R1–R3 and a dynamic program for the minimum-slack weakenings (pp. 3–5); a CPLEX cut callback with its own pool; cuts added as global and never purged, following CPLEX's conservative "cut and dive" strategy, which the authors reconstructed from its documentation and log files (pp. 5–7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Baseline = default CPLEX 8.1 with a dummy cut callback (a callback rules out irreversible transformations, so all codes lose them) (p. 10); classes easy (under 5 s for both codes), regular, hard (solved by one only) and impossible, 1,800 s on an AMD Athlon XP 2400+, geometric-mean speed-ups as in Bixby et al. (p. 10); tuning on a reduced subset of 143 instances with one-at-a-time disabling (Tables 1–7, pp. 11–14); final 513 instances: 012* solves 57 instances that CPLEX does not and misses 14 that CPLEX solves, speed-up 2.07 on regular and 17.61 on hard instances (Table 8, p. 14); no seeds.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Lesson: cut-selection criteria are "at least as important" as separation and depend on the host framework (p. 15); a labelled conjecture that large pools fed by fast heuristics plus strict selection can beat a few deep cuts, offered as one explanation of Gomory cuts' practical success (p. 15). Limits: no LP purging tried (p. 7); LINORD is easy for both codes (p. 14); speed-ups over result-defined classes cannot be compared across tables, as the authors warn (p. 11); the 69 LINORD instances of the tuning tables are, by count, all the LINORD instances of the final table, so tuning and evaluation overlap (pp. 11–14). Future: other cheap, abundant families (Gomory, corner-polyhedron cuts) and better mod-k separators, a first try of which gave less effective cuts (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ⚠ variant (2003 precursor, joint): the separator stays and the loop around it changes, as Method 2 says, but the lever is cut selection and pool management (pp. 1–2, 6–8, 15), the cure that Method 2's contrast line sets aside, not a moved query point.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (steps 1 and 3): a dummy callback in the baseline so that both arms lose the same solver features, and "too easy" or "too hard" defined for both codes, with the fairness reason written out (p. 10); a 2003 use of the device the skill quotes from ERR14 (2014).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (dated lapse against step 5): the parameters were tuned on a subset that overlaps the final evaluation (pp. 10–14).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (step 5, subtraction): each selection ingredient (efficacy, parallelism, recombination) is disabled in turn and each removal hurts (Tables 4–6, pp. 12–13); removing the cap on added cuts left the solved set unchanged and cut the running times, so the cap was dropped from the final 012* (Table 7, pp. 12–14).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 3): the guest code changes nothing else in the solver (heuristics, branching, node selection) and follows the host's strategy instead of altering it (pp. 5, 7).
- new-pattern candidate: "Reverse-engineer the host solver's strategy before plugging in" (CPLEX's cut handling inferred from its documentation and log files and then followed, to avoid "rejection" of the guest code, pp. 6–7).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Put an own pool between a prolific separator and the LP; admit cuts by efficacy above an adaptive threshold (70% of the best cut at first, lowered after repeated misses) and by a parallelism cap checked only among the cuts added at the current node (pp. 6–8).
- Give the baseline a dummy callback so that both arms run with the same solver features switched off (p. 10).
- Define easy, hard and impossible classes on the results of both codes, never of one; do not compare speed-ups across tables whose class membership changed (pp. 10–11).
- Ablate a tuned policy by disabling one ingredient at a time against the tuned version (Tables 4–7, pp. 11–14).
- Give feasibility-only ILPs (SAT) a random objective, the sum of a random half of the constraints' left-hand sides, and stop at the first integer solution (p. 9).

**Quotes**:
- "This is particularly interesting in that our separation procedure was used as black-box—all the improvements came from a more clever way to exploit the violated cuts." (p. 1)
- "If some instances are discarded because they are too easy or too hard, they should be too easy or too hard for both codes, as it may be unfair to mark as “too easy” the instances solved in less than a few seconds by just one of the two codes, in that this will put the slower code in an advantageous position." (p. 10)

### S118 · Mixed-integer cuts from cyclic groups (Mathematical Programming 109(1) 2007, DOI 10.1007/s10107-006-0726-4)
**Read level**: full (all 29 file pages of the preprint dated September 10, 2005, which says that an earlier version was presented at IPCO XI in Berlin, June 2005 (p. 3); the tables on pp. 20–29 come as column dumps and were read for two rows). First-page check: title and Fischetti, Saturni match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Cristiano Saturni · **Position in trajectory**: the IPCO 2005 version is D002 (work-list note); a companion of the Fischetti–Lodi first Chvátal closure study [9] (p. 15): both compute the bound of a whole rank-1 cut family.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Gomory and Johnson's master cyclic group polyhedron was well studied, but the practical benefit of the whole family of interpolated subadditive cuts had not been computed (p. 1); earlier work handled cyclic-group separation only through Gomory's "shooting experiment" with random points, or through a huge extended formulation (p. 2). Question: how close an approximation of the corner-polyhedron bound gets, compared with GMI cuts alone (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: For one tableau row, exact separation of interpolated subadditive cuts is an LP over the sampled values g_i = g(i/k) under subadditivity constraints (the g-system (10)–(12)), whose value is negative exactly when a violated cut exists (p. 6, (13)); weights for a non-ideal k come from the interpolation algorithm of Fig. 3 (pp. 6–8); cuts are clipped as in (14) (p. 8); continuous variables are handled by a scaling argument, so that the separator stays a black box with pre- and post-processing (pp. 8–10); bounded variables are complemented when nonbasic at their upper bound (pp. 11–12).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: ILP and MIP in standard form with rational data (pp. 1, 8); only rank-1 cuts from the single rows of the first optimal tableau, no MIP preprocessing (pp. 12–13); testbed: MIPLIB 3.0 and 2003 instances with known optimum, without negative lower bounds and with LP files under 1.7 MB, Atamtürk's multiple-knapsack instances, and random bounded and binary knapsacks (7 sizes from n = 10 to 10,000, 30 instances each) (pp. 12–13).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Proof devices: with the ideal k the values off the sample points are immaterial, so the LP is exact (p. 6); a scaling limit gives the slopes for continuous variables (pp. 8–10); a cover inequality is derived as a subadditive cut after complementing a minimal cover, which shows that choosing the variables to complement is as hard as finding a most violated cover (p. 11); the deepest cut, from a QP in T-space, is not a facet but a convex combination of two facets (pp. 16–17). Pure cutting-plane loop at the root, at most 200 cuts per round, CPLEX 9.0 on a notebook (pp. 12–13).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Arms GMI, k-cuts for k = 1, …, 50, and interpolated cuts with k = 10, 20, 30, 60 (k = 5 in some tables), all from the same stored pool, reported as final bound, closed gap, separation and total time and number of cuts (pp. 12–13, 20–29); e.g. Atamtürk set mik.250-10-100: GMI 67.41%, k = 60 72.02% (64.9 s of separation), k-cuts 74.69% (p. 26); knapsacks with coefficients up to 100 and the ideal k, n = 10 binary: GMI 77.61%, ideal k 96.92% (p. 29); mipgap 10⁻⁹ for the sensitive knapsack runs (p. 13).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A negative result named as such: on MIPLIB the interpolated cuts "seldom beat" GMI cuts, in line with Dash and Günlük's probabilistic dominance and with the k-cuts experience (p. 13); they help on Atamtürk's instances and on random knapsacks, where the narrow gap inflates closed-gap percentages (p. 13); interpolation costs strength, and large k is expensive beyond k = 20 (pp. 13–14); a labelled conjecture explains the MIPLIB saturation (p. 14). Future: a conservative branch-and-cut integration after S070, higher-rank cuts and their tailing off, two facet cuts in place of one GMI cut (pp. 15–17).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 4, measure the potential first, practice, joint): the bound of the whole rank-1 family is computed with an exact separator before any practical separator is built, and on MIPLIB the ideal family adds little over GMI (pp. 1–3, 12–13).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (step 1): every arm is derived from the same stored equation pool and the same list of complemented variables inside the same root loop (pp. 12–13).
- H7 (heuristic, not a method) ✅: the outcome is labelled a negative result and its explanation a conjecture (pp. 13–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Judge a cut family by the bound of all its rank-1 members from the first optimal tableau, computed with an exact LP separator, before writing a heuristic separator (pp. 12–13).
- Keep a separator a black box for continuous variables by scaling them to integers before the call and mapping the cut back afterwards (p. 10).
- Store the tableau rows and the at-upper-bound list once and derive every compared cut family from that pool (pp. 12–13).
- Warn that closed-gap percentages exaggerate small bound changes when the gap is very narrow, as on knapsacks (p. 13).

**Quotes**:
- "To our knowledge, the practical benefit that can be obtained by embedding interpolated subadditive cuts in a cutting plane algorithm was not investigated computationally by previous authors." (p. 1)
- "According to the tables, interpolated subadditive cuts do improve the quality of the LP relaxation, but for MIPLIB instances they seldom beat GMI cuts." (p. 13)

### S142 · Mipping closures: An instant survey (Graphs and Combinatorics 23 (Suppl 1) 2007, DOI 10.1007/s00373-007-0711-6)
**Read level**: full (all 12 file pages of an undated author preprint titled "MIPping closures: an instant survey"). First-page check: title and Fischetti, Lodi match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Andrea Lodi · **Position in trajectory**: a survey of the MIP-based separation line, which includes his own CG-closure (IPCO 2005) and projected-CG work and colleagues' split and MIR closures (pp. 4–8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Solvers already guide branching, cuts and heuristics by auxiliary LPs, and good MIP solutions often cost about as much as the LP itself, so crucial decisions of a MIP algorithm can be translated into MIP models, "MIPping" (p. 1); precedents: local branching and the De Franceschi–Fischetti–Toth VRP refinement (pp. 1–2); target: the NP-hard separation problems of famous cut classes (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Survey of separation MIPs: CG cuts by a MIP with integer coefficient variables and a small ε, (8)–(12) (p. 4); projected CG cuts, CG cuts of the projection onto the integer variables, with the same simple structure (17)–(22) and a closure equal to the split cuts with one empty side (Theorem 4.1) (pp. 5–6); Balas and Saxena's parametric MIP over a grid of u0 values refined by bisection (pp. 6–7); Dash, Günlük and Lodi's MIR separation, a linearized nonlinear model with a binary expansion of β̂, exact for a suitable K (Theorem 4.2) (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pure and mixed ILPs with rational data (pp. 2–3); MIPLIB 3.0 instances, 25 pure and 33 mixed (Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Pure cutting-plane loops of rank 1 (p. 8); good feasible solutions of the separation MIP suffice except in the last round (p. 9); only integer variables in the support of x* enter the model, at-upper-bound ones complemented (p. 9); a penalty on the multipliers makes them sparser, which went with stronger and sparser cuts (p. 9); dropping the bounds u_i < 1 sped up convergence, read as diversification (pp. 9–10); hybrid: run MIP separation for a fixed time as preprocessing, then a standard solver (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Aggregated closed gap on MIPLIB 3.0: pure instances, split 71.71% against CG 62.59% on average; mixed, split 84.34% against projected CG 36.38% (Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11); most values are lower approximations because of time limits, 20 CPU minutes for the projected-CG closure (pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Lesson: rank-1 inequalities already give a very tight approximation in most practical cases (p. 11); the studies measured closure strength, not use inside a solver (p. 10); the preprocessing hybrid solved nsrand-ipx and arki001 for the first time (p. 10). Future: avoid explicit MIP separation models and design fast heuristics that use them only implicitly (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (stated, joint; steps 2, 4 and 5): MIPping is defined as translating crucial decisions of a MIP algorithm into MIP models (p. 1); the separation MIPs are solved only to good feasible solutions (p. 9); closures are measured before any use inside a solver (p. 10); when MIP separation is too slow it moves into a timed preprocessing phase, and the stated next step is to replace the explicit model by fast heuristics (pp. 10–11).
- H7 (heuristic, not a method) ✅: the closing lesson on rank-1 cuts (p. 11) is the paper form of the "Lesson learned" that the skill quotes from a deck (SKILL.md H7, MIA06).
- new-pattern candidate: "Diversify cuts by loosening the separation model" (dropping the multiplier bounds u_i < 1 sped up the cutting-plane loop, read as a diversification effect, pp. 9–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write the separation of a cut family as a MIP and let a general-purpose solver's heuristics produce violated cuts; ask for optimality only in the last round, to prove that none is left (pp. 4, 8–9).
- Shrink a separation MIP to the support of x* and complement at-upper-bound integer variables before solving (p. 9).
- Penalize the multipliers in the separation objective to get sparser cuts (p. 9).
- Run expensive separation for a fixed time as preprocessing and hand the tightened formulation to a standard solver (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "This leads to the idea of “translating into a MIP model” (MIPping) some crucial decisions to be taken within a MIP algorithm (in particular: How to improve the incumbent solution? How to cut?), with the aim of bringing the MIP technology well within the MIP solver." (p. 1)
- "Nevertheless, the picture is pretty clear and shows that, although one can construct examples in which the rank of the facets for a polyhedron is very large, in most practical cases the inequalities of rank 1 already give a very tight approximation of the convex hull of integer and mixed integer programs." (p. 11)
