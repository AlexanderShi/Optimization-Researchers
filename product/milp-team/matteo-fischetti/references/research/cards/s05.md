### S083 · Projected Chvátal–Gomory cuts for mixed integer linear programs (Mathematical Programming 2008, DOI 10.1007/s10107-006-0051-y)
**Read level**: full (all 16 file pages of the "preliminary draft: December 15, 2005", p. 1; the published version was not read). First-page check: title and authors Bonami, Cornuéjols, Dash, Fischetti, Lodi match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Pierre Bonami, Gérard Cornuéjols, Sanjeeb Dash, Andrea Lodi · **Position in trajectory**: the mixed-integer sequel to Fischetti and Lodi's first-Chvátal-closure study (IPCO 2005, ref. [16]) (pp. 1–3); it also places Codato and Fischetti's combinatorial Benders cuts [9] inside a known closure (p. 8).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the first Chvátal closure of an ILP "often gives a surprisingly tight approximation" of the integer hull (abstract, p. 1), measured by writing CG separation as a MILP (pp. 2–3); that model does not extend to the GMI closure of a MILP, whose separation is a nonlinear [14] or parametric [4] mixed-integer program (p. 3). The question: does the tightness carry over to mixed-integer programs (p. 3)?
- **D2 Core idea**: project the LP relaxation onto the integer variables and take CG cuts of the projection: ⌊uᵀA⌋x ≤ ⌊uᵀb⌋ for u ≥ 0 with uᵀC ≥ 0, (13) (p. 4); the separation MILP is the Fischetti–Lodi model plus one constraint family, (16) (p. 4). The trade is explicit: a family "theoretically dominated by GMI cuts" for a separation model that stays "very similar" to the one that worked (abstract, p. 1; p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MILP (7) with integer x ≥ 0 and continuous y ≥ 0, rational data (p. 4). Expected fit: models whose continuous variables only express feasibility, possibly through big-M coefficients, and are absent from the objective, such as the TW-ATSP and map labeling (pp. 3, 6–7). Test sets: 43 mixed-integer MIPLIB 3.0 instances, and 21 Ascheuer stacker-crane TW-ATSP instances with up to 30 vertices on a weak model with degree equations only (pp. 9, 12, 14).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Theorem 1: the pro-CG closure equals P intersected with all split cuts one of whose disjunction sides is empty, proved by two inclusions through the CG procedure (pp. 5–6). With the continuous variables absent from the objective, every valid inequality in x has finite Chvátal rank with respect to the projection, so iterated pro-CG cuts alone solve the MILP (p. 6). Theorem 2: combinatorial Benders cuts are pro-CG cuts (p. 8). Examples: a two-variable polytope where pro-CG cuts settle max x but cannot help max y (p. 6); infeasible-path and tournament inequalities of the TW-ATSP shown to be pro-CG cuts by maximizing their left-hand sides over the LP (3.95 and 3.9875, both rounded down to 3) (pp. 8–9). Implementation: a pure cutting-plane loop; separation by CPLEX 9.0 with "mip emphasis 4", aborted when its incumbent stops improving for a number of nodes (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: 3.2 GHz Pentium 4, 1,200 s per run, % gap closed (p. 9). MIPLIB 3.0: closure reached on 10 instances, limit hit on 26, no pro-CG cut exists on 7 (p. 9); about 29% on average over 41 instances, nothing on 11, about 40% on the other 30 (p. 11); against one round of COIN-OR GMI (24%) and MIR (23%) cuts and Bonami–Minoux lift-and-project (35%), adding pro-CG cuts gives 41%, 40% and 49%, and on top of all three 55% instead of 48% (p. 11). TW-ATSP: more than 70% of the gap closed on every instance, the limit reached on all, the share of the limit needed to reach the final bound reported (p. 14). The claimed numerical advantage over tableau GMI cuts is asserted without data (p. 11).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: "Although there are cases where they are ineffective, projected CG cuts provide excellent bounds for a number of MIPLIB instances" (p. 15). Limits: no cut exists on 7 instances and none helps on 11 (pp. 9, 11); proving that no violated cut remains "can require a great deal of enumeration" (p. 14). Future: faster heuristic separation (p. 12), use beside special-purpose polyhedral separators (pp. 14–15), and scheduling and cutting or packing models with big-M links (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (steps 2 and 4, practice, joint): which cut to add is a MILP solved, heuristically (aborted on stalling, "mip emphasis 4"), by the same general-purpose solver (p. 9), and the aim is to measure a closure's potential before asking for efficient separation (pp. 3, 12). The time limit is hit on 26 of 43 MIPLIB instances and on every TW-ATSP instance (pp. 9, 14), the cost the skill lists under this method's limitations.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (joint): the continuous variables of big-M models are diagnosed as expressing feasibility only (pp. 3, 6) and projected out, the step-1 and step-3 moves, but the projection yields a cut family in the x space rather than a thinner model; Theorem 2 ties it to Codato and Fischetti's Benders-type cuts (p. 8).
- new-pattern candidate (my label): *Weaker closure, same separation MILP* (choose the cut family whose separation stays a MILP of the form that already worked, accepting dominance by a stronger family; abstract p. 1, pp. 3–4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To carry a MIP-based separation from pure to mixed-integer programs, require uᵀC ≥ 0 on the multipliers: this projects out the continuous variables and adds a single constraint family to the separation model (p. 4).
- Locate a new cut family exactly, by a closure-equality theorem (split cuts with one empty side, p. 5) and by showing that a known family is a special case (CB cuts, p. 8).
- Test whether a known inequality is a rank-1 member of the family by maximizing its left-hand side over the LP and checking that the maximum rounds down to the right-hand side (pp. 8–9).
- Report a new cut family alone, added to each standard family, and added to all of them; the increments show whether it is "genuinely different" (pp. 11–12).
- When runs hit the time limit, report the share of the limit spent before the final bound was found (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "Though theoretically dominated by GMI cuts, projected CG cuts have the advantage of producing a separation model very similar to the one introduced by Fischetti and Lodi, whose solution can typically be carried out in a reasonable amount of computing time." (p. 1)
- "This indicates that the pro-CG cuts are genuinely different from those that are currently used in MILP solvers and that it is worth exploring heuristics that generate them more efficiently." (p. 12)

### S085 · Repairing MIP infeasibility through local branching (Computers & Operations Research 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.cor.2006.08.004)
**Read level**: full (all 10 file pages of the author preprint dated February 16, 2004, revised September 3, 2005; the journal version was not read). First-page check: title and authors Matteo Fischetti, Andrea Lodi match (p. 1). This version has no conclusions section: the results (p. 6) and Tables 3–4 (pp. 7–8) are followed by the acknowledgements and references (pp. 9–10).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Andrea Lodi · **Position in trajectory**: written while the pump was new (preprint date, p. 1); it joins local branching (2003, [9]) and the feasibility pump (2005, [10]), using the pump as a cheap source of an "almost feasible" start (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: LB, like RINS, "requires the availability of a starting feasible solution, which is an issue for some difficult MIPs" (p. 2); the pump supplies an almost-feasible point at small cost (p. 2), and where the pump stalls and modifies a few components heuristically, the alternative is "to switch to a method based on enumeration" (p. 4). Second entry: the repair returns a small set of constraints whose removal makes an infeasible MIP feasible, a MIP analogue of the maximum-feasible-subsystem work for LP (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Phase I of the simplex method transplanted (abstract, p. 1): for each row violated by the start x̂, relax aᵢᵀx ≥ bᵢ to aᵢᵀx + δᵢyᵢ ≥ bᵢ, with δᵢ the violation at x̂ and yᵢ a new binary; minimize Σyᵢ; x̂ is feasible with value |T|, and LB, whose constraint acts on the binaries (now including the y's), drives the count to zero (pp. 2, 4). Rejected alternatives: continuous artificials with a big cost M, which "performs reasonably well on most of the problems" but M is hard to set when objective values are huge (p. 4); a weighted mix of cost and infeasibility, beaten in a preliminary study by the artificial objective alone until feasibility, followed by standard LB or RINS (p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: 0-1 MIP with B ≠ ∅, possibly with general integers and continuous variables; the binaries are required because LB needs them (p. 1). Testbed: 44 0-1 MIPLIB 2003 instances and 39 further hard 0-1 MIPs (p. 5), some of them received by personal communication (refs [8], [18], [22], [25], pp. 9–10); exclusions with reasons: stp3d (first LP over an hour) and 11 instances on which every algorithm found a solution in 0.0 s (p. 5).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: the asymmetric LB constraint (11), counting only 1→0 flips, chosen for small-support, covering-like relaxations (pp. 4–5); neighbourhood size k = ⌊|T′|/2⌋, halving the violation count per neighbourhood, and k = 10 below 20 violated rows (p. 5); 30 s per neighbourhood (p. 5); the start's quality varied by running FP for 0, 10 or 100 iterations (LB0, LB10, LB100) (p. 5). ANSI C on the CPLEX callable libraries (p. 6). No proofs.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: against CPLEX 9.0.3 and FP; Pentium M 1.6 GHz notebook; 1,800 s limit; every run stops at the first feasible solution; per instance nodes or iterations, start violations |T|, LB iterations and time (p. 6; Tables 3–4, pp. 7–8). LB100 is most often at least as fast as the other two variants (58 cases, against 47 for LB10 and 40 for LB0, counts that include the 11 trivial instances); LB100 is strictly faster than CPLEX in 44 cases and slower in 21; CPLEX fails 4 times, LB100 3 times; CPLEX's first solution is "typically better" in quality, not tabulated (p. 6). One run per instance; the win counts include sub-second differences (harp2: 0.0 s for CPLEX against 0.8 s, Table 3, p. 7).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: an algorithmic variant plus a model-repair byproduct (pp. 1–2). Named limits: the original objective is "completely disregarded, thus the feasible solution obtained can be arbitrarily bad" (p. 4); binaries are required (p. 1). No future-work statement in this version (pp. 6–9).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (steps 2–3, practice, joint): the repair is itself a MIP (the model plus one binary artificial per violated row), explored inside LB's neighbourhoods by the black-box solver; the codes are thin layers on the CPLEX callable libraries (pp. 3–4, 6).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (steps 3, 5 and 6, practice, joint): "we avoided any parameter tuning", with FP exactly as published (p. 5); exclusions listed with reasons, by a rule in which all algorithms play the same role (p. 5); losses printed: CPLEX faster on 21 instances and better in first-solution quality (p. 6). Not stated: whether CPLEX ran with a heuristic emphasis (step 2) (pp. 5–6).
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction (dated lapse, 2004–05, joint): single runs, and win counts that include sub-second differences, read without any measure of spread (p. 6; Table 3, p. 7); this predates the 2012 statement.
- Heuristic 2 ✅ evidence: the feasibility requirement is moved from the constraints into the objective, as in Phase I (pp. 1–2, 4).
- Signature Work Anatomy, feasibility pump ⚠ variant: the anatomy dates the return of enumeration in place of the pump's random flips to 2007; this preprint, dated February 16, 2004 (p. 1), already switches from the pump's almost-feasible point to enumeration by LB (p. 4).
- Common Misreading 5 ⚠ variant: the same text calls LB "a heuristic approach" (p. 2) and its strategy "exact in nature" (p. 3); the heuristic reading is partly the authors' own framing.
- new-pattern candidate (my label): *Phase-I relaxation with violation-scaled binary artificials* (an infeasible start is made feasible in a relaxed model whose artificials are binaries scaled by each row's violation, so that a binary neighbourhood search can count and remove the violations; pp. 1–2, 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To warm-start a binary neighbourhood search from an infeasible point, add one binary per violated row scaled by that row's violation at the point (aᵢᵀx + δᵢyᵢ ≥ bᵢ), minimize Σyᵢ, and let the search treat the y's like any other binaries; no big-M weight has to be chosen (p. 4).
- Tie the neighbourhood size to the current violation count (k = ⌊|T′|/2⌋, with a floor of 10 below 20 violations), so the first steps from a start with thousands of violated rows are not slow (p. 5).
- Measure a method's sensitivity to the quality of its start by truncating the upstream heuristic at fixed iteration counts (0, 10, 100) and running the method from each (p. 5).
- Fix the exclusions of a first-solution study by a rule every arm shares (every algorithm at 0.0 s) and list them by name (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "The basic idea of the method presented in this section is that the LB algorithm does not necessarily need to start with a feasible solution—a partially feasible one can be a valid warm start for the method." (p. 4)
- "In our experiments, we avoided any parameter tuning–FP was implemented exactly as in [10], and for the modified LB code we used a time limit of 30 CPU seconds for the exploration of each local-branching neighborhood." (p. 5)

### S101 · The linear ordering problem with cumulative costs (European Journal of Operational Research 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2006.03.071)
**Read level**: full (all 19 file pages of the author preprint dated October 13, 2004; Figures 1–3 survive only as captions and axis labels; the journal version was not read). First-page check: title and authors Livio Bertacco, Lorenzo Brunetta, Matteo Fischetti match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Livio Bertacco, Lorenzo Brunetta · **Position in trajectory**: an application paper from a Padova telecommunications collaboration (instances from the Engineering School's telecom group, p. 14; acknowledgements, p. 18), first-authored by Bertacco, whom the skill's Academic Lineage lists as a doctoral student (2005).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: an application that fits neither the Hamiltonian-path nor the linear-ordering framework (p. 2): jointly choosing the successive-interference-cancellation detection order and the transmit powers of UMTS terminals (JOPCO) (pp. 4–6). A GRASP heuristic existed [1] (p. 5); benchmarking it "was indeed our initial motivation" (p. 18).
- **D2 Core idea**: define LOP-CC, where node values propagate backwards along the permutation, α_{kᵢ} = p_{kᵢ} + Σ_{j>i} c_{kᵢkⱼ} α_{kⱼ}, total cost Σα (p. 3), and BLOP-CC with αᵢ ≤ U (p. 3); JOPCO is a BLOP-CC with pᵢ = ΓN₀/√ρᵢᵢ and cᵢⱼ = ΓN_S ρⱼᵢ/ρᵢᵢ (p. 6). Two exact methods: a MIP from the Grötschel–Jünger–Reinelt LOP model, linearizing yᵢⱼ = αⱼxᵢⱼ with big-M = U (pp. 11–12), and a depth-first enumeration that fixes the permutation from the last position backwards, so that each newly placed node adds α times the costs of the still-unplaced nodes to a lower bound, (20) (pp. 12–14), started from a heuristic permutation (decreasing ρᵢᵢ) held in a FIFO queue (p. 14).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: complete digraph with nonnegative weights and costs; BLOP-CC may be infeasible (p. 3). Data: 4 scenarios (synchronous or asynchronous, with or without scrambling) × 500 random ρ matrices for 16 users in a 580 m cell under a log-distance path-loss model; from each matrix 8 instances, n = 2, 4, …, 16 (pp. 14, 16); 16 users is the realistic maximum for the application (footnote 3, pp. 14, 16); the matrices are published (p. 16).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: NP-hardness by reduction from directed Hamiltonian path with pᵢ = 1 and cᵢⱼ = M or 2M: an induction gives π ≤ M^{n−1} + 4ⁿM^{n−2} for paths using only arcs of G_HP against at least 2M^{n−1} otherwise, with M = 4ⁿ + 1 (pp. 9–10); BLOP-CC by the same construction with U = UB_good (p. 11). MIP numerics: the redundant yᵢⱼ ≤ Uxᵢⱼ are kept because "they improve the numerical stability" of the solver (p. 12); CPLEX tolerances are tightened (absolute gap 1e-13, relative gap 1e-11, integrality, optimality and feasibility 1e-9) because the defaults "are too large to solve correctly even small instances", and are still insufficient when ρ spans orders of magnitude (p. 16).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: enumeration against CPLEX 9.0.2 on the MIP, Pentium M 1.4 GHz notebook (p. 16); per set and size: instances solved, average and maximum times, speedup, and "# fails", the instances CPLEX did not solve correctly, up to 37 of 2,000 at n = 12 (Table 1, p. 17); "up to three orders of magnitude faster", CPLEX never faster, n = 20 solved by enumeration in about 4 hours (p. 16). The MIP arm ran on 50 of the 500 instances per set at n = 14 and on 2 to 5 at n = 16 (Table 1, p. 17); the text does not say how they were chosen or whether the speedups use only them (pp. 16–18).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a new problem, NP-hardness, two exact methods, all application instances solved, and the GRASP of [1] benchmarked as a byproduct (p. 18), though no GRASP figures appear in this version (pp. 16–18). Future: a stronger MIP formulation, sharper pruning, and more complex nonlinear costs on the LOP (p. 18).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ⚠ variant (practice, joint): the general-purpose solver is asked first, on a MIP model with tightened tolerances (pp. 11–12, 16), and its failures are printed (p. 17); here the black box loses on numerics and time, and the answer is an ad hoc exact enumeration, the step-5 outcome reported as counts of wrong answers rather than stated as a lesson.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (practice, joint): fitting the instances (at most 16 users, p. 16) leads to no model at all: a plain enumeration, chosen "rather than more sophisticated ones" because its bound is cheap (p. 13); the step-2 choice is made between algorithms, not formulations.
- Method 4 ✗ contradiction (dated lapse, 2004, joint): at the two largest sizes the MIP arm is timed on a subsample (50 and 2–5 instances per set against 500) without a stated rule (Table 1, p. 17; pp. 16–18).
- Heuristic 10 ✅ evidence: the exact enumeration doubles as the independent check that counts the MIP solver's wrong answers (pp. 16–17).
- Heuristic 4 ✅ evidence: a partner's application first, and a new problem class defined from it (pp. 3–6, 14).
- Roundtable Card blind spots ⚠ variant: numerics and exactness are listed as his blind spot; here (2004, joint) default tolerances are found too loose, tightened, and the remaining wrong answers counted (pp. 12, 16–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a recursive cost forces big-M products into a linear model, check whether building solutions in the order of the recursion (here backwards from the last position) gives each partial solution an exact, cheap bound increment; a plain depth-first search with that bound can beat the MIP by orders of magnitude (pp. 13–14, 16).

- Publish the generator's raw input (here the ρ matrices) so that every derived instance can be rebuilt (p. 16).

### S115 · How tight is the corner relaxation? (Discrete Optimization 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.disopt.2006.11.010)
**Read level**: full (all 11 file pages of the author preprint dated October 25, 2005, revised April 30, 2006; the journal version was not read). First-page check: title and authors Matteo Fischetti, Michele Monaci match (p. 1). There is no conclusions heading; the closing summary is on p. 5.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michele Monaci · **Position in trajectory**: a 2005–06 measurement note next to the first-Chvátal-closure study, on the testbed and cut columns of Fischetti and Saturni [6] (p. 3); an early piece of the Gomory work the skill dates from 2008.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: forty years of theory on the group and corner relaxations (abstract, p. 1), yet "The practical utility of the relaxation remains however a quite unexplored topic" (p. 2): no computational study had asked how tight the relaxation is in practice (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: define the corner relaxation by deleting the constraints not binding at an optimal LP vertex x*, (2), which depends on the vertex but not on the basis and so, unlike Gomory's group relaxation (nonnegativity dropped on the basic variables), is not weakened by primal degeneracy (pp. 1–2); then simply compute both relaxations' optima with a commercial branch-and-cut code (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MILP min{cᵀx : Ax ≤ b, xⱼ integer for j ∈ J} with rational data (p. 1). Testbed from Fischetti and Saturni [6]: MIPLIB 3.0 and 2003 without the instances of unknown optimum, with negative lower bounds or with LP files over 1.7 MB, plus Atamtürk's bounded and unbounded multiple-knapsack instances with binary and general-integer variables (p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Proposition 1, strong NP-hardness, by reduction from Decoding of Linear Codes: d = 1 without loss of generality, binaries replaced by y ≥ 0 integer, and the MILP min{1ᵀy : z + Q/2·y = 1/2, (z, y) ≥ 0 integer}, whose LP has the unique nondegenerate vertex (1/2, 0), so that the corner relaxation is the mod-2 problem (p. 2); the proof covers the group relaxation too (p. 3). CPLEX 9.1 at default settings with its presolver off, relaxations built on the original formulation, AMD Athlon 4200+, 10-hour limit (1 hour for Atamtürk's instances) (pp. 3–4).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: percentage of gap closed by one round of GMI cuts, by k-cuts for k = 1–50 and by k = 60 interpolated subadditive cuts (these two copied from [6]), by GMI′ (printed GMI0 in the extracted text), corner and group (pp. 3–4); Table 3 checks the time-limited bounds after 1, 3, 5 and 10 hours (one day for group) (pp. 4, 8). Results: corner 34.48% against group 23.61% on average over Tables 1–2; for pure 0-1 models corner and group are not much better than one GMI round and sometimes worse; GMI′ 9.67% against GMI 25.32% (p. 5); numerical failures (z) and memory faults (†) are marked (pp. 7–8). My recomputation from the extracted rows of Tables 1–2 gives 34.47% and 23.50% for corner and group, but about 28.8% and 11.3% for GMI and GMI′ (pp. 6–7); Table 5 prints Table 4's GMI and GMI′ averages (59.87, 58.66) although its 30 rows average 56.24 and 55.04 (pp. 9–10). The journal version was not checked.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: a missing number, offered so that researchers can decide "to study (or not to study)" the relaxations (p. 3), and a complexity proof that was "implicit in the previous papers" without "an easy reference" (p. 2). Named limits: solving the relaxations took 1–2 orders of magnitude longer than the original problems, so dynamic programming or basis reduction "should be applied" (p. 3); the enumerative approach cannot optimize over the intersection with the LP polyhedron, which needs a dual (cutting-plane or Lagrangian) approach (p. 4).

**Method links**:

**Read level**: full (all 4 file pages = printed pp. 171–174). First-page check: title and authors V. Cacchiani, A. Caprara, M. Fischetti match (p. 1).

- **D2 Core idea**: keep the nominal space-time-graph ILP (arc binaries, one path per train, clique constraints for track capacity, (1.1)–(1.5), p. 2), add buffer arcs (longer stops) with their own profit b_a to the objective, (1.6), so that efficiency and robustness are traded in one weighted objective (p. 2); relax the clique constraints (1.4) in a Lagrangian way and solve by subgradient optimization, each train then needing a maximum-profit path in G, as in [1] (p. 3).

### S046 · Mixed-integer programming models for nesting problems (Journal of Heuristics 2009, DOI 10.1007/s10732-008-9088-9)
**Read level**: full (all 34 file pages of the author preprint dated 18 September 2006, revised 20 September 2007; the text layer drops the "fi" and "ff" ligatures, e.g. "eciency", and the figures survive only as labels; the journal version was not read). First-page check: title and authors Matteo Fischetti, Ivan Luzzi match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Ivan Luzzi · **Position in trajectory**: "based on the Ph.D. dissertation of the second author" (p. 2), a 2003 dissertation listed at the University of Padova and hosted on Fischetti's thesis pages (ref. [25], p. 34); whether he supervised it is not stated. A MIP-viability study in cutting and packing during his general-purpose heuristics period.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: "Our main goal is to evaluate the viability of using modern MIP techniques to address practically-relevant nesting (sub-)problems" (p. 2); nesting (non-overlapping placement of irregular polygons on a strip) is strongly NP-hard and geometrically hard (abstract, p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: two models. (i) The Daniels–Li–Milenkovic MIP: the complement of each pair's no-fit polygon is cut into convex slices, one binary per slice, big-M disjunctions (7), one slice active per pair (8) (pp. 5–7); strengthened by lifting the big-M coefficients (pp. 8–10) and by branching priorities that settle all relative positions inside a growing clique of pieces, passed to the solver's priority mechanism (pp. 11–12). (ii) After the full model fails, an approximate MIP for the multiple containment subproblem, which places small trims into the holes left by big pieces: trims replaced by their bounding boxes, holes reduced to their usable part by a Minkowski difference with a minBox (shrunk by a heuristic 0.8 factor), each hole cut into a grid of cells with row and column knapsack-type constraints (pp. 14–21); the result is repaired by a compaction/separation LP with the slice choices fixed, dropping the most overlapping trim when the LP fails (pp. 22–23), and the model is solved class by class over trims of similar size (p. 23).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: simple hole-less polygons placed by translation only (the no-fit polygon is the path of one piece sliding "without rotating" around another, p. 4); a fixed-height strip of virtually infinite length; length minimized plus an ε term that compacts the pieces toward the origin for "aesthetic" reasons (pp. 2, 7). Instances: random "broken glass" squares, whose optimum is known in advance (pp. 12–13), and garment-industry instances available on request from the second author (footnote 8, p. 25).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: the hardness is located in the disjunctions, since once the slices are chosen "the problem becomes an easily-solvable LP" (p. 8), the structure of scheduling and rectangular packing models (p. 8); each lifted coefficient is the maximum of a facet's left-hand side over another slice inside a bounding box, found by evaluating that slice's vertices (pp. 9–10); a three-rectangle example shows why fixing pairs one at a time leaves overlaps in the LP (pp. 11–12). CPLEX 7.0 (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: broken glass (AMD Athlon 1.2 GHz, CPLEX 7.0, 100,000-node cap): 5, 7 and 9 pieces, with and without priorities, lifting always on; priorities close the 7-piece instance (32.08% gap to 0%), and neither setting closes the 9-piece one (gaps near 59%) (pp. 13–14); the "limited practical impact" of lifting is stated without a table (p. 13). Multiple containment: MIP-place against the authors' greedy bottom-left heuristic on three garment instances (four rows), with efficiency gains of 2.43, 0.46, 0.55 and 0.16 points against the text's "by 1-2%"; one row is labelled "smart" (Table 2 and text, p. 28); run times "from less than 1 second to a few seconds", not tabulated (p. 25).
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: the lesson stated: MIP techniques are "still not appropriate" for real-world nesting but "can be very useful" for simplified NP-hard subproblems inside heuristics (pp. 28, 31); future: "this line of research deserves more attention" (p. 31). Named limits: the simplified model ignores the interactions between cells that differ in both row and column, hence overlaps (pp. 21–22; Figure 11, p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (steps 2 and 5, practice, joint): when the full MIP cannot meet the requirement, the lesson is printed ("unlikely to be useful" at practical size, p. 13) and the MIP moves to one sub-decision of a heuristic, the containment of trims in holes, solved in seconds by the same solver (pp. 2, 14, 25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a disjunctive geometric MIP, lift each big-M coefficient separately: the coefficient of another slice's binary is the facet's maximum over that slice within a bounding box, found at the slice's vertices (pp. 9–10).
