### S017 · Modeling and solving the crew rostering problem (Operations Research 1998, DOI 10.1287/opre.46.6.820)
**Read level**: full. The whole technical report DEIS-OR-95-6(R) (26 file pages): model, bounds, heuristic, the FARO problem, results, Tables 1–3 and the references. Numbers and formulas were taken from the pdftotext re-extraction (see page mapping), prose and quotes from the text file. Figures 1–2 were read from captions and text. The journal version (Oper. Res. 46(6), 820–830) was not read; the work list notes the homepage title "Models and Algorithms for the Crew Rostering".
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Alberto Caprara, Paolo Toth, Daniele Vigo (affiliations DEIS Bologna and DMI Udine, p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: the code for FARO, the second of the two FS/AIRO competitions announced in 1994, after FASTER (set covering, S004) (p. 3); the same group's set-covering report of October 1995 is its ref. [7] (p. 24). The skill's 1989–2006 railway-codes strand.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The problem came as a competition: FS and AIRO called for rostering codes, to be sent to FS and run on three instances with up to 1,000 duties within 90 minutes on a PC 486/33 with 8 MB, with prizes of about US$60,000 and US$30,000 (p. 3). The entry against the literature is a difference of objective: earlier rostering work addressed urban mass transit, where the minimum number of crews is easy to determine and the goal is to balance workload, whereas railway rostering has more involved sequencing rules and minimises the number of crews (abstract, p. 2; p. 3). The team won the first prize with the best solutions on all competition instances (p. 3). The stated aim is a general model for airline and railway rostering (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: when the field's papers optimise another objective than your application (balance versus crew count), make that difference the entry point (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Each roster is a looped cycle in a multigraph with three arc types (direct, simple-rest, double-rest), whose lengths encode the sequencing rules, plus loops for idle days; the remaining operational constraints become forbidden minimal infeasible looped paths (pp. 6–8). Dropping those path constraints and relaxing two counting constraints in a Lagrangian way turns the bound into one assignment problem (AP) on the arcwise minimum of the three Lagrangian costs (Proposition 1, p. 12). The subgradient search is replaced by a structural observation: because arc lengths differ by multiples of 1440, the optimal multipliers are usually λ1 = λ2 = 1440, or λ1 = 1440, λ2 = 0, so two APs give the bound (pp. 12–13). The AP's dual solution then steers a constructive heuristic that builds one roster at a time: reduced costs are said to estimate an arc's likelihood of being optimal far better than its length, so zero reduced-cost arcs are preferred (p. 13; see Quotes), and the initial duty, the next duty and the closing move are scored by the parametric increase of the bound, with tie-breaks that put "critical" duties first and weights that follow each constraint's tightness (pp. 13–16). A refining loop removes every roster whose imposition raises the global bound, changes the construction parameters "either with a random perturbation, or deterministically in an adaptive fashion", and rebuilds (p. 16). Changed with respect to standard Lagrangian practice: no subgradient, two fixed multiplier pairs. Unchanged: the AP as the bounding workhorse.
  - Transferable technique: when the relaxation's costs live on a lattice (here multiples of 1440 minutes), try the few structural multiplier values before running subgradient optimisation (pp. 12–13).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: Periodic crew rostering: every duty every day, weeks of k days with the k-th day idle, rosters as cyclic sequences of weeks; minimising the total number of weeks minimises crews (k times the weeks) (pp. 4–6). ILP (2)–(8): binary arc variables x^t_ij for the three arc types and loop variables y^t_i, in- and out-degree constraints, one loop per vertex, and constraint (6) for every minimal infeasible looped path (pp. 8–9). Constraints (6) "could be reinforced" for tighter LP relaxations; the authors decline, preferring combinatorial relaxations (p. 9). Integer variables w and z with inequalities (9)–(13), redundant while (6) holds, are added because they survive the relaxation (p. 10). FARO data: k = 6, rosters typically 30 days and at most 60 (q = 60); attributes external rest, long, overnight and heavy overnight; per week at most 2 external-rest duties, 1 long and 2 overnight; at least 40% double weekly rests (β = 0.4) and an average weekly rest of at least 58 hours; per 30 days at most 7 external-rest duties and 170 paid hours; per 7 days at most 36 working hours; one optional special one-week roster; a hierarchical objective of weeks, then technical intervals, then available days (pp. 16–19). Eight FARO instances of 21–525 duties, more than one third of the duties overnight (Table 2, p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: add constraints that are redundant in the full model but bind once the hard constraints are relaxed (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Proof devices: Proposition 1 (keep, for each vertex pair, only the arc of minimum Lagrangian cost; solve one AP; take the smallest integer w that satisfies (16); set z := ⌈βw⌉), O(n³) (p. 12); validity of the replacement inequality (15) by comparing right-hand sides (p. 11); trivial O(n) bounds from each attribute cap (p. 11). For the hierarchical objective, bounds on technical intervals and available days are valid only if every bound on the more important terms equals its optimal value (p. 20); they come from an ε added to the length of every arc that needs a technical interval, and from a dummy duty of three idle days that forces the special roster, giving LB′ and the combinations (18)–(19) (pp. 20–21). Heuristic: each step costs O(n²) through parametric AP re-solves, the whole O(n³); a look-ahead when fewer than 100 duties remain; imposed arcs are realised by setting competing lengths to +∞; no idle day unless strictly necessary (pp. 13–16). FORTRAN code (p. 21); two people are thanked for help in programming (p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: for a lexicographic objective, bound each lower level with ε-perturbed costs and declare that bound valid only where every higher level's bound is attained (p. 20).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Two layers. (a) The competition as FS ran it: seven teams entered; three produced valid solutions on all three test instances, and Table 1 gives weeks, technical intervals and available days for BO (the authors), NA (a greedy) and TO (a genetic algorithm), all under the 90-minute limit on a PC 486/33, with the authors' lower bound (p. 22). BO reached 48, 112 and 165 weeks against bounds of 48, 108 and 164 (FARO164, FARO360, FARO525); NA 48, 113, 168 and TO 49, 113, 168 (Table 1, p. 22). Times to the reported solutions: 18, 76 and 25 minutes (p. 22). Five FARO instances had been distributed before the competition and three others were used in it (p. 23). The FARO360 gap is diagnosed: the paid-time constraint binds, the Lagrangian and the paid-time trivial bounds both equal 108, and with that constraint relaxed the heuristic reaches 108 (p. 22; see Quotes). (b) After the competition, the "final version", said to contain several improvements over the competition code, was run on 36 instances, the 8 FARO ones and all 28 merged pairs, with limits of 60 and 150 minutes for n ≤ 500 and n > 500 on a Pentium 90, reporting the time to the reported solution (pp. 23, 25). Claims: bounds and solutions coincide in weeks on 26 of 36, the average gap is 0.8%, and the best trivial bound is on average 7.6% worse than the Lagrangian one (p. 24). My reading of Table 3: 25 coincide within the stated limits, and a 26th, F69F525 (bound 182, reported 183), if the separate 300-minute run is counted; the text names F164F525 for that 182-week solution, but F164F525's bound is 211, so the name looks like a slip (pp. 23, 25). One entry (F69F386, n = 455) reports 7,462 s against the 60-minute limit for n ≤ 500, and the 15-minute average quoted in the text includes it (my arithmetic, pp. 23, 25). No rival on the merged instances; single runs; no seeds, although the refining step perturbs parameters at random (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: when real instances are few, enlarge the test bed by merging every pair of them (p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: explain a large gap by the constraint that binds, and confirm it by relaxing that constraint and showing the gap close (p. 22).
- **D6 Positioning**: Prior rostering work (Jachnik, Bodin et al., Carraresi and Gallo, Hagberg, Bianco et al.), airline set partitioning (Ryan, Gamache and Soumis, Jarrah and Diamond) and cyclic scheduling are listed in one paragraph and set aside by the difference in objective, without discussion of their methods (p. 3). The competing teams are named and their reports cited (p. 22). The authors' own FASTER report is cited for the companion competition (pp. 3, 24).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract carries problem gap, relaxation, bound-driven heuristic, result and competition win in that order (p. 2). Design choices are justified by "computational experience" (p. 12) and "our experience" (p. 13) rather than by experiments shown. The general model (§1–§3) is kept apart from the FARO rules (§4, pp. 16–21). There is no conclusions section; the paper ends with the results and acknowledgments (p. 24).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: a model, a combinatorial bound computed by two APs, a bound-driven heuristic and the winning competition code (pp. 2–3). Limits shown rather than stated: the LP-strengthening route is left unexplored (p. 9); the bound is weak where paid time binds (FARO360, p. 22). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✗ contradiction (dated, before the 2002–2009 general-purpose period): a problem-specific Lagrangian code in FORTRAN; the ILP serves only to derive the relaxation and no general-purpose solver is run at any stage (pp. 8–16, 21). As in the S002 card (c03).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: the model is shaped for the relaxation rather than thinned: the path constraints are left weak by choice (p. 9) and redundant variables and constraints are added because they survive relaxation (p. 10). Step 2's "the model that fits", by addition.
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (partial): an external harness, in which FS ran every team's code on the same machine and limit and all three valid entries are printed with the authors' bound (pp. 3, 22); the post-competition version is labelled as improved (p. 23). Lapses: no rival on the 36-instance test bed; single runs; the improved version was developed after the competition instances were known (my inference from p. 23).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: randomness as diversification, in the random or adaptive perturbation of the construction parameters before each rebuild (p. 16); no seeds and no spread in the evaluation.
- Also confirmed: Research Heuristic 4, application first, in the skill's early-career version, "a public challenge" (FARO, p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *dual-guided primal heuristic* (AP reduced costs steer construction and closing, pp. 13–16; also S004 and S003 here, S002 in c03).
- New-pattern candidate: *bound-guided remove-and-rebuild* (remove the parts whose imposition raises the bound, rebuild with perturbed parameters, p. 16; also S004 pp. 13–15).
- New-pattern candidate: *Substructure harvest* (the assignment substructure gives the bound, pp. 11–12; name from s01).
- New-pattern candidate: *fix the multipliers by a structural argument* (pp. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Make the objective your application has and the literature lacks the entry point (pp. 2–3).
- [D2] Encode sequencing rules in the arc types and lengths of a multigraph, and leave only the rest to forbidden-path constraints (pp. 6–9).
- [D2] Try structural multiplier values before subgradient optimisation when costs live on a lattice (pp. 12–13).
- [D3] Add constraints redundant in the full model that bind once hard constraints are relaxed (p. 10).
- [D4] Score constructive moves by the parametric increase of the relaxation bound and prefer zero reduced-cost arcs (pp. 13–15).
- [D4] Refine by removing every roster whose imposition raises the bound and rebuilding its duties with perturbed parameters (p. 16).
- [D4] Bound each lower level of a lexicographic objective with ε-perturbed costs, valid only where the higher levels are tight (p. 20).
- [D5] Merge pairs of real instances to enlarge a small test bed (p. 23).
- [D5] Diagnose a gap by its binding constraint and confirm by relaxing it (p. 22).

**Quotes**:

- "The main reason for this behavior is that the constraint on the paid time for this instance is much more binding than in the other cases" (p. 22)

### S004 · A heuristic method for the set covering problem (Operations Research 1999, DOI 10.1287/opre.47.5.730)
**Read level**: full. The whole undated author preprint (27 pp.): abstract, §§1–9, Tables 1–8, Figures 1–4 and the references. The figures' plotted curves are read from their captions and the text only. The text file drops the fi and ff ligatures ("rst", "xing"), so quotes were chosen around them. The journal version (Oper. Res. 47(5), 730–743) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Alberto Caprara, Paolo Toth (affiliations DEIS Bologna and DMI Udine, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the FASTER code (competition of 1994, p. 2), the first of the two FS/AIRO competitions (S017 is the second); the subgradient-plus-heuristic machinery reappears in the train-timetabling code (S002 card, c03). Within the skill's 1989–2006 railway strand.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Very large set covering problems (SCP) from FS crew scheduling, up to 5,000 rows and 1,000,000 columns, which "the classical approaches" handle with considerable difficulty (abstract, p. 1). FASTER defined three size classes (micro up to 600 × 60,000, medium up to 2,750 × 1,100,000, large up to 5,500 × 1,100,000), with prizes of about US$60,000, 30,000 and 15,000 and limits of 3,000 s on a PC 486/33 (micro) and 10,000 s on an HP 735/125 (medium, large); four sample instances were distributed, the competition instances were not, and FS tried the commercial SCP packages on the samples (p. 2). The literature sets the scale: exact methods solve a few hundred rows and a few thousand columns (p. 3), greedy methods are fast but poor, and the best heuristics follow Balas and Ho's Lagrangian relaxation with subgradient optimisation (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: define the entry by size and say how far the exact and the classical heuristic methods get (pp. 3–4).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: A three-phase Lagrangian heuristic, iterated: a subgradient phase that quickly finds near-optimal multipliers "by means of an aggressive policy"; a heuristic phase of 250 iterations that explores near-optimal multipliers and runs a greedy on Lagrangian scores at each; and column fixing, which fixes a few "good" columns and recurses on the residual problem (pp. 5–6, 9, 11–12). All of it runs on a dynamic core problem priced like partial pricing in LP, and a refining procedure follows (pp. 6, 12–15). The reason for exploring many multipliers: almost equivalent near-optimal multipliers give solutions of very different quality, and the bound does not correlate with solution quality (p. 5; see Quotes). Against Held–Karp subgradient, two changes: a step-size rule (λ starts at 0.1; every p = 20 iterations the best and worst bounds are compared, λ is halved if they differ by more than 1% and multiplied by 1.5 if they are within 0.1%) (p. 7), and a "small-norm" direction computed from a prime partial cover of the near-zero Lagrangian-cost columns, which counters zig-zagging under massive dual degeneracy (more than 1,000 columns with |c_j(u)| < 0.001 per iteration on RAIL516) and is no longer guaranteed to be a subgradient (pp. 7–8). Greedy score Rule a) on γ_j, the column cost minus the multipliers of its still uncovered rows (γ_j/μ_j if γ_j > 0, γ_j·μ_j if γ_j < 0), with the γ_j form credited to Fisher and Kedia (p. 10). The core is not frozen, because good solutions need columns that are individually worse (p. 6; see Quotes). Unchanged: the Lagrangian relaxation (4)–(5) itself.
  - Transferable technique: when a Lagrangian subproblem is massively degenerate, build the ascent direction from a minimal cover of the near-zero-cost columns instead of an arbitrary subgradient (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: Pure SCP, min cx subject to Ax ≥ 1, x binary, with A a 0-1 matrix and c positive integers (p. 3). The method relies on the integrality property of the Lagrangian subproblem, by which LP duals solve the Lagrangian dual (p. 4), and on the dual degeneracy of crew-scheduling instances (pp. 7, 24). Data: FASTER rail instances with costs 1 or 2, at most 12 rows per column and densities 0.2–1.3% (pp. 16–18); Beasley's OR-Library classes 4–6 and A–H with costs 1–100 (pp. 19–20); Wedelin's airline instances, costs up to 7 digits (pp. 23–24); Balas and Carrera's airline (AA) and bus (BUS) instances (pp. 23–25). Dominated rows and columns are deliberately not removed, for three stated reasons: preprocessing is slow on large instances, pricing makes the running time insensitive to them, and the scores avoid dominated columns (p. 16). There is no optimality proof (p. 18).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Subgradient termination: bound improvement over the last 300 iterations below 1.0 and below 0.1% (p. 8), with at most 10m iterations, never reached (p. 9). After each fixing the best multipliers are restarted by a uniform random relative perturbation in [−0.1, 0.1], so that the phase converges to a different vector (pp. 6–7). GREEDY keeps its scores in O(q) through incremental updates and searches a candidate set of the min{m, |N∖S|} smallest scores refreshed by median finding (p. 11); redundant columns are removed greedily by cost while more than 10 remain, then by enumeration (p. 11). Column fixing: first every column with Lagrangian cost below 0.001 that is the only such cover of some row, then the first max{⌊m/200⌋, 1} columns chosen by GREEDY (p. 12). Pricing: a tentative core of the first 5 columns covering each row; every T iterations a new core of the columns with Lagrangian cost below 0.1 (at most 5m) plus the 5 cheapest per row; T adapted to Δ, the relative bound change at pricing (T ×10 if Δ ≤ 10⁻⁶, ×5 if ≤ 0.02, ×2 if ≤ 0.2, else T = 10; capped at min{1000, m/3}); a valid bound exists only after pricing (p. 13). Refining: an exact identity splits the duality gap into the positive Lagrangian costs of chosen columns, the negative costs of unchosen ones and over-cover terms u_i(|S ∩ J_i| − 1) (p. 14); each chosen column gets its share δ_j (9); the lowest-δ columns are fixed until a fraction π of the rows is covered (10), and 3-PHASE re-optimises the rest (p. 14); π starts at π_MIN = 0.3 and grows by α = 1.1 while nothing improves; stop when z_OPT ≤ β·L(u*) with β = 1.0 or at the time limit; the three parameters are called "very important" (p. 15). ANSI FORTRAN 77, the matrix stored columnwise in sparse form and the core both ways (p. 16).
  - Transferable technique: decompose the duality gap exactly into column and row terms and use each incumbent column's share as its fixing score (pp. 14–15).
  - Transferable technique: re-price a core every T iterations and let T follow how much the bound moves at pricing (p. 13).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: FASTER samples (Table 3): the rounded LP bound, the code's value with its time to first find, the Roma group's value (Ceria, Nobili and Sassano) and the best commercial package as reported by them (p. 17). Competition (Table 4): every group whose code FS ran successfully, according to the jury's final report (Ancona; Beasley and Mingozzi; Firenze; Roma; Trento), with the code best on all three instances (182, 691, 1065, against for example Roma's 692 and 1070), and better than every other final value after one sixth of the limit (pp. 17–18). Ablation at equal time: 3-PHASE with and without column fixing on the four samples, 211 against 213, 176 against 180, 952 against 989, 1550 against 1606 (Table 1, p. 12); the step-size and direction rules against the classical ones on RAIL516 (Figures 1–2, pp. 7–9); Rule a) alone matched a version that ran every rule and kept the best, while no other single rule did (p. 10); pricing is said to cut time by more than an order of magnitude, without a table (p. 13). Literature instances: 5,000 s on a DECstation 5000/240 (p. 18); others' times converted through Dongarra's tables, an "unavoidable approximation" (p. 21); time is to the first find of the best value, except Balas and Carrera (whole run) and Ceria, Nobili and Sassano (a time limit); Beasley and Chu's time is estimated as (k − 1)·AET + AST from their ten trials (pp. 20–21); Balas and Carrera's better and three to five times slower version is the one reported (p. 20). CPLEX 3.0 was run up to 600,000 s (depth-first with the best known value as upper bound, then best-first for the bound, which ran out of memory) to certify classes E–H; where it proved optimality it needed about 1,000 times the heuristic's time (p. 21). Results: optimal on all instances with known optima in classes 4–D (p. 21); better than Beasley and Chu on G.2, H.1 and H.2 (p. 22); against Wedelin slightly worse on A320coc and SASD9imp2 and better on SASjump (p. 23); optimal on all Balas–Carrera instances (p. 24). Headline claims checked: "92 out of the 94" optimal or best known (pp. 2, 24); my count from the tables gives at most 91, because besides A320coc and SASD9imp2 the SASjump value 7,339,537 lies above the CPLEX optimum 7,338,844 (Table 7, p. 24), and two sample values (RAIL507 174, RAIL2586 947) come only from a footnoted "ad hoc tuning" (Table 3, p. 17), on which the claim of best values on all sample instances (p. 2) rests for RAIL507. Single runs; no seeds, though the method randomises its restarts (p. 6); the time metric differs across rivals, which the paper states (p. 20).
  - Transferable technique: when rivals publish different time measures, print a per-rival rule (time to best, whole run, time limit) and the source of the machine conversion (pp. 20–21).
  - Transferable technique: certify a heuristic on open instances with long exact runs seeded with the best known value, and say that those runs are not timed (p. 21).
- **D6 Positioning**: In the Balas–Ho line, with the improvements of Beasley, Fisher and Kedia, Balas and Carrera, and Ceria, Nobili and Sassano, against surrogate relaxation (Lorena and Lopes), Wedelin's coordinate search and Beasley and Chu's genetic algorithm (p. 4). Credits are explicit: Fisher and Kedia for Lagrangian costs in a greedy (p. 10), Ceria, Nobili and Sassano as the only other heuristic with variable fixing (p. 11), Carraresi and Gallo for combining Lagrangian relaxation and column generation (p. 12); novelty is claimed "to our knowledge" for pricing with Lagrangian relaxation in SCP (p. 12). The main rival's static core is contrasted with the dynamic one (p. 17).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract leads with the application and the competition, then the two main features and the results (p. 1). Design choices are justified by "computational experience" (pp. 5, 7, 10, 13); convergence behaviour is shown on one instance (Figures 1–2, pp. 7–9). The conclusion closes on a strong claim, that the authors know of no other commercial or academic code with comparable performance (p. 24), and generalises the scheme to other 0-1 programs, provided an effective heuristic replaces GREEDY (p. 25).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: algorithmic and code, a competition winner (pp. 1–2, 24). Limits: no proof of optimality (p. 18); three refining parameters called very important with no sensitivity shown (p. 15). Future work: an extension with the "base constraints" of railway crew scheduling, with promising preliminary results, and other 0-1 linear programs (pp. 24–25).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (a precursor of step 4): the spread is exploited at the level of the dual vector, through many near-optimal multipliers and random ±10% restarts, because bound and solution quality do not correlate (pp. 5–7); the evaluation has no seeds and no spread. Consistent with the S002 card (c03).
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the stalling loop here is the subgradient ascent; it is examined on one instance by bound-against-iteration plots (Figures 1–2) and cured by changing the direction and the step the loop uses, leaving the relaxation alone (pp. 7–9). Steps 1 and 3 in a dual-ascent loop, not a cut loop.
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: a dynamic core removes the columns that play little role (pp. 6, 12–13), reversibly through pricing, and preprocessing of dominated columns is skipped on purpose (p. 16).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (partial): a third-party harness, with codes run by FS on undisclosed instances and every group that ran reported (pp. 2, 18); ablation at equal time (p. 12); the rival's best version (p. 20); a fair comparison declined where data are missing (p. 22). Lapses: converted times; a time metric that differs by rival; footnoted ad hoc tunings behind a headline claim; the 92-of-94 count (pp. 2, 17, 20–21, 24).
- Method 6 ⚠ variant (dated): the general-purpose solver appears only as an exact referee, CPLEX 3.0 run for certificates (p. 21), not as a subroutine; the heuristic is problem-specific, though the conclusion points to other 0-1 programs (p. 25).
- Trajectory note (my inference): the hard column fixing called "of fundamental importance" here (p. 5) is the kind of fixing that local branching (S001, 2003) later replaced by soft fixing (skill, local-branching anatomy); whether TURNI used this code is not stated here.
- New-pattern candidate: *dual-guided primal heuristic* (Lagrangian costs drive the greedy scores, the fixing and the core, pp. 5, 10–14; also S017, S003).
- New-pattern candidate: *bound-guided remove-and-rebuild* (gap shares choose what to fix before re-optimising, pp. 14–15; also S017 p. 16).
- New-pattern candidate: *dynamic core by Lagrangian pricing* (pp. 12–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] A small-norm ascent direction from a prime cover of the near-zero-cost columns under dual degeneracy (pp. 7–8).
- [D2] A step-size rule driven by the spread of the bound over the last p iterations: halve when wide, enlarge when flat (p. 7).
- [D2] Run the greedy at many near-optimal multiplier vectors and restart from randomly perturbed multipliers (pp. 5–7).
- [D4] Fix the columns that are the only near-zero-cost cover of some row, then the first greedy picks (p. 12).
- [D4] A dynamic core re-priced every T iterations, T adapted to the bound change at pricing (pp. 12–13).
- [D4] An exact duality-gap decomposition as the fixing score of a refinement that fixes a growing fraction of rows (pp. 14–15).
- [D5] Ablate a component at equal time on the hardest instances (p. 12).
- [D5] A per-rival time rule with the conversion source (pp. 20–21).
- [D5] Certify open instances by long exact runs seeded with the best known value (p. 21).

### S003 · Algorithms for the set covering problem (Annals of Operations Research 2000, DOI 10.1023/a:1019225027893)
**Read level**: full for this copy, which is 12 pages: the prose (pp. 1–9), the references (pp. 10–11) and Table 1 (p. 12). Tables 2–4, which §§3–4 discuss (pp. 7–9), are not in this copy, so their per-instance values were not read and only the prose summaries are used. Numbers were taken from the pdftotext re-extraction (see page mapping), prose and quotes from the text file. The journal version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Alberto Caprara, Paolo Toth (Fischetti at DEI Padova, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a survey by the authors of the leading SCP heuristic (S004, its ref. [9]) and of an exact LP code for SCP (ref. [10], a 1998 working paper) (pp. 4–5, 10), at the end of the railway set-covering strand.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: A survey of the most recent and effective SCP algorithms, heuristic and exact, with an experimental comparison on Beasley's OR Library; methods not tried on those instances are not described (abstract and p. 1). The state of the art is put in sizes: a few hundred rows and a few thousand columns are solved to proven optimality, and a few thousand rows and a few million columns to within about 1% in reasonable time (p. 2). Classes 4, 5, 6 and e are dropped as "quite easy", since state-of-the-art heuristics almost always find the optimum and exact codes need little time; the comparisons use the 40 instances of classes A–H (p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: scope a computational survey by one common test bed, and drop the classes on which every state-of-the-art method succeeds, saying so (pp. 1–2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The organising idea is that the best heuristic and exact methods rest on the LP relaxation, or on Lagrangian and surrogate substitutes that solve its dual heuristically (pp. 2–3). Any multiplier vector gives a bound, a greedy repair makes it dual feasible without lowering the bound, so near-optimal multipliers are "in some sense" a heuristic dual solution (p. 3). Surveyed devices: Lagrangian cost fixing (p. 4); Balas and Carrera's partial relaxation that keeps a set of disjoint rows, with a dynamic subgradient that keeps the multipliers dual feasible (p. 4); the authors' core with pricing (p. 4); Ceria, Nobili and Sassano's primal–dual subgradient (p. 4); Lorena and Lopes's surrogate relaxation, solved as a continuous knapsack in O(n) (p. 4); Wedelin's row-by-row multiplier updates with two values per row (pp. 4–5); and the authors' SPRINT-like exact LP code, with a Lagrangian start and multiplier adjustment that keeps the bounds increasing (p. 5). Heuristics: Lagrangian-cost greedy schemes (Beasley, Balas and Carrera, Lorena and Lopes, Ceria et al., Haddadi, the authors') and non-Lagrangian ones (Beasley and Chu's genetic algorithm; simulated annealing by Jacobs and Brusco and by Brusco, Jacobs and Thompson with "morphs") (pp. 5–7). Exact methods: LP-based branch-and-bound (Beasley 1987; Beasley and Jornsten 1992 with Gomory cuts and constraints that exclude the best solution; Balas and Carrera 1996 with two dual-based branching schemes) (pp. 8–9). A comparison principle follows: relaxations used inside heuristics are compared by the heuristics they drive, not by their bounds (p. 5; see Quotes).
  - Transferable technique: judge a relaxation used inside a heuristic by the solutions it leads to, not by its bound (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: SCP as in (1)–(3), with c_j > 0 integer (p. 1). The integrality property of the Lagrangian subproblem makes LP and Lagrangian duals coincide (p. 3). Reduction rules (dominated columns and rows, columns forced by a row with one cover) must be applied "in a careful way" on large instances, and each algorithm's preprocessing is deliberately left out to show the essential structure (p. 2). OR-Library classes A–H: 300 × 3,000 to 1,000 × 10,000, densities 2–20%, costs 1–100 (Table 1, p. 12).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Devices summarised with their validity arguments: Lagrangian cost fixing, x_j := 0 when L(u) + c_j(u) ≥ UB and x_j := 1 when L(u) − c_j(u) ≥ UB (p. 4); the partial relaxation's best bound equals the LP value (p. 4); optimal removal of redundant columns is itself an SCP on those columns (p. 6). One explanatory argument: LP-based branch-and-bound dominates because stronger bounds seem very hard to obtain by more expensive methods, which also explains why general-purpose ILP packages are competitive (p. 8). The authors' LP code calls the CPLEX simplex inside, and its time is reported with that share (Table 1 "Simplex" column, pp. 5, 12).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Table 1: the authors' LP code against CPLEX 4.0.8 primal, dual, network and barrier with crossover, each tuned "so as to guarantee the best overall performance on the 40 instances" (p. 5); average times about 420, 46, 46 and 370 s against about 10 s for the new code, of which about 7 s are spent in CPLEX calls, a factor of almost 5 over the best CPLEX algorithm (p. 5; Table 1, p. 12). Table 2 (classes A–D, optimum known; not in this copy): Beasley and Chu and the authors' heuristic are optimal on all; average times about 4 s (Lorena and Lopes), 30 (Haddadi), 40 (Beasley), 45 (Balas and Carrera), 50 (the authors') and 180 (Beasley and Chu); Lorena and Lopes misses the optimum in 15 of 20 cases, and its times exclude the initial reduction (pp. 7–8). Table 3 (classes E–H; not in this copy): the authors' heuristic gives the best known value on all; Beasley and Chu and Brusco, Jacobs and Thompson match it with three and two exceptions; average times about 200 s, 250, 400 and 7,500; no fair time comparison for the two codes that report only a time limit (p. 8). Table 4 (classes A–D; not in this copy): the exact codes need about 3,000 s (Beasley), 2,000 (Beasley and Jornsten) and 1,500 (Balas and Carrera), against about 700 s for CPLEX 4.0.8 and 750 for MINTO 2.3; given an external preprocessing (the authors' heuristic without post-optimisation, their LP code and LP reduced-cost fixing), the two solvers need about 400 and 500 s (p. 9; see Quotes). Nobili and Sassano's cutting-plane code is left out of the time comparison because only node counts were published (p. 8). All times are in DECstation 5000/240 seconds; the authors measured their own codes, CPLEX and MINTO, and converted the others with what they call a "very crude approximation" that still gives "clear indications" in most cases (p. 2). One number per source, no variability.
  - Transferable technique: compare a general-purpose ILP solver twice, on the plain instance and with your code's incumbent, LP solution and reduced-cost fixings as input, and report both (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: tune each algorithm of a general-purpose baseline (primal, dual, network, barrier) before comparing, and report the time your own code spends inside that solver (p. 5; Table 1, p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: The full history is left to Balas's survey and Ceria, Nobili and Sassano's annotated bibliography (p. 1). Each method is described in its authors' terms, with gaps in rivals' papers named (Balas and Carrera's removal order and Haddadi's subproblem solution are "not specified", p. 6) and comparisons that cannot be made declined (p. 8). The authors' own codes appear in every comparison and are best or tied throughout (pp. 5, 7–8), a survey written by the leading code's authors.
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: Organised as relaxations, then heuristics, then exact methods, each section ending in its comparison (p. 2). Hedges are explicit ("apparently", p. 8; "According to our opinion", p. 5). There is no conclusions section; the paper ends on the measured sentence about general-purpose ILP solvers (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: a comparative map of SCP methods on one test bed, with the authors' new LP code (pp. 1, 5). Limits: rivals' times converted roughly (p. 2); several comparisons impossible (p. 8). No future work is stated.

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (dated 2000, earlier than the skill's 2002–2009 start): general-purpose ILP solvers on the plain model beat the special-purpose exact codes, and the authors' own pieces do best as a preprocessing wrapper handed to the stock solver (p. 9); their special-purpose LP code itself runs on CPLEX (pp. 5, 12). Steps 1 and 2 in practice, with the explanation that stronger bounds are hard to get cheaply (p. 8).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence (step 2, dated, joint): a weaker Lagrangian bound "is not a drawback within heuristic algorithms", while exact algorithms do better with the LP solved to optimality (p. 5): an early, relaxation-level form of the split the skill records from 2016 (MATH16 p.3: a polyhedrally weak model can be preferred for heuristics).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (partial): each baseline algorithm tuned (p. 5); easy classes removed for all methods by a stated rule (p. 2); unfair comparisons declined (p. 8). Lapses: the baselines were tuned on the 40 test instances themselves (p. 5); rivals' times converted (p. 2); the authors' codes are the reference throughout.
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: the observation that almost equivalent multipliers give very different solutions, and that the bound does not predict quality, is stated as the field's ground for running the heuristic at many multipliers (pp. 5–6); no seeds.
- New-pattern candidate: *judge a relaxation by what it drives, not by its bound* (p. 5; also S004 p. 5).
- New-pattern candidate: *your code as the stock solver's preprocessor* (p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate: *dual-guided primal heuristic* (the Lagrangian-cost greedy as the field's common core, pp. 5–6; also S004, S017).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Scope a survey by one test bed; drop the classes every method solves, with the reason (pp. 1–2).
- [D2] Compare relaxations inside heuristics by the heuristics' solutions, not by bound values (p. 5).
- [D4] Lagrangian cost fixing in both directions against the incumbent (p. 4).
- [D5] Tune every algorithm of the general-purpose baseline, and report the time your code spends inside it (p. 5; Table 1, p. 12).
- [D5] Hand the stock ILP solver your incumbent, LP solution and reduced-cost fixings as a second arm (p. 9).
- [D6] Decline a time comparison when a rival reports only nodes or a time limit, and say why (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore it is not interesting to compare these methods according to the lower bound value they produce rather we will compare the performance of the associated heuristic algorithms in the next section" (p. 5)
- "This shows that the state-of-the-art general-purpose ILP solvers are competitive with the best exact algorithms for SCP presented in the literature and that their performance can sensibly be improved by an external preprocessing procedure" (p. 9)

### S013 · Solving the asymmetric travelling salesman problem with time windows by branch-and-cut (Mathematical Programming 2001, DOI 10.1007/pl00011432)
**Read level**: full. ZIB Preprint SC 99-31 (August 1999), all 35 file pages: text, Table 5.2, Tables 8.1–8.6 and the references. The column headings of Tables 8.1–8.5 are partly lost in the extraction and were matched to the printed keys (pp. 22, 25–26); Figure 5.2 was read from the text. The journal version (Math. Program. 90(3), 475–506) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Norbert Ascheuer (Intranetz GmbH, Berlin), Martin Grötschel (ZIB) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: Fischetti at DEI Padova, supported by CNR (p. 2). A computational companion to the three authors' polyhedral study of the ATSP-TW (ref. [4], pp. 7, 12), built on his ATSP and precedence-constrained ATSP polyhedra with Balas, Pulleyblank and Toth (refs. [10], [11], [22], [23]; pp. 10–12, 19); the advanced code uses the FORTRAN SD and D_k separation routines of Fischetti and Toth (p. 26). The paper does not say who did what.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: An industrial application: stacker-crane scheduling in automatic warehouses for Siemens Nixdorf (SNI), where judging online ATSP heuristics needs offline ATSP-TW optima; SNI supplied the instances (p. 3). The stated difficulty is that there is no "natural" IP formulation and no known way to tell which model computes best, so three models are implemented and computational experience decides (p. 3; see Quotes). The polyhedral route is chosen for three stated reasons: its success on the TSP and ATSP, separation codes available from related projects, and a host project built on LP techniques (p. 3). The literature gap: exact TSP-TW methods are mostly enumerative or dynamic programming, there is no standard benchmark, all tests use random data whose meaning for real instances is unclear, and windows active on about 50% of the nodes are reported hardest (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: when the formulation is open, implement the competing models on equal footing and let measurement choose, and say so in the introduction (pp. 3, 22–23).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Compare three models: Model 1, arc variables plus arrival times linked by big-M MTZ inequalities; Model 2, arc variables only, with infeasible-path elimination constraints from the companion paper; Model 3, arc variables plus integer arc-time variables y_ij without big M (Maffioli and Sciomachen, van Eijl). The projections of the first and third onto the arc variables are the second (pp. 6–8). Model 2 thus drops the time variables altogether. Cut families, all from the literature or the companion paper: infeasible-path constraints (tournament constraints (4.8), strengthenings (4.9)–(4.12) with checkable sufficient conditions in Lemma 4.13, concatenations (4.14)), lifted t-bounds, Balas's two-job cuts, ATSP inequalities (D_k^±, SD, 2-matching), SOP inequalities (π, σ, (π, σ), precedence-cycle-breaking, "special"), strengthened (π, σ) and strengthened MTZ (pp. 8–13). A family is omitted when the test bed never allows it: no strengthening of the π- and σ-inequalities was possible on these instances (p. 12). The winner then gets an advanced branch-and-cut code in ABACUS (pp. 18, 26).
  - Transferable technique: harvest cut families from every relaxation the problem contains (ATSP, SOP, one-machine scheduling), and drop the families your instances never produce (pp. 9–12).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: ATSP-TW on D = (V ∪ {0}, A) with arc durations, processing times, release and due dates, all nonnegative integers and d_i = ∞ allowed; waiting is allowed (pp. 2–3). The triangle inequality on ϑ_ij = p_i + c_ij is assumed throughout, as it holds in the application (p. 5). Finding a feasible solution is strongly NP-complete (p. 3) and deciding whether a path is feasible is NP-complete, hence the easily checkable sufficient conditions of Lemma 3.3 and Lemma 4.13 (pp. 5–6, 8–9). Data: SNI stacker-crane instances rbg*, enlarged by instances rbg27.b.x derived from rbg027a by relaxing randomly chosen windows, x being the number of nodes that keep a window (p. 22); up to 233 nodes (p. 26); downloadable (footnote, p. 22). Model 2 handles only TSP-type objectives, not makespan (p. 32).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: Preprocessing to a tighter equivalent formulation: window tightening (5.30)–(5.33), derivation of precedences, and elimination of reverse and transitive precedence arcs and of arcs whose concatenations with up to two further nodes are all infeasible, iterated; about 50% of the arcs are fixed or eliminated (pp. 13–15, Table 5.2). Heuristics: sorting rules, nearest feasible neighbour and two insertion rules, then six improvement moves in a fixed order (pp. 15–17), outperformed by an LP-exploitation heuristic run after every LP (p. 17): implicit enumeration when the LP support is very sparse (|A*| ≤ 1.25|V|), and always construction heuristics on costs c*_ij := x*_ij · c_ij (as printed; read literally this makes arcs outside the LP support free, so (1 − x*_ij)·c_ij may be meant, my reading, not checked against the journal version), improvement on the original costs, and a hash table on (solution value, heuristic) that skips repeated calls at the risk of missing an improvement, for a "dramatic" time saving (pp. 17–18). Initial LP: the 5-nearest-neighbour arcs plus the best tour, the other arcs priced at every 5th LP (pp. 18, 22). Separation: Padberg–Rinaldi for subtour constraints, then an attempt to strengthen each into a π-, σ- or pcb-inequality; shrinking for pcb and "special" inequalities; tournament constraints by enumerating the polynomially many candidate paths (Savelsbergh) and lifting; IPECs first by checking small modifications of paths already used, because in testing the LP reacted to each new IPEC by a small "detour" (p. 19); strengthened (π, σ) by a max-flow on a pruned support graph (pp. 19–20); pool separation first; a fixed order in which any routine that finds a cut ends the round (p. 20). Tailing off: branch when the last 10 LPs gave no bound improvement or the last 20 gave less than 1% (p. 22). Invariant: window updates follow only from variables fixed globally, never from local settings, so that window-based infeasible-path cuts stay valid in the whole tree (p. 21). Reduced-cost fixing; branching on the variable closest to 0.5, ties to the highest cost; depth-first for the model comparison, best-first by default in the ABACUS code after tests showed it slightly better (p. 21). Implementation: C on a SUN SPARC Station 10 with the CPLEX 4.09 callable library; the advanced code on a preliminary binary-only ABACUS with CPLEX 5.0 (pp. 22, 26). The advanced implementation exists only for Model 2, which the comparison favoured (p. 23) and which alone fitted the binary-only framework (p. 18).
  - Transferable technique: keep locally derived tightening out of global cuts: apply window updates only on global fixings, so every cut stays valid at every node (p. 21).
  - Transferable technique: after adding a cut, first re-separate small modifications of it, since the LP tends to sidestep a new cut by a small detour (p. 19).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: The model comparison uses ad hoc implementations of all three models with the same LP solver, machine and depth-first search, because the framework supported binaries only (p. 22). Eleven benchmark instances that all three models solved, "by and large" representative, in two run sets, from scratch and with the optimal solution supplied (p. 23). Metrics: nodes, LPs, rows, columns, total time, root bounds and root time, and the shares of time in LP, separation, pricing and heuristics (key, p. 22; Tables 8.1–8.2, p. 24). Result: Model 2 fastest in 16 of 22 runs, second in 2 and third in 4; Model 3 won 6, on instances with few active windows; Model 1 second in all but two (p. 23); my recount from Tables 8.1–8.2 agrees (p. 24). Model 3's trouble is diagnosed: numerical problems in the linking constraints when d_i = ∞, CPLEX's "infeasibility type 11", about 90% of the time in the LP solver, and more fractional LP solutions in Models 1 and 3 (p. 23). Instances above 69 nodes are absent from the comparison because only Model 2 solved them, which the paper says (p. 23). Not remarked in the text: supplying the optimum made all three codes slower on rbg27.b.04, Model 2 from 645 to 1,543 nodes and from 4:10.70 to 8:45.38 (Tables 8.1 and 8.2, p. 24). The advanced code: 50 instances of 12–233 nodes, 5 CPU hours each, default settings (p. 26); 32 solved and 18 stopped at the limit (my count, Table 8.3, p. 28); the largest solved has 127 nodes and the smallest unsolved 43 (p. 27). The paper finds no way to predict difficulty: rbg041a (43 nodes) ends at a 9.16% gap after 109,402 LPs and more than a million cuts handled (46,846 distinct), while rbg067a (69 nodes) takes 6 s (p. 26). Root gaps lie within 7% except on two instances, and the root bound within 5% of the optimum except on one (p. 26). Per-separator counts show 2-matching, D_k and T_k cuts seldom found, IPECs mostly tournament constraints, and inequalities (4.11) and (4.12) never found by the modification heuristic (p. 27; Tables 8.4–8.5, pp. 29–30); time shares per component in Table 8.6 (p. 31). Single runs; no seeds.
  - Transferable technique: two run sets, from scratch and with the optimum supplied, separate differences in bounding from differences in finding solutions (p. 23).
  - Transferable technique: report cuts found per separator call, and time shares per component, so that readers see which families pay (Tables 8.4 and 8.6, pp. 29, 31).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against enumerative and dynamic-programming methods for the TSP-TW (Christofides et al., Baker, Dumas et al., Balas and Simonetti, Bianco et al.) and branch-and-cut for related problems (Applegate and Cook on job shop, van Eijl on the delivery man) (p. 4); the literature's random data and lack of benchmarks are criticised (p. 4). Credits: Model 3 to Maffioli and Sciomachen and van Eijl (p. 8), MTZ strengthenings to Desrochers and Laporte (p. 12), tournament separation to Savelsbergh (p. 19), the SOP inequalities to Balas, Fischetti and Pulleyblank (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**: The abstract opens on a methodological observation, that equivalent models exist and which one computes best is not apparent, and qualifies its result, "at least for our special application" (p. 2). The conclusion concedes that the polyhedral approach is not "the unchallenged winning strategy" for the ATSP-TW and that dynamic programming may win on tight windows (p. 32), and lays numerical trouble on the models rather than on the software (p. 32; see Quotes). Implementation details get their own section (§7, pp. 18–22); a negative separator result is introduced with "Surprisingly" (p. 27).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: a computational comparison of three formulations, a branch-and-cut code that solves real instances "in a way that is satisfactory for practice", and public instances (pp. 22, 32). Limits stated concretely: Model 2 cannot handle makespan objectives (p. 32); instance difficulty is unpredictable (p. 26); dynamic programming and implicit enumeration may outperform the cutting-plane method when windows are tight (p. 32). Future work: research, "including very time consuming implementational work", to find where each methodology has its advantage (p. 32).
