### S042 · A Lagrangian heuristic for robustness, with an application to train timetabling (Transportation Science 2012, DOI 10.1287/trsc.1110.0378)
**Read level**: full (all 16 file pages of the author preprint: abstract, §§1–7, Algorithm 1, Tables 1–5). First-page check: title and authors Cacchiani, Caprara, Fischetti match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Valentina Cacchiani, Alberto Caprara · **Position in trajectory**: robust railway work of the EC project ARRIVAL (p. 14); it modifies the Caprara–Fischetti–Toth nominal TTP Lagrangian heuristic (Oper. Res. 2002, ref. [7]; p. 7) and is benchmarked against the light-robustness method of Fischetti, Salvagnin and Zanette (FSZ, Transp. Sci. 2009, ref. [11]; pp. 3, 12), whose code and validation tool Salvagnin and Zanette supplied (p. 14).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Approaches to robustness, from stochastic programming on, enlarge the model, multiply the computing time and force major changes to the nominal software (abstract p. 1; p. 2). The entry is an observation about an existing tool: the Lagrangian optimization scheme is "natively" suited to robustness once two features are added (p. 2). The aim is a heuristic that is cheap in programming effort and competitive with robust methods from the literature, borrowing an existing robustness concept rather than defining a new one (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: (a) artificial prizes b^t_a on station arcs whose stop exceeds the minimum, up to a 15-minute threshold, so that buffers enter objective (6) with a weight F_k (p. 7); (b) F_k is raised along the subgradient iterations (0.1, then 0.5 after 900 iterations, then +0.5 every 100 iterations until it exceeds 2.0), efficiency first and robustness later (p. 8). Every heuristic solution met on the way is kept, giving roughly "Pareto optimal" solutions from which the user or an expensive validation tool chooses (pp. 2, 9). The prize is weighted by the arc's position on the train's path, (1 − e^{−λp})(len(t) − p), low at both ends, after Kroon et al. (p. 8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Aperiodic TTP on one one-way corridor, a restriction made to compare with FSZ and use its validation tool, although the method is said to generalize to networks (p. 4); binary arc variables on a discretized time-space graph, with exponentially many clique constraints (4) for track capacity relaxed in Lagrangian fashion, implemented in an equivalent node-variable form (pp. 5–6); travel times fixed, so buffers are longer stops (p. 6). The validation tool assumes that all trains are scheduled and train precedences are fixed (p. 8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Subgradient optimization with a pool of recently violated cliques; at every iteration a constructive heuristic on the Lagrangian profits and a local search that reschedules shifted or stretched trains (pp. 7–8; Algorithm 1 RobuLag, p. 10). The finer robustness sum (9) is quadratic in x, so it serves only for a-posteriori selection; linearizations of (9) were tried inside the optimization and gave worse results than the rough proxy (6) (p. 9). Selection rule: for each efficiency threshold (99%, 95%, 90% of the best nominal value) validate only the solution of smallest robustness sum (p. 9). C, Pentium IV 3.2 GHz, 2 GB (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Six Trenitalia corridor instances with 41–400 trains (Table 1, p. 11); against the nominal heuristic the robust run costs 2.3 to 4.1 times its CPU time (my ratios from Table 2, p. 11); against FSZ, run by the authors with CPLEX 11.0 in two phases of 2 h each (p. 12), 13 of 18 FSZ solutions are dominated (p. 12; Table 3, p. 13), 13 of 18 strictly dominated and the opposite once under a 1,200 s limit, with the iteration schedule shortened (p. 12; Table 4, p. 14), and 14 of 18 dominated on combined instances of 500–1,000 trains with the schedule halved (p. 12; Table 5, p. 15); robustness is the average total delay from the FSZ simulation tool (p. 10); one run per instance, no seeds.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A simple modification of an existing Lagrangian heuristic that returns several robust solutions with different efficiency and robustness (pp. 3, 12–13). Limits stated: the adaptation "had to be carefully defined for the specific problem at hand", and its generality is a conviction left out of scope (p. 3); FSZ may not cancel trains while RobuLag may (p. 12). Future: other problem classes, railway networks with detours, rolling stock, train paths inside stations, and multi-objective heuristics built as modifications of single-objective Lagrangian ones (pp. 13–14).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ⚠ variant: the smallest-code step outside MIP. Robustness is added by "just a minor modification of an existing Lagrangian heuristic" instead of a larger robust model and new software (pp. 2, 7), and the expensive validator stays outside the loop (pp. 8–9).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant: for a heuristic the rougher model wins, here in the objective rather than the variables: the linear buffer-prize proxy (6) stays in the optimization, while the finer quadratic measure (9) is used only to select, after its linearizations did worse (p. 9).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the rival is the group's own FSZ code, run by the authors (pp. 10, 12), with the reference nominal value aligned to FSZ's first phase so that the efficiency thresholds mean the same in both arms (p. 12); but the arms differ in what they may do (cancellations) and in budget, and the dominance counts come from single runs (pp. 12–15).
- Taste mark 3 (modest claims) ✅: the purpose statement quoted below (p. 11).
- new-pattern candidate: "Weight sweep harvests a Pareto set" (the secondary weight is raised on a schedule within one iterative run and every intermediate solution is kept, pp. 2, 8, 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To add a second criterion to an iterative heuristic, put it into the objective with a weight raised on a fixed schedule (here after 900 iterations, then every 100) and keep every solution produced; one run returns a trade-off set instead of one point (pp. 8, 10).
- Keep the in-loop proxy linear and cheap; evaluate the real criterion offline with the expensive tool, only on solutions picked by a rule fixed in advance (one per efficiency threshold, smallest robustness sum) (pp. 8–9).
- When the rival defines its own reference value, re-run your method with the rival's reference so that thresholds are comparable (p. 12).
- Report dominance counts against the rival at two budgets and on scaled-up instances (pp. 12–15).

**Quotes**:
- "our purpose here is not to show that our approach outperforms all the methods for robust TTP available in the literature, but rather to show that, although it is a straightforward simple-minded modification of an existing approach for the nominal case, it yields comparable (in fact, better) results" (p. 11)
- "Note that the adaptation of the Lagrangian optimization scheme to deal with robustness had to be carefully defined for the specific problem at hand." (p. 3)

### D014 · Approximating the split closure (INFORMS Journal on Computing 2013, DOI 10.1287/ijoc.1120.0543)
**Read level**: full (all 26 file pages of the author preprint: §§1–5, Algorithms 1–3, Tables 1–5). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Salvagnin match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Domenico Salvagnin · **Position in trajectory**: the follow-up to the Fischetti–Salvagnin relax-and-cut framework for GMI cuts (Math. Prog. Comp. 2011; pp. 4, 15), in the closure-approximation line that the paper traces back to Fischetti and Lodi's first Chvátal closure (2007; p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The split closure closes almost 80% of the integrality gap on MIPLIB 3.0, but separating or optimizing over it exactly is NP-hard and "unacceptably slow in practice" (p. 2). The heuristic separators attack the problem from different sides, none dominates, and Bonami (2010) found that "even a trivial combination" approximates the closure better (p. 11). Entry: combine their strengths "in a sound way" (p. 11).
- **D2 Core idea**: Relax-and-cut is the backbone, chosen because it handles very many cuts, is numerically stable and is the fastest method on big instances (p. 11): it reads GMI cuts from the bases met while the Lagrangian dual is solved by subgradient, i.e. near-optimal, primal-feasible bases of the original LP (pp. 4–6). Plugged in: a lift-and-project separator over all violated elementary disjunctions at the end of each main iteration (p. 11), non-elementary disjunctions on two fractional integer basic variables with ±1 coefficients when the elementary ones fail (p. 18), and brute-force aggregation of two tableau rows with multipliers in the range {−2, 2} whenever a Lagrangian basis is used to derive GMI cuts (p. 11). The motivation is Theorem 1: a point outside the split closure is cut by a GMI cut from an integer combination of the rows of a possibly infeasible basis (p. 8), so GMI cuts from all feasible and infeasible bases (the lift-and-project closure) are not enough (p. 9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP in standard form with integer and continuous variables (p. 1); P bounded and nonempty for the Lagrangian dual (p. 4). Test set: MIPLIB 3.0 and MIPLIB 2003 without the instances where one round of GMI cuts does not improve the bound or no integer solution is known, following Dash and Goycoolea: 54 plus 20 instances (p. 12).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: A new short constructive proof of Theorem 1 (Andersen et al. 2005) from a result of Bonami (2010): the split is made elementary by a free integer variable x_{n+1} = πx, and the block structure of the CGLP basis shows that the GMI cut comes from an integral combination of the rows of a basis of A (pp. 8–9). Engineering: an eight-step GMI separator (basis refactor, row computed on its support, dense rows discarded, closest-bound complementing, slacks upgraded to integer when detected, back-substitution, dense or high-dynamism cuts discarded) (pp. 14–15); a cut pool with efficacy (10^−4) and orthogonality (cosine 0.1) filters, cuts with dynamism above 10^10 discarded, lower thresholds when the model's dynamism exceeds 10^7, caps of 1 GB and 10 times the number of rows with purges by efficacy and age (p. 13); tailing-off termination instead of a fixed iteration count (p. 15); a cache of lines from failed CGLPs that rules out later disjunctions without solving them (p. 19); aggregation limited to pairs of fractional integer basic rows, computed on the set S, with at most 10 partners per row (p. 20).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: C++, CPLEX 12.2 as black-box LP solver, Intel Core i5 2.66 GHz, one CPU per process, 1 h per instance (pp. 12–13); averages only, shifted geometric means (shift 0.1) for time and arithmetic means for gap closed (p. 12); mix2+aggr closes 71.2% in 3.28 s on MIPLIB 3.0 and 55.2% in 110.09 s on MIPLIB 2003, against about 80% for the exact closure (p. 21); a single-thread CPLEX branch-and-cut row is shown and labelled unfair (p. 21); on four hard instances (five rows, sp97ar also run with its optimum given upfront), adding the cuts to default CPLEX (4 cores, 10 h) cuts nsrand-ipx from 1,261 s to 114 s, solves a1c1s1 and sp97ar, and more than halves timtab2's final gap without closing it (pp. 22–23); one run per configuration, no seeds.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A combination of known and new ideas that approximates the split-closure bound in a "surprisingly small" time (p. 23). Limits stated: aggressive cuts pay only on very hard instances where the cuts close much of the gap (p. 22); MIPLIB 3.0 has become too easy, so even 3 s can be too much for a separator (p. 21); the cache saves about 40% of the CGLPs but only about 20% of the time (p. 20); aggregation doubles the time on the harder testbed (p. 21). No future directions are stated (p. 23).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: the loop is redesigned and the separator kept: ordinary GMI cuts are read from bases sampled along a subgradient trajectory instead of only from the optimum of the large LP (pp. 4–6, 11), and the fixed number of main iterations becomes tailing-off detection (p. 15).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 4, the ceiling): the approximation is placed against the exact closure's gap closed from the literature, "less than 9% away from the exact bound" (p. 21); the solver stays a black box for the LPs (p. 12) and receives the slack cuts in its own pool (p. 22).
- Method 4 ✗ contradiction (dated lapse, mitigated): the hard-MIP test keeps only instances where "our mix+aggr is able to close more than 70% of the integrality gap" (p. 22), a class defined by the new method's own output, which fails the swap test; the own lift-and-project code is compared with Bonami's published figures from "roughly equivalent machines" (p. 18). Mitigation: the claim is limited to "(some) hard MIPs" (p. 22) and the unfair CPLEX row is labelled as such (p. 21).
- H10 (heuristic, not a method) ✅: numerical safeguards in the separator and the pool before any cut reaches the LP (pp. 13–15).
- new-pattern candidate: "Failure certificates as a skip cache" (p. 19).
- new-pattern candidate: "Backbone plus plug-ins" (the scalable, stable method as backbone, complementary separators called at a fixed point of its loop, p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Keep the certificate of a failed separation: the pair (y, z) from a failed CGLP is a line through x* with a step interval; skip any later disjunction whose split interval lies inside it, and clear the cache when x* changes (p. 19).
- Replace a fixed number of cut rounds by tailing-off detection relative to the initial gap (below 10^−3 for 3 consecutive iterations, at most 100) (p. 15).
- Test a GMI cut's violation on the variables of S before computing the whole cut; cap cuts per basis (200) and primal simplex iterations per subgradient step (1,000) (pp. 15–16).
- To test a separator inside a solver, keep the cuts that are tight at the end of the loop in the formulation and pass the slack ones to the solver's cut pool; add a run with the optimal solution given upfront to remove heuristic variability (p. 22).
- Print the ceiling (the exact closure) and a full-solver row labelled as not comparable next to the method's numbers (p. 21).

- "Actually, our computational experience shows that not only the instance must be very hard for an aggressive cut generator to be worthwhile, but the gap closed by cutting planes must be significant to really speedup enumeration." (p. 22)

### S111 · Self-splitting of workload in parallel computation (CPAIOR 2014, LNCS, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-07046-9_28)
**Read level**: full (all 12 file pages of the author copy: §§1–5, Table 1). First-page check: title and authors Fischetti, Monaci, Salvagnin match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michele Monaci, Domenico Salvagnin · **Position in trajectory**: the conference version; the 2018 journal paper on SelfSplit for MILP by the same authors is D017 (work-list note). Here the test bed is a CP solver, with MIP named as a target (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Parallel tree search usually balances load by work stealing or a master process, with communication and synchronization that hurt scalability, and the deterministic variants are slower (pp. 1–2). Entry: modify a given deterministic sequential tree search so that each worker decides by itself which parts to process (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Every worker reads the whole input plus a pair (k, K), runs the same deterministic sampling phase (redundant work, no communication), then processes only the open nodes of its own color and discards the others; outputs are merged at the end (pp. 3–4). Vanilla version: pseudo-random colors given during sampling (pp. 4–5). Paused-node version: nodes judged easy enough are paused in a queue S and colored after sampling by sorting difficulty scores and assigning them round-robin (pp. 5–6). Balance is statistical: many more subproblems than workers (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: A deterministic original algorithm (if it is itself parallel, coloring right after a synchronization point), small input and output, a sampling phase that is not a bottleneck and a cheap merge (pp. 2, 4–5). Test instances: Gecode modeling examples that are infeasible or ask for all solutions, because first-solution speedups are uncorrelated with the number of workers; symmetry breaking added on some (p. 8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Every node is covered by at least one worker (p. 5). Difficulty estimate: log2 of the Cartesian product of the domains, compared with the root (the number of free variables for binaries), with range lists for integer variables and a capped count for set variables (pp. 6–7); a node is paused when the estimate drops by θ = 10, a 1,024-fold reduction, and if |S| < 2000 after sampling θ is doubled and the method restarted (p. 8). Implemented as a custom Gecode propagator that stores the node and fails, because a search engine was harder to write (pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Gecode 4.0, K ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64}, one thread per worker for determinism and reproducibility, ten instances; speedup at K = 64 from 36.63 (ortholatin 5, 29.3 s sequentially) to 61.34 (golomb 14, 19,051.9 s) (pp. 8–9); one parameter setting for all instances (p. 8); hardware not stated, no repeated runs.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A deterministic and almost communication-free paradigm (pp. 8–9); about 10 new lines parallelized two legacy FORTRAN ATSP codes with "surprisingly good speedups", for which no numbers are given (p. 9). Limit: unsuitable for solvers that learn global information during the search, although MIP codes collect cuts, pseudocosts and incumbents in their early nodes, i.e. during sampling (pp. 9–10). Future variants: fewer workers than colors as a heuristic or to estimate the sequential time as Ts + K·T, pause and resume, incumbent sharing, failure handling, redundant colors (pp. 10–11).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (step 3, the smallest code): the change needs "a few lines of codes" (p. 2), is written as a propagator because a search engine was harder (pp. 6–7), and about 10 new lines parallelized two legacy ATSP codes (p. 9).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (steps 3 and 5): one parameter setting on very different instances (p. 8), and the restriction of the test set is stated with its reason (p. 8).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant: variability is removed by construction (deterministic single-thread workers, p. 8) rather than measured; no seeds or spread are reported.
- new-pattern candidate: "Redundant deterministic prefix instead of communication" (all workers repeat the sampling phase, which shares the global information gathered there without any message, pp. 3–4, 9–10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Parallelize a deterministic tree search by adding (k, K), a sampling flag, a deterministic node coloring and a rule that skips other colors (pp. 4–5).
- Color paused nodes by sorting difficulty estimates and dealing them round-robin; restart with a doubled threshold if too few nodes were paused (pp. 6, 8).
- Measure scaling only on infeasible or enumerate-all instances, one thread per worker (p. 8).
- Run fewer workers than colors to bound the K-worker time from below and to estimate the sequential time as Ts + K·T (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Note that, despite the fact that our instances are very different, we used exactly the same parameter tuning on all of them, showing that the method is quite robust." (p. 8)
- "Therefore, performing the sampling phase redundantly in parallel by all workers has the advantage of sharing a potentially big amount of global information without communication—a distinguishing feature of SelfSplit." (p. 10)

### S117 · MIP-and-refine matheuristic for smart grid energy management (International Transactions in Operational Research 2015, DOI 10.1111/itor.12034)

- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: The greedy schedules appliances by nonincreasing total energy on a restricted problem (constant phase durations and energies), tries every start shift of the first phase and the best slot for each next phase within its delay window, in O(N n_max D_max d_max m) time (pp. 10–12; Algorithm 1, p. 13), and restarts with shifted priority vectors (p. 10). - **D8 Contributions and limits**: "we cannot claim deep theoretical contributions": the paper is offered as a case study (p. 3); no claim against sophisticated heuristics from the literature, which would need much more extensive comparisons (p. 16); the greedy does not use the batteries that the MIP exploits (p. 16). Future: the approach on other classes of very difficult problems (p. 17).

### S029 · On handling indicator constraints in mixed integer programming (Computational Optimization and Applications 2016, DOI 10.1007/s10589-016-9847-8)
**Read level**: full (all 18 file pages of the author preprint dated 24 April 2015: §§1–7, Tables 1–4). First-page check: title and Fischetti among the seven authors match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Pietro Belotti (FICO), Pierre Bonami (IBM), Andrea Lodi, Michele Monaci, Amaya Nogales-Gómez, Domenico Salvagnin (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: a joint study with developers at IBM and FICO (p. 1), supported by Padova's project "Exploiting randomness in Mixed Integer Linear Programming" (p. 16).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Big-M (indicator) constraints give weak continuous relaxations and numerical trouble (p. 2); the ramp-loss SVM MIQP of Brooks (2011) is solved to optimality only on a limited number of instances (p. 4). Entry: an "empirical and surprising observation": the global solver Couenne on the nonconvex complementary reformulation (11) is consistently faster than the commercial MIQP solvers on the big-M model (pp. 1, 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Explain the surprise (§§4–5), then move its cause into MILP (§6). The ingredients are aggressive bound tightening and iterative strengthening of the McCormick linearization (pp. 11–13). MILP gets them online, as locally valid big-M cuts recomputed at every node (CPLEX's local implied bound cuts on indicator constraints, at a very aggressive setting), or a priori, by iterative domain reduction that solves node-limited MILPs minimizing and maximizing each ω_i and b under the cutoff, cyclically until no bound improves (pp. 13–14).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: A convex MIQP with big-M constraints (5) (p. 4) against the nonconvex form (10)–(15); Proposition 2.1: every local optimum of the continuous relaxation of (10)–(15) is mixed binary, since a fractional z̄_i can be raised to 1 with a lower objective (p. 5). Test set: 23 artificial TypeB instances of Brooks, n = 100, d = 2, chosen to control both data and size (p. 5).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Couenne builds its own big-M through McCormick inequalities with a bound per row (p. 7), branches on the binaries by default, and branching on the continuous ϑ is better still (Table 2, p. 8); the objective cutoff gives ω_i ∈ [−√(2U), √(2U)] (p. 10); FBBT and probing (pp. 9–11) and the bound updates for ω, b, ξ (p. 12). Ablations on Couenne: McCormick cuts only at the root, or bound reduction switched off, both worsen performance dramatically (pp. 11–12). The text does not say whether each node-limited bound MILP returns its dual bound, which validity requires (p. 14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Intel Xeon E3-1220V2 3.10 GHz, 1 h limit (p. 6); Couenne (a CouenneClassifier branch, ref. [10], p. 17) solves 22 of 23 and IBM-Cplex none (Table 1, p. 6), and Gurobi and Xpress behave similarly (no table, p. 6); Couenne with continuous branching solves all 23 (p. 8); an L1-norm objective, turning the comparison into MILP versus MINLP, changes nothing (p. 9); CPLEX 12.6.1 with 4 threads: 1 of 23 in default and 15 with local implied bound cuts (pp. 14–15); iterative domain reduction solves all 23 in under a minute, below 30 s and 150k nodes on average (pp. 15–16); one run per instance, and the thread count of the Table 1 runs is not stated.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: Aggressive bound tightening is "often overlooked in MILP" and a building block when indicator constraints and disjunctions are present (pp. 3, 16); one of the two methods is part of IBM-Cplex 12.6.1 (pp. 15–16). Limits: one class of 23 instances with n = 100 (p. 5), described as "a special class of classification problems" (p. 15). Future: other ingredients of MINLP may benefit MILP (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (steps 1–2): stock solvers first on the model as given (CPLEX, Gurobi, Xpress, p. 6; Couenne out of the box, p. 5), then a built-in CPLEX feature at a very aggressive setting (p. 14), then the authors' preprocessing, which states the critical sub-decision (variable bounds) as auxiliary node-limited MILPs solved by the same solver (p. 14).
- Method 1 ⚠ variant (step 5, subtraction applied to a rival's code): the winning solver's components are switched off one at a time until removal hurts (pp. 11–12).
- Method 4 ✅ evidence: alternative explanations are removed before the claim: three MIQP solvers on the big-M model (p. 6) and an L1 objective that takes the MIQP extension out of the comparison (p. 9); the authors concede that comparing different methods and codes "is anyway hard to perform" (p. 7). No seeds.
- H1 ✅ (invert the reflex: big-M and MILP are the common approach when logical constraints are the only nonconvexity, p. 2) and H3 ✅ (borrow MINLP's bound tightening for MILP, pp. 13, 16) (heuristics, not methods).
- new-pattern candidate: "Dissect the surprising winner, transplant the ingredient" (pp. 3, 7–14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a solver that is expected to lose wins, switch its components off one at a time on your instances and run a control that removes the obvious confound (here an L1 objective) before transplanting anything (pp. 9, 11–12).
- Transplant a bound-tightening ingredient into MILP in two forms and report both: online local cuts (a big-M recomputed per node from the local bounds) and a priori domain reduction by node-limited min and max MILPs under the cutoff, repeated until no bound moves (pp. 13–14).
- Bound free continuous variables from the objective cutoff (here ω_i ∈ [−√(2U), √(2U)]) before building any relaxation (p. 10).

**Quotes**:

- "Through a careful analysis of Couenne’s features and components we have been able to isolate those that make a difference, namely aggressive bound tightening and iterative strengthening of the McCormick linearization." (p. 15)

### S086 · Proximity Benders: a decomposition heuristic for stochastic programs (Journal of Heuristics 2016, DOI 10.1007/s10732-015-9306-1)
**Read level**: partial (all 13 file pages of the author preprint dated 7 December 2015, §§1–5 and Tables 1–3 read, but the text layer drops words and phrases throughout: the first sentences of the abstract (p. 1), a recurring noun, apparently "subproblem(s)" (pp. 3–5, 12), the description of the second baseline (p. 5) and parts of several result sentences (pp. 6, 8, 10, 12); the tables are intact). First-page check: title and authors Boland, Fischetti, Monaci, Savelsbergh match (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Natashia Boland, Michele Monaci, Martin Savelsbergh · **Position in trajectory**: applies Fischetti and Monaci's proximity search (J. Heuristics 2014, ref. [10]) to Benders masters, with the Benders cut recipe of Fischetti, Salvagnin and Zanette (2010, ref. [11]) (pp. 1, 3, 13).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Benders decomposition is the usual tool for huge stochastic programs, but convergence can take so long that the method is unsatisfactory as a heuristic (abstract, p. 1); proximity search moves from a feasible solution to a nearby better one, so the two complement each other when good rather than optimal solutions are wanted (p. 2). Target: instances that a MILP solver cannot load or whose root LP is already prohibitive (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Proximity search on the Benders master: the Hamming distance to the incumbent ỹ as objective, the cutoff imposed softly with a variable z0 of large cost M0, and the master solved by any black-box MILP solver; slack-augmented second-stage problems return feasibility or optimality cuts, and the incumbent moves when all slacks are zero and the cost improves (pp. 3–4; Algorithm 2, p. 4). The new master objective changes "drastically" the sequence of master points and so the cuts (p. 4). The nesting reverses Rei et al.'s local branching inside Benders: here Benders runs inside an outer local search (p. 4). A repair heuristic raises components of an infeasible y through auxiliary problems (pp. 4–5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Binary first-stage y and LP second stages in block structure, with a feasible solution assumed easy to find (p. 3); the repair assumes monotone feasibility, y′ ≥ y feasible whenever y is (p. 5). Instances: stochastic capacitated facility location from Bodur et al. (16 instances with 250 and 16 with 500 scenarios, p. 6), stochastic network interdiction classes 3 and 4 with budgets 30–90 (p. 8), and 35 fixed-charge multicommodity network design instances with 2,000 generated scenarios (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation (brief)**: Minimum improvement θ = 10^−5 times the incumbent value, a non-aggressive policy that lets θ decrease (p. 5); large penalties M_k push the second stage towards feasibility, and the repair heuristic covers the rest (p. 12); C with CPLEX 12.6.1 (p. 5). No proofs.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Single thread, 1 h, Intel Xeon E3-1220V2 3.10 GHz, 16 GB (p. 5); arms Cplex default on the full MILP, a Benders arm whose description is lost in the text (p. 5) and ProxyBenders; primal integral, wins, improved instances and improving solutions at 100, 600 and 3,600 s (pp. 5–6). On facility location Cplex has most wins at 1 h (16 against 3 with 250 scenarios, 13 against 6 with 500), although ProxyBenders keeps the lower primal integral with 500 scenarios (37.06 against 46.30) (p. 7); on interdiction (p. 9) and network design (p. 11) ProxyBenders has the lowest primal integral and most wins at 1 h, e.g. 21 wins and 163.78 against Cplex's 5 and 236.75 on network design (p. 11), where Cplex cannot solve the root LP of 20 instances within the hour (p. 10). One run per instance and arm, no seeds.
- **D8 Contributions and limits**: A proof-of-concept claimed as the method of choice for very large stochastic programs that need good solutions fast, especially when the LP relaxation is prohibitive (p. 12); the implementation is not tailored to any problem (p. 12); the facility-location set is "inadequate to fully reveal the power" of the method (p. 6). Future: sensitivity to the initial solution, more aggressive θ schemes, cut management, not solving every second-stage problem at each iteration, randomization as in the feasibility pump, parallelization (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ evidence (step 3, change the master objective): the Benders cut recipe is kept and the master objective becomes a proximity function, which changes the master points and therefore the cuts (pp. 3–4).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence: the modified master "can be solved by any black-box MILP solver" (p. 3), and proximity search runs the solver as a black box (p. 2).
- Method 4 ⚠ variant: the facility-location set on which Cplex wins at 1 h is kept and printed (pp. 6–7), but the next sets are introduced as "(somewhat) more appropriate to show the benefits" of the method (p. 8), within the scope stated at the start (very large instances, p. 2).
- Method 3 ✗ contradiction (dated lapse): a comparison of heuristics by primal integral from one run per instance and arm, without seeds; randomization appears only as future work, credited to the feasibility pump (pp. 5, 12).
- new-pattern candidate: "Role reversal of nested methods" (an outer local search drives an inner black-box decomposition, the reverse of the usual nesting, pp. 1, 4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Turn a Benders code into a heuristic by changing only the master: Hamming objective to the incumbent and a soft cutoff (a variable with a large cost), cuts from slack-augmented second-stage problems (pp. 3–4).
