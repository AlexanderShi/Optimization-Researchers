### D013 · MIPLIB 2010 (Mathematical Programming Computation 3(2):103–163, 2011, doi:10.1007/s12532-011-0025-9)
**Read level**: full. Read completely: §§1–8 and the acknowledgments (pp. 1–26, 43–46, 51, 56–57), including Table 3's instance descriptions (pp. 19–26) and the result Tables 7–8 (pp. 47–55). Skimmed: Table 4's per-instance statistics (pp. 27–42, first rows of each page) and the references (pp. 57–61, for self-citations).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Thorsten Koch (first and corresponding), Erling Andersen, Oliver Bastert, Timo Berthold, Robert E. Bixby, Emilie Danna, Gerald Gamrath, Ambros M. Gleixner, Stefan Heinz, Andrea Lodi, Hans Mittelmann, Ted Ralphs, Domenico Salvagnin, Daniel E. Steffy, Kati Wolter. Achterberg is listed at IBM Deutschland, Böblingen (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: CPLEX years. The paper succeeds his MIPLIB 2003 with Koch and Martin (ref. [3], p. 2). It cites his ZR 08-29 for the effect of redundant constraints and bounds (p. 13) and Hybrid branching for tie-breaking (p. 13), and it includes his instance aflow40b (Table 3, p. 19). The variability part draws on Danna's 2008 talk [42] (pp. 13–14) and on her 2008–2010 work at ILOG and IBM, acknowledged on p. 57.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: MIPLIB 2003 had been solved down. At its release 22 instances were easy, 3 hard and 35 open; by the end of 2010 the counts were 41, 15 and 4 (Fig. 1, p. 3). Researchers had also been testing on topic-specific subsets of its 60 instances, which gave inadequate test sets (p. 4). A second entry is a tuning bias: solvers are tuned on models already known to be solvable, so instances beyond today's solvers were added on purpose (p. 4). The problem is stated as a community need, a library broadened in concept and assembled by academia and industry together: 361 instances in several test sets, among them an 87-instance benchmark set and a 164-instance challenge set (abstract, pp. 1–2).
  - Transferable: when a benchmark has been solved down, rebuild it as several sets that each answer one question, and add instances that current codes cannot solve, to offset the tuning bias toward solvable models (pp. 4–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: A library made of purpose-specific test sets with stated entry rules, shipped with the tools for using it.
  - Eight sets: benchmark, infeasible, primal, xxl, reoptimize, tree, unstable and challenge (Table 1, p. 5).
  - A test engine that runs every solver the same way, and an exact-arithmetic solution checker, both new for MIPLIB (p. 2; §§3–4, pp. 8–13).
  - Performance variability measured per instance and printed in the results table (§5, pp. 13–18; column VS, pp. 47–50).
  - A five-solver snapshot that declines to rank the solvers (§7, pp. 46–56).

  The benchmark set was chosen by a staged review and most other sets by predefined rules (p. 6). Against MIPLIB 2003 (p. 2), three things changed: the scale (about 2,000 candidates, 361 kept, pp. 4–6), the authorship (academia and industry, p. 1) and the tools. The easy/hard/open classification stays (p. 3).
  - Transferable: ship a benchmark with its run scripts, its checker and a file of reliable primal bounds, so that every user runs and checks it the same way (pp. 11–13).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - Format: MILP in MPS, whose 12-character fields can make the file itself an approximation of the intended model (p. 9). After reading, scaling and feasibility and integrality tolerances, the solver works on a perturbed region (p. 9). An infeasible instance can therefore become feasible within tolerances (p. 10).
  - Variable types: 116 pure binary programs; only 13 instances have no binaries, and only 72 of 361 have general integers (Table 5, p. 43).
  - Matrices: sparse, with 1.6 % average density and 11 instances above 10 %; 201 instances have more columns than rows and 160 the reverse (p. 45).
  - Entry rules, per set:
    - benchmark: solvable by at least one solver, stated as within 2 h in Table 1 (p. 5), with 24 h as the proposal's minimum and almost all solvable in under 1 h in the result (p. 6);
    - reoptimize: at least 500 simplex iterations per node LP on average, root excluded, and at least 100 nodes (p. 7);
    - tree: at least 10⁶ nodes (p. 7);
    - xxl: more than 10⁶ variables or constraints, or more than 10⁷ non-zeros, and not largely removable by standard presolve (p. 7);
    - primal: the root LP bound after presolve equals the optimum (p. 7);
    - challenge: not solved within 2 h by any solver (p. 8);
    - unstable: ill-conditioned bases, coefficients near the solvers' infinity, strong sensitivity to tolerances, or disagreeing solver answers (p. 8).
  - The thresholds depend on the computing environment and are given as guiding values (p. 7).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: No theorems. The design devices:
  - Checker: GMP rational arithmetic on the MPS text, with no rounding of decimals to nicer fractions (0.3333333 is kept as 3,333,333/10,000,000) (p. 10). It tests only the feasibility of the returned solution and the consistency of its objective value: optimality certificates of polynomial size are not expected to exist for MIP, while feasibility can be checked in polynomial time (p. 10). Absolute tolerance 10⁻⁴ for constraints and for integrality; the objective passes at an absolute error of 10⁻⁴ or a relative error of 10⁻⁷; the maximum violations are reported (pp. 10–11).
  - Test engine: deterministic mode where the solver offers it, which for multithreaded runs means removing all timing dependencies (fn. 2, p. 11). Relative gap limit 0. Wall-clock time measured externally and rounded up, file reading included (p. 11).
    - Gap: gap = (pb − db)/inf{|z| : z ∈ [db, pb]}, chosen because it is monotone in both bounds and bounds the relative gap; its undefined cases are listed (p. 11).
    - Statuses: ok/stopped/abort for the solver; ok/fail/mismatch/error for the solution, where mismatch means a contradiction with a reliable known primal bound (pp. 11–12).
    - Layout: run.sh with a run_xyz.sh per solver, parse.awk with a parse_xyz.awk per solver; a new solver needs only its two scripts (pp. 12–13).
  - Reliable primal values: round and fix the integer variables of a reported solution and solve the remaining LP exactly with QSopt_ex. The result is a truly feasible solution, not a proof of optimality (pp. 12, 43). The LP relaxation values are exact wherever QSopt_ex finished (p. 43).
  - Variability score: the coefficient of variation of solve time over n permutations of rows and columns (pp. 14–15).
  - Attention level: AL = p_ill-posed + 0.3·p_unstable + 0.01·p_suspicious, from CPLEX 12.2.0.2's histogram of basis condition numbers, with a node limit of 1,000 and a time limit of 2 days; the paper states that it measures an algorithm on an instance, not the model itself (pp. 43–44).
  - Transferable: where correctness cannot be certified, certify the part that can be (here feasibility, in exact arithmetic, on the original text, within stated tolerances) and say why the rest is not checked (pp. 10–11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Benchmark selection (p. 6):
    1. About 2,000 candidates; duplicates removed; homogeneous groups cut to fewer than ten; instances solved by SCIP or CBC in under a minute dropped.
    2. Eight teams examined the remaining 659 independently, and two instances were added late for under-represented classes.
    3. A proposal of 100 instances plus 45 substitutes was then refined: instances were exchanged for class balance, for being too easy or too hard on other machines with the developer versions, and for numerical difficulty.
    4. Tie-breaks favoured new contributions and stable performance and disfavoured unknown applications. The selection runs used a dual-core Xeon E5420 with 16 GB (p. 5).
  - Snapshot (pp. 46, 51):
    - Solvers at default settings: cbc 2.6.4, cplex 12.2.0.2, gurobi 4.5 beta0, scip/spx 2.0.1.3/1.5.0.3 and xpress 7.2 RC (Table 6, p. 46).
    - Machine: two six-core Xeon X5680, Hyperthreading and Turbo Boost off, 32 GB (p. 46).
    - Runs: one thread with 1 h and 8 GB (Table 7), and 12 threads with 1 h and 24 GB, with a speedup column (Table 8).
    - Columns: nodes; time, or the gap at the time limit; solutions failing the checker in italics; aborts footnoted (out of memory, segmentation faults, a CPLEX bug, constraint violations); and a Best column (pp. 46–55).
    - glpk and lp_solve were left out, since each solved under 20 % (p. 46).
  - Findings, stated without a ranking:
    - Every solver times out on at least 12 instances, yet only four are unsolved by all; every solver is the fastest somewhere; the geometric mean of the Best column is under half that of any single solver (p. 51).
    - Going from 1 to 12 threads gains about a factor of 3 (p. 51).
    - The three commercial solvers have equal 12-thread geometric means within measurement precision, yet their slowest-to-fastest ratio exceeds 1,000 on some instance, with a geometric mean above 5 and a median above 3 (pp. 51, 56).
  - Noise checks: on a 14 % faster machine, geometric-mean speedups ranged from 8.5 % to almost 20 % by solver, and repeated deterministic 12-thread runs differed by up to 73 s (p. 51).
  - Variability (pp. 15–16, 18):
    - Protocol: 100 permutations per model with scip/spx, on models solved within 4 h; 10 h limit; scores affected by the limit in italics (p. 15).
    - No instance behaved identically on all 100 permutations; max/min ratios ran from 1.29 to 915.
    - On 67 instances (45.1 h in total), a best-permutation oracle gives a factor of 1.77 and the worst permutation a factor of 3.82.
    - The original order is 14 % faster than the permutation mean but only 4 % faster than the median (pp. 15–16).
    - Scores range from 0.05 to 2.23 (p. 16).
    - On roll3000 (32-core machine), adding one thread lowered the node count 17 times and raised it 14 times (Fig. 4, pp. 16, 18).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Lineage named: the MIPLIB line from Bixby, Boyd and Indovina (1992), through MIPLIB 3 by Bixby, McZeal, Ceria and Savelsbergh (1996), to MIPLIB 2003 by Achterberg, Koch and Martin (p. 2).

  - Voice and opening: a collective we. The motivating numbers come first, a 55,000-fold algorithmic speedup and about 10⁸ overall in 20 years (p. 3), and are qualified at once as giving a misleading impression that all MIPs are easy (p. 4).
  - Hedges at the edge of knowledge: whether variability correlates with alternative optimal root bases is unknown, many questions are open, and it is unclear whether variability is only a disturbance (p. 14). Small score differences are not to be over-read (p. 15).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: infrastructure, made of a library, entry rules, a checker, a test engine, a variability score and a snapshot of the state of the art (pp. 1–2).
  - Limits, each concrete:

**Transferable techniques**:
- Benchmark entry rules as measurable thresholds: iterations per node, tree size, problem size, root bound equal to the optimum. Label them as guiding values and cross-check membership on several codes and machines (p. 7).
- Per-instance variability score: the coefficient of variation of time over many permutations, with the time limit stated and limit-affected scores flagged (pp. 14–15).
- To show skew, report the best and worst permutation oracles (1.77 and 3.82) and compare the original run with both the mean and the median (14 % against 4 %) (pp. 15–16).
- Check returned solutions in exact arithmetic against the original text file, with separate tolerances for constraints, integrality and the objective (pp. 10–11).
- Make primal bounds reliable by fixing the rounded integers and solving the remaining LP exactly (pp. 12, 43).
- Before trusting a timing difference, rerun on a second machine and repeat deterministic parallel runs. Here that moved geometric means by 8.5–20 % and single runs by up to 73 s (p. 51).
- A gap definition that is monotone and bounds the relative gap, with its undefined cases written out (p. 11).

### R017 · Rounding and Propagation Heuristics for Mixed Integer Programming (ZIB-Report 11-29, Zuse Institute Berlin, 2011; no DOI, urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-13255)
**Read level**: full. Read completely: the report text, file pp. 3–8 (§§1–5 and the references). Appendix Table 2 (pp. 9–18): all 82 per-instance rows were read and parsed; the p(t) charts extract as axis numbers only. The published version (D015) was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Timo Berthold, Gregor Hendel (three authors in alphabetical order, roles not stated); Achterberg is at IBM Deutschland (pp. 1, 3) · **Place in trajectory**: CPLEX years. This is an overview of SCIP's cheap heuristics, whose details are referred to his thesis [1], Berthold's master's thesis [2] and Hendel's bachelor's thesis [3] (pp. 3, 8). It is the first occurrence in his record of the primal integral, which the skill knows only from Berthold's 2013 ORL paper (Method 2, Limitations; not read) and from his own CPAIOR 2017 slides (Heuristic 8). The test set is MIPLIB 2010 (D013; p. 6).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap in measurement, not a new algorithm. Early solutions matter: they prune the tree, enable dual reductions, prove feasibility, and may satisfy a user within a gap (p. 3). The usual measures do not capture the trade-off between speed and solution quality (p. 5). These are time and nodes to optimality, and time to a first, an optimal or a gap-target solution. Scope: only heuristics built from cheap steps, rounding and propagation (abstract, p. 3).
  - Transferable: when the standard measures cannot separate your component class, define the measure first and make it part of the contribution (pp. 5–6).
- **D2 Core idea**: The report has two parts.
  - (i) An overview of SCIP's lock-based heuristics (pp. 4–5): rounding (Simple Rounding, Rounding, ZI Round), propagation (Shifting, Shift-and-Propagate) and improvement (Oneopt, Twoopt). The abstract announces eight approaches (p. 3), but the body describes these seven.
  - (ii) The primal integral (pp. 5–6). The primal gap γ ∈ [0, 1] of the incumbent (eq. (2)) defines a step function p(t), which is 1 until a first incumbent is found (eq. (3)), and P(T) is its integral up to T (eq. (4)). The measure rewards good solutions found early. P(t_max)/t_max reads as the average solution quality over the run, so a smaller value means a better expected incumbent at a random stopping time (p. 6).
  - Transferable: pair a cheap constructive heuristic with a cheap improvement heuristic aimed at its known bias. Rounding toward fewer locks over-satisfies rows, and Oneopt recovers the objective (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - Problem: MIP (1), min cᵀx s.t. Ax ≤ b, l ≤ x ≤ u, x_j ∈ ℤ for j ∈ J (p. 3).
  - Locks: a column's up-locks count its positive coefficients and its down-locks its negative ones. This presumes rows in ≤ form (p. 4).
  - Safety rule of Simple Rounding: a variable without down-locks can be rounded down, and one without up-locks rounded up, without violating any row (p. 4).
  - Primal gap: it needs an optimal or best known solution, and it is set to 1 if the two objective values differ in sign (p. 6). It is defined on one solver log in a fixed computing environment (p. 6).
  - Test set: the MIPLIB 2010 benchmark without its four infeasible instances and without mspp16, which ran out of memory in presolve: 82 instances (p. 6).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: No theorems.
  - Rounding: round toward fewer locks while no row is violated; then choose a rounding that reduces the violation, with locks as tie-breaker; otherwise abort (p. 4).
  - ZI Round (Wallace): shift each fractional value by the largest LP-feasible step toward integrality, possibly over several passes, up to an iteration limit (p. 4).
  - Shifting: also shifts continuous and integral-valued variables, and stops after a set number of non-improving shifts to avoid cycling (pp. 4–5).
  - Shift-and-Propagate: start from bounds, fix variables in nondecreasing order of impact so as to reduce infeasibility most, propagate, and on infeasibility reset and postpone the variable (p. 5).
  - Oneopt: shifts single variables in order of objective impact (p. 5). Twoopt: shifts pairs that share a set ratio of rows (p. 5).
  - LP use: propagation heuristics solve no LP, unlike diving and LNS heuristics (p. 4).
  - Measure device: each instance's P(t_max) is normalised by the run with all heuristics off before a geometric mean is taken (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Design: four SCIP settings (p. 6):
    - no heuristics (Heur OFF);
    - only the rounding and propagation heuristics (R&P);
    - all default heuristics except these (NoR&P);
    - the default (DEF).

    Limits: 1 h and 4 GB, on one 3.00 GHz Core 2 Extreme X9650 with 8 GB (p. 6). The report gives no SCIP version, no LP solver, no seeds and no repeated runs (pp. 3–8), although MIPLIB 2010, its own test set, recommends permutations in the same year (D013 p. 18).
  - Results (Table 1, p. 7), for DEF, NoR&P, R&P and Heur OFF:
    - normalised integral φ(P): 0.44, 0.47, 0.61, 1.0;
    - geometric mean time to the first solution φ(t₁): 8.54, 17.48, 10.88, 57.66;
    - solve time: worse with the cheap heuristics added, 712.90 for DEF against 676.10 for NoR&P, and this loss is printed.
  - My recomputation from Table 2 (pp. 9–18): Table 1's solve-time means equal the unshifted geometric means of the per-instance column. φ(P) cannot be reproduced from the rounded per-instance values, and no shift is stated (p. 7).
  - Illustration: on n3seq24, Heur OFF finds the optimum first, yet DEF has the smallest integral (pp. 6–7).
  - Losses not discussed in the text (my count from Table 2, pp. 9–18):
    - On 13 of the 82 instances DEF's normalised integral exceeds 1, i.e. it is worse than with no heuristics. Examples: ns1208400 at 7.76 (p. 15), pigeon-10 at 4.84 (p. 16) and neos-1601936 at 3.38 (p. 14).
    - Time-outs at 3,600 s: 26 for DEF, 22 for NoR&P, 26 for R&P and 28 for Heur OFF.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Sources: short and in-house, namely SCIP and his thesis [1], the two student theses [2, 3], Wallace's ZI Round [5] and MIPLIB 2010 [4] (pp. 3, 8).
  - Contrast: the heuristics are set against diving and LNS heuristics (p. 4).
  - The new measure: not compared with any measure from the literature (pp. 5–6).
- **D7 Writing**: Six report pages and a ten-page appendix (pp. 3–18).
  - The measure: formalised in three numbered definitions, followed by a plain reading as average incumbent quality (pp. 5–6).
  - The ranking: one graded chain, from slightly better through considerably better to much better, with the remark that the other measures show it only in part (p. 7).
  - The time loss: stated in the body (p. 7), not in the abstract (p. 3).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution: a measure (the primal integral) and an evaluation design, with the heuristic overview as context (pp. 3, 5–7).
  - Limits stated: the time loss (p. 7), and, implicitly, the need for a best known solution (p. 6).
  - Limits not addressed:
    - the instances where the default heuristics worsen the integral (Table 2);
    - variability;
    - the dependence of P on machine speed through t (p. 6).
  - No future work is named; the text ends by pointing to the appendix (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 evidence ✅ (co-signed). The four settings are step 1 of Method 4 for an interacting family: all off (Heur OFF), the group alone (R&P), the group knocked out of the default (NoR&P), and the default (DEF) (p. 6). The per-instance tables follow in the appendix (pp. 9–18).
  - Table 1 also illustrates Misreading 2 (my reading): measured alone, the cheap heuristics move φ(P) from 1.0 to 0.61; knocked out of the default, they move it only from 0.44 to 0.47 (p. 7).
  - Era: plain geometric means, no shift and no brackets (p. 7), consistent with Method 4's era note.
- Method 2 variant ⚠: the payoff is redefined for a primal component. Time to optimality, the skill's payoff, still stands beside the new measure, and its loss is printed (p. 7). That follows step 1 of Method 2, except that the loss is not in the abstract (p. 3).
  - Attribution: the skill credits the primal integral to Berthold 2013 (Method 2, Limitations), but this report has it in 2011, with Achterberg first of three authors in alphabetical order (pp. 3, 5–6).
- Also evidence for Research Heuristic 8 (metric by the user's goal): the measure is built for what primal heuristics are for, finding good solutions early (pp. 5–6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- The primal integral, step by step (pp. 5–6):
  1. γ(x̃) = |cᵀx̃_opt − cᵀx̃| / max{|cᵀx̃_opt|, |cᵀx̃|}, with γ = 0 if both values are 0 and γ = 1 if their signs differ.
  2. p(t) = 1 before the first incumbent.
  3. P(T) = Σᵢ p(t_{i−1})(t_i − t_{i−1}) over the times at which the incumbent changes.
- Normalise each instance's integral by the all-off run before averaging, so that the reference setting scores exactly 1.00 (pp. 7, 9).
- For an interacting family of components, use four settings (all off, group alone, group knocked out, default). This shows contributions that do not add (pp. 6–7).
- Print per-instance values and p(t) step charts in an appendix, so that the instances where the component hurts stay visible (pp. 9–18).
- Lock test for safe rounding: a variable without down-locks can be rounded down without violating any ≤ row, and one without up-locks rounded up (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Supercomputers deliver years of CPU time per wall-clock hour; the problem is how to turn that into solved hard MIP instances (abstract, p. 3).
  - The concrete gap: in 2010 ParaSCIP solved two of the six open MIPLIB 2003 instances, ds and stp3d, only through more than ten restarts from checkpoints (pp. 3, 5).
  - The aims: single uninterrupted runs, better bounds for the four still open, and an account of what changed (abstract, p. 3).
  - Why parallel MIP is hard (p. 5):
    - decisions in different subtrees depend on each other, as with history-based branching (his Hybrid branching [5] is cited);
    - much work comes before the tree search (presolve, cuts);
    - almost all sequential improvements shrink the tree, which makes parallelization less effective and needs more communication.
  - Transferable: state how progress elsewhere in the field makes your target harder (smaller trees leave less parallel work), and design for it (dynamic load balancing) instead of assuming that more cores help (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Wrap a state-of-the-art sequential solver in the UG framework. One LoadCoordinator and many Solvers communicate over MPI. The instance is presolved once and the presolved instance broadcast; after that a subproblem travels as a list of bound changes (pp. 5–6).
  - Ramp-up, two ways: normally, by collecting p good nodes (p is half the Solvers by default); or by racing. In racing, every Solver solves the root under different settings, with random branching at the top two levels and with row and column permutations, and the winner's open nodes are redistributed (pp. 7, 10–11).
  - Load balancing: dynamic, by a collecting mode that a dual-bound threshold triggers (pp. 7–8).
  - Development stages (p. 9): 1 = checkpointing and restarts; 2 = racing ramp-up; 3 = more communication points plus a shared-memory extension.
  - Unchanged: the sequential solver (SCIP) does the search. The paper names SCIP's own progress as a main source of the speedup, beside ParaSCIP's enhancements (p. 15).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - Classes: CIP (the problem becomes an LP once the integer variables are fixed) and MIP, both defined (pp. 3–4).
  - Instances: from MIPLIB 2003, ds and stp3d (solved before) and dano3mip, t1717, liu and momentum3 (open) (p. 9).
  - Data conditions, stated per run:
    - ds: original data, solved from scratch;
    - stp3d: extended presolved data, solved from a given optimal solution (p. 10);
    - the open instances: started from the best known primal solution (p. 18).
  - Machines: HLRN II (up to 7,168 cores), HLRN III (up to 17,088) and Titan (up to 35,200) (p. 10).
  - Versions (p. 10):
    - old: stage-2 ParaSCIP with SCIP 1.2.1.2 and CPLEX 12.1 as LP solver;
    - new: stage-3 ParaSCIP with SCIP 3.0.1 and CPLEX 12.5.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: No proofs.
  - Racing winner: the Solver with the largest dual bound among those with more than a threshold of open nodes (4,000 for ds, 500 for stp3d) (p. 10).
  - Diversification, all three at once (pp. 10–11):
    - meta-settings, 64 combinations: aggressive, default, fast or off for each of heuristics, presolve and separation;
    - random branching at the top two levels;
    - permutations of rows and columns.
  - Racing control: it can move to the second stage without waiting for every Solver, and fall back to normal ramp-up when the winner has too few nodes (p. 7).
  - Collecting mode (p. 8):
    - A node is good if (NodeDualBound − GlobalDualBound)/max{|GlobalDualBound|, 1} < Threshold.
    - The mode starts when fewer than p good nodes remain and stops above m_p·p, with m_p = 1.5 by default.
    - Solvers are chosen in ascending order of their lowest open-node bound, and more of them the longer the pool was empty.
  - Checkpoints: only primitive nodes are saved, i.e. nodes with no ancestor in the LoadCoordinator. This is cheap in I/O but slow to restart, and if only the root is saved a restart makes no progress (pp. 8–9).
  - Communication points (stage 3): after every bound change, LP solve, LP change or new incumbent (p. 9). They were added because some racing Solvers took long to stop, since messages had been read only at the start of a node (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Comparisons (pp. 10–15), each with one run per configuration (no repetitions or seeds are reported, pp. 10–18):
    - racing against normal ramp-up;
    - 4,096 against 7,168 cores (stp3d);
    - 17 restarted jobs against one single run (ds);
    - old against new version (ds).
  - Measures:
    - time split into racing, ramp-up and ramp-down (Table 1, p. 14);
    - nodes solved and transferred, with the transfer ratio (Table 2, p. 14);
    - idle-time ratio per Solver (Figs. 6 and 9; pp. 13, 15–17);
    - core-hours (pp. 13, 15).
  - Results:
    - Transfer ratio: under 10 % (old) and under 0.2 % (new) (pp. 13, 15).
    - stp3d: 1.34× speedup for 1.75× cores, and racing at least 18 h faster than normal ramp-up, a difference traced to Solver idle time (p. 13). Racing also cut the ramp-down from 77,490 s to 5,193 s (Table 1, p. 14).
    - ds, restarts against single run: the restarted runs used 58 % of the single run's core-hours (181,248 against 310,791), because the single run had to be conservative with memory (pp. 13, 15).
    - ds, old against new version: the new version is 9.4× faster with racing and 8.6× with normal ramp-up than the old version with racing. Mean idle ratios were 4.3 % (old), 6.8 % (new racing) and 11.1 % (new normal), attributed to ds having become easier (p. 15).
  - Failures reported:
    - CPLEX 12.2, given the optimal solutions, ran a month on a 32-core machine and aborted without proving optimality (p. 9);
    - two dano3mip runs, on 17,088 and 35,200 cores, improved neither bound, and 17,088 cores were not enough for the current ParaSCIP (p. 18).
  - Bounds (Tables 3–4, pp. 18, 20):
    - four better upper bounds than the Xpress MIPLIB 2003 case study's values: dano3mip by 3.2 %, liu by 1.3 %, momentum3 by 21.5 % and t1717 by 5.2 %;
    - new lower bounds for three instances;
    - momentum3's update came mainly from ParaCPLEX, UG running CPLEX (fn. 4, p. 18).
    - The runs that produced the dano3mip improvements are not described (pp. 18, 20; my reading).

- **D7 Writing**:
  - Abstract: a threefold contribution with one first-time claim, the single runs (p. 3).
  - Explanations hedged as possibilities (pp. 11, 13, 18).
  - Results: told run by run, with figures for bounds, Solver use and idle time (pp. 16–19).
  - Conclusion: concedes that evaluating a parallel MIP solver on hard instances is very difficult, and offers solving well-known hard instances as one way (pp. 18, 20). It ends with three open questions (p. 20).
  - Transferable: when no benchmark protocol fits, say so, name the substitute evidence (solving a known hard instance in one run), and end with the quantitative questions that the substitute cannot answer (pp. 18, 20).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**:
  - Contribution (computational and software): single-run solutions of ds and stp3d, improved bounds for the four open instances, and the racing ramp-up and communication-point developments (pp. 3, 9, 20).
  - Concrete limits:
    - evaluation is hard (pp. 18, 20);
    - a memory-conservative single run costs more than restarts (p. 15);
    - dano3mip is unchanged (p. 18);
    - the parameters depend on the sequential solver's version: the racing threshold had to drop to 500 (p. 15).
  - Open questions: how far ParaSCIP scales, how many Solvers the open instances need, and which of the two numbers is larger (p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Racing ramp-up recipe (pp. 7, 10–11):
  1. Grid of meta-settings: four levels each for heuristics, presolve and separation.
  2. Random branching at the top two levels, and permutations of rows and columns.
  3. Winner: the best dual bound among Solvers with enough open nodes.
  4. Fallback: normal ramp-up if the winner has too few nodes.
- Load balancing on a relative dual-bound threshold for good nodes, with hysteresis: on below p, off above 1.5·p (p. 8).
- Report a parallel run as a phase split (racing, ramp-up, ramp-down), with the transfer ratio, the idle-time ratio per rank and core-hours (Tables 1–2, pp. 13–15).
- Confirm an optimality claim reached through restarts by one uninterrupted run, and try an independent solver given the optimum (p. 9).
- Re-run a fixed hard instance with every new release of the sequential solver, and re-tune the parallel parameters to its behaviour (p. 15).
