### D030 · Multi-Row Presolve Reductions in Mixed Integer Programming (Proceedings of the Twenty-Sixth RAMP Symposium, Hosei University, Tokyo 2014, pp. 181–196; no DOI)

**Read level**: full. I read all 16 file pages, Tables 1–7 and the 20 references. The formula font is mis-mapped in the text layer (∈ appears as "œ", ≤ as "Æ", ≥ as "Ø", ℓ as "¸"), and ff/fi ligatures are lost; I read the formulas through that mapping (pp. 2–9). Nothing was skipped.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Robert E. Bixby, Zonghao Gu, Edward Rothberg (Gurobi Optimization, Houston), Dieter Weninger (Erlangen-Nürnberg) (p. 1). The order is alphabetical; roles are not stated. · **Position in trajectory**:
- Presented in October 2014 (p. 1). SKILL.md (Signature Work Anatomy, *inferred*) takes it to be his first Gurobi-era publication, months after he joined.
- It measures Gurobi 5.6.3 (p. 10).
- Its tables are "in a similar form as in [2]" (p. 11), that is A&W 2013, his own earlier paper with Wunderling, which has not been read (SKILL.md Honest Boundary).
- Its §§3–7 return, largely as the same text, as §5 of R021 two years later (R021 D6).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Presolve is framed as routines that remove redundant information and strengthen the formulation; in some cases it decides between intractable and solvable [2] (p. 1). Root and node presolve are told apart (p. 1).
  - The gap: few publications on an important subject (p. 1). Earlier CPLEX studies [7], [8], [2] had found presolve and cuts "by far the most important individual tools" (p. 2).
  - The entry is a subset: multi-row reductions, which look at several constraints at once. They are "usually more complex" than single-row ones and trade effectiveness against overhead (p. 2).
  - Problem statement: an overview of Gurobi's techniques plus computational results "to assess their impact on overall solver performance" (abstract, p. 1). No research question is posed.

- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: five components of Gurobi's multi-row engine. Each is a classical reduction with a stated implementation choice:
  1. Redundancy detection (§3, pp. 3–4). MIP-redundancy is hard to decide and can even hurt, so the target is LP-redundancy. Full LPs are solved "only in special situations"; otherwise cheaper subsets serve: the single-row supremum, parallel rows, cliques, and a rank-revealing LU for dependent equations (p. 4).
  2. Parallel and nearly parallel rows (§4, pp. 4–6): a case table for pairs of equations and inequalities. "Nearly parallel" rows differ by singleton columns and give substitutions, bound tightenings or aggregations (pp. 5–6).
  3. Non-zero cancellation (§5, pp. 6–7): subtract a multiple of an equation from another row to remove |S| − |U| non-zeros (p. 6). There is a general but expensive variant and faster structure-based ones, e.g. on cliques (p. 7).
  4. Bound and coefficient strengthening (§6, pp. 7–9): Savelsbergh's rules, with activity bounds computed over more than one row (pp. 7–9).
  5. Clique merging (§7, pp. 9–10): extend a set-packing row to a larger clique of the conflict graph, then drop the rows it dominates (p. 10).
  - What changed against the classics: the activity bounds (§6), the detection algorithms (§4) and the work limits (§§5, 7). The reductions themselves are credited to earlier authors (D6), and none is claimed as new. Why: the trade-off between effect and overhead of p. 2.

- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - General MIP with relations ◦ᵢ ∈ {=, ≤, ≥} and infinite bounds (Definition 1, p. 2); minimal and maximal row activities with conventions for ∞ (p. 3).
  - Structures exploited: parallel rows, singleton columns, equations sharing support with other rows, set packing and set partitioning rows (pp. 4–10).
  - Correctness conditions are given per case. For the aggregation of an inequality pair, both singletons must be continuous, or both integer with a_q1 = s·a_r2 (p. 6). Coefficient strengthening needs x_j integer with u_j < ∞ (pp. 7–8).
  - Test set: 2,974 public and commercial models, the part of their model library "that we have ever been able to solve within 10000 seconds using any of the Gurobi releases" (p. 11). Not listed, not public.

- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**:
  - Proof devices:
    - Non-redundancy is NP-complete: append the infeasible row 0 ≤ −1, which is non-redundant exactly when the MIP is feasible (p. 3).
    - LP-redundancy of row r is one LP, max A_r·x over the other rows; the row is redundant iff the optimum is at most b_r (p. 4).
    - Coefficient strengthening (5) is proved equivalent by two cases: at x_j = u_j the rows are identical, and for x_j ≤ u_j − 1 the new row is still redundant. It dominates at x_j = u_j − 1 (p. 8).
  - Detection devices:
    - Parallel rows: two-level hashing, first on the support, then on the coefficients normalized to max-norm 1 with a positive coefficient on the smallest index. A crowded bin is sorted lexicographically, and only neighbours are compared (p. 5).
    - Nearly parallel rows are found in the same pass: singleton columns are removed for the moment and their rows marked (p. 6).
  - Budgets and safeguards:
    - Work limits stop non-zero cancellation, whose structure-based variants are still quadratic in the worst case, and clique merging (pp. 7, 10).
    - Cancellation weights with |s| > 1000 are not used, for numerical reasons (p. 7).
    - Activity bounds can come from single-row inf/sup (cheap, loose), an LP or a MIP (two MIP solves per inequality) (p. 8). The compromise is a few constraints; a single-constraint LP is solved in O(n) (Dantzig; Balas and Zemel). Bounds can also be built per block: cheap on most blocks, costly on one or a few structured ones (p. 9).
    - OBBT is used only for selected variables, and only when the extra effort seems worthwhile (p. 9).
  - Card adds (a slip, corrected in 2016): the cancellation condition is printed as A_qS = s·A_rS (p. 6). But subtracting s times row q from row r cancels S only if s·A_qS = A_rS, which is what R021 prints (R021 p. 32).

- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup: one 4-core Intel i7-3770K at 3.5 GHz with 32 GB; reference Gurobi 5.6.3 in default settings; time limit 10,000 s (p. 10). Reductions are switched by parameters or by source changes (pp. 10–11). Threads, seeds and repeated runs are not stated.
  - The table, explained once (p. 11):
    - brackets "all" and [n,10k] for n = 0, 1, 10, 100, 1000. "All" drops models on which the two versions gave inconsistent optimal values, a floating-point effect (p. 11);
    - [n,10k] holds the models solved by at least one version where the slower took at least n s, so harder brackets are subsets (p. 11);
    - the discussion uses [10,10k] (p. 11);
    - columns: time-outs per side; counts of models at least 10 % faster and slower; shifted geometric means of time and of nodes, both with shift 1, citing the thesis [1]; and the *affected* models, whose total simplex iteration count changed (pp. 11–12).
  - Results in [10,10k]:

    | Change | Time | Unsolved | Table, page |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | root presolve off (Presolve = 0) | ×3.10 | +278 overall | 1, pp. 11–12 |
    | all multi-row presolve off | +31 % | +37 | 2, p. 12 |
    | dependent-row check on (non-default) | 5 % slower; larger trees, not LU overhead | | 3, pp. 12–13 |
    | parallel rows off | +2 % | | 4, p. 13 |
    | non-zero cancellation off | +5 % | +11 | 5, p. 13 |
    | clique merging off | +13 % (nodes +9 %) | | 6, pp. 13–14 |

  - Losses are printed: the dependent-row test, and the multi-row redundancy heuristics, none of which is on by default (p. 12). A&W's higher factor for presolve off is explained by their also switching off node presolve (p. 12).
  - Card adds (my recount of Tables 1–7):
    - excluded models (2,974 minus "all") are 44 for presolve off and 9–20 in the other tests. The text gives no count;
    - in [1000,10k], switching off parallel rows or non-zero cancellation makes Gurobi slightly faster (0.97, 0.99; p. 13). The text reports only [10,10k];
    - the four component knock-outs multiply to 1.42 (sum 37 %), against 1.31 for all multi-row presolve off (Tables 2, 4–7). Here the parts do not cover for each other. The [10,10k] sets differ (871–901 models), so this is approximate;
    - Table 7's column header names domain and coefficient strengthening, its caption bound and coefficient strengthening (p. 15).

- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Lineage paragraph: Brearley et al. as "earliest and most important" (p. 1). Then presolve for 0-1 rows (Guignard–Spielberg, Johnson–Suhl, Crowder et al., Hoffman–Padberg), LP presolve (Andersen–Andersen), MIP probing (Savelsbergh) and implementation papers, his thesis among them (p. 2).
  - Credits per technique: the hashing follows Andersen–Andersen (p. 5); cancellation follows Chang–McCormick (p. 6). Savelsbergh is the "backbone" and already described the multi-row case (p. 7). OBBT comes from global optimization, Shectman–Sahinidis (p. 9); the conflict graph from Atamtürk–Nemhauser–Savelsbergh (p. 9); the rank-revealing LU from Miranian–Gu (p. 4).
  - It positions itself against practice, not against literature: implementations "often only" do the single-row case (p. 7). No other solver is run; 20 references.

- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The sections follow one template: definition, case rules, then an "In Gurobi, …" paragraph with the implementation choice (pp. 4–10).
  - The table format is explained once, in detail (p. 11). Each later table gets one paragraph led by its [10,10k] number (pp. 12–15).
  - Negative findings are put plainly: the redundancy heuristics are not worth their cost (p. 12); a "surprisingly large degradation of 5%" (p. 13); parallel rows "pretty modest" (p. 13). The one cause offered is hedged: "We conjecture" (p. 13).
  - The conclusion repeats the two headline numbers, 31 % and a factor above 3 (p. 15).

- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - Contribution layer: engineering description plus measured impact in a commercial code (pp. 1, 15).
  - Limits and future work: none stated (p. 15).
  - Card adds: closed code and a private test set (p. 11), so nothing can be rerun. Single runs, no per-instance data, and an *affected* test that the paper itself calls approximate (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 ✅ evidence (era marker): the bracket table in its 2014 form (p. 11), credited to A&W 2013. Its parts: the [10,10k] reading rule, 10 % faster/slower counts and shift-1 means (p. 11), *affected* models by simplex-iteration counts (pp. 11–12), and the exclusion of inconsistent answers (p. 11). Variant details against 2016: one 10,000 s limit, equal to the test-set rule; *affected* figures are also given for "all"; no rule that switches act on the master solve only (pp. 10–11).
- Method 1 ✅ evidence: SKILL.md's "Multi-row redundancy checks off for MIP because they hurt (RAMP 2014 p. 192)" is file p. 12 and holds. Nuance: the measured loss is for the rank-revealing LU only (Table 3, 5 %). The multi-row heuristics have no table, only the verdict that they are not worth it and stay off (p. 12). Full-LP redundancy (p. 4) and OBBT (p. 9) are gated too.
- Method 2 ✅ evidence: work limits (pp. 7, 10). The dependent-row loss is located with the node column: larger trees, not factorization overhead (pp. 12–13).
- Method 3 ⚠ variant (loose): the activity-bound computation is presented as one family whose extremes are the single-row rule and a full MIP solve, with a compromise chosen (pp. 8–9). No sweep is reported.
- Heuristics and misreadings (not counted as methods):
  - H7 ✅: the |s| ≤ 1000 cap (p. 7); the exclusion of inconsistent answers (p. 11).
  - H10 ✅: losses printed (pp. 12–13).
  - Misreading 2 ⚠: the components do not add, here in the other direction (D5, my arithmetic).
- New-pattern candidate: "re-measure a published number and explain the gap". A&W's presolve factor is explained by node presolve (p. 12); R021 does the same twice (R021 pp. 6, 64).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) **Bracket table**:
  - group models by the slower run's time (≥ 0, 1, 10, 100, 1000 s); "all" leaves out models whose two versions disagree on the optimum;
  - per bracket: time-outs per side, counts at least 10 % faster and slower, shifted-geometric-mean ratios of time and nodes (shift 1), and the same for the models whose simplex-iteration count changed;
  - discuss the ≥ 10 s bracket (p. 11).
- (D5) **Locate a slowdown with the node column**: when a reduction makes the solver slower, compare the node ratio with the time ratio before blaming the reduction's own cost (pp. 12–13).
- (D4) **Parallel-row detection**: hash rows on their support, then on max-norm-normalized, sign-fixed coefficients. In a crowded bin, sort lexicographically and compare neighbours only. Find nearly parallel rows in the same pass by masking singleton columns (pp. 5–6).
- (D4) **Blockwise activity bounds**: split a row's support into blocks. Use cheap inf/sup on most blocks and an expensive bound (a small LP or MIP) on the one structured block (p. 9).
- (D4) **Cap aggregation multipliers** (|s| ≤ 1000) in any reduction that combines rows (p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "In the discussion below, we will usually use the [10,10k] bracket, because this excludes the relatively uninteresting easy models but is still large enough to draw meaningful conclusions." (p. 11)
- "But even this method is disabled by default for MIP as it hurts performance." (p. 12)

### R021 · Presolve Reductions in Mixed Integer Programming (ZIB-Report 16-44, Zuse Institute Berlin 2016, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-60370)

**Read level**: full. I read all 70 file pages: the text, Examples 1–10, the proofs of Theorems 1–2, all 39 tables and the 37 references. Nothing was skipped.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Robert E. Bixby, Zonghao Gu, Edward Rothberg (Gurobi Optimization), Dieter Weninger (Erlangen-Nürnberg) (p. 3). The order is alphabetical; roles are not stated. · **Position in trajectory**:
- The cover reads "ZIB Report 16-44 (September 2016)" (p. 1); the title page is dated 9 December 2016 (p. 3). It is the report version of the IJOC 2020 article (D019, not read), funded through the Research Campus MODAL (p. 1).
- It measures Gurobi 6.5 (p. 4) and widens D030 from the multi-row subset of Gurobi 5.6.3 to all of presolve (p. 4).
- SKILL.md's Signature Work Anatomy entry "Presolve Reductions" rests on it.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The opening is D030's: presolve "is often the difference between a model being intractable and solvable, in some cases easily solvable" (abstract, p. 3); few papers despite its importance (p. 4); presolve and cuts are the most important tools in the CPLEX studies (p. 4).
  - Stated contribution: a complete overview of all presolve steps of Gurobi 6.5, with enough implementation detail and computational results to show the ideas and their effect (p. 4, paraphrase).
  - Novelty claim: some techniques appear, "to our knowledge, for the first time in the literature" (abstract, p. 3). Card adds: the text does not mark which.

- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - A taxonomy of "more than thirty presolving techniques grouped into six classes" (p. 67): single-row (§3), single-column (§4), multi-row (§5), multi-column (§6), full-problem (§7) and node presolve (§8). Each class opens with a table that switches the whole class off, then come its components (pp. 8, 20, 26, 39, 46, 60–61).
  - Each technique follows one template: conditions, Gurobi's choice and limits, knock-out table, verdict.
  - Items treated beyond the textbook rules:
    - modular-inverse substitution for equations with relatively prime coefficients (pp. 15–17);
    - clique coupling constraints, propagated with two watched literals inside probing (pp. 49–51);
    - lifting from implied bounds on slack variables, and the change in 6.5 that makes it parallel (pp. 51–53);
    - complementary-slackness reductions from bounds on the duals of the continuous part (Theorem 2, pp. 57–58);
    - primal and dual detection of implied integers (p. 59);
    - two strategies for disconnected components (pp. 53–55), and biconnected components (pp. 55–56);
    - node presolve: orbital fixing, conflict propagation, global and local probing (pp. 60–67).
  - Against D030: Gurobi 6.5 instead of 5.6.3, all of presolve, a 3,600 s limit, switches in the master solve only, and no "affected" column for "all" (pp. 4–6). §5 keeps D030's text for the multi-row part (D6) and adds a data structure for short cliques (pp. 37–38) and the corrected cancellation condition (p. 32).

- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - MIP (2.1) with ◦ᵢ ∈ {=, ≤, ≥} and infinite bounds; P_MIP and P_LP defined (pp. 6–7).
  - Model features handled: SOS1 and SOS2 (pp. 18–19), semi-continuous and semi-integer variables (pp. 21–22), penalty variables (p. 41), implied integers (p. 59), symmetric variables (pp. 46–47), disconnected and biconnected components (pp. 53–56).
  - Tolerances: feasibility ε = 10⁻⁶ and "infinity" ψ = 10³⁰, both adjustable by the user (p. 9).
  - Test set: 3,182 public and commercial models, those solved "within 10000 seconds using some one of the Gurobi releases" (p. 5). Prevalence counts are printed:
    - 2,525 models have constraints that are redundant under the original bounds alone (p. 9);
    - 860 have fixed variables (p. 21);
    - 104 have SOS constraints (p. 19);
    - 55 have semi-continuous or semi-integer variables (p. 22);
    - about 20 % have disconnected components (p. 55);
    - symmetric aggregation applies to about 10 % (p. 48).
  - One reduction is left out because the structure is rare: the inverted SOS1 case, since real models "very rarely" have SOS1 constraints on variables with a negative lower bound (p. 19).
  - Proof assumptions: Theorem 1 needs ≤ rows and an infinite or implied bound on the dominating side (pp. 44–45). Theorem 2 needs ≥ rows, ℓ = 0 and a bounded LP relaxation (pp. 57–58).

- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**:
  - Proof devices:
    - Dominated columns (Theorem 1) by an exchange argument: move Δ = x*_k − ℓ_k from x_k to x_j. Every row stays feasible because (a_ij − a_ik)·Δ ≤ 0, and the objective does not get worse (pp. 44–45). An implied bound may first be replaced by ∞ (p. 45).
    - Complementary slackness on a MIP (Theorem 2): propagate bounds on the duals of the continuous columns only. These bounds do not depend on b, so they stay valid for the "fixed LP" obtained by fixing the integers at an optimal MIP solution; the proof derives a contradiction with complementary slackness there (p. 58).
    - Dual implied-integer detection: some optimal solution has its continuous part at a vertex of the fixed LP (p. 59).
    - A two-row counterexample, x₁ − a·x₂ = 0 and a·x₁ − x₂ = 0 with 0 < a < 1, gives an infinite sequence of bound reductions; this is why propagation needs limits (p. 11).
    - Kept from D030: NP-completeness of non-redundancy (p. 27), and the equivalence of coefficient strengthening (p. 12).
  - Numerical safeguards:
    - Drop a coefficient with |a_ik| < 10⁻³ if |a_ik|·(u_k − ℓ_k)·|supp| < 10⁻²·ε, adjusting b. Keep zeroing along the row while the total change stays below 10⁻¹·ε, and drop every coefficient below 10⁻¹⁰ (p. 9).
    - Tighten the bounds of continuous variables only by at least 10³·ε, and only to new bounds below 10⁸ in absolute value. One propagation round per row per pass (p. 11).
    - The gcd of fractional coefficients is found only by two heuristics: divide by a_min, or multiply by 600, "a multiple of many small integer values". The Euclidean algorithm on fractions caused numerical trouble and slowed the solver (p. 15).
    - An implied free variable is substituted only if the pivot passes a Markowitz-type test (|a_ij| at least 0.01 of the column or row maximum, raised to 0.5 or 0.9 under a higher "numerical focus") and the estimated fill-in is small. The aggregator remains "one of the main sources for numerical issues", and users are told to switch it off for numerically hard models (p. 25).
    - |s| ≤ 1000 for cancellation (p. 33). Component sub-MIPs are solved without presolve, so that an "optimal" component solution cannot violate the tolerances after uncrushing (p. 54).
  - Budgets in solver-internal units:
    - Probing: candidates are ordered by the number of binary fixings they imply directly. The work limit is counted in touched non-zeros, from the model size, and multiplied by a factor in [0.01, 100] that grows with the fixings, substitutions and bound changes found (p. 49).
    - Local probing: w is the share of the total work spent in it, and s its success ratio (cut-offs of the LP solution). Probe the fractional variables if s ≥ 2w, and the integral ones as well if w < 0.005 or s ≥ 10w (p. 66). It is "very sensitive to the tuning of the dynamic work limits" (p. 67).
    - Conflicts: at most 100 conflict rows; a new one replaces the one unused for the longest time (p. 64).
    - Work limits for the multi-row methods, cancellation, clique merging, root probing and component sub-MIPs (pp. 26, 33, 37, 54, 56, 64). Parallel rows and columns need none (p. 44).
  - Data structures:
    - Dominated-column search: only columns with an infinite or implied bound may dominate; their candidates come from their shortest row; a 32-bit support signature rules out pairs before the linear scan (p. 45).
    - Set-packing rows of length up to 100 are moved to explicit adjacency lists that store one direction per edge. This costs at most 24.75 times the memory and runs almost 3 times faster in the extreme case of length-2 rows (pp. 37–38).
    - Clique coupling variables y_q = t_q·x_Cq, with the two literals that define y's bounds watched, after Moskewicz et al. (pp. 50–51).
  - Determinism under parallelism: until Gurobi 6.0 a variable was lifted right after probing found it. The order then fixed the result, and parallel probing that is deterministic and still efficient was "virtually impossible". Since 6.5 the lifting candidates are collected in parallel and applied in a sequential post-processing step with a clique-score heuristic (p. 52). Conflict pools are private to each thread (p. 64).
  - Gates: lifting only for models with a certain fraction of continuous variables or that seem challenging otherwise, sometimes without adding non-zeros (p. 53). The local symmetry group is recomputed only when a matching symmetry structure is detected (p. 62).

- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Setup: machines with one 4-core Intel i7-3770K at 3.5 GHz and 32 GB; reference Gurobi 6.5 in default settings; time limit 3,600 s (p. 4).
  - Reductions are disabled, or non-default ones enabled, in the master solve only, by source changes. They stay on in sub-MIPs, because the heuristic decisions about sub-MIPs depend on presolve success there (p. 5).
  - The table form is D030's, with "≥ n sec" brackets (pp. 5–6). New: no "affected" figures for "all", because for models that time out in both versions the iteration and node counts cannot show whether the path changed (p. 6).
  - Headline: with presolve off (root and node), the ≥ 10 s bracket is 9.05 times slower, there are 488 more time-outs, and 504 models are solved only with presolve (pp. 5–6, Table 1). This is called "in line with" A&W's factor 11.4 for CPLEX at 10,000 s (p. 6).
  - Class totals at ≥ 10 s:

    | Class switched off | Time | Page |
    |---|---|---|
    | single-row | +26 % ("surprisingly low") | 8 |
    | single-column | +59 %, mostly the aggregator (+42 % alone) | 20, 26 |
    | multi-row | +34 % | 26 |
    | multi-column | +9 % | 39 |
    | full-problem | +45 %, mainly probing (+17 %) and implied integers (+13 %) | 46, 53, 60 |
    | node presolve | +51 %, and 84 more models intractable | 60–61 |

  - Large single components: orbital fixing, +20 % overall and +77 % on its 392 affected models (p. 63); clique merging, +14 % (p. 38). Node bound strengthening gives +13 %, more than the root's 9 %; the authors explain that it catches the root reductions when root bound strengthening is off (pp. 61–62).
  - Null or negative components:
    - Chvátal–Gomory strengthening: no gain, "Nevertheless, the reduction is turned on" (p. 14);
    - simple probing on single equations: no gain on the 20 % of models it touches, a result the authors cannot fully explain (p. 18);
    - dependent-row detection: enabled, it is 4 % slower, with the node count up by as much. Hypothesis: the redundant rows serve as base inequalities for cuts (p. 28);
    - clique and inequality subsumption: neutral (pp. 28–29);
    - biconnected components: no big benefit (p. 56);
    - global probing at nodes: neutral in time, and the default has 36 time-outs against 14 without it. The authors explain that root probing in the cut loop stayed on, so the test measures only the extra probing in the tree (p. 65).
  - Small affected sets are refused or rechecked:
    - SOS reductions: 48 affected models, 25 % faster, "too small to draw any meaningful conclusions" (p. 19);
    - semi-continuous bounds: 13 affected models, the gain coming from one model (8.3 s against the time limit) (p. 22);
    - the multi-column dual-fixing extension: 40 models; additional tests showed its speed-up to be an artifact of performance variability, and it stays off (p. 40);
    - penalty-variable fixing: about 200 affected models are "usually not large enough" to measure a 3–4 % effect (pp. 41–42).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D5) **Knock out in the master solve only**: keep the feature on in sub-MIPs, whose heuristic decisions depend on presolve success. Where a change acts mainly through model size, say that no clean benchmark exists (pp. 5, 21).
- (D5) **No "affected" figure where both sides time out**: a change of path cannot be seen there (p. 6).
- (D5) **Class total first, then components**, and explain a small component figure by the component that covers for it (pp. 8, 11, 61–62).
- (D5) **Size rule for affected sets**: treat a few dozen affected models as anecdotal; about 200 are not enough to measure 3–4 % precisely. Recheck a small-set gain for performance variability before switching a default (pp. 19, 40, 42).
- (D5) **Look at the hardest bracket separately**: a 4 % average can hide 25 % on the ≥ 1000 s models (p. 24).
- (D4) **Success-scaled work limit**: count work in touched non-zeros, set a base limit from the model size, and multiply it by a factor in [0.01, 100] that grows with the reductions found (p. 49).
- (D4) **Ratio trigger for an expensive local step**: track the work share w and the success ratio s. Run on fractional variables if s ≥ 2w, and on all variables if w < 0.005 or s ≥ 10w (p. 66).
- (D4) **Markowitz-type pivot test for substitutions**: a pivot of at least 0.01 of the column or row maximum, tighter under a numerics setting, plus a cap on fill-in (p. 25).
- (D4) **Signature pre-screen for pairwise dominance**: a 32-bit mask of the row indices mod 32 rules out most pairs before the linear scan (p. 45).
- (D4) **Fixed-LP argument**: to prove a dual reduction on a MIP, fix the integers at an optimal solution and apply LP duality to the continuous remainder, with dual bounds that do not depend on b (pp. 58–59).
- (D4) **Collect, then apply**: make an order-dependent reduction deterministic under parallelism by gathering candidates in parallel and applying them in one sequential pass (p. 52).
- (D7) **A small worked example per reduction**, and one that quantifies the effect (root node instead of almost two thousand nodes, p. 17).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yuji Shinano (first author), Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch, Michael Winkler (p. 3). Achterberg is second author, with the affiliation Gurobi GmbH; Berthold and Heinz are with FICO, Shinano and Koch with ZIB (p. 3). Roles are not stated. · **Position in trajectory**:

- Co-signed while he was at Gurobi. SCIP, his code, is the black-box solver (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - From the hardware: supercomputers with more than 10,000 cores are now the norm (Top500), and their use should bring "valuable and tangible results" (p. 3).
  - From the field: libraries let researchers compare results, and "unsolved models from these libraries provide research challenges to advance the field" (p. 4).
  - The gap: tree search "is generally considered easy to parallelize". Yet only two large-scale parallel MIP solvers had solved open benchmark instances, GAMS/CPLEX/Condor (Bussieck et al.) and ParaSCIP, both running a state-of-the-art solver as a black box (p. 4).
  - Problem statement: describe ParaSCIP's mechanism, its improved load balancing and new techniques, and report the runs on open MIPLIB instances (abstract, p. 3).

- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Base design (from earlier papers): the UG framework treats the solver and the communication library as exchangeable black boxes, so progress of the base solver carries over (p. 4). One LoadCoordinator (LC) presolves, broadcasts the presolved instance once, and afterwards sends only bound changes ("ParaNodes") to the Solvers (pp. 5–6).
  - Ramp-up (p. 6):
    - normal: Solvers send every second child to the LC until it holds p "heavy" nodes (p between 10 and 2,000 in these runs);
    - racing: all Solvers start on the root with different settings and different permutations of variables and constraints. A winner is chosen by lower bound and number of open nodes; the other trees are dropped, and only their solutions are kept.
    - The concurrent mode of commercial solvers is described as racing only (p. 6).
  - Load balancing: a node is heavy if its bound lies within a Threshold of the global bound, relative to max{|GlobalBound|, 1} (expression (2), p. 7). Solvers in collecting mode switch to best-bound order; collecting stops above 1.5·p heavy nodes (p. 7).
  - Checkpoints store only primitive nodes; the presolve at restart often outweighs the cost of rebuilding the tree (p. 7).
  - New in this paper (§§3–4):
    - the number of collecting Solvers starts at one and grows by one whenever the LC's node pool has stayed empty for 10 s; p is doubled when collecting modes come less than 10 s apart (p. 8);
    - bulk sending of a preset number of nodes (p. 8);
    - a ramp-down mode that first only collects, then redistributes. It is triggered when fewer than 90 % of the Solvers are active for 10 s and the open nodes exceed 100 times the number of Solvers (pp. 8–9);
    - collecting mode restarts when the pool runs empty (p. 9);
    - a node selector for collecting mode: best bound, ties broken by fewer bound changes, as a proxy for a larger feasible region (p. 9);
    - merging ParaNodes at a restart (pp. 9–12, Algorithms 1–2);
    - "deep probing": the branching statistics of all racing Solvers are pooled to initialize every Solver (p. 13).
  - Why: early pseudo-cost decisions are weak, and a forced restart on a supercomputer is a natural point to repair them (p. 10).
  - Unchanged: SCIP's own algorithms. The new techniques are "Applied Externally From SCIP" (§4 title, p. 9).

- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - MIP (1): min{cᵀx : Ax ≤ b, l ≤ x ≤ u, x_j ∈ Z for j ∈ I} (p. 3).
  - Instances: the open instances of MIPLIB 2010, whose paper listed 134 unsolved (p. 14), and those of MIPLIB 2003. Table 1 gives rows, columns and the numbers of general integer, binary and continuous variables of the ten instances solved (p. 14).
  - Class statement: deep probing enlarges the nodes, so it suits medium-scale machines and instances with "relatively few integer variables" (p. 13).
  - The merged sub-MIP takes componentwise minima and maxima of its members' bounds (p. 10); there is no other correctness argument.

- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: proofs: n/a. The design devices:
  - Node merging (Algorithms 1–2, pp. 11–12):
    - start from the open node P with the best bound, and repeatedly keep the subset of nodes that share the most common fixing x_j = v;
    - merge only if the shared fixings exceed τ = 0.9 of P's fixings (p. 12);
    - after the merged node's root processing, restore the original nodes if its bound fell below the original bound times (1 − δ), with δ = 0 by default (p. 11);
    - the merged node is solved from scratch with full presolve and cuts, so its bound can even improve (p. 10).
  - Thresholds and timers for the LC: 10 s, 90 %, the factor 100, 1.5·p (pp. 7–9).
  - Primitive-node checkpoints (p. 7) and off-line merging between two runs (p. 10).
  - Variability as a device: racing Solvers differ in settings and permutations, because permutations change performance through imperfect tie breaking (p. 6, citing MIPLIB 2010).

- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Load balancing (§5.1, pp. 13–14):
    - ParaSCIP on SCIP 3.0.1, with CPLEX 12.5 as LP solver, on Titan (Cray XK7) with 10,000 cores. Normal ramp-up was used, so that the load balancing shows (p. 13);
    - one instance, timtab2, chosen because finding and proving the optimum is hard and yet feasible on a supercomputer (p. 13);
    - one run per version (Figures 3–6, pp. 15–16). In the original, the active Solvers fell to about 280 although enough nodes were open, and the ramp-down took about 90 % of the time while using 3 % of the resources (p. 14);
    - the improved version is "about four times faster", with parameters that were "not tuned well enough" (p. 14).
  - Open instances (§5.2, pp. 14, 17): ten MIPLIB 2010 instances solved between March 2011 and January 2015 (Table 1).
    - SCIP 2.0.1–3.1.1 with CPLEX 12.2–12.6, four machines, 160–12,288 cores, 1–15 runs, about 2–700 hours per row (p. 14).
    - Four instances were started from the best known solution, the others from scratch. dolom1's second run started without a checkpoint but with the incumbent of the first run (p. 17). The asterisk on dolom1 in Table 1 is not explained (p. 14).
    - The abstract's "12" instances are, by my reading, these ten plus ds and stp3d from MIPLIB 2003, solved in April 2010 and reported earlier (pp. 3, 14).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D4) **Primitive-node checkpoints**: store only the nodes with no ancestor at the coordinator, and presolve again at restart. The I/O stays small, and the global presolve at restart often pays for the rebuilt tree (p. 7).
- (D4) **Throttled collecting**: start with one collecting worker and add one whenever the pool has stayed empty for a set time. Double the target number of heavy nodes when collecting phases come too close together, and call a node heavy by a relative-gap test (pp. 7–8).
- (D4) **Ramp-down switch**: when fewer than 90 % of the workers are active for 10 s and the open nodes exceed 100 times the workers, stop redistributing, collect, then hand nodes out in bulk (pp. 8–9).
- (D4) **Merge at restart, with a guard**: merge the open nodes that share at least 90 % of the best node's fixings into one relaxed node, process its root, and restore the originals if its bound is worse (pp. 10–12).
- (D5) **Phase-resolved resource plots**: plot active workers, open nodes and nodes sent and received per second over time, with ramp-up and ramp-down marked, to find where the idle time goes; state the blind spot of the idle measure (pp. 14, 17–18; Figures 4, 6, 12–14).
