### D029 · Parallelizing the Constraint Integer Programming Solver SCIP (Proceedings of the Institute of Statistical Mathematics 61(1): 47–78, 2013, no DOI)
**Read**: full — all 32 file pages of the Japanese article (制約整数計画ソルバSCIPの並列化; §§1–5, acknowledgements, references) and the English abstract (p. 32). In this text layer Tables 1–8 and Figures 1–5 are captions only (tables pp. 12–15, 18–19, 22–26, 28; figures pp. 3, 5, 7, 17, 20); their contents were read in R018, the ZIB-Report version of the same paper, and are cited there.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yuji Shinano (first), Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch, Stefan Vigerske, Michael Winkler; after Shinano the names are alphabetical and roles are not stated (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: IBM/CPLEX years: his affiliation is ILOG, IBM Deutschland (p. 1); received 2 July 2012, accepted 12 October 2012, in a special issue on statistical inference based on optimization (p. 1). It continues Shinano, Achterberg and Fujie 2008 and ParaSCIP 2012 (refs., p. 31). The one statement sourced to him alone is the code-size comparison, cited to his INFORMS 2011 talk "Comparing SCIP to CPLEX" (p. 2; ref. p. 30).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Earlier parallel MIP codes, even on supercomputers, rarely beat commercial sequential solvers; the only case the authors know of solving instances before the commercial solvers did, Bussieck et al. 2009, built subproblems by hand from CPLEX's tree files and stopped working when CPLEX stopped writing them (p. 3). A parallel tree search that does not keep the sequential solver's performance and the gains of each of its components can hardly reach even sequential speed (p. 3). SCIP had no parallel version, not even shared-memory: parallelism was not in its design, and this work found and fixed non-thread-safe code (pp. 3–4). The introduction states how a general MIP solver is built: the timing, choice, frequency and effort of each component are fixed by large computational studies and exposed as parameters, because good values depend on the instance; a new algorithm rarely helps all instances, costs effort to decide when to run it, and can slow instances solved before, so a general solver is re-tuned on many instances, formerly fast ones included (p. 2). SCIP has over 1,000 parameters plus meta-settings (fast, aggressive, none) and over 450,000 lines; CPLEX over 600,000 lines with fewer comments (p. 2). Random instances tend to be extremely easy or extremely hard, vendors' real instances are an asset, and MIPLIB is selected from a solver developer's view (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Gurobi's "Tree of Trees" (subtrees solved by the MIP solver itself) is read as the authors' earlier approach (Shinano et al. 2008) moved inside a solver; SCIP is parallelized the same way, from outside (p. 3). The C++ framework UG runs many unmodified copies of a sequential or shared-memory B&B solver ("Solvers") under one LoadCoordinator with dynamic load balancing; solver and communication library are abstract, so ug[SCIP, MPI] = ParaSCIP and ug[SCIP, Pthreads] = FiberSCIP run logically the same algorithm, which separates logic from environment effects (pp. 6–7). The presolved instance is sent once; subproblems (ParaNodes) travel as bound changes against it (p. 7). Normal ramp-up returns one child per branching until p "good" nodes are pooled; racing ramp-up gives every Solver the root with its own parameter set and redistributes the winner's open nodes, and was designed to use idle Solvers for dynamic parameter learning or tuning (p. 8). Collecting mode switches selected Solvers to best-bound search while fewer than p pooled nodes pass the relative-gap test (3.1), until m_p·p (p. 9); a Solver whose subtree bound is far above the pool's best bound (minimization) gives up its tree for a pooled node (pp. 9–10). Checkpoints keep only the coordinator's nodes with no ancestor among them (p. 10).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: CIP with condition (2.1), an LP once the integers are fixed (since relaxed for MINLP); MIP is the linear case; minimization w.l.o.g. (pp. 1, 4). FiberSCIP copies SCIP's environment and covers every class SCIP covers; ParaSCIP covers MIP only, since each new constraint handler needs transfer code (pp. 10–11, 29). Test set: the MIPLIB 2010 benchmark, 87 instances, chosen to survey a solver with fewer than 100 (p. 11); hard single instances: MIPLIB 2003 ds and stp3d (stp3d presolved by hand nine times) and MIPLIB 2010 dg012142 (pp. 27, 29). Vars × cons of the presolved instance is the memory estimate (p. 11).
- **D4 Implementation design (brief)**: In the FiberSCIP runs racing ends at 36 s (0.5 % of the 2 h limit), is extended to at most 1,800 s while the winner holds fewer than 300 open nodes, and lasts to the limit while no feasible solution exists, because FiberSCIP passes no infeasibility information (pp. 11, 16); memory is capped at 2 GB per Solver thread (p. 16). For one-job solves on HLRN II, racing ramp-up was built for that purpose (the difficulty: stable operation over more than 4,000 parameter sets), the number of collecting Solvers was capped, and memory caps trade against earlier depth-first switching, since one Solver out of memory stops every MPI process (p. 27). Per-Solver slowdown is modelled as f(x) = 1.0 − c·log(x) on the memory estimate, fitted per thread count; c grows with threads (p. 20). No proofs.
- **D5 Evaluation (one line)**: 87 MIPLIB 2010 instances on 40 identical cluster nodes (2 × Xeon E5420, 16 GB), SCIP 2.1.1 with SoPlex 1.6.0.1, 2 h, every result passed a solution checker, geometric means over instances solved in all compared settings beside a mean with time-outs and aborts at 7,200 s (Mittelmann's way), deterministic settings run once and communicating ones five times (pp. 11, 16, 20); ParaSCIP single jobs on 4,096 and 7,168 HLRN II cores (ds, stp3d) and on 256 ISM cores (dg012142, about 42 h) (pp. 27–29).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: UG with ParaSCIP and FiberSCIP, beta, in the SCIP Optimization Suite, plus records (pp. 29–30); of the post-2010 ParaSCIP runs the authors say they may lack novelty as a paper but show the field's real state (p. 27). Limits: ParaSCIP is MIP-only (p. 29); a deterministic parallel solver (same tree for the same environment and settings, even if much slower) is needed for efficient development and is future work (pp. 29–30); parallel-solver evaluation changes with the test set and the measure (speed on solved instances, number solved, Mittelmann's composite), and no method is established (p. 30); restarts that discard much work can pay, so algorithm improvement comes before energy and architecture questions (p. 30). Two claims are left unmeasured and labelled so: restarts help on hard instances (p. 10), and the single-job challenge improved the load balancing (p. 29).

**Version note**: R018 is the same paper as a ZIB-Report; a sentence-level comparison found only typesetting, reference style and a few words changed. Use R018 for the tables.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence, stated, co-signed): the premise of the default gate in general form: a new algorithm rarely helps all instances, costs effort to decide when to run, and can slow instances solved before, hence re-tuning on a large set that includes formerly fast instances (p. 2).
- Method 1 ⚠ (variant): the ramp-up default goes against the faster mean. Normal ramp-up has the larger speedups; racing solves more instances in every run and gains with more threads, so racing is the default, a choice the authors call difficult (pp. 21, 26; numbers in R018, p. 21). The criterion is stability over runs and the trend with scale, not a detector.
- Method 4 ✅ (evidence): the coordinator's presolve is switched off inside the parallel default, which comes out slower and solves fewer instances in all five runs, so the default stays (p. 21). Means use only instances all compared settings solve, with the failure-at-limit mean beside them (p. 16), and the text warns that speedups of different settings rest on different instance subsets (p. 21).
- Method 4 ⚠ (variant, the baseline): speedups are taken against the parallel code's own one-thread default (presolve twice), about 47 s slower in geometric mean than sequential SCIP, because presolving twice changes the algorithm and is FiberSCIP's default; SCIP's column is printed beside it (pp. 16–17). Heuristic H3 (baseline at its best) is met only by disclosure.
- Method 4 ✗ (contradiction with step 7 and Misreading 1): this co-signed written study repeats each nondeterministic configuration five times and averages; deterministic ones run once (p. 20). The repeats answer nondeterministic message timing, not seed variability; first author Shinano.
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence, co-signed): FiberSCIP's main value is as the debugging and development environment for ParaSCIP (a normal debugger works there, and a little code moves a change to the distributed build) (p. 11); every result passes a solution checker (p. 16); a deterministic mode is wanted for development even at a large speed cost (pp. 29–30).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): costs are located before speedups are judged: one-thread runs show the communication overhead negligible and the second presolve an algorithmic change (pp. 16–17); the per-Solver loss to memory contention is isolated on instances with the same node count at 1, 2, 4 and 6 threads (pp. 17, 20); idle time is placed mainly in ramp-up (p. 28).
- Method 3 ✅ (evidence, step 2 only): the compared schemes are settings of one code (the racing stage alone with or without incumbent exchange, i.e. a concurrent portfolio; racing or normal ramp-up; coordinator presolve on or off), summarized in one table (p. 21); no sweep and no knee.
- New-pattern candidates: *make the forced restart pay* (restarts re-sort checkpointed nodes by their updated bounds, p. 10; restarts cost fewer core-hours than one long job, pp. 28–29); *same-path subset to isolate hardware effects* (pp. 17, 20); *repeat only nondeterministic configurations* (p. 20); *count resources including lost work* (pp. 28–29); *exploit variability by racing* (pp. 8, 27); *refuse a single-number solver ranking* (p. 30).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before any parallel speedup, run the parallel code with one worker against the sequential solver in its plain and its default mode: here communication overhead proved negligible and the default's second presolve proved an algorithm change; then choose the baseline and print the sequential column beside it (pp. 16–17).
- Isolate hardware slowdown from search effects: keep the instances whose node count (and so winner) is the same for every thread count, plot speedup against a memory estimate (vars × cons after presolve), and fit a one-parameter loss per thread count (pp. 17, 20).
- Run deterministic configurations once and nondeterministic ones five times; report each run, the average, and the instances solved in every run (p. 20; R018 Tables 4–6).
- Compare restarts from checkpoints with one long job in total core-hours, counting the work after each job's last checkpoint (pp. 28–29).
- Develop in the shared-memory build of the same logical algorithm, where a normal debugger works, and port to the distributed build afterwards (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "そのアルゴリズムを動作させるべきかどうかを判定する手間を要することになり，これまでに 解けていたインスタンスを解く時間はむしろ長くなる可能性もある．" (p. 2). Gloss: a new algorithm generally needs effort to decide whether it should run, and instances solved before may even take longer.
- "遊休は，主に Ramp-Up 時に生じている．" (p. 28). Gloss: idle time arises mainly during ramp-up.

### R019 · Solving hard MIPLIB2003 problems with ParaSCIP on Supercomputers: An update (ZIB-Report 13-66, Zuse Institute Berlin 2013, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-42888)
**Read**: full — all 21 file pages: §§1–6 (pp. 3–18), Tables 1–4 (pp. 13, 18), references (pp. 20–21); Figures 1–11 are axis numbers and captions only (pp. 5, 12–13, 15–16, 19). Compared token by token with the April 2014 revision, the text read for D017 (card in c05).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yuji Shinano (first), Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch, Michael Winkler; alphabetical after Shinano, roles not stated; Achterberg's e-mail address is at IBM (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: IBM/CPLEX years; dated 21 October 2013 (p. 3), the first version of the report behind the IPDPSW 2014 paper (D017).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: In 2010 ParaSCIP solved two of the six open MIPLIB 2003 instances only through more than ten restarts from checkpoints; the aims are single runs for those two, better bounds for the other four, and an account of the developments (p. 3). Parallel B&B for MIP is hard because subtree decisions depend on each other (history-based branching; his Hybrid branching is cited), effort goes into presolve and cuts before the tree, and the field's improvements shrink trees, which raises communication needs; hence dynamic load balancing is essential (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: UG as in D029 (pp. 5–8). Three development stages: normal ramp-up with checkpointing; racing ramp-up, built to confirm the two optima in single runs; more communication points (after bound changes, LP solves, LP modifications and new incumbents), developed through a shared-memory extension (pp. 8–9).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: MIP = (A, b, c, l, u, I) (p. 3); ds and stp3d (solved before) and dano3mip, t1717, liu, momentum3 (open) (p. 9); ds from the original data and from scratch, stp3d from extended presolved data and a given optimum (p. 9); HLRN II, HLRN III and Titan; old version SCIP 1.2.1.2 with CPLEX 12.1, new version SCIP 3.0.1 with CPLEX 12.5 (p. 9).
- **D4 Implementation design (brief)**: The racing winner is the Solver with the largest dual bound among those above an open-node threshold, 4,000 for ds and 500 for stp3d (p. 10). Diversification, all at once: 64 meta-setting combinations, random branching on the top two levels, row and column permutations (p. 10). The winners ran with heuristics and separation off or fast (ds h3p2s3; stp3d h2p3s2) (p. 11). Slow racing terminations are attributed, as a possible explanation, to messages being read only when a node starts, which motivated more communication points (pp. 10–11). No proofs.
- **D5 Evaluation (one line)**: one run per configuration: ds 273,157 s with racing on 4,096 cores; stp3d 158,595 s (racing) and 225,577 s (normal) on 4,096 cores and 118,386 s on 7,168 (1.34× for 1.75× the cores); the new version on ds 29,095 s (racing) and 31,798 s (normal), 9.4× and 8.6× faster (Table 1, p. 13; p. 14); restarts 181,248 against 310,791 core-hours for one run (p. 14); bounds for the four open instances in Tables 3–4 (p. 18); two dano3mip runs on 17,088 and 35,200 cores improved neither bound (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: single-run solutions, improved bounds and the stage-2 and stage-3 developments (p. 3). The conclusion calls evaluating a parallel MIP solver on hard instances very hard, offers the paper as a reference result, and asks how far ParaSCIP scales, how many Solvers the open instances need, and which number is larger (p. 18).

**Version note** (this original against the April 2014 revision read for D017; token diff): the revision (a) replaces the plain MIP definition (p. 3 here) by SCIP and CIP definitions and adds that the most important ingredient of a parallel MIP solver is a state-of-the-art sequential solver, citing Koch, Martin and Pfetsch (D017 TR pp. 4–5); (b) adds to the conclusion that exploiting a powerful MIP solver is crucial (D017 TR p. 20), where this version ends with a reference result (p. 18); (c) adds p's default, half the Solvers, and the warning that a checkpoint holding only the root makes restarts useless (D017 TR pp. 7–9; cf. pp. 6, 8 here); (d) drops two reasons given here: memory-conservative settings because one Solver at its memory limit would have ended the run, and the racing threshold cut to 500 because the new version could not end racing at 4,000 within 12 hours (p. 14). Numbers and tables are the same. Across reports: the month-long verification run is CPLEX 12.2 here (p. 8) but CPLEX 12.0 in D029 (p. 27) and R018 (p. 22); ds's transferred nodes are 43,334,558 here (Table 2, p. 13) and 43,336,562 in R018 (Table 7, p. 22).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ (variant): the explicit "sequential solver first" sentences cited in c05's D017 card (D017 TR pp. 4–5, 20) are not in this October 2013 original. Here the stance is implicit: SCIP's own progress is "a main source" of the ds speedup (p. 17), and ds is re-run with every SCIP release (p. 14). The co-signed Japanese paper had already stated the point in 2012 (D029 p. 3).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence, with a caution): time split by phase (racing, ramp-up, ramp-down; Table 1, p. 13), idle ratios, and core-hours that include the time after each final checkpoint (p. 14); the normal-versus-racing gap on stp3d is traced to Solver idle time (p. 11). My reading: ramp-down, defined from the last moment all units were busy (p. 5), takes 27,110 of 29,095 s for the new version on ds (Table 1, p. 13), while the mean idle ratio is 6.8 % (p. 14), so phase length alone is only a proxy for waste.
- Method 4 ⚠ (variant; H5, instances trigger, sets conclude; the same reading as c05's D017 card): racing against normal ramp-up and old against new versions are judged on single runs on one or two instances (pp. 11, 14); the authors present the paper as a reference result, not a benchmark (p. 18).
- New-pattern candidates: *fixed hard instance as a cross-version probe* (p. 14); *count resources including lost work* (p. 14); *exploit variability by racing* (p. 10); *name the substitute evidence when no protocol fits* (p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Re-run one fixed hard instance at every release of the base solver and re-tune the parallel layer when its behaviour shifts; here the racing threshold had to fall from 4,000 to 500 (p. 14).
- Print the winning racing settings: here they show which components the hard instance wants switched off (p. 11).
- Read phase lengths together with idle ratios: a long ramp-down by definition is not yet lost work (pp. 5, 13–14).

**Quotes**:
- "Therefore, parameter settings of each Solver were conservative in terms of memory usage, that is more frequently the search strategy was changed to depth-first search, because if a Solver had ever reached the memory limit, ParaSCIP would have been terminated immediately." (p. 14)
- "We conducted computational experiments for solving ds every time when a new SCIP version was released." (p. 14)

### R020 · Solving Open MIP Instances with ParaSCIP on Supercomputers using up to 80,000 Cores (ZIB-Report 15-53, Zuse Institute Berlin 2015, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-56404)
**Read**: full — all 22 file pages: §§1–6 (pp. 3–21), Algorithms 1–2 (pp. 11–12), Table 1 (p. 16), references (pp. 21–22); Figures 1–14 are axis numbers and captions only (pp. 5, 14–15, 18–20). Compared token by token with the February 2016 revision, the text indexed as D018 (core; its card belongs to another batch).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yuji Shinano (first), Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch, Michael Winkler; alphabetical after Shinano, roles not stated; Achterberg and Winkler at Gurobi GmbH, Berthold and Heinz at FICO (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: Gurobi years; dated 27 October 2015 (p. 3), the first version of the report behind the IPDPS 2016 paper (D018).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Supercomputers with more than 10,000 cores are now the rule (June 2015: five Top500 entries below that), and their use should give tangible results (p. 3); open MIPLIB instances are the stated challenge (p. 4). Only two large-scale parallel MIP codes had solved open benchmark instances, GAMS/CPLEX/Condor and ParaSCIP; both use a state-of-the-art solver as a black box and parallelize the tree search outside it (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea**: UG makes the solver and the communication library abstract classes, so improvements of the base solver carry over at once (p. 4). New in the load balancing: a dynamic number of collecting Solvers and a dynamic p, bulk sending of nodes (p. 8), a ramp-down mode that only collects until enough nodes are pooled (pp. 8–9), restarts of the collecting mode, and a collecting-mode node selector (best bound, fewer bound changes as tie-breaker, as a rough volume estimate) (p. 9). Techniques outside SCIP: merging open nodes that share fixings at a restart (Algorithms 1–2), and deep probing, which initializes all Solvers' branching statistics from all racing Solvers, compared to rapid learning (pp. 9–12).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: MIP min{c^T x : Ax ≤ b, x_I ∈ Z^|I|} (p. 3); MIPLIB 2010's 134 open instances, ten of them solved here (p. 16); timtab2 as the load-balancing show case, hard both to find and to prove optimal yet solvable on a supercomputer in reasonable time (p. 13); rmine10 (pp. 16–17). Deep probing is scoped to medium-scale machines and instances with relatively few integer variables (p. 12).
- **D4 Implementation design (brief)**: Collecting Solvers start at one and grow by one whenever the pool stays empty for 10 s; p doubles when collecting modes come less than 10 s apart (p. 8). The ramp-down mode triggers when, for a longer period, less than 90 % of Solvers are active and open nodes exceed 100 × Solvers (p. 9). Merging starts from the best-bound node, keeps narrowing to the nodes that share the most common fixing, merges if n/|F_i| > τ = 0.9, and undoes a merge whose root bound falls below (1 − δ) times the original, δ = 0 (pp. 11–12); merging may lose information and weaken bounds, offset by presolving the merged node from scratch (p. 10). Re-presolving at a restart often outweighs regenerating the tree (p. 7). No proofs.
- **D5 Evaluation (one line)**: timtab2 on 10,000 Titan cores with SCIP 3.0.1 and CPLEX 12.5, normal ramp-up, one run per version: the original balancing spent about 90 % of the time in ramp-down using 3 % of the resources, with active Solvers down to about 280, and the improved one was about four times faster, with ad-hoc parameter values (p. 13); Table 1 lists ten MIPLIB 2010 instances solved 2011–2015 with date, SCIP and CPLEX versions, machine, runs, cores, hours and value, four of them started from the best known solution (p. 16); rmine10 after 41 runs, one with 79,984 Solvers on Titan: idle ratio mostly below 2 % (27.5 % in a run cut short by a hardware error), gap 0.15 % after run 1 and below 0.03 % after run 41, still open (pp. 16–17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: 12 previously unsolved instances and a record of 80,000 cores (pp. 3, 18); availability in the SCIP Optimization Suite and parallel customized SCIP solvers (SCIP-Jack, the only distributed-memory entry at the 11th DIMACS Challenge) (p. 21). Limits: deep probing "not yet fully investigated" (p. 12), ad-hoc parameters (p. 13), rmine10 open (p. 17). Design result: no need for a two-layer coordinator; combine with the solver's internal parallelization instead (p. 18). Open: balancing ramp-up and ramp-down, and subproblems with very different runtimes (p. 21).

**Version note** (this original against the February 2016 revision read for D018; token diff): mostly editorial ("LoadCoordinator" → "LC", "good" → "heavy" nodes). The revision adds p's range, 10 to 2,000 in the experiments (D018 TR p. 6), a remark that commercial solvers' concurrent mode is ParaSCIP's racing stage alone and the statement that racing keeps only the losers' feasible solutions (D018 TR p. 6), a formal definition of merged sub-MIPs by local bounds (D018 TR p. 10) and checkpoint-size maxima (774 and 16,764 nodes; D018 TR p. 18); it recasts merging's "main purpose" (p. 10 here) as an empirical observation (D018 TR p. 10) and the "ad-hoc values" (p. 13 here) as moderate values not tuned well enough (D018 TR p. 14). **Transferable techniques**:

- Cap concurrent collectors adaptively: start at one, add one after each 10 s with an empty pool, and double p when collecting modes come within 10 s (p. 8).

### R022 · Solving Previously Unsolved MIP Instances with ParaSCIP on Supercomputers by using up to 80,000 Cores (ZIB-Report 20-16, Zuse Institute Berlin 2020, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-78393)
**Read**: full — all 25 file pages: abstract (p. 3), §§1–7 (pp. 4–22), Algorithms 1–4 (pp. 8–9, 14–15), Tables 1–2 (p. 17), references (pp. 23–25); Figures 1–7 are captions or axis numbers only (pp. 7, 18, 21).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Yuji Shinano (first), Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch, Michael Winkler; alphabetical after Shinano, roles not stated; Achterberg at Gurobi GmbH (p. 4) · **Position in trajectory**: Gurobi years; dated 25 May 2020 (p. 4). A rewritten and extended account of the 2016 paper, which it cites for the intermediate rmine10 results ([16], pp. 20, 24); new title, sections and results, so not a revision of R020.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Because MIP is NP-hard, a linear speed-up cannot improve solvability, and parallelization alone is not to be relied on (p. 6). Success rests on two factors: a supervisor–worker coordination tuned for up to 80,000 Solvers with light checkpointing, and, "more importantly", the algorithmic changes UG causes, so that ParaSCIP works algorithmically differently from SCIP (p. 6). Aim: solve previously intractable instances, with MIPLIB as the challenge library (pp. 4–5).
- **D2 Core idea**: Supervisor–worker pseudo-code (Algorithms 1–2, pp. 8–9) with "heavy" nodes by the relative-gap test (2) (p. 9) and R020's load-balancing devices (pp. 9–11). Layered presolving: every ParaNode is presolved from scratch, so more Solvers mean more in-tree presolving, which sequential SCIP never does (p. 12). Racing keeps only the losers' feasible solutions (p. 13). Merging uses merged sub-MIPs defined by the minimum and maximum local bounds (pp. 13–15); deep probing as in R020 (p. 16).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: MIP with bounds, (1) (p. 4); open instances of MIPLIB 2003, 2010 and 2017 (p. 16); Table 1: 12 MIPLIB 2010 instances; Table 2: seven MIPLIB 2017 instances (two infeasible) on 72 cores of ISM3 (p. 17). That recursive presolving pays rests on the FiberSCIP study, which found fewer nodes with more Solvers on average, and is expected for this paper's instances (p. 12).
- **D4 Implementation design (brief)**: The checkpoint file holds at most max{open nodes after racing, Solvers + p + α} nodes, α the nodes still in flight after a stop message, stated without proof (pp. 11–12); p lies between 10 and 2,000 in the experiments (p. 6); the ramp-down trigger waits 10 s (pp. 10–11); merging uses τ = 0.9 and δ = 0 (pp. 14–15).
- **D5 Evaluation (one line)**: records only: Table 1 with dates 2011–2015, SCIP 2.0.1–3.1.2 with CPLEX 12.2–12.6, machines, runs, cores and hours (p. 17); rmine10 solved in December 2015 after 48 jobs, about 75 days and about 5,660 CPU-core years, one Titan job with 79,984 Solvers (pp. 17, 19–20); MIPLIB 2017 instances with time to first solution and to optimality (p. 17); no baseline and no repetition, and R020's timtab2 comparison is gone.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: 21 previously unsolved instances and the 80,000-core record (pp. 3, 20); parallel versions of customized SCIP solvers (SCIP-Jack, SCIP-SDP) (p. 22). Limits: parallel MIP evaluation is hard; strong scaling is not appropriate because UG changes the algorithm and parallel B&B shows anomalies; a method that accounts for algorithmic changes is wanted, and [17] is meant to start that research (pp. 20, 22); deep probing is not yet fully investigated and automatic restarts are under study (p. 16). Future: combine with the solver's internal shared-memory parallelization; "the key" is algorithms that do not work sequentially but work in parallel (p. 22).

**Inconsistencies noted** (not resolved; no search in this batch): dolom1 is dated March 2014 here (p. 17) and March 2013 in R020 (p. 16); rmine10 is dated December 2015 here (p. 17), while the February 2016 revision of the 2016 paper still reports it as being attacked (D018 TR p. 17); Koch is spelled "Thosten" (pp. 1, 4); hyper-threading is "(HT)" in Table 1 and "(TH)" in the text (pp. 17, 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ (variant): the base solver stays a black box whose progress carries over (p. 5), but this version weights what parallelization changes: algorithmic differences from SCIP are "more important" (p. 6), the gains are credited to SCIP's improvement combined with UG's algorithmic changes in a hedged sentence (p. 19), and the key is algorithms that work only in parallel (p. 22).
- Method 2 ⚠ (variant): the payoff is redefined as solvability: linear speed-up cannot help on an NP-hard class (p. 6), strong scaling is rejected as a measure (p. 20), and the evidence offered is open instances solved over seven years (pp. 5, 17, 19).
- Method 4 ⚠ (variant): no library-wide comparison; small-scale evaluation is delegated to the FiberSCIP paper (pp. 12, 20).
- New-pattern candidates: *make the forced restart pay* (pp. 11, 13, 16); *parallelization as an algorithm change* (pp. 6, 12, 22); *name the substitute evidence when no protocol fits* (pp. 19–20, 22).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before reporting parallel speedups, list what the parallel layer changes in the algorithm (here re-presolving every subproblem, and racing); if it changes the search, say why strong scaling misleads and name the evidence used instead (pp. 6, 12, 20).
- Bound the checkpoint size by the coordinator's own parameters, so that I/O stays predictable (pp. 11–12).
- Keep a ledger for record computations: date, solver and LP-solver versions, machine, runs, cores, hours, value and starting solution (Table 1, p. 17; p. 19).

**Quotes**:
- "For tackling unsolved MIP instances, one should not rely on parallelization alone, even if it is on a supercomputer level." (p. 6)
- "Therefore, evaluating performance by (strong) scalability would not be appropriate." (p. 20)
