### D020 · An Exact Method for the Minimum Feedback Arc Set Problem (ACM Journal of Experimental Algorithmics 2021, 10.1145/3446429)
**Read level**: full. All 28 pages, including Appendix A (pp. 18–22). Figures 1–7 are images, so only their captions and the text's reading of them were read. The reference list (pp. 24–28) was skimmed to see whose work is cited.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ali Baharev (corresponding author), Hermann Schichl, Arnold Neumaier (all University of Vienna); Achterberg is last author with the byline Gurobi GmbH, Frankfurt (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Gurobi era. Received March 2019, revised October 2020, accepted December 2020 (p. 28); published March 2021 (p. 1). The trajectory note places it in a collaboration with Baharev that goes back to a 2009 AIChE J. paper with Rév (06-trajectory.md, W17, W30). Funding is Austrian only (FWF P27891-N32, FFG 846920; p. 1). The paper states no author roles. The only Achterberg-specific traces are the byline and the citations of his thesis [1] and the SCIP paper [2] (p. 8; refs p. 24). The Gurobi internals the text relies on (MIPSOL versus MIPNODE callbacks, the internal lazy-constraint pool, "all 17" Gurobi cutting-plane algorithms; pp. 9–11) are what a Gurobi developer would supply (*inferred*, not stated).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The minimum feedback arc set (MFAS) problem is NP-hard (Karp's list), APX-hard and approximation resistant under the UGC (pp. 2–3). The set-covering formulation over the cycle matrix is "appropriate as long as all simple cycles in G can be enumerated", but sparse graphs that matter in practice can have Ω(2^n) simple cycles (abstract, p. 1; pp. 5–6, 8). The entry is an application: tearing in chemical-process flowsheeting, where an MFAS gives the computation sequence of the sequential-modular approach. This is the authors' "primary interest" (pp. 4–5). The question is put as the *practical limits* of an exact method on computationally challenging sparse graphs (p. 1). The complexity survey (DP in O*(2^n), FPT in O(n^4 4^k k^3 k!), the dense-graph PTAS; pp. 2–3) is used to rule the theory out for these instances: k is of order 10^2, so the FPT algorithm "is not an option" (p. 16).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Generate cycles lazily, and let a primal heuristic choose them (pp. 8–11; Algorithms 1–2, pp. 10–11). The loop:
  - Solve the set-cover relaxation P̃(k) over the incomplete cycle matrix and remove its feedback arc set from G.
  - A topological sort decides whether the rest is acyclic. If it is, the relaxation's solution is optimal for the full problem (p. 9).
  - If not, a greedy heuristic computes a feedback arc set F(k) of the remaining graph, which gives a feasible solution and an incumbent.
  - For every arc e in F(k), a BFS shortest path from head(e) to tail(e) closes a chordless simple cycle, which is appended as a new row.
  - Stop when the graph is acyclic or the lower bound equals the incumbent (p. 9).

  Lazy generation is credited to Dantzig–Fulkerson–Johnson (1954), with column generation as its dual (1958) (p. 10). The novelty is claimed for how the rows are chosen (Quote 2): heuristic feedback arc set → tightest BFS cycles through its arcs → new rows (p. 11). Against the set-cover formulation the change is that cycles are no longer enumerated in full. Against the triangle-inequality formulation the integer programs have Θ(n) times fewer columns and, at n = 100, about 100 times fewer rows (p. 18). What stays: the host solver does presolve, cuts and branching. The rows enter through Gurobi's `LazyConstraints = 1` and a callback (p. 9).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - **Graphs**: directed, without self-loops or multiple arcs (p. 2). Weights are nonnegative; the unweighted case is unit weight (pp. 3, 7, 10).
  - **Models**: the method solves the pure-binary set-covering IP (4) (p. 7). The baseline is the ordering IP (3) with triangle inequalities, which has O(n^2) binaries and O(n^3) constraints (p. 7).
  - **Target class**: sparse graphs. Dense graphs and tournaments are declared its worst case (pp. 11, 16).
  - **Correctness assumptions**: finitely many simple cycles, and at least one new row per iteration, give finite termination (p. 9). The safe-arc-removal rule needs an induced subgraph small enough to enumerate its cycles; a "cycle budget per SCC" enforces this (p. 21). A corner case, a cycle with exactly one node in the subgraph, is left unhandled, and the authors note that correctness is not affected (p. 22).
  - **Stated weakness**: BFS-based row choice "can potentially lead to poor performance if the arc weight distribution is pathological". This was never observed (p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**:
  - **Optimality certificate**: either the topological sort succeeds on G minus the relaxation's arcs, or the bounds meet (pp. 9–10). Termination follows from the cycle matrix growing by at least one row per iteration (p. 9).
  - **Two descriptions of the loop**: the pseudo-code re-solves P̃(i) in every iteration (Algorithm 1, line 8, p. 10). The implementation adds rows in a callback inside one branch-and-bound run (pp. 9, 11). The paper does not discuss the difference.
  - **Lazy constraints versus cuts**: lazy rows are needed for correctness of an incomplete model and are added in a MIPSOL callback, which runs "far less often" than there are nodes. Cuts are optional, go in a MIPNODE callback, and "should be added sparingly". So the method augments Gurobi's cuts and does not compete with them (p. 11).
  - **Pools**: the method never deletes rows. Gurobi keeps its own lazy pool, drops superfluous rows and separates its own cuts (pp. 9–10). The largest cycle matrix in 4,468 graphs had 2,282 rows (n = 100, c = 7, seed 43; p. 9).
  - **Presolve**: split into nontrivial strongly connected components (SCCs), then iteratively remove runs and 3-arc bypasses (p. 12). The appendix adds a generic safe-removal rule. Let c1 be the exact cost of making an induced subgraph acyclic, and c2 the cost, found by a heuristic, of also breaking every cycle of G that enters the subgraph. If c1 = c2, the heuristic's arcs are safe to remove (pp. 18–19). Fake nodes u and v, with arcs (t, u), (v, h) and (u, v), make the boundary cycles explicit (pp. 21–22). The rule was too expensive for presolve. It was kept as a diagnostic: it isolated a hard 32-node core, Problem 11, from Problem 10 and showed Problem 10 can be reduced by a factor of 73 in simple cycles (pp. 18, 22).
  - **Software**: pure Python with NetworkX, Gurobi through its API, source on GitHub [8] (pp. 10, 12, 17).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **Correctness first**: 11 chemical-engineering graphs with published optima, cross-checked against both IP formulations (Table 1, p. 13). All were solved at the root; the slowest, Problem 10, took 0.02 s (p. 13).
  - **Scalability sets**:
    - 3,737 Erdős–Rényi graphs with 101 graphs per (n, c) point and the median time plotted (Fig. 1, pp. 13–14).
    - 12 de Bruijn and 12 Imase–Itoh graphs with a 3 h limit (Tables 2–3, p. 15).
    - 707 random tournaments, 101 per n (Fig. 2, p. 16).
    - The complete graph with n = 30 (pp. 16–17).
    - The benchmark's 4,468 graphs equal 3,737 + 707 + 24. This is my sum; the paper does not itemise it.
  - **Baselines**: the triangle-inequality IP in the same Gurobi. The set-cover IP counts as intractable above 10^7 cycles, proved by enumerating 10^7 + 1 cycles with Johnson's algorithm; 10^7 cycles already need more than 4 GB (p. 12). The Grötschel–Jünger–Reinelt cutting-plane code was not run (pp. 8, 11).
  - **Fairness**: all methods start from the same simplified graphs (pp. 12, 18).
  - **Hardware and software**: Setting A is an i5-4670S with Gurobi 6.5.1, Python 2.7.11 and NetworkX 1.9.1. Setting B, used during revision, is an i5-3320M with Gurobi 8.1.0, Python 3.6.6 and NetworkX 1.11; it was used only for §5.6 and the complete graph (p. 12). §§5.5 and 5.7 were not rerun in Setting B because the suite takes more than one CPU-month (p. 12).
  - **Statistic**: median wall time per generator point. There is one solver run per graph and no solver seeds; variability enters only through the 101 generated graphs per point (pp. 14, 16).
  - **Failures printed**:
    - With the newer Gurobi of Setting B, one structured graph timed out where Setting A had none. The incumbent was optimal and the bound was off by 1 (p. 15; Table 3, p. 15).
    - Tournaments are lost from n = 32 on (p. 16).
    - The limits are stated as unsolved counts under hypothetical time limits. With 1 CPU-hour: 16 of 101 at (n, c) = (75, 9), 28 of 101 at (100, 7), and 4 of 24 structured graphs (p. 17). With 30 CPU-minutes: 40 of 101 tournaments at n = 34 (p. 18).
  - **Diagnosis of the baseline**: within 3 h the triangle IP explored at most three nodes, typically 0 or 1 (p. 16). The gain is explained by model size, about 10^4 times fewer nonzeros, with the caveat that "size is not necessarily a good measure of difficulty" (p. 18).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Exact methods**: three families are reviewed: dynamic programming, custom branch-and-bound, and IP (p. 7). The triangle formulation and the Grötschel–Jünger–Reinelt linear-ordering cutting-plane algorithm are classed as dense-graph methods with an O(n^3) scaling issue. The latter is quoted on its own admitted gaps and on its brute-force enumeration of 3-dicycles (p. 8).
  - **Heuristics**: reviewed only as far as the method needs them: Lee–Rudd's set-cover greedy, degree-score greedy heuristics, sorting heuristics, Park–Akers (pp. 5–7).
  - **Novelty**: hedged: "We are not aware of any published algorithm that would apply lazy constraint generation in the context of the minimum feedback arc set problem" (p. 11).
  - **Tone toward the cutting-plane line**: future researchers are invited to use the benchmark as a baseline (p. 8). A sparse-graph cutting-plane algorithm is left to others (p. 11).
  - **Terminology**: the three uses of "tearing" in chemical engineering are sorted out (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Contributions appear as a numbered list in the conclusion: method, benchmark, limits "clearly evaluated" (p. 17).
  - The abstract hedges: "In all cases encountered so far" (p. 1).
  - Scope fences appear twice. The first reads "We have no doubt that the preceding quoted issues can be addressed, but this is outside the scope of the present work" (p. 8); the second makes the same move for a sparse-graph cutting-plane algorithm (p. 11).
  - The worst-case experiments are announced as such and judged "satisfactory for a method designed for the sparse case" (p. 17).
  - Keeping easy graphs is justified explicitly: they show the lower end of the scalability curve and serve correctness checks (p. 12).
  - A negative experiment is reported for what it is: the complete graph "does not trigger poor performance in either method (although it could have been the case)" (p. 17).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution levels**: an algorithm; a benchmark suite of 4,468 graphs with their optima as plain text on GitHub; code (pp. 10, 17).
  - **Stated limits**:
    - Built for sparse graphs (p. 17).
    - Theoretical blow-up of the cycle matrix, never met (p. 9).
    - Possible BFS failure on pathological weights (p. 9).
    - The safe-removal rule is too costly for presolve (p. 18).
    - An unhandled corner case (p. 22).
    - The Python preprocessing cannot compete with Gurobi's C presolve (p. 12).
    - An attempt to compile an updated chemical-engineering benchmark failed (p. 13).
  - **Future work**: a better shortest-path routine (p. 9); sparse-graph cutting planes, left to others (pp. 8, 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 (Instrument first): evidence ✅ (co-signed). Correctness is cross-checked on graphs with published optima against both IP formulations before any scaling study (pp. 11–13). Easy graphs are kept because they "are indispensable for cross checking correctness of algorithms" (p. 12). The optima are published with the graphs (p. 17), the role the known-optimum files play in Method 5 step 2. The check is on final optima, not on derived objects as in Heuristic 4.
- Method 1 (Do-no-harm default gate): variant ⚠. The method is problem-specific, so there is no detector and no no-op path (the skill's Limitations: steps 1–4 only). But the class it was not built for (tournaments, the complete graph) is run on purpose, and the loss is printed (n ≥ 32; pp. 16–17). This is the "safeguard the worst case" half of the method, done as a test, not as a gate.
- Heuristic 3 (Fair yardstick): evidence ✅. All methods start from identical simplified graphs (pp. 12, 18). The baseline runs in the same Gurobi and is the formulation designed for the dense case (pp. 7, 16).
- Heuristic 10 (Print the loss and the open question): evidence ✅. The paper prints one time-out under the newer Gurobi (p. 15), the tournaments lost from n = 32 (p. 16), unsolved counts at named sizes (pp. 17–18), and open points (BFS rule, corner case; pp. 9, 22).
- Not a Method 4 study. It has no knock-out from a default, no shifted geometric means and no brackets, only medians over 101 generated graphs per point (pp. 14, 16).
- new-pattern candidate: **heuristic repair as separation oracle**. The repair heuristic's feedback arc set is both the incumbent and the seed of the next lazy rows (pp. 9–11).
- new-pattern candidate: **correctness rows lazily, the host solver's cuts kept**. Add only what an incomplete model needs for correctness and leave performance cuts to the full commercial solver. This is stated as the reason not to compete with its cutting planes (p. 11; Quote 1).
- new-pattern candidate: **scalability-grid benchmark with a deliberate worst-case family**. Generator families over size and density, 101 instances per point, easy points kept for trend and correctness, and one family the method is not built for (pp. 12–17).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Use the repair heuristic's output twice. It is the incumbent, and each of its arcs seeds a BFS shortest cycle that becomes a new lazy row (pp. 9–11).
- [D4] Put the constraints needed for correctness in a lazy (MIPSOL) callback and leave performance cuts to the host solver. Say in the paper which is which (p. 11).
- [D4] Certify a relaxation solution with a linear-time combinatorial check (topological sort) instead of the full constraint set (p. 9).
- [D4] Safe-reduction test: if the exact cost of the induced subproblem equals a heuristic's cost of also breaking every boundary cycle, the heuristic's arcs are safe to fix. Fake source and sink nodes expose the boundary cycles (pp. 19–22).
- [D5] Build the benchmark as a grid over size and density: 101 instances per point, medians, easy points kept, and one family outside the design class (pp. 12–17).
- [D5] State a method's limit as "k of 101 unsolved within T at size n", not as a single average (pp. 17–18).
- [D5] When the solver version changes during a revision, rerun the hardest set and report what changed (p. 15).

**Quotes**:
- "Any research attempting to implement a novel cutting plane algorithm has to compete with that strong baseline." (p. 11)
- "the real novelty is in how the lazy constraint generation is carried out" (p. 11)

### D021 · MIPLIB 2017: data-driven compilation of the 6th mixed-integer programming library (Mathematical Programming Computation 2021, 10.1007/s12532-020-00194-3)
**Read level**: full. All 48 file pages (journal pp. 443–490): §§1–7, footnotes, acknowledgements, Tables 1–11. Figures 1–7 were read through their captions and the text. The reference list (pp. 44–46) was skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ambros Gleixner (corresponding), Gregor Hendel, Gerald Gamrath, Michael Bastubbe, Timo Berthold, Philipp Christophel, Kati Jarck, Thorsten Koch, Jeff Linderoth, Marco Lübbecke, Hans Mittelmann, Derya Ozyurt, Ted Ralphs, Domenico Salvagnin, Yuji Shinano. Achterberg is 4th of 16, byline Gurobi GmbH, Frankfurt (pp. 1, 47) · **Position in trajectory**: Gurobi era. Received 25 July 2019, accepted 10 September 2020, published 7 January 2021 (p. 1). It is his third MIPLIB edition, after MIPLIB 2003 (first author with Koch and Martin, ref. [3], p. 44) and MIPLIB 2010 (ref. [32], p. 46). He is one of the 21 committee members (listed in p. 2, fn. 2), a committee that included "representatives of the development teams of nine MIP solvers" (p. 2). Individual roles are not stated. The acknowledgement names only the 1st, 2nd, 3rd, 9th and last authors as working within Research Campus MODAL (p. 44). His work appears as cited method: the thesis for the shift of the shifted geometric mean (p. 2, fn. 1; Quote 1) and the Gurobi presolve paper for presolve's impact (p. 12, ref. [2]).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is measured progress. On the MIPLIB 2010 benchmark, the shifted geometric mean (shift 1 s, 12 threads) of a virtual best of CPLEX, Gurobi and Xpress fell from 36.3 s (2011 versions) to 13.5 s (December 2018); the virtual worst fell from 113.0 s to 31.3 s. These are factors of 2.70 and 3.62, "roughly 16% per year, just from improvements in the algorithms" (p. 2). Open MIPLIB 2010 instances fell from 134 to 70 (p. 2). So the 2010 set "was no longer considered to sufficiently reflect the frontier" (p. 2). The second entry is scale and governance: 5,721 candidates (p. 7), an unbalanced pool, and a committee with many solver representatives. Together they called for a process that is "fair and transparent" (p. 3). The problem statement: select a collection and a benchmark subset that cover the submissions' feature space while staying balanced. The benchmark must also be balanced in performance and runnable in reasonable time (p. 24).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Turn selection into a sequence of MIPs over data:
  1. 105 instance features in 11 groups, computed after "trivial presolving" (pp. 12–14).
  2. For each large model group, a max–min-distance diversity preselection MIP (pp. 26–27).
  3. One k-means clustering per feature group instead of one clustering of the whole vector. The reasons given are the curse of dimensionality and feature scaling (pp. 28–29).
  4. A collection MIP. It maximizes the number of selected instances, with benchmark-suitable ones weighted 2. Its constraints:
     - balancedness per cluster, proportional to cluster dissimilarity;
     - at most one of any two identical feature vectors;
     - at most five per model group, and at least one benchmark-suitable instance per group that has one;
     - at least one per submitter;
     - relative caps: MiniZinc 5%, NEOS 33%, small 20%, medium 50%, pure binary 20% (pp. 31–32, Table 9).
  5. A benchmark MIP over the 499 benchmark-suitable instances. It adds 24 performance clusterings (three per solver), caps of one instance per NEOS group and two per other group, a 32-day runtime budget, and a hardness-favouring objective (pp. 33–36).

  What changed: earlier MIPLIBs were selected by hand by the committee (p. 3). What stays human: the choice of features, cluster counts, easy and hard thresholds, and the formalisations. "All of these decisions have a high impact on the final result" (pp. 3–4).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - **Canonical form**: Definition 1 has two-sided rows, bounds, and binary, integer and continuous variables. For the features, rows and objective are normalised by their largest absolute coefficient (pp. 13–14).
  - **Special constraints**: 437 instances have indicator constraints. They are allowed in the collection but not in the benchmark. No SOS, semicontinuous or piecewise-linear constraints were submitted (pp. 17–18).
  - **Benchmark suitability** (Definition 2, p. 25):
    - (B.1) solved by at least one solver within 4 h;
    - (B.2) at least 10 s for 50% of the solvers;
    - (B.3) constraint and objective dynamism ≤ 10^6;
    - (B.4) |coefficient| < 10^10;
    - (B.5) consistent results across solvers;
    - (B.6) no indicator constraints;
    - (B.7) infeasible or with a finite optimum;
    - (B.8) solution value < 10^10;
    - (B.9) at most 10^6 nonzeros.

    Table 7 prints each criterion's exclusion count; 3,407 instances are not suitable (pp. 25–26). Infeasible instances are kept on purpose. Unbounded ones are dropped because "there is currently no clear consensus on the expected behavior and output of MIP solvers" for them (p. 25).
  - **Model groups**: taken from the submissions. NEOS instances get pseudo-groups by k-means with k = 110. The largest group, cmflsp, has 360 instances (p. 11).
  - **Trivial presolve**: SCIP 5.0 with only simple reductions, run to a fixed point. 55 instances become LPs or are solved outright. Variables shrink on 66% of the instances, by 15% on average (p. 13). The distributed instances stay unmodified apart from MPS repairs (pp. 10, 12).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**:
  - **Diversity preselection MIP (1)**: maximise z subject to z ≤ (d_ij − d̄)x_i x_j + d̄, Σx_i = κ, and at least one benchmark-suitable instance. The products x_i x_j are linearised by w_ij (pp. 26–27). κ = 5 for groups of 6–10 instances and 10 for larger ones. Gurobi 7.5.1 solved each model within 500 s, except cmflsp at about 800 s with 129,242 rows (p. 27). The pool shrinks from 5,666 to 2,182 instances (p. 28).
  - **Clustering quality**: defined as the share of total pairwise distance that lies between clusters. L_k is the smallest cluster count giving at least 90% quality on the 5,666 instances (pp. 29–30). Table 8 shows 87.46–99.99%, 92% on average (pp. 29–30).
  - **Balancedness**: (1 − ε)δy_k ≤ Σ_{i∈C} x_i ≤ (1 + ε)δy_k. Here δ is the cluster's total dissimilarity, the shifted geometric mean (shift 1) of its pairwise distances. The lower side is dropped for small clusters (below half the average size) and the upper side for large ones (boxplot outliers); ε = 0.5 (pp. 29–31).
  - **Sensitivity to ε** (Table 10, p. 33): ε = 0.1 is infeasible, ε = 0.2 finds no feasible selection in 15 minutes, 0.3 selects 740 instances, 0.5 selects 1,065 (499 suitable), and 0.9 selects 1,140.
  - **Performance clusterings** (pp. 33–35): time-outs are set to the 4 h limit.
    - Absolute ranks into B = 11 equal clusters.
    - Relative performance (t_w,i + σ)/(min_{w'≠w} t_w',i + σ), with the shift used "as in the computation of shifted geometric means".
    - Binned absolute times with bin edges T_j = 10^(−3.5+0.5j)·14,400 for j = 1, …, 7.
    - In each, the unsolved instances are split into those solved by exactly one other solver and those solved by more.
  - **Benchmark MIP (8)** (pp. 35–36):
    - balancedness over all 35 clusterings;
    - the runtime budget Σt̄_i x_i ≤ τ = 32 days, with t̄_i = min(median time, 7,200 s);
    - objective weight β_i = 1 + (1/20)√(min_w t_w,i). The text names the square root as "empirically motivated" (p. 36); the radical is lost in the extracted formula.

    The result is 240 instances. The budget was not tight: a median solver would need about 14 days (p. 36).
  - **Solution checker** (pp. 22–23): exact GMP arithmetic against the original MPS file. A row now counts as satisfied when a·x* − u ≤ ε·max{(a·x*)+, (a·x*)−, |u|, 1} with ε = 10^−5; the same test applies to bounds and to the objective value. Integrality keeps an absolute 10^−4. The checker was extended to indicator constraints.
  - **Cross-solver consistency check with IPET** (p. 23): an instance counts as inconsistent when one solver's verified primal value is below another's dual bound. Example: on bc1 seven solvers agree on 3.338, while one reports 3.418 as optimal after 720 nodes (p. 23). 328 instances (5%) were removed unless the inconsistency was obviously one solver's bug (p. 23). Table 7 instead counts 334 under (B.5) on the 5,666-instance ground set (p. 26); the text does not reconcile the two numbers.
  - **Decomposition features**: GCG 3.0 in a minimal detection configuration, with an "area score" (pp. 18–19).
  - **Disclosure**: five features were found redundant only while the manuscript was being prepared. They stayed in the computations, and a footnote says so (p. 16, fn. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **Data**: eight solvers: CBC 2.9.8, CPLEX 12.7.1, Gurobi 7.5.1, MATLAB R2017b, MOSEK 8.1.0.30, SAS/OR 14.2, SCIP 4.0.0, Xpress 8.2. All use 4 threads except SCIP and MATLAB (one thread). The limit is 4 h. The runs used two clusters with two jobs per node, almost 40 CPU years in total (p. 20).
  - **Reporting rule**: only virtual best, worst and median solvers are reported. The runs were not all on the same hardware and "should hence not be reported in a way that could be confused for an actual benchmark" (pp. 20, 38). Each solver is fastest on at least one instance (pp. 3, 20).
  - **Pool statistics** (p. 20): 1,969 instances (34%) are solved by no solver within 4 h, and 1,155 (20%) by all eight. The virtual best solves more in 2 s than the virtual worst does in 4 h. Node counts are normalised to at least 1 because solvers report a solve during presolve as 0 or 1 nodes (p. 22).
  - **New versus old benchmark** (Table 11, p. 39): the virtual median needs more than 1 h on 66.7% of the 2017 set versus 17.2% of the 2010 set. The virtual best needs more than 1 h on 19.6% versus 0%.
  - **Validation of the selection**:
    - The feature space recovers the model groups. P(similarity edge | same group) ≈ 0.734, against 0.007 for a random edge, and ≈ 0.815 without NEOS (p. 37).
    - One-sided KS tests of the benchmark against the benchmark-suitable CDFs: virtual median D ≈ 0.10, p = 0.04; virtual best D = 0.17, p = 6.513·10^−5; four solvers with p < 1% and four with p > 0.1. The conclusion drawn is "hardness and representability" achieved together (pp. 40–41).
  - **Stated gaps**: the data are "a snapshot of current algorithms", gathered with a time limit, and "performance variability and parallel scalability were not captured" (p. 36). No seeds or permutations were used in the selection runs.
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Lineage**: MIPLIB began with Bixby, Boyd and Indovina in 1992 and had four updates (p. 2).
  - **Reporting standards**: Crowder–Dembo–Mulvey 1978 and Jackson et al. 1991, the latter with their call for standard test sets (p. 5).
  - **Origin of the idea**: Hooker 1993, read as making "the issue of problem choice …one of experimental design". The paper calls its approach "an extension of this idea" (p. 5).
  - **Related work**: McGeoch; Smith-Miles et al.'s instance-space analysis, called complementary (it fills gaps, whereas this paper covers a fixed pool; p. 5); algorithm selection and ASlib (pp. 5–6); the other libraries (MINLPLib, QPLIB, Netlib, COR@L, OR-Library; p. 6).
  - **Predecessors**: earlier MIPLIBs are credited with expert manual selection, not criticised (p. 3).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - Transparency is the stated reason for the level of detail (p. 4).

### D022 · Efficient Separation of RLT Cuts for Implicit and Explicit Bilinear Products (IPCO 2023, LNCS 13904, pp. 14–28, 10.1007/978-3-031-32726-1_2; read as arXiv 2211.13545v1)
**Read level**: full. The whole v1 preprint was read: 16 file pages, of which pp. 1–2 are the ZIB-Report 22-24 cover and imprint, pp. 3–15 the text and pp. 15–16 the references. The first page is arXiv 2211.13545v1 [math.OC] of 24 November 2022, with the same title and authors (pp. 1, 3). The LNCS print was not read. Page references are to this preprint and may differ from the IPCO version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ksenia Bestuzheva (ZIB), Ambros Gleixner (ZIB and HTW Berlin). Achterberg is last author, Gurobi GmbH (p. 3) · **Position in trajectory**: Gurobi era. It is the first public trace of the RLT work with ZIB, later extended as D023 (Math. Program. 2025; 06-trajectory.md, W32). The funding note covers only Research Campus MODAL, the ZIB side (p. 15). Roles are not stated. The Gurobi 10.0 beta experiments on Gurobi's internal test set (§5.4, p. 14) are the only part that needs Gurobi access, so they are probably his share (*inferred*).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: RLT gives strong relaxations, "but this comes at the cost of excessive numbers of cuts" (p. 3). Even for bilinear products, separation cost and LP size cause "considerable slowdowns" (p. 4). There are two gaps:
  - RLT is not applied to bilinear relations hidden in MILPs. Earlier approaches only introduce products by multiplication (p. 4).
  - Separation cost is "the major bottleneck for applying RLT successfully in practice" (p. 4).

  The stated goal is "to extend the applicability and improve the performance of RLT for bilinear product relations" (abstract, p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Three pieces (pp. 5–10):
  1. **Detection of implicit products**. A product w = x_i x_j with x_i binary and x_j bounded is equivalent to two big-M linear constraints (3). Pairs of linear constraints on the same three variables are therefore scanned and turned into product relations by Theorem 1 (pp. 5–7).
  2. **Row marking**. Replacing x_i x_j by w_ij in a row × bound-factor product raises the violation only for certain sign combinations of (x*_i x*_j − w*_ij) and the row coefficient. Rows are marked LT, GT or BOTH, and only the matching row × factor pairs are built (pp. 7–9).
  3. **Projection filtering**. The cut is first tested on the system projected onto the variables not at a bound. The full cut is built only if the projected cut is violated (pp. 9–10).

  Changed against the "standard separation algorithm", which loops over all rows and all product variables (p. 7). Kept: the RLT linearisation rules of §2 (McCormick, clique-implied products, secants and tangents; p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - **Problem form**: MI(N)LP (1) with bilinear relations x_i x_j ⋚ w_ij, linear rows, bounds and binaries (p. 4). Bound factors need finite bounds (p. 5).
  - **Detection conditions**: x_i binary and x_j with finite bounds (pp. 5–6). Theorem 1 needs b1b2 > 0 and γ = c2b1 − b2c1 ≠ 0 (p. 6). In practice two further filters apply: one of a1, a2 must be nonzero, and the binary's two coefficients must have opposite signs, by which "we focus on stronger implications" (p. 7).
  - **Candidate relations**: implied relations, implied bounds, cliques, constraints on x_j and w_ij, and global bounds (p. 7).
  - **Projection argument**: it assumes the McCormick inequalities hold at x*. If they do not (solver-dependent), projection gives McCormick cuts priority over RLT cuts (pp. 9–10).
  - **Test sets**: MINLPLib, 1,846 instances reduced to 1,357, and 666 MILPs (MIPLIB 3, 2003, 2010, 2017 and Cor@l) reduced to 195. Only instances with explicit or detected products were kept (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**:
  - **Proof of Theorem 1**: match coefficients. Write the target relation and both constraints as implications for x_i = 1 and x_i = 0, with scaling parameters α and β, and solve for A, B, C, D and the inequality sign (p. 6). The statement's second case prints the coefficient of w_ij as b2b2, whereas the first case and the proof give B = b1b2/γ (p. 6). It reads as a typo.
  - **Data structures**: access to each variable's product partners and to the relation of a pair; marks stored as integers 1–3 in two sorted sparse arrays `row_idcs` and `row_marks`; a sparse matrix W (p. 8); pseudo-code on p. 9.
  - **Mark-to-factor rule**: LT multiplies "≤" rows by the lower factor and "≥" rows by the upper factor; GT does the reverse; BOTH uses all four combinations. Marked equalities are multiplied by x_i itself (p. 8).
  - **Budgets** (p. 10): at most 20 unknown bilinear terms per row × factor product; separation every 10 nodes; 1 round at the non-root nodes where it is called and 10 at the root.
  - **Code**: a SCIP development branch (githash dd6c54a9d7) with SoPlex 5.0.2.4, CppAD, PaPILO 1.0.0.1, bliss 0.73p and Ipopt 3.12.11 (p. 10). Gurobi 10.0 beta implements "the same" algorithms, and "implementation details may differ" (p. 14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **SCIP setup**: a Xeon E5-2670 v2 cluster, 1 h limit, gap tolerance 10^−4 for MINLP and 10^−6 for MILP (p. 10).
  - **Metrics** (p. 11): solved counts; shifted geometric means of time (shift 1 s) and nodes (shift 100); "affected" instances, those whose LP iteration count changes; brackets [x, timelim], meaning instances that took at least x s with at least one setting and were solved by at least one; an all-optimal subset.
  - **MILP, IERLT against Off** (Table 1, p. 11): time ratio 1.03 on all instances, 1.06 on [10, tilim], 0.98 on [100, tilim] and 0.79 on [1000, tilim]. The node ratio goes from 0.98 to 0.72. Solved: 905 → 909.
  - **MINLP, explicit products** (ERLT against Off; Table 2, p. 12): time 0.85 and nodes 0.81 on all instances; 0.13 and 0.12 on [1000, tilim]. Two inconsistencies:

  - **MINLP, implicit products** (Table 3, p. 12): time +1% overall and +9% on [100, tilim]; nodes +6–7% on the two hardest brackets (p. 11). The ratio column is headed ERLT/IERLT, but its values are IERLT/ERLT (57.4/57.0 = 1.01), which matches the text.
