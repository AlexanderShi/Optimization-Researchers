### D011 · The Mcf-separator: detecting and exploiting multi-commodity flow structures in MIPs (Mathematical Programming Computation 2010, DOI 10.1007/s12532-010-0015-3)
**Read level**: full. All 41 file pages were read: text, Algorithms 1–5 and the two functions, Tables 1–7, the axis labels of Fig. 5 and the reference list (pp. 39–41). Figs. 1–4 are matrix drawings that extract as columns of digits (pp. 6–7, 10, 12), so they were read through their captions only. Some symbols are lost in extraction: the Ψ of the inconsistency ratios in places, and the cluster-count symbol on pp. 25–26 and 37, which R013 p. 29 writes as Ω.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Christian Raack (ZIB, marked as corresponding author, p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Achterberg's affiliation is IBM Böblingen (p. 1), so this is the CPLEX period. The separator ships in SCIP 1.2 and CPLEX 12.1 at the same time (p. 2). Received 30 November 2009, accepted 27 May 2010 (p. 1). Roles are not stated. *Inferred*: the CPLEX implementation and the runs on the CPLEX-internal library are Achterberg's, since that library is internal to CPLEX (p. 34). The network-design cut theory is Raack's line of work (refs [49, 50], p. 40).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is a measured blind spot of general solvers. Cut-based MIR inequalities are known to be strong for network design, but SCIP's and CPLEX's c-MIR rarely find them (p. 3, citing Raack et al. [49]). The paper names two reasons (p. 3):
  1. the solver does not know the network;
  2. the aggregation needed involves too many rows, while the aggregation limit is kept conservative because it appears in the exponent of the running time and long aggregations give dense cuts. The limit is 7 rows in SCIP and a similar value in CPLEX.

  The design constraint comes from a block quote of Bixby and Rothberg: a method should recognise the models it applies to and add little or no overhead elsewhere (p. 2). The problem as stated: given a general MIP, detect an embedded coupled multi-commodity arc-flow structure in the constraint matrix and generate cutting planes from cuts in the detected network (abstract, p. 1; p. 2).
  - Transferable technique: state exactly why a general solver's existing routine misses a known strong cut family (here: the structure is unknown, and the aggregation length is capped), then remove that reason instead of writing a new cut class (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key insight is that the classical cut-based inequalities of network design are all MIR inequalities of one base inequality (6). These are cutset, flow-cutset, (mixed) dicut and flow-cover inequalities. Inequality (6) comes from aggregating the flow rows of a node set S and the capacity rows of a subset of δ−(S) (pp. 7–9). So the separator only has to choose the aggregation:
  - What changes: the default c-MIR aggregation heuristic is replaced by a weight vector u, plus multipliers γ taken from the capacity coefficients, both computed from the detected network (pp. 2–3, 23).
  - What stays: bound substitution, complementing, scaling and rounding are done by the solver's existing c-MIR routines (p. 3, p. 23).
  - Why it works: the aggregation can involve hundreds or thousands of rows (more than 2,500 for 50 nodes and 100 commodities), yet the base inequality's support is a network cut, so it stays sparse (p. 3; p. 38).

  The other half of the idea is a consistency gate. The detector measures how well the structure it found fits, and the separator generates no cuts when the fit is poor (p. 2; Algorithm 5, step 1, p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: when the known cut families of a structure are MIR inequalities of one aggregated row, implement them as an aggregation heuristic for the solver's c-MIR, not as separate separators (pp. 3, 9, 23).
- **D3 Problem class & data model** (team.json): The input is a general MIP: min κᵀx, Ax ≤ b, x_j ∈ Z for j ∈ I, with the variable bounds inside A (p. 2). The target structure is the arc-flow formulation (2a)–(2d): one copy of the node-arc incidence matrix per commodity (the flow-conservation rows), coupled by one capacity row per arc with integer capacity modules (p. 5). Variants handled:
  - multi-facility capacity rows (9) (p. 26);
  - unsplittable flow, where the capacity rows have commodity-dependent coefficients (2b″); these rows are normalised and the flow rows scaled to match (p. 27);
  - undirected capacity rows (2b‴), selected by a modeltype parameter. Its default detects the type: on average ≥ 2 flow variables per commodity in a capacity row means undirected (pp. 27–28).

  What the detector relies on:
  - flow rows whose nonzeros share one absolute value, with no column scaling (p. 14);
  - node degree at most 10 % of the arcs (p. 14);
  - commodities of similar size: at least 3 rows and at least 50 % of the largest commodity (pp. 15, 18);
  - (almost) all arc-flow variables of an arc appearing in its coupling row (p. 26).

  It must also survive presolving. Modelers omit one flow row per commodity and drop arcs into sources or out of sinks, and solver presolving can delete more than 20 % of the nodes and arcs (pp. 12, 22).

  Test sets (Table 1, p. 21):
  - Seven public network-design sets: arc.set, avub, cut.set, fc, fctp, sndlib and ufcn, 297 instances in total (own sum). They cover single- and multi-commodity flow, splittable and unsplittable flow, binary, integer and GUB capacities, directed and undirected models, single and multiple facilities, and big-M.
  - General sets: miplib (MIPLIB 3.0 and 2003, 92 instances) and mittelmann (59, January 2009, made disjoint from miplib and fctp).
  - A CPLEX-internal library of 1266 easy instances (p. 34).

  Correctness needs nothing from the detector: every cut is an MIR inequality of an aggregation of original rows (p. 35).
  - Transferable technique: list the formulation habits of modelers and presolvers that will break a structure detector (omitted rows, removed arcs, substituted variables), and design for partial, non-isomorphic blocks from the start (pp. 12, 17).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design** (team.json): Detection runs in four steps (pp. 9–20).
  1. **Flow detection** (Algorithm 1, p. 14) is a per-commodity version of Bixby and Fourer's row-scanning-addition algorithm.
     - Rows are scored by these properties: unscaled ±1 coefficients; all-continuous, all-integer or all-binary variables; both signs present; equation. Ties are broken by the number of nonzeros and the |dual| in the first LP (pp. 14–15).
     - Only equations are reflected. A ≤ row that would need reflection gets a lower score, so no slacks enter the aggregation (p. 15).
     - Commodities that are too small are released. The algorithm terminates because each row starts a commodity at most once (p. 15).
     - Arrays counting the +1 and −1 entries per column speed up the fit test (p. 15).
  2. **Arc detection** (Algorithm 2, p. 16) scores capacity-row candidates by the commodities they cover, one flow variable per commodity, the sign pattern and unit coefficients. A row creates an arc if more than a third of its flow variables are still unassigned, which tolerates the overlap left by presolve (pp. 16–17).
  3. **Node detection** (Algorithm 3, p. 17; PatternOf and ComparePattern, p. 19) maps flow rows of different commodities whose arc-patterns overlap to the same node (pp. 17–18). A cleanup then removes commodities with no arcs or too few nodes (p. 18).
  4. **Network construction** (Algorithm 4, p. 20) lets each arc's flow variables vote for its source and target, and the majority wins (pp. 11, 18, 20).
     - The minority votes give the arc inconsistency Ψ(a); their mean is the network inconsistency Ψ(G).
     - Arcs with Ψ(a) > 0.5 are deleted. If Ψ(G) > 0.02, all network data are released and no cut is attempted (p. 11, p. 19).
     - Uncapacitated arcs are added when at least 0.8|K| commodities have an uncapacitated flow variable for the same (s, t) (p. 19).

  **Separation** (Algorithm 5, pp. 23–25) handles each node set S as follows.
  - It aggregates the tight flow rows of the demand commodities and the capacity rows of δ−(S) with slack at most 0.1.
  - It tries up to 20 multipliers, taken from the integer capacity coefficients and merged when their ratio is below 1.001, to find a violated cutset inequality (8).
  - Only if none is found does it try one mixed flow-cutset inequality (7). The most violated arc subset is found in linear time by dropping the arcs with f*_a < r·y*_a (pp. 24–25).

  **Node sets** (pp. 25–26): all singletons, plus all cuts of a graph shrunk to 5 clusters. Shrinking contracts the node pair with the largest weight w_a = s*_a − |π*_a|, the capacity row's slack minus its absolute dual. By complementary slackness these two are never both positive. A node set is kept only if both shores are connected, checked by breadth-first search.

  The separator is called in every separation round, but only at the root (p. 29). In SCIP it also generates exactly lifted knapsack covers on (6). CPLEX's flow-cover and knapsack-cover separators on (6) were tried and dropped because they did not improve performance (p. 28).

  **Invariant**: cuts are valid whatever the detector does (p. 35). The authors add a warning: with large inconsistency the method amounts to randomised aggregation and can produce dense, unstable cuts (p. 35). They also state that the pseudocode is not the implementation, which needs more involved data structures (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: give a heuristic structure detector a numeric fit measure (here, minority votes divided by the number of commodities) and use it twice: to drop bad elements, and to switch the whole component off (pp. 11, 20).
  - Transferable technique: select network cuts by shrinking with "slack minus |dual|" weights of the capacity rows, enumerating all cuts of the shrunken graph and keeping those with connected shores (pp. 25–26).
- **D5 Computational evaluation** (team.json):

  **Setup.**
  - SCIP 1.1.0.8 with CPLEX 11.2.1 as LP solver, on a 3.00 GHz quad-core with 8 GB. Its separator equals the one in public SCIP 1.2, but other plug-ins may differ (p. 21).
  - CPLEX 12.1 on a 3.33 GHz quad-core with 16 GB (p. 29).
  - Single CPU, 1 h time limit and 6 GB memory limit for all runs (p. 29).

  **Detector check against known networks (Table 2, pp. 21–23).** The detector was first checked against the known original networks of six network-design sets, with and without presolve. It finds consistent networks in almost all network-design instances. The failures are named with their causes:
  - bk4x3 and gr4x6 are not detected and ran4x64 is detected wrongly, because dense fctp rows exceed the 10 % limit;
  - cut.set fails already in flow detection, because extra coupling rows are taken for flow rows.

  In the general sets a network is found in roughly half of the instances; under presolve it is consistent in 23 miplib and 6 mittelmann instances (pp. 22–23).

  **Main test and metrics.** Each solver's default with the separator (mcf) is compared with the separator switched off (nomcf) (p. 29).
  - Easy instances are solved by at least one setting, hard instances by neither; the split depends on the solver (p. 29).
  - Every set is reported in all, sep and nosep rows. sep = the separator was on and found at least one violated cut (p. 29).
  - Easy instances: geometric means of time and nodes (no shift stated), arithmetic mean of root gap closed (SCIP), wins (≥ 10 % faster or slower), and limit hits per side (pp. 29–30).
  - Hard instances (SCIP only): closed root, dual and primal gaps and the end gap, with wins at a ≥ 10 % change of the end gap (pp. 31, 33).
  - The CPLEX-internal set adds two subsets, models needing ≥ 10 s and ≥ 100 s with the slower of the two versions (Table 5 continued, p. 33; definition, p. 34).

  **Results.**
  - SCIP time ratios: 0.52 on arc.set, 0.32 on avub, 0.53 on sndlib, 0.52 on ufcn (p. 30). The text says 14 avub, 2 sndlib and 9 ufcn instances are solved within 1 h only with mcf (p. 33). On hard avub instances the mean end gap falls from 83.9 % to 10.2 %, and the root gap closed is 72.5 % against 31.1 % (pp. 33–34).
  - CPLEX time ratios: 0.70, 0.50 and 0.45 on arc.set, avub and sndlib (pp. 32, 34).
  - Losses are reported:
    - fc and fctp lose a little time although nodes drop; the authors say it "does not pay off" (p. 34);
    - CPLEX is slower on ufcn (p. 34);
    - in SCIP, 2 of the 3 affected mittelmann models degrade, and that sep row has a time ratio of 1.83 (pp. 30, 34).
  - General MIP in CPLEX: 129 of 1388 instances (9.3 %) are affected. On these, time drops 17.9 % and nodes 23.6 %; over the whole set the drops are 2 % and 2.8 %. The saving grows with difficulty: 25 % on the 77 affected models in the ≥ 10 s subset and 36 % on the 42 in the ≥ 100 s subset (p. 34). The nosep rows show almost no overhead (p. 34).
  - Sensitivity studies: the two gate thresholds released singly and jointly (Table 6, p. 35); Ψmax swept from 10⁻⁶ to 0.8 on SCIP's easy miplib instances (Fig. 5, p. 36); the cluster count set to 3, 5, 7 and 9, with the mean number of cuts reported (Table 7, p. 37).

  **Not present.**
  - Seeds, permutations or repeated runs.
  - A split of separator time from LP time.
  - The tuning runs behind "extensive computational tests" (p. 29) and the "similar test scenarios" that fixed Ψ(a) (p. 37).
  - Results for the hard CPLEX instances (p. 31).
  - How limit hits enter the means (pp. 29–30).

  **Table checks (own arithmetic).**
  - In Table 5, CPLEX's miplib row says 67 instances, but its sep and nosep rows sum to 66. The text's total of 1388 also implies 66, and the report's table reads 66 (p. 32; p. 34; R013 p. 26).
  - Table 3 lists 3 nomcf limit hits on sndlib, while the text says 2 instances are solved only with mcf (pp. 30, 33). Time and memory limit hits are pooled in that column (p. 29).
  - Transferable technique: report every test set in all, affected and unaffected rows. The unaffected row is the overhead test (pp. 29, 34).
  - Transferable technique: on a large set, add subsets defined by the slower run (≥ 10 s, ≥ 100 s) and read the affected rows per subset. A gain that grows with difficulty is a finding in its own right (pp. 33–34).
  - Transferable technique: split the instances into easy (solved by at least one setting) and hard (solved by neither), and judge the hard ones by closed dual and primal gaps and the end gap rather than by time-outs (pp. 29, 31, 33).
- **D6 Positioning**: The work extends Marchand and Wolsey's c-MIR [39–41] (pp. 2–3). It adapts Bixby and Fourer's row-scanning-addition algorithm for embedded networks [15] (with Brown and Wright [19]) and departs from it in two stated points: no column scaling, and commodities built one by one (pp. 10, 14–15). The cut selection is "very close" to the network-design branch-and-cut literature [13, 14, 30, 45, 49] (p. 23); the shrinking heuristic (Bienstock et al., Günlük) and the connected-shores condition come from there (pp. 25–26). Polyhedral facts are cited, not re-proved, for example Atamtürk's complete description of the cutset polyhedron (p. 8). The only criticism is aimed at the default c-MIR of general solvers, and it is explained rather than attacked (p. 3). The design principle is credited to Bixby and Rothberg (p. 2).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - The contribution comes in the first sentence: a novel separation heuristic for general MIPs, named Mcf, available in SCIP 1.2 and CPLEX 12.1 (p. 2).
  - The theory section derives each known inequality from (6) in a few lines (pp. 7–8).
  - Every implementation constant is printed: 0.02, 0.5, 10 %, one third, 0.8|K|, 20, 1.001, 0.1 and 5 clusters (pp. 11, 14, 17, 19, 24–26). This comes with the disclaimer that the pseudocode is not the code (p. 9).
  - Per-instance tables and the instance list are left to the companion report [2] (pp. 20–21, 29).
  - Hedges sit next to the small counts: "not conclusive" (p. 34) and "should not be overestimated" (p. 35), against "we can trust these values" for the large set (p. 34).
  - The two numbers in the abstract are looser than the body (p. 1):
    - "a factor two on average" is never computed as one number; the per-set time ratios run from 0.32 to 1.08 in SCIP and from 0.45 to 1.05 in CPLEX (pp. 30, 32);
    - "almost 10% of the instances in general testsets contain consistent embedded networks", with its −18 %, is CPLEX's affected count (129 of 1388: separator on and at least one violated cut), not a count of consistent networks (pp. 29, 34).

    That general-MIP sentence is new in the journal abstract. The report's abstract ends with the network-design factor of two (p. 1; R013 p. 2).
  - The conclusion ends with a programme: scan constraint matrices for known structures, so that special-purpose cuts, heuristics and branching rules can be used inside MIP solvers (pp. 38–39).
  - The companion report is cited as "ZIB-Report 07-38" (p. 39), but it is ZR 09-38 (R013 p. 1).
  - Transferable technique: put the hedge in the same paragraph as the result it qualifies, where the affected counts are small (pp. 34–35).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is an algorithm and its implementation in two solvers (p. 2), evaluated on both the target class and general MIP (pp. 33–34).
  - Limits stated by the authors:
    - cut.set is not detected (p. 22);
    - dense flow rows are rejected (p. 22);
    - there is no gain on very easy sets (p. 34);
    - the public general sets are too small to conclude (p. 34);
    - the conservative choice of 5 clusters gives up the gain sndlib gets from 9 (time ratio 0.53 → 0.37), which can be "crucial" for some classes (pp. 37–38);
    - flow covers were not tried in SCIP (p. 28).
  - Visible but not stated as limits: the general-MIP evidence the authors trust is on a private library (p. 34), and the hard CPLEX runs are not reported (p. 31).
  - Future direction: exploit known structures more consistently (pp. 38–39).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D1] Name the precise reason a general routine misses a known cut family (unknown structure, a cap on the aggregation length), and remove that reason (p. 3).
- [D2] Implement structure-specific cuts as an aggregation heuristic for the existing c-MIR: compute the weights and multipliers only (pp. 3, 23).
- [D4] Use a majority vote across commodities and keep the minority votes as an inconsistency ratio that gates the component (pp. 11, 20).
- [D4] Shrink the network with slack-minus-|dual| weights to a few clusters, enumerate all cuts, and keep those with connected shores (pp. 25–26).
- [D4] Find the most violated flow-cutset inequality in linear time by starting from δ−(S) and dropping the arcs with f*_a < r·y*_a (p. 25).
- [D5] Validate a structure detector against ground truth before measuring speed: report detected and consistent counts, the maximum inconsistency, and the mean % deviation in nodes, arcs and commodities (pp. 21–22).
- [D5] Report all, affected and unaffected rows per set, with ≥ 10 s and ≥ 100 s subsets defined by the slower run (pp. 29, 33–34).
- [D5] Split easy and hard instances, and judge the hard part by gap measures (pp. 29, 31, 33).
- [D7] Hedge exactly where the affected counts are small (pp. 34–35).

### R014 · ParaSCIP - a parallel extension of SCIP (ZIB-Report 10-27, Zuse Institute Berlin 2010, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-11921)
**Read level**: full. All 14 file pages were read: the cover sheet (p. 1), the text (pp. 2–12), Tables 1–2 (p. 12), the axis labels and captions of Figs. 2–5 (p. 13) and the references (pp. 13–14). Fig. 1 (p. 6) was read through its caption and labels.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Yuji Shinano (first author), Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch (all ZIB) · **Position in trajectory**: Achterberg is the second of five authors, with an ILOG/IBM affiliation (pp. 1–2); roles are not stated. His documented links to the work are two: he is the original author of SCIP ([7, 8], p. 14), and he co-authored with Shinano the 2008 ParaLEX load-balancing paper whose collecting mode is reused here ([17], pp. 7, 14). The published version (D014) is not in this batch and was not read. The text speaks for all five authors, and nothing in it can be assigned to Achterberg alone.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: There are two entries (p. 3).
  - A stated difficulty: the decisions in branch-and-cut depend on each other, and codes learn from earlier decisions on the assumption of a sequential order. Improvements that shrink the tree also make parallelisation less effective, raise the need for communication and make the run less predictable. Dynamic load balancing is therefore essential.
  - A target: MIPLIB2003 had more than thirty unsolved instances when released, and six remained, unchanged since 2007. Earlier large-scale parallel solvers struggled to keep up with sequential solvers on really hard general instances, and many of the open instances were first solved sequentially.

  The problem: build a distributed-memory parallel SCIP that uses the HLRN II supercomputer to solve two of the remaining open instances (p. 3). The design target is at least 10,000 cores (p. 5).
  - Transferable technique: aim a systems paper at a public list of open instances. A proven optimum is an unambiguous success criterion (pp. 3, 9).
- **D2 Core idea & design**: Parallelise at the subtree level and leave the sequential solver unchanged.
  - Each SOLVER process is a full SCIP that solves the subproblem it receives.
  - A LOADCOORDINATOR presolves the instance once and broadcasts it. After that it sends only bound changes relative to the presolved instance (abstract, p. 2; pp. 5–6).
  - Three design goals are stated: exploit SCIP's complete functionality, keep the interface simple, and scale to at least 10,000 cores (p. 5).
  - Everything new is in the coordination layer: load balancing and checkpointing (pp. 5–9).
  - Transferable technique: send subproblems as lists of bound changes against a presolved instance that is broadcast once; the complete instance is transferred only that one time (p. 6).
- **D3 Problem class & data model** (team.json): General MIP as in Definition 1, with bounds and an integer subset (p. 4). Two test instances (p. 9):
  - **ds**: duty scheduling for a German public transport company, with 67,732 binary duty variables and 656 task constraints;
  - **stp3d**: Steiner tree packing in a three-dimensional grid graph (switchbox routing). It is the largest MIPLIB2003 instance, with 204,880 variables and 159,488 constraints, and even finding a feasible solution is NP-hard.

  No structure is exploited beyond what SCIP itself does (p. 2). Environment: distributed memory. HLRN II cannot swap, so a memory shortage terminates all SOLVERs and forces a restart (pp. 10–11). Correctness is asserted, not argued: changing parameters between jobs affects only the path, not the result (p. 11).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design** (team.json):
  - **Load balancing** (pp. 5–8).
    - Improved primal bounds pass through the coordinator to all SOLVERs, which prune immediately (pp. 6–7).
    - Each SOLVER periodically reports its number of open nodes and its subtree's dual bound. In return it receives BESTDUALBOUND, the best bound among the nodes in the coordinator's pool (p. 7).
    - Idle SOLVERs receive a node from the pool (p. 7).
    - The pool is kept at no fewer than p "good" nodes: nodes whose relative gap to the global dual bound is below THRESHOLD (formula (1)). When fewer are left, the coordinator enters collecting mode and asks selected SOLVERs to collect. It leaves collecting mode when the pool holds more than mp·p good nodes (p. 7).
    - Collecting SOLVERs switch to best-estimate or best-bound node order and alternately solve nodes and send them to the coordinator (p. 7).
    - Ramp-up: all SOLVERs collect until the pool holds more than p nodes (pp. 7–8).
    - A SOLVER whose subtree bound is poor compared with BESTDUALBOUND asks for a new node while it continues, then switches and "delayed" the old one (p. 8).
    - Termination: the pool is empty and all SOLVERs are idle (p. 8).
    - The values of p, mp and THRESHOLD are not given (p. 7).
  - **Checkpointing** (pp. 8–9). Saving all open nodes would cost too much I/O, so only "primitive nodes" are saved: nodes with no ancestor in the coordinator. Recovery then takes longer. On restart the nodes are sent out in dual-bound order, broken down aggressively during ramp-up, and presolved as subproblem roots. Restarting therefore works as an "implicit load balancing mechanism" that breaks down the hardest part of the tree (pp. 8–9).
  - **Extended presolving** (p. 10). Before the parallel run, on one machine: SCIP's aggressive presolving, then strong branching on all variables. A binary is fixed to the opposite value when one of its branch LPs is infeasible. The loop repeats while fixings are found. For stp3d, default presolving leaves 136,500 variables and 97,144 constraints; nine rounds of extended presolving leave 123,637 integer variables and 88,388 constraints (p. 10).
  - No proofs or invariants are given.
  - Transferable technique: checkpoint only the subtree roots held by the coordinator, and accept slower recovery in exchange for far less I/O (p. 8).
  - Transferable technique: before a very long run, repeat aggressive presolving and strong-branching fixings on one machine until nothing more is fixed (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation** (team.json):

  **Setup.** SCIP 1.2.1.2 with CPLEX 12.1 as LP solver (p. 9). Hardware: the MPP2 part of HLRN II, 960 eight-core nodes with Xeon X5570 2.93 GHz processors and 48 GB each; HLRN II was number 64 in the TOP500 of November 2010 (p. 10). Each job used 512 to 2048 cores (Tables 1–2, p. 12).

  **Results.**
  - Proven optima: 93.52 for ds, where the best known bound had been 116.59, and 493.71965 for stp3d, where it had been 500.736 (p. 9).
  - ds needed 17 jobs (16 warm starts) and about 86 h; stp3d needed 11 jobs (10 warm starts) and about 114 h (pp. 10–12).
  - Node counts: 1,174,818,123 for ds and 14,788,888 for stp3d. The authors admit these are underestimates: restarts re-solve parts of the tree, and only the final solve is counted (p. 11).
  - Idle-time ratios: 2.2 % for ds and 3.9 % for stp3d (p. 11).

  **What is reported per job.** Cores, wall time, ramp-up time, the number of SOLVERs that finished at least one sub-MIP, nodes restored and nodes solved (Tables 1–2, p. 12). Figs. 2–5 plot the primal and dual bounds and the workload and pool size (p. 13). The authors read them as typical behaviour: the primal bound reaches the optimum much faster than the dual bound, which stays nearly flat and collapses at the end; nearly all nodes stay "good", and the pool stays filled (p. 11).

  **Not present.**
  - Any baseline: sequential SCIP, fewer cores, or another parallel code.
  - Any speed-up or scaling curve.
  - Repeated runs.
  - Fixed settings: parameters were changed between jobs (p. 11).
  - Any discussion of the jobs stopped before ramp-up, although the tables print them: ds job 13 ran 30,673 s and solved 477 nodes, and stp3d jobs 4–7 ran about 1,800 s each and solved at most 100 nodes (p. 12).

  **Own arithmetic.**
  - The job times sum to about 86.0 h and 114.0 h, and the node columns sum to the stated totals (p. 12).
  - The ds improvement is 19.8 % of the old bound, or 24.7 % of the new optimum; the text gives it as "about 25%" (p. 9).
  - The stp3d improvement is 1.4 %, given as 1 % (p. 9).
  - Transferable technique: for a long checkpointed run, print one row per job (cores, wall time, ramp-up, active solvers, nodes restored, nodes solved), so that restarts and unproductive jobs are visible (p. 12).
- **D6 Positioning**: The report positions itself against earlier large-scale parallel MIP work (Xu et al. [18] for an overview, Ralphs et al. [16] on why it is hard). Most of those approaches struggled to match state-of-the-art sequential solvers on really hard general instances (p. 3). Many open MIPLIB2003 instances were first solved by sequential solvers (Laundy et al. [13], p. 3). The collecting mode is "similar to the one introduced in [17]" (Shinano, Achterberg, Fujie 2008), and the term ramp-up phase is taken from [18] (p. 7). SCIP is described as one of the fastest non-commercial MIP solvers (pp. 2, 4, citing Mittelmann [14]).
- **D7 Writing & rhetoric**:
  - A short technical report in workflow order: initialisation, node transfer, load balancing, checkpointing, then the two instances (pp. 5–11).
  - The headline is the record: two previously unsolved MIPLIB2003 instances, with up to 2048 cores (abstract, p. 2).
  - The argument that ParaSCIP profits from future SCIP progress appears in the abstract and again in the conclusion (pp. 2, 12).
  - The authors are candid about measurement: the node counts are underestimates, and a single-job rerun is planned to get precise node counts and idle ratios (p. 11).
  - The conclusion claims effective use of several thousand cores (p. 12). The support for that claim is the idle-time ratios and the pool behaviour (p. 11). Open questions follow (pp. 12–13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is a system plus a record result (pp. 2, 12).
  - Limits stated: ramp-up and ramp-down time grows with the number of cores and hampers scalability (p. 13); the speed lost compared with finer-grained parallelism is unknown and is to be estimated with a shared-memory version (pp. 12–13); other open instances remain, with unknown computing needs (p. 13).
  - Future directions: predict in advance whether an instance suits massive parallel solving, and predict the remaining running time (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: platform reuse taken to its limit. The whole sequential SCIP is the parallel unit, so the parallel layer inherits future SCIP progress (pp. 2, 5, 12). SOLVERs keep track of idle time and report statistics per sub-MIP (p. 11). But the harness was not held fixed across the study: settings were changed between jobs, and restarts leave the statistics incomplete (p. 11). Co-signed; second of five authors.
- Method 2 ⚠ variant: the run time is split into ramp-up, solving and idle time (Tables 1–2, p. 12; p. 11), and the scaling limit is located in ramp-up and ramp-down (p. 13). There is no payoff measure, though. The claim of effective use (p. 12) rests on the idle-time proxy, with no speed-up and no baseline. The authors name the missing measurements themselves (pp. 11–13).
- H5 ✗ (co-signed; the aim was a record, not a component study): the general claim about ParaSCIP's effectiveness rests on two instances (p. 12).
- H7 ✅ weak (co-signed): the model is reduced before the compute is spent (extended presolving, p. 10); the stated rule is that "all possibilities to reduce the overall running time in advance should be exploited" (p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate: *thin layer over the mature engine* (pp. 2, 12); compare D011 pp. 3, 23.
- New-pattern candidate: *heuristic errors cost time, never validity*. Here it is only asserted: changed settings alter the solver's path, not the result (p. 11). Compare D011 p. 35.

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Broadcast the presolved instance once, then send subproblems as bound-change lists relative to it (p. 6).
- [D4] Keep at least p nodes within a relative dual-bound threshold in the coordinator's pool; when the pool runs low, switch selected solvers to best-bound or best-estimate collecting, and switch back once it holds more than mp·p (p. 7).
- [D4] Let a solver whose subtree bound is poor compared with the pool's best bound request a new node and delay its current one (p. 8).
- [D4] Checkpoint only primitive nodes, and on restart presolve them as subproblem roots; this also re-balances the hardest part of the tree (pp. 8–9).
- [D4] Before a very long run, iterate aggressive presolving and strong-branching fixings on one machine until a fixpoint (p. 10).
- [D5] Print one row per job of a checkpointed run, including unproductive jobs, together with the idle-time ratio (pp. 11–12).
