### R007 · Software for Teaching Modeling of Integer Programming Problems (ZIB-Report 06-23, Zuse Institute Berlin 2006, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-9176)
**Read**: full — all 13 pages, including the solver-session figures (pp. 7–9, 12), Figure 7 (p. 13) and the references (p. 11).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Martin Grötschel, Thorsten Koch (names in alphabetical order; the contact e-mail is Koch's, p. 2; roles not stated) · **Position in trajectory**: ZIB, SCIP 0.81f with SoPlex 1.3.0 in the session logs (p. 8); the tool chain was used in the October 2005 block course of the Matheon project "Combinatorial Optimization at Work" (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Courses treat every problem as "given"; real problem solving iterates modelling, solver run and solution analysis (pp. 2–3). Toy models hide the pitfalls and running-time abnormalities of real sizes (p. 3). Commercial systems are costly, size-limited in student editions, tied to platforms, closed black boxes that cannot be changed, and unfit for teaching scientific method (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Bundle ZIB's modelling language Zimpl (rational arithmetic; about 20 % of AMPL's functionality, used for IPs with more than 30 million nonzeros), the CIP framework SCIP and the LP solver SoPlex into one free chain with source code (pp. 3–5). SCIP's parameters and statistics let a class compare components; VbcTool draws search trees (two MIPLIB instances, same settings, vastly different trees: p. 5; Figure 7, p. 13).
- **D3 Problem class**: MILP models written in Zimpl. Case study: Sudoku as a pure feasibility problem, once with 81 integer variables in {1,…,9} and pairwise |x − y| ≥ 1 (Zimpl's vabs expansion gives 3,969 variables and 4,884 constraints), once with 729 binaries and assignment equations (348 constraints) (pp. 6–7, 10, 12).
- **D4 Implementation design (brief)**: Default run: 66 presolve rounds leave 1,424 variables and 2,291 constraints, and no feasible solution is found in 600 s (p. 6; Figure 3, p. 8). The statistics show most of the time in LP solves; for a feasibility problem the LP gives no bound and its infeasibility detection looks weak (p. 7). CP-like settings (depth-first search, conflict analysis on propagation conflicts, LP solving off) process 10,000 nodes in 7.4 s instead of three minutes and find a solution in 8.2 s and 11,534 nodes, credited "mainly" to conflict analysis (p. 7; Figure 4, p. 9). The binary model is solved by presolve in six rounds (p. 10; Figure 6, p. 12).
- **D5 Evaluation**: none beyond single-instance session logs; one cross-check with CPLEX 9.03 (no feasible solution after six hours and a million nodes, footnote 9, pp. 6–7) and a remark that several thousand Sudoku instances were all solved in presolve or at the root with the binary model (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: software plus a teaching report: a course of over 80 hours with more than 100 participants from 10 countries (p. 4); a Bayreuth contest for the best n-Queens formulation, in which students beat the lecturer's (p. 10). Limits: the tool is "not in all aspects as good as the best" commercial ones (p. 2); the positive feedback is asserted without data (pp. 2, 10). Future: the Matheon digital textbook with data sets and programs (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence, classroom form): find where the time goes from the solver's own statistics (LP relaxations), ask what that component buys for this class (no bound, weak infeasibility detection), then change it (p. 7; Figures 3–4, pp. 8–9). One instance, a teaching demonstration.
- Method 4 ⚠ (variant): three settings are changed at once and the success is credited mainly to conflict analysis without a run that switches it off; only the statistics (conflicts found, Figure 4, p. 9) back the credit (p. 7).
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence, stated, co-signed): results from software one cannot look into do not qualify as a scientific experiment, and source code is needed to look behind the scenes and to change components (p. 3); a negative result is cross-checked with a reference solver (pp. 6–7).
- Also heuristic H7 (model before settings) ✅: the model matters more than the solver implementation, and mathematically equivalent formulations behave totally differently (pp. 7, 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Diagnose a slow MIP from the per-component statistics before tuning: name the component that takes the time, ask what it buys for this model class (for a pure feasibility model, no objective bound), and rerun with it off (p. 7; Figure 3, p. 8).
- Before reporting "no feasible solution", rerun with an independent solver and a much larger budget (CPLEX 9.03, six hours, a million nodes; pp. 6–7).
- Show two equivalent formulations side by side on the same data, with sizes after modelling and after presolve (3,969 × 4,884 → 1,424 × 2,291 against 729 × 348 → 0 × 0; pp. 6, 8, 12).

**Quotes**:
- "Choosing the right model is often more important (and more effective) than having the best solver implementation." (p. 7)
- "Just believing the result of some unknown software without even the possibility to check what actually happened will not qualify for a scientific experiment." (p. 3)

### R008 · Property Checking with Constraint Integer Programming (ZIB-Report 07-37, Zuse Institute Berlin 2007, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-10376)
**Read**: full — all 16 pages, including Tables 1–6 and the references.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Raik Brinkmann (OneSpin Solutions), Markus Wedler (University of Kaiserslautern) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**: ZIB, November 2007, the chip-verification application of the thesis years; SCIP 0.90i with CPLEX 10.0.1 as LP solver (p. 11); the conflict analysis is cited to his Discrete Optimization paper (ref. [1], pp. 7, 15).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: SAT-based bounded model checking fails on data paths with large arithmetic blocks, and random simulation or bit-slicing cannot prove correctness (p. 2). IP and CP solvers do not learn conflict clauses and are weak on control logic; pseudo-Boolean learning does not pay for its overhead; HDPLL uses Fourier–Motzkin elimination only after all binaries are fixed (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: one search tree that combines per-operation domain propagation on bit and word level, an LP relaxation at every node (re-solved by dual simplex; its "global" view detects infeasibility higher in the tree), conflict clauses learned from infeasible LPs and from deductions via reverse propagation, and RTL preprocessing (pp. 2–5, 7–11).
- **D3 Problem class**: property-checking CSP over bit vectors ϱ ∈ {0,…,2^w−1} with bit-linking constraints (1) and 22 RTL operations; branching fixes a bit or sets a bound on a bit vector (p. 4). Pure feasibility, no objective (p. 5). Algorithm 2.1 is stated for zero lower bounds and at most one tightening per variable; the general case relaxes bound changes step by step and splits the reduced costs into r^u − r^l (pp. 8–9).
- **D4 Implementation design (brief)**: one linearization per operation (Table 1, p. 6). Coefficients like 2^w are avoided by 16-bit words with the carry passed to the next word, and nibbles for mult (p. 5). No linearization for shl, slice, read and write: a 64-bit shl would need 30,944 inequalities and 20,929 auxiliary variables (pp. 6–7). Conflict device: the dual ray of the infeasible LP gives the proof 0 ≤ (ȳᵀA + r̄ᵀ)x ≤ ȳᵀb + r̄ᵀũ < 0, and local bounds are relaxed greedily while the infeasibility measure stays negative, since a minimum-cardinality set is NP-hard (pp. 8–9); then 1-FUIP constraints plus reconvergence-cut clauses (p. 9). Preprocessing: probing, term algebra (add and mult only), and irrelevance detection, which also stands in for SAT's conflict-driven choice of branching variables (pp. 9–11).
- **D5 Evaluation**: industrial OneSpin 360 instances (ALU, PipeAdder, PipeMult, Biquad, Multiplier; widths 5–40, Multiplier 6–14), 3.8 GHz Pentium 4, 2 GB, 7,200 s, against MiniSat 2.0 after gate-level preprocessing, chosen as the best of MiniSat 1.14, Siege v4 and zChaff; per-instance times by width; SAT failed on 58 of 258 instances, CIP on none; SAT wins on invalid properties (pp. 11–13).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an algorithmic integration with an industrial test. The loss is printed in the results and the conclusion: inferior to SAT for counter-examples, because SAT processes nodes much faster (pp. 13–14); the abstract claims only the valid-property win (p. 2). Multipliers: SAT is faster on small widths, CIP time grows more slowly, about equal at 10 × 10 (p. 13). Future: more operations and the distributivity law in the term algebra (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): SAT conflict analysis is carried over at the data-structure level. Conflict-graph vertices become bound changes, the cut frontier becomes a disjunction of bounds, each constraint class needs its own reverse propagation, and an infeasible LP is explained by a dual ray instead of a conflicting clause (pp. 7–9).
- Also heuristic H3 (fair yardstick) ✅: the SAT baseline is the best of four named codes (p. 11).
- Also heuristic H10 (print the loss) ⚠: the loss is in the results and the conclusion, while the abstract is scoped to the win (pp. 2, 13–14).
- new-pattern candidate: **Split results by answer type (proof vs witness)**: invalid properties (feasible: a counter-example to find) and valid ones (infeasible: a proof) are tabled apart and won by different methods (pp. 11–13); also R012 (p. 18) and R015 (pp. 6, 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To seed conflict analysis from an infeasible LP: take the dual ray, compute d = ȳᵀb + r̄ᵀû, and reset local bounds to global ones one at a time while d stays negative; the bounds that remain start the conflict graph (Algorithm 2.1, pp. 8–9).
- Keep huge coefficients out of linearizations by splitting a w-bit word into 16-bit words linked by carries (p. 5), and leave a constraint whose linearization would swamp the LP to propagation (pp. 6–7).
- Table time against a size parameter (register width 5–40), with proofs and witnesses apart, so that growth rates and the class where each method wins are visible (Tables 2–6, pp. 11–14).

**Quotes**:
- "In total, SAT failed on 58 out of 258 instances, while CIP was able to solve all instances within the time limit." (p. 13)
- "However, due to the much more involved procedures employed in our algorithm it is inferior to SAT for finding counter-examples of an invalid property." (p. 14)

### R012 · Constraint Integer Programming: Techniques and Applications (ZIB-Report 08-43, Zuse Institute Berlin 2008, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-10950)
**Read**: full — all 24 pages, including Tables 1–7, Figure 1 and the references.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Timo Berthold, Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch, Kati Wolter · **Position in trajectory**: October 2008, with Achterberg already at ILOG Deutschland (p. 1); SCIP 1.1.0; an extended version of the CPAIOR 2008 paper (p. 2; ref. [3], p. 20).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: most CP–MIP integrations extend a CP framework by basic MIP techniques; this one brings CP's solving methods and modelling power into the full MIP machinery (p. 2), with the LP relaxation as a second communication channel besides the domains (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: restrict CP only as far as needed to keep all primal and dual MIP techniques: a CIP is a CP whose remaining problem after fixing the integer variables is an LP (condition (1)) (pp. 3, 5). SCIP implements CP, SAT and MIP components as plugins on one tree (pp. 3, 6–7).
- **D3 Problem class**: CIP, with MIP and CP(FD) as special cases (Proposition, p. 5); counting the optimal solutions of the 30 MIPLIB instances with only integer variables, after adding c^T x = c* (p. 14); RTL property checking on bit vectors (pp. 15–16).
- **D4 Design (brief)**: the Proposition is proved by giving (A′, b′): an auxiliary objective variable z for CP(FD); A′ = A_C and b′ = b − A_I x̂_I for MIP (pp. 5–6). Components: constraint handlers (p. 7); objective propagation and root reduced-cost strengthening (p. 8); conflict analysis from a greedy dual-ray start (p. 8); cut selection by efficacy, orthogonality and objective parallelism (p. 9); 24 primal heuristics in four classes (p. 9); reliability branching and interleaved best-bound/best-estimate search with plunging (p. 10); presolving plus a root restart when at least 5 % of the integer variables got fixed (pp. 10–11); symmetry handling still being implemented (p. 11). Counting uses unrestricted-subtree detection, with dual presolve and heuristics off so that no solution is cut off or counted twice (p. 14).
- **D5 Evaluation**: MIPLIB 2003 minus three instances with a wrong answer or error; SCIP 1.1.0 with CLP, CPLEX or SoPlex LPs against CPLEX 11.0 and CBC 2.20, all in default settings, 1 h, 3 GB, Core 2 Quad 2.66 GHz; geometric means over the 27 instances all solvers solved, solved counts, and ≥ 10 % faster/slower counts against SCIP/CLP (pp. 11–14). Counting against azove, CPLEX (one thread, integrality tolerance 0), LattE and zerOne: 28, 26 (CPLEX), 24 (zerOne), 12 (azove 1.1), 2 (LattE) solved (pp. 14–15). Chip verification: valid properties against MiniSat 2.0 (pp. 16–18).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: concept, framework and three applications. Limits in the numbers: SCIP/CLP is 3.5 times and SCIP with CPLEX LPs 1.84 times slower than CPLEX in geometric mean, with 29 and 34 instances solved against CPLEX's 32 (pp. 12, 14); invalid properties are not tabled, and there CIP "usually is inferior" to SAT (p. 18). Future: nonconvex MINLP with LaGO, exact CIP with infeasibility certificates, scheduling (pp. 19–20).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence): the comparison averages only over instances every code solved, reports solved counts and 10 % win/loss counts, and drops and names the instances with a wrong answer (pp. 12–14): the `cmpres.awk` practice of Method 5 and the Workflow C exclusion rule, in print.
- Method 4 ⚠ (variant): the thesis-era counting form (≥ 10 % faster/slower against one reference), but in a solver comparison rather than a knock-out, and with unshifted geometric means (pp. 12–14).
- Method 6 ✅ (evidence): SAT restarts are imported in a restricted MIP form: once, right after the root, when at least 5 % of the integer variables got fixed, because root fixings can trigger more presolving while the cost is a second root LP (p. 11). Conflict analysis is generalised to bound changes and LP infeasibility (p. 8). The novelty claim that restarts were not used for MIPs so far is unhedged (p. 11); whether it held in 2008 was not checked here (step 6 is his self-declared weak step).
- Also heuristic H3 ✅: correctness settings on both sides of the counting test (pp. 14–15). Misreading 5: SCIP is called "almost competitive" (abstract, p. 2) and "competitive" (pp. 14, 20) beside the 3.5× and 1.84× ratios (p. 14).
- new-pattern candidate: **Split results by answer type (proof vs witness)**: only valid properties are tabled, and counter-example search is named as the losing case (p. 18).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a multi-solver table, take geometric means only over instances that all codes solved, add solved counts and ≥ 10 % faster/slower counts against one reference, and name the instances dropped for wrong answers (pp. 12–14).
- For solution counting, switch off every component that may cut off feasible suboptimal solutions or find a solution twice (dual presolve, primal heuristics), and run the reference code single-threaded with integrality tolerance 0 (pp. 14–15).
- Define a hybrid problem class by what remains after fixing the integer variables, and prove that it generalises its parent classes by constructing the residual LP for each (pp. 5–6).

**Quotes**:
- "We left out the instances momentum3, t1717, and timtab1 for which at least one of the solvers returned a wrong answer or reported an error." (p. 13)
- "In our implementation, a restart is performed if at least 5 % of the integer variables have been fixed." (p. 11)

### R013 · The MCF-Separator -- Detecting and Exploiting Multi-Commodity Flow Structures in MIPs (ZIB-Report 09-38, Zuse Institute Berlin 2009, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-11592; journal version D011)
**Read**: full — the main text pp. 1–30 with Tables 1–7, Figures 1–6 and Algorithms 1–5, and the references (pp. 30–33); Appendices A–C (per-instance Tables 8–12, pp. 33–61) seen only as captions.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Christian Raack (ZIB) · **Position in trajectory**: December 2009, Achterberg at IBM (p. 2). The separator ships in SCIP 1.2 and CPLEX 12.1 (p. 2): CPLEX-era work, for which the skill otherwise has little first-hand method evidence.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: cut-based MIR inequalities are known to work for network design, yet general solvers rarely find them. The network is unknown to the solver, and the aggregation needs far more rows than the c-MIR aggregation limit (7 in SCIP, similar in CPLEX), a limit that sits in the exponent of the running time (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: resolve the coupled multi-commodity structure backwards from the matrix: flow detection by a modified row-scanning-addition algorithm, arc detection through capacity rows, node detection by comparing arc patterns, and network construction by majority vote. The separator then hands c-MIR weight vectors u that aggregate the flow rows of a node set S and the capacity rows of its cut; it "only calculates weight vectors u" (pp. 3, 8–10, 19–21). It separates only if the network inconsistency Ψ(G) ≤ 0.02, and drops arcs with Ψ(a) > 0.5 (p. 10).
- **D3 Problem class**: arc-flow model (2a)–(2d) with integer capacity modules (pp. 4–5). Variants handled: multi-facility, unsplittable flow, undirected capacities, with a `modeltype` parameter detected automatically by default (pp. 22–23). The detection tolerates user presolving (a different node dropped per commodity) and solver presolving (pp. 10–11); flow rows are capped at 10 % density (p. 12).
- **D4 Design (brief)**: validity needs no per-cut proof, since aggregating original rows and applying MIR is always valid; inconsistency only lowers the chance of useful, stable cuts (pp. 3, 27). The base inequality (6) yields flow-cutset (7), cutset (8), dicut and flow-cover inequalities (pp. 6–7). Algorithm 5: tight rows only, at most 20 multipliers, then a linear-time most-violated subset A− (pp. 20–21). Cut collection: all singletons plus shrinking with weights s*_a − |π*_a| down to Ω = 5 clusters, connected shores only (p. 21). The detector is first checked against known networks, with solver presolve off and on (Table 2, pp. 17–19).
- **D5 Evaluation**: SCIP 1.1.0.8 (CPLEX 11.2.1 LPs) and CPLEX 12.1, one thread, 1 h, 6 GB; seven network-design sets, miplib (92), mittelmann (59) and the CPLEX-internal set of 1,266 easy models; mcf against nomcf with all/sep/nosep rows, geometric means of time and nodes, ≥ 10 % wins, time-outs per side, root gap closed, and for CPLEX subsets of ≥ 10 s and ≥ 100 s by the slower run; sensitivity of Ψmax, Ψmax_a and Ω (pp. 18, 23–29).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: on arc.set, avub, sndlib and ufcn in SCIP, 47–68 % less time and 56–67 % fewer nodes, and 25 instances solved within 1 h only with the separator (pp. 25–26); in CPLEX, 30–55 % less time on three sets (p. 27); of 1,388 general MIPs, 129 (9.3 %) are affected, −17.9 % time on them, −2 % overall, −36 % on the 42 affected models of ≥ 100 s (p. 27). Limits printed: cut.set networks not detected (p. 19), fctp flow rows too dense (p. 18), very easy fc/fctp slightly slower (p. 26), ufcn slower in CPLEX (p. 27), the SCIP general-MIP results "not conclusive" (p. 27). Future: scan matrices for known structures as a new paradigm (p. 30).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 ✅ (evidence; the canonical case): the Bixby–Rothberg remark is quoted as the design rule (p. 2); no cuts on inconsistent networks (pp. 2, 10); the nosep rows show almost no slowdown in either solver (p. 27); the looser gate that helped only SCIP was not adopted, and both refusal mechanisms stay on (p. 29); Ω stays at the conservative 5 although sndlib gains from 9 (p. 29). The skill's citations of this report (pp. 1–3, 16–17, 22, 27–29) are printed pages: here pp. 2–4, 17–18, 23, 28–30.
- Method 4 ✅ (evidence, steps 4–5): affected (sep) and unaffected (nosep) rows per set, ≥ 10 % wins and time-outs per side (pp. 24–25); conclusions refused on 16 + 3 affected general-MIP models and accepted on 129 of 1,388 (p. 27).
- Method 4 ✗ (contradiction, era note): Method 4 dates the bracket form to his papers from 2014. This 2009 report already reads the CPLEX results in subsets of ≥ 10 s and ≥ 100 s by the slower of the two runs, each with affected rows, wins, time-outs and geometric-mean ratios of time and nodes (Table 5, p. 26; p. 27). The shift is not stated, and there are no 1 s or 1,000 s brackets. Co-signed; who ran the CPLEX tests is not stated (*inferred*: Achterberg, at IBM, p. 2).
- Method 2 ✅ (evidence): the closed root gap is shown beside time, and fewer nodes with more time is judged as not paying (fc, fctp: tightening the relaxation "does not pay off", p. 26); Ω is capped because cut generation and LP time grow, with the number of cuts tabled per setting (p. 29).
- Method 3 ⚠ (variant): one-parameter sweeps of the gate (Ψmax from 10⁻⁶ to 0.8, Figure 6, pp. 28–29) and of the cut budget (Ω = 3…9, Table 7, p. 29), but no incumbent rule is nested in the family, and the default is chosen conservatively, not at a knee (p. 29).
- new-pattern candidate: **Validate the detector against ground truth**: on sets whose original networks are known, compare the detected nodes, arcs and commodities with the originals, with solver presolve off and on, before measuring the separator (pp. 18–19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Gate a structure-exploiting component on a measured inconsistency of the detected structure (minority votes per arc over the commodities, averaged over arcs): release everything above the threshold, and drop the worst elements below it (pp. 10, 16–17).
- Test the detector on its own first: on instances with a known structure, report detected-versus-true sizes with solver presolve off and on (Table 2, pp. 18–19).
- Report every test set in three rows (all, affected = sep, unaffected = nosep), so that the gain where the component fires and its cost where it does not are both visible (pp. 24–27).
- Implement a new cut family as a new aggregation for an existing separator (weight vectors u for c-MIR), so that validity is inherited (pp. 3, 19, 27).

**Quotes**:
- "The subsets cplex10s and cplex100s correspond to those instances within cplex that need at least 10s and 100s of CPU time to be solved, respectively, by the slower of the two versions, mcf and nomcf." (p. 27)
- "The results for the general MIP sets miplib and mittelmann are not conclusive since the number of affected instances is very small" (p. 27)

### R015 · MIPLIB 2010 (ZIB-Report 10-31, Zuse Institute Berlin 2010, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-12953; journal version D013)
**Read**: full — the main text pp. 3–22 (title page, Sections 1–8 with Tables 1–4 and the figure captions, acknowledgments); references (pp. 22–26) not read; the catalogue and result Tables 5–8 (pp. 27–47) seen only as captions and first rows.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: first author Thorsten Koch, with Erling Andersen, Oliver Bastert, Timo Berthold, Robert E. Bixby, Emilie Danna, Gerald Gamrath, Ambros M. Gleixner, Stefan Heinz, Andrea Lodi, Hans Mittelmann, Ted Ralphs, Domenico Salvagnin, Daniel E. Steffy and Kati Wolter; Achterberg is second author, at IBM Deutschland (pp. 1, 3), and no section is credited to him · **Position in trajectory**: the community successor of his MIPLIB 2003 with Koch and Martin (p. 4); numbered ZR 10-31, but the solver snapshot is "as of March 2011" (p. 19).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: MIPLIB 2003 was outgrown: 22 easy, 3 hard and 35 open instances at its start, 41 easy, 15 hard and 4 open by the end of 2010 (pp. 4–5); its 60 instances made topic-specific subsets too small (p. 5); codes get tuned on models already known to be solvable, so out-of-scope instances were added (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: several dedicated test sets instead of one (benchmark 87, infeasible 20, primal 40, xxl 11, reoptimize 66, tree 52, unstable 21, challenge 164; 361 instances: 185 easy, 42 hard, 134 open), shipped with a test engine and an exact-arithmetic solution checker, plus a measure of performance variability (pp. 3, 5–6, 8–15).
- **D3 Data model and selection**: about 2,000 candidates (over 1,108 instances from 57 contributors, plus NEOS and public sets). Duplicates were removed, homogeneous families cut to fewer than ten, and instances solved by SCIP or CBC in under a minute dropped; the remaining 659 were examined by eight teams; a proposal of 100 plus 45 substitutes became 87 benchmark instances (p. 6–7). Each benchmark instance had to be solvable by at least one solver (within 24 h at the proposal stage, p. 7; within two hours in Table 1, p. 6), and every other set except unstable and challenge had to be represented; exchanges were made for class balance, difficulty and numerics, favouring new contributions and stable performance and disfavouring unknown applications (p. 7). The other sets follow stated rules: reoptimize ≥ 500 simplex iterations per node outside the root and ≥ 100 nodes; tree ≥ 1,000,000 nodes; xxl > 10⁶ variables or constraints or > 10⁷ nonzeros, and not reducible by presolve; membership was double-checked on different codes and machines (pp. 7–8).
- **D4 Design (brief)**: the checker reads the MPS text in GMP arithmetic and checks the returned solution, not optimality or infeasibility, with absolute tolerance 10⁻⁴ for rows and integrality, and the objective within 10⁻⁴ absolute or 10⁻⁷ relative (pp. 8–10). The engine runs solvers deterministically with gap cutoff 0, measures wall-clock time including parsing, rounded up to the second, defines gap = (pb − db)/inf{|z| : z ∈ [db, pb]}, and compares with known primal bounds only after the check (status mismatch) (p. 11). Reliable primal bounds come from rounding the integers and solving the remaining LP exactly with QSopt_ex (pp. 11, 17–18). Variability: 100 row/column permutations per model, score VS = coefficient of variation of time (pp. 13–14). Numerical attention level from CPLEX's condition-number statistics, AL = p_ill-posed + 0.3 p_unstable + 0.01 p_suspicious (p. 18).
- **D5 Evaluation**: benchmark set, one and twelve threads, 1 h, 8/24 GB, two Xeon X5680 (hyperthreading and turbo off); CBC 2.6.4, CPLEX 12.2.0.2, Gurobi 4.5 beta, SCIP/SoPlex 2.0.1.3/1.5.0.3 and Xpress 7.2 RC in default settings (p. 20); a "Best" column and an explicit refusal to rank by average time (p. 21); variability with SCIP/SoPlex on 67 models (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: library, scripts, checker and variability study. Findings: the max/min time ratio over permutations ranges from 1.29 to 915; an oracle picking the best permutation saves a factor 1.77, the worst costs 3.82; the original order beats the permutation mean by 14 % but the median by only 4 % (p. 14); for roll3000, one more thread made 14 of 31 steps faster and 17 slower (p. 15); with one thread only four benchmark instances are unsolved by all, each solver is fastest somewhere, and the Best geometric mean is below half of any solver's (p. 21). **Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ (evidence, co-signed, at library level): test lists per set, a file of known primal bounds for the benchmark set, a time-limited run driver, a solution checker and parse scripts producing a summary table, with run/parse templates per solver (pp. 10–12); set membership double-checked on different machines and codes (p. 7). ⚠ The benchmark set itself was chosen "dynamically", by consensus under hard and soft requirements, and only the other sets by predefined rules (p. 7), unlike the thesis's rule-only selection.
- Method 4 ⚠ (variant; bears on Misreading 1): this co-signed report runs 100 permutations per model, treats performance as a random variable, and asks for large samples, permutations, robust indicators and inferential statistics (pp. 13–15). Misreading 1 and step 7 say his written studies have no seeds or permutations; this library paper (not a component study) does. No section is credited to him, and the acknowledgments credit Danna's 2008–2010 variability work (p. 22).
- new-pattern candidate: **Refuse a single-number solver ranking**: no comparison by average time, with reasons: variability (a 14 % faster machine sped up the geometric means by 8.5 % to nearly 20 %; twelve-thread reruns differed by up to 73 s), arbitrary choices of time limit, threads and machine, every solver fastest somewhere, default settings only, and many possible measures (p. 21); result tables should not be boiled down to a single number (p. 22).
- new-pattern candidate: **Split results by answer type (proof vs witness)**: dedicated infeasible and primal sets, because solvers are tuned for optimization, not for feasibility, and to the authors' knowledge not at all for infeasible problems (pp. 6, 8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Define each special test set by a rule on observed solver behaviour (simplex iterations per node, tree size, size after presolve), and confirm membership with more than one code and machine (pp. 7–8).
- Check the returned solution against the original text file in exact arithmetic with stated absolute tolerances, and compare its objective with known bounds only after it passes (pp. 10–11).
- Generate variability by permuting rows and columns, summarise it per instance by the coefficient of variation of time, and compare the original order with the median as well as the mean (pp. 13–14).
- Add a virtual "Best" solver (the fastest accepted run per instance) to show how far any single code is from what is possible (p. 21).
