### D023 · Efficient separation of RLT cuts for implicit and explicit bilinear terms (Mathematical Programming (Series B) 2025, doi:10.1007/s10107-024-02104-0)
**Read level**: full. All 28 file pages of arXiv:2211.13545v3, the extended version that became *Math. Program.* Ser. B 210(1–2):47–74: abstract and §§1–6 (pp. 1–22) read completely, Declarations (pp. 22–23) read; references (pp. 23–25) skimmed for positioning and self-citations; Appendix A, Tables A1–A2 (pp. 26–28), read row by row (for the permutation suffixes, the instance groups named on p. 16, and this card's own counts, which are labelled as such).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Ksenia Bestuzheva (first and corresponding author, ZIB), Ambros M. Gleixner (ZIB and HTW Berlin); Achterberg last, Gurobi GmbH (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: Gurobi era (skill, Research Trajectory, 2014–). Joint work with ZIB within the Research Campus MODAL (BMBF grants 05M14ZAM, 05M20ZBM; p. 22), tested by independent implementations in SCIP and in Gurobi 10.0 beta, which the paper calls a mixed-integer quadratically-constrained programming solver (abstract, p. 1; §5.7, p. 21). The stored product terms reuse his thesis concept of up- and downlocks (p. 8, ref. [24]).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: RLT relaxations are strong but come with very many cuts; earlier remedies restrict which RLT cuts are added (Sherali and Tuncbilek's selection by coefficient signs), use reduced RLT for polynomial problems with linear equations, or linearize binary quadratic problems compactly or inductively (Liberti, Mallach) (p. 2). For bilinear terms the remaining cost is twofold: separation time and the size of the resulting LP relaxations (p. 2). The paper enters on both sides. Applicability: a product of a binary and a bounded variable is equivalent to two linear constraints, so an MILP may already contain product relations, while earlier MILP uses of RLT only create products by multiplying constraints (p. 2). Cost: separating all RLT cuts is named the main obstacle to using RLT in practice, and the second contribution is a separator that avoids it (p. 3). The stated goal is wider applicability and better performance of bilinear RLT (abstract, p. 1); a stronger relaxation is not sought.
  - Transferable: make the cost of a known strong technique the research problem. Measure the separator's share of total time first (here 54.6 % on average over the MILP runs with standard separation, Table 6, p. 18), then attack that share (pp. 3, 17–18).
  - Transferable: look for a relation that a standard modelling linearization has hidden (a binary times a bounded variable written as the big-M pair (4)–(5), p. 5) and give it back to the solver, so that another family of cuts applies (pp. 2, 5).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Two ideas. (a) Implicit products. Theorem 1: two linear constraints a1 x_i + b1 w_ij + c1 x_j ≤ d1 and a2 x_i + b2 w_ij + c2 x_j ≤ d2 on the same three variables, x_i binary, with b1 b2 > 0 and γ = c2 b1 − b2 c1 ≠ 0, imply A x_i + B w_ij + C x_j + D ≤ x_i x_j if b1 γ > 0 and ≥ if b1 γ < 0, with A = (b2(a1 − d1) + b1 d2)/γ, B = b1 b2/γ, C = b1 c2/γ, D = −b1 d2/γ (pp. 5–7). In separation the linear left-hand side takes the place of w_ij (p. 7). The implementation always requires a1 or a2 to be nonzero and, through a parameter that is on by default, opposite signs on x_i, to keep only the stronger implications (p. 7). Detection pairs implied relations on variable triples or implied bounds with a complementary implied relation, implied bound, clique, two-variable constraint or global bound (pp. 7–8); a redundancy analysis keeps only products whose nonredundancy interval in x_j meets the domain of x_j (pp. 8–9). (b) Cheaper separation. The baseline multiplies every linear row by both bound factors of every product variable (p. 9). Row marking reads the LP point: substituting w_ij for x_i x_j raises a cut's violation only if a′_ri (w*_ij − x*_i x*_j) ≥ 0, where a′_ri is the row coefficient with the sign set by the bound factor (p. 10), so each row is marked with the bound factor or factors that can give a violated cut (MARK_LT, MARK_GT, MARK_BOTH; equations are multiplied by x_i itself; Algorithm 1, pp. 10–11). Projection filtering first checks the cut built from the row projected onto the variables strictly between their bounds and builds the full cut only if that one is violated (pp. 11–12). Unchanged from standard RLT: the cut form (one bound factor at a time, McCormick for unknown products, p. 4) and the omission of constraint factors (p. 5). The reason given for the change is that cost, not strength, limits RLT in practice (pp. 3, 22).
  - Transferable: before building any cut, derive from the current LP point a cheap necessary condition for violation (here a sign test per row and bound factor) and skip every candidate that fails it (pp. 10–11).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Mixed-binary nonlinear programs in an extended form, with an auxiliary variable w_ij for each bilinear term and relations x_i x_j ≤, ≥ or = w_ij, linear rows Ax ≤ b, nonlinear constraints g(x, w) ≤ 0 and possibly infinite bounds ((1a)–(1f), p. 3); general integers are treated like continuous variables and left out of the presentation (p. 3). Bound factors need finite bounds (p. 4); the big-M form and Theorem 1 need x_i binary and finite bounds on x_j (p. 5). Structures used: implied bounds stored as sorted arrays, implied three-variable relations in a hashtable keyed by the variable triple, cliques in a clique table, equality rows (pp. 5, 7). MILPs carry no explicit products, so every product in them comes from detection (p. 14). Correctness conditions: those of Theorem 1 are sufficient (p. 7); projection filtering is exact only when all McCormick inequalities hold at the LP point, which SCIP's dynamic separation of McCormick cuts does not guarantee (p. 11). Libraries: MINLPLib (1846 instances) and MIPLIB 3, 2003, 2010 and 2017 with Cor@l (666 MILPs), reduced to the instances with explicit or derived products, 1357 MINLPs and 195 MILPs (p. 13). Gurobi: an internal set of customer and public models, chosen so that no problem class is over-represented, with 5011 MILPs and 806 MINLPs (pp. 21–22).
  - Transferable: implement stronger-than-necessary validity conditions as parameters with a stated default (here opposite signs on the binary, on by default), so that each filter can later be knocked out and measured (p. 7).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Proof and invariant devices: (i) Theorem 1 by undetermined coefficients: the unknown relation and both constraints are written as implications for x_i = 1 and x_i = 0, the constraints are scaled by α and β, coefficients are matched, giving α = b2/γ, β = b1/γ and the sign condition αβ > 0 (pp. 6–7); (ii) a nonredundancy interval, found by solving each constraint for w_ij and comparing it term by term with the product relation, for b1, b2 > 0 and for b1, b2 < 0 (pp. 8–9); (iii) a violation-sign test for substitutions, which gives the bound-factor table behind row marking (p. 10); (iv) McCormick tightness at a bound (four cases: two of (12a)–(12d) together equal the product relation once a variable sits at a bound), from which a projected RLT cut is violated if and only if the full cut is, provided the McCormick inequalities hold; otherwise violations of products with an at-bound variable are ignored and McCormick cuts get priority (pp. 12–13). Linearization rules per product term: w_kj when the relation direction and the coefficient sign agree, x_j² = x_j for binaries, a secant or tangent for continuous squares, clique-implied products for binary pairs, McCormick otherwise (p. 4). Data structures: all products in one record with the product type, up- and downlocks (his thesis, ref. [24]) and linked variables and expressions (p. 8); sparse arrays of product partners, a hashtable from variable pairs to products, sorted row indices with marks as bit flags (p. 10). Cut management: RLT cuts first compete among themselves in an extra selection by efficacy and parallelism, as in SCIP's default selection, before meeting other cuts (p. 14); the paper distinguishes the node's cut storage from the cut pool (p. 9). Effort limits: at most 20 unknown bilinear terms in a row–factor product, separation every 10 nodes, 1 round at non-root nodes and 10 at the root (p. 13). An untested shortcut: when the first relation is an implied bound, only one arbitrarily chosen partner variable is considered (p. 8). Numerical safeguards (violation tolerances, coefficient scaling) and complexity bounds are not given; numerics appear only as a cause of failures (p. 14).
  - Transferable: derive implied relations with undetermined coefficients: write the target relation and each source constraint as a pair of implications on the binary, scale, match coefficients and read off the validity conditions (pp. 6–7).
  - Transferable: prove when a filter is exact (here: if all McCormick inequalities hold), then state what it does in your solver when the condition fails (pp. 11–13).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Implicit-product detection: for two linear constraints on (x_i binary, w_ij, x_j) with b1 b2 > 0 and γ = c2 b1 − b2 c1 ≠ 0, add A x_i + B w_ij + C x_j + D ≤ x_i x_j (b1 γ > 0) or ≥ (b1 γ < 0) with A = (b2(a1 − d1) + b1 d2)/γ, B = b1 b2/γ, C = b1 c2/γ, D = −b1 d2/γ; take candidate pairs from a hashtable of variable triples, sorted implied-bound arrays and the clique table (pp. 5–8).
- Row marking for RLT separation: for each product violated at the LP point, mark every row containing x_j by the sign of a_rj (w*_ij − x*_i x*_j); with MARK_LT multiply ≤ rows by the lower and ≥ rows by the upper bound factor, with MARK_GT the reverse, with MARK_BOTH both, and equations by x_i (Algorithm 1, pp. 10–11).
- Projection filtering: check the RLT cut of the row projected onto the variables strictly inside their bounds, build the full cut only if that one is violated; exact when all McCormick inequalities hold, otherwise it gives McCormick cuts priority (pp. 11–13).
- Redundancy filter for derived relations: keep a product only if the interval of x_j where it is nonredundant meets the domain of x_j; a stricter variant demands that the interval cover at least 0.3 of the domain (pp. 8–9, 18).
- Effort limits in solver units for an expensive separator: at most 20 unknown product terms per row–factor product, a call every 10 nodes, 1 round per node and 10 at the root, plus a family-internal cut selection before general cut selection (pp. 13–14).
- Subset definitions for knock-out tables: Clean = no failure in any setting; Affected = LP-iteration count differs; [x,timelim] = at least x s with one setting and solved by one; All-optimal = solved by both; shifted geometric means with shifts 1 s and 100 nodes (p. 14).
- A separation-time table: mean and maximum share of total time and counts in < 5 %, 5–20 %, 20–50 % and 50–100 % per setting, failures counted, runs of at most 0.1 s dropped (Table 6, pp. 17–18).
- A root dual-bound table: relative difference (γ2 − γ1)/γ1 in the ranges 0.01–0.2, 0.2–0.5, 0.5–1.0 and > 1.0, counted for each side and printed beside the time tables (Table 4, pp. 15–16).
- Check a node increase that follows a time saving on the All-optimal subset (Table 5, p. 17).
- Test why a filter is neutral with a control run that forces the suspected interaction (all McCormick inequalities in the LP) (p. 20).
- A size-group table: geometric mean time by number of nonzeros (0–10 up to 100000–1000000) for each knock-out, to show where each feature pays (Table 11, p. 21).
- Replicate in a second, independently implemented solver with the same algorithms, and compare directions and brackets rather than absolute numbers (pp. 21–22).
