### R010 · Counting solutions of integer programs using unrestricted subtree detection (ZIB-Report 08-09 2008, urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-10632)
**Read level**: full. All 6 file pages; the reference list (p. 6) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Stefan Heinz, Thorsten Koch (roles not stated) · **Position in trajectory**: February 2008 (p. 1); Achterberg's affiliation is ILOG Deutschland, the coauthors' ZIB (p. 2). It continues the chip-verification application with OneSpin instances (p. 5), now for counting rather than optimising.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: Applications in hardware and software verification and in discrete geometry need all feasible solutions, and industry wants multiple or all optimal ones (abstract and introduction, p. 2). Existing counters recast the problem in binary form and use SAT, BDD or 0/1-enumeration codes (p. 2), and few methods work on the integer space of a general IP (p. 3). The task is stated as computing the finite set X_IP of all feasible points of a bounded pure IP (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Keep LP-based branch-and-cut and traverse the whole tree (p. 3), but add a second kind of node that can be closed: an unrestricted subtree, in which every assignment within the local domains is feasible. It is recognised when every constraint is locally redundant at the node (p. 4).
  - Changed: a redundancy check at every node, because branch-and-cut finds solutions without checking constraints for redundancy (p. 4).
  - Kept: infeasible-subtree pruning, which conflict analysis strengthens (p. 4).
  - All optimal solutions come either by filtering X_IP or by solving once and adding the cutoff c^T x ≤ c* before enumerating (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: min{c^T x | Ax ≤ b, l ≤ x ≤ u, x ∈ Z^n} with all variables bounded and integer (p. 3). Data: eight chip-verification instances from OneSpin Solutions with 100,980 to more than 68,749 M solutions (Table 1, p. 5), and the MIPLIB instances without continuous variables (p. 6). The correctness argument is Lemma 1 (p. 4).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: A definition (a constraint is locally redundant if all assignments in the local domains satisfy it) and Lemma 1 (the subtree at node S is unrestricted iff all constraints are locally redundant at S), proved in both directions from an arbitrary point of the local box (p. 4). Example 1 with Fig. 1 shows that the branching variable decides whether an unrestricted and an infeasible subtree appear at the root (pp. 4–5); no branching rule for counting is proposed. How the solutions of a detected subtree are counted or stored is not described (not stated). Implemented in SCIP 1.00.5 with SoPlex 1.3.3 (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Basic enumeration against unrestricted subtree detection on the eight instances, with columns |X_IP|, time, nodes, maximum depth and the number of unrestricted subtrees found (Table 1, p. 5); Core 2 Quad 2.66 GHz, 4 GB, 30-minute limit (p. 5).
  - On the six instances both versions finish, nodes and time fall, e.g. veri1 from 1,618,847 nodes and 12.9 s to 17,639 nodes and 0.3 s, veri6 from 201,959 nodes to 2,087 (Table 1, p. 5).
  - veri8 is solved only with detection (77.1 s against more than 1,800 s); veri7 is solved by neither (Table 1, p. 5).
  - My arithmetic on Table 1: the basic node count is close to 2|X_IP| on veri1–veri6, exactly 2|X_IP| − 1 on veri1 and veri6, so the basic tree has roughly one leaf per solution, which is what detection removes.
  - MIPLIB: all optimal solutions within 5 minutes except cracpb1 and p2756; p0548 finishes only with detection (p. 6).
  - The comparison with azove, CPLEX, LattE and zerOne is given in words only: clear dominance on the chip instances, parity with the branch-and-cut codes on MIPLIB (p. 6); no table.
  - Single runs; the cost of the redundancy check is not split out (pp. 5–6).
- **D6 Positioning**: The report places itself between SAT and BDD counters (sharpSAT, azove, zerOne, Morgado et al.), branch-and-cut solution generation (Danna et al., available in CPLEX, which enumerates all feasible solutions with a zero objective) and Barvinok's lattice-point counting (pp. 2–3). It credits Morgado et al. with the same observation for binary programs, noting that their search needs blocking clauses to avoid counting solutions twice (p. 4). For infeasible subtrees it cites Sandholm and Shields beside his own conflict analysis (p. 4).
- **D7 Writing**: Five printed pages: an abstract with a we-propose contribution sentence (p. 2), one definition, one lemma, one toy example with a figure (pp. 4–5) and one table (p. 5). Results are restricted to eight instances for lack of space (p. 5); dominance over four other solvers is claimed without numbers (p. 6); there is no conclusion section (p. 6).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic (a pruning rule with its lemma, p. 4) and software (inside SCIP, p. 5). No limitation, open question or future work is stated (the text ends with the solver comparison, p. 6).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 (evidence ✅, partial; pp. 5–6): total time is printed beside nodes and depth, and the component's output is counted (the unrestricted-subtree column, as in Method 2 step 3). There is no split of the detection cost and no budget.
- Method 6 (variant ⚠; p. 4): the idea parallels Morgado et al.'s counting in integer domains from the SAT side. It is re-derived for branch-and-cut, where the redundancy test must be explicit at every node instead of following from fixings plus blocking clauses; nothing is moved one to one.
- Also seen, among the heuristics: Heuristics 3 and 10 are not shown here. The comparison with four solvers is asserted without numbers, and no loss or open question is printed (p. 6). This reads as a short-report exception rather than evidence against them.
- New-pattern candidate "mirror pruning for a new task" (pp. 4–5): to make an optimiser count, pair its existing pruning rule (infeasible subtrees) with the counterpart that the new task needs (subtrees that are all solutions).

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D2/D4) To make a branch-and-cut code count or enumerate, add the counterpart of infeasibility pruning. At each node, test whether every constraint holds for every point of the local box; if so, the whole subtree consists of solutions and can be closed in one step (Lemma 1, p. 4).
- (D2) To get all optimal solutions, solve once, add c^T x ≤ c*, then enumerate the feasible set of the restricted problem (p. 3).
- (D5) Report the size of what is enumerated (|X_IP|) beside time, nodes and depth, plus a count of the objects the new component produced (Table 1, p. 5). A baseline node count near 2|X| then shows that the baseline tree has one leaf per solution (my reading of Table 1).

**Quotes**:
- "the redundancy check has to be performed explicitly in every search node" (p. 4)

### R011 · On the Effects of Minor Changes in Model Formulations (ZIB-Report 08-29 2008, urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-10808)
**Read level**: full. All 11 file pages, Tables 1–6 and the reference list included.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Thorsten Koch, Andreas Tuchscherer (roles not stated) · **Position in trajectory**: August 2008 (p. 1); Achterberg's affiliation is ILOG Deutschland, the coauthors' ZIB (p. 2). Runs use CPLEX 10.0.1 and SCIP 0.90e (p. 5). This report is the source of the skill's Heuristic 6.

- **D1 Problem and entry**: A failed reproduction. Reproducing van Vyve and Wolsey's TSP model from "Approximate extended formulations", the authors saw the MIP solvers behave erratically under minor variations of the model (p. 2). With Trick's remark that modern solvers apply the usual formulation tricks automatically, so that the effect of a change is hard to predict, they ask how much such variations matter (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Write the variations as a five-factor family u-w-e-b-f (p. 5, Table 1):
  - u: bounds and integrality of the order variables u;
  - w: bounds and integrality of the flow variables w;
  - e: equality or inequality in the flow constraints (9)–(10);
  - b and f: binding the outgoing flows to y, or fixing them to zero.

  Each change leaves both the integer optimum and the LP relaxation value unchanged (p. 4); the original model is 2-1-1-1-0 (p. 5). Every combination is solved in two solvers, and runs that coincide are explained by what presolve deduces (pp. 6–7). One change to the source model, with its reason: node 1 stays in the neighbourhoods V_l, because dropping it lowers the initial bound for k < n (p. 3). A cleaner four-factor scheme i-b-w-u is offered after the fact, since b is irrelevant when f = 1 (p. 6).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The TSP in an arc formulation: Miller–Tucker–Zemlin constraints (1)–(8) plus van Vyve–Wolsey flow constraints (9)–(12) on k-nearest neighbourhoods, which imply subtour elimination constraints by their Theorem 1, quoted with a one-line flow-capacity proof (p. 3). y is binary; u is continuous, integer or implicit integer; w is continuous, binary, integer or implicit integer (Table 1, p. 5). One instance: att48 from TSPLIB with k = 13, models generated with Zimpl 2.07 and the source files posted online (p. 5). The root LP before cuts is 10604 for every variant, the optimum 10628 (p. 7).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: The presolve account (pp. 6–7):
  - with inequalities in (9)–(10), the outgoing w are column singletons with zero objective and are fixed to zero;
  - the incoming w have y as their tightest implied upper bound and are substituted by y;
  - so the number of w variables falls from n(k(k−1)+2k(n−k)) to nk(k−1);
  - the worst case u-w-1-0-0 admits no reduction at all (p. 7).

  Five size classes remain after preprocessing, from 9,791 columns and 10,370 rows (S) to 53,461 and 54,050 (L2); M2 occurs only in CPLEX, L1 only in SCIP (Table 3, p. 7). Implicit-integer declarations pass through Zimpl to SCIP, which may then treat such a variable as continuous or integer (p. 5). SCIP's restarts, triggered by fixings at the root, are counted per run (p. 8; Table 6, p. 10). SCIP's robustness across variants is attributed, as most probable, to more strong branching per node (p. 8).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: CPLEX 10.0.1 and SCIP 0.90e at default settings, except that SCIP's probing is switched off, because CPLEX applies only a very limited probing by default, and SCIP uses CPLEX 10.0.1 as its LP solver; Pentium-D 3.6 GHz, 4 GB, a one-hour limit unless noted (p. 5).
  - 128 CPLEX variants collapse to 52 distinct runs (p. 7; Table 5, p. 9); SCIP gives 130 distinct runs (p. 8; Table 6, p. 10). Table 4 gives geometric means of nodes and time per factor level (p. 8).
  - CPLEX: 27 s for 1-4-0-0-0 against no optimum within 24 hours for 5-1-0-1-0. Both have the same small size and differ, the authors say, only in which variables are continuous and which integer or binary: a factor above 3,200 (p. 7).
  - The setting the averages predict as best (1-2-0-1-0) takes 60 s, against 27 s for the winner and 244 s for the overall geometric mean (p. 7).
  - SCIP solves every variant within the hour; its slowest-to-fastest ratio is 36 against more than 3,000 for CPLEX, while CPLEX is nearly three times faster on its best run (p. 8).
  - e = 0 wins in both solvers, and continuous declarations beat integer ones (pp. 7–8). f = 1 helps SCIP but not CPLEX, and b matters much more in SCIP (p. 8).
  - In Table 4, CPLEX's e = 0 has more nodes than e = 1 (972 against 456) and is about four times faster (118.7 s against 500.7 s) (p. 8).
  - Not stated: how the three CPLEX time-outs (Table 5, p. 9) enter the means. No seeds or permutations; one instance, one k.
- **D6 Positioning**: No dispute with van Vyve and Wolsey's theory: Theorem 1 is restated as theirs (p. 3), the one deviation is stated with its reason (p. 3), and Wolsey is thanked for the original Mosel model (p. 11). Trick is the authority for the unpredictability of formulation tricks (p. 2). The node gap between CPLEX and SCIP is put down to different default branching, citing Achterberg, Koch and Martin's branching paper (p. 7).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract understates its own number (a factor above 1,000, p. 2, against above 3,200 in the text, p. 7). Causal claims are hedged with probably (p. 2), most probably (p. 8) and maybe (p. 6). Every distinct run is printed (pp. 9–10). The authors' own feature is reported as missing their expectation: SCIP's implicit integers did no better than bounded continuous variables (p. 8). The conclusion opens with the reporting lesson (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Empirical and methodological. State the model formulation exactly when reporting computations, or reproducibility suffers (p. 8). The intractability of one formulation in one solver does not make the model intractable, so look for formulation-independent strategies, with branching as a candidate (pp. 8, 11). Next step proposed: implicit integers as a first way to pass redundant information to the solver, extensible to variable bounds and to the right-hand sides and senses of constraints, but needing more development to be exploited reliably (p. 11). The one-instance design is not discussed as a limitation (not stated).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 (variant ⚠; pp. 5, 7–8): the published formulation is one point (2-1-1-1-0) of a factor family, and the whole grid is run. But the factors are formulation choices, not parameters of one rule, and the reading is by per-level means and the best run, not by a knee in (nodes, time).
- Method 2 (evidence ✅; pp. 7–8): node counts are never read as speed. SCIP's geometric mean of 62 nodes against CPLEX's 666 comes with equal time (244.9 s against 243.8 s), explained by more work per node; CPLEX's e = 0 has more nodes and runs four times faster (Table 4, p. 8).
- Method 4 (variant ⚠; pp. 5, 8): the 2008 form. Plain geometric means per factor level, with no shift stated, no time brackets, single runs on one instance. The comparability settings match Heuristic 3 (probing off in SCIP, the same LP solver in both, p. 5).
- Also seen, among the heuristics: Heuristic 6 (evidence ✅): this report is its source (pp. 2, 7–8, 11); Heuristic 3 (evidence ✅) for the comparability settings (p. 5); Heuristic 10 (evidence ✅) for the printed failure of SCIP's implicit integers (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate "formulation robustness as a design criterion" (pp. 8, 11): measure a solver's spread over equivalent formulations (slowest/fastest 36 against more than 3,000) and prefer strategies whose performance depends less on the formulation.
- New-pattern candidate "collapse variants by presolve equivalence before counting" (pp. 6–8): derive which settings presolve turns into the same model, count distinct runs, tabulate sizes after preprocessing.

**Transferable techniques**:
- (D1/D2) When a published computational result does not reproduce, turn the discrepancy into a factorial study of changes that keep both the integer optimum and the LP bound fixed, and run it in two solvers (pp. 2, 4–5).
- (D4) Before counting variants, derive which ones presolve maps to the same model (column singletons, implied bounds) and report the size after preprocessing; count distinct runs, not settings (pp. 6–7; Table 3, p. 7).
- (D5) Read a factorial by per-level geometric means, then test the setting the means predict against the actual best (60 s against 27 s, p. 7).
- (D5) Use the slowest-to-fastest ratio over equivalent formulations as a robustness number per solver (36 against more than 3,000, p. 8).
- (D5) In a two-solver comparison, switch off a component that one solver uses heavily and the other barely (here probing), and give both the same LP solver (p. 5).
- (D8) Post the model files with the report (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "omitting seemingly useful redundant information can sometimes speed up the solution process" (p. 2)

### D010 · SCIP: solving constraint integer programs (Mathematical Programming Computation 1(1):1–41 2009, DOI 10.1007/s12532-008-0001-1)
**Read level**: full. Pages 1–38 read; the reference list (pp. 38–41) skimmed. The bodies of Tables 3–6 (pp. 18–21) and Table 10 (p. 36) are images without a text layer, so only their captions are in the file; their numbers are taken from the prose. Table 7 is extracted with some fused numbers (pp. 22–27); Tables 8, 9 and 11 are legible.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**: received 6 October 2008, published online 20 January 2009, affiliation ZIB (p. 1); the paper is based on his dissertation (p. 2), and the acknowledgements mention his departure to CPLEX (p. 38). The paper dates SCIP's development at ZIB from 2001 (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: CP, SAT and MIP solvers all divide the problem into subproblems but process them differently (p. 3). MIP has the LP's global view; CP has expressive constraints whose linearisation hides structure from a MIP solver (p. 3); SAT's LP relaxation is useless, since setting every variable to 0.5 satisfies it (p. 4). Earlier CP–MIP integrations exist, so the entry is the level of integration: all techniques on one search tree, sharing statistics and LP duals, with SAT-like conflict analysis over both CP deductions and LP information (p. 2). The pulling application is property checking of arithmetic data paths, where SAT-based checkers typically fail (p. 30).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Constraint integer programs: constraints of any kind, as long as what remains after fixing the integer variables is linear in the continuous ones (Definition 2.1, condition (1), p. 4). The central objects are constraint handlers: a feasibility check alone makes the solver correct (by complete enumeration), and presolving, propagation, a linear relaxation and branching are optional callbacks for speed (pp. 7–8). Plugins communicate through the variables' bounds and the LP, which together with integrality form a MIP relaxation that problem-independent plugins can use (p. 14). New MIP ingredients, each with its measured effect:
  - inference branching and the hybrid reliability/inference rule (p. 17);
  - the product score (p. 18);
  - cut selection across all separators by efficacy, orthogonality and objective parallelism (p. 19);
  - conflict analysis generalised to CIP (p. 20);
  - restarts only at the root (pp. 12–13).

  Kept: branch-and-cut with many of the ingredients found in the literature (p. 16).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: CIP generalises finite-domain CP, MIP and SAT (Proposition 2.3, p. 5, stated with a pointer to the thesis, p. 4; no proof here).
  - MIP components are evaluated on ten sets: miplib 30, coral 38, milp 37, enlight 7, alu 25, fctp 16, acc 7, fc 20, arcset 23 and mik 41 instances (Table 2, p. 17); 244 by my sum, the thesis count recorded in the skill.
  - The solver comparison uses 122 of 159 instances from MIPLIB 3, MIPLIB 2003 and Mittelmann's site as of March 2008 (p. 21).
  - Property checking: register variables of given bit widths and 22 register-transfer operations (my count of the list, p. 31). A property is valid iff the CSP that searches for a counter-example is infeasible (Definition 5.1 and (8), pp. 30–31). OneSpin industrial instances (p. 35), 258 in the whole set (p. 37).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Solving loop (Fig. 4, pp. 11–12): presolve, node selection, heuristics, propagation, an LP loop with inner pricing and outer cutting, enforcement, branching. A restart re-presolves and restarts the tree; his experiments say that in MIP restarts belong only at the root, and only after enough root fixings, unlike SAT's periodic restarts (pp. 12–13).
  - Printed costs of the plugin design (pp. 14–15). Information hiding loses the column (dual) view used for dual presolving and symmetry detection. The remedy demanded of every handler is the number of constraints that may block increasing or decreasing each variable; this enables dual fixing and dual bound reduction and guides heuristics and branching (p. 14). Further costs: data scattered over memory with poor cache locality, problem data copied several times, and the LP solver as a black box with no short cuts (p. 15).
  - Code: 275,640 lines, 160 plugins, 8 LP-solver interfaces (Table 1, p. 15); about 18 % comment lines and 16,446 assertions, 20 % of all statements (p. 16).
  - Product score max{q−, ε}·max{q+, ε} with ε = 10^−6 (p. 18). Cut selection: score w_e·efficacy + w_p·objective parallelism + w_o·orthogonality with weights 1, 0.1, 1; cuts are added greedily, orthogonality to the cuts already chosen is updated, and candidates below efficacy 0.01 or orthogonality 0.5 are dropped; by default cuts are separated only at the root (p. 19).
  - Chip plugins, 58,367 lines (p. 31): bit- and word-level propagation, with term algebra inside the mult propagator (p. 33). Bit vectors are split into 16-bit words because coefficients such as 2^β cause numerical trouble in the LP (p. 34). shl, slice, read and write get no linearisation, since a straightforward linearisation of a 64-bit shl alone needs 30,944 inequalities and 20,929 auxiliary variables (p. 34). mult operands are swapped when that gives a smaller LP (p. 35). The choice of objective (0, +1, −1) has no large impact (p. 34).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - Components (SCIP 0.90f, 3.8 GHz Pentium 4, 3,600 s, the ten sets; p. 17): percentage change of the shifted geometric mean of time against the default, positive meaning slower, with a weighted total over the sets and a node total (captions of Tables 3–6, pp. 18–21).
  - Hybrid reliability/inference branching is 8 % faster than pseudocost branching with strong-branching initialisation, from 5 % slower on fc to 55 % faster on alu, through 27 % fewer nodes; full strong branching has the fewest nodes and still loses (p. 17).
  - The product score gains 14 %; the best weighted-sum value is µ = 1/6 in SCIP against Linderoth and Savelsbergh's 1/3 (p. 18).
  - Orthogonality gains 22 % although nodes rise 8 %; objective parallelism seems ineffective; adding only the deepest cut or all violated cuts is clearly inferior (pp. 19–20).
  - Conflict analysis, off by default in 0.90f, saves 12 % at balanced aggressiveness and almost 50 % on enlight (pp. 20–21). Node selection, child selection, primal heuristics and root restarts give 4, 9, 7 and 8 % (p. 21).
  - Solver comparison. Test-set rule: drop instances that neither CPLEX 11 nor CBC 2.2 solves within 3,600 s on a 3.4 GHz Pentium D, then drop six instances on which the six configurations disagree about the optimal value; 122 remain (p. 21). Per-instance Table 7 (pp. 22–27).
  - Table 8 (p. 28): profile-like counts of instances within 101 %, 110 %, 150 %, 200 %, 500 % and 1000 % of the fastest; solved counts (CPLEX 11 122, CPLEX 10.2 115, CBC 111, SCIP with CPLEX, CLP and SoPlex 118, 112, 102); shifted-geometric ratios of time, nodes and iterations to CPLEX 11 (SCIP/CPLEX 2.86, 1.20, 1.18); the same ratios on the 97 instances all solve. Shifts 1 for time, 10 for nodes, 100 for iterations; time-outs are counted as solved at 3,600 s, and the bias in favour of solvers that often time out is stated (p. 28).
  - Chip (SCIP 0.90i, CPLEX 10.0.1 as LP solver, 3.8 GHz Pentium 4, 2 GB, 7,200 s): the baseline is MiniSat 2.0, best of four SAT solvers tried (p. 35). SAT wins on invalid properties, CIP on most valid ones, sometimes by orders of magnitude (p. 36). On multipliers there is no clear winner, but CIP's time grows less with bit width (p. 37). Over all 258 instances SAT fails on 58 and CIP on none (p. 37).
  - No seeds or permutations anywhere.
- **D6 Positioning**: Against earlier integrated CP–MIP frameworks (SCIL, SIMPL, branch-and-infer, logic-based Benders) by the level of integration (p. 2; references pp. 38–40), and against Abacus, which treats each constraint as an isolated object (p. 14). SIP is named as the predecessor whose ideas SCIP adopts; SCIP was rebuilt from scratch for flexibility and CIP (p. 15). The literature's weights µ = 0 and µ = 1/2 appear as settings of one family (p. 18). Efficacy and orthogonality are credited to Andreello, Caprara and Fischetti, who used them within one cut class (p. 19); SatZ for inference branching (p. 17); cutting planes and primal heuristics in SCIP are referred to Wolter's and Berthold's theses (p. 10). CBC is granted similar MIP performance (p. 3). Word-level verification solvers are named but not run, because they were unavailable in the industrial partner's framework (p. 35).
- **D7 Writing**: A framework paper built on the thesis (p. 2). Each ingredient paragraph ends with a measured percentage (pp. 17–21). Claims are hedged by the author's knowledge (comment and assertion density, p. 16; novelty of the product score, p. 18), and the chip section is announced as anecdotal (abstract, p. 1; p. 31). A section prints the design's limitations (pp. 14–15). The headline factor of 1.87 against CPLEX 10.2 (p. 2) is derived: 2.86/1.53, assuming the LP solver changed little from CPLEX 10.2 to 11 (p. 29); the raw ratio to CPLEX 11 is 2.86 (p. 28). Weak spots are stated: the gap to CPLEX is smaller on the easy instances, and SCIP's algorithms are tuned for medium-sized models (p. 29); equivalence checking of multipliers does not suit the approach, because the bit-level circuit has lost its arithmetic structure (p. 37).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Software (a framework free for academic and non-commercial use, p. 2), algorithms (pp. 16–21) and an application (pp. 29–37). Limits are concrete: the plugin costs (pp. 14–15), the speed gap to CPLEX 11 (p. 28), SAT's advantage on counter-examples (p. 36) and the missing word-level baselines (p. 35). Future: MINLP by outer approximation, symmetry handling, an exact MIP solver, and more constraint handlers such as scheduling and alldiff (p. 37).

**Method links**:
- Method 4 (evidence ✅; pp. 18–21, 28): knock-out tables against the default, with a weighted total over sets and a node total; a component that is off by default (conflict analysis) is measured against disabling it (p. 20); time-outs are scored at the limit and the bias is stated (p. 28).
- Method 4 (variant ⚠; p. 28): the solver comparison states shifts of 1 (time), 10 (nodes) and 100 (iterations), not the 10 s and 100 nodes the skill records for the thesis era, so the shift is study-specific. The 2009 comparison adds a profile-like table and a solved-by-all subset.
- Method 2 (evidence ✅; pp. 17, 20, 29, 36): the 8 % gain comes from 27 % fewer nodes, while strong branching's larger node savings do not pay (p. 17). Orthogonality wins in time while nodes rise (p. 20). Speed-ups are read as nodes times throughput: CPLEX 10.2 to 11 through fewer nodes at about constant throughput; SCIP's extra work per node from aggressive strong branching (p. 29); SAT's win on invalid properties through faster node processing (p. 36).
- Method 3 (evidence ✅; pp. 16–19): the weighted-sum score family µ ∈ [0, 1] holds the literature's 0 and 1/2 (and Linderoth and Savelsbergh's 1/3) as settings; the best is 1/6, and the product score then beats the whole family (pp. 18–19). Reliability branching is presented as a generalisation of pseudocost branching with strong-branching initialisation (pp. 16–17).
- Method 6 (evidence ✅; pp. 13, 17, 20): SAT restarts are kept only at the root and only after enough root fixings (p. 13); SatZ's lookahead count of deductions becomes a pseudocost-like history and is combined with SAT's VSIDS rule (p. 17); conflict analysis is generalised to CIP (p. 20).
- Method 5 (evidence ✅; pp. 3, 7–8, 15–16, 21): SIP's successor rebuilt from scratch (p. 15). Correctness from a feasibility check alone, speed from optional callbacks, as in step 3 (pp. 7–8). ### D026 · Computation of an extractive distillation column with affine arithmetic (AIChE Journal 55(7):1695–1704 2009, DOI 10.1002/aic.11777)
**Read level**: full. The authors' preprint (p. 1), sheets 1–40: text, the reference list (skimmed), appendix tables, figure captions and tables; sheets 41–46 carry only figure labels. Pages are preprint sheets, not journal pages.
**Role**: core, co-signed; his part is narrow · **Coauthors**: Ali Baharev (corresponding author) and Endre Rév, Budapest University of Technology and Economics (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: 2009, Achterberg's affiliation ZIB (p. 1). A guest contribution of MIP-solver LP practice to an interval-methods code outside MIP (pp. 6–7).

- **D1 Problem and entry**: Steady states of counter-current multistage processes are large nonlinear systems. Standard methods give no convergence guarantee, depend on initial estimates, cannot tell a poor start from non-existence, and cannot guarantee finding all solutions under output multiplicity (pp. 2–3). Interval methods can, and the authors know of no interval computation of distillation columns (pp. 2, 4). The aim is to combine Kolev's affine-arithmetic linearisation with LP pruning and reach an industrial-scale column (p. 4).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: A branch-and-prune method: a mixed affine/interval linearisation A(X)x + B(X) = 0 over the box (p. 5); equationwise constraint propagation, the affine analogue of the interval Newton–Gauss–Seidel step with real denominators (pp. 5–6); LP pruning by minimising and maximising each x_j over the linear enclosure (p. 6); bisection of the widest component (p. 7). Achterberg's heuristic, the part named after him (pp. 6–7):
  - skip the min (max) LP for x_j when x_j already sits at its lower (upper) bound in any primal feasible point met during pruning;
  - a primal feasible point stays feasible when the objective changes, so only the first LP is solved from scratch, and each next one starts from the previous optimum in primal phase II;
  - instead of the naive order min x1, max x1, min x2, ..., take next the variable closest to one of its bounds that has not been considered, in that bound's direction, assuming the current point is then close to that LP's optimum.
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Not a MILP: square nonlinear systems f(x) = 0 on a box (p. 5). The MESH equations of continuous extractive distillation of acetone and methanol with water as entrainer, with 12 to 22 stages (p. 18; Table 5, p. 36), Wilson activity coefficients, Antoine vapour pressures and constant pressure (p. 12). The enthalpy model is simplified to heats of vaporisation, which the authors report matches commercial simulators to 2–3 digits (pp. 10–11). Affine noise symbols not belonging to x or T are merged into one, losing information for a simpler, better-scaled LP (pp. 11–12). The initial box comes from engineering bounds (vapour flows within ±32 %); the K intervals are read from graphs and then verified by proving, with the same method, that each complementary bound is infeasible (pp. 13–14).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Steps 0–5 with a stack of boxes; steps 2–4 are repeated a fixed number of times, which the authors call easy to implement though not the most effective (p. 8). The affine class is redesigned in C++ with a memory pool that replaces an STL map (pp. 5, 9); the LP solver is GLPK 4.28 (p. 14); a problem-specific bisection weight w on the distillate's acetone fraction serves as a fallback (pp. 7, 15). Convergence is asserted (abstract, p. 2), but the text gives no proof: n/a.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - One change at a time: the linearisation and memory-pool changes are measured on the earlier paper's examples, which use no LP, so that they stay comparable while the LP pruning also changes; expected effects are written down before the results (pp. 8–9).
  - Tables 1–3 (pp. 32–34): time ratios 115, 29.5 and 44.8; iteration ratios 2.24, 1.18 and 2.62; cycle-time ratios 51.3, 24.9 and 17.1.
  - Column (Table 5, p. 36): infeasibility proven at purity 0.96 for 12 and 16 trays (2.77 s, 9.49 s); solutions found at 0.92 in 22.10, 54.15 and 92.13 s; the 0.78 case beyond the limit. w = 20 solves the 0.78 case in 157.5 s (Table 6, p. 37), but is slower on five of the six reasonable cases (my count from Table 7, p. 38).
  - Achterberg's heuristic is tested on the column because the old examples now run in under a second (p. 15). With it, time falls by factors of 4.63, 5.57 and 4.89 and simplex iterations by 6.41, 6.75 and 6.39 (Table 8, p. 39). The with-heuristic times equal the totals in Tables 5 and 6 (9.49 s, 54.15 s, 157.5 s), so the ratio is on total solve time, though the text calls it pruning (my reading of pp. 15, 36–37, 39).
  - Multiple solutions: all four solutions of Meintjes and Morgan's combustion system in 1.46 s and 22,219 boxes (p. 16; Table 9, p. 40).
  - Pentium 4 at 3.00 GHz, 1 GB (p. 29). Single runs; no external solver as a baseline; the text gives 3 hours (p. 14) where Table 5 shows more than 12,000 s (p. 36).
- **D6 Positioning**: Extends Baharev and Rév 2008 (pp. 2, 17). Takes Kolev's linearisation and the published evidence that it may beat interval Newton or Krawczyk, and the earlier finding that LP pruning may suit VLE cascades (p. 4). Stadtherr and coauthors' interval-Newton applications are the background (p. 3). The novelty claim is hedged by the authors' knowledge (pp. 2, 4, 18); Kolev is thanked for reading a draft (p. 18).
- **D7 Writing**: Predictions come before measurements (pp. 8–9, 15). A coauthor's contribution is named in the text and measured separately (pp. 7, 15, 39). The gain of the skip rule is called obvious (p. 6) and is measured only together with the rest of the heuristic (Table 8, p. 39). There is an explicit limitations section (p. 17), and the summary states the main achievement in one sentence (p. 18).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Algorithmic enhancements and an application the authors claim as a first (p. 18). Limits are concrete: the implementation is in its infancy (about 3,000 lines for the affine class, 2,300 for the column), hard to extend, and hard to debug because no other mixed affine/interval implementation can give reference values (p. 17); widest-component bisection is not robust, and the choice of bisection variable is an open question (p. 7). Future: connect the solver to a modelling language (p. 18).
