### R003 · SCIP - a framework to integrate Constraint and Mixed Integer Programming (ZIB-Report 04-19, Zuse Institute Berlin 2004, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-7947)

**Read level**: full. I read all 29 file pages, the reference list included (pp. 26–29). Figures 1–5 are diagrams: operational stages, the aggregation graph, the solving loop, the LP loop and the enforcement results. Their labels and captions extract, and I read them together with the text that explains them (pp. 15, 17, 18, 20, 23).

**Version**: the cover says June 2004 (p. 1), the title page 2 January 2005 (p. 2), and the batch list gives the OPUS date 2004-05-28. So this file is a revised version; the text does not say what changed.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**:
- This is the first written description of SCIP (version 0.78) and of the CIP class, written at ZIB (pp. 2, 24).
- SCIP already lists conflict analysis as a feature (pp. 4, 13). It also has a "modification of the feasibility pump" among its eight diving heuristics (p. 24), a year before R006.
- It cites "Branching rules revisited" (ORL 2005) and MIPLIB 2003 as its own groundwork (refs [1], [2], p. 26).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - CP and MIP share the branch-and-bound split into subproblems, but they process each subproblem differently, with "complementary strengths" (abstract, p. 2).
  - Hybrid successes are cited: Timpe's chemical-industry planning with CHIP and XPress-MP, assembly line balancing, and parallel machine scheduling (p. 2).
  - The gap is a kind of framework, not an algorithm. The existing integrations are Coupe, SCIL and SIMPL (p. 3). SCIP is set apart by its audience: expert users who need full, in-depth control and high performance, rather than easy implementation and rapid prototyping (abstract, p. 2; p. 3).
  - Two failure modes motivate it:
    - the modelling-language "rapid mathematical prototyping chain" with a black-box MIP solver is too slow for some problem classes, "sometimes not even for small instances" (p. 7);
    - linearizing expressive CP constraints "can conceal their structure from a MIP solver" (p. 7).
  - Transferable technique: motivate a platform by two named failure modes of current practice (the black-box solve is too slow on some classes; linearization hides structure). Then position it against named systems by the user it serves (pp. 2–3, 7).

- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **The class.** A CIP is a CP restricted so that, once the integer variables are fixed, the rest is an LP (Definition 2.3 with restriction (1), pp. 7–8). This keeps an LP relaxation available beside CP-style constraint semantics.
  - **The architecture.** Every algorithm is a plugin, the integrality conditions included (pp. 3, 8, 11). The plugin types are constraint handlers, presolvers, cut separators, domain propagators, branching rules, variable pricers, primal heuristics, node selectors, relaxators, event handlers, conflict handlers, file readers, dialog handlers and display columns (pp. 3, 9–14).
  - **The minimal contract.** A constraint handler's feasibility check alone already gives a correct algorithm, a complete enumeration. Presolving, propagation, a linear relaxation and branching are optional help for pruning (p. 9).
  - **The main loop.** Node selection, domain propagation, relaxation solving, constraint enforcement, primal heuristics (Fig. 3, p. 18). Relaxation solving is an inner LP loop with pricing, cut separation, reduced cost strengthening and repeated propagation (Fig. 4, pp. 19–21).
  - **Enforcement.** Handlers run in priority order. The integrality handler has priority 0, so a handler with negative priority sees only integral solutions, and one with positive priority sees fractional ones too (p. 23). Each enforcement result sends the loop back to propagation, back to the LP, to branching, or on to the next handler (Fig. 5, pp. 22–23).
  - **What changes and what stays.** Against SIP, its predecessor, SCIP adds general constraints, pricing and other relaxations, and pays for them with an overhead (p. 26). What stays is LP-based branch-and-cut with cuts, heuristics and reliability branching (p. 24).
  - Transferable technique: give each plugin type a minimal correctness contract (here, the check) and make every other callback optional acceleration. Then a new constraint type is correct before it is fast (p. 9).

- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - **Definitions.** CP as a triple (C, D, f) (Def. 2.1, p. 5), MIP (Def. 2.2, p. 6), CIP (Def. 2.3, pp. 7–8).
  - **What restriction (1) allows.** Quadratic or more involved expressions are allowed, as long as the part left after fixing the integers is linear in the continuous variables (fn. 1, p. 8).
  - **Generality.** A linear objective and the absence of domains cost nothing: an auxiliary objective variable and extra constraints restore them (p. 8).
  - **Completeness.** The claim reads "the problem can be completely solved by enumerating all values of the integral variables and solving the corresponding LPs" (p. 8). There is no finiteness qualifier; R009 adds "in the case of finite domain integer variables" (R009 p. 4).
  - **Data model.** The CIP is kept apart from its LP (p. 19):
    - CIP variables are integer or continuous, and their domains may have holes;
    - constraints live in handler-specific storage, and the framework does not know their semantics;
    - the LP has columns and rows. Not every variable needs a column, one constraint can give several rows, and rows can exist without a constraint.
  - **Aggregation graph.** Presolve fixings and aggregations form a variable aggregation graph. Any expression a plugin writes on original, fixed or aggregated variables is mapped onto active variables automatically (Example 4.1, Fig. 2, pp. 16–17).
  - **Upgrading.** Linear constraints are upgraded to more specific types, e.g. to knapsack constraints when all variables are binary, the weights integral and only one side finite. Some variables may be negated on the way (pp. 16–17).
  - **Test models.** MIPLIB 2003 and Mittelmann's instances, pure MIPs only (p. 24).

- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: proofs: n/a. The devices:
  - an explicit stage machine, from init through transforming, presolving and solving to solved, with "free solve" and "free transform" stages to go back (§4, Fig. 1, pp. 14–17). The solver works on a transformed copy, so the original problem changes only in the problem-modification stage (p. 15);
  - a numerical fallback: an unstable LP solution triggers other LP solver settings. If they fail, the node's LP relaxation is discarded, and the node is processed as if no LP had been solved. Since SCIP need not solve the LP at every node, it "can easily leap over numerical troubles" (p. 20);
  - a memory fallback: near the memory limit, a memory-saving mode gives depth-first search the highest priority. The solver switches back once enough memory is released (pp. 18–19);
  - an event system, so that a constraint is propagated again only when one of its variables' domains changed. It also keeps incremental values up to date, such as a knapsack's fixed weight (p. 13);
  - cut selection by efficacy (Euclidean distance to the LP point) and near-orthogonality, after Andreello, Caprara and Fischetti [5] (p. 21);
  - reduced cost strengthening, still inside the core and to become a separator plugin later (p. 21);
  - conflict handlers, which turn a SAT-style analysis, extended to infeasible LPs, into constraints (p. 13).

  SCIP 0.78's components (p. 24):
  - cuts: Gomory fractional, Marchand's c-MIR, strong Chvátal-Gomory and lifted knapsack cover. Clique cuts and probing are missing;
  - heuristics: eight diving heuristics, one of them a feasibility-pump modification, and two rounding heuristics;
  - branching: from most and least infeasible to reliability and strong branching;
  - LP interfaces to CPLEX, SoPlex and CLP.

- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **Setup.** SCIP 0.78 against SIP and CPLEX 9.03, with CPLEX 9.03 also as SCIP's LP solver. The hardware is a 3.2 GHz Pentium-4EE with 2 GB; the limits are 3,600 s and 1.5 GB (p. 24).
  - **Test-set rule.** From MIPLIB 2003 and Mittelmann's instances, all those where CPLEX 9.02 needed at least 1,000 nodes and at most one hour (p. 24). Footnote 4: CPLEX ran at defaults except absolute mipgap 10⁻⁹ and relative mipgap 0.0, SCIP's values. The set was assembled before CPLEX 9.03 was available (p. 24). Table 1 has 29 instances (p. 25).
  - **Results.**
    - Totals: 21,388.7 s (SCIP), 16,870.8 s (SIP) and 11,675.5 s (CPLEX).
    - Geometric means: 162.5, 133.9 and 81.4 s.
    - Node geometric means: 40,564, 49,434.4 and 34,668 (p. 25).

    The text reads a factor of 2 in both the geometric mean and the total (p. 24); my arithmetic gives 2.0 and 1.8.
  - **Failures.** SCIP leaves 4 instances unsolved (mzzv11, binkar10_1, mkc1, neos3), SIP 3, CPLEX none (p. 25; my count). *Inferred*: CPLEX solves everything because a CPLEX version picked the set.
  - **Per instance.** SCIP is faster than CPLEX on 8 of 29 instances (misc07, rout, prod1, bienst1, bienst2, seymour1, swath1, swath2) and slower on 21 (my recount, p. 25).
  - **Means.** The text does not say how the means are formed. My recount reproduces them as plain geometric means, with unsolved runs entered at the time they stopped (my script comparison). One of these is binkar10_1, at 1,746.0 s in SCIP's column: below the time limit, so presumably the memory limit (p. 25; *inferred*).
  - **Version mismatch.** The selector and the runs differ in version: gesa2-o needs 658 nodes in CPLEX 9.03, below the 1,000 nodes the CPLEX 9.02 rule required (pp. 24–25).
  - **Not done.** No knock-out of SCIP's own components, no CP or CIP instance, single runs only (pp. 24–26).
  - **SIP's lead is explained** (p. 26):
    - SCIP is SIP's successor;
    - the reimplemented algorithms are not yet tuned as exhaustively;
    - the parameters are not yet adjusted;
    - SCIP carries an overhead for general constraints, pricing and other relaxations.

- **D6 Positioning**:
  - **Integration systems.** Three are described in one sentence each: Coupe (Bockmayr and Kasper: branching and inference), SCIL (Althaus et al.: symbolic constraints on top of MIP solvers) and SIMPL (Aron, Hooker and Yunes: an infer-relax-restrict cycle) (p. 3).   - **Hooker.** SIMPL (CPAIOR 2004) is cited (ref. [6], p. 26). SKILL.md says he started CIP unaware of Hooker's parallel work; by this report, that work is known and cited.
  - **A name slip.** The reference list titles Althaus et al.'s report "SCIP – symbolic constraints in integer linear programming" (ref. [4], p. 26), while the body calls the system Scil (p. 3). R009 lists the same report as "SCIL – …" (R009 p. 14).
  - **Sources.** The MIP side rests on Bixby et al. 2000 [10] for the main ingredients, on the branching paper [2], and on Atamtürk and Savelsbergh [7] for solver features (p. 6). The CP side rests on Schulte [46] for trailing against copying (p. 5). The frameworks Abacus and COIN are named (p. 7).
  - **SIP** is named as SCIP's predecessor (p. 26).

  - **A guided tour.** Feature list (pp. 3–4), definitions (pp. 4–8), one subsection per plugin type (pp. 9–14), the stages (pp. 14–24), then the computations (pp. 24–26).
  - **Proportions.** The computational part is about two of 25 text pages (pp. 24–26).
  - **The loss.** It is stated plainly, "not strictly competitive", then softened: the numbers are taken "as an indication" that SCIP "is not very far away from a state-of-the-art MIP solver" (pp. 24, 26).
  - **Worked examples.** The knapsack constraint handler (Example 3.1, p. 9) and the aggregation graph (Example 4.1, pp. 16–17).
  - **Future tense.** Clique cuts "will be added in one of the next versions" (p. 24); reduced cost strengthening will move to a plugin (p. 21).

- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Contribution.** A software framework and a problem class, checked computationally on pure MIP (pp. 2, 24–26).
  - **Limits stated.** A factor of 2 to CPLEX, SIP slightly ahead and why, and missing components (pp. 24, 26).
  - **Limits the card adds.** The integration claim in the title has no computational test: no CP or CIP instance is run (pp. 24–25). The competitor picked the test set, and all runs are single (p. 24).

**Method links**:
- Method 5 ✅ evidence:
  - SCIP 0.78 ships the plugins needed for complete MIP solves, every unit implemented as a plugin (pp. 3, 24). This is Method 5's step 3.
  - A reference-solver run comes with the study, with matched gap tolerances and CPLEX also as SCIP's LP solver (p. 24).
  - The test set is picked by the reference solver, not by the code under study (p. 24).
- Method 5 ⚠ variant: in 2004 the selector is the competitor (CPLEX 9.02), not a frozen version of his own code as in the thesis. *Inferred*: a set that the reference solves completely by construction favours the reference.
- Method 4 ⚠ variant (era): the 2004 form is totals and plain geometric means against two external solvers. Time-outs and a likely memory-out enter at the time they stopped (my recount). There is no knock-out from SCIP's default and no count of faster and slower instances (pp. 24–25).
- Method 6 ✅ evidence (design only): CP-style propagation and SAT conflict clauses enter as plugin types, and conflict analysis is extended to infeasible LPs (pp. 9–10, 13). Nothing is tested here; R004 tests the conflict analysis.
- H3 (fair yardstick) ✅ evidence, as SKILL.md already cites: equal gap tolerances, the same LP solver, and the factor of 2 printed with its reasons (pp. 24, 26).

- New-pattern candidate: *degrade, don't abort, on numerical trouble*. Retry the LP with other settings; if that fails, drop the node's LP bound and go on (p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- [D2] Split each plugin interface into a mandatory correctness callback (the check) and optional speed callbacks (presolve, propagate, relax, branch) (p. 9).
- [D3] Define the class by what is left once the integers are fixed (an LP), so that integer enumeration plus LP solves is complete. State the finiteness condition (pp. 7–8; R009 p. 4).
- [D3] Keep an aggregation graph, so that plugins may write expressions on original, fixed or aggregated variables and the framework maps them onto active ones (pp. 16–17).
- [D4] On an unstable LP, retry with other LP settings, then discard that node's LP bound and continue by branching, instead of aborting the solve (p. 20).
- [D5] Pick the test set by the reference solver with an effort window (at least 1,000 nodes, at most 1 h). Match the gap tolerances, and use the reference's LP solver inside the new code (p. 24).
- [D7] Print a factor-2 loss together with its reasons: tuning, parameters, and the overhead of generality (pp. 24, 26).

### R004 · Conflict Analysis in Mixed Integer Programming (ZIB-Report 05-19, Zuse Institute Berlin 2005, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-8537)

**Read level**: full. I read all 20 file pages: the text, Algorithms 3.4 and 3.6, Lemma 3.3 with its proof, Tables 1–3 and the reference list (pp. 19–20). Figures 1–5 are conflict graphs that extract as vertex labels. I read them through their captions and the text that walks through them (pp. 5, 7, 8, 10, 13).

**Version**: the cover says August 2005 (p. 1) and the title page 3 August 2005 (p. 2), while the batch list gives the OPUS date 2005-03-22. So this file is probably a later revision of a report first posted in March (*inferred* from the dates).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Position in trajectory**:
- R003 listed conflict analysis as a SCIP feature (R003 pp. 4, 13); here it gets its own study, in SCIP 0.79a (p. 15), two years before the thesis.
- It is the source of SKILL.md's Method 2 time split and of the conflict-analysis entry in Signature Work Anatomy.

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - **The contrast.** Conflict analysis is a key ingredient of SAT solvers, while MIP solvers, it "seems", "simply discard infeasible and bound exceeding subproblems" (abstract; p. 2). The paper sets out to generalize SAT infeasibility analysis to MIP (abstract, p. 2).
  - **Two obstacles** (p. 3):
    - MIP variables need not be binary;
    - a MIP subproblem's infeasibility usually comes from its LP relaxation as a whole, so a "(preferably simple) reason for the LP's infeasibility" must be found first.
  - **A clash of terms**, settled first: MIP's conflict graph (Atamtürk, Nemhauser and Savelsbergh [5]) is a pairwise implication graph, while SAT's is built for each infeasible subproblem. The paper adopts SAT's nomenclature (p. 3).
  - Transferable technique: when importing a technique, list the structural differences between the source and the target field (variable types; where infeasibility is detected), and give each difference a section of its own (pp. 3, 8–15).

- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **Generalized conflict graph.** Its vertices are local bound changes, not fixings, so one general integer can appear several times (§3.1, pp. 9–10; Example 3.1, Fig. 4).
  - **Infeasible LP.** It is explained through its dual ray (ȳ, r̄). The left-hand side of the aggregated proof (13) does not depend on the local upper bounds. So Algorithm 3.4 relaxes local bounds to global ones as long as the infeasibility measure d stays negative (pp. 11–12). The bound changes that remain are the initial reason; they replace the single arc from "LP" to the conflict vertex (Example 3.5, Fig. 5, pp. 12–13).
  - **Bound-exceeding LP.** The dual solution serves as the proof. Bounds are relaxed while bᵀȳ + ûᵀr̄ ≤ z̄ holds, with an optional dual re-solve (Algorithm 3.6, pp. 13–14).
  - **Non-binary variables in the conflict set.** They would need a disjunction of bound constraints (14), whose linearization needs auxiliary binaries (15). That system is judged "probably very weak", but this was not tested (p. 14). So the cut in the graph is moved until the non-binary bound changes lie on the conflict side. If such a change was a branching decision, it is dropped from the conflict set, and the clause is then valid only in the subtree below that node (pp. 14–15; Example 3.7, p. 15).
  - **Taken from SAT unchanged.** The 1-FUIP and All-FUIP cuts, reconvergence cuts and non-chronological backtracking (§2, pp. 4–8).
  - **What changes against SAT, and what stays.** An LP analysis replaces the single conflict-detecting clause (pp. 8–9). The cut selection in the graph and the clause semantics stay (p. 9).

- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - **The problem.** max{cᵀx | Ax ≤ b, l ≤ x ≤ u, x_j ∈ Z for j ∈ I}. A subproblem is given by its local bound hyperplanes B_L (pp. 3, 10).
  - **Simplifications in the exposition.** It assumes l = l̃ = 0 and at most one upper-bound change per variable. The general case processes bound changes one at a time and splits the reduced costs into r^u − r^l, using the Farkas lemma for r^u · r^l = 0 (pp. 11–12). §3.3 makes the same assumptions (p. 13).
  - **Local cuts.** They must be removed from the LP, or the conflict must be marked as valid only at the depth of the last local cut still in the LP (fn. 1, p. 10).
  - **The feasible test set.** From MIPLIB 2003 and Mittelmann's instances, all those where CPLEX 9.03 needs at least 1,000 nodes and 30 s, and at most one hour (p. 16). That gives 29 instances, listed with their sizes (Table 1, p. 17).
  - **The infeasible test set.** ALU property-checking IPs from the contributed section of MIPLIB 2003: register widths 4 to 10 bits, with eight properties each, of which properties 3–5 are trivial and not listed (pp. 16, 18). About 80 % of the variables are binary and 20 % general integer, and the coefficients reach 2^(2n) (pp. 16, 18).

- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**:
  - **Proof device.** Lemma 3.3 shows that minimal-cardinality bound-IIS is NP-hard, by a reduction from minimal-cardinality IIS (Amaldi, Pfetsch and Trotter [4]): slack variables s_i ≥ 0 serve as the bounds (p. 11).
  - **The choice of heuristic.** A preliminary implementation of a Farkas-based heuristic from the IIS literature had a "very large" running-time overhead. So the paper uses simple heuristics on the LP information at hand (p. 11).
  - **The invariant.** Relaxing bounds changes only the right-hand side of the infeasibility proof (13). So the same ray proves infeasibility as long as that side stays negative (p. 12). In §3.3, upper bounds do not affect dual feasibility (p. 13).
  - **A worked number.** d = −2 at the start. Relaxing z3's lower bound from 3 to 2 gives d = −1, and the initial reason is z1 ≤ 1, z2 ≥ 2, z3 ≥ 2 (p. 12).
  - **Implementation in SCIP 0.79a** (pp. 15–16):
    - analysed: conflicts from propagation, infeasible or bound-exceeding node LPs, and infeasible strong-branching LPs of reliability branching;
    - an initial conflict set of binaries only is added directly. 1-FUIP and All-FUIP clauses are always generated, together with reconvergence clauses for the last depth level;
    - CPLEX's dual simplex stops at the objective limit, so the LP is continued to optimality without the limit, to get useful initial sets;
    - Step 5 of Algorithm 3.6 is skipped: it removes only a few more bound changes and is not worth its cost;
    - cuts are separated at the root only, which "seems to yield the best performance on most MIP instances using SCIP";
    - conflict clauses are added on demand to local LPs and take part in propagation;
    - aging deletes clauses that did not help for a couple of consecutive iterations, the longer ones earlier.
  - **Numerics.** Feasibility and integrality tolerances of 10⁻⁹ for the ALU set, because of the coefficients up to 2^(2n) (p. 16).

- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **Setup.** A 3.2 GHz Pentium-4EE with 2 GB and a 3,600 s limit (p. 15). SCIP 0.79a uses CPLEX as its LP solver. CPLEX 9.03 is the reference, with its gap tolerances set to SCIP's values (fn. 4, p. 16).
  - **Table 1** (p. 17) compares CPLEX 9.03, SCIP at default and SCIP with conflict analysis. It gives totals and geometric means for all 29 instances and for the 24 that both SCIP settings solve ("Reduced"):
    - node geometric means: 41,268, 29,633 and 15,441;
    - time geometric means: 202.0, 257.2 and 297.8 s.
  - **What the text says** (p. 16):
    - nodes −50 % and time +15 % in the geometric mean, with "similar observations" on the reduced set;
    - conflict analysis needs fewer nodes on 21 instances and less time on 12; the default needs fewer nodes on 5 and is faster on 16;
    - SCIP is "not strictly competitive, but not far away" from CPLEX. My arithmetic gives 27 % slower in the geometric mean.
  - **My recount:**
    - all 29: nodes −48 %, time +16 %;
    - the reduced 24: nodes −44 %, time +4.5 % (179.4 → 187.4 s). The direction holds, but on the instances both settings solve, the time loss is under a third of the headline;
    - per instance: 11 faster, 17 slower, and mkc1 tied at the limit, against the text's 12 and 16;
    - nodes: 24 fewer and 5 more. The text's 21 matches if the three instances where the conflict run hit the limit (mas74, mas76, mkc1) are left out (*inferred*).
  - **Table 2** (p. 18) splits the time three ways:
    - **basic time (BTime):** presolving, node processing including the LPs, heuristics, and the overhead of conflict constraints in propagation and separation (pp. 16–17). Its geometric mean falls from 247.0 to 225.0 s; the text calls this 10 %, my arithmetic 9 %;
    - **switching time (STime):** setting up subproblems with their local constraints and bounds. The geometric mean rises from 5.7 to 7.2 s, the total from 658.8 to 2,450.4 s;
    - **conflict time (CTime):** 3,544.5 s in total, with a geometric mean of 40.7 s.
  - **The explanation in the text.** The cost of the analysis, mostly from continuing bound-exceeding LPs to optimality, outweighs the node saving. Switching time grows with the local constraints, most on mas74, mas76 and pk1 (pp. 17–18). My recount: on 17 of 29 instances, CTime plus the added STime exceeds the BTime saving.
  - **Produced objects are printed.**
    - On the feasible set, clause counts reach 257,919 (mas74), and the average length runs from 4.6 to 523.7 literals (Table 2, p. 18). On ALU it is only 1.6 to 6.2 literals (Table 3, p. 19).
    - Short clauses are offered as "a possible explanation" of the ALU success. The text then checks this against the feasible set, where length and performance "do not seem to be correlated" (p. 18).
  - **Table 3** (ALU, p. 19) has 35 instances. The reduced 21 exclude properties 2 and 6, which SCIP's preprocessing solves, and the geometric means treat values below 1.0 as 1.0.
    - Time means: 101.4, 19.7 and 11.5 s (all); 157.3, 143.1 and 58.6 s (reduced).
    - Node means: 215,146, 1,059 and 289 (all); 322,700, 110,033 and 12,671 (reduced).
    - CPLEX hits the limit on 8 of 35 instances, SCIP's default on 3, and conflict analysis on 2 (my count).
  - **The abstract's two sets of figures come from different subsets** (my recount):
    - its ALU figures, nodes −90 % and time −60 %, are the reduced-set ratios (−88 %, −59 %). On all 35 instances they are −73 % and −42 %;
    - its feasible-set figures are the full-set ratios;
    - the ALU reduction drops instances that both SCIP settings solve in presolve, where there is no conflict to analyse (pp. 18–19). So the reduced set is the informative one there.

### R006 · Improving the Feasibility Pump (ZIB-Report 05-42, Zuse Institute Berlin 2005, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-8754)

**Read level**: full. I read all 12 file pages: the text, Algorithm 2.1, Tables 1–2 (all 121 rows, recounted) and the reference list (pp. 9–10). Figure 1 holds six trajectory plots (p. 8); I read it from its axis labels, its caption and the discussion on pp. 7–9.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Timo Berthold (ZIB) (p. 2) · **Position in trajectory**:
- This primal-heuristic paper is from the year of R004 and R005.
- R003 already listed a "modification of the feasibility pump" among SCIP 0.78's diving heuristics (R003 p. 24).
- This report names no implementation platform: the word SCIP does not occur in the file (my search). The LP solver and the preprocessing are CPLEX's (p. 6).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - **The incumbent.** The pump of Fischetti, Glover and Lodi, improved by Fischetti, Bertacco and Lodi, finds feasible solutions well, but their objective value "tends to be poor" (abstract, p. 2).
  - **Why it matters.** A good primal bound prunes the tree, and the improvement heuristics (Local Branching, guided dives, RINS) need a first solution (p. 2).
  - **The diagnosis.** A good solution must satisfy three conditions: x ∈ P, integrality, and a small cᵀx. The pump pursues the first two; the objective enters only through the LP-optimal starting point (p. 4).
  - Transferable technique: write the target as a list of conditions, and show which one the incumbent algorithm drops after its first step (p. 4).

- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **The change.** The distance ∆ is replaced by ∆α = (1 − α)∆ + α (‖∆‖/‖c‖) cᵀx, with α ∈ [0, 1] decayed geometrically by α_{t+1} = ϕα_t (eq. (4), p. 5). α0 = 0 gives the original pump (p. 5).
  - **The contrast** is with the original authors' own fixes: an objective limit with a re-run of the pump, or local search (Local Branching, RINS) after a solution is found. "We take a different approach": reduce the objective's influence gradually instead of discarding it at once (pp. 4–5).
  - **Cycle detection changes too.** Under a moving objective, a revisited point is not a true cycle. So the pump stores (x̃, α_t) pairs and restarts only if x̃ was visited at some t′ < t with α_{t′} − α_t ≤ δα (p. 5).
  - **Kept:** the three stages, the rounding, the random perturbations, the stall rules and the Stage 3 MIP (Algorithm 2.1, pp. 5–6; §1.2, pp. 3–4).

- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - **The problem.** min cᵀx subject to Ax ≤ b, l ≤ x ≤ u and integrality on I (p. 2), with the assumption c ≠ 0 (p. 5). Stage 1 works on the binaries B and Stage 2 on all integers I; ‖∆‖ is √|B| or √|I| (pp. 4–5).
  - **A known weak spot is handled.** An objective made of a single artificial variable, defined by an equation, makes ‖∆‖ misleading. CPLEX's MIP preprocessing, applied before the pump, "usually resolves this issue" (p. 6).
  - **Test set.** 121 instances from MIPLIB 2003, Mittelmann's collection, and the instances of [7] as described in [6] (pp. 3, 6).

- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: proofs: n/a. The devices:
  - the objective term is normalized by ‖∆‖/‖c‖ (p. 5);
  - both versions use the original authors' settings (p. 6):
    - maxIter 10,000 (Stage 1) and 2,000 (Stage 2), maxStalls 70 and 600;
    - a randomized rounding threshold ρ(z);
    - shifting of a random number T ∈ [10, 30] of variables with fractionality above 0.02;
  - the new parameters are α0 = 1, ϕ = 0.9 and δα = 0.005 (p. 6). No sweep of them is reported (my reading of pp. 5–9).

- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **Setup.** A 3.4 GHz Pentium-4 with 512 KB cache and 3 GB RAM, CPLEX 9.03 as the LP solver, and one hour per run (p. 6).
  - **Metrics** (p. 7):
    - the objective value;
    - the gap γ = 100(c̃ − c*)/|c*| to the optimal or best known value, with two conventions: 0 when the values are equal, ∞ when c* = 0 < c̃. The best known value comes from CPLEX 9.1 run for an hour at defaults, from the MIPLIB 2003 site, or from one of the pumps;

    The geometric means are taken over the instances where both versions found a solution, with values below 1 replaced by 1.
  - **Results** (p. 7):
    - the new pump is strictly better on 89 of 121 instances, the original on 17; they tie on 11, and neither finds a solution on 4;

  - **My recount of the means.** It reproduces the time, round and restart means over the 116 instances where both versions found a solution, but not the gap means.   - **In relative terms**, the time rises about 20 % in the geometric mean, with three times the pumping rounds (my arithmetic, p. 12). "Only slightly" refers to absolute seconds: the times "differ only slightly in terms of absolute numbers" (p. 7).

### R009 · Constraint Integer Programming: a New Approach to Integrate CP and MIP (ZIB-Report 08-01, Zuse Institute Berlin 2008, no DOI; urn:nbn:de:0297-zib-10520)

**Read level**: full. I read all 16 file pages: the text, Tables 1–6 (recounted) and the reference list (pp. 13–16). Some symbols are lost in extraction, such as the bit-vector notation on p. 11; the linearizations of Table 3 (p. 12) are legible.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Timo Berthold, Thorsten Koch and Kati Wolter (ZIB); Achterberg's address is ILOG Deutschland, Bad Homburg (p. 1). Roles are not stated. The cut and heuristic parts point to Wolter's and Berthold's master's theses (refs [40], [13], [14]; pp. 7–8). · **Position in trajectory**:
- It was written in the CPLEX years, after the thesis (ref. [2], 2007) and after the journal version of the conflict paper (ref. [1], Discrete Optim. 2007) (p. 14).
- It describes SCIP 1.00 (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - **As in 2004**: integrated CP and MIP solved problems that neither method could solve alone [16, 25, 39], and integration frameworks exist [6, 10, 15] (p. 2).
  - **The claimed difference** is "the level of integration". CP constraints communicate only through the domains; CIP adds a second interface, the LP relaxation, provided most constraints can be reasonably linearized (p. 2).
  - **The fit is stated**: CIP suits "a MIP core complemented by a small number of nonlinear constraints", for example property checking (p. 2).
  - **SAT's place**: a clause linearizes easily, but its LP relaxation is "rather useless", since it detects infeasibility no earlier than propagation does (p. 3).

- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - **The definition** of R003 returns with restriction (1), now with a Proposition: CIP generalizes finite-domain CP (a) and MIP (b) (p. 4).
  - **The framework tour** (pp. 4–9):
    - constraint handlers, whose check alone gives a correct algorithm (p. 5);
    - domain propagation, with two dual methods: objective propagation and root reduced cost strengthening (p. 6);
    - conflict analysis as in R004: the NP-hardness note and a greedy heuristic on the dual ray (p. 6);
    - seven cut families, selected by efficacy and orthogonality after Balas, Ceria and Cornuéjols [12] and Andreello, Caprara and Fischetti [7] (p. 7);
    - 23 primal heuristics in four groups: rounding, diving, objective diving and improvement (pp. 7–8);
    - node selection: depth-first for a few nodes, then best estimate, and at some frequency best bound (p. 8);
    - presolving, with three tasks: shrink the model, strengthen the LP, and extract implications and cliques (pp. 8–9).
  - **Root restart.** If root processing fixed at least 5 % of the integer variables, the search is aborted, presolving is reapplied and the search starts again (p. 9). The paper claims that restarts "have not been used so far for solving MIPs", without a citation (p. 9).
  - **The property-checking solver** (pp. 10–13):
    - a CIP on bit vectors at register-transfer level, with linking constraints (2) and 22 circuit operations (pp. 10–11);
    - CP: a propagator per operation, the mult propagator using term algebra on its partial-product network (p. 11);
    - IP: linearizations (Table 3), with vectors split into 16-bit words, and none for shl, slice, read and write (p. 12);
    - SAT: 1-FUIP conflicts plus reconvergence clauses for non-chronological backtracking (p. 13).

- **D3 Problem class & data model**:
  - **The definition, revised.** The constraints are now Cᵢ: Rⁿ → {0, 1}, and condition (1) asks for some A′ ∈ R^(k×C) (p. 4). The completeness sentence now reads "in the case of finite domain integer variables, the problem can be—in principle—completely solved by enumerating …" (p. 4). R003 had no such qualifier (R003 p. 8).
  - **The Proposition** is stated without a proof; the paragraph before it gives the argument (p. 4).
  - **MIP test set.** MIPLIB 2003 without arki001, protfold and timtab1, on which at least one solver returned a wrong answer or an error (p. 9). That leaves 57 instances: 36 in Table 1, where at least one solver proved optimality, and 21 in Table 2, where all hit a limit (pp. 9–11; count mine).
  - **Property checking.** The valid properties of three industrial sets from projects with Infineon and OneSpin Solutions (pp. 13–14):
    - ALU (add, sub, shl, shr, signed and unsigned mult);

    - Multiplier (Booth and non-Booth, signed and unsigned).

- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design**: proofs: n/a; the Proposition is argued, not proved (p. 4). The devices:
  - the NP-hardness of minimal bound-IIS is restated, with a greedy dual-ray heuristic (p. 6, citing R004's journal version);
  - a numerical device: very large coefficients, such as 2^(w_r) in the add linearization, are avoided by 16-bit words. The overflow bit of one word goes into the next word's right-hand side (p. 12);
  - a size device: no linearization where it would swamp the LP. A straightforward shl linearization on a 64-bit input already needs 30,944 inequalities and 20,929 auxiliary variables (p. 12);
  - the 5 % root-restart threshold (p. 9).
