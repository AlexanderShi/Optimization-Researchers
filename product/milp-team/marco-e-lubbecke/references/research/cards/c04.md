### S010 · Curriculum based course timetabling: new solutions to Udine benchmark instances (Annals of Operations Research 194(1) 2012, DOI 10.1007/s10479-010-0700-7)
**Read level**: full. All 18 pages read, including Tables 1-8. References (pp. 17-18) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Gerald Lach (first author, TU Berlin). Lübbecke was then at TU Darmstadt (p. 1). · **Place in trajectory**: the TU Berlin applications period, before the RWTH chair. This is the "how to make it useful in practice" sequel of the WEA 2008 companion paper on the partial transversal polytope (S028, supplement batch s02, not read here; p. 2). Five years later he comments on the field alone (S048) and calls this two-stage method "a Benders' decomposition" (S048 p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a public benchmark. Timetabling has a large heuristic literature, and "practical data is available for benchmarking": the Udine instances and ITC2007's extended definitions with more soft constraints (p. 2). The claim to novelty: "for the first time" all four original Udine instances are solved to proven optimality. test4 had been unsolved within the given limits (pp. 2, 10). The authors concede up front that the instances "became rather easy for modern integer programming solvers" (p. 2). The method was "originally meant" for TU Berlin, where all constraints are hard. Feedback on the 2008 version then motivated adding soft constraints and lower bounds (pp. 3, 15). The problem statement: assign every lecture to a period and a room, with hard teacher, room and curriculum conflicts and four penalised soft-constraint types (RC, MWD, CC, RS), using the ITC2007 definitions and penalty weights (pp. 2, 4).
  - Transferable: enter an applied field through its public benchmark, and name the open instance you close (test4, p. 10). Admit that solver progress may explain part of the result, then test that admission (pp. 2, 10-11).
- **D2 Core idea**: Do not solve the natural three-index (course, period, room) model. Decompose in two stages (p. 2). Stage 1 assigns lectures to periods. For each period's bipartite course-room graph it imposes Hall's condition (3), "|Γ(U)| ≥ |U|", so that a room assignment is guaranteed to exist (p. 3). Stage 2 is a minimum-weight bipartite perfect matching per period, solvable in polynomial time (p. 3). Both stages are "exact with respect to hard constraints, that is, no solutions are lost" (p. 2). Changed: the room dimension is projected out, and time conflicts become a stable-set conflict graph (p. 3). Kept: an off-the-shelf MIP solver for stage 1 (CPLEX 11.0.1, p. 9). Soft constraints are added where they fit. RC is handled by modifying Hall's conditions with room-capacity classes, MWD and CC by indicator variables in stage 1, and RS only in stage 2 (pp. 4-6). RS breaks the matching structure ("integrality of solutions is lost") and global optimality: "It is therefore possible (and it happens) that the obtained solution is not a globally optimal one" (p. 6).
  - Transferable: project out a dimension whose feasibility a combinatorial theorem characterises (Hall), keep the theorem's inequalities in the master stage, and leave a polynomial second stage (pp. 2-3).
  - Transferable: keep a projected model small by aggregating identical resources into classes. Rooms of equal capacity share one capacity class, so the three-index y variables appear only per class (p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: A binary IP (plus integer w_c, p. 7) with an exponential family of Hall inequalities (3). How to handle them, "and why in practice there are not too many of them", is deferred to the companion paper (p. 3). In the experiments a preprocessing step generates "only the actually needed Hall conditions" (p. 15). Conflicts are pairwise packing constraints on a conflict graph (4) (p. 3). Data: the 4 Udine instances (basic formulation, no RS) and 14 ITC2007 instances (7 later ones were not available, p. 11). Also the 7 DDS instances of De Cesco et al. (p. 13, Table 6), and three simulated TU Berlin instances (small, medium, large up to 2,100 courses, 4,640 lectures and 345 rooms; Table 8, p. 17). The Berlin instances were made by a simulation tool because the real database "is in an incomplete and inconsistent state", and are posted on the web (pp. 14-15). The exactness argument covers hard constraints only. With RS in stage 2 the method is a heuristic for the soft objective, and the claim "solution quality would not significantly decrease since the RS constraints are the least important soft constraints" is asserted, not measured (p. 4). Seven extensions are modelled (lunch break, curriculum patterns, dayload, room unavailability, room sizes, individual weights, teacher preferences; pp. 8-9). Consecutiveness in Hall's conditions is "too involved for the scope of this paper" (p. 8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Two IPs solved by CPLEX with default settings, with zero-half cuts on for the ITC2007 runs (pp. 11, 13). The time budget is split between the stages, e.g. 300 s and 80 s within one CPU unit (Table 3, p. 12). The stage-2 IP keeps the one-sided bipartite matching constraints (20)-(21) plus the RS linking (19) (p. 7). The correctness device is Hall's marriage theorem (p. 3). A self-check device is "the role of the solver" (pp. 10-11): with the "several years old" CPLEX 9 they still solve test1-3 and reach value 29 on test4 in about an hour. Proving it fails because "the lower bound does not improve at all", and they credit the zero-half cuts of later CPLEX versions (pp. 10-11). SCIP and CBC "could not match" CPLEX (p. 11). Ablation by constraint class: without the CC constraints the Udine instances solve in 0.08-0.17 s instead of up to 1,607 s. Their reading is that CC "appears to destroy the combinatorial structure of the timetabling problem the most" (Table 1, p. 10).
  - Transferable: to find out how much of a gain is the solver's, rerun your own model with an older release of the solver and with open-source solvers, and report which part of the result each one reproduces (pp. 10-11).
  - Transferable: drop one constraint class at a time and show the time collapse. This identifies which modelling feature destroys the structure your method uses (Table 1, p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: A 3.4 GHz Linux PC with 1 GB of memory and CPLEX 11.0.1 (p. 9); the thread count is not stated. Gaps are relative to the upper bound (p. 9). Every solution was checked with the benchmark site's validator (pp. 9-10). Time is normalised in "CPU time units": one ITC2007 run, about 400 s, calibrated with the site's machine benchmark. Competitor budgets are counted as runs × units: the ITC2007 winner's best of 10 runs is 10 units, the best of all five finalists is 5·10 units, and SaTT's 40 runs are 40 units. The authors' own code gets 10 units, justified by determinism (p. 10). Competitors are not rerun: their numbers come from the competition and from papers, and Burke et al.'s lower bounds are "taken from a first draft ... updated results are not available to us" (Table 4 caption, p. 14). My tally of Table 2 (p. 11) covers the extended formulation on the 13 instances other than comp11, which is 0 throughout. The authors' code beats the winner's average run on 11 and loses on 2 (comp01, comp12), as the text says ("always (except twice)", p. 11). It beats the winner's best run on 8 of 13 and the best of all five finalists on 6, losing on 7. Lower bounds after 30 min exceed Burke et al.'s draft numbers on 9 of 14 instances, are lower on 3 and tie on 2 (Table 4, p. 14; my tally). The LP relaxation solves in 0.09-2.92 s against 3.58-192.35 s (Table 4). Those times come from different papers, and the formulation has "about a factor of two to three" fewer constraints and variables (p. 13). On the DDS instances the extended-formulation objective falls from 1024 to 132 (DDS1) and from 261 to 68 (DDS4) (Table 6, p. 15). With the dayload and lunch-break extensions, 7 of 11 instances stay merely feasible, and 5 of them have gaps of 70-99% (Table 5, p. 14; my count). Losses stay in the tables (comp01: 13 against 5; comp12: 405 against 333; p. 11). There is one deterministic run per instance, so no seeds are involved.
  - Transferable: when a deterministic code meets randomized competitors, give it the total budget a competitor spent across all its seeds, name the unit, and print the competitor's average run next to its best run (pp. 10-11).
  - Transferable: validate every reported solution with the benchmark's public validator and offer the solution files on request (pp. 9-10).
- **D6 Positioning**: Continues the integer-programming line (Burke et al., Carter, Daskalaki et al., Qualizza-Serafini, Schimmelpfeng-Helber; p. 2) against the heuristic mainstream. The authors claim to "very well compete with, and often beat" the tailored heuristics (p. 2). Burke et al.'s branch-and-cut is recognised as the other structure-exploiting work, but "is not yet suited to produce feasible integer solutions" (p. 13). The authors read IP's past role in timetabling as "formal and precise identification of relevant decisions and constraints" rather than computation (p. 15).
- **D7 Writing**: Two registers. The abstract sizes the claim: the algorithm "is not an overall winner" but robust (p. 1). The introduction is bolder: it can "often beat" the strongest heuristics (p. 2), while against the best finalist result the record is 6 wins and 7 losses (Table 2; my tally). The reader is guided: "if you are in a rush, the most important conclusions can be drawn from Tables 2, 4, 6, and 8" (p. 10). Limitations are stated where they arise, not collected at the end (RS, pp. 4, 6; CC, pp. 5, 10). The closing remains a belief: optimal or high-quality timetables "will not be computed with off-the-shelf solvers, but will call for tailored algorithms" (p. 15). The final self-assessment is hedged: "not best in all possible situations" (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is mainly algorithmic and applied: proven optima for Udine test1-4, competitive hard-feasible ITC2007 solutions with lower bounds, better DDS results, and a modelling kit for further soft constraints (pp. 10-16). The approach is said to serve as "a very robust starting point" (p. 16). Limits named: RS costs optimality and integrality (pp. 4, 6); CC hurts times (p. 10); the dependence on a commercial solver threatens reproducibility, which is why they "did not submit our results to the ITC2007 competition" (p. 11); interactive use "would need some tuning" (p. 15). Future work: exploit the structure hidden in the soft constraints (p. 15), and warm-start timetables "in such a way, that small changes in the input result in small changes" (pp. 16-17). Robust optimization for that is "beyond the scope" (p. 17). Not measured: the natural three-index model, which the paper says is harder ("results in easier integer programs", p. 2) without running it here.

**Method links**:
- Method 1, variant ⚠: the decomposition is justified against the natural three-index model (p. 2), but this paper runs no compact-model baseline in a current solver. The yardstick is the competition record. The decomposition is a projection, which Lübbecke later calls Benders (S048 p. 2), not Dantzig-Wolfe. The closing belief in "tailored algorithms" over off-the-shelf solvers (p. 15) is stated, not measured against the compact model.
- Method 3, evidence ✅ and variant ⚠: the solver-attribution reruns (CPLEX 9, SCIP, CBC; pp. 10-11) and the visible losses in Table 2 match steps 3 and 5. The variant is that competitors' numbers are borrowed and normalised by CPU units rather than rerun under identical conditions; the text says so (pp. 10, 14). Step 6 is mixed: "not an overall winner" (p. 1) against "often beat" (p. 2) with 6 of 13 wins against the best finalist.
- Taste B1 (penalty-weighted objective), practice before precept: here the authors optimise exactly the ITC penalty sum (5·w_c, 2·v_cu,p; pp. 5-6). In 2015 he calls such objectives "apples and oranges" (S048 p. 2). He adopted a given benchmark definition, so this reads as a later warning, not a contradiction.
- Taste G4 ✅: the matching structure is exploited, and the measured loss of structure under CC (p. 10) is the evidence.
- New-pattern candidates: (1) "exact projection by a combinatorial feasibility theorem" (Hall's condition; pp. 2-3). (2) "budget parity for deterministic vs randomized competitors" (p. 10). (3) "modelled is not solved": the extensions can be written down (pp. 8-9) but leave 70-99% gaps (Table 5, p. 14); compare S048 p. 3. (4) "near-real instance generator when the real data are inconsistent" (p. 15); compare S025 pp. 15-16.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Project out a dimension through a combinatorial feasibility theorem (Hall's condition) so that the second stage becomes polynomial; generate only the inequalities actually needed (pp. 3, 15).
- Aggregate identical resources (rooms of equal capacity) into classes so that the projected model stays two-index (p. 4).
- Attribute the gain: rerun the same model with an older solver release and with open-source solvers (pp. 10-11).
- Drop one constraint class at a time to show which one destroys the structure (Table 1, p. 10).
- Against randomized competitors, give a deterministic method the competitor's total budget across seeds, in a named unit, and print both the competitor's average and its best run (pp. 10-11).
- When real data are incomplete, publish a simulator-built instance set "with near real-world character" (p. 15).
- Add a one-line reader's guide to the decisive tables (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "Our algorithm is not an overall winner, but it is very robust in the sense that it deterministically gives satisfactory lower and upper bounds in reasonable computation time without particular tuning." (p. 1)
- "Since in contrast our approach is entirely deterministic, it is fair to allow ourselves a solution time equivalent to what is used in total in the respective runs of these various groups." (p. 10)

### S015 · Automatic Dantzig-Wolfe Reformulation of Mixed Integer Programs (Mathematical Programming 149(1-2) 2015, DOI 10.1007/s10107-014-0761-5)
**Read level**: full. Pages 1-30 read: Sections 1-7, Tables 1-3, and Table 3 (p. 30), which I parsed row by row. Figures 1-16 are images in the extraction, so only captions and the text's descriptions were read. The per-instance appendix figures announced on p. 13 are not in this file (not read). References (pp. 28-29) skimmed. This is the 2013 preprint, not the typeset journal version.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Martin Bergner (first author, RWTH; DFG Priority Program "Algorithm Engineering" grants LU770/4-1 and 4-2), Alberto Caprara, Alberto Ceselli, Fabio Furini, Enrico Malaguti, Emiliano Traversi (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the journal version of IPCO 2011 (S032, batch c03; "A preliminary version of this paper appeared in [3]", p. 1). The code is the authors' own, built from CPLEX 12.2 and hMETIS, not GCG; GCG is cited only as another framework (pp. 3, 10). S009 (2017) takes up the open question this paper ends with (p. 27).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a consensus to be tested. Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation "is not implemented in any state-of-the-art MIP solver as it is considered to require structural problem knowledge and tailoring to this structure" (p. 1). "It is generally agreed that DWR needs tailoring". Measured against the automatic use of generic cutting planes, "this is unsatisfactory" (p. 2). The question: can the reformulation be automated for a general MIP "even when the latter seemingly does not expose the matrix structure" (p. 2)? And what makes a "good" decomposition? "it is not even known" (p. 4). Only a "computational proof-of-concept" is promised (pp. 1-2).
  - Transferable: open against a stated consensus and against the success of the rival generic tool (automatic cuts), then promise a proof of concept rather than a solver (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Any constraint matrix can be "forced" into arrowhead (double-bordered block-diagonal) form. "it cannot happen that there is no such structure to detect" (p. 7). Detection is a minimum-weight balanced k-partition of a row-net or a row-column-net hypergraph, solved heuristically (pp. 6-7). The reformulation keeps the original x variables in the master (an explicit master with linking variables, "a variant of Lagrangian decomposition", p. 5). Among many candidates, one is chosen by a proxy, the relative border area β (8) (p. 16). The proxy comes out of "a rigorous experimental study" (p. 2). Two old components, matrix re-ordering and generic DWR, "have not been combined in a MIP context before" (p. 2). The lesson from the IPCO version: "we are not looking for 'the' decomposition of a matrix" (p. 8). The temporal knapsack illustrates the forcing: no visible blocks in the original matrix, then a "detected" arrowhead with k = 10 (Fig. 2, pp. 7-8).
  - Transferable: keep the original variables in the master. Generic cuts, branching and preprocessing then work "without any additional implementation issues" (pp. 5-6).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: General MIP max{cᵗx : Ax ≤ b, Dx ≤ e, x ∈ Z^{n−r}×Q^r} (p. 4). The bound gain comes from partial convexification and exists when P_IP ⊊ P, citing Geoffrion (p. 4). The pricing problems are general MIPs, and "The effectiveness of the overall approach hinges crucially on our ability to solve this subproblem" (p. 4). Test set: 39 instances (23 from MIPLIB 2003, 16 from MIPLIB 2010; p. 24). They were filtered by density 0.05-5%, at most 20,000 nonzeros and at least 20% discrete variables, each criterion with its reason: too dense means no arrowhead, too large defeats the partitioner, too little integrality means no bound gain (pp. 8-9). Instances with known structure are excluded on purpose (p. 8). The decomposition sample has three hypergraph settings: no linking variables (k = 2..20), balanced (weights 1/2/5, k = 2..10) and few linking constraints (weights 1/2/10⁵, k = 2..10). Dummy vertices make up 20% of the nonzeros. This gives "around 1500 decompositions of 39 instances" (pp. 11-12); 39 × 37 = 1,443 by my arithmetic. The comparison with CPLEX assumes that apart from the bound "the remaining machinery in branch-and-price and branch-and-cut algorithms is equivalent" (p. 24), an assumption not tested.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: hMETIS with default parameters, "except the random seed that was set to 1" (p. 11). Pricing MIPs are solved by CPLEX 12.2 single-threaded. "Generic multiple pricing" adds every improving incumbent with negative reduced cost. An unbounded subproblem has its integrality relaxed and contributes an extreme ray (p. 10). Master LPs are solved by CPLEX simplex. Dual stabilisation follows du Merle et al.: the penalty is random in [0, 10⁻⁴] per dual and per iteration, δ := 0.00005·|z−v| capped at 50, and it is active on problems with more than 400 constraints when the gap is 1-80% ("these values gave best performance", p. 10). Only the root node is computed; there is no branching. "As we did not go for efficiency, no further performance improvement method was implemented" (p. 10). No new theorem. The reasoning devices are three. First, a degenerate extreme rules out the dual bound as the quality measure: it "can be trivially maximized by reformulating the whole original problem into a single subproblem" (p. 20). Second, two extremes bracket the time trend: an all-border matrix is an LP, a border-free one is the original MIP (p. 17). Third, an "Expectation k" derived from theory precedes each "Observation k" (pp. 13-21). Two automatic variants are compared: "DWR auto" (minimum border area) and "DWR best", the best bound over all candidates, which is "extremely time consuming and only meant as an assessment of the method's potential" (p. 22).
  - Transferable: rule out a candidate quality measure by its degenerate extreme before ranking candidates with it (p. 20).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Single thread on an i7-870 (2.93 GHz, 8 GB), Linux, 1 h limit (p. 10). Output measures: the root dual bound as gap closed, 1−|OPT−DWR|/|OPT−LP|, with OPT the best CPLEX solution in one hour, and the root column-generation time as a per-instance z-score (t−µ_h)/σ_h. Trends are LOESS fits with span 0.75 (p. 13). Input measures: direct (k, % linking variables, % linking constraints, border area) and indirect (block density; subproblem integrality gap with 60 s of CPLEX, "for a methodological insight only"; pp. 12, 19). Baseline: CPLEX 12.2 with full defaults, its root bound after cuts (pp. 22-23), and the time CPLEX needs, at the root or in the tree, to reach the DWR-auto bound, reported as a ratio (pp. 24-25). Dolan-Moré profiles cover time and bound (pp. 25-26). Results by border-area quartile: Q1 closes 71.97% of the gap against 12.80% in Q4 (Table 1, p. 21). The best-measure decomposition has linking constraints or linking variables but not both in 36 of 39 instances, and no linking variables in 26 of 39 (p. 21). The text says the "DWR approach" beats CPLEX's root bound on "16 out of the 23 MIPLIB 2003 and 11 out of the 16 MIPLIB 2010 instances" (p. 24). My count from Table 3 (p. 30) matches the DWR best column (16 of 23; 10 of 16); DWR auto wins on 12 of 23 and 8 of 16. Average gap closed: DWR auto 62.53% / 50.33%, CPLEX root 68.57% / 34.26%, DWR best 77.16% / 55.71% (MIPLIB 2003 / 2010; Table 3). Time: DWR auto reaches its bound faster than CPLEX on 2 of 23 and 1 of 16 instances (8.70%, 6.25%), and within a factor of 10² on 52.17% / 56.25% (Table 3; I rechecked the summary rows against the instance rows). In the profiles DWR auto is fastest on "around 8%", DWR best on "around 19%" (p. 25). Variability is called "very pronounced" (p. 3). It is shown as spread across decompositions (Figs. 13-14, pp. 23-24; sensitivity, p. 8), not across seeds or row permutations; the partitioner seed stays at 1 (p. 11). Two column generations did not finish (tr12-30, neos-686190); their best Lagrangian bound is reported instead (p. 24).
  - Transferable: print the a-priori rule (DWR auto) next to the ex-post best candidate (DWR best). The reader then sees the rule's quality and the method's ceiling separately (p. 22).
  - Transferable: compare a bound-only prototype with a full solver by how long the solver needs, at the root or in the tree, to reach the prototype's bound (p. 25).
  - Transferable: before pooling running times across instances in one scatter plot, standardise them per instance (z-score) (p. 13).
- **D6 Positioning**: The authors join matrix re-ordering from numerical linear algebra and parallel computing (Aykanat et al., Ferris-Horn, Weil-Kettler; the exact detection of Borndörfer et al.; p. 3) with generic DWR frameworks (BaPCod, DIP, G12, GCG). In all of those frameworks "the bordered block-diagonal matrix structure needs to be known and given to the algorithm by the user" (p. 3): "we provide the missing link" (p. 2). Tebboth's LP-only evaluation is called rudimentary (p. 4). The pattern of best decompositions is read as reproducing a modeller's intuition: Lagrangian relaxation against Lagrangian decomposition (p. 21). The field context: generic cuts have had "about 15 years" of head start, an outer-approximation "headway" over inner approximations (p. 27). Follow-up work is listed (DIP, SAS; p. 27).
- **D7 Writing**: The frame is hedged ("computational proof-of-concept", p. 1). Each analysis section pairs a pre-stated "Expectation" with an "Observation" and flags mismatches: Observation 7 "is not fully matching a modeler intuition", followed by "an attempt to explain" (p. 16). The headline words outrun the counts. The contribution list says the reformulation "consistently closes a significant portion of the integrality gap" (p. 2). It also says performance is "globally better" on "a non-negligible fraction" of instances (p. 3); that fraction is 3 of 39 (Table 3). The proxy "proves to be unbiased" because "no obvious correlation appears" in Table 2 (p. 21). The discussion sizes the work again ("not intended to produce a competitive tool", p. 27). It warns that the "seeming omnipresence of arrowhead structure" may "simply be an artifact of the model and algorithm we use" (p. 27). A referee is thanked for "a more meaningful experimental setup" (p. 28).
  - Transferable: before each measurement, write the direction theory predicts ("Expectation k"); afterwards, report "Observation k" and say whether it matched (pp. 13-21).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an experimental methodology plus a proxy (relative border area), and evidence that automatically forced DWR gives root bounds competitive with or stronger than CPLEX's on average (Table 3). It is not software: the code is a proof of concept, not GCG. Limits stated: root node only; "Only further experimentation can show whether the advantage in the root node can be retained throughout the search tree" (p. 27). The detection is "just one out of probably many conceivable approaches" (p. 27). The proxy design leaves balance and density open (p. 27). A theoretical a priori measure "currently appears to be out of reach" (p. 28). Future work: characterise a good decomposition (p. 27); add generic cuts (p. 27); "distinguish a priori, only based on the instance, whether it can pay to apply a decomposition" (p. 27), the question S009 takes up.

**Method links**:
- Method 2, evidence ✅: the option space is written down and computed. The authors fix the hypergraph type, weights and k, sample about 37 candidates per instance on real MIPLIB instances (step 3; pp. 11-12), and read the map for surprises ("not looking for 'the' decomposition", p. 8; Obs. 7, p. 16; step 4). They test six input measures against outcomes and keep one proxy (step 5; pp. 12-21).
- Method 3, evidence ✅ for steps 1-3: instances "for which a decomposition method would not be the first choice" (p. 1), selected by stated filters (pp. 8-9); CPLEX rerun on the same machine and limit (pp. 10, 22). Variant ⚠ for step 6: "consistently", "globally better" on 3 of 39, and the 16/11 count that belongs to DWR best but is written as "our DWR approach" (p. 24 against Table 3). There is no second, general test set, because this is a bound study. Variability is described but has no seed protocol (pp. 3, 11), as the skill's Method 3 limitation says.
- Method 1, evidence ✅: step 6 (original variables kept in the master for cuts and branching, pp. 5-6) and step 2 (CPLEX defaults on the original MIP as the yardstick, pp. 22-23). The stop rule appears as an instance filter: without enough integrality "no gains in the dual bound are possible" (p. 9).
- H1, evidence ✅: generic multiple pricing returns every improving column found (p. 10).
- Taste G4 ✅: the artifact warning (p. 27) is the source of the skill's quotation.
- For the synthesis: the skill's Signature Work Anatomy line for this paper ("dynamic knapsack in minutes where CPLEX 12.2 hit the hour"; "eight MIPLIB 2003 instances") is not in this 2015 text. Here the temporal knapsack is only cited from [5] (p. 8), and 12 (auto) or 16 (best) of 23 MIPLIB 2003 instances beat CPLEX's root bound (Table 3). Those numbers probably come from IPCO 2011 (S032; not checked).
- New-pattern candidates: "Expectation-then-Observation reporting" (pp. 13-21). "Oracle column next to the rule": DWR best here (p. 22), OPT in S009 (pp. 5-6). "Rule out a metric by its degenerate extreme" (p. 20).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Keep original variables in the reformulated master so that generic MIP machinery keeps working (pp. 5-6).

### S025 · An adaptive discretization MINLP algorithm for optimal synthesis of decentralized energy supply systems (Computers & Chemical Engineering 95 2016, DOI 10.1016/j.compchemeng.2016.09.008)
**Read level**: full. Preprint pp. 1-28 read, including Appendix A (equipment and cost models, pp. 26-28). Figures 1-8 are images: the heatmap cell values of Figs. 6-7 and the curves of Fig. 8 were not read, only their captions and the text's summary. References (pp. 21-25) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Sebastian Goderbauer (first author, OR and Mathematics II, RWTH), Björn Bahl, Philip Voll and André Bardow (Technical Thermodynamics), Arie M.C.A. Koster (corresponding author) (p. 1). Lübbecke is fourth of six. Per SKILL.md Academic Lineage he was first referee of Goderbauer's 2019 doctorate. · **Place in trajectory**: an RWTH interdisciplinary energy-systems collaboration, outside his decomposition core. The companion test library DESSLib (Bahl et al. 2016) is S055/S081 (batch s05, not read here).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The application drives the problem. Synthesising a decentralized energy supply system means choosing the structure (which units and how many), the sizing and the operation simultaneously, because "only a simultaneous optimization will find a global optimal solution" (p. 2). The incumbent approaches fall short. Metaheuristics "do not provide any measure of optimality" (p. 3). Piecewise-linearised MILPs give "only ... approximated solutions" (p. 3), and in this study their designs are infeasible in the MINLP on 273 of 320 instances (p. 19). BARON "reach[es] computational limits for relative small test cases" (p. 4). "For several nontrivial test instances it, is even hard for solvers to compute a feasible solution", so "the need of a problem-specific solution method providing primal MINLP solutions is evident" (p. 8).
  - Transferable: before proposing a fix, measure how often the field's standard shortcut gives designs that are infeasible in the true model (273 of 320, p. 19).
- **D2 Core idea**: Approximate, fix, repair, refine (pp. 8-15). Unit sizes are discretised into k_max (odd) equidistant points, so the nonlinear investment cost of each point is computed in advance (pp. 8-9). Operation levels are discretised into j_max ≥ 2 points and linearised piecewise between them, so the energy balances can still hold with equality (p. 10). This gives a MILP (16)-(26). The sizes and on/off decisions of its solution are then fixed. The remaining problem splits into one nonconvex NLP per load case, NLP_ℓ, solved by BARON (p. 12). Finally the size grid is adapted around the chosen point: contracted at an interior point, shifted at an interval end, contracted within the bounds at a bound (pp. 12-13). The MILP is warm-started because "the discrete decisions are still feasible in the adapted grid" (p. 15). Compared with prior discretisation approaches (Leyffer et al. 2008, Geißler et al. 2011, Gupte et al. 2013 and others), the repair in the original MINLP and the adaptive grid are new (p. 8). Kept: CPLEX for the MILP, BARON for the NLPs (p. 16).
  - Transferable: refine a discretisation by contracting around the chosen point with an odd number of points. The incumbent then stays a grid point and warm-starts the next MILP (pp. 13, 15).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: A nonconvex MINLP. It has bilinear terms in the objective and the balances, nonconvex investment-cost functions and nonlinear equality balances (p. 7), binaries for selection and on/off status, and minimum part-load constraints (8) (pp. 6-7). The superstructure allows several units of one type, "in strong contrast to classical process synthesis problems" (p. 4). Data: DESSLib (Bahl et al. 2016) has 320 instances in 32 categories (S4, S8, S12, S16 units × L1-L24 load cases). Each category has 10 instances made by Latin hypercube sampling with ±5% demand variation around one real-world case, Voll et al. 2013 (pp. 15-16). The equipment models come from measured CHP data, the literature and industrial partners. They assume "the part-load operation is not depending on the size of equipment" (p. 26). There is no guarantee that the repair succeeds: "It is not guaranteed that NLPℓ provides a feasible solution" (p. 12).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: GAMS 24.4.3 through its Python API, CPLEX 12.6.1.0 and BARON 14.4.0, all at default settings (p. 16). The parameters k_max = 5 and j_max = 10, reduced so that operation steps stay at least 200 kW, were "determined in preliminary studies" (p. 16). Limits: the MILP gets 300 s in the first iteration and 100 s with a warm start afterwards; each NLP gets 100 s; gap tolerances are 0.1% and 0.001%. The algorithm stops at 1 h or at less than 0.1% improvement over two iterations (p. 16). Preprocessing removes operation binaries whose output exceeds the demand, "its effect may not be underestimated" (p. 17). Invariants: with k_max odd the chosen size stays a grid point after contraction (p. 13); the shifted grid "respects the initial size bounds" (p. 13); the warm start stays feasible (p. 15). Abandoned paths: a pure discretisation that only requires demands to be met suffers energy excess from CHP economics. Two remedies, extra constraints and an excess penalty, are "outperform[ed]" by the piecewise-linear version. No numbers are given (Remark 1, p. 15).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: One core of an i7-3770 (3.40 GHz, 32 GB) (p. 16). Baselines, all run by the authors on that machine: BARON on the MINLP (1 h), and the Voll et al. MILP (1 h) followed by the repair MINLP_lin,feas (1 h), which keeps the MILP's structure and on/off decisions and minimises the normalised distance to its sizes and operation (pp. 7, 16). Metrics: the primal gap to a reference solution (Definition 1, p. 17), and shifted geometric means with shift 100 for seconds and percent and 10 for iteration counts, citing Achterberg 2007 (p. 17). Per-category heatmaps report averages over the instances where both methods found a solution, with the solved counts in brackets (pp. 17-18). Results: BARON solves S4L{1, 2, 4, 6} to optimality and finds a feasible solution on 202 of 320 instances (63%). The adaptive algorithm finds one on all 320 (p. 18) and is "up to 40%" better except in the smallest categories (p. 19). On S4L6 it is 0.1% better than BARON's "globally optimal" solutions. The authors attribute this to BARON cutting off optimal solutions because of limited machine accuracy; no check is reported (pp. 18-19). Against the linearisation pipeline it is "comparably good or slightly better" (p. 20). It needs "a fraction of an hour" against 1 h (BARON) and up to 2 h (MILP plus repair). 93% of its time goes to the MILP, and it averages 5 iterations (p. 20). Not done: no dual bounds; only one MINLP solver, chosen "due its robustness in a preliminary study" that is not reported (p. 16); no variability runs. The text and the caption of Fig. 6 disagree on the order of the bracketed counts (p. 17: AdaptDiscAlgo left; p. 18: MINLP left).
  - Transferable: when averaging over the instances both methods solved, print each method's solved count beside every average so that failures stay visible (pp. 17-18).
  - Transferable: build the test library as a grid of size categories × load cases with ten perturbed copies of one real case per cell (Latin hypercube, ±5%), and publish it (pp. 15-16).
- **D6 Positioning**: Placed in process systems engineering (Papoulias-Grossmann 1983, Bruno et al. 1998, Varbanov et al. 2004-05, Voll et al. 2012-13; pp. 3-4) and in MINLP discretisation and piecewise-linear methods (Leyffer et al. 2008, Geißler et al. 2011, Gupte et al. 2013, Kolodziej et al. 2013; p. 8). - **D7 Writing**: The abstract is broader than the results. It claims to outperform "state-of-the-art MINLP solvers as well as linearization approaches with regard to solution quality and computation times" (p. 1). In fact one MINLP solver was run (p. 16), quality against the linearisation is "comparably good or slightly better" (p. 20), and the outperformance holds "except for the very smallest test instances" (p. 20). The preprint keeps an editing placeholder ("?unlesbar?", German for "illegible", p. 2) and typos ("outperformes", p. 1).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an applied algorithm, a problem-tailored primal method for nonconvex DESS synthesis, plus a public instance set (pp. 15, 21). The method is called "an efficient solution method" (p. 21). Limits stated: no optimality guarantee ("can not ensure optimality", p. 18); repair feasibility is not guaranteed (p. 12); BARON's dual bounds are "mostly very weak" (p. 21).
