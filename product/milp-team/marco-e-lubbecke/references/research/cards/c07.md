### S050 · A branch-and-price algorithm for capacitated hypergraph vertex separation (Mathematical Programming Computation 2020, doi:10.1007/s12532-019-00171-5)
**Read level**: full (the preprint version, see the header). Main text §§1–4 with every proof (pp. 1–17) read completely, references (pp. 17–18) skimmed; Appendix A read completely for its text (A.1 proof of Prop. 8, A.2 fallback branching, A.4 feature impact, A.5 observations by test set, A.6 table legend; pp. 22–23, 26–28); the instance tables of A.3 (pp. 23–26) and the result Tables 5–12 (pp. 33–42) skimmed, the netlib and miplib tables (pp. 33–35, 41–42) row by row; Figures 1–22 legible as captions and axis labels only. Checked on p. 1: the title, and "MARCO E. LUBBECKE" (umlaut lost in extraction) as second author.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michael Bastubbe (RWTH, first author; by S005's contribution statement the redesigner of GCG 3.0's detection, card c06, S005 p. 39) · **Place in trajectory**: experiments with SCIP 3.2.0 and CPLEX 12.6.1 (p. 11); the latest work cited is a 2017 Optimization Online report on "The vertex k-cut problem" by Cornaz, Furini et al. (ref. [10], p. 17), apparently the problem the 2026 preprint arXiv:2602.04984 returns to (SKILL.md Signature Work Anatomy; link [inferred]). It sits in the structure-detection line: the stated main motivation is the matrix decomposition behind Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation, cited to Bergner et al. 2015 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The CHVS asks for a minimum-cardinality vertex set whose removal leaves at most k shores (k a number or ∞) of at most u vertices each, with no hyperedge meeting two shores (p. 1); it is NP-hard and, by Bui and Jones, hard to approximate within an additive term even on graphs of maximum degree three with k = 2 (p. 1). Entry by application: "Our main motivation for studying the CHVS comes from a matrix decomposition problem" (p. 1): rows are vertices, columns are nets, the border is the separator, and the result is single-bordered block-diagonal form, used in parallel QR factorization and for "determining how to apply Dantzig-Wolfe reformulations to mixed-integer linear programs [6]" (p. 1; [6] = Bergner et al. 2015, p. 17). Entry by a failure of the existing models: the only exact approach, Borndörfer, Ferreira and Martin's branch-and-cut on an assignment model (B), is symmetric in k, so "even the tailored approach struggles with larger k"; Oosten et al. assume k = ∞; of Cornaz et al.'s formulations: "Yet, they can also hardly handle larger k." (p. 2). The paper aims at the range of k that all of them miss (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: locate the gap in a parameter range, not only in a problem: name the index (here k) in which the standard model is symmetric and in which every existing exact code fails, and aim the reformulation at that range (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Start from a compact model (P) with shore-assignment variables x and hyperedge-touch variables y (p. 3), and name its two drawbacks: a uniform fractional assignment already attains the trivial bounds, and every permutation of shore indices gives another solution (p. 3). Dantzig–Wolfe-reformulate (P) with (P.2)–(P.3) of each shore as a subproblem; the k identical subproblems are aggregated into one, which removes the symmetry, and (P.1) stays as master: the shore-based model (M) with one shore per hyperedge (M.1) and at most k shores (M.2), exponentially many variables, solved by branch-and-price (pp. 3–4). Branch on aggregated original variables z_v = Σ_{R∋v} λ_R, for which it is "remarkable" that this works well (p. 3), although the rule is incomplete in theory (Example 1, Fig. 2, p. 4); integral z is completed by a bin-packing argument (Props. 2 and 5, pp. 4–5; Algorithm 1, p. 6) and, for theory, by a Ryan–Foster fallback (App. A.2, pp. 22–23). Pricing, a variant of the Next Release Problem (p. 7), is NP-hard (Prop. 7), polynomial without the cardinality bound by a minimum cut (Prop. 8, after Barahona and Jensen; App. A.1, p. 22), and preprocessed by Prop. 9 (pp. 7–8); it runs as a cascade of greedy, hill-climbing and an exact binary program, with complementary restarts (pp. 8–9, 11). Around it: two-phase hypergraph preprocessing (pp. 9–10), a randomized-rounding primal heuristic (p. 10), and exchange vectors (dual-optimal inequalities), proved valid but switched off by default (pp. 10–11). What changed against Borndörfer et al.: the variable, a whole shore instead of a vertex–shore pair, and with it the symmetry; what stayed: an exact LP-based tree, here inside SCIP (pp. 2–3, 11).
  - Transferable: when a Dantzig–Wolfe reformulation has identical blocks, aggregate them and branch on the aggregated original variable; then prove that an integral aggregated solution can be disaggregated by an auxiliary problem with a known LP-rounding property, and keep a classical rule only as a fallback for completeness (pp. 4–6, 22–23).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pure binary programs throughout: (B) (pp. 1–2), (P) (p. 3), the master (M) with one packing row per hyperedge and one cardinality row (p. 4), and the pricing program (Pr) (p. 9). Stated assumptions: no isolated vertices, w.l.o.g. (p. 1); in pricing p_v > 0 and c_e ≥ 0, the latter since β_e ≥ 0 (p. 7); the binary program (Pr) is stated for c_e ≥ 0 (p. 9). The completeness argument holds "under mild assumptions" (p. 4): the bin-packing instance of the components must have the integer round-up property (Prop. 5, p. 5); whether this can fail here "is an open question" (p. 6), and Remark 6 recalls that non-IRUP bin-packing instances exist (Marcotte 1986; Kartak et al.: from 10 items on) (p. 6). Data: 182 instances in four sets: 55 netlib LP basis matrices with more than 50 and at most 500 rows (those also used by Borndörfer et al.), 40 DIMACS colouring graphs with 20 to 500 vertices, 37 coefficient matrices of MIPLIB 2010 instances presolved by SCIP 3.2.0 (no selection rule stated; Table 3 lists 19 to 498 rows), and 50 random hypergraphs in 10 groups (p. 12; Tables 1–4, pp. 24–26). Capacity u := ⌈|V|/k⌉ for fixed k (p. 12); for k = ∞, u ∈ {4, 12, 36, ⌈|V|/4⌉, ⌈1.05|V|/2⌉} (p. 14). Structure exploited: hyperedge conflicts, which act only through the clique graph G(H) (pp. 9–10), and identical subproblems (p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Aggregation of k identical subproblems into one, eliminating symmetry (p. 3); the restricted master is feasible with no columns at all (p. 4).
  - Branching on z_v ∈ {0, 1} (vertex in the separator or in a shore), with an explicit incompleteness example: a basic solution with λ = 0.5 on four columns and z integral (Example 1, Fig. 2, p. 4).
  - Prop. 2: if z is integral, every connected component of the induced hypergraph has at most u vertices; proof by chaining tight packing rows along hyperedges, so all vertices of a component lie in the same positive columns (p. 5).
  - Prop. 5: map λ to a fractional Gilmore–Gomory solution of the bin-packing instance of the components; with the integer round-up property, k ≥ ⌈w_λ⌉ ≥ ⌈w*_LP⌉ = w*, so an integral solution of equal value exists (p. 5); Algorithm 1 finds it or reports failure (p. 6), which "in our numerous computational tests" never happened (p. 6).
  - Fallback same/differ branching after Ryan and Foster: Prop. 11 guarantees a vertex pair with fractional joint coverage; pricing handles "same" by merging the two vertices with added weights and "differ" by a conflict row x_v1 + x_v2 ≤ 1; pricing preprocessing is switched off under such decisions (pp. 22–23).
  - NP-hardness of pricing by a reduction from Clique with unit hyperedge costs and "irresistable" vertex profits |E| + 1 (Prop. 7, p. 7).
  - Polynomial uncapacitated pricing by a minimum s–t cut with capacities M = Σ_v p_v + 1 on the vertex–net arcs (Prop. 8; proof in App. A.1, p. 22).
  - Prop. 9: some capacitated optimum lies inside any uncapacitated optimum, shown with an exchange inequality on the intersection and the union of the two optima (pp. 7–8); used to drop, for one pricing call, every vertex outside the uncapacitated optimum (p. 8).
  - Heuristic pricing: a greedy by marginal profit that adds every intermediate set with positive reduced cost, so several columns per call (Alg. 2, p. 8); shotgun steepest-ascent hill-climbing over four neighbourhoods (vertex symmetric difference 1, hyperedge symmetric difference 1–3, the last explored at most once per run) (Alg. 3, pp. 8–9); exact pricing by (Pr) (p. 9). Order greedy, hill-climbing, exact; the cascade stops at the first algorithm that finds a column (p. 11).
  - Complementary pricing: after a column R is found, rerun the same algorithm on the hypergraph induced by V \ R, in rounds (default at most 8; hill-climbing restarted three times per round), because complementary sets "are supposed to combine well to integer feasible solutions" (pp. 9, 11; after Ghoniem and Sherali).
  - Hypergraph preprocessing: phase 1 removes contained and duplicate hyperedges; phase 2 replaces the hyperedges by a clique edge cover of G(H) found with the Kou–Stockmeyer–Wong heuristic, accepted only if the number of hyperedges drops; same clique graph, same integer solutions, possibly a different LP bound (pp. 9–10, 14).
  - Primal heuristic: randomized rounding of z with an offset δ drawn from six values, 200 runs, after each node and every 50 column-generation iterations, with Algorithm 1 as the feasibility check (p. 10).
  - Exchange vectors ν_e (removing a hyperedge with its vertices from a shore), equivalently the dual-optimal inequalities β_e ≤ |e|; validity (z_MLP = z_AMLP) by contradiction on an optimal dual (Prop. 10, pp. 10–11); disabled by default (p. 11).
  - Implementation in SCIP 3.2.0, with CPLEX 12.6.1 (single thread, dual simplex) for the IP subproblems; "Its default settings were derived in extensive experiments with the described features, see Appendix A.4." (p. 11).
  - Transferable: preprocess an NP-hard pricing problem with its polynomial relaxation (drop the cardinality bound, solve a minimum cut) and exclude everything outside that optimum, after proving that some optimum of the hard problem lies inside it (Props. 8–9, pp. 7–8, 22).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Baseline: the compact model (P), "also solved by CPLEX 12.6.1 under exactly the same conditions"; Intel Core i7-2600 with 16 GB, openSUSE 12.1, Linux 3.1.10, time limit 1800 s (p. 11). Sweep over k ∈ {4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 16, 24, 32, 64, 128, 256} and five capacities for k = ∞ (pp. 12–14, 28). Metrics: share solved to optimality by test set, by clique-graph density and by capacity (Figs. 3, 5, 7), share of instances above gap thresholds σ (Fig. 4), Dolan–Moré performance profiles (Figs. 6, 8, 9, 15–22), the shifted geometric mean (shift 1) of the root integrality gap (Fig. 10), and per-instance times, TL/ML markers and best values (Tables 5–12, legend p. 28) (pp. 12–16, 27–42); no shifted geometric mean of time and no node counts. Results with losses: for k < 8 cplex is better, at k = 8 the two are similar, for k > 8 base wins; base gets easier and cplex harder as k grows (up to k = 32) (p. 13); for k ≤ 12 every test set keeps unsolved instances, and about 5% of the instances stay above a 200% gap for every k (p. 12); on miplib cplex "seems to perform better" for k ∈ {4, 6, 8, 10}, with both solving about 20% at k = 10 (p. 28); at k = 32 base is "at least six times faster than cplex on all netlib instances" (p. 28). Ablations at k ∈ {4, 8, 12} on all instances: phase-2 preprocessing solves 10 to 20 percent more instances than phase 1 alone, and the clique-graph (edge) formulation is far worse (p. 14); pricing preprocessing helps slightly, mainly at k = 8 (p. 26); exchange vectors make performance slightly worse (p. 26); complementary pricing helps (p. 27); removing heuristic pricers is plotted without a verbal conclusion (p. 27). Competitors not re-run: Borndörfer et al.'s code is dropped because "a comparison between base and the algorithm in [7] is obsolete" once cplex beats base at k = 4 (pp. 13–14); against Oosten et al. the paper uses their published results (base solves all their netlib instances within 25 s in total, three of which they did not solve within 60 min), with no word on hardware (p. 14); Cornaz et al. are not run (p. 2). Not done: no seeds or repeated runs, although the hill-climber starts from random solutions and the primal heuristic rounds at random (pp. 8, 10); the experiments the defaults come from (App. A.4) use the same instances as the evaluation, with no held-out set (pp. 11, 26–28).
  - Transferable: sweep the structural parameter (here k from 4 to 256) against the compact model in a commercial solver and report where the compact model wins, by test set, with per-instance tables in an appendix (pp. 13, 27–28, 33–42).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Close the completeness gap of branching on aggregated variables with identical subproblems: show that integral aggregated values split into components within capacity, pack the components by bin packing, use the integer round-up property for the count, and keep Ryan–Foster as a fallback (pp. 4–6, 22–23).
- Preprocess the pricing problem with a polynomial relaxation whose optimum provably contains an optimum of the hard problem (Props. 8–9, pp. 7–8).
- Reuse one feasibility routine twice: Algorithm 1 completes branching and checks randomized roundings in the primal heuristic (pp. 6, 10).
- Complementary pricing: after a column is found, rerun the pricer on the complement of its vertex set for columns that combine into integer solutions (p. 9).
- Re-encode conflicts by a smaller clique edge cover with the same clique graph (same integer solutions, possibly a stronger LP), and measure the LP effect by the root gap across the encodings (pp. 9–10, 14, 16).

### S091 · Welcome to SN Operations Research Forum! (SN Operations Research Forum 2020, doi:10.1007/s43069-020-0001-1)
**Read level**: full. All 3 pages read completely, including the funding and licence notes (p. 3). Checked on p. 1: the title, and "Marco Lübbecke" as first and corresponding author, with Panos M. Pardalos.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Opens on the field, its essence being "to quantitatively support decisions" (p. 1), then on the colleagues' question "why another journal?" (p. 1). The answer is an evolution in three parts: of the field (synergy with artificial intelligence, machine learning and data science), of scientific culture, and of how research is shared (p. 1); classical journals "are more challenged by the increasing diversity in what we consider a research result and how we share such results with the community" (p. 1): software, tools, blogs about failures or projects, data and results, coding challenges, educational videos (p. 1), much of it found only on social media, personal websites, repositories and preprint archives (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open a position text with the reader's sceptical question and answer it in numbered parts ("Number one is obvious.", p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: A journal as a "Forum", "a community effort" (p. 2), that widens what counts as a result: besides original articles, surveys and reviews, book and event reviews, short communications, trends and perspectives and opinion pieces, formats "that may have a hard time getting published elsewhere": interdisciplinary work, impact in practice and "articles that describe the hurdles of putting operations research in practice", "failed experiments and wrong paths taken to learn from", trends, science for the general public (also as blog or video), and "descriptions of research software and how to build and use tools" (p. 2). Norms for computational work: "making all data publicly available, ideally also the code, in order to help achieve reproducibility of results"; synthetic stand-in data when the data are proprietary; application papers with explanatory material on implications, significance or impact (p. 2). Ideas not yet implemented: pre-registration of experimental studies, in which a study is accepted on its question and published whatever the outcome, adapted to computational OR; two-way communication on articles; versioning (p. 2). Access and process: hybrid, not open access, with content of the first two years free and SharedIt sharing (pp. 2–3); classical anonymous peer review, no trade of quality for speed, immediate assignment of accepted papers to issues, post-publication review perhaps later (p. 3); reform "from within the current system" with a traditional publisher (p. 3).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Entry by measured obsolescence of MIPLIB 2010: by the end of 2018 its open instances had fallen from 134 to 70, and the shifted geometric means (shift 1 s, 12 threads) of a virtual best and a virtual worst of three commercial solvers had fallen from 36.3 and 113.0 s to 13.5 and 31.3 s on the same hardware, "roughly 16% per year, just from improvements in the algorithms" (p. 2, n. 1); so the 2010 benchmark set was "no longer considered to sufficiently reflect the frontier of new challenges in the field" (p. 2). Problem statement: a benchmark set suitable for a meaningful and fair comparison of the average performance of MIP solvers across a wide range of instances in reasonable computing time, and a larger collection without runtime restrictions that also holds unsolved, numerically problematic, indicator and memory-heavy instances (p. 3). New difficulties: more submissions, a larger target collection, more solver representatives (p. 3). Scope fixed up front: "Average performance on this set is not a suitable criterion to decide which MIP solver to use in a particular application scenario." (p. 3).
  - Transferable: justify a new benchmark by numbers on the old one: open instances closed, and speed-ups of the virtual best and worst solvers on fixed hardware, with shift and threads stated (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Replace manual selection by committee expertise (p. 3) with "a new, heavily data-driven process" (p. 3), "the main new feature of MIPLIB 2017" (p. 3): a sequence of MIPs over clusterings (p. 24). (a) Diversity preselection inside each model group of more than five instances: a max–min-distance MIP (1a)–(1e) choosing κ = 5 or 10 instances, at least one benchmark-suitable (pp. 26–27), which cut 5666 instances to 2182 (p. 28). (b) Instead of one clustering in the 105-dimensional feature space, which suffers from the curse of dimensionality and from scaling, one k-means clustering per feature group (11 groups), each with the fewest clusters reaching 90% quality where achievable (pp. 14, 28–30). (c) The collection MIP (5): maximize Σ β_i x_i (β = 2 for benchmark-suitable instances) under balancedness (4a)–(4b) with tolerance ε = 0.5 scaled by each cluster's total dissimilarity δ, no two identical feature vectors (4c), at most five per model group and one benchmark-suitable if any (4d)–(4e), one per submitter (4f), and caps on MiniZinc, NEOS, small, medium and pure binary instances (4g, Table 9) (pp. 30–32): 1065 instances, 499 of them benchmark-suitable (p. 33). (d) Benchmark suitability (B.1)–(B.9): solved by at least one solver within 4 h, at least 10 s for half the solvers, dynamism at most 10⁶ and coefficients below 10¹⁰, consistent results, no indicator constraints, infeasible or with finite optimum, a solution value below 10¹⁰, at most 10⁶ nonzeros (p. 25). (e) 24 performance clusterings, absolute, relative and binned for each of the eight solvers, so that no solver looks different on the benchmark set than on the collection (pp. 33–35). (f) The benchmark MIP (8), with one instance per NEOS group and two per other group, a 32-day budget on median times capped at 2 h, and an objective that favours instances hard for the virtual best solver (pp. 35–36): 240 instances (p. 36). What stayed: MIPLIB 2010's split into benchmark set and collection (p. 42).
  - Transferable: write test-set selection as an optimization model with one balance constraint per feature cluster and per solver-performance cluster, so that every choice is a parameter you can report, vary and re-solve (pp. 30–36, 43–44).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIPs in a canonical form with ranged rows, bounds and binary, integer and continuous variables (Definition 1, p. 13); features computed after "trivial presolving" with SCIP 5.0 (simple reductions only, to a fixed point), while the instances are distributed unmodified (pp. 12–13). 105 features in 11 groups (Table 4, pp. 14–15), among them 17 linear constraint classes (Table 5, p. 17) and 10 decomposition features (Table 4, p. 15; §4.5, pp. 18–19), the latter "Not available for all instances" (p. 15). Block structure that is obvious in a model is often lost once the model is filled with data and written as MPS (p. 18). Indicator constraints are allowed in the collection (437 submitted instances) and barred from the benchmark set; no submission used SOS, semicontinuous variables or piecewise linear constraints (pp. 17–18, 25). Data: 128 submissions from 66 submitters with 54 affiliations (38 academic, 16 industrial), 3670 new instances plus 2051 MIPLIB 2010 submissions, 5721 in all (pp. 6–7); NEOS instances re-run with CPLEX 12.6 and sampled by clustering before submission (pp. 7–9); 5666 after removing LPs and instances emptied by trivial presolving (p. 24); model groups by submission or, for anonymous NEOS instances, by k-means with k = 110 (pp. 10–11). Decomposition data model: a partition of rows and columns into k blocks plus linking rows and columns, with nonzeros only between matching blocks (p. 18), scored by the area score, the share of the matrix that is "white" (p. 19). - **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Distribute raw, measure presolved: instances ship unmodified so that presolve itself is tested, and features come from trivial presolving to a fixed point (pp. 12–13); 55 instances became LPs or were solved and were dropped (p. 13).
  - Canonical form with the objective and every row normalized by the largest absolute coefficient; logarithmic and sign-preserving "siglog" scaling (pp. 13–14, 16).
  - Feature extractor, a C++ extension of SCIP, released for download (p. 16); five redundant matrix features were found to duplicate others "Only during the preparation of the manuscript" (p. 16, n. 5).
  - GCG 3.0 in a minimal detection configuration: group constraints by SCIP type, MIPLIB type and number of nonzeros, merge the smallest groups, try each subset as linking rows, assign the remaining rows with the connected finisher, keep the decomposition of maximum area score (p. 19); Fig. 2 shows the re-arranged matrix of b1c1s1 (p. 19).
  - Solution checker in GMP arithmetic against the original MPS file, with a new mixed absolute–relative violation rule, violation ≤ ε·max{(aᵀx)⁺, (aᵀx)⁻, |u|, 1} with ε = 10⁻⁵, integrality still checked absolutely at 10⁻⁴, and indicator support (pp. 22–23).
  - Cross-solver consistency with IPET: a verified primal value below another solver's dual bound marks an inconsistency (instance bc1: seven solvers agree on 3.338, one returns 3.418 as optimal) (p. 23); such instances leave the benchmark pool unless a bug is obvious, 328 by the text (p. 23) and 334 under (B.5) in Table 7 (p. 26), the difference unexplained.
  - Cluster quality (share of total pairwise distance that falls between clusters) and total dissimilarity δ (shifted geometric mean, shift 1, of pairwise distances) (pp. 29–30); small and large clusters by half the average size and the boxplot rule, with one side of the balance constraint dropped for each (pp. 29, 31).
  - Sensitivity sweep of ε from 0.1 to 0.9: 0.1 infeasible, 0.2 without a feasible selection after 15 minutes, 0.5 giving 1065 (Table 10, p. 33).
  - Benchmark objective coefficient growing with the square root of the virtual-best time (formula (9), the root sign lost in extraction; "The concrete choice of the square root was empirically motivated.", p. 36).
  - Feature-space validation: a pair from the same model group is among each other's five nearest neighbours with probability 0.734, against 0.007 for a random pair; 0.815 without NEOS (p. 37).
  - Maintenance by re-optimization: a variant of the benchmark MIP computed a minimal, balance-preserving replacement of two numerically problematic instances (June 2019) (pp. 43–44).
  - Transferable: compute instance features on a lightly presolved, normalized canonical form but distribute the original files, so that the features describe the model and the benchmark still tests presolve (pp. 12–14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Every submitted instance run with eight solvers (Table 6: CBC 2.9.8, CPLEX 12.7.1, Gurobi 7.5.1, MATLAB R2017b, MOSEK 8.1.0.30, SAS/OR 14.2, SCIP 4.0.0, Xpress 8.2), 4 threads except for single-threaded SCIP and MATLAB, two jobs per node on two Linux clusters (Xeon X5672 and E5-2670 v2), 4 h limit, almost 40 CPU years (p. 20). Outcomes: 1969 instances (34%) solved by no solver, 1155 (20%) by all eight (p. 20); virtual best and worst solvers for time and nodes (pp. 20–22). Per-solver results are deliberately not reported, since runs were spread over different hardware "and should hence not be reported in a way that could be confused for an actual benchmark" (p. 38). Hardness of the new benchmark set: the virtual best solver needs more than 1 h on 19.6% of it (0% for 2010), the virtual median more than 4 h on 48.3% (5.7% for 2010) (Table 11, p. 39); one-sided Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests of each solver's runtime CDF on the benchmark set against the 499 suitable instances: D ≈ 0.10, p = 0.04 for the virtual median, D = 0.17, p = 6.513·10⁻⁵ for the virtual best, p < 1% for four solvers and p > 0.1 for the other four (pp. 40–41). Stated gaps: a single time limit, "performance variability and parallel scalability were not captured", correctness only within the tolerance, and performance clusterings that may contradict each other (p. 36). After release: two benchmark instances replaced for numerics (pp. 43–44), 68 originally open instances solved (p. 44).
  - Transferable: test whether a hardness-biased benchmark still represents each solver with a one-sided KS test of its runtime CDF on the set against the pool of suitable instances (pp. 40–41).

- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: the library (1065 and 240 instances) and the fully data-driven, optimization-based selection methodology, named the main contribution of the paper (p. 42); for the first time supplementary data on matrix structure, decomposability and source models (p. 3); a web page with tags, among them "instances for which good decompositions are known", per-instance decomposition information, the five most similar instances, the feature extractor, the selection MIPs and scripts to run and validate experiments (pp. 41–42).
