### S034 · Sorting with Complete Networks of Stacks (ISAAC [LNCS 5369, pp. 895–906] 2008, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-92182-0_78)
**Read**: full. File pp. 1–12: abstract and §1 with contribution, related work and applications (pp. 1–3), §2 formulation and the circle-graph relation (pp. 3–5), §3 hardness with proofs (pp. 5–7), §4 approximation (pp. 7–9), §5 stacking constraints, proof sketch only (pp. 9–11), §6 conclusions and open problems (p. 11), references (p. 12). Figures 1–3 survive only as labels and are read from their captions (pp. 4, 10–11).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Felix G. König · **Voice**: joint ("we") · **Position in trajectory**: TU Berlin, Institut für Mathematik (p. 1); the complexity side of the group's stacking work, whose model-and-heuristic side for steel and container logistics is König, Lübbecke, Möhring, Schäfer, Spenke (ESA 2007), cited as [16] (pp. 3, 12).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Knuth, Tarjan, and Even and Itai characterized which permutations stacks in series, acyclic networks or parallel stacks can sort; once the network has a cycle every permutation is sortable, so the question becomes how to sort with the fewest shuffles (moves between stacks), which the classical work never asked (abstract; pp. 1–2). The applications cited (rail yards, tram depots, bus parking, container stowage) were studied as sortability with parallel stacks, although shuffles are "a natural part of real life stacking" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: give each item the colour of its stack in the permutation's circle graph. A proper k-colouring is a shuffle-free sorting; when none exists, monochromatic edges bound the shuffles from both sides, γ ≤ (n−1)·L (Lemma 1) and L ≤ 2γ (Lemma 2) (p. 6), although fewest monochromatic edges is not fewest shuffles (Fig. 1 example, p. 4), so the colouring serves the hardness proofs, not as a model (p. 5). Positive side: merge sort gives 2·n log n shuffles for k ≥ 3 (Fact 1, p. 7); with αk+1 stacks a phase algorithm on an α-approximate circle-graph colouring gives 3n·L* (Thm 3, pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: not a MILP. A directed network with source s and sink t, k ≥ 2 stack nodes each on an s–t path, at least one cycle (p. 3); complete networks for all hardness and approximation results (pp. 4–8); unbounded stack heights. Thm 4 adds a conflict digraph D on items (i may not sit directly on j) for fixed k ≥ 3, and drops the initial configuration, the height bounds and the k-as-input that the earlier PSPACE proof in [16] needed (p. 9).
- **D4 Proof devices** (brief): gap amplification, each chord replaced by s = n^{2/ε−1} parallel chords and all copies recoloured after the least-conflicting one, so an optimal k-colouring has 0 or at least s² = N^{2−ε} monochromatic edges (Thm 1, pp. 5–6); charging, each shuffle pays for at most n−1 monochromatic edges (Lemma 1, p. 6); phases with shuffle-and-reverse (Lemma 2, p. 6); one forced shuffle per phase, so L* ≥ p (Thm 3, p. 8); a reduction from Nondeterministic Constraint Logic with OR/AND gadgets on two stacks per node, compressed to three stacks (Thm 4, pp. 9–11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a (no computation).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: complexity theory for a new optimization version: O(n^{1−ε}) inapproximability for k ≥ 4 even on complete networks (Thm 2, pp. 6–7), O(n^{2−ε}) for MIN k-PARTITION on circle graphs as a by-product (Thm 1, p. 5), the augmented O(n)-approximation (Thm 3) and PSPACE-completeness with conflicts for every fixed k ≥ 3 (Thm 4, p. 9). Limits stated specifically: the tightness of the positive results against the hardness bound is called unsatisfactory (p. 7); the augmentation is best possible "in a certain sense" (p. 2), namely for this algorithm (Remark 2, p. 8); Thm 4 is proved only in a promised full version (p. 10). Open: k = 2 and 3, the logarithmic gap, a lower bound on shuffles other than circle-graph colouring, queues and the midnight constraint (p. 11). On the way the paper notes that a known log ω barrier falsifies Unger's claimed 2-approximation for colouring circle graphs (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 variant ⚠ (step 6 carried into theory): the positive results come with their gap to the lower bound and the reason no better bound is in reach (circle-graph colouring is the only handle, clique-based bounds cannot beat log ω) (pp. 7–9); there is no computation, so steps 1–5 do not apply.
- Method 4 variant ⚠ (step 5 in a research paper, not a synthesis): the paper ends on open problems, among them a question it cannot answer, an alternative lower bound on shuffles (p. 11).
- New-pattern candidate "hardness result as licence for the method": the conclusion reads the hardness results as explaining the lack of elegant and efficient algorithms (p. 11); the same move in S051 (p. 10) and D002 (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate "optimization version of a characterization problem": enters where the literature characterized feasibility (sortability) and asks for the minimum-cost version that applications need (abstract; pp. 1–3, 11); the same entry in S051 (p. 2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To turn the NP-hardness of deciding whether the optimum is 0 into inapproximability, blow each element up into s copies so that any nonzero optimum jumps to at least s², and choose s so that the gap is N^{2−ε} in the new size N (Thm 1, pp. 5–6).
- When you reduce to a known problem whose objective is only related to yours, show a one-instance counterexample to equivalence (Fig. 1, p. 4) and prove two-sided bounds instead (γ ≤ (n−1)L and L ≤ 2γ, p. 6).
- If a simple algorithm already guarantees O(n log n), a linear guarantee is needed only on the instances whose optimum is o(log n); return the better of the two solutions (Remark 1, p. 7).

**Quotes**:
- "It is surprising however, that theory avoided the apparent need for shuffles—only sortability, not sorting itself has been considered so far." (p. 11)
- "Even though their tightness w.r.t. our hardness result is unsatisfactory, we will discuss that they are the best we may currently hope for." (p. 7)

### S051 · On minimum k-modal partitions of permutations (Journal of Discrete Algorithms 6(3) 2008, DOI 10.1016/j.jda.2008.01.002)
**Read**: full. File pp. 1–12: abstract and §1 with contribution and motivation (pp. 1–2), §2 preliminaries and colouring relations (pp. 2–3), §3 NP-hardness (pp. 3–5), §4 MIP formulations and the comparison of LP relaxations (pp. 5–7), §5 approximation (pp. 7–8), §6 online algorithms (pp. 9–10), §7 computation and §8 conclusions with Table 1 (pp. 10–11), references (pp. 11–12). Figures 1–8 are read from their captions; some relation symbols are lost in the extraction (e.g. the bound on k in the contribution paragraph, p. 2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Gabriele Di Stefano, Stefan Krause, Uwe T. Zimmermann (his doctoral advisor according to SKILL.md's Academic Lineage) · **Voice**: joint ("we"); Lübbecke is corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lübbecke at TU Berlin with TU Braunschweig co-authors (p. 1); journal version of a LATIN 2006 extended abstract (p. 1; the conference version is cited again on p. 9); originally motivated by "the smallest number of stacks needed for sorting" (p. 2), the question S034 takes up.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: partitioning a permutation into the fewest monotone subsequences is NP-hard (Wagner [18]); the paper extends this to k-modal subsequences and, unlike the structural literature on guaranteed subsequence lengths (Erdős–Szekeres, Chung, Steele), wants optimum partitions computed (pp. 1–2). Application: tracks in shunting yards and tram depots, each track holding a subsequence of the arrival order, entry and exit at one or both ends giving increasing, monotone, unimodal and "unimodec" sequences (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: a decreasing subsequence is an s–t path in the split permutation graph with unit-cost source arcs and lower bound 1 on the element arcs, so a minimum-cost flow solves DECREASING; chaining k+1 such components with binary lower-bound selectors (Σ_j y(e_i^j) = 1) gives a network-design MIP for k-MODAL (pp. 5–6). The set-partitioning model over feasible subsequences is the Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation of this compact model; its pricing is a shortest path in an acyclic network and always integral, so the two LP bounds coincide (Lemma 2, p. 7), and the extended model buys modelling flexibility at the cost of implementation effort (p. 7). LP rounding of the compact model gives the approximation algorithms (pp. 7–8).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: permutations of {1,…,n}, k fixed (p. 3). The network-design MIP has continuous arc flows and binary lower-bound selectors (pp. 5–6). The LP-rounding theorem assumes generalized bound constraints with at most ρ ≥ 2 variables each and the scaling property ρ·P ⊆ P, which flows without upper bounds have (p. 8); the flow construction relies on the transitivity of the order, which is why it does not carry over to posets (p. 11).
- **D4 Algorithm & proof devices** (brief): NP-hardness by reduction from the restricted MONOTONE problem (ℓ increasing and m decreasing lines) with block gadgets A_h, B_h and an induction claim on how many k-modal lines cover them (Thm 1, pp. 3–5); LP-bound equality from integral pricing (Lemma 2, p. 7); a ρ-approximation by rounding the selectors up to a representative with y ≥ 1/ρ and re-solving the then integral flow LP, since ρ·x_LP stays feasible (Thm 4, p. 8); an integrality gap of at least 3/2 shown by [6,2,1,4,3,5] (LP 2.0, IP 3.0, p. 8); an Ω(log n) online lower bound by an adversary that halves the remaining range (Thm 6, p. 9); tight n/4 examples for Next Fit and Best Fit (Lemmas 7–8, pp. 9–10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: random permutations (Knuth's shuffle) with n = 30, 60, 90, 120 (90–120 called a reasonable size for rail car shunting), 100 instances per size, CPLEX 9.0 with default settings, 900 CPU s, an Athlon MP 2800+ (p. 10); average and worst-case ratios to the optimum for greedy, LP rounding, the LP bound and the online rules, and times for the flow MIP and for the fully generated set-partitioning IP, which averages 869 s at n = 90 for MONOTONE and almost always exceeds the 900 s limit at n = 120, for UPPER UNIMODAL already at n = 90 (Table 1, p. 11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: NP-hardness of k-modal and unimodec partitioning, the first MIP formulations for provably optimal partitions (abstract, p. 1), LP-rounding factors 2, 3, k+1 and 2(k+1) (Cor. 5, p. 8), the online lower bound and two exactly n/4-competitive rules (pp. 9–10). Limits named: the set-partitioning model was enumerated, not priced, and a true branch-and-price code would probably win on larger instances (p. 10); a bounded track length turns pricing into an NP-hard resource-constrained shortest path (pp. 10–11). Open: a PTAS, beating the 1.71-approximation with LP techniques, an online algorithm that matches Ω(log n), look-ahead, posets (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (steps 1–2 and the stop rule): the compact network-design model comes first and the set-partitioning model is derived from it by Dantzig-Wolfe (p. 5); the bound gain is shown to be zero because pricing has the integrality property (Lemma 2, p. 7); the compact MIP in CPLEX is far faster than the enumerated set-partitioning IP, averaging 218 s (MONOTONE) and 255 s (UPPER UNIMODAL) at n = 120 where the latter almost always times out (Table 1, p. 11); the extended model is kept only for its flexibility with "dirty" side constraints (pp. 7, 11).
- Method 3 evidence ✅ (step 6): worst cases next to averages (Table 1, p. 11), a "conservative judgment" of the times (p. 10), and the fairness remark that a real branch-and-price code would probably beat the compact MIP on larger instances (p. 10).
- Method 3 variant ⚠ (step 1; taste B2): a single test set of random permutations sized by the application, no real or public instances and no second set (p. 10).
- H8 evidence ✅: a LATIN 2006 extended abstract first, then this journal version (p. 1).
- G1 evidence ✅: every heuristic is measured as a ratio to the MIP optimum (Table 1, p. 11), and the approximation factors are proved (p. 8).
- New-pattern candidate "hardness result as licence for the method": hardness is said to legitimate expensive approaches such as large MIPs (p. 10); with S034 (p. 11) and D002 (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate "approximation or heuristic derived from the exact model": the LP of the network-design MIP yields the constant-factor rounding algorithms (pp. 7–8, 10); compare D002, whose fast heuristic comes from its exact interval model (D002 pp. 15, 19).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before building branch-and-price for a set-partitioning model, write its compact flow counterpart and check whether pricing has the integrality property; if it does, the LP bounds coincide (Lemma 2, p. 7) and the compact model in a MIP solver is the benchmark to beat (Table 1, p. 11).
- LP rounding for choice constraints Σ_{j∈I_q} y_j = 1 over a scalable polyhedron (ρ·P ⊆ P): in each group fix to 1 a representative with y_LP ≥ 1/ρ and re-solve the remaining, integral, flow LP; this is a ρ-approximation (Thm 4, pp. 7–8).
- Give the smallest instance you know with a large integrality gap ([6,2,1,4,3,5]: LP 2.0, IP 3.0, p. 8).
- In a heuristics table, report the average and the worst-case ratio to the proven optimum per instance size, and mark the runs that hit the time limit (Table 1, p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "Theoretical hardness legitimates studying and applying computationally expensive approaches like solving (probably large scale) mixed integer programs." (p. 10)
- "To be fair at this point we remark that a true branch-and-price algorithm would probably outmatch the network design MIP in terms of computation time on larger instances." (p. 10)

### D002 · Sequencing and Scheduling in Coil Coating with Shuttles (Dagstuhl Seminar Proceedings 09261, Models and Algorithms for Optimization in Logistics 2009, DOI 10.4230/dagsemproc.09261.26)
**Read**: full. File pp. 1–29: abstract and §1 (pp. 1–3), §2 problem formulation (pp. 3–9), §3 graph model with the NP-hardness proof (pp. 9–15), §4 algorithm (pp. 15–21), §5 lower bounds by branch-and-price (pp. 21–24), §6 computational study with Table 3 (pp. 24–26), §7 conclusions (pp. 26–27), captions of Figs. 8–9 and acknowledgments (pp. 27–28), references (pp. 28–29). The figure contents (setup diagrams, bar charts) are not readable in the extraction; their captions are.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Wiebke Höhn, Felix G. König, Rolf H. Möhring · **Voice**: joint ("we") · **Position in trajectory**: TU Berlin, Möhring's group (p. 1); an industry project with PSI Business Technology for Salzgitter Flachstahl (SZFG), production start planned for April 2009 (pp. 1, 27). Same author team and project as the Management Science 2011 paper that SKILL.md cites for H2 (DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1100.1302); the 2009 text does not mention that paper. It is not the MAPSP 2009 abstract S106.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: an industrial planning problem: coating steel coils on a line with three shuttle coaters (two tanks each) and one classical coater; a 24-hour plan for typically 20–40 coils must fix the sequence, the tank assignment and a schedule for setup work done concurrently with production by one scarce work team (pp. 2–5, 8, 25). Entry: shuttles turn sequencing into an integrated resource allocation and scheduling problem that is needed already to evaluate one sequence (p. 2), and the problem does not decompose into its subproblems but has to be tackled in its entirety (p. 7). Presented as the first optimization work on coil-coating planning (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: for a fixed sequence, represent all tank assignments with feasible concurrent-setup schedules as weighted rectangles (coater interval × work interval) of a 2-union graph, so that an optimal pair (T, S) is a maximum-weight independent set (pp. 9–15). With enough work resources the graph is an interval graph and MWIS is fast (p. 15); with one team the problem is strongly NP-hard (Thm 1) but fixed-parameter tractable in the number of coaters (pp. 12, 17–19). The practical algorithm drops the coupling resource, scales the interval lengths by α ∈ [0, 1] and solves MWIS on an interval graph inside a genetic algorithm for the sequence, with earliest-deadline-first scheduling of the setups (pp. 16, 19–21). Quality is certified by a combinatorial relaxation solved by branch-and-price (pp. 21–24).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: makespan minimisation over (π, T, S); local setups (scrap coils, sample runs) depend on consecutive coils, global setups (colour and roller changes) on the previous coil on the same tank (pp. 5–7); one non-preemptive concurrent-setup team with slowdown λ ≥ 1 (p. 8). The FPT bound needs max p_i/(λ t_c) polynomially bounded, about one in real data (p. 19). The lower-bound IP (assignment variables x_{p,j}, columns z_s for blocks of k coils, y_{i,j} for the local setup between blocks) is valid only for an online tank rule that concatenates, so the FIFO rule is assumed (pp. 21–22); the variant valid for any tank assignment was not pursued as too costly (p. 23). Data: SZFG batches, numbers withheld (p. 25).
- **D4 Algorithm & proof devices** (brief): strong NP-hardness of maximum independent set on diagonally k-partitionable 2-union graphs by a 3-SAT reduction (Thm 1, pp. 12–14); a DP over states (coil, one rectangle per coater) solved as a longest path in the state DAG, O(n²·N^{2k}) (pp. 17–18), with a lemma bounding the candidate start times of work intervals (Lemma 1, pp. 18–19); the "don't look back too far" relaxation, exact global cost only inside blocks of k = 4 coils (pp. 21, 26); pricing by depth-first backtracking with a dual-based pruning rule, because a subsequence's cost is known only once it is complete (p. 23); branching on the original variables x_{p,j} by forbidding or enforcing coil j at position p in pricing, incompatible columns bounded to zero, artificial columns for feasibility (pp. 23–24); the master seeded with subsequences of the GA's FIFO solution (p. 23); branch-and-price in SCIP, not tuned, used "as a proof-of-concept only" (p. 25).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: nine representative SZFG instances in Table 3, normalized to the GA makespan because the data are confidential; three lower bounds (trivial, TSP on local setups, IP with k = 4) with gap% = 100·(GA − LB)/LB, 4.28–15.08 for the IP bound, and SZFG reference plans at 1.06–1.35 times the GA makespan on the six instances that have one (pp. 25–26); one GA parameter set fixed by testing (population 200, restart when all individuals are within 5%) (p. 25); no hardware, run times or time limits are reported beyond the 180-second requirement (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an exact model of the whole planning task, a structural graph model with hardness and FPT results, a deployed heuristic and a branch-and-price certificate (pp. 26–27). Negative results are reported: the interval-model tank-assignment heuristics and local search at best slightly beat the planners' FIFO rule on these data (pp. 25, 27). The bound is called improvable, since not all IP instances were solved to integer optimality and only k = 4 was used (p. 26), while the conclusion says the IP is solved to optimality (p. 27). Future work is only the hope that the ideas carry over to similar applications (p. 27).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (steps 5–6): branch-and-price on SCIP for the bounding IP (p. 25), branching on the original assignment variables carried into pricing (pp. 23–24).
- Method 1 variant ⚠: decomposition used to certify a heuristic, not to solve the problem; no compact formulation of the relaxation is run as a yardstick (none reported, pp. 21–26).
- Method 3 evidence ✅: real planner data, the planners' own plans as the baseline (Table 3, p. 26), and the authors' own tank-assignment ideas reported as no better than FIFO, with the caveat that other data may differ (p. 25).
- Method 3 contradiction ✗ (step 6): the headline gap, within 5–15% of the optimum (abstract p. 1; p. 3; p. 27), drops the condition §5 attaches, that the bound holds for plans under the FIFO tank rule (pp. 20, 22–23); the IP is said to be solved to optimality (p. 27) against "did not solve all instances to integer optimality" (p. 26); and the conclusion praises itself in the way taste B3 warns of, saying that the practitioners came to know their machines fully only through the authors' theory (p. 27).
- H2 evidence ✅, with a caveat: the deployed GA comes with a branch-and-price lower bound, motivated as the practical question of how much optimization potential is left (p. 21); the certificate is conditional on FIFO (p. 22).
- Workflow A step 4 / H3 evidence ✅: the planners validated the plans repeatedly until cost calculations and work schedules were right (pp. 24–25); they first insisted on the FIFO rule and dropped the requirement after seeing the savings (p. 20).
- New-pattern candidate "hardness result as licence for the method": the strong NP-hardness of the subproblem is offered to justify the heuristic approach (p. 3; Thm 1, pp. 12–14); with S034 (p. 11) and S051 (p. 10).
- New-pattern candidate "approximation or heuristic derived from the exact model": the fast heuristic is the exact interval model with the coupling resource relaxed and the lengths scaled by α, and the text says the heuristics could not have been developed without the structural sections (pp. 15, 19); compare S051 (pp. 7–8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To certify a heuristic for a sequencing problem with history-dependent costs, relax the history: cost blocks of k consecutive items exactly, charge only the local cost between blocks, and solve the block-concatenation IP by branch-and-price (pp. 21–23).
- State the condition under which your bound is valid (here, an online tank rule that concatenates) wherever you state the gap, the abstract included (p. 22 against p. 1).
- Branch on an original assignment variable (item j at position p) by removing j from position p (down branch) or all other items from p (up branch) inside pricing, bound incompatible columns to zero, and keep one penalized artificial column per covering row (pp. 23–24).
- Seed the restricted master with columns cut from your heuristic's best solution (p. 23).
- From an exact but slow model of the subproblem (MWIS with a shared resource), get a fast heuristic by dropping the shared resource and scaling the interval lengths by a tuned factor α, best near 0.5 here (pp. 19, 25).
- With confidential data, normalize every bound and the practitioners' plan to your heuristic's value and report gap% per instance (Table 3, p. 26).

**Quotes**:
- "Assessing the quality of heuristic solutions is not only a theoretical contribution, but an important question in practice: How much optimization potential is left?" (p. 21)
- "Hence, we use the FIFO rule in the integer program and thereby obtain lower bounds for the case when the FIFO rule is used." (p. 22)

### S040 · A Branch-and-Price Algorithm for Multi-mode Resource Leveling (SEA [LNCS 6049, pp. 226–238] 2010, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-13193-6_20)
**Read**: full. File pp. 1–13: abstract and §1 with the contribution (pp. 1–2), §2 problem and related work (pp. 2–4), §3 time-indexed model, master and pricing (pp. 4–7), §4 branching, propagation and heuristics (pp. 7–9), §5 computational study with Tables 1–2 (pp. 9–12), §6 summary (p. 12), references (pp. 12–13). Fig. 2's schedules and Fig. 3's calendars survive only as numbers and captions (pp. 5, 10).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Eamonn T. Coughlan, Jens Schulz (both TU Berlin) · **Voice**: joint ("we") · **Position in trajectory**: Lübbecke at TU Darmstadt (p. 1); the application, turnaround scheduling, comes from Möhring's group at TU Berlin (Megow, Möhring, Schulz [MMS10], pp. 1, 13); same SEA 2010 volume and the same SCIP 1.2 generation as the GCG paper in SKILL.md's Signature Work Anatomy.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: turnaround scheduling from chemical engineering; once the horizon is fixed it is a multi-mode resource-leveling problem with precedence constraints and working shifts that minimises the total resource availability cost Σ c_k R_k (pp. 1–3). Entry: heuristics dominate because time-indexed MIPs have very weak LP relaxations and often fail already on 30 jobs (abstract, p. 1; p. 4); in the closest column-generation lower bound (Drexl and Kimms), Lagrangian relaxation beat column generation at 30 jobs (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: decompose the time horizon by the resource calendars: each working shift is a subproblem whose sub-schedules (configurations) are the columns, one configuration per job is enforced in the master, and start and completion times are linked to the configurations by equalities whose duals reward or penalize times in pricing (pp. 5–6). Kept: the precedence constraints stay in the master on the original start and completion variables (p. 6). The pricing problem per shift is itself an NP-hard leveling problem, written as an IP (pp. 6–7); how it is solved is not stated (pp. 6–7, 10).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: MPSm,∞|prec,shifts|Σ c_k max r_k (p. 3): non-preemptive jobs, each using exactly one resource in one of several modes, renewable resources with availability periods, a fixed horizon (p. 2). The baseline is the Pritsker–Watters–Wolfe time-indexed binary model adapted to leveling (pp. 4–5). No public instance set fits (the PSPLib sets lack shifts or modes), so the authors generated their own: 30 jobs with 60 edges, 50 with 70 and 50 with 100, 10 instances each, 3 modes, two resources, five calendars C1–C5, horizons from heuristic makespans (pp. 3–4, 9–10).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design** (brief): a two-job example shows the time-indexed LP losing the timing information and branching on x_{jt} misleading, which motivates branching on start times (Fig. 2, p. 5); the branching order R_k, then S_j, then C_j was found by experiments and applies only where a configuration variable is fractional (p. 7); edge-finding propagation adapted to multiple modes also passes branching decisions to pricing (p. 7); ready scheduling (all mode combinations of s = 6 jobs) and a binary-search leveling heuristic from [MMS10], every feasible schedule found being added as columns (pp. 8–9); SCIP 1.2.0.6 with custom plug-ins for pricer, branching and heuristics (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: 150 runs (30 instances × 5 calendars) with a 30-minute limit, branch-and-price on an Intel 2.66 GHz machine against the time-indexed MIP in CPLEX 12 with quad-core parallelization on a stronger machine, plus an ablation without the LP-solution and ready-scheduling heuristics (still started from the leveling heuristic's solution) on the 30-job set (p. 11); reported per calendar: mean time, how often the best known lower and upper bounds are reached, timeouts (pp. 10–11). From the timeout columns, branch-and-price solves 118 of 150 (78.7%) and CPLEX 64 of 150 (42.7%) (Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11), which the text rounds to 80% and 43% (p. 12). CPLEX is faster on calendar C3 in all three scenarios and ahead on C2 with 70 edges in time, best lower bounds and timeouts (Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an exact shift-based branch-and-price that solves instances with up to 50 multi-mode jobs, plus heuristics (p. 2). One limit is named: the problem-specific heuristics barely change the number of instances solved (p. 11). The summary ends with the belief that 30-job instances are still hard and the encouragement to go on (p. 12); no other limitation or direction is spelled out.

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (steps 2, 3, 5, 6): the compact time-indexed MIP in a current solver is the baseline (pp. 4, 10); the larger object chosen as a variable, a shift sub-schedule, comes from the calendar structure that the authors name as their motivation (pp. 5, 12); the code is a set of SCIP plug-ins (p. 10); branching is on original variables (p. 7).
- Method 1 variant ⚠ (stop rule not checkable): the pricing problem is an NP-hard leveling IP whose solution method the paper does not state (pp. 6–7), so the stop rule's test, pricing that is more than a generic MIP, cannot be applied from the text.
- Method 3 evidence ✅: a per-component ablation (branch-and-price without heuristics, Table 1, p. 10); the competitor's different, stronger machine is flagged in the text, as the checkpoint asks (p. 10); the CPLEX wins stay visible in the tables (pp. 10–11).
- Method 3 contradiction ✗ (step 6): the contribution paragraph says the heuristic improves on the results reported in [MMS10] (p. 2), but no comparison with [MMS10] appears in the computational section (pp. 9–12); "significantly outperforms" (p. 12) without a test; the claim that it "achieves the best results across all shift configurations" (p. 12) holds for best upper bounds in every row but not for lower bounds, time and timeouts on C2 with 70 edges (Table 2, p. 11); the heuristics' time gain is an average over all instances (207 to 187 s), while C1 gets slower with them (435 to 446 s) and C4 does not change (Table 1, p. 10), which the text does not mention (p. 11); 78.7% is reported as 80% (pp. 10–12).
- Method 3 variant ⚠ (step 1; taste B2): one self-generated factorial set modelled on PSPLib, justified by the absence of a public set with shifts (p. 9); no second, general set.
- H2 evidence ✅: the heuristic is judged against the branch-and-price bounds, the problem approached "from both ends" (p. 2); the leveling heuristic reaches the best upper bound in 68% of all instances (p. 11).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a time-indexed MIP is weak and the resources have calendars, decompose the horizon by shifts: one column per sub-schedule of a shift, a one-configuration-per-job constraint, and start/completion linking equalities that feed time-dependent duals into pricing (pp. 5–6).
- Branch on original variables in a fixed order (capacities, then start times, then completion times), only where an associated column variable is fractional, and propagate (edge-finding) so that pricing sees the decisions (p. 7).
- Add every feasible schedule a primal heuristic finds to the master as columns, to cut pricing rounds (p. 9).
- Report, per instance class, mean time, the number of times each code reaches the best known lower and upper bound, and timeouts, plus a column for your code with its heuristics switched off (Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11).

**Quotes**:
- "For the standard MIP (1)–(6) we used CPLEX 12 with quad-core parallelization on a stronger machine." (p. 10)
- "It achieves the best results across all shift configurations, in the worst case 88% of the best upper bounds are found." (p. 12)

### S029 · Nested Column Generation Applied to the Crude Oil Tanker Routing and Scheduling Problem with Split Pickup and Split Delivery (Naval Research Logistics 59(3–4) 2012, DOI 10.1002/nav.21489)
**Read**: full. File pp. 1–13: abstract and §1 (pp. 1–2), §2 model (p. 2), §3 nested decomposition with the level 1–3 models (pp. 3–6), §4 algorithm in SCIP (pp. 6–9), §5 instances, settings and results with Tables 1–4 (pp. 10–12), §6 conclusions (pp. 12–13), acknowledgments and references (p. 13). Fig. 1 is read from its caption (p. 4).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Frank Hennig, Bjørn Nygreen (NTNU Trondheim) · **Voice**: joint ("we"); Hennig is corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: Lübbecke at RWTH Aachen, Chair of Operations Research (p. 1), but most of the work was done at TU Berlin (p. 13); received January 2010, revised March 2012 (p. 1); the application and the instances come from the tanker work of Hennig, Nygreen et al. [10] and a working paper by Hennig et al. (pp. 1, 10, 13).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: crude oil tanker routing and scheduling with split pickup and split delivery (COTRASP): a heterogeneous fleet, several crude grades, many-to-many supply and demand, time windows, arc-dependent weight and volume limits and quantity-dependent service times (abstract; pp. 1–2), with large sums at stake per sailing day and per port visit saved (p. 1). Entry: exact methods exist for the split delivery VRP (Desaulniers; Ceselli et al.), but for split pickup and split delivery the authors know two related papers, neither of which fits (p. 2), and the SDVRP's key property, at most one split customer per route, fails here (pp. 2–3). As "probably more important" than the application the paper claims a general methodology to solve such problems exactly (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: decouple routing from service. Two split locations, several commodities and arc capacities destroy label dominance and make reduced costs non-local (Fig. 1), so a single labelling DP is unlikely to be efficient (p. 3). Instead, the level 1 master over all ships convex-combines extreme cargo patterns of routes; level 2, one per ship, chooses a route and quantities as a MIP and is itself solved by branch-and-price; level 3 generates routes as an elementary shortest path problem with time windows by labelling (boost RCSP) (pp. 3–6). Kept from the SDVRP work: extreme cargo patterns as columns (p. 3). The approach is exact in principle but run as a heuristic (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: level 1 has binary route variables, continuous pattern weights and an integer count of unused ships (p. 4); level 2 is a MIP with a big-M linearization of the timing constraint (p. 5). Assumptions: deterministic sailing times (p. 2); a ship visits a time window at most once (pp. 5–6); maximum waiting times enforced only approximately and possibly cutting off optimal routes (p. 6); at most two voyages per route in pattern generation, consistent with the data (p. 9). Instances: twelve "reality related" instances from a co-author working paper, set A with 2–6 ships and set B with 8–15 ships and up to 47 time windows (Tables 1–2, p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design** (brief): the route variables are substituted out of the convexity and visit constraints so that a new route does not add a row (p. 4); branching on the original arc and node flows of each ship, at both master levels, with SCIP's built-in branching (pp. 6–7); level-1 pricing stops a level-2 branch-and-price after the first column below c_lim = −1 or after t_lim = 60 s, with a ship queue that later re-examines timed-out ships without the limit (pp. 7–8, 10); a greedy cargo-pattern generator, at most 30 patterns per voyage, extreme patterns generated only if it fails (pp. 9–10); heuristic label dominance on cost and time first, exact dominance when it yields no column (p. 9); SCIP's presolve, cuts and propagation disabled during branch-and-price to keep the dual bound correct (p. 10); a final CPLEX run on the level-1 master with all generated columns (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP 1.1.0.6 with CPLEX 11.2, Core 2 Duo 3 GHz, 6 GB, one core per run, two runs per machine when memory allowed, 12 h of branch-and-price followed by the CPLEX run (p. 10). Optimality proven for 3 of 12 instances; root gaps 0.4–6.6% where the root LP finished, no valid bound for instances 8–12; level 2 takes 96–100% of the time and the DP at most 5.8% of that (pp. 10–12). Against two column-pregeneration algorithms of the working paper: up to 8% and 1–7% better, not on the two largest instances, and the costs are said not to be directly comparable (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an accurate model and a nested column generation code on SCIP, called a "satisfactory proof-of-concept" (p. 13). Limits named concretely: tail-off at level 2, long level-2 LP times (single RMP 2 solves over 1000 s), memory limits in the final run, and a primal heuristic too weak to replace the second run (p. 12). Future: parallel level-2 solves, better pattern generation, branching on column sets, valid inequalities, tailored primal heuristics, exact and fast cargo-pattern generation, a generic view of the problem class (pp. 12–13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (steps 5–6): SCIP chosen for its tree management and plug-in support, "for a proof-of-concept" (p. 6); branching on original arc and node variables at both levels (pp. 6–7).
- Method 1 variant ⚠ (steps 2 and 4): no compact model is run as a yardstick; the decomposition is chosen from the argued failure of label dominance (p. 3), and the comparison is with column-pregeneration codes (p. 12).
- Method 3 evidence ✅ (step 6 in the body): optimality claimed only for instances 1, 2 and 4, no gaps reported where no valid bound exists, "a general conclusion should not be drawn" from the root-gap pattern, the two largest instances not improved, and the cost comparison marked as not directly comparable (pp. 10, 12).
- Method 3 variant ⚠ (the abstract; step 3): the abstract says the schedules "improve on those in previous studies" (p. 1) without the exception of the two largest instances (p. 12); the competitors are not re-run under identical conditions (p. 12), and the runs share machines (p. 10).
- H1 evidence ✅: partial and heuristic pricing at the inner level, stopped after a few negative columns because of tail-off (p. 7); heuristic dominance before exact dominance (p. 9); heuristic cargo patterns before exact ones (p. 9).
- New-pattern candidate "split a pricing level when label dominance fails": routes by labelling, quantities by column generation over the extreme points of the quantity polyhedron, nested inside the per-ship pricing (pp. 3–6, 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If quantities make reduced costs non-local and kill label dominance, keep labelling for the routes only and generate quantity patterns as extreme points of a per-route polyhedron by column generation; convex-combine them in the master (pp. 3–5).
- Substitute route variables out of the convexity and visit constraints so that the master does not grow by a row per new route (p. 4).
- Time-box the inner pricing: stop at the first column below a threshold or after t seconds, and re-examine the timed-out subproblems without the limit before declaring the LP optimal (pp. 7–8).
- Check which framework features would invalidate a column-generation dual bound (here presolve, cuts, propagation) and switch them off during pricing (p. 10).
- Close with price-and-branch: solve the final master with all generated columns as a MIP in a commercial solver (p. 10).

**Quotes**:
- "It should be noted that the approach is exact in principle, even though we will use it as a heuristic." (p. 3)
- "During branch-and-price we have to disable SCIP's preprocessing, cut generation, and propagation functionalities to ensure a correct dual bound." (p. 10)

### S070 · Automatic Decomposition and Branch-and-Price—A Status Report (SEA [LNCS 7276, pp. 1–8] 2012, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-30850-5_1)
**Read**: full. File pp. 1–8: abstract, §1 modelling with the bin-packing example (pp. 1–3), §2 Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation (pp. 3–4), §3 the need for automatic decomposition (p. 5), §4 structure detection (pp. 5–6), §5 GCG (pp. 6–7), §6 discussion and acknowledgments (p. 7), references (pp. 7–8). Fig. 1 (two pictures of the 10teams matrix) is read from its caption (p. 6).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole author) · **Voice**: sole; "we" is the project, "I" appears only in the acknowledgments, which name Bastubbe, Bergner, Ceselli, Desrosiers, Furini, Gamrath, Pfetsch and Traversi (p. 7); written as the abstract of a talk (pp. 5, 7) · **Position in trajectory**: RWTH Aachen (p. 1), DFG priority programme 1307 "algorithm engineering", project "generic decomposition algorithms for integer programs" (p. 7); the first stable GCG release was imminent (p. 7). This is the sole-authored SEA 2012 status report that SKILL.md's Honest Boundary lists as not read.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: an adoption gap. Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation, column generation and branch-and-price are well understood and successful in many special cases, but not usable out of the box by everyday OR practitioners: the generic codes (BaPCod, DIP, G12) need the user to say which constraints are local, and those with their own codes start over with every new application (p. 5). The interest dates from collecting material for the 2005 primer, when he learned of Vanderbeck's BaPCod (p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea**: close the last gap by detecting a decomposable structure in the flat matrix and handing it to a generic branch-and-price solver (pp. 5–7). The opening derives the bin-packing pattern model from the compact model as a Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation, and the aggregated set-partitioning model from that, noting that aggregation removes the symmetry but loses the unique reconstruction of x (pp. 1–3).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: integer programs min{c^T x : Ax ≥ b, Dx ≥ d, x ∈ Z^n_+} with D block-diagonal, i.e. a bordered block-diagonal coefficient matrix; X is assumed bounded, "but this is no restriction" (pp. 3–4); the input is an LP file plus an optional decomposition file (p. 6); the only instance shown is MIPLIB's 10teams (p. 6).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design** (brief): a hypergraph with a vertex per nonzero and a hyperedge per row and per column; connected components are blocks, and removing a minimum set of row hyperedges reveals a bordered block-diagonal form, an NP-hard problem attacked by heuristics that need the number of blocks k, so several small k are tried (pp. 5–6); clustering is being tried to avoid fixing k (p. 6). GCG on SCIP reformulates by a best guess, offers convexification or discretization, aggregates identical blocks, keeps both formulations for branching and separation on original variables, has Ryan-Foster branching and generic primal heuristics, and uses SCIP's pseudo-costs, presolving and propagation (pp. 6–7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: none in the text. The abstract promises computational results (p. 1); the experiments on structured instances are reported in the talk (p. 5); the paper shows only one detected structure (Fig. 1, p. 6).
- **D6 Positioning**: Vanderbeck's generic branching and BaPCod as the inspiration, DIP and G12 as other generic codes, all of which require a user-specified decomposition (p. 5); the IPCO 2011 experiments on general MIPs as the first step (p. 5).
- **D7 Writing**: a tutorial opening on bin packing (pp. 1–3); before the argument, four reader types with their pain points, from the modeller who cannot solve moderately sized instances to the one with a running code who does not want to adapt it for every new problem, closed by "Read on" (p. 4); a personal motivation in the plural ("we were fascinated", "It simply felt wrong", p. 5); hedges on the detection results (they look artificial; preliminary results look plausible, p. 6); a discussion that states the goal and the open problem instead of summarising (p. 7).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: a status report: the motivation for automatic decomposition, the detection idea and GCG's features (pp. 5–7). The goals are stated as success criteria: beating branch-and-cut on the average instance is not realistic, anything but beating it on structured instances would be a failure, so the challenge is to detect structure reliably or conclude that none is there (p. 7). Open: a detection algorithm tailored to the sparse degree-2 hypergraph, clustering instead of a fixed k (p. 6), and a theory of what makes a good structure, expected to show how to set up a good IP model in the first place (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (steps 1 and 6): the compact original formulation comes first and the column models are derived from it (pp. 1–3); GCG keeps both formulations so that branching and cutting work on original variables (p. 7); the branch-and-cut solver is the yardstick (p. 7).
- Method 1 variant ⚠ (the dating of the increment): the 2012 sole voice frames the generic solver as needing no user input or expert knowledge (abstract, p. 1) and hopes to make the algorithm useful to a non-expert (p. 7), the framing SKILL.md says was overturned after 2017. Yet among the four reader types it already names the one who does not know whether a hand-built branch-and-price code is worth the time and considerable effort (p. 4), the seed of the probe in step 4.
- Method 3 evidence ✅ (steps 1 and 6): the test target moved from general MIPs, a proof of concept, to instances that bear structure (p. 5), and the success criterion is stated before any result: no win expected on the average instance, a win on structured ones required (p. 7).
- Method 3 variant ⚠: the abstract promises computational results that the written text does not contain (p. 1; pp. 1–8).
- Method 2 evidence ✅ (stated, weak): several small k are tried rather than one picked (p. 6); computational experiments are to guide the design and a theory of good structure is to follow (p. 7), the order of Method 2's exit B.
- Method 4 evidence ✅: collecting material for the primer is where the generic branch-and-price idea came from (p. 5).
- H4 evidence ✅: the weak step of the group's own tool, detection, becomes a research problem in hypergraph partitioning and clustering (p. 6).
- G4 evidence ✅: detected structures "sometimes look artificial" (p. 6), an early form of the 2015 warning that a found structure may be an artifact.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Introduce a decomposition by deriving it from the compact model step by step (assignment model, per-bin patterns, aggregated patterns) and say what each step loses (symmetry, unique reconstruction) (pp. 1–3).
- Detect bordered block-diagonal structure with the row–column hypergraph of the nonzeros: delete a minimum set of row hyperedges to split it into k components, and try several small k (pp. 5–6).
- Write down the success criterion of a generic method before testing it: what it need not beat (branch-and-cut on the average instance) and what it must beat (structured instances) (p. 7).
- Open an expository paper with a short list of reader types and the question each brings (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "On the other hand, anything but outperforming the general-purpose solver on instances that contain a decomposable problem structure would be a failure." (p. 7)
- "Testing on general mixed integer programs was very nice and gave a successful proof-of-concept, but diverted our attention from the true target instances of this research: those which bear structure." (p. 5)
