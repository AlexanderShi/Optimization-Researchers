- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The chapter enters from an observation: "Almost all" applications of the Dantzig-Wolfe principle in the literature are integer programs (bin packing, cutting stock, edge coloring, generalized assignment, aircraft routing, crew pairing) (p. 4). It separates itself from the LP version by requiring that the pricing problem produce solutions that respect the integrality conditions of the compact formulation (p. 4).
  - Problem statement: the ILP (4.1) with its constraints grouped into a linking set A and a pricing domain D, both integer sets. Integrality in D is optional, "although it is precisely how the magic happens" (p. 5).
  - Scope: the root node only; branching and the technical side of column generation are postponed to ch. 7 (p. 4).
  - A second entry, for the structure-detection part: a solver usually sees only the "flat" model of an LP or MPS file, with generically numbered rows and columns, so the grouping A/D that the chapter took for granted is not given (p. 60).
  - Transferable: pose a decomposition question as the choice of which constraints go into the pricing domain D, and with which integrality, and read every result (bound, pricing difficulty, branching options) as a consequence of that choice (pp. 5, 27).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - *Kept* from LP Dantzig-Wolfe (ch. 3): substitute a representation of the pricing domain into the objective and the linking constraints, solve the master LP by column generation with the same RMP–pricing information flow (Figs. 4.2 and 4.7, pp. 10, 21), and keep the Lagrangian bound z_RMP + Σ_k c̄^k ≤ z⋆_MP ≤ z_RMP, whose ch. 3 proof "continues to hold" (Prop 4.12, pp. 38–39).
  - *Changed*: D is an integer set. Convexification replaces D by conv(D), represented by its extreme points and rays (Theorem 4.1, p. 6), and keeps x integer in the master IMP (4.5) (p. 6). Discretization represents D by a finite set T of integer points, interior ones included, with binary λ_p and integer λ_r (Theorem 4.2, via Nemhauser and Wolsey 1988, Thm 6.1, pp. 11–12; IMP¨ (4.20), p. 13); integrality of x then becomes redundant (Prop 4.3, pp. 14–15).
  - The two reformulations give the same RMP and the same bound (Prop 4.2, p. 14); the distinction "rather becomes a conceptual discussion than a real practical difference" (Note 4.4, p. 14). It shows only in branching and cutting, since discretization lets one branch and cut on λ as well as on x (pp. 20, 24–25).
  - *Why integrality in D*: z⋆_LP ≤ z⋆_MP ≤ z⋆_IMP = z⋆_ILP (Prop 4.1, p. 7), but if the pricing formulation has the integrality property, the master bound equals the LP bound (Prop 4.7, p. 27; block version Prop 4.13, p. 39). Since we control D, pricing "can be as easy or as difficult as we wish" (p. 27); the choice is a trade-off between bound and pricing effort that the authors call hard to predict (p. 27, quote 2).
  - Pricing must return extreme points or rays of conv(D) (p. 10). Interior integer points are dominated by extreme points and so are never generated at the root, the "structural deficiency" of Degraeve and Jans (2007), recovered by branching in ch. 7 (Note 4.8, p. 20).
  - Further constructions on the same substitution logic: identical blocks aggregated into one pricing problem with Σλ_p = |K| (pp. 32–34) and disaggregated through a transportation problem (Prop 4.10, p. 36); unused blocks through a binary activation variable (pp. 46–50); shared variables through block copies y = y^k, which the authors liken to Lagrangian decomposition (Guignard and Kim 1987) (pp. 50–52); non-linear costs and coefficients through discretization of a finite D (Prop 4.14, p. 41); reverse Dantzig-Wolfe (Props 4.15–4.17, pp. 52–58); algorithmic grouping of constraints (pp. 60–66).
  - Transferable: before building, check whether your pricing formulation has the integrality property. If it does, the reformulation cannot raise the root bound (Props 4.7 and 4.13, pp. 27, 39), and any gain must come from somewhere else (see the first new-pattern candidate below).
- **D3 Problem class & data model** (team.json):
  - Class: pure ILPs, x ∈ Z^n_+, assumed feasible with a finite optimum; the presentation "can be generalized" to MILP (p. 5), which the literature does by convexifying the continuous part (Vanderbeck and Savelsbergh 2006, cited p. 111). Discretization needs rational data and rays scaled to the shortest integer vector (pp. 11–12, 18). Note 4.3 adds that Q versus R is moot in practice because every model runs on computer code (p. 13).
  - Correctness assumptions: the encoding functions c(x) and a(x) must preserve addition and scalar multiplication (4.70) (p. 40). The one exception is a finite D under discretization (Prop 4.14, p. 41); on an unbounded domain a quadratic cost breaks the cost split, 97 against 73 at the point (9,4) (p. 42). Prop 4.17 holds if and only if the pricing formulation has the integrality property and 0 ∉ A or z⋆ ≤ 0 (p. 55).
  - Structures exploited: the single-row-bordered block-diagonal form, plus column-bordered and double-bordered (arrowhead) forms (Fig. 4.10, p. 29); identical subproblems (p. 32); binary pricing domains, where convexification and discretization coincide (Prop 4.4, pp. 21–22); semi-assignment constraints, whose reformulation returns the compact model (Prop 4.5, pp. 22–23; generalized assignment, p. 91); knapsack, shortest-path, circulation and matching pricing problems (pp. 67–79, 107–110). Big-M appears as the linearization of time precedence (4.134), with a poor LP bound (pp. 84–86).
  - Data: block counts from published applications, from 2 to 1131 blocks (pp. 28–29, 111). MIPLIB matrices are used only as pictures of detected structure: ns1778858 from MIPLIB 2010 (Koch et al. 2011) permuted into 16 blocks (p. 60), b2c1s1 in two forms (p. 61), 10teams into 5 or 8 blocks (p. 62), ic97_potential in staircase form (p. 63). MIPLIB 2017 (Gleixner et al. 2021) is cited for the constraint types (p. 63).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design, proofs and devices** (team.json):
  - Proof devices:
    - The bound ordering follows from a chain of feasible-domain inclusions (4.8) (p. 7).
    - The finite set T exists because each ray multiplier splits into a fractional and an integer part (4.15)–(4.17); the set "only serves to show the existence" of T (pp. 11–12).
    - Domination of interior points by extreme points explains what pricing can and cannot produce (p. 20).
    - Disaggregation is a zero-cost balanced transportation problem, solved greedily by hand (Prop 4.10, p. 36).
    - Necessity in Prop 4.17 is by contradiction: a fractional LP point x• is scaled by an integer x0 into an integer ray that the given points cannot express (p. 58).
    - A 2-D numerical counterexample shows non-linear costs failing on unbounded domains (p. 42); 2-D illustrations show T for a polytope, a cone and two polyhedra (pp. 17–19).
    - Exercises add an optimality test (a pricing solution feasible for the compact ILP is optimal if π_b⊺(b − a_p) = 0, Exercise 4.7) and the independence of the Lagrangian bound from the π0^k (Exercise 4.6) (p. 114).
  - Implementation rules stated:
    - Do not impose the redundant upper bounds λ_p ≤ 1 (observation 4, p. 24, echoing Note 2.15).
    - Keep the relation to x in post-processing, because otherwise branching and cutting decisions on x would vanish (p. 25).
    - Dropping the aggregated convexity constraint "does not serve the implementation", since branching brings back two constraints; keep it as a static variable v ∈ [0, κ] with dynamic bounds instead (Note 4.16 and (4.88), pp. 49–50).
    - Cast extreme rays to an integer scale where they leave pricing (p. 20).
    - Model blocks that need not be used by a binary x0^k with D^k x^k ≥ d^k x0^k and x^k ≤ u^k x0^k, which gives ≤ 1 convexity rows (pp. 46–48).
  - Pricing design:
    - Partial pricing at Air France: only 15 of 55 to 1131 crew subproblems are solved per iteration (p. 29).
    - For the multi-commodity flow, one Dijkstra tree prices |N| − 1 commodities at once with "barely any overhead", keeping a diversified subset of the columns, instead of all-pairs Floyd-Warshall (pp. 94–95).
    - State-space networks and dynamic programming for a finite D (pp. 58–59).
    - A rule of thumb that restricts the pricing domain must, if never turned off, empirically show no false negatives with respect to integrality (p. 82).
  - Structure detection:
    - Graph methods: rows and columns as a bipartite graph, a minimum vertex |K|-separator, hypergraph partitioning, and graph clustering by modularity, where the number of blocks is an output (Khaniyev et al. 2018). Identical blocks are found by subgraph isomorphism, and staircase forms by the same means (pp. 61–63).
    - Constraint classes: classify constraints by type, number of variables, coefficients or names; try subsets of whole classes as the border (2^ℓ of them); count the connected components of the remaining bipartite graph (p. 63).
    - Scoring: the best dual bound is no goal, because reformulating all constraints attains it (p. 64). The proxies are border size (a linear penalty in Bergner et al. 2015, exponential decay in Khaniyev et al. 2018) and many small, balanced, identical blocks (pp. 64–65).
  - Transferable: generate candidate decompositions from constraint classes rather than from matrix shape. Assign whole classes to the border, count the connected components of what remains, and score only the survivors (p. 63).
- **D5 Computational evaluation** (team.json):
  - There is no benchmark campaign: no test set, hardware, seeds or time limits. Instead there are ten worked examples on toy data (pp. 67–110):
    - TCSPP, nine pricing domains D1–D9 on one 6-node network: master sizes from 2×9 to 7×278 and z⋆_MP from 7 to 13, against z⋆_LP = 7 and z⋆_ILP = 13 (Table 4.6, p. 80). Redundant arc-count constraints leave the bound unchanged (D2, D4), while one family of inflow bounds (D7) reaches 13 with five master rows (p. 81).
    - Big-M: z⋆_LP falls from 3.900 to 3.020 (Table 4.8, p. 84) and from 3.800 to 3.020 (Table 4.9, p. 86) as M grows from 25 to 1000. A grouping that leaves the node time bounds in the master gives z⋆_MP = 7 against z⋆_IMP = 15 (p. 87).
    - Scene selection, one instance (11 actors, 19 scenes, W = 5, 5 days; p. 96). With CPLEX 11.2 at its default symmetry setting, LP time and IP gap are 4.00 s and 58.8 % for the compact model, 5.79/8.53/2.75 s and 47.0/52.9/34.5 % for three orders of the variable-reduction technique, and 1.34 s and 1.1 % for the master (Table 4.10, p. 97). The reduced-variable bound depends on the input order; the master's does not (p. 98).
    - Size counts: the all-pairs multi-commodity flow on 1,000 nodes and 10,000 arcs has 999,010,000 constraints and 9,990,999,000 variables, while the master has 10,000 rows, under 0.001 % (pp. 93–94).
    - Cutting stock: a single-item instance with width W/2 + ε has a 50 % integrality gap in the weak compact formulation and none in the extended ones (p. 77). Student teams: 80,730 teams enumerated, 56,916 after practical rules, in a model with 26 + 2 rows (pp. 102–103).
  - Negative results are reported: groupings that gain nothing (pp. 81, 87, 95), reformulations that give back the compact model (pp. 91, 110), a vote that cannot be decoded even partially (p. 106), and the false negatives of a pricing filter (Table 4.7, p. 82).
  - The structure-detection claims rest on cited studies and are not re-measured here: seemingly small changes to a decomposition change bound and time (Bergner et al. 2015), and random grouping gives poor bounds (Bastubbe et al. 2018) (p. 66).
  - Transferable: when comparing reformulations, tabulate for each candidate the master size (rows × columns), z⋆_MP and ⌈z⋆_MP⌉ against z⋆_LP and z⋆_ILP (Table 4.6, p. 80); wherever a big-M appears, report z⋆_LP as a function of M (Tables 4.8–4.9, pp. 84, 86).
- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Lineage: Minkowski-Weyl; Hilbert (1890) as proven by Giles and Pulleyblank (1979), via Nemhauser and Wolsey (1988) (p. 11); Vanderbeck and Wolsey (2010) (pp. 16, 113); Geoffrion (1974) for the integrality property, with Lagrangian relaxation (Fisher 1981, Guignard 2003) as "the dual point of view" (p. 111); Barnhart et al. (1998) for the name branch-and-price (p. 88); Ford and Fulkerson (1958) as the decomposition approach "without the name" (p. 112); Degraeve and Jans (2007) for structural deficiency (p. 20).
  - Devil's advocate against their own thesis: two well-known papers exploit the integrality property of the pricing formulation, Held and Karp (1970) and Ford and Fulkerson (1958) (pp. 27–28).
  - Credit and novelty flags:
    - Prop 4.5 was written after an observation by Krunal Kishor Patel, a PhD student at Polytechnique Montréal (fall 2021) (p. 112).
    - Prop 4.15 is due to Daniel Villeneuve (1999) (p. 111).
    - Prop 4.11, the "Not all blocks are used" material and Props 4.16–4.17 are marked as new, the last two as late answers to François Vanderbeck (pp. 111–112). The second edge-coloring compact formulation is new to their knowledge (p. 113).
    - A box on the Floyd-Warshall algorithm's repeated reinvention is titled "Reinventing the wheel, several times" (p. 112).
  - Structure detection: graph-based methods go back to Ferris and Horn (1998) and Aykanat et al. (2004); Bergner et al. (2015) "pioneered" graph partitioning for automatic Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation of general MILPs; constraint-class methods are "documented in code (only)", in GCG (p. 112).

- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: theory and exposition in one notation, with no software or benchmark; new results flagged as new (pp. 111–113); ten worked examples that compute the effect of each design choice (pp. 67–110).
  - *Limits, stated concretely*: root node only (p. 4); MILP by remark only (pp. 5, 111). For non-linear encoding functions on unbounded domains there is no practical exact method, and whether an error bound ε exists is posed as a question (p. 42). The mixed-integer non-linear case is left as a conjecture (Note 4.14, p. 43). The bound-versus-pricing trade-off is sometimes impossible to predict (p. 27). Decomposition scores are proxies suggested from experience and experiments (p. 64), and partition refinement for detection is "not fully exploited to date" (p. 64).
  - *Future*: branching and cutting in ch. 7 (pp. 20, 24); Exercise 4.21 asks for other cases in which a reformulation gives back the original formulation (p. 120).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Method 1 (compact model first; decomposition as a measured bet), evidence. The success criterion is stated against the compact formulation (p. 25, quote 1), and a reformulation is a detour when the compact model has no integrality gap (Prop 4.6, p. 25). Prop 4.7 is the formal half of the stop rule: no bound gain when the pricing formulation has the integrality property (p. 27). Step 6 (keep original variables in view) gets its formal device in reverse Dantzig-Wolfe, which recovers a compact formulation from a column model (pp. 52–58): the two formulations "always co-exist" (p. 52), and intuitive column-generation models "often rely on discretization whether knowingly or not" (p. 50). The chapter presents branch-and-price as a general method for general MILPs while saying that tailoring helps (p. 66), which matches the skill's Method 1 increment (GCG as a first try and an estimate, tailored studies built on SCIP).
- ⚠ Method 1, variant. SKILL.md's stop rule (stay with branch-and-cut "when the probe shows no bound gain") is narrower than the chapter. The multi-commodity flow is called an ideal choice for Dantzig-Wolfe although z⋆_MP = z⋆_LP, because the master has under 0.001 % of the compact rows (pp. 92–95). The scene-selection and student-team reformulations are motivated by symmetry and by a quadratic objective (pp. 98, 100–102). For "unstructured" models, whose subproblems are only smaller general MILPs, graph-based detection "may be a good way to deal with them" (p. 65).
- ✅ Method 2 (enumerate small, then explain or automate), evidence. Nine pricing domains of one TCSPP are computed and tabulated, with the surprise that redundant constraints shrink the column set without raising the bound while one family of inflow bounds closes the gap (pp. 80–81). The decomposition space is written down (hundreds or thousands of candidates; 2^ℓ subsets of constraint classes) and must be scored, the best bound being no goal (pp. 63–64); "We cannot speak of “the” decomposition" of an ILP (p. 66). Exit A, learning the structure and whether to use it, is cited (Basso and Ceselli 2023, Kruber et al. 2017) (p. 66). The checkpoint (a proxy that looks right is not evidence) appears in the authors' words, "don't be deluded by the beauty" (p. 65). Exit B: an open conjecture is published as such (Note 4.14, p. 43).
- ⚠ Method 3, variant. The chapter's only timed comparison is one scene-selection instance with CPLEX 11.2 and no hardware stated, under a general lead sentence ("Exploiting the structure helps a lot!", p. 95; Table 4.10, p. 97). It is a textbook illustration, but the claim is not sized to one instance. On pricing strategy the authors defer to measurement: "One should obviously carry out some experiments" (p. 95).
- ✅ Method 4 (synthesis writing as a research instrument), evidence. One notation covers convexification, discretization and their block variants (pp. 5–58). Open questions are published: the ε bound (p. 42), the Note 4.14 conjecture (p. 43), partition refinement not yet exploited (p. 64), Exercise 4.21 (p. 120). Step 6, credit: a PhD student's observation becomes Prop 4.5 under his name (p. 112), and Villeneuve is credited for Prop 4.15 (p. 111).
- ✅ H1 (heuristic inside an exact frame), evidence: partial pricing of 15 of up to 1131 subproblems per iteration at Air France (p. 29); many commodities priced per Dijkstra tree, keeping a diverse subset (pp. 94–95); the Lagrangian bound for arbitrary duals (4.68), the bound H1 says to monitor (p. 39); a filter on the pricing domain is acceptable only if, never turned off, it shows no false negatives (p. 82).
- ✅ H5 (reopen a verdict by changing the formulation), evidence: "the integrality property is associated with the formulation or model used, not with the problem statement" (p. 67); two cutting-stock compact formulations give the same master bound (pp. 72–77); a grouping that leaves the time constraints out of pricing makes the reformulation "useless" (pp. 82, 87); "Compact formulations are not created equal" (p. 110).
- ✅ Taste G4 (structure, not a pretty decomposition), evidence: p. 65 is the source of G4's "book p. 237" quote. The chapter adds the operational test that a "structured" model has subproblems for which there is "a good oiled machinery ready to go" (p. 65).
- New-pattern candidate: name what the reformulation buys (bound, size, symmetry or non-linear cost). pp. 27–28, 41, 92–95, 98, 100–102.
- New-pattern candidate: constraint-grouping rules from bound theory. Semi-assignment rows go to the master (Prop 4.5, pp. 22–23, 65, 91); integrality-breaking constraints and the constraints that matter go into pricing (pp. 65, 82, 87); redundant constraints in pricing do not raise the bound (p. 81); identical blocks are aggregated (pp. 33, 65).
- New-pattern candidate: audit a pricing-domain filter for false negatives; a practical rule that would reject a better solution anyway is really a constraint of the problem. pp. 82, 103.

**Transferable techniques**:
- Integrality-property screen before building: if the pricing formulation is a shortest path, an assignment, a circulation or any other formulation with the integrality property, the master bound equals the LP bound (Props 4.7 and 4.13, pp. 27, 39). The one-to-many patterns of the generalized assignment problem (p. 90) and the multi-commodity flow (p. 95) are the chapter's cases.
- Bound-ladder table on a toy instance: define a sequence of pricing domains by adding constraint subsets or implied constraints, and tabulate master size, z⋆_MP and ⌈z⋆_MP⌉ against z⋆_LP and z⋆_ILP (Table 4.6, p. 80).
- False-negative/false-positive table for a heuristic restriction of the pricing domain, classifying each object against both the master-LP optimum and the integer optimum (Table 4.7, p. 82).
- Big-M sensitivity row: report z⋆_LP for M = 25, 50, 100, 500 and 1000 (Tables 4.8–4.9, pp. 84, 86).
- Disaggregate an aggregated identical-block solution into binary per-block λ^k by greedily filling a zero-cost transportation problem (p. 36), or by the lexicographic formula (4.184) of Exercise 4.9 (p. 115).
- Keep the aggregated convexity constraint as Σλ_p − v = 0 with a static variable v ∈ [0, κ] whose bounds the branching decisions change (p. 50).
- Blocks that need not be used: a binary x0^k with D^k x^k ≥ d^k x0^k and x^k ≤ u^k x0^k turns the convexity rows into ≤ 1 (pp. 46–48).
- Reverse Dantzig-Wolfe check for any column model: write the compact formulation it reformulates (Props 4.15–4.17, pp. 53–58) and confirm that this compact model keeps the decision variables of the real problem (Note 4.18, p. 58; the edge-coloring case, p. 110).
- Structure detection by constraint classes: try subsets of whole constraint classes as the border and count the connected components of the remaining bipartite graph (p. 63).
- Size argument when there is no bound gain: count the rows and columns of the compact model against the master (pp. 93–94).
- Initialize column generation for a multi-depot vehicle scheduling problem by aggregating all depots into one single-depot network-flow problem and using its solution in the master (Exercise 4.20(h), the strategy of Oukil et al. 2007, p. 120).
