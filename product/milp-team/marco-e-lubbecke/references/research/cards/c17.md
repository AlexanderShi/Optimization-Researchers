- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - No gap is claimed. The reader is expected to know LP already, so the chapter "serves to formalize terminology under a common notation", with the promise of something for "even our most seasoned readers" (p. 2). The abstract lists the programme: primal and dual formulations, optimality conditions, the primal simplex algorithm and some aspects of ILPs (p. 1).
  - The frame is decision support. OR is introduced as analytical methods that help decision-makers build or adjust operational, tactical and strategic plans (p. 2). Dantzig's epigraph makes solving the originating problems the test of a theory (p. 1); Chvátal's ties LP to problems of management (p. 6).
  - Scope is chosen backwards from the later chapters. The polyhedral definitions come first because postponing them would hinder the flow of the advanced material (p. 3). The primal simplex algorithm is the only LP algorithm presented, because the column generation algorithm of the next chapter "is the primal simplex algorithm" with pricing over an implicit set of variables (Note 1.8, p. 22). Dual simplex, primal-dual, ellipsoid and interior point methods are named in Note 1.8 and set aside (p. 22).
  - The optimality conditions already contain the later opening. A variable with positive reduced cost is “useless”: removing it leaves the optimum unchanged, and the authors ask how many such variables there are (p. 11). Read together with ch. 2, this is the entry point for column generation [inferred link].
  - ILP enters as LP plus integrality: NP-hard, the LP relaxation as a lower bound, and dual variables meaningful only in that relaxation (p. 23).
  - Transferable: open a monograph or thesis with a foundations chapter that says which later chapter needs each fact, and state what you leave out and why (pp. 3, 22).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - One idea organizes the LP half: pricing. The minimum reduced cost c̄(π) is written as an optimization program SP (Note 1.2, p. 16). The same notation returns in column generation (ch. 2), Dantzig-Wolfe (chs. 3–4) and Lagrangian relaxation (ch. 6), and "their purpose is always the same: pricing the variables" (p. 16). Fig. 1.5 (the LP sends π to SP; SP returns c̄(π) and an entering variable) is the template that is later adapted to column generation, Dantzig-Wolfe and Lagrangian relaxation (p. 18).
  - Five changes against a standard LP review:
    - (a) Every primal constraint carries its dual variable in brackets, [π], and every dual constraint its primal variable, [x] (p. 8). Duals are listed even when only the primal is shown (p. 11).
    - (b) The dual is written with right-multiplication (b⊺π), so that the same recipe, Table 1.1, recovers the primal from the dual (Note 1.1, p. 8).
    - (c) Extreme points and extreme rays get a binary flag, 1 for a point and 0 for a direction, because their vectors can coincide (Fig. 1.2). This is needed "notably" in the Dantzig-Wolfe chapters (pp. 5–6).
    - (d) Optimality can be tested with any x ≥ 0 and any π ≥ 0 (p. 11). Note 1.4 then gives a lower bound at every simplex iteration, z_LP + κ c̄(π) ≤ z⋆_LP ≤ z_LP, and generalizes it to arbitrary π ≥ 0 (p. 19).
    - (e) Pricing is itself written as an LP over convex combinations of columns ((1.49)–(1.50), p. 34), with positive weights such as w_j = ‖a_j‖ ((1.51)–(1.52), pp. 34–35). Two variants change which reduced costs are fixed at zero: the improved primal simplex (IPS) imposes c̄_j = 0 only on positive variables, and minimum mean cycle-canceling (MMCC) optimizes all reduced costs; both give only non-degenerate pivots (p. 35).
  - Kept from the standard: Dantzig's rule, the ratio test, degenerate pivots, Phase I and big-M, the bounded-variable simplex (pp. 16–22), LP-based branch-and-bound after Land and Doig and branch-and-cut after Padberg and Rinaldi (pp. 28–33).
  - The ILP half's idea: strength belongs to the formulation. A formulation is a polyhedron P with Y = P ∩ Z^n, and "stronger" means strict inclusion (Defs 1.18 and 1.21, pp. 24–25). Extended formulations add variables and project back onto the "original" ones (Defs 1.23–1.24, pp. 25–26). The trivial extended formulation, one λ per integer point, is perfect but impractical (1.44) (p. 26). The integrality property is an attribute of the model, not of the problem (p. 26, quote 1).
  - Transferable: present every model as a primal–dual pair with [π] after each constraint, so that the pricing problem and every bound are one line away (pp. 8, 11, 16).
- **D3 Problem class & data model** (team.json):
  - Forms: LP in inequality form, min c⊺x, Ax ≥ b, x ≥ 0 (1.2) (p. 6); standard form with A of full row rank for the simplex (1.17) (p. 14; Note 1.3, p. 19); bounds ℓ ≤ x ≤ u (1.37) (p. 21); a pure ILP with x ∈ Z^n_+ (1.43) (p. 23). MILP appears only in Note 1.10 (pp. 23–24), as in chs. 4 and 7 (cards S084, S077).
  - Data: rational throughout, "even when we write" P ⊆ R^n (p. 4). This is justified by an irrational polyhedron whose integer hull has infinitely many extreme points and is not a polyhedron, and by an ILP (1.55) that is feasible and bounded below by 0 yet has no optimal integer solution (pp. 35–37).
  - Structures: totally unimodular matrices (Theorem 1.2, Corollary 1.4, p. 27); the capacitated minimum cost flow problem with balanced supplies on a node-arc incidence matrix (1.45) (pp. 27–28), with an unbalanced variant repaired by one extra node (Exercise 1.12, p. 40); consecutive-ones matrices (Exercise 1.13, pp. 40–41); minimum-weight perfect matching with blossom inequalities (Exercise 1.15, pp. 41–42); and a knapsack extended formulation deferred to Example 4.1 (p. 26). No model library and no MIPLIB instance appear.
  - Assumptions stated for correctness:
    - At least one extreme point, for Minkowski–Weyl (Theorem 1.1, p. 4).
    - A κ that bounds Σ_j x_j in every optimal solution, for the bound (1.35) (p. 19).
    - c⊺x > 0 for all feasible x, for the 1/(1−γ) reading of a gap stop (p. 30).
    - Integer b for Theorem 1.2; integer b and c for Corollary 1.4 (p. 27).
    - Integer objective coefficients in Illustration 1.1, which licenses rounding a node bound up (p. 31).
    - The relative integrality gap is undefined for z⋆_ILP = 0 (p. 25).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design, proof devices** (team.json):
  - **Proof devices**:
    - Prop 1.1 (an optimal extreme point exists): contradiction on the optimal face A ∩ {c⊺x = z⋆_LP} (p. 7).
    - Prop 1.3 (the dual of the dual is the primal): the recipe applied twice (p. 8).
    - Weak duality: multiply Ax ≥ b by π and A⊺π ≤ c by x (Prop 1.5, p. 9). Complementary slackness: (c⊺ − π⊺A)x = 0 and π⊺(Ax − b) = 0 are sums of non-negative products (Prop 1.7, pp. 10–11).
    - Constructive proofs with the algorithm just taught: Farkas' lemma via Phase I and strong duality via the optimal basis are left as Exercises 1.3 and 1.8 (pp. 9, 39).
    - Minkowski–Weyl: the step-by-step proof is left to Nemhauser and Wolsey and replaced by "three important ideas" (p. 5).
    - Prop 1.9 (integrality property if and only if conv(A ∩ Z^n_+) = A), the ⇒ direction: an extreme point of A outside the integer hull, plus an objective that selects it uniquely (pp. 26–27).
    - Theorem 1.2 through integer inverses of totally unimodular bases; Corollary 1.4 through the transpose of a TU matrix plus strong duality (p. 27).
    - Prop 1.10 (node potentials can be shifted by any α): α cancels in c_ij − π_i + π_j (p. 28).
    - The lower bounds (1.35)–(1.36) from κ and c̄(π) (p. 19; Exercise 1.9, p. 40).
    - Reduced-cost fixing from the lower-bound sensitivity line (1.16): if c̄_j > UB − LB, then x_j = 0 in every optimal solution, and a feasible rather than optimal dual suffices (p. 30). The upper-bound version is Exercise 1.14 (p. 41).
    - Row differencing to prove total unimodularity of consecutive-ones matrices (Exercise 1.13 hint, p. 41). Blossom separation, first as an ILP that maximizes left-hand side minus right-hand side, then polynomially through the cut form (1.59) (Exercise 1.15 (e)–(f), p. 42).
    - A counterexample computed as a table: the first ten extreme points of the upper part of an irrational integer hull, whose slopes converge to √3 (Table 1.3, pp. 36–37).
  - **Implementation design**:
    - Per iteration the simplex needs only π, b̄ and ā_ℓ. The basis inverse is never formed. An LU factorization costs O(m³) once and O(m²) per solve, with the updates of Bartels–Golub, Forrest–Tomlin and Reid, and a refactorization "must occur" once numerical errors accumulate (Note 1.5, p. 20). Algorithm 1.1 annotates its lines with costs from O(m³) down to O(1), partly legible (p. 20).
    - Termination: finite without degeneracy; 2^n − 1 pivots in the worst case under Dantzig's rule (Klee–Minty); cycling is possible under degeneracy (Hoffman) (Note 1.6, p. 20).
    - Phase I and II merged with big-M. An artificial variable that stays positive in an optimal basis means either that M is too small or that the LP is infeasible, so feasibility "becomes more tricky to handle" (p. 19). Ch. 2 replaces this by Farkas pricing (card S060, pp. 10–11).
    - Solver reality: there are as many simplex variants as programmers. A modern solver's options include presolve, structure exploitation, partial pricing, factorization updates and numerical stability settings (Note 1.7, p. 21).
    - Bounded-variable simplex: non-basic variables sit at a lower or upper bound, and the sign of c̄ decides which may enter (pp. 21–22).
    - Branch-and-bound (pp. 28–30):
      - Node values are inherited from the parent until solved and are monotone along a descendant line; LB is the minimum over live nodes (1.46).
      - Three pruning rules (bound, integrality, infeasibility) and the sandwich z⋆_LP ≤ LB ≤ z⋆_ILP ≤ UB (1.47). The method terminates with a certificate of optimality when no node is live (p. 29).
      - Depth-, breadth- and best-first node selection; depth-first is "memory-friendly" (pp. 29–30).
      - The dual simplex re-optimizes after branching, since the old basis is infeasible but usually still optimal (p. 30).
      - A relative-gap stop with tolerance γ, carrying the 1/(1−γ) guarantee, and reduced-cost fixing (p. 30).
    - Branch-and-cut: exact or heuristic separation within a limited effort, otherwise branch. A compromise must be struck between separation time and tree size (p. 33). Note 1.12 names the "choreography" of preprocessing, cutting-plane strategies, primal heuristics, node selection and branching, deferred to ch. 7 (p. 33).
  - Transferable: prove each bound or fixing rule for an arbitrary feasible dual vector, not only for the optimal one; the duals of a truncated or heuristic solve then still bound and fix (pp. 19, 30).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Write every model as a primal–dual pair, with the dual variable in brackets after each constraint, so that reduced costs, pricing and bounds can be read off directly (pp. 8, 11).
- State lower bounds and reduced-cost fixing for an arbitrary feasible dual vector: z⋆ ≥ π⊺b + κ c̄(π), and fix x_j = 0 when c̄_j > UB − LB. Duals from truncated, heuristic or early-stopped solves then still bound and fix (pp. 19, 30; Exercises 1.9 and 1.14, pp. 40–41).
- With integer objective coefficients, round a node bound up before the pruning test: ⌈7110.5⌉ = 7111 prunes node 29 against UB = 7111 (p. 31).
- When you drop integrality because a formulation has the integrality property, check that your LP algorithm returns an extreme point. The property guarantees that an integer optimum exists, not that the solution returned is integer (p. 26; Exercise 1.11, p. 40).
- Before concluding that a problem is hard for LP-based methods, try another formulation: a positive integrality gap belongs to the model, not to the problem (p. 26).
- Write the pricing step as an LP over convex combinations (min Σ c̄_j y_j with Σ y_j = 1, or with a weighted normalization Σ w_j y_j = 1). Pricing variants such as normalized, IPS-type or MMCC-type rules then come from one model (pp. 34–35).
- Report a stopping tolerance together with the bound it is measured against. In Illustration 1.1 the same incumbent gives 0.0050 or 0.0054, depending on whether the current best bound or the parent node's value is used (p. 32).
- Before comparing node counts across codes, ask when each code creates child nodes. Eager creation counts nodes that are pruned at creation (nodes 5 and 6 in Fig. 1.7), and lazy creation never makes them (p. 32); the cross-code consequence is my inference.
- Present a classical theorem you only use through a reference for the full proof plus the ideas your later chapters rely on (p. 5). Prove Farkas' lemma and strong duality with the algorithm you have just presented (Exercises 1.3 and 1.8, pp. 9, 39).
- When you repeat a folklore performance figure, quote its source together with what the source leaves unspecified (phase, start, pivoting rule), as the chapter does with Dantzig and Shamir (pp. 20–21).
