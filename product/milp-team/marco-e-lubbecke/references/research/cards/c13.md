- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The chapter enters from what the book has not yet done. The aim "is to solve an integer linear program", but so far only the linear relaxation of a Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation is solved, that is, the root node (pp. 1, 3). When that solution is fractional, "we need to cut it, or branch" (p. 1). The bird's-eye answer (column generation in every node, plus valid inequalities) is set against "some subtleties" that "have to be respected or can even be exploited" (p. 1). Three entry questions follow. What does "integer" mean for a master solution? An integral λ gives an integral x but not conversely; aggregated identical blocks need a disaggregation before the test; integrality can depend on how rays are scaled (pp. 5–6). Where does a decision go? There are two variable spaces and two constraint groups (pp. 7–10, 22–24). What does it do to pricing (Note 7.3, p. 9; Note 7.7, p. 23)? The applied motive is given in the reference notes: in two decades of GENCOL applications "the main difficulties always came from the design of branching and cutting rules: they were different in all applications" (p. 94). Even the name is argued: column generation changes an implementation so much that cutting complements branch-and-price, hence branch-price-and-cut rather than branch-and-cut-and-price (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable: before designing cuts or branching for a column-generation code, write the integrality test for your master in the order "λ integral? otherwise x integral, after disaggregation if blocks are aggregated?" (pp. 5–6). It tells you in which space you will have to branch.
- **D2 Core idea**: Every cut and every branching decision is a constraint. It can be written in the original x or in the master λ, and it can go into the master constraint group A or into the pricing domain D (pp. 8–10, 23–24). In A it is robust: the pricing domain is unchanged and only the reduced-cost objective gets new dual terms (p. 8). In D it is convexified and potentially stronger by the inclusion (7.29), but the pricing algorithm may change and incompatible columns must leave the RMP (pp. 10, 17). The deciding distinction is not the space but whether the master coefficients are linear in x. When they are not (Chvátal-Gomory, clique, odd-circuit, Ryan-Foster, inter-task), pricing must compute them with extra variables, an extended formulation of the ISP (pp. 18, 22, 49–50). Master-variable branching follows Vanderbeck. Never branch on a single λ: its down-branch "has essentially no effect", and pricing would have to be kept from regenerating the column (p. 29). Branch instead on the sum of λ over the columns that satisfy a condition on x (a hyperplane, or a small set of bounds) that pricing can check (pp. 29–38). Kept from branch-and-bound: the tree, the variable disjunction, candidate ranking, node selection and primal heuristics (pp. 23, 44, 50, 59). Changed: a node costs much more, so "branching decisions can become even more critical", and every decision must reach pricing (p. 23). Convexification and discretization coincide at the root and differ only through branching, which reaches interior points of D in both: "how you branch determines how you decompose(d)" (pp. 45–49).
  - Transferable: ask two questions, in this order, of any proposed cut or branching rule. Is its master coefficient a linear function of x? Then it is robust and only changes the pricing objective (pp. 8, 22). If not, which extra pricing variables and linear constraints compute it, and does the pricing algorithm survive them (pp. 18, 21, 31–32)?
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: The object is the pure ILP min c⊺x, Ax ≥ b, Dx ≥ d, x ∈ Z^n_+, assumed feasible with a finite optimum (p. 4). The mixed-integer case is "not explicitly discussed"; its generalization is called natural, and convexification and discretization may then be mixed over continuous and discrete variables (p. 50). There are two reformulations: convexification over the extreme points and rays of conv(D) (IMP, p. 4) and discretization over its integer points and integer-scaled rays (p. 5). Both have the same root relaxation (p. 5). The λ-branching theory assumes D a polytope or a polyhedral cone and is written for one pricing problem, adaptable to identical or non-identical blocks (p. 29).
  - Correctness rests on four facts: Hilbert-Giles-Pulleyblank (the λ of any subset of columns sum to an integer in an integer solution, p. 29); a basic solution has at most m positive λ (p. 37); an RMP has no identical columns (Ryan-Foster, which therefore also holds for interior-point solutions, p. 39); and integer data wherever objective values or times are rounded (pp. 13, 15, 25, 88). Master cuts are restricted to rank 1 with integer coefficients (p. 18), with rank 2 treated later (pp. 64–65).
  - Structures exploited: set-partitioning masters with identical blocks (vertex coloring, bin packing; pp. 27, 74–76) or with resource-constrained path pricing (VRPTW, crew pairing; pp. 39–41, 78–85); block-diagonal non-identical blocks (capacitated p-median, generalized assignment, pilot rostering, locomotive assignment; pp. 72–74, 85–93); acyclic networks with labeling algorithms (pp. 51–52, 82–85). Sufficiency is class-bound: inter-task branching suffices for pure set partitioning with (E)SPPRC pricing, but not with side constraints (p. 41).
  - Test material: hand-sized instances (a VRPTW with four customers, a six-node time constrained shortest path problem, a one-variable ILP; pp. 14, 46, 68) and published applications (Solomon's RC101.25, Air Canada, Canadian National; pp. 79–80, 85–93). No MIPLIB instance appears.
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Integrality test with disaggregation: test λ first, then x; with aggregated identical blocks, disaggregate first by a zero-cost balanced transportation problem, preferred to the even split because it is likely "more λ-integral" (pp. 5–6, 28).
  - Cuts. Algorithm 7.1 interleaves separation on the original variables with column generation (p. 9). A cut placed in D requires the incompatible columns to be removed (p. 10). Clique cuts in the original variables of vertex coloring are satisfied by every independent-set column, so they cannot cut: "All our efforts were correct but in vain!" (pp. 11–12). Note 7.5 generalizes this with Witt's 2019 experiments (p. 12). Rounded capacity cuts enter pricing as adjusted arc costs (p. 14). z-cuts LB ≤ c⊺x ≤ UB placed in the pricing domain delete non-optimal integer columns and solve the TCSPP example without branching (pp. 15–17). Master cuts have their coefficients computed in pricing: Chvátal-Gomory rank 1 with the ceiling written as y ≤ x + 1 − ε, y integer (pp. 19–20); clique cuts on the conflict graph with |Q| + 2 pricing constraints per cut and a maximum-weight-clique separation (p. 21). When there are too many cuts: a pool and standard selection (Note 7.6, p. 21).
  - Branching on x, in A (dual slopes in pricing) or in D (domain split, columns removed). The second is "usually preferred" for the bound; the first means less interference with pricing (pp. 23–24). An explicit master lets a MILP solver branch on x "without “telling” the pricing problem", so the local bounds must be copied into pricing by hand (p. 24). Multiway branching on an arc sequence uses five branches for its 16 configurations (pp. 25–26); time-window branching follows Gélinas et al. 1995 (p. 25).
  - Implementation invariant (Note 7.8): there is "only one" LP in the whole tree. Columns are therefore never deleted but fixed by local bounds, applied "not when a branching decision is made, but when a node or one of its descendants is visited to be processed" (p. 26).
  - Branching on λ. Proposition 7.1 (Vanderbeck 2000) is proved by separating one fractional column from the convex hull of the other fractional ones with an integer hyperplane (pp. 30–31). Proposition 7.2 is proved by halving the fractionality: split at the midpoint of a coordinate where two fractional columns differ. This at least halves the fractionality, so at most t bounds are needed when it is below 2^t, and fewer than log m for a basic solution (pp. 35–37). The 0/1 coefficient g_x is computed in pricing by the two inequalities (7.63), which need bounds ℓ ≤ f⊺x ≤ u, by one inequality per branch that uses the sign of the branching dual (7.66), or as g_x = x_j for a binary x_j (pp. 31–35). Nested partitions keep the number of pricing problem types linear in the number of λ-branchings (p. 33). The search for bounds restarts at each node (pp. 37–38).
  - Ryan-Foster. Existence is proved from a row covered by two fractional columns plus a row that separates them (pp. 38–39). In a weighted-independent-set pricing, same and differ become a contraction of r and s and an inserted edge, so the combinatorial algorithm stays (p. 75). In bin packing the differ branch turns the binary knapsack into a strongly NP-hard knapsack with conflicts (p. 76). Inter-task (follow-on) branching is a different rule, not a special case: "This is not true!" (p. 40). It stays cheap in labeling, with one extra label component for the last task covered (pp. 39–41; Exercise 7.10, pp. 97–98). Rules 1–3.4 branch on sums of λ for cost intervals, for the columns per block and for pairs of binaries (pp. 41–42).
  - Ranking candidates: priorities from the application (global design decisions before local ones; "usually, the down-branch is weaker than the up-branch"), pseudo-costs, strong branching, reliability and hybrid branching; most-fractional branching rejected (p. 44). For branch-and-price, strong branching runs in two phases: an RMP re-solve for every candidate, then column and cut generation with heuristic pricing for a shortlist (Pecin et al. 2017b; p. 58).
  - Exact acceleration indexed by three indicators: column generation iterations, RMP time against ISP time, and number of nodes (pp. 52–53). Each indicator has its menu (pp. 53–59):
    - many iterations: many or complementary columns, dual strategies, early branching on a good Lagrangian bound (pp. 53–54);
    - RMP time: degeneracy remedies, density reduction, lazy constraints, column management (remove m columns when the RMP holds 3m, at least 5 iterations apart), column selection (pp. 54–55);
    - ISP time: partial pricing (stop after 3 successful pricing problems, or after 2 failures and at least 1 success; full pricing in the last iteration), heuristic pricing, variable fixing, relaxed (ng-route) pricing, and rolling back the last round of cuts when pricing gets too hard (pp. 55–57);
    - many nodes: bound strengthening, strong branching, column enumeration, preprocessing (pp. 57–59).
  - Reduced-cost arc fixing: forward and backward labeling give dual intervals, and hence the maximum reduced cost of an arc. It equals the minimum reduced cost of a path through that arc ((7.96), pp. 51–52).
  - Primal heuristics: the restricted master ("price-and-branch"), complementary pricing, diving and rounding (pp. 60–62). A dive fixes λ only when it is close to 1 (fractional parts ≥ 0.9 for several fixings at once) and otherwise fixes arcs; it backtracks through several up-branches (p. 61). Then sub-MILPs, large neighbourhood search, diving with a sub-MILP and dive-first-then-branch (pp. 62–63). Heuristics run on both formulations, the ILP and the IMP (p. 63).
  - Numerics appear in three places only. ε enters the ceiling linearization (p. 20). A big-M initialization of the RMP is too small (M = 100, while the first feasible column needs M > 140) and almost prunes a feasible node: "Big-M is not big enough." (p. 70). One branches when a node LP fails "because of numerical difficulties" (p. 54). Tolerances appear only as an optimality tolerance for truncated branch-and-cut pricing (p. 56). Exact arithmetic, parallel or deterministic search and performance variability do not appear (searched in pp. 1–101). Symmetry appears only as the aggregation of identical blocks and its loss through disaggregation (pp. 28, 50, 94).
  - Transferable: implement branching decisions as local bounds that a node applies when it is processed, never as column deletions in the shared LP (p. 26).
  - Transferable: when a λ-branching needs a fractional sum and the fractional parts add up to an integer, split at the midpoint of a coordinate where two fractional columns differ. Each split at least halves the fractionality, so the loop ends after fewer than log m bounds (pp. 36–37).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: The authors run no study of their own. The evidence is of two kinds.
  - Hand-computed toy runs. In a four-customer VRPTW, one rounded capacity cut for S = C raises the bound from 65.5 to 73.5 against an optimum of 74, closing 94.1% of the gap, and the objective cut closes the rest (pp. 14–15). z-cuts solve the TCSPP without branching (pp. 16–17, Fig. 7.3). A five-node tree solves it by x-branching (pp. 68–72).
  - Results quoted from the literature, with hardware and age:
    - Kohl et al.'s 2-path cuts take Solomon's RC101.25 from 406.625 to the integer optimum 461.1 with nine cuts and no branching (p. 80).
    - Gamache et al. (1998), Air Canada preferential bidding: 24 monthly problems, 710 pilot-problems; the largest has 568 pairings and 108 pilots, hence 5886 pricing problems, which take 72% of 5.7 hours on an HP9000/735 with 128 MB, "more than two decades ago". 15 of the 24 needed z-cuts on 51 pilot-problems (2.3 cuts on average, at most 7), with an average integrality gap of 15% and a maximum of 99% (p. 89).
    - Ziarati et al. (1999b), weekly locomotive assignment at CN (1988 trains, 26 types, 171 critical locomotives): 1090 locomotives in the manual plan, 1024 without facets, 1013 with facets. That is a 1.1% saving, "more than $30 M", at 33% more CPU time (2.45 to 3.27 h). A 1794-train case went from 1044 to 1030 locomotives for 24% more time (p. 93).
    - Benchimol et al. (2012): RMP time on MDVSP instances cut by a factor of up to 7 (p. 95). Jepsen et al. (2008): subset-row cuts "in many cases" prove optimality at the root (p. 85). Vanderbeck's remark that bound sets of more than one are seldom needed (p. 37).
  - Metrics: gap closed, bound sequences, integrality gaps, numbers of cuts and of locomotives, CPU hours and the share spent in pricing. Slowdowns are reported next to the gains (p. 93).
  - Absent: MIPLIB, baselines re-run under identical conditions, shifted geometric means, seeds or permutations. Several defaults come without data in the chapter: a few dozen to a few hundred columns per iteration (p. 53), the 3m/m/5 column-management rule (p. 55) and the 3/2/1 partial-pricing rule (p. 56).
- **D6 Positioning**: The branching half is presented as a synthesis of François Vanderbeck's work. The material is "largely inspired by his œuvre" (p. 28), and "we hope having well summarized his work" (p. 95). His photograph appears (p. 28), and an exercise asks who he is, noting that he is cited or mentioned close to 50 times in the book (p. 96).
  - Credit is historical. λ-branching (Ryan and Foster 1981) came "a few years before" x-branching (Desrosiers et al. 1984) (pp. 94–95). The cut theory follows Desaulniers et al. (2011) (p. 94).
  - Critique is direct and names the error. A few papers wrongly call inter-task branching a special case of Ryan-Foster (p. 40). Papers on "column generation approaches" to integer programs often implement no branching and so "do not present a true branch-and-price approach" (p. 64). Wikipedia's flowchart becomes an exercise: "What is wrong with it?" (p. 96). Most-fractional branching, "likely the most popular in research papers", is rejected on the evidence of Achterberg et al. (2005) (p. 44).
  - The MIP-solver literature is the reference point for the tree (Note 7.7, p. 23; reliability and hybrid branching, p. 44; preprocessing, p. 59; heuristics, p. 63). Competing frameworks are cited (Pessoa et al. 2020, 2021; Sadykov and Vanderbeck 2021; p. 96), while SCIP is recommended and GCG described (p. 67). - **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is textbook exposition and synthesis, plus small new items stated as such: clique cuts on master variables for set partitioning, "we are the first to write this down in this generality" (p. 94); the floor-function form (7.65) of the branching coefficient (pp. 32, 95); and z-cuts in the pricing domain, designed for preferential bidding (pp. 15–17, 94). The limits are stated concretely:
  - Proposition 7.1 does not show how to choose a hyperplane, "let alone one that is good for the branching tree" (p. 33); simple bounds alone may give no separating condition (p. 35); in practice a fractional sum may not be provable, and "then we may not care about theory" (p. 43).
  - Odd-circuit cuts do not guarantee integrality (p. 78). No efficient separation exists for k-path cuts (p. 79), and none was designed for the strong capacity cuts (p. 83). Relaxed pricing can enlarge the tree (p. 57).
  - Mixed-integer branching is only sketched (p. 50), and Vanderbeck's 2011 generic scheme "goes beyond our presentation" (p. 95).
  - For the two large applications: "If we can find good-quality integer solutions, we are very happy." (p. 68). An implementation may turn out to be fruitless (p. 67).
  - Future directions are left as exercises. The last one asks for new branching and cutting strategies for nine applications because "theory and practice have evolved", and it is the only one without a solution: "We provide the solution to all exercises in the book except this one." (p. 100).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (stated, joint): the implementation guide is Method 1 in the authors' own order.
  - "Step one is solely on paper. Don’t skip this." (p. 65).
  - Implement the compact MILP with real data first, both as a pipeline and as a benchmark of "how large (or small) instances can be solved in acceptable time?" (p. 66). The reader is pictured arriving at branch-and-price after the branch-and-cut of a MILP solver "left you unsatisfied with the results" (p. 66).
  - Reformulate by Dantzig-Wolfe rather than writing master and pricing directly, and "maybe keep it simple first" (p. 66).
  - A framework manages the tree: "It is not a secret that, e.g., SCIP is well-suited for this." (p. 67). GCG predicts a success probability before a costly implementation (p. 67).
  - Acceleration comes only after a basic branch-and-price has run on a representative set of relatively large instances, read through three indicators (pp. 52–53); this is Method 1 step 4 and Workflow C step 3. - Method 1 variant ⚠ (step 6 and the stop rule):
  - Step 6 holds as "keep the original variables in view", but they are not always the branching object. Original-variable branching "directly works" when pricing problems are not identical (p. 49). With aggregated identical blocks, the aggregated x may be integral while λ is fractional (bin packing, p. 27), and disaggregation brings the symmetry back (pp. 28, 50). Decisions in original variables alone cannot be stronger than decisions that use extra variables (p. 22). So when blocks are aggregated, branch on λ-sums or on auxiliary variables.
  - The stop rule's "pricing is only a generic MIP" is softer here. MILP pricing is used where no combinatorial algorithm exists: odd-circuit pricing by branch-and-cut (p. 78), the knapsack with conflicts that "can be solved as an integer program, of course" (p. 76), and truncated branch-and-cut as heuristic pricing (p. 56).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before implementing a cut family, check it against the columns. If every column (e.g., every independent set) already satisfies it, the reformulated cut can never be violated (vertex-coloring clique cuts, pp. 11–12).
- Add cheap a priori cuts: the objective-rounding cut c⊺x ≥ ⌈z_MP⌉ for integer costs, and the rounded capacity cut for S = C (pp. 13, 15). With integer costs, impose LB ≤ c⊺x ≤ UB inside pricing to delete non-optimal columns (pp. 15–17, 88).
- Design master-variable branching as "the sum of λ over the columns that satisfy a condition on x" (a cost interval, a customer count, a bound on one x_j). Compute the 0/1 coefficient in pricing with one inequality per branch, using the sign of the branching dual ((7.66), p. 32; g_x = x_j in the binary case, p. 35).
- With a labeling pricing, prefer inter-task (follow-on) branching to Ryan-Foster and track only the last covered task in the label (pp. 40–41; Exercise 7.10, pp. 97–98). With a graph pricing, implement same/differ as node contraction and edge insertion (p. 75).
- Never delete columns in the tree; fix them by local bounds that a node applies when it is processed (p. 26). With an explicit master inside a MILP solver, copy its local x-bounds into pricing yourself (p. 24).
- Diagnose before accelerating: run the basic code on relatively large instances and read column generation iterations, RMP against pricing time, and node count. Then pick from the technique family for the dominant bottleneck (pp. 52–59).
- Starting defaults, to be measured: remove m columns when the RMP holds 3m, with at least 5 iterations between removals (p. 55). In multi-block pricing, stop after 3 successful pricing problems, or after 2 failures and at least 1 success, and price fully at the end (p. 56).
- Separate the weak robust version of a cut with existing heuristics, and lift it to the strong non-robust version only if it stays violated after re-optimization (Note 7.14, p. 83). Roll back the last round of cuts and branch instead when pricing exceeds a label-count threshold (p. 57).
- Run strong branching in two phases: re-optimize only the RMP for every candidate, then run column and cut generation with heuristic pricing for a shortlist (p. 58).
- Run the MIP solver's own heuristics on the compact model to seed the RMP. Dive on λ only when it is close to 1, and otherwise fix arcs. Finish by solving the RMP of all generated columns as a MILP (diving with sub-MILP) (pp. 61–63).
- When the RMP starts from big-M artificials, check M against the reduced cost the first feasible column needs before pruning a node as infeasible (p. 70).
- Branch first to make a low-dimensional substructure explicit, then cut with its facets on that branch (at most two locomotive types per train, pp. 91–92).
- Call a code without a branching rule a restricted-master heuristic (price-and-branch), not branch-and-price (pp. 60, 64).
