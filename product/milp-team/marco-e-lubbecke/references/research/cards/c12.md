### S076 · Fast Subproblem Solving in Benders Decomposition for Affine Potential-Based Flow Problems with Topology Switching and Scenarios (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2611.15203)

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The application pressure comes first. The German grid is shifting to many decentralised renewable producers, and re-dispatch cost rose from €113.3 million (2013) to €2,689.2 million (2022) (pp. 1–2). Contingency analysis may be legally required (BNetzA 2016). The structure "naturally" lends itself to Benders: the master holds the shared switching and demand decisions, and one subproblem per contingency checks flow feasibility and cost (p. 2).

  The gap is placed in a component, not in the decomposition. Earlier Benders work on potential-based flows improves the master (cuts, LP relaxation time, tree size), while subproblem efficiency is "relatively underexplored" (p. 2). Related Work repeats the gap and answers the obvious objection: stronger cuts mean fewer iterations, but a specialised subproblem algorithm "may still reduce overall running time" (p. 3).

  The entry fact is classical: the flow in a passive affine potential network is unique (Birkhoff and Diaz 1956; Koch et al. 2015; p. 3). Alexander Helber pointed this property out to the authors (acknowledgments, p. 32). Problem statement: from a fixed master solution (b̂, ẑ, η̂_C), decide for each contingency whether to add an optimality cut, a feasibility cut or nothing, with a constant number of linear systems (pp. 9–11, 16). The premise that subproblems dominate is quantified only in the results: with Gurobi on the subproblems they take 72.49% of the running time, and the LP solve alone takes 49.46% (pp. 28–29).
  - Transferable technique: before improving the master of a Benders code, measure the time split between master and subproblems. If the subproblems dominate and have a known uniqueness property, attack the subproblem solver rather than the cuts (pp. 2–3, 28–29).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: The key insight is that once topology and demands are fixed, "there is nothing to optimize" in a subproblem, so the general simplex method is overhead (p. 32). Three linear systems replace the LP:
  - the primal flow comes from one system whose matrix is the negated weighted Laplacian of the contracted network ((10), p. 14);
  - the optimal dual for an optimality cut comes from a system with the same matrix and another right-hand side ((16), p. 20);
  - the Farkas ray for a feasibility cut comes from that matrix bordered by one row and one column (pp. 22, 26–27).

  At most two systems are solved per subproblem (p. 32). The pipeline (p. 11):
  1. affine potential functions are reduced to the identity (Prop. 1, pp. 7–9);
  2. inactive switch arcs are dropped (Prop. 2, p. 12);
  3. active switch-arc components are contracted into representative nodes (Theorem 1, pp. 12–13);
  4. the primal system gives potentials, overloads o = max{…}, the objective value and the hard-bound checks, which decide the cut type (pp. 14–16);
  5. for an optimality cut, a primal basis is chosen deterministically and the dual system is solved; the switch-arc duals are recovered on spanning trees (pp. 16–20);
  6. for a feasibility cut, exactly one cut is added per infeasible subproblem, flow violations before potential violations (p. 21).

  What stays unchanged is the Benders scheme itself: branch-and-Benders-cut with the textbook cuts (6) (p. 10). The evaluation changes "exclusively" the subproblem method, "leaving the output unchanged" (p. 28). What changes is the LP solver inside the subproblem. Factorizations are also reused across subproblems (master solutions, contingencies, primal and dual), "rather than bases of the same problem" (p. 4). The reason for the change: the simplex method may solve "a potentially very high number" of linear systems, this algorithm "only one or two of equal or smaller size" (p. 11).

  The authors call it "a specialized simplex algorithm" that obtains (a sufficiently large part of) an optimal basis in one step (p. 3), "simplex-free" but "simplex-inspired" (p. 32). They place it among specialised Benders subproblem algorithms (Fischetti et al. 2017, knapsack; Contreras et al. 2011, semi-assignment; p. 3).

  The factorization updates:
  - switching one line arc changes the matrix by a rank-1 term q rᵀ, updated in O(|V|²) (Stange et al. 2007; p. 23);
  - a switch-arc change merges, splits or leaves the active components (three cases, p. 24);
  - a switch arc paired with a single line arc is treated like a contingency of that line (p. 24).

  Algorithm 1 updates from the previous master topology, walks the contingencies applying and reverting rank-1 updates, and reverts all contingency changes at the end of the round (pp. 24–27).
  - Transferable technique: when a decomposition subproblem is an LP whose primal is fixed by equations, select an optimal primal basis deterministically from the primal solution and derive the dual from it. Then check whether the dual system shares the primal matrix. If it does, one factorization serves the feasibility test, the optimality cut and, bordered, the feasibility cut (pp. 16–20, 26).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: An MILP whose binary switching variables z enter big-M on/off constraints (1a)–(1b). The big-M parameters are chosen so that the bound "can never" be met while the arc is on (p. 6). The other parts (pp. 6–7):
  - continuous demands b with cost F; per-contingency flows, potentials and overloads o ≥ 0 with cost F^o;
  - a cardinality constraint (2b) allowing at most Z switches away from the default state;
  - one copy of flows and potentials per contingency, (2c)–(2n);
  - single-arc contingencies only, "for the sake of simplicity", although the methods are said to "extend naturally" to arbitrary contingencies (p. 6).

  Islanding-freeness (all demand nodes connected by active arcs) is left out of the model (p. 6). It is enforced by separation and checked by a minimum cut on the contracted graph (p. 11). Potential functions are affine with positive slope (p. 7). The problem is NP-hard already for the identity function (Lehmann et al. 2014; p. 9).

  The proofs use these assumptions:
  - positive conductances (p. 4; Theorem 2, p. 15);
  - no line arc inside a switch-arc component, and at most one line arc between two components (p. 5; used in the contraction, p. 13);
  - balanced demands, Σ b_u = 0 (p. 5; Theorem 3, p. 21);
  - a connected subproblem graph, with nodes outside the demand component removed (p. 11).

  The objective sums the overload costs over contingencies, and a maximum is named as an alternative (p. 6). The test structure is DC security-constrained optimal transmission switching (SCOTS) (p. 28):
  - r = 1, with s modelling, e.g., transformers;
  - most line arcs are switchable, modelled as a pairing of "two correspondingly parametrized line arcs and one switch arc"; substations are switch-arc components;
  - N−1 contingencies: one per line arc, unless it splits the network.

  Because every affine instance transforms into an identity-potential (and so SCOTS-type) instance, the authors call these instances "representative for the larger class" (p. 4; the transformation is repeated on p. 28). [reading] The transformation shows that the class maps into SCOTS form. It does not show that PGLib-derived instances are representative in size or structure.
  - Transferable technique: reduce a parameterised family to one canonical form (affine to identity potential functions by rescaling conductances and shifting bounds, demands and objective). One algorithm and one benchmark class then cover the family (Prop. 1, pp. 7–9; p. 28).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: The decomposition is branch-and-Benders-cut (cf. Rahmaniani et al. 2017):
  - the master holds b, z, (2b), Σ b = 0 and one η_C ≥ 0 per contingency (p. 9);
  - every integer-feasible master solution is checked against all subproblems; no separation at fractional master points is described (p. 10);
  - feasibility cuts come from Farkas multipliers ((6)); optimality cuts have the same left-hand side with η_C on the right (p. 10);
  - cuts are added through Gurobi callbacks (p. 28), at most one per subproblem per round, and all subproblems are solved every round (p. 32).

  The proof chain that keeps the cuts exact:
  1. change of variables x′ = x + s/r, with rescaled conductance µ′ = µ/r, shifted bounds and demands, and a constant objective term (Prop. 1, pp. 7–9);
  2. zero-flow extension plus a restriction argument for dropping inactive switch arcs (Prop. 2, p. 12);
  3. contraction preserves line flows: the nodal balances of the two merged nodes are summed ((7)+(8)), by induction over arcs. Switch-arc flows are recovered from the leaves of a spanning tree, "in analogy to non-basic variables in a network simplex algorithm" (Theorem 1, p. 13);
  4. uniqueness: a reference node (a spanning-tree leaf) is fixed, since flows do not change when all potentials are shifted. The reduced Laplacian is weakly diagonally dominant, strictly so in a row next to the reference node, and irreducible, hence of full rank (Johnson et al. 2023; Theorem 2, pp. 15–16);
  5. the potential bounds are checked by shiftability: (9e) is violated exactly when the largest upper-bound violation cannot be shifted away without violating a lower bound (p. 16);
  6. a deterministic optimal basis: one variable per constraint, all nonzeros included, and a spanning forest of the active switch arcs. Independence follows from the unit-vector columns plus the uniquely solvable square system (p. 17);
  7. the dual system by complementary slackness: duals of constraints with positive slack are zero, σ is fixed by the primal overloads ((13b)–(13c)), α is eliminated by summing (13a) over each component, and φ is merged onto the representatives. This gives (16), with the primal matrix (pp. 18–20);
  8. optimal dual: because demands are balanced, the dual objective does not depend on the reference value. Zero duals on tight bounds are justified by loosening the bound by ε: the primal is unchanged, then complementary slackness and weak duality apply (Theorem 3, pp. 20–21);
  9. Farkas ray: a homogeneous system plus a normalisation row that fixes the dual objective at a positive value ("here, e.g., 1"). It is argued to be solvable because the primal is infeasible and the dual feasible (pp. 21–22). Unlike the optimality case, this is argued in two sentences and not stated as a theorem (p. 22);
  10. LU bordering for the feasibility-cut system: solve Uᵀℓ = b̂ and Lu = s, then set v = x̄ − ℓᵀu, each step O(|W|²) (p. 26).

  Complexity per Benders round (p. 27):
  - simplex: O(|C|·m·m²), using the empirical O(m) iteration count (Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis 1997), i.e. O(|A|⁴) when |C| grows like |A|;
  - SLE: O(|C|·2|V|³);
  - SLE-U: O(|V|³ + |C|·Ĉ·|V|²), i.e. O(|A|³) under N−1.

  [reading] This compares an empirical iteration count with worst-case bounds. The authors themselves call their analysis "overly conservative" for their method (p. 27).

  The numerical safeguard is that factorizations are "recomputed in regular intervals", among other times whenever the matrix dimension changes (p. 28). The interval, the pivoting and any tolerances are not stated. The word "tolerance" does not occur in the text, and the bound checks and the test S* > η̂ are written as exact comparisons (p. 16). Standard simplex acceleration techniques are "out of the scope of this work" (p. 27).
  - Transferable technique: prove each structural reduction (rescale, drop, contract) as a two-way solution-transfer statement ("Any optimal solution … can be transformed directly into an optimal solution … and vice versa", Theorem 1, p. 13). The exactness of the cuts then rests on a chain of small lemmas (pp. 7–13).
  - Transferable technique: to justify zero duals on tight bounds, relax the bound by ε, observe that the primal is unchanged, and apply complementary slackness and weak duality (p. 21).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Test set and setup (p. 28):
  - 174 custom SCOTS instances, made by joining parts of PGLib-OPF instances. The original set has too few instances solvable in time and many that are SCOTS-infeasible. How the set was built is deferred to "an upcoming publication".
  - 25 instances are dropped, leaving 149. They are infeasible or time out everywhere; the text says "where all instances encountered the time limit", presumably meaning all methods.
  - The runs use "scenarios with a reduced number of available switch arcs". How many arcs, the value of Z and the costs are not stated.
  - Hardware: 2× Intel Xeon L5630 (8 cores, 2.13 GHz, 16 GB), Debian 11, 3-hour limit per instance.

  The three variants (pp. 27–28):
  - GRB: Gurobi 13.0.0 solves the subproblem LP, with one model object modified between subproblems. Whether warm starts are used is not stated.
  - SLE: the linear systems without factorization updates.
  - SLE-U: the linear systems with updates.

  The master is the same in all three: Gurobi via PowerModels 0.21.85, JuMP 1.30.0 and Julia 1.12.6. The linear systems are solved with LinearSolve 3.75.0, and the update code is new (p. 28). Not run: a monolithic solve of (2a)–(2n), any other subproblem method (e.g. Alsac et al. 1983, cited p. 4), and any seeds or permutations. The thread count is not stated.

  Results:
  - Table 1 (p. 30): 141, 146 and 147 of the 149 instances solved (GRB, SLE, SLE-U). On the 136 instances solved by all, the arithmetic mean is 2321.66, 986.11 and 906.20 s, and the shifted geometric mean (shift 10) is 958.75, 367.88 and 276.02 s. My arithmetic: 1 − 276.02/958.75 = 71.21%, which matches p. 4.
  - Time split (Fig. 4, p. 29): the GRB subproblems take 72.49% of the time, of which the LP 49.46%. The master's share rises to 75.25% (SLE) and 82.93% (SLE-U). In SLE-U the linear systems take 0.70% + 0.69%, plus 0.77% for updates.
  - Performance profiles (Figs. 5–6, pp. 30–31): on total time SLE-U is fastest on 83.22% of instances and SLE on 14.77%. The text does not say which method is fastest on the remaining 2.01% (my arithmetic). On subproblem solve time SLE-U is fastest on every instance, and GRB is at least 30× slower on 86.58%.
  - Gaps of timed-out runs lie within 1.00% "for almost all instances" and are omitted (p. 28).

  Two disadvantages are reported:
  - the SLE variants solve more subproblems on average (95,747 for GRB, 98,962 for SLE, 101,760 for SLE-U), "mildly disadvantageous" and possibly a sign of weaker cuts (p. 29);
  - the miscellaneous subproblem time "remains a noticeable contributor" (p. 29).

  The theory is checked against the data with synthetic curves SLE^{2/3} and SLE^{1/2} (t^{2/3} and t^{1/2} of the SLE times). They suggest that SLE-U runs in near-linear time (Fig. 7, p. 31), with the caveat that this "only serves as an intuition" (p. 31). The instance statistics are given: 1.4683 arcs per vertex and 1.80% nonzeros (pp. 30–31). The headline 97.98% reduction in subproblem solve time (p. 1; p. 4) is not derived in any table or figure; I could not trace its computation.
  - Transferable technique: when a paper swaps one component, plot each variant's time split (master, primal solve, dual solve, update, miscellaneous) in relative and absolute terms (Fig. 4, p. 29). This shows where the gain lands and what becomes the next bottleneck: here the master, at 75–83%.
  - Transferable technique: keep the variant without the acceleration (SLE without updates) in every table and profile, so that the gain of the structural idea and the gain of the update scheme are reported separately (Table 1, p. 30; Figs. 5–6, pp. 30–31).
- **D6 Positioning**: The work builds on:
  - Birkhoff and Diaz 1956 (uniqueness), Koch et al. 2015, Groß et al. 2019, and the 2026 overview by Pfetsch et al., which is credited with the step of solving the system first and checking the bounds afterwards (p. 14);
  - Rahmaniani et al. 2017 for Benders (pp. 3, 10);
  - Fischetti et al. 2017 and Contreras et al. 2011 as precedents for specialised subproblem algorithms (p. 3);
  - Vanderbei 1998 and Bertsimas and Tsitsiklis 1997 for the simplex reading (pp. 3, 27).

  Prior art for factorization updates on weighted Laplacian matrices (Alsac et al. 1983) is conceded openly (p. 4), and Stange et al. 2007 supplies the rank-1 LU update (p. 23). Earlier work is placed, not attacked: Benders papers on potential-based flows "usually focus on improvements to the master problem" (p. 2), or on problem, decomposition or cut design (p. 3). The new parts are the dual and feasibility-cut systems, the switch-arc handling and the updates across subproblems (pp. 3–4).

  [reading] The reference list (pp. 33–35) contains no literature on cut selection (e.g. Pareto-optimal cuts), although the paper notes that its duals are not unique and its cuts may be "slightly weaker" (p. 29).
  - Transferable technique: position a component paper by naming where the field's attention goes (the master) and answering the obvious objection (better cuts mean fewer subproblem solves) before claiming that the neglected component still pays (pp. 2–3).

The conclusion interprets rather than summarises: "Our work can be interpreted as exposing this special problem structure to the simplex algorithm" (p. 32). It ends with two future directions, "one easier, one more challenging" (p. 32).   - Transferable technique: state the one-sentence insight in the conclusion and use it to reread the contribution in the baseline's own terms (here the simplex method). Then rank the future work by difficulty (p. 32).
  - Transferable technique: when you re-prove a known result you depend on, say in one sentence why: an error there would make your cuts wrong (p. 14).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: The contribution is algorithmic: an exact, LP-free Benders subproblem solver with proofs, a complexity analysis, a Julia implementation and a study on a custom benchmark (pp. 3–4, 27–31).

  Stated limits and future work:
  - one cut per subproblem, with all subproblems solved every round, is "only one option". More cuts per round (several violated components, extra related subproblems) could save master time, and this algorithm makes them cheap (p. 32);
  - non-affine potential functions are not covered, because the algorithm relies on linear subproblems to build linear systems. Uniqueness of the flow still holds for continuous, strictly increasing ψ with ψ(0) = 0 (Pfetsch et al. 2026), so "there is room for improvement also in these cases" (p. 32);
  - simplex acceleration techniques are out of scope (p. 27);
  - the miscellaneous subproblem overhead remains (p. 29);
  - only single-arc contingencies are run (p. 6).

  [reading] Not stated:
  - the benchmark cannot be rebuilt from this paper: its construction is deferred and no code or data link is given (p. 28);
  - there is no compact-model baseline, no second platform and no variability protocol (pp. 27–31);
  - SLE-U's advantage depends on small contingencies and sparse graphs (Ĉ independent of network size, p. 27; 1.4683 arcs per vertex, p. 30), and no instances outside that regime are tested.
  - Transferable technique: state in complexity terms the condition under which the speedup holds (here Ĉ independent of the network size, |C| bounded by |A|, N−1 as the natural case). Then report the instance statistics that place the benchmark inside it (pp. 27, 30–31).
