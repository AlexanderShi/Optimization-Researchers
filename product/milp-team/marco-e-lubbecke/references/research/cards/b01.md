- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problem came from an industrial project funded by the BMBF (p. 6): short-term scheduling of switching engines at in-plant railroads of steel mills, where the only earlier discrete-optimization paper is Charnes & Miller (1956) (p. 14) and the planning hierarchy is discussed "for the first time" (p. 13). Unlike timetabled rail problems there is no fixed schedule, which "complicates matters considerably" (p. 14). The operational motive: at peak load dispatchers work "loudest-shout first-serve" and railroads with more than eight engines keep up to a fifth of their engines as overhead (p. 24); existing systems only track decisions ("passive", p. 24), and a schedule counts as good when it is feasible (p. 25). The modelling entry is a mismatch with the literature model: the m-PDPTW "is too general" for railroads (p. 29), where engines haul two or three special cars and loading is last-in first-out (p. 29), and railroad officers report that only the simplest patterns occur in practice (p. 31); published PDPTW instances share a small capacity ratio ρ (p. 33). Problem statement: give every engine a request-disjoint concatenation of full-truckload, overlapping and embedding patterns so that all requests are served (Def. 1.6, p. 32); optimisation and feasibility are both strongly NP-complete (Lemmas 1.7–1.8, p. 32).
  - Transferable: before modelling, ask which solution structures practice actually uses and check whether the benchmark instances of the literature share the restriction (pp. 31, 33).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: (a) Pattern concatenation: precast feasible load sequences turn pairing, precedence and capacity constraints into properties of the pattern family (Lemma 1.3, p. 30), so the problem becomes one of selecting patterns that partition the nodes (p. 34); the approach stays exact when the pattern family is inherent in the problem, and it is not cluster-first route-second (p. 33). (b) Variables are whole concatenations: a set-partitioning master over the admissible request sets of each engine (pp. 92–93), proven at least as strong as the compact MIP (Lemma 3.3, pp. 93–94), with its LP solved by column generation (Ch. 4). (c) Pricing: the label-correcting dynamic programs of the Montréal school for the SPPTW and the 1-PDPTW (pp. 107–116) are changed in the extension unit, whole patterns instead of nodes, with states grouped at delivery nodes (pp. 116–117), and get a new pruning test: a lower bound built from dual values fathoms labels (pp. 121–124), "proposed here for the first time" for a pricing DP (p. 123). Kept unchanged: label dominance (Lemma 4.10, p. 116) and the labeling skeleton; most feasibility-based elimination tests become useless because patterns never build infeasible labels (p. 120). (d) Integer solutions by price-and-branch: column generation at the root only, then branch-and-bound on the final restricted master (pp. 128, 131), because the final masters came out integer feasible on the data (p. 128); a branch-and-price code is recommended as "emergency reserve" for an industrial system (p. 128).
  - Transferable: change the extension unit of a known labeling algorithm to the structure of the application (patterns instead of nodes) and re-check which classical elimination tests still bite (pp. 116–120).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Binary set-partitioning master with one convexity row per engine; engines are heterogeneous through admissibility sets; a column is an admissible request set at the cost of its cheapest concatenation (pp. 92–93, 99). The compact MIP (ESPMIP) uses pattern-to-engine binaries, pattern-sequence binaries and continuous arrival times linked by big-M constraints with the smallest feasible M (pp. 88–90); its LP relaxation gives the trivial bound (Lemma 3.2, pp. 90–91) and its symmetry under engine reindexing is "an attribute of the formulation not of the problem at hand" (p. 92). Objective in the code: total waiting plus deadheading time, whose small integer costs make both the master and its dual degenerate (p. 131). Assumptions: deterministic data; knowledge of requests over about two hours is "sufficiently certain" (p. 20); human estimates in the input are treated as irrevocable (p. 24); integral arrival times (p. 108); request-disjoint concatenations (p. 30); bounded subproblems in Ch. 2 "for the purpose of notational convenience" (p. 42); in Ch. 6 a single vehicle, non-binding capacity and no time windows (p. 151). Data (Table 5.2, p. 138): vps, one real 8.5-hour shift with 100 requests, 6 engines and 21 tracks, time windows extracted from handwritten sheets with the railroad officers' approval (p. 137); eko, 820 requests from a six-day log of car movements on 273 tracks with synthetic time windows of 200–300 minutes, which "cannot be regarded as truly practical data" (p. 137); eko80x, deliberately hard variants with wide windows and short distances (p. 137); Sol's 80 random PDPTW instances, restricted to F1∪2 concatenations (pp. 137–138).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Master: all-artificial start with penalty M (default 1000); too large an M distorts the duals and weakens the label bound (pp. 100–101, 125, 131); greedy, arc-exchange, split-and-blend and backtracking heuristics for start columns (pp. 101–103); temporal decomposition of the horizon and temporary fixing of variables above 0.99 (p. 104). Pricing: ESPP is strongly NP-complete (Prop. 4.3, pp. 105–106); a pricing MIP is rejected because it "suffers from basically the same weaknesses as (ESPMIP) itself" (p. 106); a time-expanded pattern graph gives a pseudo-polynomial DP for the non-disjoint relaxation but grows too large (Lemma 4.5, p. 109); the exact algorithm works on states (R, i) (Alg. 4.11, pp. 117–118); dual-bound fathoming (4.19)–(4.24), tightened by sets of mutually incompatible requests and used for pattern exclusion in preprocessing (pp. 121–124); costlier Lagrangian bounds are rejected on a run-time trade-off (p. 125). Heuristics: stop at the k-th negative column, re-cost small columns by backtracking, graph reduction, aggregation, γ-excessive fathoming with a relative-error guarantee (Lemma 4.12, p. 127), cardinality caps (p. 127); re-optimisation from old labels is discussed and left aside (p. 128). Implementation: every component is a command-line switch (Table 5.1, p. 130); a column pool (p. 133); "multiple levels of column generators, ordered by increasing quality", the exact one "only called when unavoidable" (p. 134); engines priced in turn with re-optimisation after each (p. 136); a reduced-cost threshold of 10^-10 against cycling (p. 136); deviations of about 10^-15 rounded to integers (p. 139). Proof devices: strong NP-completeness by restriction from the m-TSPTW (Lemma 1.7, p. 32) and by reduction from LONGEST PATH (Prop. 4.3, pp. 105–106); a fractional solution in which every engine serves every concatenation with weight 1/|E| (Lemma 3.2, pp. 90–91); Dantzig–Wolfe decomposition of the compact LP to compare bounds (Lemma 3.3, pp. 93–94); the bound chain LB ≤ z* < 0 for the γ guarantee (Lemma 4.12, p. 127); counting by induction and Stirling's formula (Lemmas 6.4–6.9, pp. 153–156); path surgery on a line under the triangle inequality for a tight factor 3 (Prop. 6.12, p. 158); a perfect-matching reduction (Prop. 6.13, p. 159); an expansion of Balas's layered graph (Thm 6.15, Cor. 6.16, pp. 160–163).
  - Transferable: when an exact pruning test exists, derive a heuristic from it by scaling the bound and prove its error at once (Lemma 4.12, p. 127).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Setting: 700 MHz Pentium III, 1 GB, Linux 2.2, CPLEX 7.0 callable library, gcc -O3; the code is "a research prototype, and not a production implementation" (p. 136). Test sets: the vps10–vps50 ladder (Table 5.3, p. 141); vps40 with defaults and 33 option settings, mostly one at a time (Table 5.4, p. 142); eko820a–c in 32 groups of 25 requests (Table 5.5, p. 144); eko80xa–c (Table 5.6, p. 145); Sol's 80 instances (Tables 5.8–5.10, pp. 146–148). Metrics (p. 139): LP value, integer value, % gap to the LP, engines used and the optimum if known, pricing calls, columns, simplex iterations, B&B time, total CPU, CPU with label elimination off, memory. Baselines: (i) his own code without label elimination, with "speedups of orders of magnitude" (p. 140), e.g. vps35 in 58.45 s against 501,309.84 s (p. 141); (ii) Sol's optimal route durations, with the differences stated: a restricted path structure, another objective, a longer horizon that lets one solution beat Sol's optimum (pp. 143, 147); (iii) the compact MIP only on vps10, as "An interesting remark aside": about 1.5 CPU hours, more than 130,000 nodes, a 25% gap when the optimum was found (p. 149), and on a different objective, route duration, from the code's waiting plus deadheading (p. 131); the solver is not named there, but the code can write ESPMIP as a GAMS file (-g, p. 130). Time cap: † means "every reasonable time bound is exceeded, even for testing purposes (here: one CPU week)" (p. 141). Losses are kept in the tables: † rows (pp. 141, 142, 145), gaps of 17.39% (vps39, p. 141) and 36.00% as a group average (eko requests 701–800, width 300, p. 144), settings that worsen the default (p. 142), and a run-time profile with label treatment 55.24% and CPLEX 1.49% (Table 5.7, p. 145). Observations reported: final masters integer feasible with default options; best columns generated late (p. 139); a critical relative time-window width of 40–50% of the horizon (p. 143). No seeds, permutations or statistical tests appear in the parts read.
  - Transferable: give every table a column for the run with your new component switched off (−L, Table 5.3, p. 141).
- **D6 Positioning**: He continues the Montréal school (Desrosiers, Dumas, Soumis, Solomon, Desaulniers) for PDPTW labeling and pricing (pp. 107–116) and calls the surveys of Barnhart et al. (1998) and Desrosiers et al. (1995) and Vanderbeck's thesis "truly insightful" (p. 38); Sol (1994) is the PDPTW reference and the source of one data set (pp. 27, 137–138); the implementation review of Desaulniers, Desrosiers and Solomon (1999) "can hardly be outmatched" (p. 129). Priority claims: the in-plant planning hierarchy (p. 13), PDP paths of restricted structure (p. 32), dual bounds inside a pricing DP (p. 123), with independent similar work by Mehrotra & Trick (1998) and Mehlhorn & Ziegelmann (2000) credited (p. 122). Criticism of others is direct and reasoned: Kim & Nazareth give no convergence proof and a test bed that "is not representative by far" (p. 76); Boucher & Smeers' conclusion is "questionable" by their own iteration counts (p. 77); branch-and-cut for the PDP had not reached practical sizes (pp. 27, 96).
- **D7 Writing and rhetoric**: A literary epigraph opens every chapter (e.g., Desrosiers at Ch. 5, p. 129). The preface fixes typographic conventions and states "All claims and results developed in this thesis are proved." (p. 6). Priority claims are hedged "to the best of our knowledge" (pp. 13, 32, 123). Sections end with Concluding Remarks that name gaps (pp. 57–58, 73, 84, 149, 164). Candour about data and code (pp. 136, 137) and hedged readings in Ch. 5 ("We cannot conclude general rules, but trends become visible", p. 140) stand against firmer summary claims: §7.2 calls bounds and solutions "excellent" for practical and artificial problems alike (p. 168), and the German summary says all instances can be solved integrally in acceptable time (p. 192), which holds for the hardest eko80x instance only with non-default options and a 13% gap (pp. 143, 145). People who raised objections are thanked by name (Kallehauge, p. 125; Lindner, Matsui and Scheel, p. 151).
  - Transferable: close each survey section with the gaps it leaves open, and open the limitations section with a sentence of intent ("A fair discussion of our work must exhibit its limits as well.", p. 169).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Stated contributions (§7.2, pp. 167–168): the pattern-concatenation concept and its complexity; a weak polynomial-size MIP against a stronger set-partitioning model; a pattern-based labeling algorithm with dual lower bounds, an idea "new in the pricing context" (p. 168), plus a pricing heuristic with a guarantee; a prototype that solves LP relaxations with more than 35 requests and six engines in a few minutes (p. 168); counting results, a factor-3 approximation and polynomial cases (Ch. 6); a 190-entry bibliography, 114 entries from 1990 or later, and the Ch. 2 synthesis, which "was not available formerly" (p. 168). Levels: modelling, algorithm, prototype software, theory. Limits are specific (§7.3, pp. 169–170): the exact pricing is the bottleneck and needs a small pattern family; request disjointness drives the search space; no general advice on which dual solution to pass to pricing; no branch-and-price, justified by integral masters, with branching compatibility left open; no polyhedral study of the compact MIP; deterministic only; online versions and dependencies between engines open. Ch. 5 adds missing time-window data, no comparison with realised schedules, and practicability still to be shown (p. 149). Future directions: hybrid node-or-pattern labeling and problem-independent dual cutting planes (p. 169), irregular pattern families and TSP special cases (p. 164).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 (compact model first; decomposition as a measured bet): variant ⚠. - Heuristics and taste: H1 evidence ✅, the 2001 origin of the pricing advice: an exact pricing algorithm is needed for the optimality certificate, but early iterations may take any negative column (p. 125), the global guideline is to call the exact algorithm as seldom as possible (p. 133), always running it exactly is "entirely impractical" (p. 140), and the Lagrangian and Farley bounds are surveyed as early-termination tests (pp. 79–80). **Transferable techniques**:
- Kill a compact big-M model on paper before running it: construct the fractional solution in which every engine serves every concatenation with weight 1/|E| and show that the LP relaxation attains only the trivial bound (Lemma 3.2, pp. 90–91).
- Compare the LP bounds of two formulations by Dantzig–Wolfe-decomposing the compact LP and mapping each extensive solution into it (Lemma 3.3, pp. 93–94).
- Fathom labels in a pricing DP with a dual bound: the best label cost at a state minus the positive duals of requests still admissible, compared with min(0, incumbent); tighten it with sets of mutually incompatible requests (pp. 121–124), and report run times with the bound switched off (Table 5.3, p. 141).
- Turn an exact pruning test into a pricing heuristic with a guarantee: scale the bound by γ in (0, 1] and prove a relative error of at most 1 − γ (Lemma 4.12, p. 127).
- Layer the column generators: column pool, then a greedy ranking by dual value over time-window slack, then the exact DP stopped at the k-th negative column, then the full exact DP only to prove optimality (Alg. 5.2, pp. 133–135).
- Make every algorithmic component a command-line switch, print the defaults, and publish a sweep of the switches on one instance with integer value, gap, time and memory (Tables 5.1 and 5.4, pp. 130, 142).
- Build a size ladder from one real instance by chronological truncation (vps10 to vps50) and mark runs beyond a stated cap, one CPU week, with a sign (pp. 137, 141).
- Add a run-time profile by subroutine to locate the bottleneck (Table 5.7, p. 145).
- When a literature benchmark uses another model, compute its objective from your solutions, tabulate both with their ratio, and name each difference (objective, horizon, path structure) in the text (pp. 143, 147).
- Price a structural restriction with a tight worst-case factor: under the triangle inequality, place the optimal path on a line and repair interrupted patterns, traversing no segment more than three times; give the tight example (Prop. 6.12, p. 158).
- For deployment: deliver a heuristic schedule at once, improve it with exact methods in the background, treat the output as a suggestion and never overrule manually fixed parts (p. 167).
