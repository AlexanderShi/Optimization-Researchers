- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - The chapter enters from a question: "While solving the MP by column generation, a primal algorithm, what happens in the dual space?" (p. 5). Its practical promise is that the dual perspective "enables us to improve the column generation algorithm in practice" (p. 4).
  - A second entry is a statement to clarify: Lagrangian relaxation "is said to be dual to the Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition" (p. 4). With it comes a misconception to correct: Lagrangian relaxation is "a relaxation of the ILP, and not an equivalent reformulation" (p. 12).
  - A third entry is a framing of two schools. A 1993 song stages Madsen (who favours dual methods) against Desrosiers (who favours primal ones) at the NorFa summer course in Narvik. The authors write the chapter in the Montréal spirit of collaboration, hoping the two hammers are stronger together (p. 4). The reference notes add that Lindberg invited both to teach at the first column generation school (p. 84).
  - The phenomena behind the second half are three: subgradient overshoot with oscillating multipliers (Fig. 6.13, pp. 32–33), unstable duals in (very) degenerate RMPs, and the observed speed of dual boxes (p. 48).
  - Problem statement: the compact ILP (6.1), with constraints grouped into a linking set A and a pricing domain D, assumed feasible with a finite optimum: "We are (still!) interested in solving the ILP" (p. 5). From §6.3 on, the authors drop A and D and work with a generic master with equality rows, (6.53) (p. 35).
  - Transferable: to explain what an algorithm does, ask what it does in the dual space, and answer on a toy small enough to draw every dual constraint. The five TCSPP iterations of Table 6.1 become five ever tighter relaxed duals, with four path cuts in all (Fig. 6.3), and the five paths never generated show up as redundant constraints (pp. 6–9).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Key insight: column generation in the primal is the cutting-plane method of Kelley and of Cheney and Goldstein in the dual, and the pricing problem is its separation problem (p. 5; Note 6.1, p. 6). Everything after that restricts or steers the dual so that fewer cuts are generated in irrelevant dual regions (pp. 35, 42, 48).
  - Lagrangian relaxation is developed under the same assumptions and with the same grouping of constraints as Dantzig-Wolfe, and the multipliers deliberately carry the master's dual names (p. 15). The two subproblems differ only by a constant (6.24) (p. 16). The best Lagrangian bound equals the master bound (Prop 6.2, p. 17), so z⋆LP ≤ z⋆LDP = z⋆MP ≤ z⋆IMP = z⋆ILP (6.32) (p. 19).
  - What changes between the two methods is the dual update. The subgradient method uses only the last extreme point and outputs no primal solution (pp. 31, 33). Column generation re-solves an LP over all extreme points found so far (p. 34). LP size once argued for Lagrangian relaxation; with modern LP solvers it no longer does, and discarding the pricing solutions found along the way is called "unreasonable" when an integer solution is wanted (p. 34).
  - Dual-side accelerators, in the order presented:
    - dual-optimal inequalities (DOIs, satisfied by all optimal duals) and deep DOIs (satisfied by at least one), added to the RMP as auxiliary columns (pp. 35–37);
    - dual boxes around a dual estimate (pp. 38–39);
    - stabilized column generation, which charges a penalty for leaving a moving trust region (pp. 48–54);
    - smoothing, interior-point and aggregated-row alternatives (pp. 55–56);
    - initialization, dual estimates and anticipation (pp. 57–58).
  - Why this route: set-partitioning masters are very degenerate (p. 9), columns useless in the primal can swing the duals (p. 48), and initial duals from big-M artificial columns carry only penalty information (Note 6.19, pp. 47–48).
  - Transferable: box the dual around your best estimate, solve the boxed master, and accept its primal solution only when the box's surplus and slack columns are zero (Props 6.9–6.10, p. 39); otherwise relocate the box and repeat (p. 48).
- **D3 Problem class & data model** (team.json):
  - Class: pure ILPs with x ∈ Z^n_+, feasible with a finite optimum (p. 5). Finiteness implies bounded x and no extreme ray with negative reduced cost at any optimal dual (Note 6.3, p. 10). The subgradient method assumes a bounded pricing domain, which upper bounds can always ensure (p. 29). Block-diagonal structure and identical blocks are covered (pp. 13, 18). A MILP appears only in Exercise 6.15, a warehouse location model with continuous assignment fractions (pp. 89–91).
  - Signs: multipliers are non-negative for ≥ rows, free for equalities and non-positive for ≤ rows (p. 12). The §6.3 master has equality rows, so its duals are free (p. 35); replacing partitioning by covering restricts them to π ≥ 0, often without loss of optimality (pp. 35–36), and with non-negative costs in the capacitated p-median (p. 64).
  - Correctness assumptions: Prop 6.2 needs a finite z⋆ILP (p. 17). Prop 6.10 needs an optimal dual strictly inside the box, so the stabilization penalty must be smooth: the trust region may never shrink to a single point (Note 6.21, pp. 50–51). A zero subgradient, the Lagrangian optimality certificate, is "very rarely observed in practice" (Note 6.13, p. 26).
  - Structures exploited:
    - set-partitioning or covering masters (pp. 9, 35);
    - cutting stock with items ordered by width (ranking DOIs, pp. 36–37), and triplet instances whose optimal duals are known in closed form, π⋆i = wi/W (p. 40);
    - the multi-depot vehicle scheduling problem (MDVSP) as a multi-commodity flow, NP-hard for two or more depots (Bertossi et al. 1987), priced per depot (pp. 44–45);
    - generalized assignment, capacitated p-median, VRPTW, cutting stock and edge coloring (pp. 59–68);
    - the symmetric TSP through 1-trees (pp. 71–76);
    - printed circuit board (PCB) assembly with an assignment-problem pricing (pp. 77–81).
  - Big-M: an artificial column with cost 100 bounds π0 ≤ 100 in the TCSPP (p. 7); the MDVSP starts from artificial columns of a million each (p. 45); the authors advise replacing such penalties by dual estimates (p. 58).
  - Data: the TCSPP toy with 6 nodes and nine paths (pp. 6, 23); ten randomly generated 501-item triplet instances (p. 40); one MDVSP instance with 4 depots and 800 trips (p. 45); the symmetric instance tsp225 (p. 70); manufacturer data for the PCB case (p. 80); a two-variable toy (p. 82). No MIPLIB instance appears.
  - Transferable: when costs are non-negative, write a partitioning master as covering, which restricts the duals to π ≥ 0 (pp. 35–36, 64), and check that every dual estimate you plan to box can sit strictly inside an open interval (Prop 6.10, Note 6.21, pp. 39, 50–51).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design, proofs and devices** (team.json):
  - Proof devices:
    - Weak Lagrangian duality: re-add the relaxed rows and drop a non-positive penalty term (Prop 6.1, p. 12).
    - z⋆LDP = z⋆MP: rewrite the max–min as a linear program with an epigraph variable µ and recognize the dual of an alternative master problem (AMP). The AMP comes from the master by the change of variables µ = b⊺πb + π0, an affine map between the two dual domains (pp. 9–10, 17). A second proof keeps b⊺πb as an offset and recovers the dual of the original master (Note 6.6, pp. 17–18). Optimal master duals solve the Lagrangian dual, and the convexity duals can be recovered for one block or identical blocks (Prop 6.3, Cor 6.1, p. 18).
    - The Lagrangian function is the pointwise minimum of finitely many affine functions (Prop 6.5, p. 21), or equivalently the boundary of an LP's feasible polyhedron, its hypograph (Note 6.10, p. 22). Every pricing optimum yields a subgradient (Prop 6.6, p. 22).
    - Optimality: a zero subgradient (Prop 6.7), or a pricing optimum that is feasible for the ILP with complementary slackness (Prop 6.8). For relaxed equalities the second test follows from feasibility; for inequalities it does not (p. 25), as a two-variable counterexample shows (pp. 25–26).
    - Dual boxes: weak and strong duality when the box contains an optimal dual (Prop 6.9), complementary slackness when an optimal dual lies in the open box (Prop 6.10) (p. 39). A start with only slack and surplus columns is feasible, and with symmetric boxes its objective is b⊺π⋆ + Σ δi|bi| in closed form (Prop 6.11, p. 43).
    - Stabilization: lower bounds z^s ≤ b⊺π^s ≤ z⋆MP by weak duality (Prop 6.12, pp. 53–54); strict improvement by a case split on the trust region's interior (Prop 6.13, p. 54); convergence by contradiction on the limit of an increasing sequence (Prop 6.14, p. 54). [reading] The written proof of Prop 6.14 shows that the limit equals z⋆MP and assumes that, for s large enough, the limit point lies inside the trust region; the step from a limit to "a finite number of stabilized iterations" is not spelled out.
    - Held and Karp's 1-tree bound: tour costs are invariant under node-potential shifts (Prop 6.15, p. 72). The subtour constraints on S̄ = N \ S are redundant, shown by double counting degree sums (Note 6.23, pp. 69–71).
  - Algorithms:
    - Subgradient with the Polyak step: ε is halved after three non-improving iterations, and an iteration limit or step tolerance stops the run, which "makes the subgradient algorithm a heuristic" (Algorithm 6.1, p. 31). ε = 2 is "a common default value" reached by trial and error (p. 30); for the TCSPP an initial ε ∈ (0.75, 1) is derived analytically (p. 32).
    - Stabilized column generation (Algorithm 6.2, p. 52). If the dual solution lies in the trust region, shrink the region and raise the penalties; otherwise recenter, enlarge and lower them. Alternatively, update only at a "serious step" that improves the lower bound (p. 51). Stop when no column prices out and every auxiliary variable is zero, (6.78); the final penalty constraints are then deep DOIs (p. 51).
    - Smoothing (Wentges 1997; Neame 1999), with a mis-price check at the RMP duals before stopping (pp. 55–56). Alternatives: ACCPM, primal-dual interior-point column generation (Gondzio et al. 2016) and dynamic aggregated rows (Costa et al. 2022) (p. 56). Over-stabilization by arbitrary dual inequalities, followed by recovery (Gschwind and Irnich 2016) (pp. 36, 55).
    - Hybrids. Subgradient steps in the initial phase avoid degeneracy; in VRPTW, starting the multipliers at 0 also avoids the cycles that big-M duals create in labelling (Kohl 1995) (p. 57). A boxed or stabilized master then delivers primal and dual solutions (p. 57). A few subgradient steps can replace the re-optimization of a degenerate RMP, and slightly perturbed duals between pricing problems can diversify the columns (p. 58). Example 6.6 runs four subgradient steps and finishes with a two-column RMP whose solution is integer (pp. 82–84).
  - Implementation rules stated:
    - A dual box is a surplus and a slack column per row, with costs −δ1 and δ2; the RMP is then feasible with no generated columns (Note 6.18, p. 39; Prop 6.11, p. 43).
    - Positive auxiliary columns in a final solution call for a primal recovery step (Note 6.15, p. 36); exchange vectors also serve as recovery moves (Note 6.16, p. 37).
    - Artificial, slack/surplus and perturbation variables form one class of auxiliary variables; this is "a good time to make sure one uses object-oriented programming" (Note 6.20, p. 50).
    - The cost of an artificial variable bounds a dual variable, so choose it as a dual estimate; smaller penalties constrain the dual space more tightly (p. 58).
    - Starting from the columns of an integer solution can be worse than starting from scratch (Note 6.19, pp. 47–48).
  - Numerical safeguards: very small boxes fail on numerical precision (p. 46); perturbation is known to help with degeneracy (Dantzig 1963) (p. 51). Two warnings are quoted from the classics: Kelley (1960) on near-parallel cuts and limited precision near termination (p. 25), and Held and Karp's IBM/360 code with double precision, periodic reinversion and zero checks (p. 76).
  - Transferable: if your code already has artificial or perturbation columns, a dual box costs one surplus and one slack column per row, priced from the estimate ±δ; nothing else in the master changes (pp. 38–39, 50).

- **D6 Positioning**:
  - Lineage for the dual view: Kelley (1960) and Cheney and Goldstein (1959), published around the time of Dantzig and Wolfe (1960) (pp. 5, 24). For Lagrangian relaxation: Geoffrion (1974), Shapiro (1979a), Nemhauser and Wolsey (1988), Guignard (2003) and Fisher's (1981) survey. Magnanti et al. (1976) established the equivalence of the Lagrangian dual and the Dantzig-Wolfe master, and Barahona and Anbil's (2000) volume algorithm is a subgradient variant that nearly matches the master's primal output (p. 85).
  - Lineage for stabilization: Marsten's (1975) boxstep method, the pioneer that lacks self-adjustment (p. 48); du Merle et al. (1999) and Ben Amor et al. (2006b) as the template; Valério de Carvalho (2005) for DOIs (p. 85); bundle methods (Lemaréchal, Kiwiel, Frangioni) and the comparisons of Ben Amor et al. (2009) and Briant et al. (2008) (p. 55).
  - Re-reading a classic: besides its Lagrangian/subgradient approach to the symmetric TSP, Held and Karp (1970) contains a Dantzig-Wolfe column-generation counterpart, which is "much less known" (p. 86). The chapter rebuilds it as an alternative master (6.124) and lets the 1970 authors describe its slow convergence in their own words (p. 76).
  - The classics state their own limits: Kelley (1960) on precision near termination (p. 25), Guignard (2003) on the unpredictable convergence of the subgradient method (p. 29), Gondzio et al. (2016) on well-centered duals (p. 56).
  - Concessions to rival methods. Following Applegate et al. (2003), "branch-and-cut is the only exact method tractable for instances larger than 100 vertices" for the TSP (p. 71). For some applications a Lagrangian relaxation alone gets near integer optimality without a master problem (p. 59). In the PCB case, the authors judge that solving the RMP would be simpler than the cited subgradient implementation (p. 81).
  - Montréal history: the VRPTW initialization problem (Kohl 1995) was "the first incentive to start the research at the GERAD center on controlling the values of the dual variables" (p. 57). [reading] Chapter 2 of the same book names another starting point for stabilized column generation: a GENCOL programmer's ±2-minute tolerance and Desrosiers's question about its dual analogue [card S060, p. 52]. The two accounts are compatible if they describe different steps, but neither text says so.
  - Tool pointer: for quadratic stabilizing terms the reader is sent to the solvers of Gurobi Optimization (p. 55); no open-source QP solver is named. [reading] This adds one instance to SKILL.md's Tension 7 (open tools against practice).
  - Own work: "Gauthier et al. (2016, 2018)" (p. 85), that is Gauthier, Desrosiers and Lübbecke (S017 text file, p. 668), are placed next to the aggregation and improved-primal-simplex line of El Hallaoui et al., Metrane et al. and Omer et al.
  - Transferable: when the literature says a method failed, re-read the original paper for the variant its authors tried and the safeguards they used, and list what has not been tested since (Held and Karp 1970; pp. 76, 86).

- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - *Contribution*: exposition and theory in one notation. There is no software, no new benchmark and no result flagged as new. The chapter covers column generation as dual cutting planes (§6.1); Lagrangian relaxation, its equivalence to the master bound and the subgradient method (§6.2); DOIs and dual boxes with their exactness conditions (§6.3); stabilized column generation with convergence propositions, and hybrids (§6.4); and six examples (§6.5) (pp. 5–84).
  - *Limits, stated concretely*:
    - There is no general theory of how to split the constraints, nor of what a good decomposition means (Note 6.8, p. 19).
    - The subgradient method is a heuristic with no primal output (p. 31), its practical convergence is unpredictable (Guignard, p. 29), and it misses the precise optimum even on the toy (Note 6.14, p. 33). A zero subgradient is rare (p. 26).
    - Perfect dual information is unlikely in general (p. 57), and boxes that are too tight fail numerically (p. 46).
    - For the symmetric TSP it is unclear whether formulations (6.111) and (6.119) have equal LP bounds (p. 74). Four questions on Held and Karp's column generation have never been tested: a hybrid, stabilization, constraint aggregation and the dual master (p. 76).
  - *Future*: dynamic refinement of the boxes' radius and center (p. 47); dual heuristics for good estimates (p. 57); a self-adjusting rule for δ and ε (Exercise 6.10(b), p. 88); deep DOIs for two-echelon routing (Exercise 6.16, pp. 91–92).
  - Transferable: end a synthesis with the questions nobody has tested, each phrased so that one experiment can answer it (p. 76).

**Method links**:
- ✅ Method 1 (compact model first; decomposition as a measured bet), evidence. The compact ILP stays the object of study (p. 5). The bound chain (6.32) with Corollary 6.2(b) restates the no-gain case: if the Lagrangian (or pricing) formulation has the integrality property, the bound equals z⋆LP (p. 19). The examples apply the test before any computation. Relaxing the generalized assignment problem's semi-assignment rows leaves knapsacks and a possible bound gain; relaxing its capacities leaves an integral problem and no gain (pp. 60–61). The same split appears for cutting stock (pp. 65–66), edge coloring (p. 68), the 1-tree (p. 74) and the PCB assignment pricing (p. 78).
- ⚠ Method 1, variant. Step 4 (probe before you build) gets a cheaper probe. The Lagrangian dual has the master's bound (Prop 6.2, p. 17), and a subgradient run needs no master implementation at all (p. 34). The chapter also sizes this probe: the subgradient gives a heuristic lower approximation with no primal output (pp. 31, 33), and in Example 6.6 its four steps give 6, −8, 0.125, 5.5 and 4.125 against z⋆LP = 7 (p. 83). Step 2's yardstick is absent: none of the timed comparisons runs the compact model in a MILP solver, and the baseline is always plain column generation (pp. 41, 45, 52).
- ✅ Method 2 (enumerate small, then explain or automate), evidence, as an explanation device. On the TCSPP all nine paths are enumerated, the whole Lagrangian function is drawn, and its breakpoints and subdifferentials are computed (Table 6.2, Fig. 6.9, pp. 23–24). The authors warn that such enumeration is impossible in practice (Note 6.14, p. 33). Note 6.8 supports writing the option space down instead of picking by taste: there is no theory of a good split, and experience from Lagrangian relaxation is a second source for choosing master constraints (p. 19).
- ⚠ Method 3 (adversarial, identical-conditions evaluation, claim sized to the measurement), variant. On the positive side, failures and costs are reported (pp. 41, 46, 53), the perfect-information caveat is raised and answered with estimated duals (pp. 40, 52), and the data are trusted over the plot (p. 52). The gaps:
  - Every timed number is borrowed from cited studies, two of them co-authored by Desrosiers (Ben Amor et al. 2006b, 2009; S017 text file, pp. 660–661).
  - They come from ten random instances (averages only) or from one instance, with no hardware, LP solver or time limit (pp. 40–41, 45, 52).
  - The general advice that good dual information helps whichever variant is used rests on two illustrations (p. 57).
  - A speed estimate for modern hardware is not re-run (p. 80).
  - Two numbers differ between text and table or figure (pp. 40–41, 75).
  Random instances are among SKILL.md's warning signs (B2). Here they illustrate a mechanism, not a benchmark claim.
- ✅ Method 4 (synthesis writing as a research instrument), evidence. Dantzig-Wolfe and Lagrangian objects share one notation (pp. 14–15); a common misconception is corrected (p. 12); open questions are published as such (pp. 19, 47, 57, 74, 76); the forgotten half of a classic is recovered (Held and Karp 1970; pp. 76, 86). The authors report that writing the book kept pulling towards this chapter: they were tempted several times to forward-reference it (p. 57). Step 1 (start from an effect you could not fix): in the 2001 thesis code, unstable duals resisted his static stabilization [card S027, pp. 139–140, 142]; this chapter is the book's systematic answer to that effect.
- ✅ H1 (heuristic inside an exact frame), evidence. Every column-generation iteration yields a Lagrangian bound, useful for early termination (Note 6.7, p. 19). On the TCSPP the relative gap between the best Lagrangian bound and the best upper bound is 53.3 %, so the next step is the search tree, not more Lagrangian iterations (p. 26). H1's advice to take duals from subgradient iterations when the master is hard to solve has its written form here: a few subgradient iterations instead of re-optimizing a degenerate RMP (p. 58), or a subgradient start (p. 57).
- ⚠ H1, qualification. The free bound holds only for iterations in which pricing is solved to optimality or as a relaxation; heuristic pricing gives an outer approximation of the Lagrangian function and no valid bound (Note 6.11, p. 24). Chapter 2 says the same for column generation (Note 2.17) [card S060, p. 20].
- ✅ H6 (too-good results are a bug signal), evidence. The radius-0.01 objective below the known optimum is read as a box that is too tight and as a precision limit, not as a gain (p. 46); the count of updates is taken from the data, not from the plot (p. 52).
- ✅ Taste G1 (a quality claim comes with a proof or a bound), evidence. Every proposition is proved (pp. 12–54). Heuristic outputs come with gaps: 53.3 % (p. 26), under 1 % and about 5.7 % (p. 75), 2.01–2.49 % (p. 80). The end state of Table 6.3 "reminds us that we have not solved the original problem" (p. 32).
- ✅ Common Misreading 3 (not a column-generation partisan), evidence: branch-and-cut is credited for large TSPs (p. 71); Lagrangian relaxation alone can get near integer optimality in some applications (p. 59); solving the RMP is judged simpler than the cited subgradient method (p. 81).
- ⚠ Workflow G step 3 (stabilization last, an inferred order), variant. In the chapter's large case the RMP takes 75 % of the time, and stabilization cuts total time by 89 % (p. 52). The chapter ties the dual-side hybrid to masters that are costly relative to pricing (p. 58). The same tables support Workflow C step 3, measuring master against pricing time first (pp. 41, 52).
- New-pattern candidate: explain a column-generation behaviour by its dual meaning before tuning it (pp. 5–9, 24, 42, 47–48, 51, 58).
- New-pattern candidate: seed the duals, not only the columns (pp. 40–41, 45–47, 52, 57–58).
- New-pattern candidate: pick the accelerator by the measured master/pricing time split (pp. 41, 48, 52, 58).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Dual box as two columns per row: a surplus y1 with cost −δ1 and a slack y2 with cost δ2 (6.63). Start the RMP with no generated columns, and accept its λ-part as a master optimum only when y1 = y2 = 0, which an optimal dual strictly inside the box guarantees (Props 6.10–6.11, pp. 39, 43).
- Radius sweep: run the box at radii ∞, 100, 10, 1, 0.1 and 0.01, and tabulate final and initial objective, time, iterations, columns and pivots. A final objective below the known optimum marks a box that is too tight, or too small for the arithmetic (Table 6.6, pp. 45–46).
- Stabilized column-generation loop (Algorithm 6.2, p. 52). If the dual solution lies in the trust region, shrink the region and raise the penalties; otherwise recenter, enlarge and lower them; or update only on a serious step that raises the Lagrangian bound. Stop at zero reduced cost with every auxiliary variable at zero (6.78) (p. 51).
- Dual estimates without solving. In a set-partitioning routing master, estimate a customer's dual from the savings of removing it from its route and from the cost of inserting it once more (or of a new vehicle), and use the two values as a box (pp. 57–58). In a multi-depot problem, take the duals of the aggregated single-depot network-flow problem (p. 52).
- Set artificial-variable penalties to dual estimates instead of a big-M: each penalty bounds a dual variable, and smaller penalties restrict the dual space more (p. 58).
- Cutting-stock DOIs: with items sorted by width, add m − 1 zero-cost exchange columns, the ranking inequalities πi+1 ≤ πi, to the RMP. Swap them out of the final solution to recover a master solution (pp. 36–37).
- Smoothed duals with a mis-price check: price at π̂t = απ̂ + (1 − α)πt (Wentges 1997). If no column prices out at π̂t, check the RMP duals πt before stopping (pp. 55–56).
- Subgradient start: keep every subgradient's column (its cost and b − ap) for the first RMP (Fig. 6.14, p. 34; p. 57). For VRPTW-type pricing, start the multipliers at 0 and raise them slowly, which keeps adjusted costs positive and avoids the cycles that big-M duals create in labelling (p. 57). Derive the scale of the step parameter on a small case, and halve it after three non-improving iterations (Algorithm 6.1, p. 31; p. 32).
- Reduced-cost fixing from a dual box: fix xj = 0 when its reduced cost at π⋆ exceeds Σi δi|aij| (Exercise 6.11, p. 89).
- Integrality-property screen for each candidate grouping before decomposing. For the generalized assignment problem, relaxed semi-assignment rows give knapsack pricing and a possible bound gain; relaxed capacities give an integral problem and no gain (pp. 60–61).
