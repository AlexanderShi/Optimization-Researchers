### S021 · On compact formulations for integer programs solved by column generation (Annals of Operations Research 139(1) 2005, DOI 10.1007/s10479-005-3455-9)
**Read level**: full, of the TU Berlin report version No. 2003/25 (9 December 2003). File pp. 1-14 read in full; the report-series list on file pp. 15-17 skimmed. The acknowledgments already thank two anonymous referees (p. 12), so this is a revised version; how it differs from the Annals of OR text was not checked.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Daniel Villeneuve (first author, AD OPT Technologies), Jacques Desrosiers (HEC Montréal and GERAD), François Soumis (École Polytechnique de Montréal and GERAD); Lübbecke at TU Berlin (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: the Montréal line two years after his thesis. The same report series lists his 2003/42 report on dual-variable fathoming and the 2003/43 line-planning report (p. 15). The paper is the theory behind Method 1 step 6 and behind the two-formulation design that GCG implements seven years later (S011 pp. 4-5).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a known pain of branch-and-price: branching rules that do not fit the pricing oracle (p. 3). The abstract recalls that most such compatibility problems disappear when branching constrains the subproblem's variables, and the authors generalise this to branching on the variables of a compact formulation (p. 2). The gap is an applied one: in applications often only an extensive formulation and a pricing oracle are given (p. 3). The stated contribution is to prove by construction that a compact formulation exists, and to show how it reduces compatibility issues (p. 3). The problem form is an integer program E with general integer column variables, no convexity row, and a pricing oracle of any kind: a MIP, a combinatorial algorithm or anything else (p. 4).
  - Transferable: when a trick works in special cases (branching on subproblem variables), ask whether the structure that makes it work always exists, and prove existence by construction (pp. 2-3, 6).
- **D2 Core idea**: Reverse Dantzig-Wolfe (p. 2). The oracle is written as a surjective map from a set X onto the column set (p. 4). The construction makes κ copies of the oracle's domain, where κ bounds the number of columns in a solution, and requires that the copies' images add up to the right-hand side (formulation (6), p. 7). The result is block-diagonal with identical blocks, each contributing one column to an integer solution (pp. 2, 6). Dantzig-Wolfe applied to it gives back E, with κ column generators in place of the one oracle (p. 6). Branching then works on reconstructed original vectors, by bisecting boxes that bound each copy's domain, so the oracle does not grow as branching decisions accumulate (p. 9). What stays the same: bounds still come from the extensive LP; solving the compact model integrally differs from ordinary integer programming only in how bounds are computed (pp. 3, 11). The literature section places every earlier rule on one trade-off: a more elaborate rule complicates the oracle, often in proportion to tree depth (pp. 5-6).
  - Transferable: branch on a reconstructed original vector, by splitting the domain box of one subproblem copy, instead of on master variables; the pricing problem keeps its form at every depth (p. 9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pure integer programs with general integer column variables, a finite column set without duplicates, and a dummy zero column used as a slack (pp. 4, 7). Assumptions for the construction: the oracle's domain is closed and bounded inside a box (p. 6), and κ is known (equation (5), p. 7). The cost may be non-linear (p. 9), and so may the set X of the compact model (p. 3). The abstract calls these assumptions mild (p. 2); no instance class is tested against them. Structures discussed: cutting stock with the Gilmore-Gomory master and a knapsack oracle (p. 10), and set-partitioning or set-covering masters with constrained shortest-path oracles in vehicle routing and crew scheduling, whose compact forms are multicommodity flows (p. 11).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: (i) A counterexample (4) with three variables and one row shows that under Dantzig's minimum reduced cost rule one column can never be generated, and that for some costs this column forms the unique integer optimum; the generated columns can miss the integer optimum even when they are feasible (p. 5). Branching can therefore be needed to generate the right columns, and pricing must run at every node (p. 5). (ii) Proposition 1: the copy model is equivalent to a binary disaggregated master; the proof partitions each copy's domain into classes with equal column images and keeps one representative per class (pp. 7-8). (iii) Proposition 2: if all copies share one domain, the disaggregated master is equivalent to E, by aggregation and an explicit split of any integer solution into κ unit parts (p. 8); an LP solution maps over by dividing by κ (p. 9). (iv) A branching scheme with three cases, one of them for a non-linear cost (p. 9). (v) A computing rule: keep identical copies aggregated in one oracle and add separate convexity rows only when branching makes copies differ (p. 9). There is no implementation and nothing on numerics.
  - Transferable: before proposing a remedy, show the defect of the standard rule with a minimal instance, here three variables and one row (p. 5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a. There is no experiment; the applications section rebuilds compact formulations for known models without runs (pp. 10-11).
- **D6 Positioning**: The approach is presented as a generalisation of Desrosiers, Soumis and Desrochers (1984), who for vehicle routing with time windows branched both on flow variables and on totals of cost and vehicles, and of the multicommodity-flow framework of Desaulniers et al. (1998) (p. 6). Earlier rules are described, not attacked: k-th best oracle solutions (Sweeney and Murphy), Ryan-Foster, Vanderbeck's discretisation, Vanderbeck-Wolsey, GUB and SOS branching, and tailored rules (pp. 5-6). Villeneuve's thesis supplies the analogous generation defect for Dantzig-Wolfe (p. 5). The weak cutting-stock model (14) is attributed to Kantorovich (1960) (p. 10). This is one of the three attributions Uchoa and Sadykov later called incorrect, and the 2026 book corrected it (05-peer-critique.md §6, not re-checked here). Valério de Carvalho's network-flow model is credited as an alternative compact formulation with the same bound (p. 11).
- **D7 Writing**: Ten pages of text with an abstract in English and French (p. 2). The contribution sentence is one line of the introduction (p. 3). The register is modest: the method is called a straightforward but useful complement to Dantzig-Wolfe and Lagrangian relaxation (p. 3), the conclusion calls the approach extremely simple and nearly twenty years old (p. 11), and choosing a compact formulation is left to imagination and experience (p. 11). An extension is labelled as belief, not claim: other pricing rules probably have similar bad examples (p. 5).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory and framing: a compact formulation exists under mild assumptions, and a branching scheme exists that fits any oracle (pp. 6-9). Limits the authors state: the disaggregated master has κ times more variables plus κ convexity rows (p. 9); compact formulations are not unique (p. 11); the price is administering two formulations at once (p. 11); bisection is only an illustration (p. 9). Not addressed: any implementation or test, and how κ is found in practice. Future: branching rules adapted to the oracle's structure and to the linking constraints, especially in branch-and-cut (p. 3), and using the freedom of formulation choice against symmetry (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 1, evidence ✅ (step 6): this is the theory behind keeping original variables in view. Because the original variables must be integer, they are the natural basis for branching and cutting decisions (p. 4), and a compact formulation always exists under mild assumptions (pp. 6-8). The cuts in the applications are written on flow variables of the compact model (Kohl et al. 2-path cuts, Gamache et al. deep cuts, p. 11).
- Method 1, variant ⚠ (steps 1-2): here the compact formulation is recovered after the extensive one, as a branching device. It is not written first, and it is not run as a benchmark (pp. 3, 9).
- Method 4, evidence ✅ (step 6): the Kantorovich attribution on p. 10 is the one the 2026 book later corrected (05-peer-critique.md §6).
- Cross-link: the stated price of two concurrent formulations (p. 11) is what GCG pays by running two synchronized SCIP instances (S011 pp. 4-5), and GCG's aggregation of identical blocks matches p. 9 (S011 p. 7).
- New-pattern candidates: a minimal counterexample against a standard rule (p. 5); reverse a decomposition to recover the object you branch on (pp. 6-9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Show the defect of a standard pricing rule with a minimal instance before proposing a remedy (p. 5).
- Recover a compact model from any extensive model by κ copies of the oracle domain, then branch by bisecting one copy's domain box, so the oracle never changes form (pp. 6-9).
- Keep identical copies aggregated in one oracle until branching makes them differ, and add their convexity rows on demand (p. 9).
- Use the freedom in choosing the compact formulation to avoid symmetry (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "use them as the source of information in guiding branching and cutting decisions" (p. 4; "them" are the original variables x)

### S022 · Solutions to real-world instances of PSPACE-complete stacking (ESA 2007, LNCS 4698, 729-740, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-75520-3_64)
**Read level**: full. All 12 pages of the published text read, including Tables 1-2; references (p. 12) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Felix G. König (first author), Rolf H. Möhring, Guido Schäfer, Ines Spenke, all at TU Berlin; the author line misprints his first name as Macro (p. 1). Industrial partner PSI-BT; support from the DFG Research Center MATHEON (p. 1). · **Place in trajectory**: the TU Berlin applications period in Möhring's group, between the 2005 survey and GCG. Later versions or follow-ups of this paper were not checked.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is industrial: storage planning for steel slabs between casting and rolling, from a cooperation with PSI-BT, a company that writes planning software for steel plants (pp. 1-2). The casting sequence differs from the rolling sequence, slabs must leave the caster within tight time windows, and the buffers are a bottleneck (p. 2). The literature on such stacking problems is mostly heuristic, simplifies the precedence constraints, and offers no exact approach (pp. 1, 3). The authors define a general problem class: incoming items with time windows on parallel inputs, buffer stacks with height bounds, a conflict graph saying which item may lie directly on which, target stacks with a collection order, at most w target stacks in parallel, and precedences among target stacks; the objective is the minimum number of moves (pp. 3-4). The question is how to compute provably good solutions fast for a problem that turns out to be PSPACE-complete (pp. 1-2).
  - Transferable: frame the industrial problem so that it is general enough to be a clean class and specific enough to transfer; the paper names rail-car shunting and container stowage (p. 1).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three parts. Hardness: the problem is in PSPACE, its feasibility version is PSPACE-complete even with no incoming items, stacks of height at most three and one requested item, and building a target sequence without any restacking is NP-hard for uniform heights of at least six (pp. 4-6). Algorithm: a search in the state graph of buffer configurations, guided by a valuation function in the spirit of approximate cost-to-go functions (p. 6). For the application the valuation counts *false positions*, the items lying on top of an item that is needed before them, with less weight for target stacks built later (pp. 8-9). The search is greedy with tie-breaks, a lookahead parameter f, a rule that prefers moves onto target stacks, a precomputed order of target stacks, and an optional rollout (p. 9). Certificate: per-instance lower bounds. The cold buffer gets a counting bound; the hot buffer gets the MIP of a relaxation that assumes as many stacks as items, ignores conflicts, keeps the time windows and maximises direct moves (pp. 9-10). Relative to the heuristic literature the bound is new; the solutions still come from a heuristic.
  - Transferable: when the full MIP is out of reach, drop the feature that makes it hard (here stacks and conflicts), keep the timing, and use that relaxation's value as a per-instance certificate for a heuristic (pp. 9-10).
  - Transferable: check a search framework on a puzzle whose optimal algorithm is known; a valuation built from two facts about the Towers of Hanoi makes the greedy search reproduce the unique optimal move sequence (pp. 7-8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Not a MILP; MIP enters only as the bound model (pp. 9-10). Modelling assumptions: at most one item is available per input at a time (p. 3); transport times matter for feasibility only, being small against pick-up and drop-off (p. 4); the initial configuration is feasible (p. 3); a few practical details are omitted (p. 3). The data come from PSI-BT. There are four hot-buffer instances (horizon up to 12 h, 3 caster inputs, 3 target stacks in parallel, 14-16 buffer stacks, a crane carrying one item) and fourteen cold-buffer instances (50 buffer stacks, 5 target stacks in parallel, no incoming items, a crane carrying several items up to a weight limit) (pp. 10-11). The MIP bound does not apply to the cold buffer, which has no incoming items (p. 11).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Membership in PSPACE follows from Savitch's theorem: configurations fit in polynomial space, and a nondeterministic search keeps only the current state (p. 4). PSPACE-completeness is shown by reduction from Hearn and Demaine's nondeterministic constraint logic, with and/or gadgets of two buffer stacks each, dummy items and conflict arcs (pp. 4-5). NP-hardness without restacking comes from mutual exclusion scheduling on permutation graphs (Jansen) (p. 6). The authors name what the first hardness proof relies on, a non-empty start and a dense conflict graph, and Theorem 3 removes both (p. 6). Search: the valuation (3)-(4) with a discount factor and the lookahead f, a greedy choice of neighbour, and a rollout in the sense of Bertsekas et al. (pp. 8-9). A bound computed with respect to the given target-stack precedences alone proved too weak to guide the search (p. 9). Bound: the MIP is solved with CPLEX 10.1 (p. 10). Section 4 says the bound comes from the LP relaxation (pp. 9-10), while Section 5 says the MIP was solved to integer optimality for three instances and the LP bound used for v3, where no integer solution was found (p. 10).
  - Transferable: prove hardness with as few features as possible, then name the ingredients the proof uses and show that hardness survives without them (pp. 5-6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Hardware: a standard PC running Linux, no further details (p. 10). No time limit is stated; the bound MIP took minutes to a few hours (p. 10). No competing algorithm and no record of current practice was run. Crane data from the manual operators did not exist, so the only comparison with practice is the plant's statement that at least about half of all transports are restacks, against under 20% in the computed plans (p. 11). Metrics: moves, gap to the bound, and time per move, below 0.1 s on the hot buffer and below 3 s on the cold buffer (pp. 10-11). Greedy gaps are 4.9-11.6% (hot) and 13.5-24.8% (cold); the rollout reaches 3.5-9.5% and 6.2-18.1% (Tables 1-2, pp. 10-11). My computation: Table 1's gaps are (UB-LB)/UB and Table 2's are (UB-LB)/LB, and neither caption says which. Measured against the bound, the hot greedy gaps are 11.4, 13.1, 5.2 and 8.5%, so the statement that the gap is below 12% (p. 10) holds only in the table's own measure. The 5/4 claim holds on all 18 instances; the largest ratio is 131/105, about 1.248 (instance ab1). Section 3 says the effect of the lookahead f is reported in Section 5 (p. 9), but Section 5 gives no values for f (pp. 10-11). Failures reported: the rollout multiplies the run time by more than 1,000 for a marginal gain and is ruled out for practice (p. 11); the v3 MIP found no integer solution (p. 10). The greedy search is deterministic, and one result per instance is reported (pp. 9-11).
  - Transferable: report an enhancement you tried, with its cost factor and its quality gain, and say plainly that it is not usable (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: Against a heuristic literature (simulated annealing, simulation, genetic algorithms, state-space evaluation) that simplifies the problem, especially its precedence constraints (p. 3); the authors say no general formulation of this kind, let alone solutions of proven quality, existed before (p. 2). Computer-science context: the Towers of Hanoi, sliding-block puzzles known to be PSPACE-complete, and sorting with networks of stacks (p. 2). Algorithmic lineage: approximate cost-to-go functions and rollout from Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (pp. 6, 9).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract runs hardness, a fast algorithm, the guarantee, and a firstness claim hedged with to the best of our knowledge (p. 1). The discussion answers the expected objection that 25% is weak with three comments: a conjecture that the true quality is much better than provable, an analogy with approximation algorithms, where a factor of 5/4 counts as very good, and the recent progress of MIP (pp. 11-12). The analogy sets an a-posteriori gap on 18 instances beside an a-priori worst-case guarantee, which the paper does not have (pp. 1, 11-12). The paper closes with practice: a prototype evaluates the buffers of two steel plants, and practitioners who saw animated plans were impressed by the low share of restacks (p. 12).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions on four levels: complexity (PSPACE-completeness, NP-hardness), algorithm (guided search), certificate (the relaxation bound) and use in practice (a prototype that evaluates the buffer performance of two steel plants) (pp. 4-12). Limits stated: better lower bounds would have to capture restacking (p. 11); characterising instance hardness by restacking complexity was not obvious in ongoing work, even for two buffer stacks (p. 11); there are no data on today's manual operation (p. 11). Not stated: the gap definitions and the missing lookahead analysis (D5). Future: characterise hardness by restacking complexity (p. 11), and apply discrete optimisation to more PSPACE-complete problems (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Taste G1 and H2, evidence ✅: the headline claim, within 5/4 of optimal on every real instance, rests on per-instance lower bounds (pp. 1, 10-11), and the prototype that went to two plants came with that certificate (p. 12).
- Method 3, evidence ✅: the claim is sized to the measurement (the 5/4 holds on all 18 instances, my computation), and a failed enhancement is reported with its cost (p. 11).
- Method 3, variant ⚠: no baseline was run (step 3); the two tables define the gap differently without saying so, and a promised analysis of the lookahead is missing (step 5); the approximation analogy stretches the claim (step 6) (pp. 9-12).
- Method 1, variant ⚠: a full MIP is declared intractable because it would need exponentially many variables (p. 10), but no compact model is run; MIP serves as the certificate on a relaxation, not as a competitor.
- New-pattern candidates: hardness first, then a fast heuristic with a per-instance certificate (pp. 2-10; compare S004 pp. 13-15 and S023 pp. 4, 9-10); a known puzzle as a test of a search framework (pp. 7-8); a relaxation that keeps only the timing as a certificate (pp. 9-10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove hardness with the fewest features, then name the proof's ingredients and remove them in a second hardness result (pp. 5-6).
- Certify a heuristic per instance with a relaxation that drops the feature making the MIP intractable but keeps the time windows (pp. 9-10).
- Test a guided search on a puzzle with a known optimal algorithm before applying it (pp. 7-8).
- Report an enhancement you tried and rejected, with its run-time factor and its quality gain (p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "We assess the quality of our solutions by computing instance-dependent lower bounds" (p. 1)

### S023 · Optimizing the cargo express service of Swiss Federal Railways (Transportation Science 42(4) 2008, DOI 10.1287/trsc.1080.0246)
**Read level**: full. All 16 pages read, including Figure 2 and Table 1; the network drawings (Figures 4-5, pp. 13-14) and the references (pp. 15-16) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Alberto Ceselli (first author, Milano), Michael Gatto and Marc Nunkesser (ETH Zürich), Heiko Schilling (TU Berlin); Lübbecke at TU Berlin (p. 1). Received November 2006, accepted May 2008 (p. 1); support from the EU project ARRIVAL (p. 15). · **Place in trajectory**: the TU Berlin applications period and the start of SCIP-based pricing. The authors say this is the first time SCIP was used to implement a column generation algorithm (p. 15), two years before GCG (S011). Same ARRIVAL project as S004.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a real service, SBB Cargo Express, an overnight hub-and-spoke freight system with two hubs whose planners build the yearly schedule by hand, without a decision support system (pp. 1, 3). Existing freight-rail models do not produce detailed schedules, and Gorman's tabu search can violate train loads and time windows, which are hard and tighter at SBB; the authors judge these models unfit for this service (p. 2). The problem, MHSOP, asks for a fleet size and a set of pairwise compatible, scheduled, time-consistent and load-admissible routes that move all shipments within hub capacities, at minimum route and engine cost (Definition 1, p. 4). It is strongly NP-hard, which the authors take to justify computationally expensive MIP approaches (p. 4).
  - Transferable: write down the current planning practice (by hand, adjusted in a trial period) and exactly which features earlier models drop, before choosing a model (pp. 2-3).
- **D2 Core idea**: Three models of increasing detail and algorithmic complexity, compared to find the best trade-off between modelling detail, tractability and implementation effort (p. 2). Model 0 is a compact three-index vehicle-flow ILP for a general solver, without hub overloading and with exactly one reclassification per shipment (pp. 3-4). Model 1 follows the planners' hierarchy, cluster first, route second, schedule third: a partition MIP, pickup and delivery routing transformed into distance-constrained VRPs and solved by branch-and-cut, and a time-indexed scheduling ILP that minimises the peak hub load (pp. 4-5, 7-10). Model 2 is a set-partitioning model over scheduled routes with hub-capacity rows, solved by column generation (pp. 6-7, 10-13). The case for Model 2: hierarchical planning loses optimisation potential and can hand infeasible inputs to the later steps (p. 6).
  - Transferable: build a ladder of models on the same instance, compact, then hierarchical, then integrated, and report what each rung costs to implement and what it buys (pp. 2, 15).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Binary and integer variables; time is discretised into 15-minute slots, with arrivals and departures at slot ends, a restriction the authors call common and mild (pp. 6, 13). Hub capacity is a hard constraint in Model 2 and the objective in Model 1's scheduling step, where it is treated as soft because yard capacity is a range rather than a threshold (p. 5). Set partitioning is relaxed to covering, because some optimum serves each shipment by exactly one route (p. 10). Structures exploited: VRP formulations, resource-constrained shortest paths, knapsack, and flow conservation over time (pp. 7-12). Data: SBB's network with 651 nodes and 1,488 edges, reduced by shortest-path pruning and contraction of degree-two nodes to 121 nodes and 332 edges (p. 13). A monthly demand matrix was turned into a daily average and rounded, giving 200 shipments. The real time windows were not available, so the current plan's pickup and delivery times, relaxed by one hour, served instead (p. 13). Parameters were set to realistic values: 25 cars per train, 60 km/h, 27 minutes for coupling and for shunting, a hub capacity of 80 cars, and an engine cost of 1,000 against 1 per km (pp. 13-14). Model 1's demands were aggregated together with the SBB planners (p. 14).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Model 1: Lemma 1 transforms each routing problem into a DCVRP with K extra nodes of large demand and negative edge weights; its proof is in a 2005 ETH report (pp. 7-8). The DCVRP is solved on Ralphs et al.'s open branch-and-cut code, extended with distance cuts. These are separated heuristically on components of the support graph, first as locally valid cuts, then as globally valid ones through 1-tree and assignment lower bounds (pp. 8-9). The authors note that heuristic separation can weaken bounds but not correctness (p. 9). A simplified scheduling variant is equivalent to a linear-arrangement problem that is NP-hard (Theorem 1, from the ETH report), yet the arising instances are small enough for the time-indexed ILP (6a)-(6j), solved within a few minutes (pp. 9-10). Model 2: of three constraint families any two imply the third, so one is dropped (p. 7). The restricted master starts with one direct-engine variable per shipment, so it is feasible at once and those variables are never priced (pp. 10, 12). Pricing is a labeling algorithm for a resource-constrained shortest path. Routes need only be *shipment elementary*, elementarity is enforced only on pickup stations, labels are pruned by a fractional-knapsack completion bound (citing Lübbecke 2005), and whole time slots can be dominated (pp. 11-12). Pricing of hub connections reduces to a knapsack (p. 12). Acceleration: network preprocessing; heuristic pricing (stop after a fixed number of negative columns, cap the labels, aggregate shipments, drop the heuristics once they find nothing); right-hand-side perturbation; column aging with a pool; and interior-point stabilisation, which improved convergence but made pricing harder and did not pay off (p. 12). The CPLEX barrier had severe numerical problems on the master (p. 12). A dive-and-generate heuristic rounds a column up, rounds incompatible ones down, re-solves, prices a fixed number of rounds, repairs an infeasible master by simulating simplex Phase I, and ends with a local improvement (pp. 12-13). Branching on the assignment of shipments to hubs was implemented but hardly tested (p. 15).
  - Transferable: seed the restricted master with one trivially feasible column per demand (here a dedicated direct engine), so the master is feasible from the start (p. 10).
  - Transferable: dive with column generation between fixings, and repair master infeasibility after rounding by a Phase I on the master (pp. 12-13).
  - Transferable: enforce elementarity only on the resources where it matters (pickup stations), so labeling stays tractable while the bound stays strong (p. 11).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: A standard Linux PC with a 3 GHz processor and 4 GB of memory; CPLEX 9 as the ILP solver for Model 0 and the LP solver for Models 1 and 2 (p. 13); SYMPHONY 5 with LEDA 4.5 (p. 14); SCIP with CPLEX for Model 2 (p. 15). No thread counts or time limits are given. The test set is one real instance of 200 shipments, plus an 11-shipment toy instance for Model 0 (pp. 13-14). Model 0 found no feasible solution of the toy instance in seven hours; feasible, not optimal, solutions for its first five and last six shipments separately took about 20 minutes (p. 14). Model 1 (Table 1, p. 14): the four routing instances took under a minute, 90 minutes, under a minute and 15 hours, with one 5.9% gap. Solving all routing instances, with the fleet size raised step by step, took about ten days in total. The plan uses 35 engines and 7,686 km at cost 42,686, and peak hub loads of 106 and 80 cars against a capacity of 80 leave a slight overload at Däniken. Model 2 (p. 15): tailing-off at the root after about four days, at a relaxation value of 29,217. The best solution, 35,276 with 27 engines and 8,276 km, came after 85 hours; similar quality came within 12 hours, without numbers. Comparison: Model 1 costs 7,410 more, which the paper calls 20% (p. 15); it is 21.0% of Model 2's cost and 17.4% of Model 1's (my computation). The gap between Model 2's solution and 29,217 is 17.2% of the solution value (20.7% of 29,217; my computation). ### S004 · The concept of recoverable robustness, linear programming recovery, and railway applications (Robust and Online Large-Scale Optimization, LNCS 5868, 1-27, 2009, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-05465-5_1)
**Read level**: full. All 27 pages of the published text read; references (p. 27) skimmed.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Christian Liebchen (first author), Rolf H. Möhring, Sebastian Stiller, all at TU Berlin; support from the EU FP6 project ARRIVAL (p. 1). · **Place in trajectory**: the ARRIVAL work on railway robustness of 2007-2009, beside S023. The formal definition first appeared in a 2007 ARRIVAL report by the same four authors, and the LP-recovery results are tied to Stiller's 2009 dissertation (pp. 3, 27). It is his fourth most cited work by 01-publications.md (517 citations) and lies outside the MILP lens. Who wrote which part is not stated.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is a gap between two textbook paradigms and railway practice. Robustness is a precondition for optimised plans to be used at all, and it is usually added afterwards by rules of thumb (p. 1). Two-stage stochastic programs are too large for timetabling, and classical robust optimisation is necessarily over-conservative there: each ride would carry the total likely disturbance of the whole network (p. 2). Practice does something else, slack plus limited recovery during operation, but plan, slack and recovery are not optimised together (p. 2). The problem is to optimise a plan and a limited recovery strategy jointly, combining the flexibility of stochastic programming with the guarantee and compactness of robust models (p. 3).
  - Transferable: locate a research gap by writing down what practitioners do between two textbook methods, then formalise that practice (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: A *precaution* pairs a plan with an admissible recovery algorithm; it is recovery robust if the algorithm repairs the plan in every scenario of a limited set (Definitions 1-4, pp. 4-5). Strict robustness is the special case in which recovery leaves the plan unchanged (p. 5). Limits on recovery are of two kinds, on its actions and on its computing power; extra information can be passed to the recovery, and the bound on recovery cost can be a planned variable (pp. 5-7). For timetabling, with at most k disturbed arcs, strict robustness still puts the full buffer on every arc, even for k = 1 (p. 9), while delay propagation alone already escapes that conservatism (p. 10). Cancelled transfers give a big-M MILP (p. 12). A rule-based delay management, with a waiting-time matrix by train type, gives a recovery that is fast, transparent to passengers and still optimisable as a MIP (pp. 13-15). Linear recovery: if the recovery is an LP, dualise it. Over an l1 ball of right-hand-side disturbances the worst case sits at a scaled unit vector, so the robust problem becomes one LP of polynomial size; over S_k it becomes one LP with a recovery copy per k-subset of rows (pp. 16-20). An integer planning model keeps its own solver and is extended by a linear block linked through slack variables (p. 21). This gives robust network buffering for periodic timetables and train platforming (pp. 21-25).
  - Transferable: when an inner recovery problem is an LP, dualise it and optimise over the few scenarios that can be worst cases, not over the whole set (pp. 19-20).
  - Transferable: robustify an integer model by adding a linear block linked through its row slacks, leaving the model and its solver untouched (pp. 21, 24-25).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: LPs, ILPs and MILPs with right-hand-side uncertainty in S_1 or S_k (pp. 18-21). The recovery must be an LP, since with integer recovery the approach fails in general (p. 20), and k must be very small in practice (p. 20). Disturbances are small, bounded by Δ per arc after row scaling, on at most k arcs; catastrophic events are deliberately left out of a published timetable (p. 9). The event-activity graph is acyclic without loss of generality (p. 8). The three-player formulation assumes that the extrema are attained (p. 18). Model classes used: the event-activity LP (p. 9), a big-M MILP for cancelled transfers (p. 12), PESP as a MIP with integer period offsets (p. 22), and a platforming ILP with lexicographic weights (pp. 24-25). The authors call the assumption of full scenario knowledge at recovery time a fundamental weakness, since real delay management must be non-anticipative (p. 13).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: The key device is strong LP duality for the inner recovery problem, which turns the max-min into a joint maximisation (equation (28), p. 19). Its maximum over the l1 ball is attained at Δ times a unit vector, which gives a reformulation with one recovery copy per row (pp. 19-20). Theorem 1 extends this to S_k with one copy per k-subset of rows, with Corollaries 1-2; the proof is a two-sentence sketch by analogy (p. 20). Further devices: the scenario expansion set against a three-player game of planner, scenario and recovery, both with the same feasible plans (pp. 17-18); row slacks as the channel between the original ILP and the robustness LP (p. 21); a cited PSPACE-hardness result for multistage delay management as the reason to restrict recovery to rules (p. 13); and a footnote that defines non-anticipative algorithms (p. 13). There is nothing on numerical safeguards.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: None is run in this paper. Section 5 reports a study by Caprara, Galli, Stiller and Toth, an ARRIVAL report of 2008 (pp. 3, 24). Table 1 (Palermo Centrale, seven time windows) shows the maximum propagated delay cut by 14.29-35.03%, with both methods platforming the same number of trains (pp. 25-26). The text adds that every instance of the study shows a double-digit cut, one of almost 50%, and about a quarter on average, over instances not all shown (p. 26); the Palermo average is 24.1% (my computation). The CPU times grow from 6-11 s to 18-3,826 s, by factors of 2.6 to 547 (my computation from Table 1). The text does not discuss them, and it says that the method does not add to the complexity of the original problem (p. 24), which holds for the model's size. No hardware, solver, time limit or seed is given here.
  - Transferable: when you cite another study's table, carry its time columns into the text and say what the method costs (missing here, pp. 24-26).
- **D6 Positioning**: Two-stage stochastic programming and classical robust optimisation (Soyster 1973; a 2006 Mathematical Programming special issue) are set up as the two exact methods; classical robustness is renamed strict robustness and made a special case (pp. 1-2, 5). Delay-resistant timetabling had so far been treated only by stochastic programming, at limited sizes (p. 9). Related ARRIVAL work on shunting, timetabling, multistage and dynamic recovery is listed without comparison (p. 3). Outside critiques (Shu and Song 2014 on equality constraints, Dewilde et al. 2014 on the worst-case delay cost, and a rival model by Cicerone et al.) are recorded in 05-peer-critique.md §8; I note only that the LRP here is written for inequality systems (pp. 16-17).
- **D7 Writing**: The framework is built in three named steps: the original problem, the scenarios and the recovery (p. 3). The framing claims are strong: a robust timetable is said to make sense only as a recovery-robust one (pp. 7-8). The scoping is honest: the general definition is very broad and the theorems cover only strong specialisations (p. 4); the mathematical programs state the problems and are not necessarily the way to solve them (p. 12); S_k works only for very small k (p. 20). The advantages are listed as three bullets (p. 24). The conclusion summarises, ends on a drastic cut in delay propagation against a state-of-the-art method, and names no future work (p. 26).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: a concept (definitions and a taxonomy of recovery limits, pp. 3-7), models (timetabling, delay management, PESP, platforming; pp. 7-15, 21-25), theory (LP-solvable LRPs over S_1 and S_k, pp. 16-21), and a cited application (pp. 24-26). Limits stated: very small k (p. 20), no integer recovery (p. 20), scenario expansions too large to solve (p. 12), the online weakness (p. 13), and waiting rules that keep a train waiting for a hopelessly late connection (p. 15). Not stated: the measured time cost (D5). Matrix disturbances are only pointed to in Stiller's work (p. 3). Future work: none in the conclusion (p. 26).

**Method links**:
- Method 3, evidence ✅ (step 6, in the theory): the general concept is scoped explicitly, the theorems are said to cover only strong specialisations (p. 4), and the S_k result is limited to very small k (p. 20).
- Method 3, variant ⚠ (steps 3 and 6): the evidence is another group's study, not re-run (pp. 3, 24). The text passes over the CPU columns of its own table (factors of 2.6 to 547, my computation) while saying the method adds no complexity, and calls an average cut of about a quarter drastic (pp. 24, 26).
- Method 1, variant ⚠ (step 5 in spirit): the original integer model and its solver stay untouched, and robustness is a linear add-on (pp. 21, 24-25). This is the reuse of an existing solver from Method 1 step 5, applied to robustness instead of decomposition.
- Taste G3, evidence ✅: the problem comes from railway practice and needs a new concept rather than an off-the-shelf method (pp. 1-2).
- New-pattern candidates: hardness as the reason for a tractable subclass shaped by practice (rule-based delay management, pp. 13-15; compare S022); worst-case scenarios at the vertices of the uncertainty set (pp. 19-20); robustify by adding, not rewriting (pp. 21, 24-25).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Dualise an LP recovery problem and restrict the scenario set to its possible worst cases: unit vectors for S_1, k-subsets for S_k (pp. 19-20).
- Add robustness to an existing integer model as a linear block linked through row slacks, and keep the original solver (pp. 21, 24-25).
- Replace an intractable recovery by an optimisable rule, here a waiting-time matrix by train type, that is fast and transparent (pp. 13-15).

**Quotes**:
- "The theorems in this paper only apply to strong specializations of that concept." (p. 4)

### S011 · Experiments with a generic Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition for integer programs (SEA 2010, LNCS 6049, 239-252, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-13193-6_21)
**Read level**: full. All 14 pages of the published text read, including Tables 1-3 (Table 3 checked row by row); references (pp. 13-14) skimmed. The SKILL's Signature Work Anatomy quotes the Optimization Online preprint; the passages it quotes recur in this text (pp. 2, 4).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Gerald Gamrath (first author, ZIB); the details are in his TU Berlin Master's thesis (pp. 5, 9, 13). Lübbecke at TU Darmstadt (p. 1). · **Place in trajectory**: the origin of GCG (SKILL Signature Work Anatomy). It joins the compact-formulation theory of S021 and the SCIP pricing experience of S023 (S023 p. 15), and its closing question became the group's current research on detecting whether and how to decompose (p. 13), which the SKILL places in IPCO 2011 and the 2015 journal paper.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is an asymmetry in the field: branch-and-cut is generic in commercial and open solvers, while almost every branch-and-price application is ad hoc, started from scratch or from earlier projects, and needs expert knowledge (p. 1). Even the generic decomposition codes of the time (BaPCod, DIP, G12) rebuild standard MIP machinery such as preprocessing and tree management (p. 2). The goal is stated as the log of a future solver that reports an optimal solution, the cuts applied and a Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition with three subproblems, with no user involvement (pp. 1-2). The paper's own problem is narrower: given a MIP and the user's assignment of rows to blocks, decompose it and solve the reformulation by branch-price-and-cut inside SCIP (pp. 1, 4).
  - Transferable: state the end goal as the output a future tool would print, then scope the paper as one measured step toward it (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Turn the framework into a solver (p. 4). GCG runs two SCIP instances, one for the original problem and one for the extended problem. The original is primary and coordinates the search; the extended one is driven by a relaxation handler inside the original (p. 4). The two trees correspond node by node: branching is decided in the original and transferred, and the extended instance solves the master by column generation and returns bounds and solutions (p. 5). The authors chose this over transforming once and solving the extended problem, because it fits SCIP better and uses more of what SCIP already provides (p. 5). Kept: SCIP's presolve, propagation, branching, cuts and heuristics, and every later improvement of them (p. 2). Changed: the node relaxation becomes the master LP (p. 5). Both convexification and discretisation are supported, and identical blocks are aggregated (pp. 3, 7). Pricing runs through a variable pricer that calls black-box pricing solvers, with Farkas pricing for infeasible masters (p. 6). Branching is on original variables, with pseudocosts, or by Ryan-Foster for set-partitioning masters (p. 7).
  - Transferable: add a new relaxation to a solver as a relaxation handler, keep the original problem as the coordinating instance, and inherit every component the solver has (pp. 2, 4-5).
  - Transferable: make subproblem solvers pluggable, with the general MIP solver as the default, so that a specialised solver is one plugin and one ablation column away (pp. 6, 9).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIPs whose constraints split into linking rows and a (bordered) block-diagonal part; bounded blocks are assumed for ease of presentation, and discretisation needs pure integer blocks (p. 3). The block structure is supplied by the user in a file; without it SCIP runs plain branch-and-cut (pp. 4-5). Test structures: the capacitated p-median problem with one non-identical block per facility (p. 9); bin packing with identical blocks and a set-partitioning master, on the 540 instances of Scholl and Klein's data set 1 (p. 10); and a temporal knapsack with staircase rather than block structure, forced into blocks of 32 or 64 capacity rows by copying shared variables and linking the copies, as in Caprara, Furini and Malaguti (pp. 10-11). The p-median and knapsack instances came from Ceselli and Malaguti (p. 13).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: The correctness invariant is that corresponding nodes are pruned together, because they share the dual bound and both instances share the primal bound, each solution of one mapping to a solution of the other with the same value (p. 5). A fractional master solution may still give an integral original solution (p. 5). Lagrangean bounds are computed whenever all pricing problems are solved to optimality in a round, and pricing stops early when all objective values are integral and the rounded bound reaches the master value (p. 6). Branching decisions can be enforced in the pricing problems, which strengthens bounds but requires propagation to fix incompatible master columns to zero (p. 7). Cuts come from the original variables, but only from separators that need no LP basis, because the convex combination is not a basic solution of the original LP: knapsack cover, MIR and flow cover cuts, but no Gomory or strong Chvátal-Gomory cuts (pp. 7-8). SCIP's heuristics run on the extended instance; most of them cannot run on the original, which has no LP of its own (p. 8). The authors list what version 0.7 lacks: per-row dual stabilisation, a column pool, heuristics on original variables, and an LP basis of the original variables for Gomory cuts (p. 13). There is no proof beyond the pruning argument.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: GCG 0.7 on SCIP 1.2.0.5, with CPLEX 12.1 as the LP solver (p. 8). Sections 4.1-4.2 ran on a 2.66 GHz Core2 Quad (4 MB cache, 4 GB RAM) and Section 4.3 on a 2.83 GHz Core2 Quad (6 MB cache, 16 GB RAM) (p. 8); thread counts are not stated. Averages are shifted geometric means, with shift 10 for time and nodes and 100 for gaps (p. 8). The only baseline is SCIP on the original formulation. No other decomposition code, no tailored code (Ceselli-Righini for the p-median, Caprara et al. for the temporal knapsack) and no commercial MIP solver is run (pp. 8-12). Capacitated p-median (Table 1, p. 9): a declared subset, every second instance up to 150 nodes and 12 instances at 200 nodes, with no time limit stated. GCG takes 220.1 s against SCIP's 294.0 s, with 9 against 19 timeouts, 1.34 times faster (my computation). Most-fractional branching doubles the time (439.7 s), and a knapsack pricing solver cuts it by 61.7% (84.3 s). Bin packing (Table 2, p. 10): all 540 instances are solved, in under 90 minutes in total according to the text; the table's totals sum to 2,495.9 s, about 42 minutes (my sum). The text also says GCG solves each test set faster than SCIP solves its first instance, but prints no SCIP numbers. Temporal knapsack (Table 3, pp. 10-12): 70 instances, a one-hour limit. SCIP solves 5 and leaves gaps of 0.1-3.0%; GCG solves 56 with either block size. Shifted geometric mean times are 2,772.4 s against 727.4 s and 670.7 s, which the paper calls about four times faster (3.8 and 4.1, my computation; SCIP's times are capped at 3,600 s, so its true times are longer). Mismatches between text and table (my reading): the text gives 21 nodes for 32-row blocks, where Table 3 shows 32.2; and the total time is said to fall by 8% from 32 to 64 rows, which matches the shifted geometric mean (7.8%), while the totals fall by 2.8% (p. 12). Losses the text does not mention: on new4 1-3, which SCIP solves in 4.8-16.1 s, GCG needs 17.9-26.1 s with 32-row blocks and 15.6-28.4 s with 64-row blocks (Table 3, p. 11; only on new4 1 is GCG with 64-row blocks faster, by 0.5 s). The single large gap with 64-row blocks is explained: a root master not solved within the hour (pp. 12-13). One result per instance is reported; there are no seeds.
  - Transferable: put the host framework's own run in the first column and the component's switches as ablation columns of the same table (pseudocosts off, specialised pricer on), with timeouts in brackets (Table 1, p. 9).
  - Transferable: when you thin a test set to save time, state the rule (every second instance; three per number of facilities) (p. 9).
- **D6 Positioning**: Frameworks that support branch-and-price (ABACUS, BCP, MINTO) are set apart from codes that decompose a general MIP: Vanderbeck's BaPCod, COIN-OR's DIP, and G12's mapping to branch-and-price; of these only DIP was public, as trunk development without a release (p. 2). Vanderbeck's features and the Vanderbeck-Wolsey survey are credited (pp. 2-3). The claimed difference is to complement a well-maintained MIP solver instead of starting from scratch (p. 2). Tailored codes are expected to win (p. 8).
- **D7 Writing**: The title says experiments, and the first section is called Situation (p. 1). The vision is hedged: the authors grant that a complete automatism may meet scepticism, then forecast that a public generic implementation is months rather than years away (p. 2). The summary sizes the result: first experiments with a basic implementation, very promising for structured problems (p. 13). It names the open question: whether decomposition pays on a significant share of problems whose structure is not known, which requires detecting whether and how to decompose (p. 13). The slips are listed under D5.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is software and design, a generic branch-price-and-cut solver on SCIP, with a proof of concept on three structures (pp. 4-12).
