### S005 · The SCIP Optimization Suite 6.0 (ZIB-Report 18-26 2018, no DOI or arXiv id; ZIB OPUS docId 6936)
**Read level**: full. All 42 file pages: §§1–7 (pp. 4–38) and "Code Contributions of the Authors" (p. 39) read completely; Tables 1–4 (pp. 14, 15, 23, 37) read; Figures 1–8 legible as captions only; references (pp. 39–42) skimmed. Checked on pp. 1 and 3: the title, and "Marco E. Lübbecke" among the authors.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: 25 authors, Ambros Gleixner first; affiliation 2, the RWTH Aachen Chair of Operations Research, covers Bastubbe, Lübbecke, Puchert, Walter and Witt (p. 3) · **Place in trajectory**: dated June 2018 on the cover (p. 1) and July 2, 2018 on the title page (p. 3); presents GCG 3.0 (§5, pp. 29–36) and announces that the next MIPLIB "will contain structure information provided with GCG 3.0" (p. 34). The code-contribution statement assigns the GCG work to MB (detection redesign, visualization), SM (interaction with the Benders framework), CP (pricing loop, primal heuristics), JoW (master initialization, column and cut management, dual stabilization) and MW (cmake build files), and names no contribution by Lübbecke (p. 39).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A release report, so the problem is a list of gaps in the software; "One emphasis of this release lies on new functionality for decomposition methods" (p. 4). Benders' decomposition was possible in SCIP from the start through a constraint handler but "still involved an understanding of problem-specific details" (p. 8). GCG's earlier detection ran static detectors on the whole matrix; the hmetis parameters limited what the group could steer, so "very regular textbook decompositions of structured models" were not consistently detected (p. 30), and for the capacitated p-median instance p2050-1 a hypergraph approach would have to remove about half of the hyperedges to reveal its 50 blocks (Fig. 4, p. 30). The old pricing loop did no exact pricing before all pricing problems had been solved heuristically or heuristic pricing was aborted (p. 35). The stated aim is decomposition without user knowledge of structure, "more widely applicable also to non-experts" (p. 29).
  - Transferable: motivate a redesign with a named textbook instance on which the old component fails (p2050-1, Fig. 4, p. 30).
- **D2 Core idea**: GCG 3.0 (a) builds decompositions in rounds of small detectors, each fixing the roles of some constraints and variables in a partial decomposition ("seeed") through propagate, finish or postprocess callbacks, with duplicates filtered and the results kept as a family tree (p. 31); (b) classifies constraints (nonzeros, constraint handler, MIPLIB 2010 type, names within small edit distance) and variables (type, objective sign) and tentatively assigns whole classes to the master, or to linking or static roles (p. 32); (c) proposes block numbers from votes for greatest common divisors of class cardinalities, in place of Wang and Ralphs's vote by numbers of nonzeros (pp. 32–33); (d) scores complete decompositions by rewarding "textbook" ones (a set-partitioning master with identical blocks) and other scoretypes, weighted experimentally together with the white-area share (p. 33); (e) detects Benders structure and hands it to SCIP's new framework, with a score that encourages linking variables and penalizes linking constraints (p. 34); (f) prices through jobs (pricing problem, solver, heuristic flag) in a priority queue under a pricing controller that may abort the loop, with repeated heuristic iterations at increased limits (p. 35). SCIP 6.0 adds Benders plugin types with branch-and-check (pp. 6–13), Farkas and conflict diving (pp. 16–18), lookahead branching (pp. 18–19), GCD-based scaling in CMIR separation (p. 19) and the directed cutoff distance for cut selection (pp. 19–20).
  - Transferable: treat structure detection as search over partial decompositions by small composable detectors instead of one monolithic partitioner (p. 31).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP (1) and possibly nonconvex MINLP (2) inside constraint integer programming (pp. 4–5). Benders targets the bordered block-diagonal form (3)–(7) (p. 6); its cut plugins cover continuous variables in both stages, a mixed-integer first stage with a continuous second stage, and a binary first stage with a mixed-integer second stage (p. 10). GCG handles single-bordered block-angular forms, applies a Lagrangean decomposition when linking variables appear (p. 30), and can now detect double-bordered staircase forms (p. 31). Its modelling assumption is explicit: "We assume that MIP modelers typically define sets of constraints" that either distribute among the blocks or stay wholly in the master (p. 32). Test sets: for SCIP, 666 MIPs from COR@L and the five MIPLIB versions without duplicates and instances marked "numerically unstable", restricted to the 425 that some version solved, and 143 MINLPs from MINLPLib2 restricted to 113 (p. 13); for GCG's detection, all 361 MIPLIB 2010 instances (p. 34).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: Benders' branch-and-check uses cons_benders below the integer handler's enforcement priority, so only potential incumbents are checked, and cons_benderslp above it for the two-phase method, active at the root by default (pp. 10–11); subproblems are merged into the master when no suitable cut exists or numerical trouble occurs (pp. 9, 12); a presolve step bounds the auxiliary variables (pp. 12–13); multiple decompositions are called in priority order (p. 13). GCG falls back to "SCIP-mode", with an empty master and one block, when nothing is detected (p. 31). Because presolvers are not decomposition-aware, GCG leaves out conflict analysis and several SCIP presolvers, and can classify and detect on the original problem and translate the result (p. 32). Further: a column pool on the model of SCIP's cut management, dual stabilization (smoothing combined with the ascent of Pessoa et al.) at the root only, the original LP relaxation solved for a first dual bound and for SCIP's heuristics, artificial variables with big M "when this is numerically safe", discretization when continuous variables are convexified, and a price-and-branch heuristic (p. 36). Numerical safeguards on the SCIP side: Farkas rays double-checked by default (p. 22) and a SoPlex status OPTIMAL_UNSCALED_VIOLATIONS as last resort (p. 23). The only stated invariant is Observation 2.1: a conflict lock implies a model lock in the same direction (p. 18).
  - Transferable: give a generic decomposition code two monolithic fallbacks: run the compact model when no structure is found, and merge a subproblem back into the master when it cannot produce a cut (pp. 9, 12, 31).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: SCIP 6.0 against 5.0: five seeds including the release seed, each instance-seed pair one observation (2125 MIPs, 565 MINLPs), numerically inconsistent results excluded, shifted geometric means with shifts 1 s and 100 nodes, subsets "affected", [t,T] brackets, "both-solved" and "diff-timeout", MIPLIB against COR@L (pp. 13–14); Intel Xeon Gold 5122 at 3.6 GHz with 92 GB, GCC 5.4, SoPlex 3.1.0 and 4.0.0, Ipopt 3.12.5 with MUMPS 4.10, bliss 0.73, limits 7200 s (MIP) and 3600 s (MINLP) (p. 14). Results: MIP time ratio 1.08 on 2113 pairs, 1.03 on MIPLIB, 1.11 on COR@L, 1.18 in [100,7200] (p. 14); MINLP 1.23 overall, 1.65 in [100,3600], 31 more solved (p. 15). Component claims rest on shorter runs: Farkas diving 2% "using two random seeds" (p. 17), directed cutoff distance 4% (9% on harder instances) (p. 20), abbreviated lookahead almost 40% fewer nodes, 35% by the fair node number (p. 19); SoPlex's aggregation presolver is shown on one instance (sgpf5y6, 718 s to 22 s, p. 23). UG: 87 MIPLIB 2010 benchmark instances, five repeated runs because the parallel runs are not deterministic (pp. 36–37). GCG gets no such protocol: the comparison with GCG 2.1.4 is stated as "Preliminary results" without numbers (p. 29); the new detection is measured by detection counts on the 361 MIPLIB 2010 instances (the basic setting fails 10 times, 3 of them timeouts; almost 300 instances with at least 2 blocks), not by solve outcomes (p. 34); against CPLEX 12.8's automatic Benders, "CPLEX is clearly faster on MIPLIB models that both can solve to optimality", while GCG is "much more successful in identifying structure amenable to Benders’ decomposition", without counts (p. 34). Harm reported: gauge separation and quadratic disaggregation deactivated because they "deteriorate SCIP’s performance on average" (p. 15); conflict diving off by default until evaluated (p. 18).
  - Transferable: the SCIP release protocol (instance-seed pairs as observations, stated shifts, difficulty brackets, both-solved against diff-timeout) shows whether a speedup comes from newly solved instances or from the already-solved majority (pp. 13–15).
- **D6 Positioning**: The suite as coordinated development, with GCG's use of SCIP's Benders framework as the showcase (p. 4). The Benders lineage is credited (Benders; Laporte and Louveaux; Carøe and Tind; Hooker and Ottosson; McDaniel and Devine; Magnanti and Wong) (pp. 6–12). ### S052 · The strength of Dantzig–Wolfe reformulations for the stable set and related problems (Discrete Optimization 2018, doi:10.1016/j.disopt.2018.07.001)
**Read level**: full. All 20 in-press pages: §§1–7 with every proof (pp. 1–19) read completely; Figures 1–4 (pp. 7, 15, 17, 18) legible as captions only; references (pp. 19–20) skimmed. Checked on p. 1: the title, and "Marco E. Lübbecke" as first and corresponding author.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jonas T. Witt (RWTH); Lübbecke is the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: received 30 November 2017, accepted 5 July 2018 (p. 1). S058 cites an earlier version as the RWTH report 2015-029 by Witt and Lübbecke, "Dantzig-Wolfe reformulations for the stable set problem" (S058 p. 12, ref. [14]), and starts from it (S058 p. 2). A companion paper by Witt, Lübbecke and Reed (Math. Program., online first 2017) proves Theorem 8 by another technique (p. 9; ref. [22], p. 20). The computational sequel is S058.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In general one knows only the sandwich P_IP ⊆ P_DW(I′) ⊆ P_LP (1) and Geoffrion's necessary condition (2), which is not sufficient: "Interestingly, this is already all we know about the strength of Dantzig–Wolfe reformulations of integer programs in general." (p. 2). Entry: "focus on a particular problem but stay generic in terms of reformulations" (p. 2). The stable set problem is chosen because other relaxations (Sherali–Adams, Lovász–Schrijver, Lasserre, the corner relaxation) were compared on it, and because conflicts in general IPs appear as stable-set structure in the conflict graph (p. 2). Earlier Dantzig-Wolfe and Lagrangian work on the stable set problem (Warrier et al., Sachdeva, Ribeiro et al., Campêlo and Corrêa, Gabrel) is called "rather empirical" and limited to specific classes of subgraphs (pp. 2–3).
  - Transferable: to study a property of a generic method, fix one canonical problem whose polytope is well studied and vary only the method's free choice (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Identify the convexified constraints with a subgraph G′ = (V, E′) and characterize both extremes over all E′ (pp. 3–4): DW(G, G′) = FRAC(G) iff G′ is bipartite (Theorem 2, pp. 4–5), and DW(G, G′) = STAB(G) iff G′ contains all odd induced cycles of G (Theorem 7, p. 9), from necessity (Theorem 3, p. 5) and sufficiency (Theorem 6, pp. 8–9); sufficiency rests on a weighted generalization of Andrásfai's theorem to facet-graphs (Theorem 5, pp. 6–7) and on Lemmas 2–3 (pp. 7–8). By-products: Geoffrion's necessary condition is sufficient for the edge formulation (p. 5); Theorem 8, pointed out by Bruce Reed, characterizes odd pairs polyhedrally (p. 9); every critical edge of a facet-graph lies in an odd induced cycle, a weakened answer to a question in Sewell's thesis (p. 18). Transfer: clique and node cover directly (p. 10), matching through the blossom description (Theorems 12–13, pp. 12–13), set packing only partly (Corollaries 2–4, Proposition 1, pp. 14–17).
  - Transferable: test whether a known necessary condition for strengthening (Geoffrion's) becomes sufficient on your formulation; it gives the "weakest" half of a characterization in a few lines (pp. 5, 12).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pure IPs with bounds, max cᵀx, a_iᵀx ≤ b_i, x ∈ Zⁿ ∩ [ℓ, u] (p. 1); maximum weight stable set in the edge formulation x_u + x_v ≤ 1 with binary x and weights w ∈ Z≥0^V (p. 3); matching in the node formulation, convexifying the degree constraints of a node subset V′ through MP(G, V′) (p. 11); set packing Ax ≤ 1 with A ∈ {0,1}^(m×n) and its conflict graph G(A) (pp. 13–14). The characterizations are polytope identities, hence independent of the objective (S058 p. 2 says so of the report version), while the strengthening actually obtained depends on the objective (p. 19).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design (here: proof devices)**:
  - Half-integral odd-cycle witness: x̄ = 1/2 on an odd cycle and 0 elsewhere lies in FRAC(G) but violates the odd-cycle inequality, so it is outside STAB(G′) (Theorem 2, p. 5); the same point, written as half the sum of the two alternating stable sets of G′, lies in DW(G, G′) when one edge of an odd induced cycle is not convexified (Theorem 3, p. 5); reused for matching (Theorems 12–13, pp. 12–13) and, with a five-node support, for odd antiholes (Corollary 4, p. 17).
  - A five-node counterexample shows that odd induced cycles cannot be replaced by odd cycles in Theorem 3 (p. 5).
  - Weighted Andrásfai (Theorem 5, pp. 6–7): take maximum weight stable sets S of G0, avoiding u and v by Lemma 1, and S⁺ of G0 − e; show that u and v lie in one component of the bipartite graph on S⁺ \ S and S \ S⁺, since otherwise a component exchange (S⁺⁺) would contradict the maximality of S; close a shortest u–v path into a chordless odd cycle through e.
  - Induction that adds back the edges of a minimal critical set: each added edge either misses the current odd induced cycle or is its chord and creates a shorter odd induced cycle through it (Lemma 2, pp. 7–8).
  - A modified deletion order in the construction of an α_π-critical subgraph after Lipták–Lovász and Sewell (first delete non-critical edges that lie in no odd induced cycle) leaves only edges that lie in odd induced cycles (Lemma 3, p. 8); sufficiency then follows facet by facet (Theorem 6, p. 9).
  - A reformulation identity read as a polyhedral theorem: DW(G + vw, G) = {x ∈ STAB(G) : x_v + x_w ≤ 1} (Theorem 8, p. 9).
  - The complement graph and the affine isomorphism x ↦ 1 − x carry the results to clique and node cover (p. 10).
  - A gadget graph G̃ makes MP(G, V′) isomorphic to an ordinary matching polytope (Lemma 4, pp. 11–12), so Edmonds' blossom description and the Edmonds–Pulleyblank facets (2-connected, factor-critical) apply (Theorems 10–11, Corollary 1, pp. 11–12).
  - For set packing, a sandwich with a perfect graph whose cliques are dominated by rows of A (Proposition 1, p. 15), via Chvátal's perfect-matrix theorem (p. 14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: none. "Computational implications of our work may be interesting, but are out of the scope of this paper." (p. 19)
- **D6 Positioning**: Geoffrion 1974 is the only general predecessor, and the abstract places the paper right after it (pp. 1, 3). The polyhedral tools are credited by name (Nemhauser–Trotter, Padberg, Lipták–Lovász, Sewell, Andrásfai, Edmonds, Edmonds–Pulleyblank, Chvátal; pp. 4–14). Earlier stable-set decompositions are summarized and labelled empirical, not criticized in detail (pp. 2–3). Bruce Reed is credited with the polyhedral reading of Theorem 7 (p. 9); the reviewers with the push to unify the stable-set and matching proofs, and Matthias Walter with clarified matching proofs (p. 19). The authors' SEA 2015 paper is cited as practical evidence that generic cuts hardly strengthen DW reformulations (p. 19, ref. [36]).
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract claims "the first non-trivial general results" since Geoffrion (p. 1); the body adds "To the best of our knowledge" (p. 3). The introduction disclaims computational use at once (p. 3). The discussion calls the authors' own focus "a seminal step" ("As a seminal step towards an answer", p. 18). Theorem–proof style with one motivating paragraph per section; §6.2 opens "Once our argumentation is in place, we can see other polyhedral results from the literature through this lens" (p. 10). An alternative proof is kept with a stated reason, "for its insights into the structure of facet-graphs" and into separation (p. 9). Open problems are stated precisely: "Unfortunately, we were not yet able to find a counterexample or prove that the sufficient condition" of Proposition 1 is also necessary (p. 16).
  - Transferable: when your theorem is proved independently elsewhere, keep your proof only with one sentence on what it adds (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theoretical: two characterizations, structural results on facet-graphs, a polyhedral corollary on odd pairs, transfers to clique, node cover and matching, and partial conditions for set packing (pp. 18–19); a separation remark: facet-inducing cuts need only the subgraph spanned by the edges that lie in odd induced cycles (p. 18). Limits, concrete: deciding whether an edge lies in an odd induced cycle is NP-complete (Bienstock), and W[1]-complete when parameterized by hole size (p. 19); even if built, the strongest reformulation's LP is expected to be "too hard to solve effectively in practice" (p. 19); the set-packing gap stays open and is tied to the perfect graph sandwich problem, whose complexity is open (pp. 16, 19); for general IPs no proper hull-giving subset may exist, "So one would need to settle with less." (p. 19). Future: reformulations between the extremes, e.g. convexifying all odd induced cycles up to size k (p. 19); the strength of DW reformulations relative to other relaxations on the same model (p. 19).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 evidence ✅ (exit B): the pattern is proved for one class, extended where complete hull descriptions exist, and what resists is published as open (set packing; reformulations in between; pp. 13–19).
- Method 2 variant ⚠ (order): the theorem comes before the enumeration; this paper computes nothing (p. 19), and S058 cites the 2015 report version as its starting point (S058 p. 2). This confirms with pages the skill's limitation note that "The 2018 study starts from a theorem".
- Method 3 evidence ✅ (claim sized in a theory paper): no computationally viable procedure is implied (p. 3); computational implications are out of scope, recognition is NP-complete, and theoretical and practical strength may differ (p. 19). Counterpoint, Taste B3 variant ⚠: an unhedged priority claim in the abstract (p. 1) and "seminal step" for the authors' own focus (p. 18).
- Taste G1 evidence ✅: every strength statement is a theorem with proof (pp. 4–17).
- Inner Tension 6 evidence: in 2018 the authors already cite their own practical evidence that DW reformulations "cannot (much) be further strengthened by generic classes of cutting planes" (p. 19), the pole the skill dates to 2020.
- New-pattern candidate "extremes first, then in between": both extremes are characterized and the family between them is named as the next step (pp. 3–4, 9, 18–19); S058 then asks what happens "in between these extremes" (S058 p. 1).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Prove that a decomposition does not improve on the LP, or does not reach the hull, with the half-integral odd-cycle point: 1/2 on the cycle violates the odd-cycle inequality, and as half the sum of the two alternating stable sets it lies in the reformulated polytope when one cycle edge is left out (pp. 5, 12–13, 17).
- Prove that a decomposition reaches the hull facet by facet: for each non-trivial facet, find a subgraph inside the convexified part on which the facet is already valid (an α_π-critical subgraph whose edges all lie in odd induced cycles) (pp. 8–9).
- Generalize an unweighted critical-graph theorem to weights through the symmetric difference of two maximum weight solutions, a component exchange and a shortest path (pp. 6–7).
- Turn a partially relaxed polytope (degree constraints only on V′) into a standard one with a gadget graph, so that known complete descriptions apply (Lemma 4, pp. 11–12).
- Close a structural theory paper with the complexity barriers to using it (NP-completeness, W[1]-completeness) and a candidate family in between that might stay tractable (p. 19).

- "Then again, the actual strengthening of the formulation by Dantzig–Wolfe reformulation also depends on the objective function, and a theoretically weak reformulation may be strong in practice (and vice versa)." (p. 19)

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Elisabeth Lübbecke (first author; INFORM Software, p. 1; her 2015 TU Berlin PhD thesis on bidirectional traffic holds the algorithmic details, pp. 16, 33) and Rolf H. The cooperation partner was the canal administration's enlargement planning group (p. 31); an 87-ship instance of the paper's MIP, shipsched, belongs to the MIPLIB 2010 challenge set and is still unsolved (p. 27). The file is the manuscript submitted to Operations Research (p. 1), not the typeset article.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A new practical problem, the ship traffic control problem (STCP), at the world's most frequented artificial waterway (p. 1): the canal is bi-directional, large ships can pass each other only in sidings, and one must decide "who is waiting for whom, where, and for how long" so as to minimize the total waiting time (pp. 1–2). Two entries: the planners' existing tool, a distance-time diagram (pp. 4–5), and a strategic question, since growing ship sizes may make the canal inoperable and an enlargement is planned (p. 2). The problem is placed between single-track train scheduling, read as job-shop scheduling, and collision-free AGV routing by successive quickest paths (pp. 3, 9); none of the existing approaches handles arbitrary start and target positions, an arbitrary conflict graph and continuous halting positions (pp. 9–10). Complexity: on a single segment the problem is polynomial for train scheduling but APX-hard for ships with arbitrary conflicts (p. 10), and the relaxation without siding constraints is already NP-hard (p. 10).
  - Transferable: place a new applied problem between two known algorithm families and list exactly what each fails to handle (pp. 9–10).
- **D2 Core idea**: Separate the combinatorics (on each transit segment, which of two conflicting ships precedes) from the geometry (each ship's dynamic route as a polyline in space and time) (pp. 2, 11). For the relaxation without siding constraints, any realizable precedence structure is realized in polynomial time by greedy routing of one ship at a time, or proven unrealizable (Theorem 1, Algorithm 1, pp. 13–14). With siding constraints, a Dijkstra-like quickest-path algorithm with interval labels and forbidden entry-time windows routes one ship against all others without time or space discretization (§4, pp. 15–21). Because every sequential routing can be arbitrarily bad (Lemma 2, p. 21), the search acts on the precedences: a local search whose neighbours reorder a waiting ship with the ships it waits for, realized and repaired by the backtracking Algorithm 1b (pp. 22–24), inside a rolling horizon of 2 hours with 1-hour steps (pp. 25–26). Kept from AGV routing: successive quickest paths (Gawrilow et al.); changed: labels as time intervals, entry times forbidden instead of whole arcs blocked, waiting positions chosen dynamically (pp. 3, 15–17).
  - Transferable: when discrete precedence decisions are coupled with continuous timing, search over the precedences and realize each candidate with an exact polynomial routine (pp. 13, 22–24).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Geometric model: the canal as an interval partitioned into segments, sidings with passage and waiting tracks, requests (s_i, t_i, v_i, r_i, h_i), vertical distances v_ijE that may be asymmetric, parking distances h_i (pp. 5–7); feasibility as passing rules and disjoint parking rectangles (pp. 7–8). Assumptions: constant full speed for each ship, "as planners do" (p. 4); no overtaking of a moving ship (p. 4); at most one wait per siding (p. 7); a look-ahead of about two hours (p. 5). The combinatorial relaxation is a compact MIP (1)–(8) with visit times, waiting times and binary precedence variables z_ijE linked by big-M implications (pp. 10–11); its LP relaxation gives the trivial bound zero (Remark 2, pp. 11–12). Siding capacity as a knapsack constraint is weaker than the rectangle-packing model (Remark 1, p. 8). Data: GPS itineraries of about 43,000 ships in 2007, giving 365 daily instances with 185 requests on average (83 to 247) (p. 25).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: MIP (1)–(8), with optimal visit times from an LP once all z are fixed (p. 11); a unified precedence condition d_iE + Δ(i, j, E) ≤ d_jE with asymmetric and possibly negative Δ, checked at segment boundaries (pp. 10–12). Algorithm 1 extends the prefix routes of "free" ships; Lemma 1 is proved by a loop invariant and induction, where the absence of a free ship would mean a cyclic dependency contradicting realizability (pp. 13–14), and Corollary 1 gives a unique earliest-arrival solution (p. 14). Algorithm 2 is label-setting with interval labels, dominance by interval inclusion and priority by earliest arrival; Algorithm 3 splits labels by forbidden windows (pp. 16, 20). The network has α, β and γ arcs per siding and direction and no fixed waiting positions; candidate waiting positions are tested with step h_i from the siding's end until a label interval no longer shrinks, which "often reduces the number of constructed labels drastically" (pp. 17, 19). The running time is polynomial in the number of windows per arc (at most 2n), the number of segments and the siding-length ratio (p. 21); Corollary 2 covers the knapsack relaxation (p. 21). Lemma 2 uses a cyclic waiting pattern: in every optimum each ship waits only a small ε, while under any sequential order the first ship routed does not wait and makes another wait a much longer K (pp. 21–22). The local search uses myopic benefit/loss estimates, worsening steps and a taboo list (pp. 23–24), and the surrogate objective Σ_i (a_i w_i)^f with priorities a_i and f > 1 (p. 24). Times are exact to the second (p. 25).
  - Transferable: before engineering a constructive heuristic class, look for a small cyclic instance showing that it can be arbitrarily bad in any order (pp. 21–22).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Java on "a commodity desktop PC running Linux"; quality is measured as average waiting time per ship, and a ship that cannot be routed is disregarded and charged 2 hours (p. 25). A time-discretized model (TS-OPT, one-minute steps) was tried and dropped: "computation times were exorbitant", with a loss in accuracy, and no numbers are given (p. 25). Components on all 365 days: sequential routing (SeqR), local search over the whole day (LS-240), the rolling horizon (RH-1) and RH-3, compared as box plots of instance-wise ratios (pp. 26–27); Table 1 gives the ships without a route (RH-1 1.05 per day on average, maximum 6; SeqR 1.26, maximum 9) and CPU minutes (RH-1 1.34 on average, maximum 19.65; LS-240 7.97) (p. 27). Bounds: MIP (1)–(8) with CPLEX 12.3 on 21 sub-instances of at most 35 ships over 2 hours, built by biased random removal (p. 28); the average gap between LB/MIP and relaxRH-1 is about 5 minutes (maximum 18), and below 7 minutes (maximum below 22) when the siding conditions are respected, two instances excluded (p. 28); by the CPU-minute labels of Fig. 15, CPLEX hit the memory limit on 4 of the 21 (my parse, p. 28). Practice: the GPS itineraries are turned into precedences and re-realized under the model's rules (GPS-re) (p. 29); waiting is down "by some 25% on average" (p. 29), and days that were harder in reality are harder for the heuristic too (p. 30).
