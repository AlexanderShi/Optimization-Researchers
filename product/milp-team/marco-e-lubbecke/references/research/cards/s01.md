### S083 · Algorithmen zur Enumeration der Ecken und Facetten konvexer Polyeder (Diplomarbeit, TU Braunschweig 1996, no DOI)
**Read level**: partial. Read: title page, declaration and the German and English abstracts (pp. 1-3); contents (pp. 5-6); introduction and acknowledgements (pp. 7-8); the opening of Ch. 2 with assumptions, classification and complexity notions, and the double-description outline (pp. 21-25); the opening of reverse search (p. 35); its complexity theorem (p. 47); the partial-reverse-search remark (p. 52); the RVP hardness proof (pp. 56-57); Ch. 4 complete (pp. 59-72); the opening of Ch. 5 and its main results (pp. 73-76, 80-86); Ch. 6 complete (pp. 87-88); Appendix A skimmed (pp. 89-94). Not read: Ch. 1 (pp. 9-20), the rest of Ch. 2 and 3 (pp. 25-34, 36-46, 48-55), pp. 77-79, bibliography and index (pp. 95-101). The text is OCR: umlauts and symbols are often misread (the author appears as "Ltibbecke", p. 1), and tables and figures in Ch. 4 are partly garbled (pp. 67-70). First-page check: title, author Marco Lübbecke, July 1996, topic and supervision Uwe Zimmermann (p. 1). The card is in English. The thesis body is German, so my renderings of it carry no quotation marks; both quotes come from the thesis's own English abstract (p. 3).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole-authored; discussions with Michael Bussieck acknowledged, p. 8) · **Place in trajectory**: his first research text, five years before the PhD (S027, batch b01). Satz 5.10 (p. 80) and the CPLEX-based design (p. 62) return in S020 (Bussieck and Lübbecke, Computational Geometry 1998; its first page, checked for this link only: strong P-enumerability for 0/1-polytopes with a CPLEX-based implementation). PORTA, one of the codes compared here (p. 59), is the counterexample finder of S063 (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The problems are the conversions between the two representations of a polyhedron, inequalities on one side and vertices plus extreme rays on the other (p. 3). The open question is whether some algorithm is polynomial in input and output size (pp. 3, 24). The entry is two then-new methods, Avis and Fukuda's reverse search and the backtracking of Fukuda, Liebling and Margot. The aim is to work out their meaning for theory and practice: which problem sizes their implementations handle, and whether they help answer the polynomiality question on polyhedra from combinatorial optimization (p. 8).
- **D2 Core idea**: First a survey of insertion methods (double description), pivoting methods and perturbation (pp. 21-25). Then two own codes. cheers is reverse search built on a commercial LP solver through an LP-solver interface (pp. 61-62, 91). btbm is backtracking specialised to bipartite matching polytopes (p. 65). The design reason for cheers: leave data management and the pivot operation to the developers of LP software, and build on an efficient LP code instead of lrs's own multiprecision tableau code (pp. 61-62). Last, a search for polyhedron classes on which the backtracking subproblem RVP (restricted vertex of a polyhedron) is polynomial (pp. 73-74).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pointed polyhedra. Nonnegativity is assumed so that vertices exist, and boundedness comes from an added inequality sum x_i ≤ M (p. 22). Degenerate polyhedra are kept in view rather than perturbed away, because that is where the serious problems lie (p. 22). Complexity notions: polynomial in input and output (Def. 2.2), P-enumerable (Valiant, Def. 2.3), and strongly P-enumerable with output-independent polynomial space (Fukuda, Liebling, Margot, Def. 2.4) (pp. 23-24). The test families are hypercubes (simple), bipartite matching polytopes and cross polytopes (degenerate) (pp. 66-70). The special classes studied are bipartite matching polytopes, 0-1 flow polyhedra, integral 0-1 polyhedra {Ax = b, 0 ≤ x ≤ 1}, and flow and transportation polyhedra with integer capacities (pp. 74-85).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (brief): cheers 0.30 is written in ANSI C on the CPLEX Callable Library and uses Bland's rule (pp. 62-63). The library has no public pivot routine, so each pivot is forced through CPXloadbase/CPXoptimize with a refactorisation (p. 63). Forcing pivots by temporarily modifying the data failed, because CPLEX ranks numerical stability above feasibility of the current solution (p. 64). Arithmetic is in double precision, with a zero tolerance that has to be fixed arbitrarily and unavoidably affects the results (p. 65). A random right-hand-side perturbation changed the number of vertices output, so it is off by default and available for testing only (pp. 64-65, 90). The abandoned variants are documented as the program's development stages (p. 63). Proof devices: RVP's known NP-completeness (p. 3) is re-proved by restriction to directed Hamiltonian path (pp. 56-57). RVP is polynomial for bipartite matching polytopes, via maximum-cardinality matching in an auxiliary graph (Satz 5.3, p. 75). It is polynomial for 0-1 flow polyhedra via Hoffman's existence condition and max flow (Satz 5.8, p. 80). For integral 0-1 polyhedra it needs one LP feasibility check per fixing (Satz 5.10, p. 80). Feasible spanning tree extension (FSTE) is strongly NP-complete for integer-capacity flow and transportation networks; the proof uses a restriction proposed by Margot (Satz 5.12-5.13, pp. 82-85).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: porta, qrs, lrs 2.51, cheers 0.30 and btbm ran on one HP-9000/735-125 with 144 MB under a 20 CPU-hour limit, with memory aborts and time-outs marked in the tables, plus gprof profiles (pp. 59-70). On hypercubes cheers needs about 20% of lrs's time, and the difference is attributed to data handling because both enumerate the same bases (p. 66). On degenerate polytopes the pivoting codes lose catastrophically to insertion, and only btbm gives usable results (pp. 68-69).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contributions: the survey, two codes released by ftp (Table 4.8, p. 72), a measured comparison, and a nontrivial class of polyhedra with strongly P-enumerable vertex sets (Satz 5.10, which includes the 0-1 flow polyhedra; p. 88). Limits stated: with the known pivot rules reverse search is unusable on degenerate polyhedra (pp. 71, 87). Backtracking's contribution is mainly theoretical because RVP is NP-complete (p. 88). The generalisation to integer flow and transportation polyhedra fails on strict inequalities (p. 88). The speedup is only a factor, not an order of magnitude (p. 71), and the examples are limited (p. 70). Future work: switch cheers to the lexicographic rule (p. 71), and search for classes by polyhedron properties rather than by problem (p. 88). A partial reverse search for 0-1 IPs is proposed but untested, with the caution that branch-and-bound's lead may come from research and good implementations (p. 52).

**Method links**:
- Method 3, evidence ✅ (sole voice, 1996): five codes run on one machine under one limit, with failures marked (p. 66). A speedup is attributed by holding the enumerated bases fixed (p. 66), and profiles come before explanations (pp. 66-69). The claim is sized: a factor, not an order of magnitude (p. 71). A simple family where the method can show and degenerate families where it fails (pp. 66-70) follow the logic of step 1.
- Method 1, evidence ✅ (step 5 in 1996 form): build on an efficient LP code rather than writing data management and pivoting yourself (p. 62), and document what that choice costs (pp. 63-65).
- Method 2, variant ⚠: exit B without a computation-led conjecture. Positive results are proved for restricted classes (Satz 5.3, 5.8, 5.10) and hardness for the generalisation (Satz 5.12-5.13), with the general question left open (pp. 73, 88).
- Method 4, variant ⚠: the thesis opens with a survey of the published methods before testing them (pp. 3, 21). The survey does not start from an effect in his own runs.
- Taste G2 ✅: the failed development stages are written down (p. 63) and the codes are released (p. 72).

**Transferable techniques**:
- To attribute a speedup, compare two codes that enumerate exactly the same objects; what remains is implementation (p. 66).
- Mark memory aborts and time-outs with distinct symbols in every table instead of dropping the instance (p. 66).
- Profile where the time goes (gprof) before explaining it (pp. 66-69).
- When a research code sits on a commercial solver's library, document what the interface does not allow (no public pivot routine; stability ranked above feasibility) (pp. 63-65).
- If a perturbation changes the counted result, ship it off by default and keep it for testing (pp. 64-65, 90).

**Quotes**:
- "Despite having nice theoretic properties, the reverse search technique shows to be ill suited when the polyhedra are degenerate." (p. 3)
- "Unfortunately, the search for such special cases is succesful only for very restricted problem classes." (p. 3)

### S037 · Optimal scrap combination for steel production (OR Spectrum 20(4) 1998, DOI 10.1007/s002910050072)
**Read level**: full. All 8 pages read. The model's formulas are garbled in the extraction (pp. 3-6), so the constraints are read from the prose; Tables 1-2 were parsed row by row (pp. 6-7). First-page check: title, and M.E. Lübbecke (OCR "Lub¨ becke") among the four authors (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: K.-P. Bernatzki (Hüttenwerke Krupp Mannesmann), M.R. Bussieck and T. Lindner (corresponding author), the last two at TU Braunschweig; Lübbecke is fourth of four (p. 1). The detailed model is in Lindner's 1997 master's thesis (ref. [4], p. 8) · **Place in trajectory**: his first industrial MIP paper, two years after the Diplom thesis, on the same CPLEX and HP-9000 setting (p. 5; S083 p. 66).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The entry is practice at the HKM steel works: about 60 heats a day, more than 1,500 steel types, and scrap for cooling with limits on tramp elements (p. 1). The current rule picks one of eight predetermined scrap combinations per steel type and ignores scrap supply and transport. So the dispatcher often has to deviate, relying on experience and paying more (pp. 2-3). The problem: a minimum-cost combination of scrap classes and iron ore for the next 5-8 heats under cooling, material and transport constraints, solved within 20 seconds (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea**: A compact MIP with continuous amounts of scrap, ore and extra hot metal and binary indicators for cars and tracks; a car is paid in full even if partly used (pp. 3-5). The pure formulation "can not be solved in the desired time of 20 seconds" (p. 5), so the model is strengthened, not decomposed. The strengthening uses variable fixing and elimination from the track structure, and coefficient reduction: a 600 t track bound is cut to 400 t because a train takes at most eight cars. It adds cutting planes on the fifth and third cars of single-class tracks, and branching priorities on the first-car indicators, branching down first (pp. 5-6). Kept: CPLEX's LP-based branch-and-bound (p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: A mixed binary program. On single-class tracks cars come from the front only, and a train takes at most eight cars from at most three tracks (p. 3). The model covers a single train and ignores transport cost (pp. 3, 7). All scrap classes are assumed to cool equally "(at the moment)" (p. 1). The data are 17 real instances, the pure model having 229-534 constraints and 481-1,299 variables (Table 1, p. 6).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (brief): CPLEX 4.0.7 on an HP-9000/735-125. Perl scripts turn the plant data into CPLEX input, and a CPLEX/LEDA tool (OSCAR) displays the train and the yard (p. 5). There is no correctness argument beyond the validity of each reduction and cut (pp. 5-6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Tables 1-2 (pp. 6-7), my tally. Without the acceleration techniques 6 of 17 instances exceed 20 s, the worst taking 12,468.31 s. With them all 17 finish within 18.94 s; instance 17 drops to 11.30 s, the "factor 1000" (p. 6). Three instances (1, 5, 12) become slightly slower, which the text does not mention. Against the HKM rule the savings are 0.8%-10.1%, about 5% on average (p. 6); my recomputation gives 0.76%-10.07%, mean 4.91%. There is no per-technique ablation and one run per instance.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an applied model and tool that HKM intends to install (p. 7). Limits: the single-train model is greedy. Multi-train models are sketched, but disregarded in practice because disturbances destroy long plans (p. 7). The data are estimates, so the program must always deliver a solution, and small input changes give unstable solutions; reliability therefore wins over optimality, and the result is a proposal only, with what-if parameters (pp. 7-8). The 5% saving is the pure model's and will shrink (p. 8). Future work: crane movements, train assembly and simulation use (p. 7).

**Method links**:
- Method 1, evidence ✅ (group, 1998): a compact MIP on a current solver with the full data-to-solution pipeline, from perl to CPLEX to the OSCAR display (p. 5). It is strengthened until it meets the 20 s limit, with no decomposition (pp. 5-6): Method 1's stop rule (stay with branch-and-cut) in practice.
- Method 3, evidence ✅ and variant ⚠: step 6 holds, since the savings are labelled the "pure model"'s and expected to shrink (p. 8). The baseline, though, is the plant rule, which ignores transport constraints (p. 2), and it is not re-run under identical constraints. The acceleration is shown as one before/after pair without ablation, and three slowdowns go unmentioned (Table 1, p. 6; my tally).
- H3 and Workflow A, evidence ✅: current practice (the rule, the dispatcher, the information system) is described before the model (pp. 2-3).
- New-pattern candidate: *design for planner acceptance*, the name used in b01 for S027: reliability over optimality, a proposal rather than a decision, and freedom for what-if analysis (pp. 7-8).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Cut a big-M coefficient with a count bound implied by another constraint: eight cars of 50 t give 400 instead of 600 (pp. 5-6).
- When a precedence structure makes taking the first car of a track equivalent to using the track, merge the two binaries (p. 5).
- Give branching priority to the structural binaries and test the branching direction; down first was much better here (p. 6).
- Report savings against the practice rule as an upper bound for the deployed system, i.e. the pure model's (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "Consequently, the conflict between optimality and robustness of solutions should rather be decided in favour of reliability, and implemented in the system accordingly." (p. 7)
- "Clearly, the implementation of reliability and stability will reduce the savings of about five per cent, calculated for our pure model." (p. 8)

### S063 · Yet another note on "An efficient zero-one formulation of the multilevel lot-sizing problem" (Technical report, Dept. of Mathematical Optimization, TU Braunschweig 1998, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 3 pages read. First-page check: title, and Marco E. Lübbecke (OCR "Lub¨ becke") as third author (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Michael R. Bussieck (Mathematical Optimization) and Andreas Fink (Business Administration), both TU Braunschweig (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a refutation note from his research-assistant years. His bio there says he was then "mainly involved in locomotive scheduling" (p. 3); the Computational Geometry publication the bio mentions (p. 3) matches S020 in the index. The counterexamples came from PORTA, which his Diplom thesis had compared (S083 p. 59).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: An error chain. McKnew, Saydam and Coleman claimed that the LP of their 0-1 formulation is integral, which would make the problem polynomially solvable. Rajagopalan showed that the total-unimodularity argument fails, but his counterexample contains an error. Kuik and Salomon's counterexample needs negative holding costs (pp. 1-2). Open, then: does the approach solve practically relevant instances, with non-negative costs, by LP (p. 2)?
- **D2 Core idea**: Compute the polyhedron of a tiny instance with PORTA: for n = 2 and T = 4, about 10% of the vertices are fractional (p. 1). Then construct a counterexample with non-negative costs: four items, eight periods and a demand of 300 per period for item 4. There the LP optimum, 18,756.5, lies strictly below the binary optimum, 18,808 (digit separators lost in the extraction; p. 2).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Uncapacitated multilevel lot sizing in assembly systems, with instantaneous production, zero lead times and T periods. The formulation has O(nT²) inequalities and variables, as corrected by Domschke, Scholl and Voß (p. 1). Setup and holding costs are non-negative (p. 2).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (brief): the device is a counterexample with a strictly positive LP-IP gap, found by enumerating the vertices of a small instance (pp. 1-2). The note refutes only that this approach is polynomial, not anything about the problem's complexity (p. 2).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a. There is one example; the model in GAMS and AMPL and the PORTA files are posted by ftp (p. 2).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Contribution: the McKnew et al. approach is shown not to be polynomial (p. 2). Limit stated: the problem's complexity status "remains unsettled". A literature review and inquiries to researchers (Pochet; Cornuéjols, about an erroneous report) found neither a polynomial algorithm nor an NP-completeness proof (p. 2; refs [4], [9], p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 2, evidence ✅ (group, 1998): enumerate the complete vertex set of an instance small enough to allow it, read the map (a tenth of the vertices fractional), then build the counterexample (pp. 1-2). This is the earliest practice of Method 2's small enumeration in his record.
- Taste G1, evidence ✅: a claimed polynomial algorithm is checked by computation, and both the claim and its published refutation are audited (p. 1).
- Taste G2 ✅: the model and the PORTA files are public (p. 2).
- H10, variant ⚠: two named experts are asked by private communication, but to settle a status question, not to referee (p. 2; refs p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: *audit the refutation too* (pp. 1-2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before trusting an integrality claim, enumerate the vertices of the smallest instance with a polyhedral code (PORTA) and count the fractional ones (p. 1).
- Audit the refutation as well: is the published counterexample valid, and does it need unrealistic data such as negative holding costs (pp. 1-2)?
- Post the counterexample in two modelling languages together with the polyhedral input files (p. 2).
- Before declaring a status open, ask the field's experts and cite the private communications (p. 2; refs p. 3).

**Quotes**:
- "We show by means of an example that solving the linear program is not sufficient." (p. 1)
- "Thus, the computational complexity status of the problem remains unsettled." (p. 2)

### S067 · Discrete optimization in rail transport (Extended abstract, TU Braunschweig, Mathematische Optimierung 1998, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 7 pages read; pp. 5-7 are mostly references, whose numbering is partly lost in the extraction. First-page check: title, and M. E. Lubbecke among four authors, dated May 22, 1998 (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: M. R. Bussieck, T. Winter and U. T. Zimmermann; the names are in alphabetical order (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: an overview from the Braunschweig group. Its locomotive-scheduling paragraph (pp. 4-5) announces his PhD topic (refs [22], [23] by Lübbecke and Zimmermann, p. 6).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Passenger rail grows while freight moves to trucks; private in-plant railroads with heavy internal freight remain an economic case (p. 1). Computers now supply enough data for discrete optimization models, and the text collects references on mathematical programming in rail planning over the last 5-10 years (pp. 1-2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Lay out the planning tasks as a hierarchy: OD matrix, line planning, train schedule, rolling stock and crews, and real time (pp. 2-4). Name what the hierarchy buys (subproblems of manageable size, strategic, tactical and operational horizons) and what it costs (no overall optimum) (p. 4). Then point to freight problems with no counterpart in passenger planning: blocking, train makeup, empty cars, and locomotive scheduling without a timetable (pp. 4-5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Locomotive scheduling at industrial freight railroads. Requests arrive on demand and are known only hours ahead; each has origin and destination tracks, a time window, a load size and a set of admissible locomotives. Each locomotive gets one schedule per shift, each request is served within its window by exactly one engine within its tractive power, and total time or distance is minimised (pp. 4-5).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: n/a (an overview, with no algorithm or proof).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a short reference overview with the group's projects placed in it (pp. 2-5). Its scope leaves out pricing, seat allocation and most freight issues (p. 2). There is no limits or future-work section beyond pointers to ongoing projects (pp. 3, 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 4, variant ⚠: synthesis writing used to place ongoing projects in a planning hierarchy, not to collect explanations of an effect in his own runs (pp. 2-5). Four authors wrote it, so no sentence is his alone.
- New-pattern candidate: *enter through an assumption the literature hides*. The locomotive-scheduling literature assumed a train schedule, which industrial freight does not have (p. 4); compare S066 pp. 1-2.

**Transferable techniques**:
- State a sub-field's gap as a hidden assumption of its literature, here the existence of a timetable (p. 4).
- Put what a hierarchical decomposition costs (no overall optimum) next to what it buys (manageable subproblems, planning horizons) (p. 4).

**Quotes**:
- "Only few attention has been given in the literature to this issue, and if any, only trains subject to a train schedule were considered" (p. 4)
- "The disadvantages of the hierarchical planning are obvious, since the optimal output of a subtask which serves as the input of a subsequent task, will not result, in general, in an overall optimal solution." (p. 4)

### S054 · Computer aided scheduling of switching engines (Mathematics—Key Technology for the Future, Springer 2003, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-55753-8_52)
**Read level**: full. All 14 pages read. Figures 2-6 are garbled images in the extraction, so their captions and the prose describing them were read instead (pp. 4-5, 7, 10, 12). The file is a manuscript (it cites the thesis as "2000. In preparation", p. 13), not the typeset chapter. First-page check: title, and Marco E. Lübbecke (OCR "Liibbecke") as corresponding author with Uwe T. Zimmermann (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Uwe T. Zimmermann, his PhD advisor (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: the BMBF project report on his PhD project (grant 03-ZI7BR2-1, p. 1), written while the thesis (S027, batch b01) was in preparation (p. 13). Its companion is the Transportation Science paper of 2003 (S066 ref. [19], p. 12).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Deregulation forced private railroads to use their engines better (p. 1). Dispatching is manual; in peaks requests go "loudest-shout first-serve", and a human can judge the dependencies only locally (p. 2). The project wants a computer tool that suggests a schedule (p. 2). The paper's stated purpose is to sketch the steps of a joint university-industry project, from problem identification to installation (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: The natural MIP, with explicit decisions on assignments, sequences, service times and tonnage, "turned out to be highly symmetric" (p. 3). So the authors solve a set partitioning model over feasible request subsets per engine by column generation (pp. 3-6). Pricing is NP-complete (Theorem 2, p. 6). It is done by a dynamic program that concatenates whole request configurations instead of single locations, pruning by dominance and by a dual-variable lower bound (2): a partial tour is discarded when its best possible extension cannot beat min{0, incumbent} (pp. 7-8). Integer solutions would need branch-and-price, but in their experiments the root restricted master programs "happen to be already integer feasible" (p. 8).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Each request has an origin, a destination, service times, time windows, tonnage and a list of eligible engines. Tractive power must never be exceeded, the engines are assumed independent, and requests are served in simple configurations (pp. 2-4). The Engine Scheduling Problem (ESP) is NP-hard (Lemma 1, p. 3). The practical data had to be post-processed by hand and are scarce (p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (brief): the paper lists the open choices in the generic scheme: initial solution, exact or approximate pricing, pricing rule, columns per iteration, column pool, master size and termination (p. 9). Its answer: solve pricing exactly only when the heuristics fail, keep a column pool and add several columns per iteration (p. 9). There is no proof beyond the cited complexity results (pp. 3, 6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Because data are scarce, only trends are reported (p. 9). More than forty requests on six engines give an integer solution in a few minutes on a PC, with rarely more than 20,000 columns and fewer than 200 iterations. The optimality gap is under ten minutes, against an optimum of around half an hour of waiting and empty running (p. 9). One instance shows the effect of implementation details (Fig. 5, p. 10). The generic scheme ends in a memory-allocation failure; completing exact pricing only when the heuristics fail makes the instance solvable; and the bound (2) plus a heuristic network reduction speed it up drastically. The garbled figure labels include "13.000 CPU Sec." and "30 CPU Sec.". The same paragraph asks for "a few seconds" for interactive use and reports "a few minutes" for the forty-request case (p. 9).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is an academic prototype plus lessons from the project (pp. 9-11). The lessons: get practitioner feedback at every stage, and leave constraints out on purpose for a quick first prototype. The manager is not the user. Explain why the solution looks different, because "When it does not feel right, it is not right." Treat stability and reliability as what quality means in practice: compute a heuristic solution first and improve it silently, and never overrule manual fixes (pp. 10-11). Limits: productivity may drop at first after the introduction (p. 11), and engines are assumed independent, so precedence across tours is not handled by the set partitioning model (p. 12). Future work: a project on freight-car management (pp. 12-13).

**Method links**:
- Method 1, variant ⚠: the switch from the natural MIP to set partitioning rests on a symmetry argument with a citation (p. 3) and on the LP-bound remark "(SP) performs very well in this respect" (p. 8). No compact-model run is reported here.
- Method 3, evidence ✅ (step 6) and variant ⚠: the claim is sized to the data ("encouraging" trends, pp. 1, 9). But there is no instance table and no baseline, not even the dispatchers' plans, and the effect of components is shown on a single instance (p. 10).
- Method 2, evidence ✅ (exit B, label the reading as a reading): integral root masters are reported with the remark that this is "no mathematically satisfactory explanation" (p. 8).

### S057 · MaxFlow-MinCut duality for a paint shop problem (Operations Research Proceedings 2002, Springer 2003, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-55537-4_61)
**Read level**: full. All 6 pages read. Formulas and the figure matrices are partly garbled (pp. 2-6), and the theorems are stated without proofs in this text. First-page check: title, and Marco E. Lübbecke (OCR "Liibbecke", TU Braunschweig) as third author (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Thomas Epping and Winfried Hochstättler (BTU Cottbus) (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: a combinatorics side project from the Braunschweig years, building on Epping, Hochstättler and Oertel's complexity paper [2] (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: In car manufacturing the paint shop should make as few color changes as possible along a given sequence of car bodies. The sequence is taken as fixed because reordering it would hurt the later shops, and since bodies and colors can now be decoupled the task becomes a new combinatorial problem (p. 1). The formal problem is PPW; it is NP-complete already with two colors or two letters (Theorem 1, from [2], p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: For 1-regular instances with two colors, PPW is recast as choosing a minimum set of points that hits every interval an odd number of times (Problem 2, p. 3). The interval matrix has the consecutive-ones property and is therefore totally unimodular (p. 3). Per-interval lower bounds come from maximum weighted stable sets in interval graphs (p. 4). Next comes a pair of dual LPs (pp. 4-5), and then a binary-matroid version, a shortest circuit through a fixed element, to which Seymour's MaxFlow-MinCut theorem applies. Without an F7-minor containing that element, the maximum disjoint odd row-sum packing equals the minimum number of color changes (Theorems 5-6, pp. 5-6).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: 1-regular instances, in which each letter occurs exactly once in each of two colors (p. 3). The complexity of this restriction is unknown (p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (brief): devices are the problem reduction, consecutive ones giving total unimodularity, and stable-set bounds rounded up to the next odd integer (pp. 3-4). Further devices: a shift of the feasible set between two IPs (Theorem 3, p. 4), Seymour's theorem (p. 6), and one example with and one without an F7-minor (Fig. 4, p. 6). Proofs are omitted.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: a "rough statistic". The share of instances whose bound is not tight grows from under 5% at alphabet size 5 to over 90% at 25, and Conjecture 2 rests on "recent computational experiments" (p. 5). Neither instance counts nor a generator are given.
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a set of structural duality results for a restricted class. Still open: polynomial solvability of the 1-regular two-color case (p. 3), Conjecture 1 for k > 1 (p. 2) and Conjecture 2 (p. 5). The "algorithmic approaches" of the abstract are bounds and LPs; no algorithm is evaluated (pp. 1, 4-5).

**Method links**:
- Method 2, evidence ✅ (exit B; co-authored, Lübbecke third): the pattern is proved for one class (Conjecture 1 for k = 1, Theorem 2; the duality where no F7-minor exists, Theorem 6), and what computation suggests is published as a conjecture (Conjecture 2) (pp. 2, 5-6).
- Method 3, variant ⚠: the computational evidence is a statistic without counts (p. 5), the kind his later taste warns against (B2), although it is labelled as rough.
- Taste G4 ✅: the instance class is solved through structure, namely consecutive ones, total unimodularity and matroid minors (pp. 3-6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Recast a coloring problem on a word as an interval problem and look for consecutive ones (p. 3).
- Bound each interval recursively over the intervals it contains, via max-weight stable sets, and round up to the right parity (p. 4).
- When an LP duality is not tight, move to the matroid formulation and test for the excluded minor (Seymour) (pp. 5-6).
- Give one example on each side of the dividing condition, with and without the F7-minor (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "We do not know whether PPW is polynomially solvable when restricted to such instances, but we give some indications that might support such a conjecture." (p. 3)
- "Due to recent computational experiments we conjecture the following." (p. 5)

### S066 · Combinatorially simple pickup and delivery paths (Central European Journal of Operations Research 12, 2004, no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 12 pages read. In Lemmas 2-6 and Prop. 2 the formula layout is split across lines (pp. 4-9), so those proofs were read in outline. The file is the submitted manuscript dated September 9, 2003 (p. 1). First-page check: title, and Marco E. Lübbecke (TU Berlin) as sole author (p. 1).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole-authored; thanks to Thomas Lindner, Tom Matsui, Hannes Scheel and the referee of the earlier version in his thesis [17], pp. 10-12) · **Place in trajectory**: theory spun off the PhD thesis (ref. [17], p. 12; the applications are discussed "in more detail in [17]", p. 3). The work was done in Braunschweig and written at TU Berlin (pp. 1, 10).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Many PDP experiments use instances that allow only restricted solution structures: temporal constraints in dial-a-ride, mini-clusters, small capacity-to-weight ratios, or simplicity required explicitly (pp. 1-2). The aim is to show that well-defined restricted paths have theoretical, possibly algorithmic benefits (p. 2). The setting is a single vehicle without time windows, a subproblem of richer variants (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: Paths are built only from patterns that serve one request or two requests in one of two interleavings (P1, P2). The resulting problem is close to the TSP, with the choice of patterns added (pp. 2-3). The paper counts the restricted paths against all PD paths and against paths with capacity 2, bounds the length cost of the restriction, and finds polynomial special cases (pp. 4-10). Patterns are selected while the concatenations are built, not enumerated beforehand as in crew pairing or cluster-first heuristics (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Pairing, precedence and capacity constraints (Def. 1, p. 2); the problem is strongly NP-hard (Lemma 1, p. 2). The counting assumes unit loads and capacity L = 2 (p. 4). The special case uses Balas's position restriction (4), with a given order and an integer k (p. 8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (brief): Lemma 2 gives closed counts by combinatorial counting, dividing by i! for symmetry. Lemma 3 gives a Fibonacci-like recursion proved by induction, with the odd case omitted (pp. 4-5). The ratios to all PD paths and to L = 2 paths tend to zero (Lemmas 4-6, pp. 5-6). The factor-3 bound comes from serving the two holds of an optimal L = 2 path one item at a time, and it is tight (Lemma 7, p. 6). If pattern order is irrelevant, a perfect matching solves the problem (Prop. 1, p. 7). Balas's layered network is expanded with copies for the P2 patterns (Prop. 2, Cor. 1, pp. 8-10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a; there are no computations, and the concept "is successfully applied in [19]" (p. 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is theory that prices a restriction both ways, by the size of the solution space and by the worst-case length (p. 10). Limits stated: the approximation is no better than a TSP approximation (p. 6), the counting holds only for unit loads and L = 2 (p. 4), and time windows are left out apart from a remark (pp. 2-3). No future work is named (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 3, evidence ✅ (sole voice, theory variant): the first ratio is dismissed as unfair and replaced by a comparison with "an almost identically constrained vehicle" (p. 5); the guarantee is reported together with its weakness (p. 6).
- Method 2, variant ⚠: the size of the option space is derived analytically, not by enumeration (pp. 4-6).
- Taste G1 ✅: what the restriction costs comes with a proven, tight factor (Lemma 7, p. 6).
- H4, variant ⚠: a modelling restriction from his own engine-scheduling work (the configurations of S054 Fig. 2, p. 4; here refs [18, 19], p. 3) becomes a theory paper. The step was not weak; its cost was simply unknown.
- New-pattern candidates: *restrict to the structure practice uses, then price the restriction*, the name used in b01 for S027 (pp. 1-6), and *enter through an assumption the literature hides* (pp. 1-2).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Compare a restricted solution space with the closest fair alternative (the same capacity), not with the unrestricted space (p. 5).
- Price a restriction twice: by counting the search space and by a worst-case length factor with a tight example (pp. 4-6).
- Look for polynomial special cases by dropping the TSP part (pattern selection by matching) and by restricting positions (Balas) (pp. 7-8).

**Quotes**:
- "The aim of this paper is to demonstrate that well-defined restricted pickup and delivery paths also have theoretical, possibly algorithmically useful benefits." (p. 2)
- "Already for very small n the ratio is almost zero, but this was to be expected: The comparison is not fair." (p. 5)

### S033 · Dual variable based fathoming in dynamic programs for column generation (European Journal of Operational Research 162(1) 2005, DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2003.05.006)
**Read level**: full. Version read: TU Berlin, Institut für Mathematik, report No. 2003/42, dated August 19, 2003 (pp. 1-2), not the typeset EJOR text. File p. 1 is the report cover and pp. 2-5 are the note; the report-series list on pp. 6-8 was skimmed. First-page check: title, and Marco E. Lübbecke as sole author (pp. 1-2).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: none (sole-authored; BMBF grants 03-ZI7BR2-1 and 03-ZIM2BS; done at Braunschweig, p. 5) · **Place in trajectory**: the generic note distilled from the engine-scheduling pricing. The bound first appears as (2) in S054 (pp. 7-8), and the experiments are those of the Transportation Science paper [9] (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Dynamic programs serve as column generators for the LP relaxations of set partitioning problems; the goal is to shrink their state space (p. 2). The note says outright that it is "rediscovering the fathoming technique in dynamic programming" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: For a state (R, k), LB = c(R, k) minus the sum of the positive duals of elements not yet in R. Fathom the state when LB ≥ min{0, incumbent} (p. 3). Refinements restrict the set of positive-dual elements, using a maximum set size, incompatible pairs or a known completion cost such as the return to the depot (pp. 3-4). Scaling the bound by γ in (0, 1] gives a heuristic with relative error at most 1 − γ (Lemma 1, p. 4). A preprocessing variant discards an element p when u_p < 0 and the sum of the positive duals plus u_p is at most 0 (p. 4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: min cᵀx, Ax = 1, x ≥ 0, with binary A and c ≥ 0, and costs additive along DP transitions (pp. 2-3). The bound stays valid with convexity constraints but fails once non-binary columns are allowed (p. 4).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design** (brief): the device is to relax the extension constraints completely to obtain a cheap bound, then add structure back (p. 3). Lemma 1 is proved by comparing the incumbent with γ·LB (p. 4). The test is local: it needs only the current state, whereas dominance needs a global overview of the states (p. 4).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: one sentence: a state-space reduction of more than 90% in the locomotive-scheduling application [9]. The note gives no table and no instance count (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: The contribution is a generic, locally checkable fathoming rule plus a heuristic variant with a guarantee (pp. 3-4). Limits: in general the bound "is possibly weak" (p. 3). Stronger refinements cost effort that has to be weighed against the gain, and the bound is invalid for non-binary columns (p. 4). Heuristic pricing is "not followed up here" (p. 3). An independent similar bound (Mehrotra and Trick) is acknowledged (p. 4).

**Method links**:
- Method 3, evidence ✅ (step 6, sole voice): the claim is modest, "a simple generic lower bounding criterion" that "is possibly weak" (pp. 2-3). Variant ⚠: the one performance figure (more than 90%) has no instance set or table behind it in the note (p. 4).
- H1, evidence ✅: exact pricing is needed "at least when one wishes to prove optimality of the RMP" (p. 3), and the γ rule turns the exact DP into a heuristic with a known error (p. 4).
- Taste G1 ✅: a heuristic shipped with a proven bound (Lemma 1, p. 4).
- H4, evidence ✅: a component of his own application code becomes a generic note (S054 pp. 7-8, here pp. 3-4).
- New-pattern candidate: *bounding carried from branch-and-bound into the pricing DP*, as named in b01 (pp. 2-4).

**Transferable techniques**:
- In a labelling or DP pricer, fathom a state when its reduced cost minus all positive duals it can still collect is at least min{0, incumbent} (p. 3).
- Strengthen the bound with structure known from the application: maximal route size, incompatible pairs, return cost (pp. 3-4).
- Turn an exact pricer into a heuristic with a known guarantee by scaling the bound by γ (relative error ≤ 1 − γ) (p. 4).
- Preprocess: drop elements whose negative dual cannot be made up by all positive duals (p. 4).
- When an idea has precedents, say "rediscovering" and cite the independent similar result (pp. 2, 4).

**Quotes**:
- "This note provides a simple means to reduce the state space of these algorithms, rediscovering the fathoming technique in dynamic programming" (p. 2)
- "Computational experiments with (2), conducted in the context of a locomotive scheduling problem at in-plant railroads [9], indicate considerable state space reductions of more than 90%." (p. 4)
