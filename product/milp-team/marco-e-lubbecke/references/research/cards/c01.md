### S020 · The vertex set of a 0/1-polytope is strongly P-enumerable (Computational Geometry 11(2) 1998, DOI 10.1016/s0925-7721(98)00021-2)
**Read level**: full. All nine file pages read: definitions, Algorithms 1-2, Theorems 2-3, Corollaries 4-6, the conclusions and the ten references (pp. 8-9). An author-typeset copy whose nine file pages do not follow the journal's pp. 103-109 (not mapped; p. 1 footer). OCR text layer: prose, theorem statements and proofs are legible; some formulas are garbled (pp. 3-7) and were read from their surrounding text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michael R. Bussieck (first author); both at the Department of Mathematical Optimization, TU Braunschweig, Lübbecke the corresponding author (p. 1) · **Place in trajectory**: his first journal paper (research note 06, 1998 row; not stated in this text). It generalizes a result of his own 1996 Master's thesis on enumerating all vertices and facets of convex polyhedra (ref. [7], cited on pp. 5 and 8), three years before the PhD on column generation.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: An open complexity question, not an application: whether all vertices of a polyhedron given by inequalities can be listed in time polynomial in input and output size is "still an open question" (abstract, p. 1; pp. 2-3). The entry is the stricter notion of strong P-enumerability (linear in the output, space independent of it), from Valiant and from Fukuda, Liebling and Margot (pp. 1-2; Definition 1, p. 2), and a class on which the known tools are weak: the backtracking of Fukuda, Liebling and Margot needs an NP-complete decision per output, Provan's network-LP algorithms are quadratic in the output, and reverse search covers only simple polytopes (p. 3).
  - Transferable: open a complexity note by placing the class you can settle between the general open question and the special cases already settled, each with the precise reason the known method fails on your class (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: If every vertex of P lies among the vertices of a "squashed cube" P^□ (a polytope combinatorially equivalent to the hypercube, given by n pairs of opposite facets), each vertex corresponds to one choice of a facet per pair; Algorithm 1 builds these choices in a binary tree and keeps a branch alive only if one LP says that P meets the chosen facets (pp. 3-4). Against the backtracking it builds on, the node test changes from an NP-complete decision to an LP feasibility check, which the cube structure makes sufficient (pp. 3-5). For 0/1-polytopes the unit cube is P^□, so the extra input is free (Corollary 5, p. 5). Section 3 turns the method into an algorithm on the combinatorial object itself: Algorithm 2 lists all integral 0/1 flows of a graph by fixing arcs to 0 or 1 and testing existence with a maximum-flow computation instead of an LP (pp. 6-7).
  - Transferable: turn a polyhedral enumeration into a combinatorial one by replacing each LP feasibility test with the problem's own existence test (max flow for 0/1 flows), keeping the recursion and its correctness argument (pp. 6-7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Rational polytopes in class Π, the convex hulls of vertex subsets of some P^□ (p. 3, Fig. 1), given by Ax ≤ b together with a description Lx ≤ l, Ux ≤ u of P^□, which must be supplied as redundant input because the authors cannot compute it in advance (p. 4); P^□ is assumed full-dimensional (p. 3). All 0/1-polytopes need no extra input (p. 5). The authors fence the result: it says nothing about enumerating combinatorial objects such as Hamiltonian cycles, whose input is a graph and whose polytope description is exponential or unknown (p. 5). No instance library (n/a).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Correctness (Theorem 2, pp. 4-5) by a hereditary property: P intersected with a facet of P^□ is again in Π, so one LP decides whether a vertex lies below a node, and each leaf of the binary partition tree is reached once.
  - Complexity (Theorem 3, p. 5): O(v·n·T_FP) time with at most n LPs per output vertex, and O((m+n)n) space besides indices; with a polynomial LP method this gives strong P-enumerability (Corollary 4, p. 5) and, with the unit cube, for every 0/1-polytope (Corollary 5, p. 5).
  - The 0/1-flow version: each fixing turns one bound of (3) into an equality, so Theorem 2 carries over, with O(m) max-flow existence tests per output (Corollary 6, p. 7).
  - Implementation: the program zerone on top of CPLEX, released by anonymous ftp (p. 6).
  - Transferable: when an algorithm needs a structure you cannot compute, take it as redundant input, prove the result conditional on it, and name the case in which it comes free (pp. 4-5).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Two timing examples on an HP9000/735-125 workstation: the vertices of the 20-dimensional unit cube in under twenty minutes (this copy prints 1,048,580, four more than 2^20) and the 5,040 vertices of a bipartite matching polytope on two times seven nodes in about eight minutes (p. 6). No comparison, by choice: a comparison with double description or reverse search would be "obviously unfair" (p. 6). The benefit claimed is memory independent of the output (p. 6). No test set, baseline or variability (n/a).
- **D6 Positioning**: Builds on Valiant's P-enumerability and on Fukuda's and Fukuda, Liebling and Margot's refinements (pp. 2-3); presents Corollary 4 as generalizing earlier special cases, Fukuda and Matsui's bipartite matchings and the 0/1-polytopes described by equalities of his own Master's thesis [7] (p. 5); contrasts with double description and reverse search without measuring them (p. 6); names Bremner, Fukuda and Marzetta's primal-dual facet enumeration as a possible partner (p. 8). Ten references, one his own (pp. 8-9).
- **D7 Writing**: Definitions before results, two theorems with short proofs and three corollaries (pp. 2-7), then "Conclusions and Open Problems" that restate the result and list what is not known (pp. 7-8). Hedges sit where the result stops: a construction "not known to the authors" (p. 8), a conjecture labelled as one (p. 8), a counterexample that "one easily checks in dimension three" (p. 8). One claim outruns the body: the abstract calls the CPLEX implementation "superior to general vertex enumeration algorithms" (p. 1), while the body declines every comparison (p. 6).
  - Transferable: end a theory note with the exact obstacle to the next generalization (strict inequalities; the polar leaving the class) rather than a general hope (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Theory (strong P-enumerability for class Π and all 0/1-polytopes, Corollaries 4-5, p. 5), a released program (p. 6) and a derived combinatorial enumeration (p. 7). Limits stated specifically: the result depends on the description of P^□, which the authors cannot construct efficiently (pp. 7-8); "no immediate generalization" of Algorithm 1 (p. 8); backtracking conjectured not to give more general results (p. 8); facet enumeration open, the polar being no help (p. 8). Future: combine Algorithm 1 with Bremner, Fukuda and Marzetta to list the facets of class Π (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 variant ⚠: enumeration is here the object of a theorem, not a map of design options. The bridge is the use the authors name: listing all integral vertices of a model's polytope "even when nothing is known about the 0/1-property", a frequent task when designing combinatorial models (p. 6), an instrument for checking integrality by computation [inferred link to the habit, recorded in Method 2's evidence, of letting computation suggest the theorem].
- Method 2 evidence ✅ (exit B in its theory form): proved for one class (0/1-polytopes, class Π), the general case published as an open question and a conjecture (pp. 5, 7-8).
- Method 3 evidence ✅ (step 6 for the theoretical claim): the scope is fenced by an explicit caveat on input size with a worked counterexample, Hamiltonian cycles (p. 5).
- Method 3 variant ⚠ (step 6 for the computational claim): "superior" in the abstract (p. 1) rests only on Theorem 3's memory bound (p. 5) and two timings (p. 6), against a body that declines a comparison as unfair (p. 6). Co-signed, 1998.
- Taste G2 evidence ✅: the code is released (p. 6). Taste G4 evidence ✅: Algorithm 2 works "directly on the problem rather than on the associated polytope" (p. 6).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Correctness by a hereditary property: show that every face the recursion enters stays in the class, so that one LP (or one combinatorial existence test) per node decides whether an output lies below it, and the tree visits each output once (pp. 4-5).
- Count work per output: bound the LPs per output by the recursion depth (here n) to get time linear in the output and memory independent of it (p. 5).
- Take a structure you cannot compute as redundant input, prove the result conditional on it, and name the important case where it is free (the unit cube for 0/1-polytopes) (pp. 4-5).
- Specialize the polyhedral algorithm to a combinatorial one by swapping the LP test for a max-flow test (pp. 6-7).
- Before trying to prove a formulation integral, enumerate the vertices of its polytope on small instances (the use named on p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "We prefer not to give comparative computational results because a comparison with more general algorithms such as the double description method or the reverse search technique is obviously unfair." (p. 6)
- "We emphasize that these statements do not measure the complexity of combinatorial optimization or enumeration problems" (p. 5)

### S024 · Engine routing and scheduling at industrial in-plant railroads (Transportation Science 37(2) 2003, DOI 10.1287/trsc.37.2.183.15251)
**Read level**: full. All 15 pages read, including Tables 1-6, the acknowledgments and the references (p. 15, checked for self-citations). The published article: file p. N = printed p. 182+N (pp. 183-197). Figures 1-2 are fragments in the text layer; only their captions and the text's descriptions were read. Table numbers whose decimal points are lost are read as stated below.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Uwe T. Zimmermann, his doctoral advisor; both at the Department of Mathematical Optimization, TU Braunschweig (p. 1); Lübbecke first author. BMBF grant 03-ZI7BR2-1; data from EKO Trans, Verkehrsbetriebe Peine-Salzgitter and Marc Sol; Jacques Desrosiers thanked for his "careful reading of earlier drafts" and for guidance that led "to a more focused presentation"; received January 2002, accepted February 2002 (p. 15) · **Place in trajectory**: the journal paper of his 2001 thesis (Lübbecke 2001b, to which models, proofs and details are deferred on pp. 3-6 and 10); S001 lists it among the applications of integer column generation (S001 p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: An industrial planning gap: in-plant railroads of large plants dispatch switching engines first-come, first-served by the dispatcher's experience, "purely by pencil and paper", under pressure from truck competition and deregulation, with almost no OR literature since Charnes and Miller 1956 (pp. 1-2). The problem is posed as a multiple-vehicle pickup and delivery problem with time windows plus one restriction the railroad dictates: "Railroad yard managers insist that every admissible schedule for an engine must have a very simple structure" (p. 2), so engine routes are concatenations of three patterns (direct delivery, overlapping, embedding) that the authors call "part of the problem, not its simplification" (p. 2).
  - Transferable: when practitioners restrict the shape of solutions, write the restriction into the problem definition as a finite pattern set and say why it is not a relaxation device (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Two formulations: a mixed integer program (ESPMIP, Table 1, p. 4), written "In order to gather insight into the problem's structure" (p. 3), and a set-partitioning model over admissible request sets per engine (ESPSP, p. 4) that is formally its Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation, the subproblem being solved by dynamic programming because its LP bound is poor (p. 4). The LP relaxation of ESPSP is solved by column generation (p. 5); pricing is an elementary constrained shortest path over pattern concatenations, solved by "A new exact label correcting algorithm" (abstract, p. 1) on a pattern graph (pp. 5-6), with a dual-variable-based label elimination carried over from branch-and-bound bounding (pp. 6-7). Integer solutions come from price-and-branch: columns are generated at the root only, then branch-and-bound runs on them, named "by analogy with the folklore notion cut-and-branch" (pp. 8-9). New: the pricing algorithm, its pruning and the heuristics around it; kept: the established set-partitioning column generation methodology (p. 5).
  - Transferable: before writing master and pricing, write the arc-based MIP to see the structure, then derive the set-partitioning model formally as its Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation (pp. 3-4).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Requests with origin and destination tracks, loads, service times and time windows; a heterogeneous fleet with tractive efforts; a planning horizon of about two hours; patterns must respect tractive effort and must not be time-window infeasible a priori (p. 2). Strongly NP-complete even for feasibility, by reduction from the multiple TSP with time windows (p. 3); the pricing problem is strongly NP-complete by reduction from LONGEST PATH (p. 5). The implemented objective is total deadheading and waiting time, not additive in arc weights (p. 9); ESPMIP's completion-time objective "serves as a convenient example only" (p. 3). Data: vps, 100 actual requests of an 8.5-hour morning shift at a steel mill with six engines and 21 tracks, time windows taken from handwritten sheets after manual modifications approved by railroad officers, and instances vps10-vps39 formed by the first nn requests (p. 10); eko820a-c, 820 requests from a six-day log on 273 tracks with 10 engines and no time windows, so windows of 200, 250 and 300 minutes are generated around actual start times and many distances estimated: "Admittedly, the result cannot be regarded as real-life data" (pp. 10-11); eko80xa-c, generated to be extremely hard (wide windows, short distances) (p. 11); and Sol's eight classes of ten random Euclidean m-PDPTW instances, used as raw data, restricted to pattern concatenations and with windows no longer truncated (p. 11). Correctness assumptions: 0 ≤ a_i (p. 4); integer cost coefficients, with dual-based intermediate values off by about 10^-15 rounded to the nearest integer (p. 11).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Label-correcting pricing (pp. 5-6): states (R, i) of visited request set and delivery node, two-dimensional labels (time, reduced cost) kept as lists of nondominated labels; labels stored chronologically so that a time-window failure ends the treatment of a state, since "none will be feasible either" (p. 6); paths extended by whole patterns; request disjointness checked at treatment. For several columns per call, (14) is evaluated at every delivery node, which gives some diversity (p. 6).
  - Dual-variable-based label elimination (pp. 6-7): with C_inc the incumbent, LB_R = (cheapest label at state R) minus the sum of positive duals of admissible requests not in R (eq. 16), and a state is dropped as soon as LB_R ≥ min{0, C_inc} (eq. 15), "while guaranteeing that an optimal concatenation will be identified" (p. 6). The device rests on the similarity of dynamic programming and branch-and-bound as implicit enumeration (Ibaraki) and comes from Lübbecke's 1999 report (pp. 6, 15).
  - Heuristics, freely combinable (pp. 7-8): stop at the k-th negative label; recompute the cost coefficient of a found request set exactly by backtracking when |R| ≤ 4; reduce the pattern graph; treat states by out-degree or by dual value; drop states by request-set size against a preset parameter (the symbols are lost in the text layer, p. 8).
  - Column management (pp. 9-10): a column pool checked before any pricing, where pool columns of small positive reduced cost are added only in an iteration that also adds a negative one, "Otherwise, cycling could occur" (p. 9); next a greedy heuristic that ranks requests by dual value per minute of time-window slack (p. 10); next the label-correcting algorithm stopped at the 100th negative column (pp. 10-11); only when no engine yields a column are all heuristics switched off and exact pricing proves LP optimality (p. 10). Engines are priced cyclically with an RMP re-optimization after each, so that later engines see the newest duals (p. 10).
  - No branch-and-price: a "failsafe industrial implementation" would need one, "However, this is beyond the scope of this paper." (p. 9).
  - Transferable: prune the states of a pricing DP with an optimistic bound computed from the duals not yet collected, and keep the exactness argument explicit (pp. 6-7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: 700 MHz Pentium III, 1 GB, Linux 2.2, CPLEX 7.0 callable library, gcc -O3 (p. 10). Metrics: LP optimum, best integer value, gap in percent, branch-and-bound and total CPU time (p. 11), plus columns added and simplex iterations (Table 4, p. 13) and engines used against Sol's optimal number (Tables 5-6, p. 14). Ablation: Table 2 reruns all vps instances without criterion (15), capped at one CPU week (p. 7). vps10-vps36 close at 0.00% gap and vps37-vps39 at 4.34%, 4.34% and 17.39%; without (15), vps36-vps39 exceed the week; read as two-decimal values (the decimal points are lost, a reading consistent with the cap), vps35 takes 58.45 s with the criterion and 501,309.84 s without (p. 7). The text reports speedups of orders of magnitude with smaller memory, largest late in column generation (p. 11). Further findings: final RMPs integer feasible under default options, gaps worth "only minutes" in practice, seldom more than 5,000 columns (p. 11); always running exact pricing is impractical, an artificial start basis is competitive with a greedy one, and deleting columns from the RMP does not pay (pp. 11-12). Failures and limits are printed: wider time windows make eko820 much harder and short distances make the approach impractical (p. 12); for the last group of eko80x no solution is obtained at the two wider window widths (Table 4, p. 13); class D gets slower and worse (p. 13); the direct-delivery-only reduction gives a marginal speedup and worse solutions (p. 8). Heuristic rescues are reported apart: 356.0 against an LP optimum of 243.0 in 1,000 CPU s, and a 13% gap in 400 CPU s on the hardest eko80x instance (p. 12). Baselines: no competing code. Against Sol's known optima, route durations are on average at most 20% longer, under a different objective and a longer horizon that sometimes beats Sol's optimum, both stated (pp. 12-13); against practice no comparison is possible because assignments and completion times are not recorded (p. 13). Deterministic code, no seeds or permutations (n/a).
  - Transferable: when your instances relax a literature benchmark (longer horizon, another objective), say so next to the comparison and explain any result that beats the published optimum (pp. 12-13).
- **D6 Positioning**: Places the problem in the m-PDPTW literature (Savelsbergh and Sol's survey; Dumas, Desrosiers and Soumis; Sol) and separates the pattern concept from airline crew pairing (pp. 2-3); takes column generation and labelling techniques mostly from Montréal work (Desaulniers et al. 1998; Desrochers and Soumis; Desrosiers et al. 1983; Gegen et al. for elementary paths) (pp. 4-6); recommends the Desaulniers, Desrosiers and Solomon acceleration review as "hardly" to be "outmatched" and to be "definitively considered before an implementation" (p. 9). Self-citations: the 1999 fathoming report, a 2001 report on path restrictions, the thesis and a practice chapter (p. 15). No competing method is criticized.
- **D7 Writing**: The abstract promises that "All the claims are demonstrated by computational experiments on both artificial and real-life data" (p. 1). The implementation section speaks from experience ("It is our experience", p. 9). Hedges are specific: price-and-branch's success is "not being a mathematically satisfactory explanation" but an encouragement (p. 9); "Admittedly" for the synthetic time windows (p. 11). The last section is "Remarks", not a summary: what could not be compared, the bottleneck, what is not done (p. 13). One sentence outruns the tables: branch-and-price is skipped as "well justified by our always obtaining (even optimal) integer solutions" (p. 13), while Table 4 on the same page has instances without a solution (p. 13). Formal descriptions and proofs are deferred to the thesis (pp. 3-6, 10).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Modelling (two formulations), an exact pricing algorithm with dual-based pruning, pricing heuristics, a price-and-branch heuristic and a study on real and artificial data (pp. 1-2, 13). Limits stated concretely: no comparison with practice; exact pricing is the bottleneck and the method needs a small pattern family; better label data structures and network reduction not studied; "everyday computability is to be established in a next step" (p. 13). Future: stochastic engine availability and request arrival, and the online version (p. 13).

**Method links**:
- Heuristic H1 evidence ✅ (strong; co-signed practice of 2002-2003, seventeen years before the CO@Work 2020 statements): exact pricing is needed only for the optimality certificate (p. 7); several columns per round, a pool, a greedy heuristic and a truncated exact DP come before any full exact call, with the guideline to call the exact algorithm as seldom as possible (pp. 9-10); engines are priced partially and cyclically (p. 10).
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (steps 1 and 3): the compact MIP is written first, for insight (p. 3), and the extended model is derived from it by Dantzig-Wolfe (p. 4); pattern concatenations become variables because their pricing is solvable by dynamic programming while the subproblem's LP bound is poor (p. 4).

- Method 1 evidence ✅ (step 5 context): no branch-and-price; the paper names the "additional effort", justifies the omission by the observed integrality (p. 13) and names branch-and-price as the industrial safeguard (p. 9). The skill's podcast reason (he would have had to write the tree) fits "additional effort".

- Workflow A and Heuristic H3 evidence ✅: the pattern restriction comes from yard managers (p. 2), data modifications are approved by railroad officers (p. 10), the horizon is what officers consider sensible to plan (p. 13), and vehicle minimization is left out because railroad management does not favor it "(yet)" (p. 12).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Dual-bound pruning in a labelling pricing algorithm: LB_R = cheapest label at state R minus the sum of positive duals of admissible requests outside R; drop the state when LB_R ≥ min{0, incumbent} (pp. 6-7).

### S012 · A fast algorithm for near cost optimal line plans (Mathematical Methods of Operations Research 59(2) 2004, DOI 10.1007/s001860300332)
**Read level**: full. All 16 file pages read: the report cover (p. 1), the manuscript (pp. 2-12) with Algorithm 1 and Tables 1-2, Figures 2-5 (pp. 13-14), whose curves survive only as axis labels, so only their captions and the text's reading of them were used, and the report-series list (pp. 15-16). This is TU Berlin report 2003/43, manuscript of 11 November 2003 (pp. 1-2), not the typeset journal version: file p. N = preprint p. N-1; journal pp. 205-220 are not mapped. In Table 2 the mark for an optimal solution of the fixed model prints as "?" (p. 11).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Michael R. Bussieck (first author; GAMS Development Corporation) and Thomas Lindner (Siemens Transportation Systems); Lübbecke third, at TU Berlin (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: Braunschweig line-planning work begun in 1997 ("when we set out with this research in 1997", p. 11), delivering "updated material from" Bussieck's 1998 thesis (p. 3; ref. [2], p. 12); finished in his TU Berlin postdoc years, where the report list places it beside his 2003 fathoming and compact-formulation reports (p. 15).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: Cost-optimal line planning for the Dutch Railways: routes, frequencies and train lengths at minimum total cost (pp. 2-3). The entry is a gap in the exact work: Goossens et al. obtained excellent lower bounds by branch-and-cut, but "primal solutions of matching quality cannot be found in reasonable time" (p. 2), and earlier rounding and Lagrangian proposals "failed in finding good primal solutions" (p. 8). A second, methodological entry: to further the integration of nonlinear and mixed-integer optimization, "all too often regarded separately" (p. 2), by reviving the MINLP model that Claessens had dropped from her publication (p. 3). The aim is stated for practitioners: very good solutions in very short time, "this—and only this—is relevant to practitioners" (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: Each model does what it is good at. The continuous relaxation of the nonconvex MINLP, initialized from the LP solution of an integer linearization, only decides which routes are unpromising; their variables are fixed to zero in the linear model (ILP), the restricted ILP gives an incumbent within three minutes, and then the fixing is removed, valid inequalities are added and standard branch-and-bound continues from a warm start (procedure FIX, Algorithm 1, pp. 9-10). The authors call it "a very careful initialization of branch-and-bound" (p. 9). Kept: the commercial solver's branch-and-bound and Claessens et al.'s binary linearization BLP (pp. 5-6); new: a more compact frequency-based linearization ILP, a capacity-based ILP-C, problem-specific cuts and the nonlinear guidance (pp. 6-9).
  - Transferable: let the relaxation of model A (here a nonconvex MINLP) fix only zero-variables of model B, take an incumbent from the restricted B, then release the fixing and continue exactly, so that the heuristic step cannot cut off the optimum (pp. 9-10).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: A rail network G = (V, E), a predefined route set, line frequencies from a small integer interval including zero, per-edge frequency bounds, seat demand converted to coaches Λ_e for a single coach type, coach-count bounds per train, fixed and per-kilometre costs for trains and coaches; strongly NP-hard (p. 3). The MINLP has bilinear capacity terms x_r·y_r and a ceiling term for the trains needed (pp. 4-5); its simplification ⌈T_r x_r⌉ ≤ ⌈T_r⌉ x_r is exact for F = {0,1} and costs an average relative error of at most 3% on the solutions found, since almost all lines run hourly (p. 5). BLP handles holes in domains (F = {1,2,4}; no five-coach trains in Dutch rolling stock) by dropping variables (p. 6); ILP and ILP-C use general integers (pp. 6-7). Data: four Dutch Railways instances ic97, ic98, ir98 and ar98 (InterCity, InterRegio, AggloRegio; the InterRegio network has 86 vertices and 113 edges), variants of which "have been circulating in the community for years" and, except ir98, had withstood proven optimality (p. 4 caption; p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Three linearizations: BLP with one binary per route, frequency and train length (pp. 5-6); ILP with binary route-frequency and integer extra-coach variables, more compact (p. 6); ILP-C with binary route-capacity and integer frequency variables, which for F = {0,1} reduces to BLP with ϕ ≡ 1 (pp. 6-7).

Separation of (4) is NP-hard in general, so only |E'| = 1 (which dominated in the experiments), the polynomial families (5) and (7), and |R'| = 1 for (6) are checked, at the root only (cut-and-branch); ILP with these cuts is model CUT (p. 8).
  - FIX (Algorithm 1, p. 10): LP of ILP, initialize the NLP, solve the continuous NLP, fix x_{r,ϕ} = 0 where x_r^NLP ≤ 10^-5, solve the fixed ILP, drop the fixings, add (5) and then (4), (6) or (7) if violated, re-solve the LP and remove non-binding cuts, warm-start, branch-and-bound.
  - Correctness needs nothing beyond the exact final branch-and-bound; the fixing is called a heuristic (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: GAMS 20.6 with CONOPT2 and SBB for the nonlinear models and CPLEX 7.5 for the linear ones, a 700 MHz Linux PC, three hours per run (p. 9). Goossens et al.'s three-minute optimal solve of an ir98 variant is reported but not compared, their data being slightly different (pp. 10-11). Single runs, no variability (n/a).

### S001 · Selected topics in column generation (Operations Research 53(6) 2005, DOI 10.1287/opre.1050.0234)
**Read level**: full. All 35 file pages read: the report cover (p. 1), the survey (pp. 2-25), the references (pp. 26-33, checked for self-citations and for the sources of the numbers relayed) and the report-series list (pp. 34-35). TU Berlin report 2004/08, dated 2 December 2002 and revised 9 March 2004 (p. 2): not the journal version (pp. 1007-1023, not mapped) and not the October 2004 revision read for research note 01; file p. N = preprint p. N-1.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jacques Desrosiers (HEC Montréal and GERAD); Lübbecke first author, at TU Berlin (p. 2) · **Place in trajectory**: the survey that research note 01 traces to chapter 2 of his 2001 thesis (not stated in this text); the companion Primer (S002) is "based on our recent survey" (S002 p. 27); his in-plant railroad paper (S024) is one of the applications in Table 1 (p. 4).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: A literature gap, not an application: Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition and column generation, "devised for linear programs", are a success in large-scale integer programming, and the recent contributions are "not yet found in textbooks" (abstract, p. 2). The survey is "biased toward solving integer programs" and means to cover the whole integer column generation process, merging recent research with classical strategies (p. 3). It opens by correcting a misconception: "in general one cannot simply impose integrality constraints on the generated variables" (p. 3). The organising thesis is the growing understanding of the dual point of view (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea and algorithm design**: One notation from the linear decomposition principle to integer programs, then the algorithm parts in the order of the solution process: restricted master (§4), pricing (§5), tailing off (§6), integer solutions (§7) (p. 3). The dual view organises the parts: the restricted master exists to provide dual variables (p. 13); pricing is a separation problem for the dual, Kelley's cutting plane method (p. 15); the Dantzig-Wolfe bound equals the Lagrangian bound (pp. 8-9); initialization, dual-optimal inequalities, aggregation and stabilization all act on the duals (pp. 13-16, 20-22). For integer programs "essentially it all boils down to branch-and-bound" (p. 22), on the original variables of a compact formulation (p. 24). One recommendation changes the classical picture: classical Dantzig-Wolfe as a pricing scheme for large LPs is not recommended (p. 25).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: Integer programs min c^T x, Ax ≥ b, x ∈ X with X = P ∩ Z^n, possibly with nonlinear aspects, and a finite optimum (p. 8); block-diagonal D for several subproblems (p. 7); set-partitioning and covering masters (pp. 15-17, 24). Each tool comes with its condition: bound (5) needs an upper bound κ on the sum of the λ, Farley's bound needs c ≥ 0 (p. 6); discretization gives a linear integer master even for nonlinear costs when X is bounded, and for X ⊆ [0,1]^n convexification and discretization coincide (p. 11); Villeneuve et al.'s compact formulation exists "under very mild assumptions" (p. 11); early termination needs integer costs (p. 22); Walker's dual retrieval needs a linear subproblem (p. 23); Ryan-Foster needs a 0/1 matrix (p. 24); set-partitioning masters suffer massive degeneracy once columns have more than about ten nonzeros (p. 16). Applications are listed in Table 1 (p. 4); no instance library (n/a).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Bounds: z̄ + κ c̄* ≤ z* ≤ z̄ at every iteration, z̄/(1 − c̄*) for c ≡ 1, Farley's ratio bound (p. 6), and z̄ + Σ_k c̄^k* for blocks (pp. 7-8); early termination at ⌈LB⌉ = ⌈z̄⌉ and pruning at ⌈LB⌉ ≥ incumbent (pp. 22-23); Gilmore and Gomory's stall rule "is not robust against temporary stalls" (p. 23).
  - Convexification against discretization (Theorem 1, p. 10), and the counterexample (28): without branching, a variable that the unique integer optimum needs is never generated by Dantzig's rule (p. 12).
  - Restricted master: big-M initialization and the heading-in effect, warm starts, simplex against barrier, sifting, subgradient and volume algorithms, analytic centres (pp. 13-14); dual-optimal inequalities such as u_1 ≤ ... ≤ u_m for cutting-stock orders sorted by width (p. 15), with the reported speedups (p. 16); dynamic row aggregation (p. 16).
  - Pricing: any negative column helps and "an approximation suffices until the last iteration" (p. 17); partial pricing with up to 300 subproblems (p. 16); dominance and redundancy of columns, with Proposition 2 (a strictly redundant column is never optimal for the ratio pricing rule (31)) (p. 17); steepest edge, Devex, deepest cut and lambda pricing (pp. 17-18); the integrality-property trade-off (p. 18); combinatorial pricing and many columns per dynamic programming call (p. 19).
  - Tailing off and stabilization: Boxstep, Wentges's weighted Dantzig-Wolfe with proved finiteness, the trust-region method, and du Merle et al.'s penalized box (34)-(35) with its stopping criteria and update rules (pp. 19-22).
  - Integer solutions: pricing out the original variables (Walker; Poggi de Aragão and Uchoa's x ≥ ε) (p. 23); compatible branching on master variables complicates pricing to k-th best solutions (p. 23); Ryan-Foster (Proposition 3), branching and cutting on original variables, and decisions preferably imposed in the pricing problem (p. 24).
  - Transferable: compute the Lagrangian bound z̄ + κ c̄* at every iteration and, with integer costs, stop the node's column generation once ⌈LB⌉ = ⌈z̄⌉, then branch (pp. 6, 22-23).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a: a survey with no computation of its own. It relays other authors' numbers with their sources: dual multipliers constrained to a small interval around their optima accelerate column generation by a factor of 100 (Ben Amor, p. 16); dynamic aggregation cuts the RMP time by a factor of eight at 1,600 rows (Elhallaoui et al., p. 16); stabilization gives speedups of up to ten (p. 22); Hadjar et al. remove more than 90% of the flow variables by reduced-cost fixing (p. 23).
- **D6 Positioning**: The history runs from Ford and Fulkerson, Dantzig and Wolfe and Gilmore and Gomory to Desrosiers, Soumis and Desrochers's 1984 branch-and-bound embedding (p. 3). Generic algorithms are credited to Barnhart et al. and to Vanderbeck and Wolsey, efficacy to Desaulniers, Desrosiers and Solomon, earlier reviews to Desrosiers et al., Soumis and Wilhelm (p. 3). The weight lies on Montréal work (Ben Amor, du Merle, Elhallaoui, Villeneuve) and on Vanderbeck (pp. 13-24). Old ideas are judged, not only listed: subgradients use only local information (p. 10), aged pricing proposals need "assessment from a modern implementation point of view" (p. 18), classical Dantzig-Wolfe is not recommended for LPs (p. 25), and Chvátal's 1983 pessimism about integer solutions is "no longer true" once a compact formulation is available (p. 25). Own work: Lübbecke and Zimmermann (Table 1, p. 4) and Villeneuve et al. 2003 (pp. 11-12). The weak compact formulation of the cutting stock problem is attributed to "Kantorovich's formulation (1960)" (p. 12).
- **D7 Writing**: Two parts, theory then algorithms, announced in the introduction (p. 3). Opinion is marked as opinion ("In our opinion", p. 13; "Our point is", p. 22) and open points as open: the theoretical understanding of tailing off "has only been partly achieved to date" (p. 19), bundle and analytic-centre methods are "still to be evaluated in the context of integer programming" (p. 10), complementary slackness has "not been satisfactorily exploited" (p. 18). The conclusion first repeats the abstract's dual-view sentences almost word for word (pp. 2, 25) and then concludes: a recommendation against classical Dantzig-Wolfe for LPs, a status claim for generic codes, and advice to identify the bottleneck before investing in sophisticated ideas (p. 25). The status claim carries no count and no citation: generic integer column generation codes "solve many large scale problems" that no standard commercial MIP solver could cope with (p. 25).
  - Transferable: in a survey, end each topic with a sentence on what is untested or not understood, so that the list of open questions writes itself (pp. 10, 18, 19).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: Synthesis and teaching: one notation, a catalogue of techniques and a dual-side reading of them (pp. 2-3, 25). Limits are given as open questions (pp. 10, 18, 19) and as conditions of use: stabilization needs a problem-specific choice of parameters (p. 22), the implementation burden comes from "the vast possibilities to tune each" component (p. 25), and the compact formulation must be represented besides master and pricing to recover solutions (p. 25). Future: "ample room for research and experiments is left" (p. 25).

**Method links**:

- Heuristic H1 evidence ✅: any column of negative reduced cost suffices, pools, partial pricing, premature stops with a lower bound from the current duals (pp. 14-15).
- Heuristic H5 evidence ✅: one master, several compact formulations with different bounds, symmetry and branching (pp. 22-26); the integrality property of a pricing subproblem "has to be evaluated relative to its own compact formulation" (pp. 25-26).
- Workflow B evidence ✅: a richer subproblem without the integrality property can raise the bound (p. 13), and a cut that destroys the pricing structure is worth it only if the bound gain pays (p. 14).
- New-pattern candidates "one tiny running example through every component, cross-checked" (pp. 1-21, 27; check the 2026 book before pooling) and "the dual lens" (the generation process and the stopping criteria are driven entirely by the dual multipliers, p. 15; with S001).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Phase I by minimizing the artificial variables instead of big-M; with big-M, a too-small M can stop column generation with no negative column although feasible columns exist (p. 5).
- Arc elimination by reduced cost from full duals at the end of the root (pp. 12-13).
- Add c^T x ≥ LB (and ≤ UB) to a single-block subproblem when the bound gain pays for the lost structure; try it on a small case first (pp. 12, 14).
- Warm the root with a small dual box around a subgradient estimate of the multipliers (pp. 19-20).
- Choose among compact formulations of the same master by branching and symmetry, not by bound alone (pp. 24-26).
- Draw the Lagrangian function as the lower envelope of one line per column to explain non-monotone bounds and stabilization on a small case (pp. 20-21).
