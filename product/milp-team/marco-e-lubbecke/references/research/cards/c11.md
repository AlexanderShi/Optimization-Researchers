### S060 · Column generation (Branch-and-Price, ch. 2, pp. 43–102, Springer 2026, DOI 10.1007/978-3-031-96917-1_2)
**Read level**: full.
- Read: file pp. 1–60 (printed pp. 43–102), i.e. §§2.1–2.4, the Reference Notes (§2.5) and all twenty exercises.
- Figures: read through their captions and the surrounding text.
- Garbled: the boxes of Algorithms 2.1 and 2.2 (pp. 8, 10), read through the text around them.
- First-page check: the title and this chapter's DOI appear under "J. Desrosiers et al., Branch-and-Price" (p. 1). In the chapter text, Lübbecke's name appears only in citations of his own 2005 works (p. 50). Authorship follows the four-author book.

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jacques Desrosiers, Guy Desaulniers, Jean Bertrand Gauthier. They are the book co-authors; the voice is joint. · **Position in trajectory**: the opening algorithm chapter of the 2026 book. §2.1 follows the lines of Lübbecke and Desrosiers (2005) (p. 50), so the 2005 survey is still the spine 21 years later. The chapter comes before Dantzig–Wolfe (ch. 3–4), routing and crew scheduling (ch. 5), the dual view (ch. 6) and branch-and-bound (ch. 7), all of which it points forward to (pp. 3, 9–10, 13, 17).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The chapter starts from a general stance: optimization often first drops a hurdle (integrality, or too many constraints) and then works back to it by branching or cutting. Column generation does the same for too many variables (p. 3).
  - Why so few variables are needed: in an extreme-point solution almost all variables are zero, and the fraction actually generated is *astoundingly tiny* (p. 3).
  - It keeps column generation apart from Dantzig–Wolfe, with which it is sometimes confused, and presents it first (p. 3).
  - It sets expectations early. As an LP method it never became competitive except for special cases, and tailored matrix-structure codes did not beat the simplex method. Inside integer programming it is a winner (p. 4).
  - History enters through a long Nemhauser (2012) quotation and a correction of the method's origins: Dantzig's two 1958 credits, Jewell, Manne, Dzielinski–Gomory, and Kantorovich–Zalgaller (1951) as *work zero* following Uchoa and Sadykov (2024) (pp. 3–4).
  - Transferable: open a methods text by saying what the method is not good for (pure LP), then where it pays (p. 4).
- **D2 Key idea & algorithm design**: Column generation is the primal simplex method with one change. The pricing step becomes an optimization problem over an implicit index set X, which returns a negative-reduced-cost column or proves that none exists (p. 5). The master and the pricing problem talk only through π (Fig. 2.2, p. 7; Note 2.7, p. 8). The chapter makes five changes to the usual textbook presentation:
  - (a) Master variables are indexed by pricing solutions (λ_x, x ∈ X). Each column then carries its generating solution, and the later Dantzig–Wolfe extreme points and rays need no index mapping (Note 2.3, p. 5).
  - (b) An object → representation → encoding triad. The object is the planner's (a route, a pattern), and objects belong to the end user, who defines the rules. The representation is x ∈ X in the pricing model; the encoding is (c_x, a_x) in the master. A pricing model is correct if and only if every object is represented and every solution can be read as an object (pp. 11–12, Fig. 2.3). Decoding a master solution back into objects can be as hard as pricing, so store x with its encoding (Note 2.11, p. 13).
  - (c) A zero object (zero cost, zero column), kept distinct from the empty object and modelled by a binary x_0 or a reverse depot arc (Note 2.12, p. 13; pp. 31–32, 41). It is *a mathematical construct* for the later cone, not an efficiency device (p. 32).
  - (d) Heuristic pricing is part of any worthwhile implementation, and exact pricing is ideally needed only once, in the last iteration (p. 17).
  - (e) A textbook / simple / advanced comparison shows that the real work starts after the textbook loop (pp. 27–28).
  - What stays unchanged is the simplex logic, so cycling and degeneracy are inherited (Note 2.9, p. 9).
  - Transferable: choose notation so that each variable's index is the object that generates it (Note 2.3, p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - **Master and assumptions**: an LP over a finite but huge index set (m ≪ |X| < ∞), assumed feasible with a finite optimum, with only non-negativity on λ (p. 5).
    - Upper bounds on λ are advised against. A non-basic variable at its upper bound can have negative reduced cost at optimality and be generated again indefinitely. Set-partitioning bounds should therefore stay implicit (Note 2.1, p. 5; p. 17).
    - The integer master IMP is what one actually wants to solve, and z*_MP ≤ z*_IMP (Note 2.2, p. 5).
    - The Dantzig–Wolfe framework needs encoding functions that preserve addition and scalar multiplication. Real costs, such as crew pay rules or the coefficients of Chvátal–Gomory cuts, are non-linear; conditions for them are deferred to §4.5 (Note 2.10, p. 13).
    - The bounds need a κ^k on each block's Σλ (2.10, p. 19). Corollary 2.2 needs |K| = 1 and c_x = 1; Farley's bound needs c_x ≥ 0 (pp. 20–21).
  - **Structures exploited**:
    - Integer-knapsack pricing for cutting stock; the same problem can be encoded in two ways, which *cannot be taken for granted* (pp. 30–31).
    - Matching pricing for edge colouring, with a set-covering master that drops the colour symmetry (pp. 35–36).
    - Acyclic shortest-path pricing for single-depot vehicle scheduling with a set-partitioning master (pp. 38–41).
    - Relaxed, non-elementary resource-constrained shortest paths for the VRPTW, at the price of a weaker bound (p. 26).
    - A time-window dynamic program with linear node costs for aircraft routing with synchronized departures (pp. 44–47).
  - Covering may replace partitioning when dropping covered elements costs nothing (Note 2.20, p. 35; Exercise 2.14, pp. 57–58).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design, proof devices**:
  - **Loops and initialization**: Algorithm 2.1 starts from a feasible RMP; Algorithm 2.2 uses big-M artificials and returns a certificate of optimality or infeasibility (pp. 8, 10). Farkas pricing handles an infeasible RMP: price with zero costs against the dual ray (max π^T a_x) to destroy the proof of infeasibility, with no M to tune (2.4), pp. 10–11. It is credited to SCIP (p. 11).
  - **Column management**: several columns per pricing call; never select by reduced cost alone; complementary pricing for diverse columns; a pool for the excess; deletion of inactive columns by age; |X′| kept to about 3m (p. 16).
  - **Pricing strategy**:
    - A cascade from the column pool to costlier heuristics, then truncated branch-and-bound, then partial pricing over subsets or over a rotating batch of subproblems. Master and pricing time should be balanced (pp. 17–18).
    - Several subproblems see one π and may return similar columns, so solving all of them, or all of them in parallel, can be wasted work (Notes 2.13–2.14, p. 15).
  - **Proof devices**:
    - (i) Proposition 2.1, π^T b + Σ_k κ^k c̄^k(π) ≤ z*_MP. The objective is split into reduced cost plus π^T a, and the two sums are bounded separately ((2.12)–(2.14), p. 19).
    - (ii) Corollary 2.2, via dual feasibility of π/(1 − c̄(π)) (p. 20).
    - (iii) Farley's bound, via dual feasibility of l(π)·π (pp. 20–21).
    - (iv) Note 2.17: the bounds survive any under-approximation of the minimum reduced cost (for example truncated branch-and-bound). Primal-heuristic or partial pricing gives no bound (p. 20).
    - (v) Note 2.6: the last iteration returns c̄(π) = 0 by complementary slackness (p. 8).
    - (vi) A hypergeometric estimate: degenerate pivots become likelier as the entering column touches more basic variables (p. 22). Exercise 2.19 gives 89.1% for H(20,20,200) (pp. 59–60).
  - **Numerical safeguards**:
    - The RMP optimality tolerance must equal the pricing stop tolerance; otherwise columns with reduced cost in (−ε, 0) cause infinite loops.
    - Column coefficients are rounded at the pricing solver's integrality tolerance and the reduced cost is recomputed (worked example).
    - Rational arithmetic is for mission-critical cases only (pp. 23–24).
  - **Other devices**:
    - Early termination on a relative gap γ, using ⌈LB⌉ when the objective is integer (Note 2.18, p. 23).
    - Auxiliary variables relax the master and bound the duals (π_j ≤ M); this is one reason the RMP becomes easier (pp. 25–26).
    - Coefficients rescaled to relative departure times (at most 1,440 minutes instead of 10,080) to reduce simplex instability (p. 48).

### S075 · Integrated Topology and Redispatch Optimization of Real-World Power Transmission Systems using Branch-and-Benders-Cut (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2611.18302)
**Read level**: full.
- Read: pp. 1–27 (abstract to Concluding Remarks) and the back matter on p. 28 (acknowledgements, CRediT, data and code, competing interests, generative-AI declaration).
- Skimmed: the references, pp. 28–32, for the companion papers.
- First-page check: the title matches, and the authors are Tim Donkiewicz, Oliver Gaul and Marco Lübbecke of the RWTH Chair of Operations Research (p. 1).

**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Tim Donkiewicz and Oliver Gaul (equal contribution, p. 1). Lübbecke's CRediT roles are conceptualization, funding acquisition, methodology, supervision, and writing (review and editing); the original draft is the two first authors' (p. 28). · **Position in trajectory**: arXiv v1 of 13 Nov 2026 (p. 1).
- It comes from the BMWK project InnOpTEM (September 2022 to August 2025), run with the four German transmission system operators (TSOs), FGH and Gurobi (pp. 1–2).
- It belongs to a 2026 cluster of chair papers on Benders decomposition for flow and network-design problems: subproblem solving, cut filtering, subproblem selection and instance generation (pp. 3–4, 29).
- It is a turn away from the 2024–26 branch-price-and-cut-on-SCIP line in SKILL.md: Benders, energy, Gurobi and Julia.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The entry is the application.
  - Congestion management costs run to billions of euros a year (p. 1).
  - German law puts cheap network measures first. Yet only redispatch and phase-shifter taps are optimized algorithmically, while topology is handled manually or separately from redispatch (p. 2).
  - The project with the four TSOs let the authors identify the practically relevant and sufficient constraints and test against real instances and time requirements (p. 2).
  - The computational hook: the compact MILP replicates the DC power flow for every contingency. For a national grid, with thousands of buses and hundreds of N-1 scenarios, it can exceed workstation memory, so a monolithic solve is ruled out (p. 2).
  - The problem is stated as an extended SCOTS (security-constrained optimal transmission switching) with N-1 security (p. 2).
  - Transferable: state the compact model's failure mode (here memory) as the reason to decompose, and keep the compact model as a measured baseline (pp. 2, 22).
- **D2 Key idea & algorithm design**: Benders decomposition along the contingency structure (pp. 13–15).
  - The master holds the topology, transformer, commitment and redispatch decisions, plus one cost variable η_C per contingency.
  - One subproblem per contingency checks power-flow feasibility and overload cost and returns an optimality or feasibility cut. The cuts are separated lazily inside the MILP tree at integer, or fractional, nodes.
  - Each piece is then made cheap:
    - Subproblems are solved as linear systems on a contracted graph. Duals come from a second system with the same matrix, and the LU factorization is updated by rank-1 steps between subproblems (SLE-U, pp. 15–17).
    - Only a scored subset of subproblems is solved per iteration (§3.3), and cuts are filtered by violation (§3.4).
    - The base-case flow stays in the master (partial decomposition, §3.5).
    - Connectivity is handled by preprocessing and bridge-based cuts (§3.6).
    - A sequential restricted-switching heuristic serves as a warm start or as a parallel source of incumbents (§3.7).
  - Against plain Benders, the subproblem solver, selection, filtering and partial decomposition change. The classical dual-based cuts and Farkas-ray cuts stay (pp. 14–15).
  - Why this order: after SLE-U, the master takes 51.90% of solve time instead of 7.60%, so the next components control master growth (p. 23).
  - Transferable: re-profile after each component and aim the next one at the new bottleneck (p. 23).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**:
  - **Variables**: an MILP with binaries (switching states, commitment, redispatch and change indicators, startups and shutdowns, coupler actions), general integers (tap positions κ ∈ Z, deviation counters) and continuous flows, angles and overloads.
  - **Big-M**: the Kirchhoff constraints carry big-M terms B·θ̄^off that switch off the coupling when a line is off (pp. 7–10).
  - **Physics and security**: a lossless DC approximation with angle bounds (typically ≤ 30°) (pp. 6–7). The N-1 contingency set includes the base case, and topology, generation and taps are shared across contingencies (p. 6).
  - **Time coupling**: a multi-period horizon with minimum up and down times, ramps and switch-back limits (pp. 9–11).
  - **Substations**: breaker and busbar nodes. Substation arcs only equalize angles, assuming small electrical distances (pp. 7–8).
  - **Connectivity and simplifications**: an islanding-free requirement with exponentially many cut constraints (17) (p. 11), and, without loss of generality, one generator per node (p. 9).
  - **Correctness basis**: the linear-system subproblem rests on the uniqueness of a fixed-network power flow (Birkhoff and Diaz 1956) (p. 15).
  - **Not implemented**: symmetric branch utilization, generation-distribution symmetry and emergency flexibility are formulated but not implemented in the solver (p. 13).
  - **Synthetic data**: 130 benchmark and 31 tuning instances, generated from PGLib-OPF by random selection, randomized breadth-first reduction, Kamada–Kawai layout and coupling edges. They have no substation, generator-group, transformer, symmetry or flexibility data (p. 22).
  - **Real data**: German grid instances with about 3,000 buses, 5,000 branches, 2,300 generators and 2,800 loads; over 300 substations of 2–5 busbars, over 1,300 switchable branches and about 1,000 contingencies (p. 25). The data are confidential (p. 28).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design, proof devices**:
  - **Cuts**: the optimality cut (32a) has per-arc terms from the big-M right-hand sides (32b–c); the feasibility cut (33) comes from a dual ray (pp. 14–15).
  - **Templates**: one master and one subproblem template per worker. The template is updated across contingencies, and master decisions are refreshed in every template when the master solution changes (p. 14).
  - **SLE-U** (pp. 15–17):
    - Contract the active substation arcs, solve the Laplacian system for the angles, and check the remaining constraints.
    - Overloads follow in closed form. Duals come from a system with the same matrix, and feasibility rays from an expanded system.
    - Rank-1 LU updates, one per differing arc, cut one Benders iteration from O(|A|·|V|³) to O(|A|·|V|²).
    - The claim that all duals built this way are optimal is asserted here and developed in Gaul et al. (2026) (p. 16).
  - **Subproblem selection** (pp. 17–18):
    - The score combines reliability, share of cuts, average violation, staleness, diversity from k-medoid clustering on line-outage distribution factors (LODFs), and a random component, with decay.
    - Stop rules cover cut counts, a score threshold and a primal-bound test. Minimum numbers of feasibility and optimality cuts are kept for numerical and convergence stability.
    - Full stabilization rounds run every k iterations.
  - **Cut filtering** (pp. 18–19): by violation, with absolute, relative and gap-share caps. Discarded cuts can be aggregated into one, and at least one cut is always kept.
  - **Partial decomposition** (pp. 19–20): contingencies chosen by congestion-proximity, LODF switching-sensitivity and violation scores, or by k-medoid diversity.
  - **Connectivity** (pp. 20–21): pre-fix branches that would disconnect the grid together with a contingency; count islands; find a splitting contingency through bridges; add cut (40).
  - **Heuristic** (p. 21): restricted SCOTS with fewer switchable arcs and switching limits 1…Z, passing on the best solution. Arcs are ranked by an LODF-corrected susceptance sensitivity, computed from master LP duals with the discrete decisions fixed.
  - **Proof devices**: there are no theorems. The devices are the uniqueness of the linear system, the rank-1 complexity count and bridge detection.
- **D5 Computational evaluation**:
  - **Methodological study (p. 22)**: 130 instances with switching limit Z = 1 and a 3 h limit that includes the warm start. SCOPF (the switching-free problem) and each warm-start stage are capped at 30 min.
    - Hardware and software: Debian 11; two Xeon L5630 at 2.13 GHz; 16 threads; 128 GB. Gurobi 13.0.2 with 4 threads for the master, 1 thread for Benders and subproblems; Julia 1.12.6, JuMP 1.30.1, PowerModels 0.21.6.
    - Parameters were tuned on a separate set of 31 instances, mostly one component after another.
  - **Table 2 chain (pp. 22–23)**: shifted geometric mean with a 10 s shift; failures charged 10,800 s.

    | Configuration | Solved (of 130) | Shifted geometric mean (s) |

    | subproblem selection (random order) | 130 | 59.71 |

    | four subproblem threads | 130 | 36.86 |

  - **Practical study (pp. 23–25)**: Z = 1…5, 6 h per run, and 96 h runs for upper bounds at Z = 3. An improvement counts only above 0.0001.
    - Five configurations are compared by the share of instances within 0.0001 of the best known solution. The exact solve reaches it more often than the sequential heuristic for Z ≤ 3, but more slowly, and less often for Z ≥ 4. For every Z, warm start and the parallel heuristic reach it at least as often as the other three configurations, and warm start reaches it more often than the parallel heuristic for Z ≥ 2 (p. 24).
    - Most of the mean objective reduction is reached by Z = 2; the median is much smaller, and the gaps to the upper bounds widen (pp. 24–25).
    - At Z = 5, 31 of the 37 improvements arrive within 10 min (p. 25).
  - **Real world**: an older version of the algorithm, similar to configuration 5 (p. 25).
    - 71 days × 24 hourly instances, up to five line openings, no substation switching. A feasible topology was found for all of them within 5 h, with about 7% less simulated redispatch volume and a median CPU time of about 34 min (p. 25).
    - Seven solutions were shown to operators, and all passed the AC checks (p. 26).
    - Case study: loadings of 132% and 130% fall to 107% and 110%, and the calculatory redispatch cost drops by 20.40% (p. 26).
  - **Variability**: subproblem selection has random elements (p. 17), and the tuned setting is random order (p. 22). Yet each configuration is run once and no seeds are stated (text search). "statistically significant" appears without a named test (p. 23).

- **D8 Contribution & limits**:
  - **Levels**: model (the full formulation, §2), algorithm (branch-and-Benders-cut with integrated components, §3), computation (§4), application and operator evidence (§5), and software (TopologyOptimizer.jl, p. 28).
  - **Limits, stated concretely**:
    - DC solutions need not be AC-feasible, and some operational constraints are unknown or omitted (pp. 25–26).
    - The real-world gains cannot be compared with the TSOs' actual actions (p. 25).
    - The real-world runs used an earlier, slower version of the algorithm (p. 25).
    - The synthetic instances lack substations and several constraint groups (p. 22).
    - The gaps to the upper bounds widen with Z (p. 25).
    - Hours are optimized one after another, and AC is considered only after the topology is fixed (p. 26).
    - Operators judged some actions not worth the effort, so a human stays in the loop (p. 26).
  - **Where the conclusion reaches beyond the evidence**:
    - It claims *provably optimal solutions in reasonably small time* (p. 27). This holds at Z = 1, where all 130 instances are solved within 3 h (pp. 22–23). At Z = 2 the paper counts 107 optimal two-switch solutions among the 130 instances (p. 26). For larger Z, the gaps to the upper bounds widen and the benefit is left unresolved, even with 96 h runs at Z = 3 (pp. 23–25).
    - It says the model can be changed *without a need to alter the algorithm itself* (p. 27), while §3 says the methodology *does not naturally extend* to some extensions (p. 13).
  - **Future**: an open-source release, curative measures and transmission expansion planning (pp. 27–28).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the evaluation as a component chain: each row names its predecessor and gives solved count, mean and shifted geometric mean, with the shift and the time charged to failures stated. Put the compact model in row 1 and add a performance profile (Table 2, pp. 22–23).
- Tune every parameter on an instance set kept separate from the benchmark (31 against 130, p. 22).
- After each component, re-measure the master's share of time and aim the next component at the new bottleneck (p. 23).
- For a flow model with fixed topology, solve Benders subproblems as linear systems on the contracted graph, and update the LU factorization by rank-1 steps between subproblems instead of re-solving LPs (pp. 15–17).
- Solve only a scored or random subset of subproblems per iteration, with periodic full rounds. Filter cuts by violation under absolute, relative and gap-share caps, always keeping one (pp. 17–19).
- Partial decomposition: keep the base-case flow in the master before trying contingency flows (pp. 19, 23).
- Reuse one subproblem template per worker and change only the contingency-dependent rows (p. 14).
- Argue for integrated optimization with counts of exact optima that break a heuristic's premise (18 of 107; 28 of 107, p. 26).
- Count an improvement only above a stated absolute threshold, to filter numerical noise (p. 23).
- For real-world claims, write what the evidence does not establish and say when the reported algorithm version differs (pp. 25–26).
- Position against prior work with a scope-and-status table, and define *exact* in its note (Table 1, p. 5).
