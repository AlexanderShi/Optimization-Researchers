### S030 · Minimizing the stabbing number of matchings, trees, and triangulations (Proceedings of the 15th ACM-SIAM Symposium on Discrete Algorithms (SODA) 2004, arXiv cs/0310034v1; no DOI)
**Read level**: full. All 13 file pages of arXiv cs/0310034v1 of 16 Oct 2003 (p. 1), which the arXiv comment marks "to appear in Symposium on Discrete Algorithms" (work-list note); the printed proceedings text was not compared. Read: abstract and introduction (pp. 1–3), preliminaries (p. 3), hardness sketches (pp. 3–7), LPs (pp. 7–8), iterated rounding (pp. 8–10), computational results (pp. 10–12), notes and conclusion, acknowledgments and references (pp. 12–13). Identity checked on p. 1: the title, with Fekete, Lübbecke and Meijer as authors.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Sándor P. Fekete (TU Braunschweig), Henk Meijer (Queen's University, Kingston) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: written during his TU Berlin years; the extended abstract of D001 (DCG 2008). Lübbecke's footnote credits a DFG travel grant for visits to Kingston and Stony Brook in May 2002 (p. 1). This card reads deeper than the abstract-level S030 card in a2-01, and confirms that card's guess that the computations are in this version (pp. 10–12).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Whether minimum-stabbing structures can be found efficiently is one of the original 30 problems on the Demaine–Mitchell–O'Rourke list (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). Earlier work gave extremal bounds (Welzl, Matoušek, de Berg and van Kreveld, Shewchuk) and applications (ray shooting, collision detection). It gave "no published results or conjectures on the complexity of stabbing problems", no good combinatorial lower bounds, and no approximation beyond the O(√n) that follows from Welzl (p. 2). Joe Mitchell is thanked "for pushing us to work on this problem" (p. 12).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Hardness first, then one LP for three jobs. It gives lower bounds in polynomial time by the ellipsoid method, a heavy fractional edge for Jain-style iterated rounding, and, solved by the simplex method and with integrality added, exact or near-exact solutions in practice (pp. 2–3). Stabbing rows break Jain's lemma that some edge has weight at least 1/2: Fig. 7 shows a unique optimum whose heaviest edge has weight 1/3 (pp. 8–9). The authors replace the lemma with geometry. Some optimal fractional solution has planar support, so a vertex of degree at most five carries an edge of weight at least 1/5 for matchings; for trees, weight n − 1 spread over at most 3n − 6 edges gives an edge of more than 1/3 (Lemmas 5.1–5.2, Theorem 5.1, p. 9). Iterated rounding fixes a heaviest edge to 1 and re-solves (p. 10). A "one good shot at once" variant takes a maximum-weight perfect matching on the LP support (p. 12). For trees, the exponential cut formulation is chosen over polynomial-size ones for its "particularly useful properties" (p. 8). The text does not name the properties; presumably they are the cut structure on which §5's rounding builds (p. 8) [inference].
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: Binary edge variables on the complete straight-line graph of a planar point set, plus a common bound k that is minimized. There is one "≤ k" row per representative stabbing line: O(n) per direction, because the count changes only at a vertex, and O(n²) combinatorial directions (pp. 7–8). Matchings use Edmonds' degree rows and exponentially many blossom rows (4.1)–(4.3) (p. 7); trees use cut rows (4.5)–(4.8) (p. 8). Stated assumptions: n is even, "if necessary by omitting one of the points" (p. 3); the LP results need no general position and work for any line family with polynomially many representatives, while the hardness proofs use collinear points (p. 3). Instances: TSPLIB, Solomon's clustered VRPTW C-class, grids with 20% of the points removed, and random points (p. 10). On the grids, collinear points cut down the number of stabbing rows (pp. 10–11).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design (brief)**: Hardness is shown by 3SAT reductions whose collinear "barriers" already use up the stabbing budget, so gadgets cannot interact (Theorem 3.1, p. 3; Lemmas 3.3–3.4, pp. 4–5). Corollary 3.1 rules out any factor below 6/5 (p. 4), using the decision threshold 5 of Theorem 3.1. Perturbation by powers of ε carries the result to general lines (Corollary 3.2, p. 4). Other devices: pigeonhole over the 2×2 cycles of a crossing-number barrier (Lemma 3.5, p. 5) and a triangle count between two rows of points (Lemma 3.6, p. 5). The LP is weakly polynomial by the ellipsoid method, with polynomial-time blossom separation (Padberg–Rao) and polynomially many stabbing rows; a strongly polynomial method would touch the open budgeted-matching problem (p. 8). Planar support comes from "an uncrossing 2-exchange argument", with the proofs deferred (p. 9). Every proof in this text is a sketch (p. 3). Implementation: CPLEX 8.0 for LPs and IPs (p. 10). No separation routine or cut loop is described, and the text does not say whether the tabled LPs contain blossom rows ("this is also true when blossom inequalities are added", p. 10). [Inference: the five odd instances have finite LP values (Table 1, p. 10). So the tabled LP cannot have held the odd-set row for S = V, whose cut is empty (p. 7).]
- **D5 Computational evaluation (one line)**: CPLEX 8.0 on a Pentium III 700 MHz with 1 GB under Linux, CPU seconds, four-hour IP limit (p. 10); 62 instances, all of axis-parallel matching St-Mat2(P): 10 TSPLIB up to 442 points, 16 Solomon C-class with 100 points, 25 grids, 11 random up to 100 points (p. 10; Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11); per row, the LP value and time, the IP optimum or incumbent ("≤12") and time, and the rounding value; no competing method; 16 rows hit the limit (†), and CPLEX fails within four hours to detect that the five odd TSPLIB instances are infeasible (p. 12); by my count, the rounding value is within a factor 1.93 of the LP bound on every row, and it beats CPLEX's four-hour incumbent on lin318 and pcb442 (11 against ≤12, 18 against ≤19), which the text does not mention.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: Contribution: hardness for matchings (strongly NP-complete at stabbing number 5, no factor below 6/5, weakly NP-hard for all directions), for spanning trees (four variants, Theorem 3.2) and for triangulations (axis-parallel crossing number, Theorem 3.3) (pp. 3–7); LP bounds in weakly polynomial time (p. 8); the heavy-edge theorem (p. 9); and the computational study (pp. 10–12). The limits are concrete. Proofs are sketches (p. 3). The constant-factor guarantee needs an "extra argument" because the objective is a maximum, not a sum (p. 10), and it remains "the most interesting open problem" (p. 12). The hardness proofs need collinear points (p. 3). Only one variant is computed, and the prototypes are "not yet ready to solve even larger instances" (p. 12). The IP solver fails on the odd instances (p. 12). Future work (p. 12): the approximation proof; small constant stabbing numbers (St-Tre2(P) = 2 and St-Matall(P) = 2 are decidable in polynomial time, Theorem 7.1); the average stabbing number, which reduces to minimum length (Theorem 7.2); stabbing-independent subsets; the one-shot heuristic; and computations for St-Matall(P), St-Tre2(P) and triangulations "in the full paper".

**Transferable techniques**:
- Give each instance one table row with the LP bound, the heuristic value, and the IP optimum, or its incumbent written as "≤ u" when the time limit stops the IP. Every heuristic value then carries its own gap certificate (Tables 1–2, pp. 10–11).
- Trace the LP value along the iterations of a rounding heuristic to see where quality is lost. On lin318, about 100 iterations are needed; the LP value exceeds 9.000 only in the last ten and ends at 11, against an LP bound of 8.113 (p. 12).
- When every LP point is a convex combination of integral structures (here perfect matchings), try one-shot rounding: compute a maximum-weight integral structure on the LP support graph and compare it with iterated rounding (p. 12).
- For a min–max objective over an infinite family of probes, use a sweep argument: the count changes only at a vertex, so O(n) lines per direction and O(n²) directions are enough (p. 8). Points that share coordinates shrink the row set further (pp. 10–11).
- When a known rounding lemma fails under side constraints, show the LP where it fails (Fig. 7, p. 9). Then prove a structural property of some optimal solution (planar support, by uncrossing) and read a heavy variable off a counting bound (degree ≤ 5, at most 3n − 6 edges) (Theorem 5.1, p. 9).
- Screen instances for trivial infeasibility before a long IP run and keep infeasible ones out of the tables. Here the five odd TSPLIB instances ran four hours in CPLEX 8.0 without the infeasibility being detected (p. 12), although the paper's own rule says to drop a point so that n is even (p. 3). Summing the degree equations gives 2Σx = n, which already excludes odd n [this parity check is mine, not the paper's].
