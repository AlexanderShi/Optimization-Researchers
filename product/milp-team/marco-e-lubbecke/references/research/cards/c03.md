### S016 · A constraint integer programming approach for resource-constrained project scheduling (CPAIOR 2010, DOI 10.1007/978-3-642-13520-0_34)
**Read level**: full. All 5 pages, including Table 1 and the references. The detailed results that Table 1 points to (ZIB-Report 10-03, reference [4]) are a separate document and were not read (p. 4).
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Timo Berthold and Stefan Heinz (ZIB), Rolf H. Möhring and Jens Schulz (TU Berlin); Lübbecke at TU Darmstadt (p. 1). The order is alphabetical, so it carries no lead signal. · **Place in trajectory**: the TU Berlin and ZIB applications period, outside decomposition. It is the only work in this batch whose software contribution goes into SCIP itself (the cumulative constraint, p. 2) rather than using SCIP to host a branch-and-price code. The batch note says S104 is a second Scholar row of this paper.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: The RCPSP is entered as a benchmark that "consistently resists computational attempts" to obtain solutions of proven quality (p. 1); PSPLib instances with 60 jobs "have not been solved for many years" (abstract, p. 1). The entry is a gap between three communities: IP, CP and SAT each attack the problem, "but no single one alone has been able to crack the problem", and the currently best known approach, lazyFD, is a CP-SAT hybrid (p. 2). "Conceptually, it is the logical next step to integrate the IP world as well" (p. 2). The study is framed as an evaluation of potential and a proof of concept (p. 2). Problem: non-preemptive jobs with processing times and demands on renewable resources, a precedence graph, minimise the makespan (pp. 2-3).
  - Transferable: when two communities have hybridised on a hard benchmark, ask which third toolbox is missing and make the paper the evaluation of that piece (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: One branch-and-bound tree in SCIP, following the constraint integer programming (CIP) paradigm, that combines CP propagation of the global cumulative constraint, lower bounds from the LP relaxation, SCIP's conflict analysis and a problem-specific heuristic (p. 2). The IP model is the time-indexed formulation with binary start-time variables of Pritsker et al. [9]; knapsack constraints are generated from the capacity cuts inside the cumulative constraint (p. 3). Changed relative to lazyFD: LP bounds are added to propagation and conflict learning (pp. 2-3). Kept: CP's cumulative constraint as the modelling core (p. 2). A bound change found by core-time propagation is explained by the set of jobs with non-empty cores in the interval, so the solver's generic conflict analysis can learn from it (p. 3).
  - Transferable: make a problem-specific propagator deliver its explanation (the jobs whose cores cause the bound change), so that a generic conflict analysis can use it (p. 3).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: a pure binary IP (time-indexed start variables) next to the CP model (1) with cumulative constraints (pp. 2-3). Test sets: PSPLib with 480 instances of 30 jobs and 480 of 60 jobs; the 120-job instances are used only for lower bounds (p. 4). No correctness argument is needed beyond the completeness of the branch-and-bound search (p. 3).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: core-time bound propagation with explanations for the cumulative constraint (p. 3). Pairs of jobs that cannot run in parallel are filtered into a "global disjunctive bounds constraint", which propagates faster and separates cuts from forbidden sets (p. 3). Primal heuristic: list scheduling, ordered by the LP start times when an LP solution exists and otherwise by weighted local bounds and α-points, followed by a justification improvement heuristic whenever a better solution is found (pp. 3-4). Hybrid branching on integer variables only; propagation and re-propagation are left to SCIP (pp. 3-4). No theorem. One open question is stated: whether finding a minimum set of jobs that explains a bound change is NP-hard (p. 3).
  - Transferable: order a list-scheduling heuristic by the LP solution's start times, and fall back to bound-based or α-point orders when no LP solution is available (p. 3).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: Intel Xeon 2.66 GHz, 8 GB, Linux; SCIP 1.2.0.6 with CPLEX as LP solver, printed "cplex release version 12.10" (presumably 12.1.0, given the 2010 date [inferred]); one hour per instance (p. 4). Design: a 2×2 ablation ("default", "noconflict", "norelax", "none"), a per-instance best-of-four row ("bestset") and lazyFD (Table 1, p. 4). Metrics: instances proven optimal, best known solution found, worse than best known; total and shifted geometric mean of nodes and time, with shifts 100 (nodes) and 10 s (time) chosen to damp the very easy instances (footnote, p. 4). Results (Table 1, p. 4): optimality is proven on 460/480 (30 jobs) and 385/480 (60 jobs) with default, on 480 and 429 by lazyFD, on 460 and 391 by bestset. Both features together cut nodes most (60 jobs: 34,104k total nodes against 126,714k with neither). The text concedes that the node reduction "does not directly transfer to the running time" (p. 4). My reading of Table 1: by shifted geometric mean time, default ranks third of four on both sets ("norelax" 6.5 s and "none" 7.7 s against 7.8 s on 30 jobs; 26.1 s and 26.9 s against 27.3 s on 60 jobs); default leads in proven optima and in total time. The lazyFD row has no node or time entries, and the text does not say whether lazyFD was re-run or taken from [11]. Five 120-job lower bounds are improved, each named with its value (p. 5). No seeds or variability.
  - Transferable: evaluate a two-feature design as a 2×2 grid plus a per-instance best row, and report node and time means separately, because node gains need not become time gains (p. 4).
- **D6 Positioning**: builds on SCIP and the CIP paradigm (Achterberg [1]) and on hybrid branching [2] (pp. 2, 4). The rival is lazyFD (Schutt et al. [11]), "the currently best known approach" (p. 2). Upper bounds found recently by [11] were met "independently of our work" (p. 2). The survey [6] is the field reference (p. 2). No direct criticism of others.
- **D7 Writing**: a five-page short paper with a labelled "Our Contribution" paragraph (p. 2). The register rises from evaluating "the potential of such a hybrid" as "a proof-of-concept" (p. 2) to "First of all the results show that our approach is competitive to the current best known method" (p. 4) and "We have shown the power of integrating CP, IP, and SAT techniques" (p. 5). "It is remarkable that this holds for both, upper and lower bounds" (p. 2). The conclusion summarises (p. 5). Detailed results are deferred to the report (p. 4).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: contribution: software (the cumulative constraint made "generically available within the CIP solver scip", p. 2), a computational proof of concept, and five improved lower bounds on PSPLib 120-job instances (p. 5). Limits: a "basic implementation"; future work is a stronger LP relaxation by cutting planes and dynamic edge-finding with SCIP's re-propagation, "subject to current research" (p. 5). The complexity of minimum explanations stays open (p. 3).

**Method links**:
- Method 3, evidence ✅ for step 5: per-feature ablation, and shifted geometric means with each shift stated and justified (p. 4). Variant ⚠ for steps 3 and 6: lazyFD's figures come without provenance, nodes or times; "competitive to the current best known method" (p. 4) and "shown the power" (p. 5) stand next to lazyFD proving more optima on both sets (480 against 460; 429 against 385; Table 1, p. 4). "Using both features, however, leads to the best performance" (p. 4) holds for optima and total time, not for the shifted geometric mean time (D5).
- Method 1 step 5, evidence ✅ outside decomposition: the hybrid lives in SCIP's tree, not in a hand-written search (pp. 2-3).
- H9 and Taste G2, evidence ✅: the cumulative constraint as a reusable SCIP component is named as a contribution (p. 2). Taste G1 ✅: proven lower bounds on named open instances are what the paper can claim beyond the counts (p. 5).
- H4, weak evidence: a step of the tool (explaining a bound change) is turned into an open complexity question (p. 3).
- New-pattern candidate: oracle column next to the rule (the name card c04 uses). "bestset", the best of four settings per instance (p. 4), is the device card c04 found as DWR best (S015) and OPT (S009); S035 below has it as the "Best alpha" bars (S035 p. 16).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Ablate component by component (each off, both off, both on) and add a per-instance best-of-settings row, so that the reader sees each component's share and the ceiling of the portfolio (p. 4).
- Report shifted geometric means with the shift for each measure and the reason for it (p. 4).
- Report nodes and time side by side, and say when a reduction of the search space does not pay in time (p. 4).
- State improved bounds on named open benchmark instances with their values (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "It is the purpose of this study to evaluate the potential of such a hybrid and to give a proof-of-concept." (p. 2)
- "We use a shift s = 10 for time and s = 100 for nodes in order to decrease the strong influence of the very easy instances in the mean values." (p. 4)

### S007 · Branch-Price-and-Cut Algorithms (Wiley Encyclopedia of Operations Research and Management Science 2011, DOI 10.1002/9780470400531.eorms0118)
**Read level**: full. All 18 pages of the authors' manuscript ("April 8, 2010; revised July 7, 2010", p. 1), including the references. Not compared with the printed Wiley text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Jacques Desrosiers (HEC Montréal and GERAD), first author; Lübbecke at TU Darmstadt (p. 1). Joint voice throughout. · **Place in trajectory**: written in 2010, after the SEA 2010 GCG paper (cited as [40], p. 14) and before IPCO 2011 (S032). Companion to S008, which it cross-references as entry "1.1.2.7" (p. 2). The closing chapter of the 2026 book carries the same title (card c13, S077).

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the entry names a misconception: having understood branch-and-bound, column generation and cutting planes, "one may think that the above explanation is the end of the story. Actually, this understanding is precisely where the story begins." (p. 2). Cutting and branching "interfere with column generation and may entirely ruin the mentioned advantages of a decomposition when done naively" (p. 2). Early ad hoc fixes gave way to "a generic theoretical understanding" built on the relation between the original problem and its extended reformulation, first used by Desrosiers, Soumis and Desrochers in 1984 [2] (p. 2).
  - Transferable: open an expository text at the point where the reader's understanding stops and the real difficulty begins (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea**: one organising relation, between the original problem (3) in the original variables x and the extended (master) formulation, in two versions: convexification (Minkowski-Weyl, integrality kept on x, (5)) and discretization (Vanderbeck, integrality on the master variables λ, (8)) (pp. 3-5). Each later design question (how to state cuts, §3; how to branch, §4; what to implement, §5) is answered by asking where it sits in that relation. Two additions widen it: extended formulations beyond Dantzig-Wolfe (a multi-commodity spanning-tree model, symmetry-breaking pair variables, capacity- and time-indexed formulations; pp. 6, 10), and "reversing" Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition. For a model written directly in columns, "Without a decomposition, there is no original problem", but one can be built by duplicating the pricing domain K times, and the authors call such an original problem very helpful for designing branching rules and cutting planes (pp. 6-7, citing Villeneuve, Desrosiers, Lübbecke and Soumis 2005 [18]).
  - Transferable: if you model directly in columns, reconstruct an original formulation (K copies of the pricing domain in block-diagonal form, (11)) before you design branching and cuts (pp. 6-7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: MIP (3) whose set X is "relatively easy" to optimise over; the entry assumes X is a pure integer set (q = 0) and only points to the mixed case (p. 3). Discretization needs a pricing problem that can return integer points in the interior of X; with X bounded, a non-linear cost becomes linear in the master (p. 5). Identical blocks create symmetry, which "should be avoided by all means" (p. 5). The reversal assumes an integer upper bound K on the number of columns used, and "a mild assumption which is typically true" (p. 7). Applications named: bin packing and cutting stock, coloring, machine scheduling, p-median, generalized assignment, vehicle routing (p. 2).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**: expository devices with references; no new theorem.
  - Cuts on original variables transfer to the master and change only the pricing objective (14), or they are imposed in the pricing domain X_F, where they are convexified and may give a stronger bound (15) (p. 8).
  - Cuts on master variables need their duals in pricing; otherwise "we may re-generate 'cut off' variables and end up in an infinite loop of separation and pricing" (p. 9). A non-linear coefficient function g (Chvátal-Gomory rank-1 cuts) is handled by extra pricing variables y = g(Ax), for example one new resource per cut in a resource-constrained shortest path (p. 9).
  - Branching on master variables is infeasible under convexification and unbalanced under discretization (p. 10). Branching on an original variable can go into the master (19) or into the pricing problem (21); pricing is "to be preferred" if it stays tractable, because the bound change is then convexified, inequality (22) (p. 11).
  - Identical subproblems: aggregate them, then use Vanderbeck's lexicographic projection (24) to obtain a unique original solution (p. 12). Branching options: aggregate original variables, auxiliary pair variables with Ryan-Foster, and the nested partition of the convexity constraint, which gives the strongest bound with a search-tree depth that is "polynomially bounded" (pp. 12-13).
  - Implementation: keep the linking constraints (the explicit master) to read x values and to branch by bound changes (pp. 13-14). An open drawback: x* is usually not a basic solution, which generic cuts and heuristics need; a crossover was suggested (Galati, referring to Forrest), "but there are no experiences reported on this yet" (p. 14).
  - Transferable: when you add a cut on master variables, put its dual into the reduced-cost computation from the start, or pricing will regenerate the columns it cut off (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a. No experiments and no reported numbers. The empirical remarks are pointers only ("very successful applications", p. 2; "good experience" with cuts on capacity- and time-indexed variables, p. 10).
- **D6 Positioning**: the theory is credited to Vanderbeck (discretization, generic branching), to Vanderbeck and Wolsey (the "thorough exposition" whose classification §4 follows), to Desaulniers, Desrosiers and Spoorendonk (cuts) and to Ralphs and Galati (decompose and cut) (pp. 4, 8-10, 12). Barnhart et al. 1998 is "The classical—by now a bit outdated—survey" (p. 15). Frameworks (ABACUS, BCP, MINTO, SCIP, SYMPHONY) and generic codes (BaPCod, DIP, G12) are listed without ranking; GCG is "The attempt to turn the branch-price-and-cut framework SCIP into a branch-price-and-cut solver" (p. 14). Reference-list slips: [14] and [15] are the same 2006 ORL paper with different author lists, and the 2005 survey appears as "J. Desrosiers and M.E. Lübbecke" [3] (pp. 15-16), while its 2004 preprint names Lübbecke first (S001 p. 1).
- **D7 Writing**: teaching register with cross-references to other encyclopedia entries ("see also 1.4.2.1", p. 2). Evaluative asides: "Symmetry is beautiful in many areas of mathematics, however, for an integer program it may be a major source of inefficiency" (p. 5). The implementation section is candid ("it will be a whole lot of work, even in 2010", p. 13). Section 5.2, "When everything fails. . .", ranks three answers (p. 14). Practitioner shortcuts are named for what they are: price-and-branch with branching on master variables "is not elegant, but it often works" (p. 14). The entry ends with a naming remark credited to Adam Letchford (p. 15). A manuscript trace: the unresolved cross-reference "?.?.?.?" (p. 8).
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: an expository synthesis: one notation for convexification and discretization, and a map of where cuts and branching decisions go (pp. 3-13). Open points are stated as such: generic cuts on original variables "sometimes seem to have little or no effect" on the already strong bound, and "Currently, problem specific valid inequalities are the alternative to go." (p. 8); cuts on master variables have been reported "only for a very few problems" (p. 9); the non-basic x* "has not been satisfactorily addressed so far" (p. 14). Further reading: the 2005 Column Generation book "is, in fact, a book on branch-and-price" (p. 15).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 steps 1 and 6, evidence ✅: the entry argues from the original formulation throughout. A model written in columns should get one constructed (pp. 6-7); integrality and branching sit on the original variables under convexification (pp. 4, 10-11); the explicit master keeps them accessible (pp. 13-14).
- Method 1 (decomposition as a measured bet), evidence ✅: pricing ideally has a tailored combinatorial algorithm, "and if not, we need to solve it with some standard IP solver which may be very time consuming" (p. 4); the build is "a whole lot of work" (p. 13). This is the 2010 form of Workflow B's checkpoint (b).
- Method 4, evidence ✅: an encyclopedia entry that states what is open (pp. 8, 9, 14) and credits the sources of ideas, names and suggestions (Letchford, p. 15; Galati and Forrest, p. 14). The skill's Honest Boundary lists the encyclopedia entries as unread; this one is now read.
- Workflow G, evidence ✅ (joint, stated): §5.2 gives ranked answers for a branch-price-and-cut code that fails. "Research your problem!" comes first: combinatorial pricing instead of MIPs, cuts, or "re-thinking the entire formulation". The most practicable answer is to "run a profiler" and use acceleration techniques. The quickest, "certainly the dirtiest", is a heuristic: price-and-branch, ideally with variables fixed by branching and further columns generated (p. 14). The skill labels Workflow G's order [inferred]; this is a stated source for it, and for H5.
- Tension 6, evidence that it is older and narrower than stated: already in 2010 the entry separates generic cuts on original variables (little or no effect) from problem-specific ones ("the alternative to go", p. 8). Card c13 (S077) calls the tension partly resolved in the 2026 book.
- H1, variant ⚠: here the heuristic advice is at the tree level (price-and-branch as the "dirtiest" answer, p. 14), not the heuristic pricing inside an exact frame that S008 and H1 describe.

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a pure column model, reverse the decomposition: K copies of the pricing domain give an original formulation on which to design branching and cuts (pp. 6-7).
- Enforce an original-variable branching decision in the pricing problem when pricing stays tractable (the bound change is convexified, (22)); otherwise add it as a master row whose dual enters the pricing objective (p. 11).
- Put the duals of master-variable cuts into pricing, and carry a non-linear cut coefficient as an extra pricing resource (p. 9).
- With identical subproblems, aggregate and recover a unique original solution by lexicographic projection before branching (p. 12).
- When the code fails: research the structure first, profile second, go heuristic last; if you go heuristic, fix variables and keep generating columns (p. 14).

**Quotes**:
- "Even when the whole lot of work of implementing a branch-price-and-cut algorithm will pay off, it will be a whole lot of work, even in 2010." (p. 13)
- "The most honorable answer to that is: Research your problem!" (p. 14)

### S008 · Column generation (Wiley Encyclopedia of Operations Research and Management Science 2011, DOI 10.1002/9780470400531.eorms0158)
**Read level**: full. All 19 pages of the author's manuscript ("June 7, 2010; revised: July 1, 2010", p. 1), including the references. Not compared with the printed Wiley text.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: none; sole author, at TU Darmstadt (p. 1). This is his own voice. · **Place in trajectory**: "This article is based on [47]", the 2005 survey (p. 15): the survey condensed into an encyclopedia entry five years on, in mid-2010, when the first GCG paper (SEA 2010) had appeared (S007 cites it, p. 14; this entry does not). S007 is its companion entry.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: the entry first corrects a framing: "We make one point clear right from the beginning." Column generation, once called generalized linear programming, "never became competitive for solving linear programs, except for special cases", and tailored LP implementations did not beat the simplex method; "In contrast, column generation is a real winner in the context of integer programming" (p. 2). "We cannot overstress the fact that the primary use of column generation is in this context", that is, inside branch-and-price (p. 5). Problem statement: master (1), restricted master and pricing problem (2)-(3) as an optimisation over a structured domain X (pp. 2-3).
- **D2 Core idea**: the dual view organises everything. The restricted master exists to deliver duals, and "we never need a primal feasible solution before optimality is reached, not even to calculate dual bounds" (p. 6). Pricing is separation for the dual, so column generation is a cutting-plane method for the Lagrangian dual (13); the instability of the duals explains tailing off and motivates stabilization (pp. 6, 9-10). The Dantzig-Wolfe and Lagrangian bounds coincide, L(π) = v(RMP) + v(PP) (p. 5).
  - Transferable: read each algorithmic choice in column generation as a choice about dual information, and ask what the restricted master contributes as a dual oracle (p. 6).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: an LP master with ≥ rows and many columns; X is finite in the notation, "but this needs not be the case" (p. 3). The Dantzig-Wolfe setting (6), with block-diagonal D (10) and staircase forms (nested column generation) (p. 4). For integer programs, the master bound beats the LP bound only if the subproblem lacks the integrality property (Geoffrion), which sets the trade-off "between a strong dual bound (by adding also complicating constraints to the subproblem) and the manageability of the subproblem (by avoiding this)" (p. 9). Examples: cutting stock (pp. 3, 12), set partitioning in vehicle routing and crew scheduling (p. 8).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Bounds and stopping: v(RMP) + κ·v(PP) ≤ v(MP) (5), which is not available when pricing is heuristic; the dual bound is not monotone (the "yo-yo effect", p. 3). With integer costs, stop when the rounded-up lower bound equals the rounded-up master value; for quick integer solutions, branch "as soon as column generation starts tailing off" and give the node its parent's bound, which "even is exact in principle" (p. 6).
  - An infeasible restricted master: big-M phase I, heuristic dual estimates, warm starts, or Farkas pricing, that is, pricing with zero costs against the dual ray; the method belongs to the "folklore" and was named within SCIP (pp. 6-7).
  - Master solvers: primal or dual simplex, sifting, barrier (no warm start "yet"), the analytic center cutting plane method, subgradient, bundle and volume methods. Switching from the simplex method to a subgradient method after some iterations "may considerably reduce computation times" (pp. 7-8). Row aggregation for degenerate set partitioning (p. 8).
  - Pricing: "Any variable with negative reduced cost will do", and heuristics are "first choice in terms of speed" (p. 8); partial and cyclic pricing; Devex, deepest-cut and lambda rules (pp. 8-9); dynamic programs and MIP solution pools return several columns at once (p. 9).
  - Stabilization (§3): interior points, Boxstep, piecewise-linear penalties (16)-(17) (five pieces "a good compromise"), bundle methods (a quadratic penalty), convex combinations with earlier duals, and dual-optimal inequalities (18) for cutting stock (pp. 10-12). For the convex combination, a misprice still improves the bound, so the gap shrinks by at least a factor (1 − α)^−1 (p. 12). Successful stabilization reduces iterations but makes pricing harder on average (p. 10).
  - Numerics: tolerances for "What is negative reduced cost?", for when primal and dual bounds match, and for the size of a perturbation, typically 10^−3 to 10^−6; comparing against 0.0 "easily ends up in an infinite loop"; ε-optimality is the rigorous route; exact rational arithmetic is reserved for mission-critical problems (p. 13).
  - Transferable: with integer costs, stop column generation once the rounded-up bound equals the rounded-up master value, and when a node tails off, branch early and inherit the parent's bound (p. 6).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: n/a. No experiments of its own. Literature results are reported with their source, for example restricted-master time reduced "by a factor of eight" on an instance with 1,600 set-partitioning constraints by dynamic aggregation [34] (p. 8), and "no clear winner" between bundle and classical stabilization [12] (p. 11).
- **D6 Positioning**: stabilization is credited to the non-linear programming world ("The principles are well-established", p. 10) and to non-differentiable convex analysis (p. 14); smoothing with the best dual is "a re-discovery" of Wentges' weighted Dantzig-Wolfe decomposition (p. 12); Vanderbeck [15] for stabilization as possibly the largest gain (p. 10). SCIP appears as a framework and as the origin of the name Farkas pricing (pp. 7, 13). GCG and the SEA 2010 paper are not mentioned. Reference [47] gives the 2005 survey as "J. Desrosiers and M.E. Lübbecke" (p. 17), as S007 does.
- **D7 Writing**: an emphatic, imperative register: "We make one point clear right from the beginning" (p. 2), "We cannot overstress" (p. 5), "This point cannot be overestimated." (p. 13). Section 4.3, "Some Simple Ideas which often Work", is a run of imperatives with the punchline repeated ("Again: Think of heuristics everywhere." and "Again, use heuristics first", pp. 13-14). Uncounted "often" about practice appears at least six times (pp. 5, 9, 13-14). That suits an encyclopedia entry, but not the skill's Mentor Voice rule that he never says "often" without counts. Manuscript slips: "Particular emphasis in put", "Langrangian", "some advise" (abstract, p. 1); "shear size" (p. 2). The conclusion reads "Still, column generation and branch-and-price are available as generic implementations, and we are eager to see this change." (p. 14); the next sentence, "Until this happens", implies a missing "not" [inferred].
- **D8 Contribution and limits**: expository: the algorithmic choices around the dual view, ending in implementation advice (abstract, p. 1). Open questions stated: selection criteria for columns, as for cuts, "remains to be seen"; characterisations of dual facets "do not appear to have any practical impact so far"; pricing rules aimed at stabilization would be interesting (p. 9); barrier has no warm start (p. 7). Agenda: generic implementations are wanted. Until then, tailoring to problems and even to instances is "questionable in terms of general purpose applicability" but "the driving force for pushing the border", and cuts and columns should be integrated ("the future will lie in integrating the two methods into one anyway", p. 14). One claim carries no count or citation: branch-and-price codes solve many large-scale problems that "no standard commercial solver could cope with" (p. 14).

**Method links**:
- H1, evidence ✅ (sole, 2010): heuristic pricing is "first choice in terms of speed" (p. 8); several columns per pricing call (p. 9); "Try to avoid solving the pricing problem to optimality too often. ### S035 · Integrated Sequencing and Scheduling in Coil Coating (Management Science 57(4) 2011, DOI 10.1287/mnsc.1100.1302)
**Read level**: full. All 20 pages, including the appendix proof of Theorem 2 (pp. 18-19) and the references. Formulas lose Greek letters and some symbols in the text layer (the parameter α, for example, is blank on p. 13), so formulas were read through the surrounding prose; Figures 8-11 are bar charts whose values are not in the text layer.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Wiebke Höhn, Felix G. König and Rolf H. Möhring (TU Berlin), and Lübbecke (RWTH Aachen), listed last (p. 1). Accepted by Dimitris Bertsimas, optimization (p. 1). Michael Bastubbe is among those thanked for help in implementing the branch-and-price and genetic algorithms (p. 17). · **Place in trajectory**: the TU Berlin applications period; the paper the skill cites for H2 (certificate before deployment). INDEX.md also lists a 2009 Dagstuhl version (D002), not read here.

- **D1 Problem and entry point**: an industrial integrated problem: sequence coils and schedule color tanks and setup work on a coil coating line with shuttle coaters, where a setup can run on an idle tank while the other tank coats (pp. 1-2). The entry is the application and its owner: the algorithm "has been added to PSI Metals' planning software suite" and runs at Salzgitter Flachstahl (SZFG) (p. 2); planners "require a plan covering 24–72 hours to be computed within 180 seconds" (p. 11). Literature gap: only Tang and Wang (2009), with tabu search on a model without shuttles; "The present work is the first incorporating shuttles and concurrent setup work in a thorough mathematical investigation" (p. 2). The objective is the makespan, "essentially equivalent to minimizing nonproductive time, or cost" (p. 3).
  - Transferable: enter an application through the owner's hard requirements (horizon, run time) and fix one objective they accept before modelling (pp. 3, 11).
- **D2 Core idea**: for a fixed sequence, model concurrent setup scheduling (CSS) as a maximum-weight independent set of "saving rectangles" (a tool interval combined with a setup interval) in a 2-union graph (pp. 5-8). This gives a polynomial dynamic program for any fixed number of shuttle coaters, NP-hardness when that number is part of the input, and a fast heuristic inspired by the dynamic program, embedded in a genetic algorithm for sequencing (p. 2). Plan quality is assessed by a lower bound from a combinatorial relaxation solved by branch-and-price (p. 2). Kept: standard metaheuristic machinery (a genetic algorithm, the Lin-Kernighan-Helsgaun heuristic for the local-cost ATSP; p. 12). Changed: setup cost is global (it depends on the whole tank history), and the interval model captures it (pp. 3-5).
  - Transferable: when a cost depends on long histories, encode each partial decision and its saving as an interval, and turn the subproblem into an independent-set problem on a structured intersection graph (pp. 5-7).
- **D3 Problem class and data model**: coils with a color per coater, a width and a processing time; a setup is a color change (different color) or a roller change (a wider coil after a narrower one on the same tank), each of duration t, (1) (p. 3); transition coils are local cost (p. 3). Plant rules: one team of workers, at most one concurrent setup per coater, none during transition coils, no preemption (p. 4). CSS is generalised to m cells with two tools each and r resources; the results hold for arbitrary setup times and any r, but "our results are strongly based on the number of tools being two" (p. 4). Data: real SZFG instances, long-term (up to 120 coils, 72 hours; 16 instances) and short-term (20-40 coils, at most 24 hours; number not stated); numbers are normalised "because of nondisclosure agreements" (p. 15).
- **D4 Algorithm and implementation design**:
  - Theory: Lemma 1 bounds the end points of setup intervals through the greatest common divisor of setup durations (pp. 7-8); Lemma 2 solves r ≥ m by interval graphs (p. 8); Theorem 1 shows that maximum independent set in m-composite 2-union graphs is strongly NP-hard, by reduction from 3SAT (pp. 9-10); Theorem 2 shows CSS NP-hard for any fixed r < m, via 1-in-3-3SAT (appendix, pp. 18-19); Theorem 3 gives a dynamic program whose states are m-tuples of nodes, as a longest path in a DAG of compatible states (pp. 10-11); Corollary 1 combines them (p. 11). Negative weights are removed by adding a constant weighted by length (p. 8).
  - Heuristic: the exact dynamic program is "obviously not feasible for practical use because of its runtime" (p. 12). Its idea becomes a heuristic that treats the coaters separately and adds α times the possible concurrent setup work to each tool interval's weight, α ∈ [0, 1]; then a maximum-weight independent set in an interval graph, and earliest-deadline-first scheduling of the setups (pp. 12-13). Baseline rule: SZFG's previous FIFO tank rule (p. 13).
  - Genetic algorithm: an initial population from sorting by triples of coil criteria, plus a Lin-Kernighan-Helsgaun tour, plus random sequences; 200 individuals, 100 new ones by mutation (inversion) and 100 by crossover per generation, the best 200 survive; when all are within 5% of the best, the worst 90% are replaced by a new initial population (pp. 11-12, 15).
  - Lower bound: concatenate subsequences of a fixed number of coils whose global cost is computed exactly ("don't look back too far"), with only local cost between them, as an integer program (4)-(11) with original position variables x_pj, subsequence columns z and link variables y (pp. 13-14). The bound is valid only for online tank rules, because only they concatenate, so it is computed for FIFO (pp. 13-14). Pricing is a depth-first enumeration of the O(n^σ) subsequences with a pruning rule based on the duals, since "there is no principal alternative to a brute force method" (p. 14). Branching on the original variables x_pj by modifying the pricing problem, artificial variables to stay feasible, initial columns from the heuristic solution, implemented in SCIP (pp. 14-15).
  - Transferable: derive the practical heuristic from the exact algorithm's state space: decouple what couples the dynamic program, and fold the coupling into a parameterised weight (pp. 12-13).
  - Transferable: before trusting a bound from a relaxation, state the condition under which its pieces concatenate (here, online assignment rules), and restrict the claim to it (pp. 13-14).
- **D5 Computational evaluation**: genetic-algorithm parameters were "determined by rigorous testing", aiming at one fixed set for all data (p. 15); a 180 s limit, with the best solutions of most short-term instances found within 30 s (p. 15). Baselines: plans of expert planners "actually gone to production", the FIFO rule, and trivial and TSP lower bounds (pp. 15-17). Results: makespan reductions of "up to 25%, on average over 13%" against the planners (p. 15). Long-term: with the best α from 0.1 to 0.8 per instance, the independent-set heuristic beats FIFO on 12 of 16 instances, cutting cost by up to 30% and makespan by up to 6%; with α fixed at 0.5 it is better on eight and worse by at most 1% in four (p. 15; Figs. 8-9, p. 16). Short-term: both rules are "on a par for most instances", with small gains in three cases (p. 16). Bounds: for all short-term instances the integer program proves the FIFO-based solutions within 10% of the optimal makespan under FIFO, with subsequences of at most six coils and not all integer programs solved to optimality (p. 16; Fig. 10, p. 17). Bound computation times were huge and are not reported (p. 15). No bound is reported for the long-term instances. Two further notes from my reading: the 10% is measured on makespan, which contains the fixed sum of processing times (the trivial bound, p. 13); for nonproductive time, Figure 11 shows the share of the gap closed, on an axis up to 0.8, with values not in the text layer (p. 17). Plans were verified on site (p. 15). No seeds or run-to-run variability of the genetic algorithm; "In most cases" the planners' plans were improved, without a count (p. 15).
  - Transferable: report the ex-post best parameter per instance next to one fixed default, and count where the default loses (p. 15).
- **D6 Positioning**: setup scheduling is surveyed by Allahverdi et al.; local setups reduce to ATSPs (Balas et al.); non-local setups are rare (Koulamas and Kyparisis); setups in common-server scheduling are local (Hall et al.)
