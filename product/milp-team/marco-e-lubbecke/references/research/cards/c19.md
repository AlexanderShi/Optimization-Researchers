### S089 · Branch-and-price strikes back for the k-vertex cut problem (arXiv preprint 2026, arXiv:2602.04984)
**Read**: full. Text of the v2 PDF (stamp "arXiv:2602.04984v2 [math.OC] 5 Jun 2026", p. 1), pp. 1–48: §§1–5 (pp. 1–32), references (pp. 32–33), Appendices A–E (pp. 34–38) and G (pp. 41–48) read in full. **Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Fabio Ciccarelli and Fabio Furini (Sapienza University of Rome), Christopher Hojny (TU Eindhoven), Lübbecke (RWTH Aachen) last (p. 1). **Voice**: joint "we" throughout; the file has no author-contribution statement, acknowledgment or AI-use declaration (grep). **Position in trajectory**: the 2024–26 line of tailored branch-price-and-cut built directly on SCIP (SKILL.md, Research Trajectory). The experiments ran on the cluster of the RWTH Chair of Operations Research (p. 22). The paper revisits two earlier exact methods whose author lists both include the co-author Furini: the branch-and-price of Cornaz et al. (2019) and the branch-and-cut of Furini et al. (2020) (references, pp. 32–33).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Problem: the weighted k-VCP, a minimum-cost vertex set whose removal leaves at least k connected components (Definition 1, p. 2). It is NP-hard when k is part of the input and for every fixed k ≥ 3, and polynomial for k = 2 (p. 3).
  - Entry: the three exact methods in the literature are CPLEX on a compact model, a branch-and-price on an extended model and a branch-and-cut on a natural model with exponentially many constraints, and they "still fail to solve many benchmark instances to optimality" (p. 3). The goal is an algorithm that "markedly improves upon the BP approach" (p. 3). The conclusions name the verdict being reopened: Furini et al. (2020) suggested that branch-and-price was not the right technique (p. 31, quote 1).
  - The cut: the authors look for "unused potential in an existing branch-and-price framework" (p. 31). Once the natural binaries are added to the old extended model, integrality on its columns is no longer needed (pp. 7–8).
  - Transferable: when a technique has been declared the wrong one for a problem, list what the losing implementation assumed (here binary columns, a two-level branching that breaks pricing, and a hand-written tree; pp. 6, 25–26) and test those assumptions one by one before accepting the verdict.
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Key idea: put the natural vertex-cut binaries x and the cluster columns λ into one model ILPE2, with x_v as the slack of the old vertex-packing row (4c) (p. 8), and prove that λ may be continuous (Theorem 1, p. 8; quote 2, p. 7).
  - *Kept* from Cornaz et al. (2019): cluster columns, clique rows that forbid adjacent clusters, singleton columns as the start, and min-cut pricing (pp. 4–6, 15, 17).
  - *Changed*: (i) λ ≥ 0 continuous with x binary (Theorem 1, p. 8). (ii) Branching only on x, with reliable pseudo-cost branching (Achterberg 2007), and no second Ryan–Foster level (p. 19); in the old scheme the "different-clusters" branch needs an incompatibility constraint, which "destroys the min-cut structure of the pricing problem" (p. 6). (iii) Equality rows relaxed to inequalities (Prop 3, p. 14). (iv) A greedily built edge-covering family of maximal cliques C′ instead of an edge-partitioning one (p. 15; ablation p. 24). (v) Symmetry handling on x (pp. 20–21), a connectivity cut and a constructive start heuristic (p. 22; Apps. C–D, pp. 35–37). (vi) SCIP instead of a custom branch-and-bound code (pp. 22–23, 26).
  - Why: with λ continuous, branching never touches columns and the pricing network only receives infinite-capacity arcs (p. 19). The new LP bound is not weaker than either old one (Theorem 3, p. 12). The authors also explain how the relaxed rows shorten column generation: the inequality form lets the master combine existing columns instead of waiting for a merged cluster, and keeps it feasible after branching without new columns (p. 25).
  - Transferable: when you add original variables to a column model, ask whether integrality can then be dropped on the columns. If it can, branch only on the original variables and the pricing problem keeps its structure (pp. 7–8, 19).
- **D3 Problem class & data model** (team.json):
  - Class: a pure binary ILP with exponentially many variables and constraints (p. 8). Definition 1 has non-negative vertex costs (p. 2), and the theorem statements allow c ∈ R^V (pp. 8, 10, 12–14). The proof of Prop 3, however, uses c_v > 0 ("because cv > 0", p. 14), so the stated hypothesis is weaker than the one used. This is harmless on the benchmark, whose costs are 1 or drawn from {1,…,10} (p. 38).
  - Correctness assumptions: Theorems 2 and 3 assume a feasible instance (pp. 10, 12). Feasibility holds if and only if G has a stable set of size k (Lemma 3, p. 15), and this is used to filter instances by α(G) ≥ k (p. 37).
  - Structures exploited: cliques of G (rows over all cliques, enough over maximal ones, p. 8); the forest polytope of the cycle matroid (Edmonds 1970; Lemma 1, p. 9; App. A, p. 34); a totally unimodular pricing matrix once the cardinality row is dropped (p. 16); graph automorphisms (Prop 5, p. 21).
  - Test data: 118 graphs (50 from the 2nd DIMACS challenge, "Coloring"; 9 from the 10th DIMACS challenge, "Partitioning"; 59 intersection graphs of de Souza and Balas 2005) with 11–297 vertices and 20–7763 edges, for k ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20} (p. 37). Fixings and elimination of trivial cases follow Furini et al. (2020) and leave 304 instances, each used unweighted and weighted (pp. 37–38; Table E.1, p. 38). The filtering and preprocessing are "implemented in Gurobi 11.0.3" and run offline (p. 38).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design, proofs and devices** (team.json):
  - Proof of Theorem 1, in three moves. First, Φ(x), the largest number of forest edges in the reduced graph, is the value of an LP, by Edmonds' description of the forest polytope (Lemma 1, p. 9; App. A, p. 34). Second, dualizing that LP turns the bilevel model (6) of Furini et al. into the single-level mixed-integer non-linear model (8) (pp. 9–10). Third, Theorem 2 constructs an optimal solution in which λ selects the connected components and β = x, which removes the bilinear terms (p. 10; App. A, p. 34). Corollary 1 merges components to obtain exactly k (pp. 10, 34–35), and the leftover rows are shown redundant by summing (9e) and substituting (p. 11).
  - LP dominance (Theorem 3, p. 12): Lemma 2 counts forest edges inside each column (p. 12); Prop 1 sums the redundant edge rows over a forest to recover the natural model's inequalities (3b) (p. 12); Prop 2 eliminates x through (4c) and compares with LPE1, with equality when C′ contains all cliques (p. 13).
  - Prop 3 (p. 14): the equalities are relaxed and the clique rows restricted to an edge-covering family; the last step is an optimality argument that uses c_v > 0 (p. 14).
  - Restricted master: all x and the singleton columns, which is feasible exactly when a k-vertex cut exists (Prop 4, p. 15); x ≤ 1 is not imposed (p. 16). The cover by maximal cliques is built greedily in the input order and has at most |E| cliques (p. 15).
  - Pricing (pp. 16–19): maximize µ(S) minus the duals of the cliques hit, with S ≠ ∅. Without the cardinality row the matrix is totally unimodular, so pricing is polynomial through |V| LPs, and by the equivalence of optimization and separation so is the master LP (pp. 16–17). In practice the min-cut network of Barahona and Jensen (1998) is used (p. 17), with a second stage when the first cut returns S = ∅: |V| further min cuts, each with the arc (s, v) raised by the dual σ (p. 18; worked example in Figure 3, pp. 18–19).
  - Branching (p. 19): x_v = 1 adds an infinite-capacity arc (v, t); x_v = 0 adds infinite-capacity arcs (w, v) from every neighbour w, so S cannot contain a neighbour of v without v. In the ILP form of the pricing these become fixings and precedence rows that keep total unimodularity (p. 19).
  - Symmetry (pp. 20–21): graph automorphisms are symmetries of ILP⋆E2 (Prop 5, p. 21; proof App. A, p. 35). The lexicographic order is defined on x only, ignoring the λ that are not known explicitly (p. 21). Of two approaches, global lexicographic reduction and a partial order along the branching variables (after Ostrowski 2009) with orbital fixing, "Preliminary experiments" favoured the second, and only it is analysed (p. 21). How the automorphism set Γ is computed is not stated (p. 21).
  - Connectivity cut (13): the total cut cost is at least the weighted vertex connectivity, computed by vertex-split min cuts over all non-adjacent pairs (App. C, pp. 35–36). It is switched on only for k ≤ 15, after preliminary experiments (p. 36). The start heuristic IDH repeatedly deletes the cheapest separator of the component with the smallest weighted connectivity, and its result is optimal if one round suffices (App. D, pp. 36–37).
  - Implementation: C++ on SCIP 9.2.3 with custom plugins, SoPlex 7.1.5 as LP solver, LEMON for max flow (p. 23). No tolerance, numerical safeguard or exact arithmetic is mentioned (grep).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Before branching on columns, test whether the column variables can be continuous once the original binaries are in the model. Construct an integral optimal solution explicitly (here the connected components as columns), then enforce integrality on the original variables only (Theorems 1–2, pp. 8–11, 34–35).
- Turn a bilevel natural model into an extended formulation: write the follower's value as an LP over an integral polytope, dualize it, and remove the bilinear terms by exhibiting an optimal solution in which the duals equal the leader variables (pp. 9–10; App. A, p. 34).
- Relax master equalities to inequalities when every optimal solution meets them anyway, prove it with the cost signs, and measure the effect on column generation (Prop 3, p. 14; p. 24).
- Min-cut pricing that may return the empty set: rerun |V| min cuts, each with the source arc of one vertex raised by σ, the dual of the master row Σλ_S ≥ k (10b) (pp. 16–18).
- Encode branching on original binaries as infinite-capacity arcs in the pricing network (x_v = 1: arc (v, t); x_v = 0: arcs into v from every neighbour), so the pricing stays a min cut (p. 19).
- Inside branch-and-price, define the symmetry handling on the original variables only: a lexicographic order on x, a partial order along the branching variables, orbital fixing (pp. 20–21).
- Re-run every competitor on your hardware with the same limit, one thread and a current LP solver; ask the authors for unpublished code; explain the gap to their published counts (pp. 22–23, 27–28).
- Add a control arm that copies the competitor's choice for one component, to separate the effect of your formulation from that of the component (p. 24).
- Show that pricing is not the bottleneck: report the share of time in pricing and the numbers of root, Farkas and total columns (Table 1, p. 27).
- Define *newly closed* against the union of all methods in the reference study, and list in an appendix every instance that others solve and you do not (pp. 29, 42).
- Filter infeasible instances with a polynomial certificate (here α(G) ≥ k by Lemma 3), and start the restricted master so that it is feasible exactly when the instance is (pp. 15, 37).
- Ablate the construction of the initial row family (edge-covering cliques, edge-partitioning cliques, single edges) on both solved counts and root gaps (pp. 24, 30–31).

### S115 · The Multi-Stop Station Location Problem: Exact Approaches (SSRN preprint n.d., DOI 10.2139/ssrn.7177419)

Pp. 1–41 read: §§1–5 (pp. 1–30), CRediT, funding, data and AI statements (pp. 31–32), references (pp. 32–36), and Appendix Tables A.1–A.4 (pp. 37–40), which were also recounted by script; Figure A.1 (p. 41) was read from its captions. The software versions (Gurobi 9.5.2, SCIP 8.0.2, p. 22) and the 2023 visit dates of the software references (pp. 32–35) suggest that the campaign is older than the text [inferred]. The 2024 Optimization Online version was not read.
**Role**: core · **Coauthors**: Erik Mühmer (corresponding), Miriam Ganz and Felix J. L. Willamowski, all at the RWTH Chair of Operations Research with Lübbecke (p. 1). **Voice**: joint "we". The CRediT statement names Lübbecke for "Conceptualization, methodology, supervision, writing - review and editing" and Mühmer for, among others, software, data curation and the original draft (p. 31), so no passage is Lübbecke's own and the code is the first author's. **Position in trajectory**: the chair's own problem, introduced in repORt 2020–60 by Willamowski, Ganz and Mühmer (pp. 5, 36), and one of the two 2024–26 tailored branch-price-and-cut studies on SCIP (SKILL.md, Research Trajectory). The work had no specific funding (p. 31).

- **D1 Problem & entry**:
  - Application entry: siting charging infrastructure for electric intercity buses, where the operator trades infrastructure cost against detours (p. 2).
  - The gap: three structural features (ordered multi-stop trips, shared station decisions, detours between consecutive stops) are "not captured directly" by origin-destination refueling, flow-covering or relay models (p. 2). Four categories of literature are contrasted (pp. 3–5), and the instance of Figure 1 separates them: a coverage approach needs at least three stations, the MSLP two (p. 5).
  - Source of the problem: the chair's own definition and approximation algorithm (Willamowski, Ganz and Mühmer 2020; p. 5).
  - Scope: the authors "deliberately adopt a foundational scope" and leave out charging times, timetable consistency, demand feedback and limited charging resources (p. 3).
  - Transferable: separate your problem from its neighbours with one small instance on which their optimal solutions differ (Figure 1, p. 5).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**:
  - Compact model first: the arc-based MILP AA (2)–(14), with entering, leaving, station-to-station and shortcut arc variables per trip segment, station variables and continuous remaining-range variables (pp. 7–8).
  - Three reformulations of AA (pp. 9–13). AT decomposes by trips, a "slightly modified Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation" (p. 9): its master has trip-covering and station-linking rows, and its pricing problem is a single-trip MSLP. AS decomposes by segments: its master keeps the station, shortcut and remaining-range variables and the range-coupling rows, and its pricing problem is a shortest path (pp. 11–13). AN applies AS inside AT's pricing (p. 13). What AT modifies is not spelled out (p. 9); its pricing problem carries its own copy of the station variables (21), (24), (32) (p. 11).
  - Why: AA has O(N²M) variables and O(NM) constraints (p. 9); AS was tried "because the resulting pricing problems can be solved very efficiently" (p. 11). The outer master of AT and AN seldom needs branching (8.7% and 14.8% of instances, median 2 nodes when it does), whereas AS branches on 96.4% of instances (median 921 nodes) (p. 26). AN's advantage over AT "is primarily due to the nested pricing approach" (p. 26).
  - Transferable: when one reformulation has the strong master and another the cheap pricing, nest them: use the cheap one to solve the strong one's pricing problem (p. 13; payoff p. 26).
- **D3 Problem class & data model** (team.json):
  - Form: binary arc, shortcut and station variables with continuous remaining-range variables; the range-coupling rows (8) link r to the shortcut variable with coefficient b_t + ℓ̄ (p. 8). The master rows (16) and (35) are written in covering form, justified by non-negative costs (pp. 10, 12).
  - Data model: a complete directed auxiliary graph of precomputed routes, with separate costs c^E and lengths ℓ, a candidate station set F, and a range b_t per trip (pp. 5–6). c^E is assumed to satisfy the triangle inequality (p. 6). Full recharging, linear driving costs and no station capacities are kept and called "common in the literature" (p. 3).
  - Synthetic instances: TSPLIB graphs with at most 500 nodes, ℓ = c^E, F = V, and a uniform station cost 5·c_trips/|F|, so that 20% of the stations cost as much as all trips without detours (p. 23). The number of stops per trip is uniform in [2, √|V|]; stops are drawn uniformly (I1) or from a normal distribution over the distances to the last three stops (I2) (pp. 23–24). The range is b_t = ⌊(2/3)·ℓ_trips/|T|⌋ (p. 24).
  - Real instance: GTFS intercity bus data (Europe, January to March 2020), stops clustered by postal code and 10 km, a 250 km range, and 1644 nodes, 2408 trips and 17405 segments; lengths in kilometres, costs in minutes, both rounded down (p. 24). One station costs 65, 15% of the mean trip time (p. 24). The authors note: "Minor numerical deviations from the triangle inequality may therefore remain" (p. 24).
  - Correctness assumptions: the preprocessing "exploits the triangle inequality" (p. 21) and is used "throughout the computational study" (p. 22). The text does not say whether the GTFS optimum (p. 27) is optimal for the unreduced instance when that inequality fails.
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design, proofs and devices** (team.json):
  - Primal heuristic: adapted from the chair's approximation algorithm, it computes one shortest feasible path per trip on a graph built from the allowed stations, with station costs set to 0 (p. 14). For a fixed station set it is optimal (p. 15). Stations are allowed when their LP value is at least τ = 0.5, a threshold chosen by preliminary tests (p. 15). Columns the heuristic needs but the master lacks are generated (p. 15).
  - Farkas pricing by the heuristic, which "produces a feasible solution if and only if the problem instance is feasible" (p. 15), with several station-cost settings at the start (p. 15).
  - Reduced-cost pricing: the heuristic is called at every root iteration for AT and the outer AN, at most once per node for AS (p. 15). No pricing is needed once all station variables are fixed (p. 16), and the Lagrangian lower bound is updated in every iteration (p. 16).
  - AS pricing is a shortest path: the sign restrictions of the duals make all arc lengths non-negative, so no negative cycles occur (p. 13). This still holds with cuts, whose duals are ≤ 0 (p. 17), and branching is enforced by deleting edges (p. 19).
  - Cuts in AS's master: cover-like range cuts (50)–(52), valid because every feasible integer path has a segment between s1 and s2 with all related variables zero (pp. 16–17). They can be derived from the aggregated range rows (37)–(39) with Wolsey's (1990) GUB knapsack argument (p. 17) and are translated to AA as (53) (p. 17). Separation is a single-pass greedy scan that "may miss violated cuts" (p. 18).
  - Branching: on station variables (AT, AS, AN), on shortcut variables (AS), and a three-way distance branching for AS in which b1 + b2 + ℓ̄ = b_t, so that no feasible solution is lost (pp. 18–19). Completeness: for AT, once the station variables are integral, LP optimality gives equal costs to all positive patterns of a trip, so one can be picked (p. 20); for AS, cuts, distance branching and shortcut branching together remove every fractional path that is infeasible (p. 20). Selection uses a hierarchy (station, distance, shortcut) for multi-trip AS and a collapsed one for single-trip problems (pp. 20–21), with pseudo-costs and strong branching until a candidate has been branched on five times (p. 21).
  - Preprocessing: station–segment assignments that are dominated by the heuristic's solution are forbidden, using the triangle inequality (pp. 21–22).
  - Implementation (pp. 22–23): AA in Gurobi 9.5.2, with the heuristic (once per node) and the cuts added through callbacks; branch-price-and-cut on SCIP 8.0.2 with a custom pricer, branching rule, constraint handler and separator, SCIP's default separators disabled; Boost Graph Library 1.75; OpenMP. Pricing runs in parallel, but new columns enter "in a deterministic order" (p. 23). AT's pricing problems are solved by Gurobi with the AA code, and AN's by AS (p. 23). Nothing on tolerances apart from the GTFS rounding (p. 24).
- **D5 Computational evaluation** (team.json):
  - Test set: one instance per TSPLIB graph, trip count (10, 20, 30, 40) and family (I1, I2) (p. 23). The text gives shares, not the total. My recount of Tables A.1 and A.3 finds 66 graphs, so 528 instances (pp. 37, 39).
  - Baseline: AA, the compact model in Gurobi 9.5.2 with the same heuristic and cuts (pp. 17, 22). The hardware is Debian 11 nodes with two Xeon L5630 (16 logical processors) and 128 GB, with a one-hour limit (p. 24). Thread settings for Gurobi and for the OpenMP parts are not stated (pp. 22–24).
  - Metrics: performance profiles on time (Figure 8, p. 25; per family and trip count, Figure A.1, p. 41), solved shares (Figure 9, p. 26), box plots of the remaining relative gap, with infeasible instances counted as 0% (p. 25; Figure 10, p. 27), branching incidence and node counts (Figure 11, p. 28), and per-instance times and gaps (Tables A.1–A.4, pp. 37–40).
  - Results: AN solves 82.4% and AS 77.8% (p. 25); AN is fastest on 44.1% and AS on 42.6% (p. 25). AS is stronger on I1 (78.8–100.0% per trip count) and AN on I2 (83.3–97.0%) (pp. 25–26). AA cannot reach a bound on some instances (p. 26).
  - Recount (Tables A.1 and A.3, pp. 37, 39): it reproduces 82.4% (435) and 77.8% (411) and the per-family ranges above, and gives AT 304 (57.6%) and AA 260 (49.2%). AA solves more I1 instances than AT at 10 and 20 trips (53 vs 40; 35 vs 32).
  - Real instance: only AN, without a time limit, on an i7-8700 workstation (12 logical processors, 32 GB): 40.06 hours, "with an optimal and feasible solution" (pp. 24, 27). AA could not be run for memory (p. 27). Diagnostics: 297 stations, a mean relative increase of 1.21%, a median of 0.22%, a 331-minute worst case, and seven detour areas holding 44.1% of the extra time (pp. 27–30).
  - Ablations are not reported. The cuts are an "integral part" (p. 23): "Preliminary runs without these cuts led to a severe degradation in performance, so we do not report this variant separately." (p. 23). τ, the heuristic frequency and the branching hierarchy come from preliminary tests (pp. 15, 21). No seed is given for the random generator, and variability is not studied (grep).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Derive every reformulation as a Dantzig-Wolfe reformulation of your compact model and show its blocks in the permuted coefficient matrix (Figures 3 and 5, pp. 9, 14).
- When a pricing problem is itself a structured MILP, decompose it again with a nested column generation and keep the stronger outer master (AN; pp. 13, 26).
- Write convexity-type master rows in covering form when costs are non-negative, and say why equality holds at the optimum (pp. 10, 12).
- Keep shortest-path pricing valid under cuts and branching: check that the duals' signs give non-negative arc lengths, and enforce branching decisions by deleting edges (pp. 13, 17, 19).
- Use a heuristic that finds a feasible solution exactly when one exists as your Farkas pricing (p. 15).
- Call the heuristic from inside pricing with the stations whose LP value is at least τ, and skip pricing once all station variables are fixed (pp. 15–16).
- Derive cover cuts by aggregating range-coupling rows into a GUB knapsack (Wolsey 1990), and translate them to the compact model so that the baseline gets them too (p. 17).
- Split a length budget in a three-way branching (b1 + b2 + ℓ̄ = b_t), so that each child forbids one part of an infeasible fractional path (p. 19).
- Run pricing in parallel, but insert the new columns in a deterministic order (p. 23).
- Report branching incidence and node counts conditional on branching, to show which reformulation's master is stronger (Figure 11, p. 26).
- Read the optimal solution of a real instance as a planning diagnostic: an ECDF of relative increases, the tail of the worst trips, and recurring detour areas with their cumulative shares (pp. 27–30).
- State the evaluation questions before the results (p. 24), and list both the abstracted features and the graded extensions (pp. 3, 30).
