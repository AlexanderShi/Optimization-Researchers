### S036 · Shunting minimal rail car allocation (Computational Optimization and Applications 31(3) 2005, DOI 10.1007/s10589-005-3229-y)
**Read level**: full. All 12 file pages: abstract and §1 (pp. 1–3), §§2–4 models, lemmas and extensions (pp. 3–9), §5 computation and conclusions (pp. 9–10), acknowledgments and Tables 1–5 (pp. 10–11), references (p. 12). The file is the authors' preprint dated June 19, 2003, revised July 21, 2004 (p. 1), not the typeset article; Figures 1–3 survive only as captions and cost labels (pp. 5–6).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Uwe T. Zimmermann (Braunschweig); Lübbecke at TU Berlin and corresponding author (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the car-allocation side of the in-plant railroad application; locomotive scheduling is left to "our previous work [11]" (Transportation Sci. 2003) (pp. 2, 12), the line SKILL.md's trajectory dates to 1996–2001. Funded by BMBF grant 03-ZIM2BS, with data and insight from CSC Ploenzke and Dortmunder Eisenbahn (p. 10).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Requests at an in-plant railroad name a track, a car type and a quantity; cars come from stock, possibly substituted, or are rented, and the task is to design the "blocks" of work at minimal total transportation cost (abstract, p. 1; pp. 1–2). Entry from practice: planning is first come first served and software only administrative (p. 2); the in-plant combination of empty-car distribution, blocking and shunting has "not been mentioned, let alone solved" in OR beyond one 1956 paper (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Decompose along the planners' own regions (p. 3). An upper-level transportation problem (1)–(4) distributes cars among regions (p. 3); a lower-level shunting MIP (9)–(14) per region picks groups of same-coloured cars from stack-like tracks (pp. 4–7). The intuitive objective (8) is nonlinear; the linear objective (9) overcounts the unused cars of each deepest accessed group, but an optimal z already encodes a cheapest allocation and the y values are recovered greedily (p. 7). An integrated model (15)–(20) is built "for reasons of benchmarking" (p. 8). Detail is limited on purpose, since too detailed suggestions "cannot be implemented in practice" (p. 3; also p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: an integer transportation problem, solvable in polynomial time (p. 3); binary group-access variables z with the precedence rows z_{t,g} ≤ z_{t,g−1} (10) and continuous y (p. 7); optional region-limit binaries w (5)–(7) (p. 4) and locomotive-capacity rows (21), which destroy the transportation substructure (p. 9). In the integrated model x stays continuous because any binary z leaves a transportation problem, a point an anonymous referee raised (pp. 8, 10). Stated assumptions: no limit on intermediate storage, no cost for pushing cars back, no prescribed train order (p. 5); a static model with at most three blocks per request and no transshipment (pp. 8–9).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design (brief)**: complexity first: Lemma 1, weakly NP-complete by a reduction from partition (p. 4); Lemma 2, strongly NP-complete by a reduction from vertex cover with one "black" car at the bottom of every track (p. 5); Corollary 3, no 7/6 − ε approximation (p. 6). Greedy has no constant factor (bad instance, Fig. 3), the "helicopter" relaxation gives only the trivial factor C, and a dynamic program over position vectors costs O(T^C) (p. 6). The MIPs go to CPLEX 8.0 directly (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational evaluation (one line)**: one steel-mill railroad (683 tracks, 42 regions, about 1500 cars of 126 types), two shifts (de1, de2) and three derived instances: dens duplicates cars, load merges both shifts, perm permutes cars randomly "to destroy the manual preordering by the planner" (p. 9); CPLEX 8.0 on a standard Linux PC, every model within two seconds (p. 9); baseline an emulated FIFO planning, which raises transportation cost by 10% and shunting by one third on average (p. 9; Table 2, p. 11); ablations of car substitution and of the region limit W, where W = 2 is nearly as good as no limit (p. 10; Tables 3–4, p. 11); the integrated model has root gaps of at most 0.0217% and runs in 0.06–0.36 s against 1.32–1.56 s for the decomposed one (Table 5, p. 11); swapping the cost weights leaves the shunting effort "almost unchanged" (p. 9). No failures, seeds or variability.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: models, complexity results and an application study whose results encourage "an installation in practice" (abstract, p. 1). Limits are concrete: absolute savings depend on the cost scaling (p. 10), the transitive substitution classes do not yet match the planner's knowledge (p. 10), and the model is static and ignores locomotive availability and transshipment (pp. 8–9). Future work: optimal shunting of incoming trains (p. 10).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 variant ⚠: the integrated (monolithic) model is built as the benchmark, and it wins: it is faster than the decomposed model and saves almost the whole difference between the shunting cost "lower" and its bound "llower" (pp. 8, 10; Tables 1, 5, p. 11). The decomposition is kept anyway because it mirrors the solution structure planners are used to and needs no commercial MIP solver (pp. 8, 10). The yardstick role matches step 2; the reason to decompose is a planning hierarchy, not a measured bound gain, and the decomposition is not Dantzig-Wolfe.
- Method 3 variant ⚠: the baseline is an emulation of current practice plus one-feature ablations (pp. 9–10), and the savings are hedged by the cost scaling (p. 10); the test set is five instances from one plant (p. 9).
- H3 evidence ✅ (early form): what is truly required is read off practice, detail that planners cannot enforce is left out (pp. 3, 5), and the choice between integrated and decomposed model "has to be decided by planners" (p. 8).
- G1 evidence ✅: the LP root bound is offered "as an estimate of the quality of some given solution" (p. 10), and llower is a computed lower bound on shunting (p. 9).
- new-pattern candidate: practice-emulation baseline (p. 9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Emulate the practitioner's current procedure inside your own model (here each request solved optimally, but one at a time, FIFO) and report the savings as an interval between that emulation and full simultaneous planning (pp. 9–10).
- Derive stress instances from one real data set, each built to break one property: duplicated supply (dens), a merged horizon (load), randomly permuted positions that remove the planner's manual preordering (perm) (p. 9).
- When the natural objective is nonlinear, try a linear objective that overcounts in a way the optimal binary pattern does not care about, and recover the continuous part greedily afterwards (p. 7).
- In an integrated model, check whether fixing the binaries leaves a transportation problem; if so, the remaining variables can stay continuous (p. 8).

**Quotes**:
- "one should not try to make suggestions on a very high level of detail. It turns out that such suggestions cannot be implemented in practice." (p. 3)
- "Not least for reasons of benchmarking, it is interesting to simultaneously model the hitherto separated problem levels" (p. 8)

### S053 · Rectangle covers revisited computationally (Experimental and Efficient Algorithms, WEA 2005, LNCS 3503, pp. 55–66, 2005, DOI 10.1007/11427186_7)
**Read level**: full. All 12 file pages (printed pp. 55–66): abstract and introduction (pp. 1–3), the IP pair and fractional solutions (pp. 3–6), LP-based approximation ideas (pp. 6–10), computation (pp. 10–11), conclusions, acknowledgments and references (pp. 11–12). Figures 1–3 survive as captions only (pp. 5, 6, 9).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Laura Heinrich-Litan (TU Braunschweig); Lübbecke at TU Berlin (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the extended abstract of S041 (S041 p. 1); algorithmic geometry alongside his column-generation work; Sándor Fekete is thanked for discussions (p. 12). That the code and instances of this paper could still be sent on request in 2022 is a self-report recorded in 02-methodology.md, not in this text.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Enters from a long-open question in computational geometry, whether rectangle cover has a constant-factor approximation, with Erdős's question whether θ/α is bounded (pp. 1–2). The reply offered is "computationally, yes" (p. 1). The authors call theirs the first exact algorithm and the first use of LP/IP techniques in the area (p. 2), and note that a published 21/17 example "cannot be verified" (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Model the cover as set cover over maximal rectangles, the cliques of the pixel visibility graph; there are only quadratically many, so the LP relaxation is polynomial (p. 3). Its formal dual is the stable-set IP, and the LP optimum, the fractional stable set size ᾱ ≥ α, is the stronger lower bound (pp. 3–4). Fractional solutions are read for structure: in Szemerédi's example the odd hole shows up as variables at 0.5 (pp. 4–5), which gives the odd-hole inequality (Lemma 1, p. 5). The dual view yields a primal-dual scheme (pp. 6–7), dual fitting in which a pseudo stable set pays only for a constant fraction of the cover, called new (p. 7), and Quasi-Greedy with a dual counterpart (p. 8).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: a pure 0/1 set-covering IP; polygons as pixel sets, and only maximal rectangles, which loses nothing because rectangles are counted, not areas (p. 3); the IP extends to the weighted case and to covering only corners or the boundary (p. 3); for perfect visibility graphs the LP is integral (p. 4). Instances: VLSI masks, CCITT fax images, black-and-white images and two kinds of random polygons (p. 10).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design (brief)**: Lemma 1 (validity of the odd-hole row) and Lemma 2 (each rectangle of an optimal cover has a private pixel, by contradiction) (p. 5); no odd antihole on 7 vertices is representable, so odd holes are the only source of imperfection (p. 5); a 2-approximation for thin polygons and a constant factor for bounded-width polygons (p. 7); the chain |C| ≤ |S| ≤ c·ᾱ = c·θ̄ ≤ c·θ (9) (p. 7); a counterexample showing that boundary covers cannot bound interior covers (Fig. 3, p. 9); hole breaking sketched (pp. 9–10). Beyond these, results are only sketched for space (pp. 2–3).
- **D5 Computational evaluation (one line)**: 16 named instances in Table 1 with LP–IP gaps of at most 0.039% (p. 10); random polygons only as "thousands of random polygons (not listed here)" (p. 10); CPLEX (the 9.0 manual is cited) solves in a few seconds while generating the IP takes much longer (pp. 11–12); Quasi-Greedy against the optimum in Table 2 (p. 11); no timing table and no machine beyond "a standard PC" (p. 4). Two table errors: the Table 2 caption says the covers "differ by only 3–7%", while its rows give 0–11.3% (VLSI2: 4701 against 4224), mostly below 3%; and the marbles row lists 56354, the rectangle count of Table 1, as the optimum (pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: an exact IP, a dual-based lower bound and LP routes toward a constant factor; "A fruitful contribution of our work is a number of open questions it spawns" (p. 3). The conclusion restates Erdős's question as whether both integrality gaps are bounded by a constant, with lower bounds 16/15 and 15/14 conjectured tight (p. 11). Limits: memory for very large instances (p. 4) and IP generation time, with column generation proposed as the remedy (p. 11).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 evidence ✅ (Exit B, without an enumerated option space): computed solutions are read for structure, one class is proved (thin and bounded-width polygons, p. 7), and the rest goes out as conjectures and open questions (pp. 4–5, 9, 11); the conclusion names the reversal of theory and computation (p. 11).
- Method 3 contradiction ✗ (step 6 and the checkpoint): the Table 2 caption does not match its rows, random-instance results are asserted but not shown (pp. 10–11), and "computationally, yes" answers an approximability question with experiments (p. 1). S041 corrects the first two.
- Method 1 variant ⚠: the compact set-cover IP is solved first; column generation is proposed only once generating the IP, not solving it, is the bottleneck (p. 11).
- Method 4 variant ⚠ (step 5 only): publishing what stays open is part of a research paper here, not of a survey (pp. 3, 11).
- G1 evidence ✅: "provably optimal solutions" (abstract, p. 1) and a stronger LP lower bound as the contribution (p. 2).
- new-pattern candidates: first exact IP in a theory-dominated area (p. 2); LP relaxation as approximation-design instrument (pp. 6–9).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Solve the primal IP and its dual IP on every instance and print both LP and IP values side by side; LP duality makes the common LP value a lower bound for any heuristic (pp. 3–4; Table 1, p. 10).
- Look at the fractional LP solution of a known counterexample: variables at 0.5 on an odd hole point to the valid inequality to add (pp. 4–5).
- Restate an open combinatorial question as a question about the integrality gaps of an IP pair, with a small example that gives explicit lower bounds on both gaps (p. 11).
- For a dual-fitting argument, measure on every instance the two quantities that would give the factor (most pseudo-stable pixels in one rectangle; share of the cover paid for) (Table 2, p. 11).

**Quotes**:
- "we are the first to introduce linear/integer programming techniques in this problem area" (p. 2)
- "It is common that theory is complemented by computational experience. In this paper we did the reverse: We found promising research directions by a careful study of computational experiments." (p. 11)

### S112 · On Minimum Monotone and Unimodal Partitions of Permutations (TU Berlin preprint, DepositOnce 2005, DOI 10.14279/depositonce-14343)
**Read level**: full. All 13 file pages: the extended abstract (pp. 1–8), references (p. 9), and "Additional Material for the Referees" with the proofs of Theorem 1 and Lemma 1 and Figures 1–4 (pp. 10–13). The referee appendix marks the file as a conference submission; the published LATIN 2006 paper is S046.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Gabriele Di Stefano (L'Aquila), Stefan Krause and Uwe T. Zimmermann (Braunschweig); Lübbecke at TU Berlin (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: precursor of S046 and of the journal version S051 (JDA 2008, not read in this batch); motivated by train and tram depot problems from the cited literature [3, 6, 13] (p. 2).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Partitioning a permutation into a minimum number of monotone subsequences is NP-hard; the paper extends this to unimodal subsequences and asks for exact, approximate and online algorithms (abstract, p. 1; pp. 1–2). The application is assigning arriving trains or trams to depot tracks with one or two open ends (p. 2); in graph terms the problems are cocoloring of permutation graphs (pp. 2–3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Start from a min-cost-flow LP for the easy one-direction case, with a lower capacity of 1 on one arc per element (p. 4), "this expensive approach" chosen because it extends (p. 4). Two complementary copies of the network, joined by binaries x_i + x'_i = 1 that decide which copy carries an element's lower capacity, model monotone covers (p. 4); removing some source and sink arcs and linking the copies models unimodal covers (p. 5). Rounding the switch variables at 0.5 and re-solving the fixed LP is a 2-approximation (Lemma 2, p. 5).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: a MIP with binary switches over an acyclic flow network; correctness rests on path decomposition of acyclic flows (p. 4) and on the transitivity of the element order (p. 8). The theory holds for any sequence of distinct reals (p. 2).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design (brief)**: Theorem 1, strong NP-completeness by a reduction from a restricted monotone problem using "starter" point sets (p. 3; proof pp. 10–11); Lemma 1, approximation factors transfer between the two problems with a factor 2 (p. 3; proof p. 12); Lemma 2, the lower capacities are "at most doubled" by rounding (p. 5); an integrality-gap witness [6, 2, 1, 4, 3, 5] with LP value 2 and IP value 3 (p. 6); Theorem 2, an adversary that bisects the range of unseen numbers gives a logarithmic lower bound on the competitive ratio, printed as log2(n − 1)/3, under the assumption that a subsequence's direction is fixed with its first element (p. 6); Next Fit and Best Fit are n/4-competitive, Best Fit stated without proof (p. 7).
- **D5 Computational evaluation (one line)**: prose only: random permutations from Knuth's shuffle with up to 120 elements are solved optimally "within a few seconds or minutes", LP rounding "significantly stays below" its factor 2, and the online algorithms behave as their analysis predicts; no tables, solver, hardware or counts (p. 8), more "in the full version" (pp. 7–8).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: complexity, the first exact approach, a 2-approximation and online bounds (abstract, p. 1); the hardness "legitimates" expensive MIPs (p. 7). Future work: a set covering formulation solved by column generation, with the flow models as subproblems, for practical side constraints such as bounded track length (p. 8); open questions on a PTAS, matching online bounds, the direction assumption and partially ordered sets (p. 8).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (step 3, early form): the compact flow MIP comes first; for side constraints the variables become whole subsequences priced by the flow model (p. 8). Steps 2 and 4, measuring the column model against the compact one, are not reported.
- Method 2 evidence ✅ (Exit B): the conjecture that the gap is below 2 comes "From our computational experience" (p. 6), followed by the list of open questions (p. 8).
- Method 3 contradiction ✗ (warning sign B2): small random instances and adverbs without counts ("significantly", "acceptable solution quality on the average and also in the (empirical) worst case") (p. 8).
- Method 4 variant ⚠ (step 5 only): "There remain several open questions, spawned by our work" closes a research paper (p. 8).
- H8 evidence ✅: a submission with a referee appendix (p. 10) that became the LATIN paper S046.
- new-pattern candidates: integer only where needed (binary switches over an integral flow core, pp. 4–5); LP relaxation as approximation-design instrument (p. 5).

**Transferable techniques**:
- Build the MIP for a hard covering problem from the LP of its easy special case: duplicate the network per subsequence type and let one binary per element choose which copy carries the element's lower capacity (pp. 4–5).
- Get an approximation factor from the same model: round the switch binaries at 1/2, re-solve the fixed LP, and bound the loss by how much the rounding raises the lower capacities (p. 5).
- Put a small explicit integrality-gap witness next to any conjecture about the gap (p. 6).
- For an online lower bound, let the adversary bisect the range of still-unseen numbers after each decision of the algorithm (p. 6).

**Quotes**:
- "Its LP relaxation can be solved via a dynamic column generation algorithm, where our network flow based models appear as subproblems." (p. 8)
- "Also, the proof relies on the assumption that the online algorithm has to immediately decide whether a subsequence will be increasing or decreasing. Can we drop this assumption?" (p. 8)

### S046 · On minimum k-modal partitions of permutations (LATIN 2006: Theoretical Informatics, LNCS 3887, pp. 374–385, 2006, DOI 10.1007/11682462_36)
**Read level**: full. All 12 file pages (printed pp. 374–385): abstract and introduction (pp. 1–3), complexity (pp. 4–5), MIPs (pp. 5–7), approximation (pp. 7–8), online algorithms (pp. 8–10), conclusions and references (pp. 10–12). Figures survive as captions.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Gabriele Di Stefano, Stefan Krause, Uwe T. Zimmermann (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the published version of S112; the journal version S051 (JDA 2008) is not read in this batch. This card records what changed against S112.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: as S112 (pp. 1–3), generalized to k-modal subsequences, with at most k internal extrema and the first one a maximum (pp. 2–3). New is a concession: the railway relation "may seem to be artificial", the paper "primarily" studies the theory, and the MIPs "can be tailored to fully capture the" real situation (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: the flow MIP extends to upper k-modal partitions with an extended network of k + 1 layers (p. 7); LP rounding gives a factor k + 1, because one of the k + 1 variables of each element has value at least 1/(k + 1) (p. 8); an easy 1.71(k + 1) factor via the Fomin–Kratsch–Novelli algorithm comes first (pp. 2, 7–8).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: as S112; the proofs are given for upper unimodal only, the k-modal details are left to "the full paper" (p. 4); polynomial solvability of the LPs is taken from the ellipsoid method (p. 8).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design (brief)**: Theorem 1, strong NP-hardness, now proved in the body (pp. 4–5); Lemma 1 with the factor k + 1 (pp. 7–8); Lemma 2 as in S112 (p. 8); Theorem 2 drops S112's direction assumption at the price of a weaker bound, log2(n + 1)/6 (p. 9). The proof first says that A generates "at least h/2 subsequences" and later "at least h subsequences"; the stated bound fits h/2 (p. 9). Best Fit's n/4 now has a proof with a tight instance (p. 10).
- **D5 Computational evaluation (one line)**: prose only, deferred to "the full paper": permutations of "more than 100 elements" solved optimally in seconds or minutes, and LP rounding "significantly stays below" its guarantee (pp. 10–11); no tables, solver or hardware.
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: three firsts in the abstract: provably optimal partitions, an approximation algorithm for k-modal partitions, online results for cocoloring permutation graphs (abstract, p. 1; p. 2). The set covering model "is able to capture more “dirty” side constraints" (p. 11). Open questions: a PTAS, matching online bounds and look-ahead, partially ordered sets (p. 11); S112's question about the direction assumption is gone, answered by the new Theorem 2 (p. 9).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 evidence ✅ (step 6, application claim): the authors themselves size the practical relevance (p. 3), and S112's open question returns as a theorem with a weaker constant, not as a stronger claim (p. 9).
- Method 3 contradiction ✗ (warning sign B2): computational claims without counts or instances, as in S112 (pp. 10–11).
- Method 2 evidence ✅ (Exit B): the gap conjecture "From our computational experience" (p. 8) and the open questions (p. 11).
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (step 3): the set covering model with flow subproblems, now justified by side constraints the compact model does not fit (p. 11); not measured here.
- Method 4 variant ⚠ (step 5 only): the open-question list stays in the published version (p. 11).
- H8 evidence ✅: LATIN 2006 extended abstract, with a full paper announced (pp. 4, 10).

**Transferable techniques**:
- When a proof needs an assumption a referee-stage version flagged as open, try to drop it and accept a weaker constant; state the new bound plainly (S112 p. 6, here p. 9).
- Say in the motivation how far the application link really goes, before a reviewer does (p. 3).
- Generalize a two-copy flow model to k + 1 layers and take the rounding factor from the pigeonhole on the k + 1 fractional values per element (pp. 7–8).

**Quotes**:
- "This relation may seem to be artificial, and we concede that the purpose of this paper primarily is to study the more theoretical background" (p. 3)
- "The theoretical hardness legitimates applying computationally expensive algorithms like solving (probably large scale) mixed integer programs." (p. 10)

### S041 · Rectangle covers revisited computationally (Journal of Experimental Algorithmics 11, 2.6, 2007, DOI 10.1145/1187436.1216583)
**Read level**: full. All 22 file pages of the manuscript "Submitted to ACM Journal on Experimental Algorithmics" (p. 1; copyright line "20YY"), not the published article: text pp. 1–13, Tables I–IV (pp. 14–17), acknowledgments and references (p. 18), Figs. 7–14 (pp. 19–22), of which only axes and captions survive.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Laura Heinrich-Litan (now at Advanced Driver Information Technology, Hildesheim); Lübbecke at TU Berlin (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the journal version of S053 (p. 1). This card records what changed.

- **D1 Problem & entry**: as S053 (pp. 1–2), sharpened: the main difficulty for approximation is good lower bounds (p. 3, citing Anil Kumar and Ramesh), and a constant factor is "the remaining white spot" of the complexity landscape (p. 2). A new aim: to raise awareness of mathematical programming in computational geometry (p. 3).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: the same IP pair (pp. 3–4). Added: iterated rounding after Jain, where every LP in the experiments had a variable of value at least 0.5 (pp. 6–7); by complementary slackness, private pixels are the only candidates for a fractional stable set (p. 6); a boundary-cover example that leaves an odd hole (Fig. 5, pp. 10–11); a study of corner and boundary covers (p. 13).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: as S053 (pp. 3–4); random polygons from two generators, flip-coin and union, 100 per setting, 1800 in all (pp. 12, 16).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design (brief)**: Lemmas 2.1 and 2.2 (pp. 5–6), the primal-dual conditions (7)–(8) (pp. 7–8), the dual-fitting chain (9) (p. 8); the caption of Fig. 4 adds a proven 5-approximation for that example (p. 11). The constant factor stays a conjecture (pp. 12–13, 16).
- **D5 Computational evaluation (one line)**: Tables I–II on 16 and 15 named instances, Tables III–IV on 1800 random polygons (pp. 14–17); gap zero for 1564 of 1800 (p. 12); CPLEX 9.1.0 (p. 16); the class that looks hardest, dense flip-coin polygons, pushed to 1000 × 1000 at 90% density in an attempt to refute the conjecture (p. 13); still no running times or hardware. Corrections against S053: ccitt3's optimum drops from 21086 to 21085 because of the solver's optimality tolerance (Table I caption, p. 14); the Quasi-Greedy caption now reads "typically around 1.01", with a ratio column (p. 15); the marbles row is fixed (p. 15). One slip remains: Table II still lists 21086 as ccitt3's optimum (p. 15).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: the conjecture moves from "could be an 8-approximation" (S053 p. 11) to an empirically supported (4 · 4/3) factor, because the pseudo stable sets "consistently pay for more than 75% of the cover size" (p. 13); θ ≤ 2·θ_corner appears to hold (p. 13); column generation is now "experimented with" for larger instances, without results shown (p. 16). The restated Erdős question and the 16/15, 15/14 conjecture stay (p. 16).

**Method links**:
- Method 3 evidence ✅ (steps 5–6, in the journal pass): a count (p. 12) replaces S053's "thousands" (S053 p. 10), the caption is made true to the table (p. 15), a published optimum is corrected with its cause named (p. 14), and instances are built to refute the authors' own conjecture (p. 13). What remains: the uncorrected Table II entry (p. 15), "overwhelming evidence" (p. 12), and a per-instance "consistently" over tables of averages (pp. 13, 17).
- Method 2 evidence ✅ (Exit B): "a geometric argument why this is always the case would be most interesting" (p. 7); conjectures read off the data (pp. 13, 16); the reversal of theory and computation is kept (p. 16).
- H6 evidence ✅: a number that looked fine came from an instrument setting, the solver's optimality tolerance, and the error is disclosed in print (p. 14).

### D001 · Minimizing the Stabbing Number of Matchings, Trees, and Triangulations (Discrete & Computational Geometry 40(4) 2008, DOI 10.1007/s00454-008-9114-6; arXiv cs/0310034)
**Read level**: full. All 25 file pages of arXiv cs/0310034v3 of 5 Sep 2008 (p. 1), not the typeset journal article: introduction and preliminaries (pp. 1–4), hardness (pp. 4–16), integer programs (pp. 17–18), iterated rounding (pp. 19–23), conclusion, acknowledgments and references (pp. 23–25). The gadget figures and the LP values of Fig. 11 survive only as captions and stray numbers.
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Sándor P. Fekete (Braunschweig), Henk Meijer (Roosevelt Academy, Middelburg) (p. 1) · **Position in trajectory**: the journal version of the SODA 2004 extended abstract (p. 1; S030, which has no open text in the index); Lübbecke's footnote credits a DFG travel grant for visits to Kingston and Stony Brook (p. 1).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: The complexity of a minimum-stabbing-number spanning tree is one of the original 30 problems of The Open Problems Project (abstract, p. 1; p. 2); earlier work studied extremal bounds, not algorithms (p. 2). Joe Mitchell is thanked "for pushing us to work on this problem" (p. 24).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: After hardness there are two ways on, lower bounds for approximation and optimality despite hardness, and one (integer) linear program is presented as serving both (p. 17). Matchings: Edmonds' perfect-matching constraints plus stabbing rows, the sum of edges a line meets at most k, minimizing k (pp. 17–18); trees: cut constraints plus stabbing rows (p. 18). The stabbing rows break Jain's 1/2 bound for a heaviest fractional edge (Fig. 10, pp. 18–19), so the paper proves its own: some optimal fractional solution has planar support, hence an edge of weight at least 1/5 for matchings and more than 1/3 for trees, which drives iterated rounding (pp. 19–23).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: point sets in the plane; a min-max objective over a polynomial set of representative lines, O(n) axis-parallel or O(n²) general (pp. 17–18); exponentially many blossom or cut constraints, separable in polynomial time (p. 18). The hardness constructions need collinear, degenerate point sets, while the LP results assume nothing about the points (p. 3; p. 23).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design (brief)**: 3SAT reductions with barrier gadgets, collinear rows that use up the whole stabbing budget in one direction (pp. 5–8, 9–12, 12–15); a triangle-counting lemma for triangulations (Lemma 4, pp. 8–9); an outdegree argument for tree barriers (Lemma 6, p. 12); pigeonhole for the crossing-number barrier (Lemma 10, pp. 15–16); perturbation by powers of ε to pass from axis-parallel to general lines (pp. 8, 15, 16); polynomial-time LP solution by separation and the ellipsoid method (p. 18); uncrossing: among optimal LP solutions take one of minimum total stabbing number and shift ε from a crossing pair to a non-crossing pair, with a case analysis for the blossom constraints (Lemma 13, pp. 19–22); the degree-5 vertex of a planar graph and the 3n − 6 edge bound (Theorem 15, p. 23). Two slips: p. 2 swaps the constants (1/3 for matchings, 1/5 for trees) against p. 3 and Theorem 15 (p. 23), and the tree proof ends on blossom inequalities where cut inequalities are meant (p. 22).
- **D5 Computational evaluation (one line)**: none in this paper: practice is reported in a separate technical report [12] (instances "up to several hundred vertices", p. 2; "Details are omitted from this theoretical paper", p. 3); one figure shows an LP solution on 100 random points (Fig. 11, p. 20).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: settles the complexity (NP-hardness for matchings, triangulations and trees; no approximation below 6/5 for axis-parallel matchings, p. 8) and gives an IP framework with polynomial-time LP bounds and an iterated rounding conjectured to be constant-factor (abstract, p. 1; p. 23). Limits are concrete: the step from Jain's argument to a max objective is still missing, with hope resting on a 2003 personal communication (p. 23; ref. [18], p. 25); hardness needs degeneracy; triangulations under the general crossing number are open; and the ellipsoid method "has to be considered a drawback" (p. 23). Small cases (stabbing number 2) are polynomial, and minimizing the average stabbing number reduces to minimum length (pp. 23–24).

**Method links**:
- Method 2 evidence ✅ (Exit B): the constant-factor claim is labelled a conjecture in the abstract and the conclusion (pp. 1, 23), and a reading of LP solutions (no constant lower bound on all support edges, Fig. 11) stays an indication (p. 19).
- Method 3 variant ⚠: no computation in the paper; the empirical statements are hedged ("seems to be even better", p. 23) and point to the separate report [12] (pp. 2–3).
- Method 4 variant ⚠ (step 5 only): open questions close the paper (p. 23).
- H10 evidence ✅: outside experts named for the parts outside his school: Mitchell for the problem, Kamal Jain on iterated rounding (p. 24; ref. [18], p. 25).
- H8 evidence ✅: SODA 2004, then DCG 2008 (p. 1).
- G1 evidence ✅: polynomial-time lower bounds from the LP (abstract, p. 1; p. 18).
- new-pattern candidates: first exact IP in a theory-dominated area ("the first algorithmic paper on stabbing numbers", p. 23); LP relaxation as approximation-design instrument (pp. 17–23).

**Transferable techniques**:
- For a min-max objective over lines (or any other family of probes), add one row per representative probe with a common variable k, and cut the family down by a combinatorial argument (critical lines through one or two points) (pp. 17–18).
- When a known rounding lemma fails for your constraint type, prove a structural property of some optimal LP vertex instead (planar support), then read a heavy variable off that structure (pp. 19–23).
- Uncrossing device: among all optimal LP solutions choose one that minimizes a secondary potential (total stabbing number); a crossing pair would allow an ε-shift that lowers it (pp. 19–22).
- In hardness gadgets, use collinear rows that exhaust the budget in one direction, so the gadgets cannot interact (barriers) (p. 5).

**Quotes**:
- "Most previous work on stabbing and crossing problems has focused on extremal properties, and little has been known about the computational complexity of actually finding structures of low stabbing number, or possible approximation algorithms." (p. 2)
- "We are hopeful that this argument can be completed some time in the future [18]." (p. 23)

### S028 · Optimal university course timetables and the partial transversal polytope (Experimental Algorithms, WEA 2008, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-68552-4_18)
**Read level**: full. All 14 file pages: abstract, introduction and related work (pp. 1–3), the two IPs and the decomposition (pp. 3–4), the partial transversal polytope with its facets and their number (pp. 5–10), computation (pp. 10–11), discussion (pp. 11–12), references (pp. 12–13) and the appendix "A Auxiliary Results" with Lemmas 8–12 (pp. 13–14). The file has no printed page numbers (01-publications.md gives printed pp. 235–248).
**Role**: supplement · **Coauthors**: Gerald Lach (TU Berlin), whose 2007 Master's thesis on room allocation at TU Berlin is ref. [18] (p. 13) · **Position in trajectory**: the first of the Lach–Lübbecke timetabling papers listed in 01-publications.md (W18); the soft-constraint companion on the Udine instances is ref. [17] (p. 12). In 2015 Lübbecke called this two-stage approach a Benders decomposition (TOP comment, p. 360, as recorded in 05-peer-critique.md §7; not in this text).

- **D1 Problem & entry**: Course timetabling is dominated by metaheuristics; earlier exact IPs solved instances smaller "by at least an order of magnitude" or much simpler ones (pp. 2–3). The entry is a diagnosis: IP was used for timetabling "only because of its modeling power", not for the problem's combinatorial properties (p. 2). Aim: a "proof-of-concept" that optimal timetables can be computed for large universities (p. 2).
- **D2 Core idea & algorithm design**: Replace the three-index IP (1)–(5) over course, time and room (p. 3) by a two-index IP (6)–(10) over course and time whose Hall inequalities (8) guarantee that rooms can be matched later; rooms then come from a sequence of minimum-weight bipartite matchings, so "clearly, this decomposition approach is exact" (p. 4). Hall's inequalities could be separated by max flow, but the authors want the strongest formulation (p. 4): the partial transversal polytope is a polymatroid, and Edmonds' theorem gives its facets (pp. 5–6).
- **D3 Problem class & data model**: binary course–time variables; time conflicts as a conflict graph with stable-set structure, room conflicts implicit (pp. 3–4); the objective is the instructors' time preferences (p. 3); hard constraints only, with room capacity promoted from soft to hard (p. 2). The number of facets stays small when rooms have few inclusive features: at most 2^φ − 1 defining C-sets per connected component (Lemma 6), O(1) for a fixed number of features (Corollary 7) (pp. 9–10); the enumeration is polynomial only if that number is (p. 9).
- **D4 Algorithm & implementation design (brief)**: Theorem 2: a set induces a facet if and only if it is a "defining C-set", an f-flat whose deficiency exceeds that of any split, proved through f-inseparability (p. 6); Corollary 3 strengthens Hall's conditions (p. 6); Lemma 4 builds facets by "flat-union" (p. 7); an example with 2^{|R|} − 1 defining C-sets (p. 7); Theorem 5, at most def(G) atomic defining C-sets, by induction on |R| with inclusion–exclusion over a supermodular function (pp. 8–9, 13–14). Implementation: enumerate all defining C-sets and add every facet up front instead of writing branch-and-cut, because there were few, about 6400 non-trivial facets on the largest instance (pp. 9–10, 12). The algorithm itself is postponed to a full paper (p. 9).
- **D5 Computational evaluation (one line)**: CPLEX 10.1 on one 3.2 GHz Pentium 4 with 1 GB (p. 10), times per stage (facets, IP, matchings) (p. 10); the seven ITC2007 instances solve in under 0.6 s with zero violations, for the hard constraints only (p. 10; Table 1, p. 11); three simulated instances, the largest "almost identical" in key data to TU Berlin's, take up to 1235 s + 5106 s + 5 s (p. 11; Table 2); the only baseline is the authors' own three-index IP, with gaps of under 2% and 7% after 30 s and 510 s and "no solution" after a day on the large instance (Table 3, p. 11; the stopping rule is not stated); almost 80% of instructors get their first-choice slots (p. 11). No other method is compared, and the generator is not described (pp. 10–11).
- **D8 Contribution & limits**: practical and polyhedral at once (p. 2): exact solutions at sizes called "the largest reported in the literature by far" (abstract, p. 1), and a facet description of the partial transversal polytope with a strengthening of Hall's theorem (pp. 2, 6). Limits: hard constraints only, while the sentence that almost all soft constraints "can be easily included" without a significant slowdown has no data behind it (p. 10); the real TU Berlin database "is severely inconsistent and incomplete" (p. 12); interactive use needs tuning (p. 11). Future work: repair the data and timetable the whole university (p. 12).

**Method links**:
- Method 1 evidence ✅ (steps 2 and 4): the compact three-index IP is kept and run as the yardstick the reformulation must beat (pp. 4, 11), and the build decision is measured: few facets, so all go in up front and no branch-and-cut is written (p. 12).
- Method 3 evidence ✅: the "proof-of-concept" framing (p. 2) and the stated reason for simulated data (p. 12).
- Method 3 contradiction ✗ (step 6): "the largest reported in the literature by far" (abstract, p. 1) and "a significant step forward" (p. 2) rest on comparison with the authors' own compact IP only, on three simulated instances, with the soft constraints of the competition instances set aside (pp. 10–11); the claim about soft constraints is not measured (p. 10).
- G4 evidence ✅: structure, not modelling power, as the key to solving large instances (p. 2).
- B1 evidence ✅ (consistent): one owned objective, instructor preferences, with hard constraints kept hard rather than a bag of weighted penalties (pp. 2–3).
- new-pattern candidates: integer only where needed (rooms projected out by Hall-type inequalities and recovered by matchings, p. 4); first exact IP in a heuristic-dominated area (pp. 2–3).

**Transferable techniques**:
- If one assignment dimension is a bipartite matching once the others are fixed, project it out with Hall-type inequalities and recover it by matchings afterwards; the decomposition stays exact (p. 4).
- Before writing a separation routine, count the inequalities you would separate; if there are few, add them all up front (pp. 10, 12).
- Keep the intuitive compact model as a baseline table (size, run time, gap) next to the reformulation (Table 3, p. 11).
- When real data are unusable, say why, and generate instances that match the real key figures (pp. 10–12).
